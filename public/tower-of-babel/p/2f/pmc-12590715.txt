Impact of body mass index on neck muscle changes and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing hemithyroidectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pengyan Zhang, Chunjie Hou, Jiajie Xu, Qiaohong Hu, Jinglan Tang, Liping Gong, Litao Sun, Pei Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study primarily investigates the impact of body mass index (BMI) on complications and neck muscle function in patients following unilateral thyroid lobectomy.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on patients who underwent hemithyroidectomy and central compartment lymphadenectomy between May 2022 and March 2024. We compared various variables, including population characteristics, neck muscle thickness changes, and complications, between normal (BMI < 25.0 kg/m²) and overweight (BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m²) patients.ResultsA total of 376 patients were included in the research. Notable differences in age and surgical approach were identified between overweight and normal patients (p < 0.05). Overweight patients exhibited significantly elevated omohyoid muscle thickness (P = 0.009) and Scar Cosmesis Assessment and Rating (SCAR) scores (P < 0.001). Conversely, normal patients demonstrated a markedly higher risk of postoperative hemorrhage (P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe overweight cohort had a higher proportion of male patients, who were predominantly treated with sternocleidomastoid leading-edge approach (SLEA) surgery, a technique linked to reduced postoperative hemorrhage. However, these patients experienced more pronounced alterations in omohyoid muscle thickness and recorded elevated SCAR scores in comparison to their normalcounterparts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03250-8.

Background

Obesity represents a growing clinical and public health burden in both developed and developing countries, with elevated body mass index(BMI). Thyroidectomy consistently linked to metabolic and cardiovascular disorders, various malignancies, and increasingly recognized as a contributing factor to the rising incidence of thyroid diseases [1, 2].

Thyroidectomy is a widely performed surgery for the treatment of both benign and malignant thyroid diseases [3]. Thyroid cancer has emerged as one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, with papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) representing the predominant histological subtype [4]; however, other well-differentiated and more aggressive variants also occur and are typically managed by surgeons with expertise in head and neck or endocrine surgery [5, 6]. Benign indications for thyroidectomy encompass nodular or multinodular goiters presenting with compressive symptoms, aesthetic concerns, or hyperthyroidism refractory to medical management [7, 8].

Despite extensive research, the impact of overweight on thyroidectomy outcomes remains debated, with conflicting findings on postoperative morbidity, but consistent evidence showing longer operative times in patients with higher body mass index [9–13]. Concurrently, the prognostic factors after thyroidectomy in overweight patients remain unclear. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, overweight is defined as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² [14].To improve cohort comparability, the analysis was restricted to patients who underwent unilateral lobectomy, a surgical approach associated with a lower risk of perioperative complications and a reduced likelihood of requiring lifelong thyroid hormone supplementation compared to total thyroidectomy [10].

This study primarily investigates the common postoperative complications and prognostic factors in overweight patients undergoing unilateral thyroid lobectomy, with a specific focus on the effect of BMI on the neck muscles (sternocleidomastoid, omohyoid, sternohyoid), vocal function, and swallowing function.

Materials and methods

Study design, patients, setting, and time frame

First, this study included 433 patients entering the surgical evaluation process who been to Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital between May 2022 and March 2023. Then patient eligibility was determined according to the following predefined inclusion criteria: (1) age 18 to 65 years (2) with the presence of unilateral thyroid pathology and a clear surgical indication for unilateral thyroidectomy combined with central compartment lymphadenectomy, as determined by multidisciplinary clinical evaluation. Patients with a history of cervical surgery or trauma, lesions infiltrating cervical musculature, preoperative cervical discomfort, phonatory or deglutition dysfunction, or multifocal tumors in anatomically distinct regions were excluded from the study. The exact process is shown in our flowchart (Fig. 1). This investigation was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Patients were afforded the opportunity to select their preferred surgical approach, and informed consent was duly obtained in writing from all participants. We declare that all our research processes have been completed on the basis of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Demographic characteristics, perioperative parameters, and pathological data were retrospectively retrieved from the electronic medical records of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Representative demographic data included the following: Perioperative variables comprised: BMI, age, sex, thyroid ultrasound data, such as lesion laterality, maximum diameter (mm), pathology (PTC/nodular goiter); Operative details: surgical approach(endoscopic thyroidectomy by gasless unilateral axillary approach (ETGUA), sternocleidomastoid leading-edge approach (SLEA), conventional open thyroidectomy (COT)), Procedure duration (minutes), drainage output (ml), lymph nodes sampled (count); Postoperative outcomes: hospitalization length (days), The most recent postoperative review of blood tests, including: C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, hypocalcemia, parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels, and transient hoarseness.

Assessment of body mass index (BMI) status

All patients were retested for height and weight after admission using the same instrument. To ensure group homogeneity and enable a reliable comparison of surgical outcomes while minimizing potential selection bias, a 1:2 matching of overweight to normal patients was performed. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the standardized body mass index classifications established by the World Health Organization: BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (overweight group) and BMI < 25 kg/m2 (normal group) [15, 16]. BMI was determined at the initial clinical evaluation based on standardized measurements of patient height and weight.

Muscle thickness assessment and postoperative functional outcomes

A Siemens ultrasound system was employed to assess the bilateral thickness of the sternocleidomastoid, sternohyoid, and omohyoid muscles in all patients. To ensure measurement consistency, a single experienced sonographer performed all assessments. The resulting data underwent joint assessment and approval by two ultrasound imaging professors prior to statistical entry, guaranteeing reliability and minimizing errors. The muscle thickness differential was derived by subtracting the operative side measurement from the non-operative side (Fig. 2). Postoperative functional outcomes were evaluated using established scales: the Voice Handicap Index (VHI) for voice function, the Swallowing Impairment Score (SIS) for swallowing function, and the Scar Cosmesis Assessment and Rating (SCAR) for surgical cosmesis [17–19]. Additionally, the Neck Injury Index (NII) quantified surgical trauma extent in the neck (Supplementary Tables 1–4). A single clinical physician conducted all evaluations using these four scales. To ensure data integrity, the final results were jointly reviewed and confirmed by two surgery professors before statistical analysis.

Selection of surgical technique

The choice of surgical approach (conventional open thyroidectomy vs. endoscopic thyroidectomy) was made after a comprehensive discussion with the patient regarding the risks, benefits, and expected cosmetic outcomes. The primary criteria for recommending the endoscopic approach were: (1) a strong patient desire for a scarless neck; and (2) the presence of benign thyroid nodules less than 4 cm in diameter or low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC). The conventional open approach was recommended for patients with large nodules (> 4 cm), suspected advanced malignancy, radiologic evidence of extrathyroidal extension or lymph node metastasis, or those who simply preferred the conventional method.

Patients were considered eligible for unilateral thyroidectomy if they met all of the following criteria: (1) Preoperative ultrasound and cytology suggesting a classic variant PTC. (2) Tumor diameter ≤ 4 cm. (3) No radiological evidence of extrathyroidal extension (ETE) on ultrasound. (4) No evidence of multifocal disease in the ipsilateral lobe on preoperative imaging. (5) No evidence of cervical lymph node metastasis (cN0) on preoperative ultrasound. (6) No history of head and neck radiation or familial thyroid cancer. Conversely, total thyroidectomy was performed for patients with any of the following: (1) Preoperative or intraoperative evidence of multifocal disease, ETE, or lymph node metastasis. (2) Tumors located in the isthmus.

(3) A history of radiation exposure or familial thyroid carcinoma. (4) Patient preference after discussion of the risks and benefits of both procedures. By applying these stringent criteria, we aimed to select a low-risk cohort for whom unilateral thyroidectomy is an oncologically safe procedure with the benefit of avoiding lifelong hormone replacement therapy and potentially reducing surgical complications [20–22].

Thyroidectomy procedures

Patients were positioned supine for ETGUA and COT procedures, or posterior supine for SLEA, with appropriate neck alignment. Standardized 4 cm incisions were created as follows: along axillary skin folds (ETGUA), as a low collar lateral incision 2 cm above the clavicle following natural skin lines (SLEA), and two finger breadths above the sternal notch along the skin grain (COT). Dissection techniques varied by approach: ETGUA: A subcutaneous tunnel extended from axilla to sternocleidomastoid muscle; strap muscles were elevated using a “U”-shaped hook inserted between their clavicular and sternal heads, with endoscopic visualization via a 30-degree camera through the tunnel. SLEA: Dissection proceeded along the sternocleidomastoid’s medial margin to the lateral boundary of strap muscles adjacent to sternothyroid muscle. COT: Skin flaps were elevated, linea alba cervicalis opened, and strap muscles divided under lateral traction. All procedures employed an ultrasonic scalpel (Harmonic Scalpel, Johnson & Johnson Medical, Cincinnati, OH) for thyroid dissection and hemostasis. Final steps included strap muscle approximation (with suturing in COT) and layered wound closure [23].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data reported as frequencies and percentages. For intergroup comparisons, ANOVA test was applied to normally distributed continuous variables across three or more groups, Pearson’s chi-square test to categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test to non-normally distributed continuous variables between two groups. Fisher’s precision probability test was used for non-normally distributed categorical variables for small samples and rank sum test for large samples. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 served as the threshold for statistical significance. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In summary, a total of 376 patients were enrolled. Among them, 343 were histopathologically confirmed with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), while 33 were diagnosed with nodular goiter. 286 were female (76.1%) and 90 were male (23.9%). Thus 256 patients (normal group) had BMI < 25 and 120 patients (overweight group) had BMI ≥ 25. Baseline characteristics stratified by obesity status are summarized in Table 1. The overweight and normal cohorts demonstrated comparable baseline distributions of age, pathological subtype, maximum lesion diameter, and lesion side (p > 0.05 for all comparisons) (Table 1). Including mean BMI (22.2 vs. 26.7 kg/m2, P<0.001) and female sex (215/256 vs. 71/120, P<0.001). Age distribution (P = 0.384) and maximum lesion side (P = 0.846) were comparable between groups. Maximum lesion diameter, assessed via ultrasonography, demonstrated comparable distributions between groups (median 6.0 mm for both cohorts; P = 0.846). Notably, overweight patients disproportionately underwent SLEA (50.0% vs. 27.7%) versus ETGUA (15.8% vs. 47.7%) approaches (χ² = 36.388, P < 0.001).

Changes in muscle thickness

All participants received unilateral thyroidectomy. Changes in sternocleidomastoid muscle thickness showed no significant intergroup difference (P = 0.868). For the omohyoid muscle, median thickness changes were significantly smaller in the normal group compared to the overweight group (P < 0.05). Although sternohyoid muscle changes were reduced in normal patients relative to overweight counterparts, this difference lacked statistical significance (P = 0.536). Importantly, no perioperative complications—including vascular compromise, neural injury, or muscular disruption—occurred in any patient (Table 2).

Surgical profile

Normal patients exhibited a significantly longer median duration of surgery than overweight patients [82.5 min (65,115) vs.67.5 min (55,90)] (P < 0.001). No significant intergroup differences were observed in hospitalization duration, postoperative length of stay, pain scores, or intraoperative blood loss incidence (all P >0.05). Although overweight patients showed reduced median lymph node yields and drainage volumes versus normal patients [3 (2–6) vs. 4 nodes (2–6), P = 0.076; 100 mL (75–131.8) vs. 110 mL (84.3–150), P = 0.087], these comparisons were not statistically significant. Postoperative metrics—including body temperature, hypocacemia, CRP, TSH, and PTH levels—remained within normal limits for the majority of patients in both cohorts, with no statistically significant intergroup differences observed (Table 4).

These correlations were analyzed using the bivariate Spearman test (Table 5).

Multiple linear regression analysis

We noted that, in our results presented above, a greater proportion of patients in the overweight group underwent SLEA compared to the normal weight group. Therefore, the significant difference in SCAR outcomes could potentially be influenced by the differing surgical approaches, meaning the disparity might be a result of the procedure itself rather than solely attributable to BMI. To substantiate our conclusions and account for this potential confounding effect, we performed a multiple linear regression analysis. We conducted a multivariate linear regression analysis with the SCAR score as the dependent variable. The independent variables included BMI group (normal weight vs. overweight) and surgical approach (categorized as COT vs. SLEA vs. ETGUA) (Table 6). After adjusting for the surgical approach, BMI group remained independently associated with a higher SCAR score [β = 1.82, 95% CI (0.94–2.70), p < 0.001]. Of note, the influence of the surgical approach on the SCAR score cannot be overlooked. Our results demonstrated that both the Sternocleidomastoid-Lateral Approach (SLEA) [β = 2.15, 95% CI (1.17 to 3.13), p < 0.001] and ETGUA [β = 0.95, 95% CI (0.01 to 1.89), p < 0.001] were significant factors influencing the SCAR score. Furthermore, operative duration may also be associated with the type of surgical procedure, which differed significantly between the two groups.Similarly, we conducted a multivariate linear regression analysis for operative time. After adjusting for the surgical approach, BMI group (overweight) remained a significant independent predictor of longer operative time [β = 12.85, 95% CI (5.35–20.35), p < 0.001] (Table 7). Similarly, both SLEA [β = 18.30, 95% CI (9.45 to 27.15), p < 0.001] and ETGUA [β = 25.10, 95% CI (15.05 to 35.15), p < 0.001] were also identified as significant factors influencing operative duration.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a significantly higher prevalence of thyroid disease in females (76.1%), aligning with numerous other studies. Additionally, women are more prone to reporting diminished quality of life compared to men [24]. Mishra et al. (2013) demonstrated that females exhibited notably improved outcomes in quality of life following thyroid surgery. It appears that women derive greater benefit from thyroidectomy compared to men [25]. In our study, we observed a higher proportion of males in the overweight group, which may contribute to the increased complication rates associated with elevated BMI, particularly in thyroid cancer surgeries. Surgeons must contend not only with the technical challenges posed by excess adipose tissue but also with the comorbidities commonly linked to obesity, both of which can influence surgical outcomes [9]. Buerba et al. [10] highlighted that obese and morbidly obese patients face a heightened risk of wound complications following thyroid and parathyroid surgery. However, their study did not specify the surgical approach employed, leaving the clinical significance of these findings uncertain. Our study demonstrates that individuals with normal weight are more likely to opt for the ETGUA surgical method, whereas overweight patients tend to prefer the SLEA technique.

The limited impact of ETGUA and SLEA on voice and swallowing function is attributable to minimal strap muscle disturbance [23], as these approaches separate the thyroid from strap muscles without cutting or suturing infrahyoid muscles. Consequently, we evaluated BMI’s influence on strap muscles (sternohyoid/omohyoid) and sternocleidomastoid muscle. Results indicate minimal BMI effects on sternocleidomastoid and sternohyoid muscles. However, omohyoid muscle alterations were significantly less pronounced in normal versus overweight patients. Additionally, the ETGUA approach offers a notable advantage in scar satisfaction, as the concealed incision in the armpit reduces skin tension, thereby minimizing the likelihood of visible scarring [26, 27]. In our research, normal patients are more inclined to choose the ETGUA surgical approach, which explains their clinically significant and statistically meaningful advantage in SCAR scores compared to the overweight group. This finding is consistent with results from previous studies [28–30].

Obesity increases technical difficulty in thyroid surgery, as excess adiposity can obscure visualization of critical structures requiring routine identification and preservation—notably the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands [31]. This aligns with reports of elevated permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve injury rates in overweight patients [32]. However, our study found no significant differences in VHI/SIS scores or postoperative transient hoarseness between normal and overweight groups. Correlation analyses further revealed positive associations of SCAR, VHI, and SIS scores with both omohyoid muscle thickening and BMI. Collectively, these findings suggest that BMI may underlie the compromised voice/swallowing function and omohyoid thickness changes observed in overweight individuals.

As demonstrated in numerous studies, obesity is associated with longer operative times, particularly in neck surgeries [11, 33]. Although overweightpatients experience neck extension deficits, which limit surgical site exposure and increase the complexity of the procedure, potentially leading to longer operative times and increased blood loss, their studies did not address the specific impact of the surgical approach on surgery duration [9, 10]. Our findings in this study contradict the previously obtained results. Patients with a BMI < 25 had longer operative times compared to those with a BMI ≥ 25, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant. In the BMI < 25 group, 47.66% underwent the ETGUA surgical approach, which was associated with longer operative times, extended drain placement duration, increased drainage volume, and prolonged hospital stays. In the BMI ≥ 25 group, only 15.83% chose the ETGUA surgical approach, which may explain the contrasting results compared to previous studies.

Consistent with Finel JB et al. [33], our study found no significant differences in specific postoperative complication rates after thyroidectomy. We specifically investigated endocrine-specific complications in normal versus overweight patients undergoing endocrine surgery. Hypocalcemia occurred in 17.5% of patients with BMI >25. Compared to this cohort, patients with BMI ≤ 25 showed a lower hypocalcemia incidence (13.28% vs. 17.5%), though without statistical significance. This aligns with variable hypoparathyroidism rates reported in prior studies [34]. Although the risk of hypocalcemia is substantially lower following unilateral thyroidectomy than after total thyroidectomy, it remains a possible complication due to inadvertent compromise of parathyroid function on the operative side, with a reported incidence ranging from 1% to 27%. Our observed rate of 13%~17% is consistent with these previous reports [35–37]. Notably, hypocalcemia remains a critical complication of total thyroidectomy. While typically transient and self-resolving, prompt recognition and treatment of tetany are essential.

Obesity is considered to increase the likelihood of postoperative infections. The prevention of hospital-acquired infections has a clear clinical significanc. The correlation is most evident in orthopedic, cardiac, vascular, and gastrointestinal surgeries. This has also been consistently demonstrated in thyroid surgery [38, 39]. In this study, the infection rate was slightly higher in the overweight group compared to the normal group (8.22% vs. 8.33%, P = 0.966). The incidence of postoperative infections was greater than that reported in previous studies [40]. This may result from the cervical lymph node dissection and postoperative drainage in all patients. The extensive surgery, drain placement, and longer operative time could increase the infection risk [3, 41].

This study presents several key limitations. Firstly, this study is limited by its retrospective design and single-center data, highlighting the need for validation through prospective, multicenter research. The retrospective research of our study is associated with an inherent potential for selection bias, and our findings should be interpreted within this context. Furthermore, the assessment of thyroid nodules via ultrasound, while standard practice, is operator-dependent and may introduce variability. Secondly, the assessment of functional outcomes was based on patient-reported questionnaires, which, although validated, are not objective physiological measurements. Subsequently, more direct and objective measurements of functional recovery should be collected to further substantiate our conclusions. Moreover, there was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of surgical approaches between the two patient groups. Although we performed multiple linear regression analysis to adjust for this confounding factor, we cannot entirely rule out its potential influence on the observed outcomes, which may not be solely attributable to the role of BMI. Future studies utilizing larger, multi-center clinical datasets with rigorous control of variables will be required to further validate our results.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. It provides a direct comparison of clinicopathological parameters and perioperative outcomes between patients with normal weight and overweight, a comparison that is still underexplored in the context of thyroid surgery. Furthermore, our cohort specifically assessed outcomes after unilateral thyroidectomy, a less commonly studied procedure due to its strict indications, particularly in Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) [42, 43]. Lastly, the inclusion of patients who underwent endoscopic thyroidectomy by gasless unilateral axillary approach (ETGUA) enhances the generalizability of our findings to contemporary surgical practices.

Conclusions

In this study of 376 hemithyroidectomy patients, no significant differences were observed across BMI classes in Sternocleidomastoid and Sternohyoid muscle metrics, NII, VHI, SIS, or endocrine-specific surgical complications. We found that the overweight cohort had a higher proportion of male patients, mostly treated with SLEA surgery, a technique associated with reduced postoperative hemorrhage. However, these patients experienced more pronounced alterations in omohyoid muscle thickness and recorded elevated SCAR scores in comparison to their normal counterparts. The small sample size may not capture the infrequent surgical complications after thyroid surgery. Larger, multi-institutional studies on endocrine outcomes and quality measures are needed to validate these findings.
