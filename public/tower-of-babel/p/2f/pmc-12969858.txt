Maternal food and nutrition literacy and its association with growth indicators in children and adolescents: a cross-sectional study in Türkiye
Authors: Çağdaş Salih Meriç, Kezban Şahin Demirci, Hacı Ömer Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood growth is critical for long-term health and development. Maternal food and nutrition literacy is one of the most closely related determinants shaping children’s eating habits and growth patterns; it is a multidimensional construct comprising knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. There are limited studies in Türkiye investigating the relationship between maternal food and nutrition literacy and child and adolescent growth across a wide age range. This study aims to assess the relationship between maternal levels of food and nutrition literacy and their children’s anthropometric growth indicators.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted with mothers living in Türkiye who had at least one child aged 2–19 years (n = 263). The height and weight measurements of mothers and children were recorded based on self-reports; Weight-for-Height (WHZ), Height-for-Age (HAZ), BMI-for-Age (BAZ), and Weight-for-Age (WAZ) z-scores were calculated using World Health Organization references for the children’s growth assessment. Mothers’ food and nutrition literacy was measured using the Food and Nutrition Literacy (FNL) Scale, and FNL scores were divided into tertiles (T1, T2 and T3). Nutritional practices and growth indicators were compared between the three groups using appropriate statistical tests. In addition, multivariate linear regression models were created to examine the relationships between growth indicators and FNL and its subscales.ResultsThe mean age of mothers was 38.10 ± 8.17 years, and a significant number of the mothers had limited knowledge (39.92%), attitudes (47.15%), and behaviors (51.33%). Mothers with high FNL levels were more likely to start complementary feeding after 6 months, pay attention to label information, and monitor their children’s food consumption more frequently (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in the children’s WHZ, HAZ, BAZ, and WAZ values among the three FNL groups (p > 0.05). According to multivariate regression analysis, the behavioral subscale of maternal food and nutrition literacy was positively and significantly associated with HAZ in children and adolescents aged 5–19 years after adjustment for maternal BMI and feeding practices (β = 0.158, p < 0.05 in Model 2; β = 0.177, p < 0.05 in Model 3). No significant relationship was found between the knowledge and attitude subscales and growth indicators.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that maternal food and nutrition literacy, particularly its behavioral component, is associated with HAZ, a measure of long-term growth in children and adolescents. The limited effect of the knowledge and attitude subscales alone on growth highlights that the behavioral translation of nutrition literacy is critical for child health. The findings suggest that family-based and behavior-focused educational interventions may be more effective than those focused solely on knowledge transfer. Future studies with longitudinal designs and incorporating additional variables such as the nutrition environment, diet quality, and physical activity will provide a more comprehensive explanation of the effects of maternal food and nutrition literacy on growth.Keywords: Food and nutrition literacy, Child growth indicators, Maternal nutrition behaviors, Parenting

Introduction

Optimal growth throughout childhood and adolescence is fundamental to long-term health and human capital. Deviations from normative growth patterns, whether in the form of undernutrition (stunting, wasting) or excess adiposity, are associated with elevated risks of infectious and noncommunicable disease, impaired cognitive development, and diminished social and educational outcomes [1]. Many middle-income countries, Türkiye among them, now confront a complex nutrition landscape in which residual undernutrition coexists with rising rates of overweight and obesity. This “double burden” complicates public-health priorities and calls for interventions that operate across both structural and household levels [2, 3].

Within households, maternal capacities that determine everyday food choices are proximate determinants of children’s dietary exposures and growth trajectories [4]. Food and nutrition literacy (FNL) denotes a set of interrelated competencies that enable individuals to obtain, interpret, and apply nutrition information in everyday contexts. Beyond factual knowledge, FNL comprises functional skills (for example, meal planning and label reading), interpersonal capacities (such as negotiating family meals and communicating about diet), and critical appraisal skills that allow caregivers to judge the trustworthiness of nutrition messages and resist misleading commercial marketing [5–7]. Because mothers commonly control food procurement, preparation, and feeding routines, maternal FNL plausibly influence complementary-feeding practices, dietary diversity, timing of food introduction, portioning, and other behaviors that determine energy and nutrient intake across developmental windows [8, 9].

Although maternal FNL exerts its most immediate influence during early childhood, when caregivers directly shape feeding practices such as complementary feeding, portioning, and meal preparation, its relevance extends well beyond this period [1, 9]. As children transition into middle childhood and adolescence and gain greater autonomy over their dietary choices, maternal FNL continues to operate through more indirect yet structurally influential pathways. These pathways include shaping the home food environment, determining food availability and accessibility, modeling eating behaviors, establishing meal structures and food-related norms, and providing guidance and monitoring around dietary choices [6–8]. Mothers with higher levels of FNL are therefore better positioned to foster environments that support healthier eating patterns, limit routine exposure to energy-dense and ultra-processed foods, and reinforce nutritious choices even as adolescents exercise increasing independence [2, 7]. Over time, such sustained environmental and behavioral influences may contribute to cumulative nutritional exposures that are reflected in long-term growth outcomes, particularly linear growth indicators such as height-for-age [1, 3].

There are several plausible pathways by which maternal FNL could affect child growth. Improved knowledge and hands-on skills can raise the nutritional quality of complementary foods and support diets that meet children’s requirements during sensitive periods, thereby fostering linear growth and healthy weight gain [10]. In contexts of limited resources, caregivers with stronger FNL are also more likely to identify and prioritize nutrient-dense foods, reinterpret packaging information to make cost-effective choices, and adapt recipes to boost micronutrient density [11, 12]. Finally, critical literacy enables caregivers to navigate conflicting information sources, public health guidance versus commercial advertising, and to choose feeding strategies that protect children from the adverse effects of heavily marketed ultra-processed foods [3, 13, 14].

Recent empirical work offers partial support for these mechanisms but remains heterogeneous [15, 16]. Over the past five years, cross-sectional and experimental studies have linked greater maternal nutrition literacy to more appropriate infant and young-child feeding practices and, in some settings, to improved anthropometric outcomes; conversely, limited FNL has been associated with poorer feeding behaviors and higher malnutrition risk [1, 12]. For example, Tahreem et al. reported widespread suboptimal feeding in a Pakistani cohort [1], with complex and sometimes attenuated associations between maternal literacy and anthropometry; Hashemzadeh et al. [2] observed that enhanced maternal FNL related to lower odds of childhood overweight in Iran [3], with household food-security status modifying this relationship. These findings suggest that the influence of FNL is context-sensitive and often contingent on broader socioeconomic and food-access constraints.

Evidence from Türkiye mirrors these international patterns but also highlights specific gaps. National and single-centre studies have associated higher maternal literacy with healthier complementary-feeding indicators [17] while clinical reports suggest associations between caregiver nutrition literacy and child wasting or anemia [17–19]. Nevertheless, much of the Turkish literature remains cross-sectional, focuses primarily on infancy, or employs instruments that capture only one facet of literacy. As a result, the extent to which maternal FNL continues to influence growth in middle childhood and adolescence, and whether different FNL domains (knowledge, attitudes, behaviors) exert distinct effects, remains unclear [20, 21].

Methodological heterogeneity further complicates synthesis. Studies differ in how they operationalize FNL (single-domain knowledge scales versus multidimensional instruments), in which anthropometric outcomes they examine (Height-for-Age z-scores (HAZ), Weight-for-Height z-scores (WHZ), BMI-for-Age z-scores (BAZ), and Weight-for-Age z-scores (WAZ), and in the extent to which they measure and adjust for critical confounders such as maternal education, caregiver body mass index (BMI), household food security, and concrete feeding practices [22–25]. These methodological limitations impede causal inference and obscure potential age and culture-specific pathways linking maternal literacy to growth.

The present study seeks to address several of these limitations. Using the validated, multidimensional FNL instrument developed by Demir and Özer to examine relationships between maternal FNL and multiple anthropometric indices (HAZ, WHZ, BAZ, WAZ) in a Turkish sample of children and adolescents aged 2–19 years [5]. By categorizing maternal FNL into tertiles and explicitly modelling maternal BMI and measured feeding practices as covariates, the study evaluates whether the behavioral component of FNL demonstrates a distinct association with linear growth (HAZ) and other growth parameters during mid-childhood and adolescence. This approach expands the existing evidence base beyond infancy, integrates behavioral and contextual mediators, and informs whether maternal-focused literacy interventions might plausibly support healthy growth trajectories across later developmental stages in Türkiye. The objective of this study was to examine the association between maternal FNL and child and adolescent growth outcomes, assessed by multiple anthropometric indices (HAZ, WHZ, BAZ, and WAZ), in a Turkish population aged 2–19 years, while accounting for maternal BMI and feeding practices.

Methods

Study populations

This descriptive cross-sectional study focused on mothers living in Türkiye with children under the age of 19. Data collection occurred between January 2024 and June 2024. This online cross-sectional study used a non-probability sampling approach. Participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling via social media platforms, determining that a minimum sample size of at least 255 participants was necessary. This calculation was made using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 program, with a statistical power of 90%, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and an effect size (d) of 0.20. The study included mothers who had at least one child aged 2–19, possessed a minimum of a primary school education, had online access, and consented to participate. Exclusion criteria included mothers with a diagnosed problem related to their child’s or adolescent’s nutrition, those with more than one child aged 2–19, and mothers whose children had food allergies. Additionally, mothers with children aged 0–2 were excluded, as breastfeeding during this period could influence the study’s results. A total of 416 individuals accessed the online survey link. Of these, 386 initiated the survey and 308 completed all questionnaire sections. After excluding respondents who did not meet the inclusion criteria or provided incomplete data, a total of 263 mothers were included in the final analysis.

At the beginning of the research, all participants were informed in detail about the study and signed an informed voluntary consent form. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bandırma Onyedi Eylul University (approval number: 04.01.2024-627). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures contributing to this work were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations.

Data collection

The data for the questionnaire were collected using an online form created with Google Forms. The questionnaire was distributed to mothers through various social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, TikTok, and Telegram. In the first section of the online questionnaire, participants were given information about the purpose and scope of the study. All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study. The questionnaire consists of three sections: an introductory information form, details about the children, and the Food and Nutrition Literacy (FNL) Instrument.

Anthropometric variables

The body weight (kg) and height (cm) of mothers and their children were collected based on self-reported data. The Body Mass Index (BMI) for mothers was calculated by dividing their body weight (kg) by the square of their height (m2). The World Health Organization (WHO) reference values were used to evaluate BMI. According to this classification, individuals with a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 are considered underweight; those with a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2 are classified as having a normal weight; individuals with a BMI between 25.0 and 29.9 kg/m2 are deemed overweight or slightly obese; and those with a BMI of 30.0 kg/m2 or higher are classified as obese. Additionally, both the body weight and height of children were reported by their mothers, and their BMI was calculated accordingly. To assess the growth of children and adolescents, the following parameters were utilized: WHZ, HAZ, BAZ, and WAZ. The WAZ was specifically calculated for children aged 5 to 10 years. Z-scores for children aged 2 to 4 years were analyzed using the WHO Anthro software (version 3.2.2), while z-scores for children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years were calculated using the WHO AnthroPlus software (version 1.0.4).

Food and Nutrition Literacy (FNL) instrument

The FNL instrument is a self-report scale developed by Demir and Özer in Turkish to evaluate food and nutrition literacy [5]. It consists of three domains: knowledge, attitude, and behavior. These domains should be used together. The knowledge domain contains 13 questions, while the attitude and behavior domains include 13 and 10 questions, respectively, making a total of 36 questions. The scoring ranges for each domain are as follows: knowledge (0–13 points), attitude (13–65 points), and behavior (10–50 points). In the knowledge domain, scores of 9 or below indicate limited knowledge, scores between 10 and 11 indicate insufficient knowledge, and scores of 12 or above indicate excellent knowledge. In the attitude domain, scores of 43 or below indicate a limited attitude, scores between 44 and 51 indicate an insufficient attitude, and scores of 52 or above indicate an excellent attitude. For the behavior domain, scores of 25 or below indicate limited behavior, scores between 26 and 33 indicate insufficient behavior, and scores of 34 and above indicate excellent behavior. Accordingly, the total FNL score ranged from a minimum of 23 to a maximum of 128 points [5]. An increase in participants’ scores across these domains can be interpreted as an improvement in their food and nutrition literacy knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. In this study, the FNL levels were categorized into tertiles based on their percentile distribution: T1 (< 33.3 percentile), T2 (33.3–66.6 percentile), and T3 (≥ 66.6 percentile).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using descriptive and exploratory methods appropriate for the data type. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test together with graphical methods (histograms and Q–Q plots). Depending on these assumptions, one-way ANOVA, Welch’s ANOVA, or the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to determine the difference in children’s and adolescents’ growth indicators according to tertile groups. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. The Pearson chi-square test was used to compare categorical feeding practices variables related to children among tertile groups, and the Fisher exact test was used in cases with low cell frequencies. Multivariate linear regression models were constructed with maternal variables added stepwise to determine the relationship between the total food and nutrition literacy score, its subscales and growth indicators. In all models, multicollinearity, residual distribution, and homogeneity assumptions were assessed using standard diagnostic tests. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software, and the level of statistical significance was set at two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents an overview of the general characteristics of individuals participating in the study. The average age of the mothers was 38.10 years (± 8.17), with 35.74% holding a university degree. Additionally, 58.94% were unemployed, 57.42% reported an income that matched their expenses, and the majority (77.19%) resided in urban areas. The BMI of the mothers was 26.55 kg/m² (± 5.00), with 42.97% classified as having a normal BMI. Moreover, the mothers had a mean food and nutrition literacy score of 90.16 (± 10.37). However, many exhibited knowledge (9 or below score) (39.92%), attitudes (43 or below score) (47.15%), and behaviors (25 or below score) (51.33%) classified as limited regarding food and nutrition literacy. The average age of the children involved in the study was 9.67 years (± 4.31), with 52.47% being female. The mean BMI z-score for the children was 0.44 (± 1.75), and more than half (55.14%) were classified as having a normal BMI according to the z-score. The total FNL score theoretically ranges from 23 (minimum achievable score) to 128 (maximum achievable score), allowing the observed mean score of 90.16 ± 10.37 to be interpreted as a moderate-to-high level of food and nutrition literacy.

Table 2 presents the feeding practices of children and adolescents based on their mothers’ FNL levels, categorized into tertiles. During the first six months, a majority of children and adolescents in T1 (63.41%), T2 (79.57%), and T3 (72.73%) were exclusively breastfed (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference in the duration of breastfeeding among the groups (p > 0.05). In the T3 tertile, a higher proportion of children and adolescents began complementary foods at or after six months (69.32%, p < 0.05). Additionally, more mothers in T3 reported receiving nutrition information from a dietitian (37.50%) and participating in healthy nutrition education (47.73%) (p < 0.001). When purchasing food for their children, mothers in T3 paid closer attention to label information (78.41%) compared to those in the other tertiles (p < 0.001). Furthermore, mothers in T3 monitored their children’s food consumption more frequently (95.45%) and reported receiving more support from fathers in this area (63.64%). They were also less likely to reward their children with food (18.18%) (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study comprehensively evaluated the relationship between maternal food and nutrition literacy (knowledge, attitude, and behavior), and the growth indicators of children and adolescents. It demonstrated that maternal nutrition behaviors have a significant and positive relationship with the HAZ, which indicates the linear growth of children and adolescents. However, it is noteworthy that the knowledge and attitude subscales were not found to be associated with growth. These results indicate that not only the cognitive aspect of nutrition literacy but also its practical application in behavior is critically important for child health.

In the current study, the observation that maternal food and nutrition knowledge was not associated with growth indicators is highly consistent with the wide body of evidence in the literature. In Türkiye, Mammadova et al. reported that maternal nutrition knowledge was not significantly related to children’s physical growth, while behaviors were stronger determinants [19]. Similarly, Debela et al. showed that maternal general nutrition knowledge was inadequate in predicting growth outcomes, and that only more practical types of knowledge, such as knowing the consequences of poor nutrition, were associated with HAZ [26]. In addition to these findings, a large-scale study conducted by Fadare et al. in Nigeria showed that the effectiveness of maternal nutrition knowledge on child growth is limited by socioeconomic conditions and food access [27]. According to the results of the study, maternal knowledge alone is insufficient to explain stunting or poor nutrition indicators; knowledge can only have a meaningful impact when food diversity and household food security are adequate [27]. This result confirms that the effect of information has a mechanism that is strongly mediated by structural factors. Subih et al. reported that despite the high level of knowledge among mothers, obesity is prevalent among children [28]; Modjadji reported that economic constraints prevent the application of knowledge. Similarly [29], a systematic review by Prasetyo et al. revealed that knowledge alone is ineffective in improving child nutrition and that behavioral change is the determining factor [21]. Rezaeizadeh et al. meta-analysis reported that maternal nutrition knowledge capacity affects growth only when it shapes food choice and the home nutrition environment [25]. All these findings indicate that the absence of a relationship between the knowledge sub-dimension and growth in our study is an expected result strongly supported by the literature.

Not finding a relationship between maternal nutrition attitudes and growth in the present study is consistent with the literature showing that attitudes alone are not sufficient to determine behavior or clinical outcomes. Masilela and Modjadji reported that despite mothers’ positive attitudes toward healthy eating, the prevalence of stunting in children was high, attributing this to socioeconomic factors (economic deprivation, cultural barriers, and problems accessing food) that prevent attitudes from changing into behavior [30]. Similarly, Girma & Alenko found that maternal nutrition attitudes only become meaningful when combined with decision-making power, domestic control, educational level, and access to resources [31]. Findings from another study also show that attitudes can be easily overshadowed by conditions such as the school environment, peer influence, and the home food environment [28]. In addition to these findings, the influence of attitudes weakens further as adolescence approaches. Zarychta et al. revealed that adolescents are more influenced by concrete behavioral models than by their parents’ verbal attitudes [32]. Considering the broad age range of the study population (2–19 years) and the substantial developmental differences across early childhood, middle childhood, and adolescence, this finding should be interpreted with caution. In the present study, analyses were stratified according to WHO-defined age categories (2 to < 5 years and 5 to 19 years) and age-appropriate growth indicators were applied accordingly. Within this stratified analytical framework, the lack of a significant association between maternal food and nutrition literacy attitudes and growth outcomes suggests that attitudinal components alone may be insufficient to influence anthropometric indicators across the examined developmental contexts. This finding likely reflects the varying degrees of child autonomy, environmental exposure, and behavioral mediation across developmental stages, rather than a uniform developmental effect of maternal attitudes.

The strongest finding of this study, and the one most consistent with the literature, is that maternal feeding behaviors are significantly associated with children’s and adolescents’ HAZ scores. Behavior is the implementation of knowledge and directly shapes the home food environment, food selection, meal patterns, and children’s daily energy and nutrient intake. Therefore, it is the closest determinant of growth. One study showed that maternal behaviors were the strongest variable in determining children’s nutritional quality and risk of malnutrition [19]. Similarly, Kenyan researchers reported that behaviors were a stronger determinant than the mother’s level of knowledge and even socioeconomic status [26]. In Canada, Kakinami et al. showed that parents’ healthy eating behaviors had a significant impact on children’s adiposity indicators [33]. Another study also revealed that parental behaviors are the primary determinant of BMI change in adolescents [32]. This finding, supported by qualitative data, indicates that mothers’ nutrition-related practices shape children’s growth outcomes [29]. These results once again emphasize the central role of behavioral change in child health. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses also reinforce this evidence. Behavior-based parenting practices have been reported to be the most effective interventions for improving child growth indicators, with behavioral change yielding stronger results than knowledge-based programs, particularly in linear growth [21, 25]. The fact that behavior was only found to be associated with HAZ in our study is also consistent with the literature; because HAZ is a biological indicator sensitive to long-term nutritional practices and is less affected by short-term weight fluctuations [34].

Strengths and limitations

The most significant strength of this study is that it addresses maternal food and nutrition literacy within a multidimensional framework encompassing knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors, and comprehensively analyzes the distinct effects of these components on the growth indicators of children and adolescents. Thanks to its sample covering a wide age range (2–19 years), it fills an important gap in the literature regarding how food and nutrition literacy functions during childhood and adolescence. Considering the scarcity of studies examining the relationship between maternal FNL levels and child growth in the Turkish context, the study offers unique and methodologically valuable contributions to the national literature. The validity of the measurement tool, the use of standard methods for anthropometric measurements, and the statistical evaluation of the independent effects of the subscales enhance the reliability of the results. Furthermore, the findings provide clear and practical messages for public health applications, emphasizing the importance of behavior-focused strategies over information-based interventions, thereby strengthening the practical effect of the present study.

However, the study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the causal interpretation of the relationship between variables and makes it difficult to determine the direction of the relationship between behavior and growth. The fact that FNL data is based on maternal self-reporting increases the risk of social desirability or recall bias. The failure to consider other caregivers, such as fathers and grandmothers, who influence child nutrition within the family leads to the assessment of the nutritional environment solely through the mother. Furthermore, the limited control of important determinants such as socioeconomic status, food security, the home food environment, and the mother’s decision-making power makes it difficult to explain the relationships between the knowledge and attitude dimensions more robustly. The use of a non-probability, online sampling strategy may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and cannot be generalized to all mothers in Türkiye. Finally, basing growth indicators solely on anthropometric measurements may exclude other important variables such as micronutrient status, diet quality, and physical activity, which are influenced by nutrition behaviors.

Conclusion

This study showed that maternal food and nutrition literacy is associated with linear growth in children and adolescents, but that knowledge and attitude sub-dimensions alone are not associated in a multidimensional manner. The findings reveal that the cognitive components of food and nutrition literacy only contribute to child health when they are transformed into sustainable behaviors in daily life, and that education based on traditional knowledge transfer is insufficient. In this regard, interventions targeting mothers and caregivers should focus on applied behavioral change strategies such as food selection, portion control, shopping and meal preparation skills, label reading, and organizing the home food environment. Considering that socioeconomic conditions and food access may limit the translation of knowledge into behavior, it is important to strengthen social and structural support mechanisms and to adopt inclusive approaches that involve not only mothers but also other caregivers in the family. In the future, longitudinal studies evaluating more comprehensive variables such as family dynamics and diet quality will contribute to a more holistic explanation of the role of maternal food and nutrition literacy in child health.
