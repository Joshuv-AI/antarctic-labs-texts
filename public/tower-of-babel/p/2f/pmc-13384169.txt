Diagnosis of postoperative atrial fibrillation using smartwatch monitoring after cardiac surgery: protocol for the FAWATCH single-centre, open-label, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Christophe Beyls, Alexis Hermida, Nicolas Mollet, Matthias Vermander, Fabio Noe, Henri Bara, Estelle Josse, Marwa Khamari, Momar Diouf, Osama Abou-Arab, Yazine Mahjoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) remains the most frequent arrhythmic complication after cardiac surgery, with most episodes occurring after discharge from the intensive care unit (ICU) when continuous rhythm monitoring is no longer routinely available. Early detection of POAF is clinically relevant and influences long-term cardiovascular outcomes. Wearable devices combining photoplethysmography technology with single-lead ECG capability offer a potential solution for continuous rhythm monitoring in conventional surgical wards. However, their effectiveness and feasibility in the immediate postoperative in-hospital setting remain insufficiently evaluated. The FAWATCH trial aims to determine whether smartwatch-based rhythm monitoring improves POAF detection within the first 5 days after ICU discharge compared with standard.Methods and analysisFAWATCH is a single-centre, open-label, randomised controlled trial conducted in the cardiac surgery department of Amiens-Picardie University Hospital. Adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass and transferred to the conventional surgical ward within 96 hours are eligible for inclusion. Patients are randomised in a 1:1 ratio using a centralised, computer-generated block randomisation sequence implemented through secure electronic software. Randomisation is stratified by age (>65 years), diabetes status and type of surgery (valvular, coronary or combined procedures). Participants are allocated to either standard care or smartwatch-based monitoring using a conformity European-marked single-lead ECG device (ScanWatch, Withings Move ECG, Withings, France). In the intervention group, patients wear the smartwatch continuously for 5 days and perform on-demand single-lead ECG recordings when alerted or symptomatic, with confirmation by standard 12-lead ECG. In the control group, POAF is diagnosed exclusively by a 12-lead ECG performed on clinical suspicion, as per routine practice. The primary outcome is the incidence of POAF within 5 days following ICU discharge. Secondary outcomes are the proportion of asymptomatic POAF, the association between left atrial reservoir strain and POAF occurrence, the feasibility of smartwatch monitoring, recurrence of POAF and major adverse cardiovascular events at 30 days, 3 months and 6 months. Recruitment began in January 2023, and the trial is currently ongoing, with 258 patients enrolled to date.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Amiens-Picardie University Hospital (IRB-ID: 2022-A00497-36). All participants provide written informed consent in accordance with French regulations for interventional clinical research. The study’s results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT05573633.

Introduction

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is the most common arrhythmia after cardiac surgery, occurring in approximately 30% of patients despite prophylactic strategies and perioperative optimisation.1 POAF is associated with increased risks of heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, progression to persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), prolonged hospital stay and higher healthcare costs.2

Importantly, most POAF episodes are asymptomatic and paroxysmal,3 making detection highly dependent on the intensity and method of rhythm monitoring.4 After discharge from the intensive care unit, rhythm surveillance on conventional surgical wards is often limited. Continuous monitoring depends on the availability of telemetry beds, while routine detection typically relies on intermittent nurse pulse checks and confirmation by 12-lead ECG.5 This pragmatic approach likely leads to underdiagnosis of transient AF episodes.

In recent years, wearable devices based on photoplethysmography (PPG) technology—particularly single-lead ECG smartwatches—have emerged as accessible tools for AF screening and monitoring.5 These devices use PPG-derived pulse irregularity algorithms to trigger alerts and can confirm AF through on-demand 1-lead ECG recordings interpreted by a physician. European guidelines acknowledge the role of wearable technologies in detecting AF.5

Several studies have demonstrated higher POAF detection rates with extended home monitoring compared with conventional 24-hour Holter ECG.6 However, diagnostic performance varies according to device-specific algorithms and validation methodologies. Although direct comparisons between smartwatch brands are complicated7 by differences in hardware, proprietary algorithms and software updates, contemporary devices generally demonstrate high and broadly comparable accuracy for AF detection in validation studies.8

Despite the rapid expansion of wearable technologies, evidence supporting the diagnostic efficacy and real-world integration of PPG-based monitoring in conventional cardiac surgery wards remains limited. It is therefore uncertain whether systematic smartwatch-based surveillance improves the timely and accurate detection of POAF during the early postoperative period, when the vast majority of POAF episodes occur and patients remain under in-hospital care, compared with standard intermittent monitoring.

The FAWATCH trial was designed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of systematic monitoring with single-lead ECG smartwatches in conventional wards for detecting POAF after cardiac surgery.

Randomisation

Participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio, in a parallel-group design, to either standard care (control group) or smartwatch-based monitoring (experimental group) (figure 1).

Study design and follow-up timeline. Schematic representation of the FAWATCH trial design. Patients undergoing on-pump cardiac surgery are screened and randomised at ICU discharge to either standard care monitoring or single-lead ECG monitoring via a smartwatch for 5 days in the surgical ward. After hospital discharge, patients are followed at 30 days, 3 months and 6 months. ICU, intensive care unit; POAF, postoperative atrial fibrillation.

Randomisation will be stratified by age >65 years, diabetes mellitus and type of cardiac surgery (valvular, coronary or combined procedures), as these variables are well-established risk factors for POAF and are associated with both its incidence and temporal pattern.

The randomisation process will be performed by a data manager using Ennov Clinical software, with participants allocated to either the ‘Standard Care’ group or the ‘Intervention’ group.

Standard care in both groups

All patients are transferred from the cardiac intensive care unit to the conventional cardiac surgery ward in accordance with the institutional postoperative pathway. Following randomisation at intensive care unit (ICU) discharge (J0), both groups receive standard postoperative care for the first 5 days, corresponding to the study intervention period.

In the conventional ward, nursing assessments are performed every 4 hours, including evaluation of heart rate and rhythm. Patients undergo two clinical examinations per day by the resident or attending physician. Whenever symptoms suggestive of AF—such as palpitations, dyspnoea or haemodynamic instability—occur, or pulse irregularity or tachycardia is detected during routine monitoring, a 12-lead ECG is performed immediately. Scheduled study visits occur on day 1 (V1), day 3 (V2) and day 5 (V3), or at hospital discharge if earlier. After the initial 5-day monitoring period, all patients are followed for clinical outcomes at 30 days, 3 months and 6 months. The total participation duration for each patient is 6 months.

All ECG recordings (1-lead or 12-lead) are independently analysed by two blinded cardiologists, with adjudication by a third cardiologist in cases of diagnostic disagreement.

No study-specific therapeutic algorithm is mandated. Smartwatch-detected events require ECG confirmation, and subsequent management of documented POAF follows current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines5 and local institutional protocols at the discretion of the treating physicians.

Control group

Patients randomised to the control group receive conventional rhythm monitoring only. In this group, POAF is diagnosed exclusively on the basis of a 12-lead ECG performed on clinical suspicion or detection of pulse irregularity during routine care. No continuous rhythm monitoring or wearable device is used.

Intervention group

Patients randomised to the intervention group receive a single-lead smartwatch (ScanWatch 42 mm, Withings Move ECG, Withings, France) at inclusion. The ScanWatch is a conformity European (CE)-marked Class IIa medical device (CE 1282) approved for the detection of AF. The device is installed by the investigator, and patients are provided with standardised instructions to wear it continuously, day and night, throughout the 5-day monitoring period. The smartwatch enables continuous rhythm monitoring using PPG and allows patients to perform 30 s single-lead ECG recordings on demand.

In the event of clinical symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia or on iterative, on-demand notification of a rhythm abnormality displayed by the smartwatch, patients are instructed to perform a 30 s single-lead ECG recording and immediately inform the nursing staff.

After acquisition of the single-lead ECG, an automated classification is displayed on the smartwatch screen: normal rhythm, AF, high heart rate or inconclusive (figure 2).

Examples of smartwatch single-lead ECG recordings. Representative 30 s single-lead ECG tracings obtained using the ScanWatch device. (A) Normal sinus rhythm. (B) Sinus tachycardia. (C) Atrial fibrillation. (D) Non-conclusive tracing.

Smartwatch data are systematically transferred during scheduled study visits via Bluetooth to the Health Mate application using a dedicated tablet. Historical data are subsequently stored in a secure study database, allowing longitudinal data retrieval and analysis throughout the follow-up period. The device is removed at day 5 (V3) or earlier on hospital discharge.

Only episodes of POAF documented by a single-lead ECG or confirmed by a standard 12-lead ECG are retained for analysis.

All single-lead ECG recordings, as well as smartwatch algorithm outputs (classified as normal, AF, inconclusive or high heart rate), are independently reviewed by two cardiologists. In cases of diagnostic disagreement, adjudication is performed by a third cardiologist.

Primary endpoint

POAF is defined in accordance with the 2020 ESC guidelines as an episode of AF lasting more than 30 s.5 In the smartwatch group, POAF is defined as AF documented either by a single-lead ECG recorded using the ScanWatch and subsequently confirmed by a standard 12-lead ECG, or directly by a standard 12-lead ECG performed according to routine clinical practice.

In the standard care group, POAF is diagnosed exclusively on the basis of a 12-lead ECG obtained in accordance with routine clinical practice.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary outcomes are the proportion of asymptomatic POAF in two arms during the first 5 days following ICU discharge. Asymptomatic POAF is defined as AF documented by a single-lead or 12-lead ECG in the absence of related clinical symptoms. Symptoms are assessed using the European Heart Rhythm Association score.5

The association between POAF and left atrial reservoir strain (LASr) is also evaluated. LASr is measured by transthoracic echocardiography9 at the time of transfer from the cardiothoracic intensive care unit to the conventional ward (day 0), expressed as a percentage (%) and analysed for its association with subsequent POAF occurrence (comparison between patients with and without POAF).

The feasibility of rhythm monitoring using the smartwatch is assessed over the 5-day monitoring period and quantified by total device wear time.

Cardiovascular prognosis is evaluated using a composite endpoint of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) at 30 days, 3 and 6 months after inclusion. MACE is defined according to European Society of Anaesthesiology standards10 and is considered present if at least one of the following occurs: stroke, myocardial infarction, mesenteric ischaemia, unplanned hospitalisation for acute right and/or left heart failure decompensation, resuscitated cardiac arrest or cardiovascular death. Results are expressed as the percentage of patients experiencing at least one MACE event.

Finally, the proportion of patients presenting recurrence of POAF after inclusion is assessed in both study groups at 30 days, 3 and 6 months.

Data collection and outcome definition

At baseline, comprehensive demographic, clinical, surgical and biological data are collected. These include age, sex, body mass index, cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities (coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, prior stroke, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension and chronic kidney disease), as well as chronic medications, including long-term diuretic therapy. Surgical variables comprise the type of procedure (valve surgery, coronary artery bypass grafting or combined procedures), preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction, baseline haemoglobin level, cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp durations, intraoperative transfusion and cumulative vasopressor dose administered during surgery.

The European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II is calculated and recorded systematically. Components required to calculate the Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age ≥75 years [2 points], Diabetes mellitus, Stroke/transient ischemic attack/thromboembolism [2 points], Vascular disease, Age 65–74 years, Sex category [female] (CHA₂DS₂-VASc) and Hypertension, Abnormal renal and/or liver function, Stroke, Bleeding history or predisposition, Labile international normalized ratio, Elderly [>65 years], Drugs and/or alcohol (HAS-BLED) scores are documented, and both scores are computed for each patient.

Following randomisation and throughout the study period, all clinical, haemodynamic, pharmacological and laboratory data are prospectively collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

The primary endpoint is assessed 5 days after inclusion or at hospital discharge, whichever occurs first. All 12-lead ECGs performed during hospitalisation are scanned and integrated into the electronic patient record. Single-lead ECG tracings from the smartwatch are extracted from the Health Mate application.

All ECG recordings are anonymised and uploaded to the Ennov electronic platform, where they are archived in the patient’s electronic case report forms (eCRFs).

At 30 days, 3 months and 6 months after cardiac surgery, follow-up data will be collected. POAF recurrence and its management will be assessed, including current rhythm status (AF present or absent) and any therapeutic interventions such as electrical cardioversion, catheter ablation or pharmacological treatment.

If the patient is hospitalised, AF recurrence will be documented by a standard 12-lead ECG. For non-hospitalised patients, follow-up data will be obtained through structured telephone interviews and medical record review, including documentation of cardiology consultations, cardioversion procedures and other relevant clinical visits.

MACE is defined as the occurrence of at least one of the following events: stroke, myocardial infarction, mesenteric ischaemia, unplanned hospitalisation for acute right and/or left heart failure decompensation, resuscitated cardiac arrest or cardiovascular death.

Standardised definitions of postoperative outcomes established by the European Society of Anaesthesiology are applied.10 Stroke is defined as an embolic, thrombotic or haemorrhagic cerebral event associated with persistent motor, sensory or cognitive dysfunction (eg, hemiplegia, hemiparesis, aphasia, sensory deficit or memory impairment) and confirmed by neuroimaging. Myocardial infarction is diagnosed based on a compatible clinical presentation, dynamic electrocardiographic changes suggestive of infarction and a rise and/or fall in cardiac troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile of the upper reference limit. Mesenteric ischaemia is confirmed by imaging or exploratory laparotomy. Acute heart failure decompensation is defined as new or worsening signs and symptoms of heart failure requiring intravenous diuretic therapy, vasoactive support or unplanned hospitalisation and confirmed by clinical assessment with supportive imaging or biomarker evidence when available. Cardiac arrest is defined as the cessation of cardiac mechanical activity confirmed by the absence of signs of circulation. All adverse clinical events are prospectively documented and recorded in eCRFs.

Statistical methods and sample size calculation

For the primary endpoint analysis, the incidence of POAF will be compared between the two study arms using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided alpha level of 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

For the secondary endpoint analyses, the proportion of asymptomatic POAF in the interventional arm will be estimated with its corresponding 95% CI. Among patients in the interventional arm diagnosed with asymptomatic POAF, the proportion experiencing MACE within 6 months will also be calculated with 95% CI.

The association between baseline LASr at day 0 and POAF at day 5 will be assessed using univariable logistic regression, with results expressed as ORs and 95% CIs. The discriminative performance of LASr parameters will be evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, with corresponding 95% CIs. In the interventional arm, smartwatch wear time will be described as mean±SD or median (range), depending on data distribution.

All data are prospectively collected through the hospital electronic medical record system by nursing staff and investigators.

For echocardiographic secondary analyses, patients with missing baseline echocardiographic measurements (eg, left atrial reservoir strain) will be excluded from the specific analyses requiring these variables, as they are essential for model construction. The number of excluded patients and the reasons for exclusion will be clearly reported.

All tests will be two-sided, and p values<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. No interim analysis is planned. Statistical analyses will be performed using SAS software V.9.4.

Intention-to-treat analysis

Patients requiring continuous monitoring or transfer back to the ICU due to significant clinical adverse events will be analysed according to their initial group allocation (standard or intervention) under the intention-to-treat principle.

Sample size calculation

The initial sample size calculation was based on data from the CARDIOX study, in which the incidence of POAF was approximately 30%, with most diagnoses occurring by postoperative day 2 or 3 during the ICU stay. Under this assumption, 340 evaluable patients (170 per group) were required to detect an absolute difference of 15% with a two-sided alpha risk of 5% and 80% power. Accounting for an anticipated 5% of non-evaluable patients, the total planned sample size was 358 patients (179 per group).

After inclusion and follow-up of 140 patients, a blinded interim assessment of event incidence revealed a substantially lower POAF rate of approximately 15% in both groups.

Based on these observed data, a blinded sample size re-estimation was performed. The control group incidence was revised to 15%, reflecting a more realistic estimate. In this updated calculation, the expected absolute difference between groups was adjusted to 10% rather than 15%, acknowledging that with a baseline incidence of 15%, detecting a 15% absolute difference would be statistically unrealistic.

Under these revised assumptions, the required sample size was 324 patients, or 162 per group. This protocol amendment was submitted and approved, and the total planned enrolment was therefore adjusted accordingly.

Amiens-Picardie University Hospital performs approximately 550 cardiac surgeries requiring cardiopulmonary bypass annually. Based on an estimated 50% eligibility rate, up to 250 patients per year were initially expected to qualify for inclusion. Taking into account practical considerations such as elective surgery scheduling and investigator availability, the projected enrolment period was initially estimated at 24 months.

However, during the study, the actual recruitment rate ranged from 5 to 10 patients per month. This lower-than-anticipated enrolment was mainly attributable to multiple concurrent clinical studies that were not fully anticipated at the time of protocol design, thereby limiting the pool of eligible patients. As a result, a formal request for an extension of the inclusion period was submitted and approved to ensure the planned sample size was achieved.

Data management and monitoring

Registration

Clinical, haemodynamic, pharmacological and laboratory data are continuously recorded during the ICU stay using the electronic patient management system (Diane REA, Bow Medical, France) and subsequently through the hospital electronic medical record system during the surgical ward stay (DxCare, Medasys, France).

All 12-lead and single-lead ECG recordings are anonymised, scanned and uploaded into the patient’s eCRF.

A dedicated local research technician is responsible for data collection and entry into the eCRFs, while a research coordinator supervises data centralisation, quality control and validation procedures.

Record keeping

The University Hospital of Amiens-Picardie is required by French law to retain consent documents and eCRFs for a minimum of 15 years.

Study organisation

The study is coordinated by the Clinical Research and Innovation Directorate of Amiens-Picardie University Hospital (CHU Amiens-Picardie), France.

Study duration and timeline

Protocol development, ethics committee approval, funding acquisition and eCRF implementation were completed in December 2022. Patient enrolment at Amiens-Picardie University Hospital began in January 2023.

A protocol amendment, including modification of the statistical analysis plan and an extension of the inclusion period, was submitted on 4 February 2024, and approved by the ethics committee on 21 February 2024. The corresponding update to the clinical trial registry was validated on 30 January 2026.

The trial is currently ongoing. As of 22 February 2026, 258 patients had been enrolled. The end of study enrolment is planned for October 2027.

The database will be locked after patient enrolment and follow-up are completed. Data analysis will then be performed, followed by manuscript preparation and submission for publication.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Amiens-Picardie University Hospital (IRB ID: PI2021_843_0228) and by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Île-de-France I (CPP ID: 2022-A00497-36) on 31 August 2022.

All participants receive both verbal and written information, and written informed consent is obtained prior to inclusion (patient consent in the online supplemental file). Only patients capable of providing informed consent themselves are eligible; no deferred or proxy consent procedures are implemented.

The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05573633), initially posted on 10 July 2022, and subsequently updated on 4 February 2026, following a sample size modification, to ensure transparency and public access to protocol details and study results.

Discussion

We hypothesised that continuous rhythm monitoring with a smartwatch-based single-lead ECG after cardiac surgery, implemented on a conventional surgical ward, could improve POAF detection during the first 5 days after ICU discharge. In addition to enhancing diagnostic yield, we aimed to evaluate patient adherence to smartwatch use, the feasibility of patient-initiated single-lead ECG recordings and the device’s real-world diagnostic performance (sensitivity and specificity) in a postoperative hospital setting. The technical constraints inherent to PPG-based monitoring that may influence these outcomes are summarised in online supplemental table 1.

Several limitations of our research protocol should be acknowledged. First, the study used a smartwatch whose diagnostic performance has been previously reported and may differ from that of other wearable technologies.11 However, it is a CE-certified medical device with extended battery life, a critical factor for feasibility during continuous in-hospital monitoring. Furthermore, the AF detection algorithm requires a sustained AF burden (approximately ≥30 min) before generating an alert, which reduces false-positive notifications but may also limit detection of short, self-terminating episodes.12 In addition, our investigation was restricted to the in-hospital phase. Post-discharge remote monitoring would require a dedicated workflow and structured digital infrastructure, including a secure connectivity portal, which was not available at the time of study design.13 These limitations may influence overall diagnostic performance and potentially lead to an underestimation of the true POAF incidence after cardiac surgery.

Despite these constraints, the study has important strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first randomised controlled trial evaluating the use of a smartwatch for in-hospital detection of POAF in a conventional surgical ward. Assessing the feasibility and integration of a simple wearable device into routine postoperative care is a necessary step before evaluating its impact on therapeutic decision-making and clinical outcomes. To date, no randomised data have assessed the clinical implications of POAF detection by a smartwatch during the early postoperative in-hospital period.

This trial therefore represents a foundational step in evaluating the efficacy, safety and potential clinical value of wearable-based rhythm monitoring in cardiac surgery patients.

The results may inform the design of future large-scale, multicentre trials to define the role of digital rhythm-monitoring strategies in perioperative cardiovascular care.
