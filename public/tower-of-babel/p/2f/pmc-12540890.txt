Impact of health education on knowledge and perception of cervical cancer and screening among rural women in Bangladesh
Authors: Morsheda Alam Mili, Md Jamil Hossain, Mahmuda Akter Poli, Sree Bepul Kumar, Bishwjit Bhowmick, Lakshmi Rani Kundu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women worldwide, causing nearly half of all diagnosed cases to result in death. Most of the cases, women are dying due to ignorance and lack of adequate knowledge, which is completely unacceptable. This study aimed to assess the effects of health educational intervention on knowledge and perception of cervical cancer and its screening among women in Tangail District of Bangladesh. This was a quasi-experimental (pre-post) study conducted in Tangail District, Bangladesh. Baseline data were collected using a pre-validated questionnaire from 400 rural women, followed by a health education intervention. After one month, a post-intervention survey using the same questionnaire was conducted to assess changes in knowledge and perception. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square test, McNemar’s test, and Friedman test to evaluate pre-post differences and associations with socio-demographic variables. All statistical tests had been conducted using Microsoft Excel (Version 2024) and SPSS (version 27). Out of 400 participants, the majority (44.8%) was aged between 25 and 34 years and most (96.8%) of the women were housewives. A significant improvement was observed in knowledge about cervical cancer and its screening after the health education intervention. Before the intervention, 69.5% of the participants were aware of cervical cancer but after the intervention it increased to 100% (p < 0.001). Additionally, the awareness about cervical cancer screening rises from 20.3% to 100% (p < 0.001). Moreover, before the intervention, only 14.8% of participants were aware of the HPV vaccine, while this figure increased to 98.3% (p < 0.001). Furthermore, after the intervention the percentage of participants receiving cervical cancer screenings increased from 4.3% to 9.8% (p = 0.004). The study observed that health education intervention programs significantly increased women knowledge and perception regarding cervical cancer and its screening. Cervical cancer mortality rate can be highly reduced if it can be detected early and take proper measures. Introduction

Cervical cancer is a significant global health concern and the most common cancer among women. In 2022, there were approximately 660,000 new cases of cervical cancer diagnosed, resulting in 350,000 deaths per year1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report, without further interventions, it is projected that the annual number of new cases will reach 700,000, and the number of deaths will increase up to 400,000 by 20302. The high distribution of cervical cancer is particularly notable in low- and middle-income countries, such as Bangladesh, due to low levels of knowledge and the absence of screening and prevention programs1. Around 50 million women in Bangladesh are at risk of developing cervical cancer3.

Several risk factors increase the likelihood of developing cervical cancer. Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection is consider the most prominent factor, which is responsible for 70% of cervical cancer cases and is primarily transmitted through sexual contact4,5. Other associated risk factors include early marriage, engaging in sexual activity with multiple partners, long-term use of oral contraceptive pills, frequent childbirth, certain medications, and specific dietary factors6,7. HIV infected women are six times more likely to develop cervical cancer compared to the general population, and it has been estimated that around 5% of all cervical cancer cases are due to HIV8. Furthermore, cervical cancer disproportionately affects younger women, with 20% of children experiencing the loss of their mothers due to this disease9.

Cervical cancer is a major cause of death in developing countries, accounting for about 87% of deaths in this LMIC counties10. In South Asia, more than 1.3 million people are diagnosed with cervical cancer every year, which contributes to one-fourth of the total incidence in the region11. In India, nearly 100,000 cases of cervical cancer are diagnosed annually, with a death rate of 21.5%12,13. A study on Pakistan highlightedthat over 34,066 women were affected by cervical cancer, and the death rate was 26.76%14,15. However, Bangladesh does not have a comprehensive national central cancer registry that can provide nationwide data16. As a result, the actual prevalence and death rate from cervical cancer in the country remain unknown. A study indicated that cervical cancer constitutes 12% of all female cancer cases, making it the second most common cancer in Bangladesh17. In 2018, there were 5,214 deaths (7.1 per 100,000 women) and 8,068 new cases (10.6 per 100,000 women) of cervical cancer reported17. Without timely intervention, it was projected that by 2070, over half a million women in Bangladesh would die from cervical cancer, and by 2120, this figure would increase to 1 million18. The higher mortality rate from cervical cancer in Bangladesh is associated with several factors, including insufficient knowledge about signs and symptoms, risk factors, screening campaigns, and preventative actions related to cervical cancer19. A previous study highlighted that only 10% of respondents have a comprehensive understanding of the causes and preventive actions for cervical cancer20.

Early detection and effective treatment can lead to a complete cure for cervical cancer. It is essential to recognize the signs and symptoms and consult a medical professional to address any concerns1. It has been verified screening programs, such as Pap smears, HPV DNA tests, and VIA tests, have significantly reduced the incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer in developed countries7. However, in Bangladesh, only 7.5% of women aged 30–49 have undergone VIA screening, indicating a low coverage rate21,22. A previous study shown that the majority of Bangladeshi women did not get screened due to a lack of knowledge and insufficient resources19.

According to the 2022 census report, Bangladesh has a high population density, with a total population of over 165 million people. Out of this total population, about 83 million are female, with the majority residing in rural areas23. To achieve sustainable development goals (SDGs), the Government of Bangladesh (GOB) should prioritize noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), particularly cervical cancer treatment. Supporting healthy behaviors and educational initiatives that promote healthier attitudes can significantly enhance people’s well-being. Various educational programs, such as web-based applications, face-to-face oral counseling, films, and printed materials, can effectively improve health knowledge23–25. Raising awareness about cervical cancer and its screening among women is critical for reducing the disease’s high mortality rates. Despite numerous challenges, prioritizing education and screening is essential for enhancing detection and treatment.

In our literature search we found one intervention study on cervical cancer but that was limited on university students26. However, most of the people in Bangladesh lived in village and they had higher risk of cervical cancer as per their limited knowledge and education qualification. So, Therefore, this study assessed the impact of health education intervention on rural women’s knowledge, perception, and screening practices related to cervical cancer in the Tangail District of Bangladesh. The findings aim to inform targeted health education programs and policy actions to reduce cervical cancer morbidity and mortality in rural communities.

Methods

Study design and participants

A community based before-after designed intervention study was conducted among rural women aged 25–64 years in six villages in the Tangail District, Bangladesh. Multistage sampling techniques were adopted to select 400 participants. Tangail district consists of 12 subdistricts. Three Upazilas from Twelve Upazilas were randomly selected. At the second stage 2 villages from each Upazila were also selected randomly. Finally, a total of 400 participants were selected from six villages (67 each) using convenience sampling technique due to lack of sampling frame. To identify eligible participants, our field team visited the selected villages and approached women aged 25–64 years. The study design, purpose, and procedures were explained, and only women who provided informed consent were enrolled. Participants’ contact details and addresses were collected to facilitate follow-up during the intervention stage. Privacy and confidentiality were strictly maintained throughout the study.

This study was carried out from 20 August 2024 to 30 November 2024. In our national strategies for cervical cancer screening, women aged 30–60 years are recommended for regular screenings27. However, in our study, we expanded the age range to include women aged 25–64. This broader group allows for a more comprehensive understanding of awareness and readiness for screening, particularly considering early exposure to risk factors and the evaluation of policy implications.

Procedures

This study focuses on 400 rural women aged 25–64 years, who are willing to participate and live in Tangail district. The study excludes male members, women under 25 and above 64, and those in urban areas. The study was conducted into three stages: (i) First stage (pre-interventional stage), (ii) Second stage (intervention stage) and (iii) Third stage (post-interventional stage).

First stage (Pre-intervention stage)

The following information was gathered from the respondents using a pre-designed structured questionnaire: the respondent’s knowledge, sociodemographic information and perception regarding cervical cancer and screening. For the post-test session, each participate was given a specific ID number that could be used to identify them. All the intervention documents used in this study were validated by a group of experts from the Department of Public Health and Informatics at Jahangirnagar University.

Second stage (Intervention stage)

During the intervention session, materials (such as video, power point presentation and discussions) were used. All the meetings took place in the respondents’ specific village, which were convenient for the participants. In each selected village, approximately five sessions were conducted. Participants were assigned to groups of 10 to 15 individuals each to ensure that the educational intervention was given and comprehended by everyone. This educational intervention lasted for around 45–60 min. It included three sessions, each with a specified duration. First 15 min for video session, second 20 min for power point presentation and last 20 min for discussions. Sessions for the pre-test and the intervention did not hold on to the same day. Every session, one or two respondents from intervention group were invited to show and in order to ensure that the respondents could comprehend the instructional materials, they were asked to describe what they had learned. The participants who agreed to take on this activity were also chosen at random.

Health education intervention

The primary goal of health education programs is to improve health status. However, measuring changes in intermediate outcomes or impact provides a more direct way to assess effectiveness. The link to health status is often considered to be conceptual or theoretical, as health education interventions work through intermediate results. The term “health education intervention strategy” helps in conceptualization using heuristics and planning a wide range of activities. The literature contains a wide variety of studies and reports that either examine specific intervention techniques or document broader health promotion initiatives that incorporate numerous techniques28.

Video

Participants were shown a cervical cancer-related video as part of a health education intervention. The video was obtained from the BBC News Bangla YouTube channel (30). It covered information on the prevalence of cervical cancer, its risk factors, symptoms, prevention methods, treatment options, and screening procedures. The entire video was in Bangla to ensure easy understanding.

Power point presentation

For health educational intervention, cervical cancer related power point presentation was made in Bangla language. The topics covered in this power point presentation included the prevalence of cervical cancer, its risk factors, symptoms, and methods of prevention and treatment, and screening process, availability of screening centre of cervical cancer.

Third stage (post-intervention stage)

A post-test survey was conducted among 400 participants one month after the intervention using the same questionnaire. To streamline the post-intervention data collection process, each participant was assigned an ID number during the pre-intervention stage to enable identification of the same participants. The participants visited the nearest Upazila Health Complex for a VIA test. A blue card was provided for negative test results.

Data collection tools and procedure

After conducting a comprehensive review of existing literature, we developed a structured questionnaire for the study29,30. The questionnaire was primarily prepared in English and then translated into the native language Bangla. The data was collected through in-person interviews, which took an average of 15–20 min per participant. The questionnaire comprised four sections- Section A: Sociodemographic Characteristics, Section B: Knowledge of cervical cancer and its screening, Section C: Perception of cervical cancer and its screening and Section D: Practices and utilization of cervical cancer screening.

Measures

Socio-demographic characteristics

Participants’ socio-demographic information, such as age, marital status (married, unmarried/single, divorced, or widowed), level of education, occupation (housewife, student, or employed), religion, parity, total family income, and family history of cervical cancer (yes or no), were gathered using closed-ended questions.

Knowledge of cervical cancer and its screening

The knowledge evaluation section of the questionnaire consisted of eight items with four different response options. These items aimed to assess participants’ familiarity with the risk factors, symptoms, screening tests, and preventive measures for cervical cancer. To gauge their knowledge of cervical cancer and its screening, participants were asked whether they had ever heard about cervical cancer and if they were familiar with cervical cancer screening. Additionally, they were asked where they had first learned about cervical cancer and cervical cancer screening.

Perception and attitude to cervical cancer and screening

Eight questions were employed to gauge the participants’ attitudes about cervical cancer and screening. One of those perception questions had a multiple-choice structure with response possibilities.

Practices or utilization of cervical cancer screening

The screening for cervical cancer was assessed by asking a single question and obtaining either a yes or no response. The reasons of taking cervical cancer screening and the reasons of not taking cervical cancer screening was evaluated with multiple choice structure.

Statistical analysis

First, import the dataset into Microsoft Excel, clean any duplicate or erroneous values, and prepare it for final analysis. Descriptive statistics (percentages and frequencies) used for categorical variables; and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. The study used the Mc-Nemar test and Friedman test to examine differences between the pre-intervention and post-intervention stages. Prior to administering the Mc-Nemar tests, every question was converted into a binary response (right or wrong answer). The p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistical significance for all statistical tests. All analysis was conducted using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) version 27 and Microsoft Excel (version 2024).

Ethical considerations

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Institutional Research Ethics. Formal ethical approval was obtained from the Biosafety, Biosecurity, and Ethical Review Board of Jahangirnagar University, Savar, Dhaka-1342, Bangladesh (Ref. No: BBEC, JU/M 2024/08 (122)). All participants were initially provided with information about the study’s goals and their rights to participate or not in the study. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. They were assured that their identity and contact information would not be disclosed without their consent. All information has been kept private to protect respondents’ privacy for ethical reasons. Finally, there is no conflict of interest because this investigation was conducted impartially.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

Among the 400 respondents, 44.8% were aged 25–34 years, and 94.8% were married. The majority (96.8%) of the women were housewives, and a significant portion (32.5%) had no formal education. Additionally, most of the women practiced Islam (97.0%), with a small percentage identifying as Hindu (3.0%). Regarding parity, 67.8% had 0–2 children and 30.5% had 3–5 children. Over half (56.3%) of the women’s family income was less than 20,000 BDT, and 93.3% had no family history of cervical cancer (Table 1).

Knowledge and awareness regarding cervical cancer and its screening before and after intervention.

The study highlighted a significant improvement in participants’ knowledge levels about risk factors and symptoms after an educational intervention (p < 0.001). Before the intervention, awareness of early marriage (27%), STDs (25.8%), HPV (7.2%), multiple partners (22%), prolonged contraceptive use (7.5%), smoking (0.8%), and poor hygiene (44.8%) was low. Post-intervention, recognition of these factors increased to 98.8%, 97.8%, 63.2%, 94.3%, 88.5%, 37.3%, and 99.3%, respectively. Additionally, participant’s knowledge were significantly improved insymptom recognition, with knowledge of weight loss rising from 2.5% to 70.8%, and awareness of intermenstrual bleeding and increased vaginal bleeding/discharge reaching 98.5%.

Significant improvements were observed in knowledge of screening methods and HPV vaccine (p < 0.001). Awareness of VIA increased from 16.3% to 99.5%, and Pap smear knowledge rose from 1.8% to 74.3%, while misconceptions regarding blood tests (1.8% to 0.0%) and ultrasonography (4.5% to 0.0%) declined. In addition, awareness about the HPV vaccine increasing from 14.8% to 98.3%, avoidance of multiple sexual partners from 18.8% to 79.8%, and consistent condom use from 49.3% to 88.9% (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of health educational intervention on raising knowledge and perception of cervical cancer and cervical cancer screening among rural women in Bangladesh. In our literature search, this is the first study exclusively conducted among rural women focuses on health education interventions to enhance awareness and knowledge of cervical cancer and its screening in Bangladesh.

The study showed that before the intervention, 69.5% of rural women were aware of cervical cancer. After the intervention, 100% of them had heard about it. This result is consistent with a study in Ghana found that 84.2% of participants knew about cervical cancer before the intervention, and all rural women had heard about it after the intervention24. These findings highlight the effectiveness of health education interventions in raising awareness which has been also noticed many other countries include Jamaica, Egypt and Nigeria29,31,32. In three previous studies conducted in Bangladesh, it was found that 65%, 71.8%, and 87% of rural Bangladeshi women had heard about cervical cancer without any intervention stage21,30,33. Health education interventions work effectively because they provide structured, culturally sensitive, and context-specific information through direct engagement, which helps correct misconceptions, improves health literacy, and motivates women to prioritize preventive health behaviors34–36.

Our findings highlighted a significant knowledge improvement regarding the risk factors and symptoms of cervical cancer following the health education intervention. Similar improvements were observed in another study that utilized only a video-based educational intervention to address cervical cancer risk factors and symptoms24. Notably, identification of marriage as a risk factor increased from 27% pre-intervention to 98.8% post-intervention. Participants also identified other major risk factors such as STDs, HPV, multiple sexual partners, prolonged use of contraceptive pills, smoking, and poor hygiene. These results are consistent with earlier Bangladeshi studies, where early marriage, STDs, and poor genital hygiene were the most frequently reported risk factors21,31. In contrast, studies from Ghana highlighted HPV infection and prolonged oral contraceptive use as primary risk factors24. This may be due to cultural differences between the two countries regarding marriage and sexual life. Correctly identifying the risk factors and symptoms is crucial for preventing the development of cervical cancer. The significant improvement in knowledge in our study underscores the effectiveness of culturally tailored health education programs to spread over Bangladesh.

Our study revealed significant improvements in women’s awareness of cervical cancer symptoms after the health education intervention. Before the intervention, knowledge of key symptoms such as weight loss (2.5%), foul-smelling vaginal discharge (11.3%), bleeding between periods (24.5%), abdominal discomfort after sexual intercourse (1.3%), and postmenopausal bleeding (21.8%) was low. After the intervention, awareness rose sharply, with up to 99.5% of participants correctly identifying certain symptoms. These findings align with evidence from Ghana24 and previous studies in Bangladesh21,31, which similarly reported low level of knowledge but noticed significant improvement after targeted education. Such changes underscore the effectiveness of structured, context-specific interventions in correcting misconceptions and enhancing symptom recognition. The notable increase in knowledge may be due to the fact that health education interventions provide simple, culturally relevant information through direct communication, which makes messages easier to understand and sustain among the participants36.

Our study revealed low status of knowledge about cervical cancer prevention among rural women, with only 14.8% recognizing the HPV vaccine, 18.8% identifying avoidance of multiple partners, and 11% reported condom use as preventive measures. However, in post intervention stage, there has been a significant increase in their knowledge level. A Bangladeshi study similarly highlighted the importance of HPV vaccines, avoiding multiple partners, and using condoms to prevent cervical cancer21. Maintaining a single sexual relationship and proper cleanliness were the best preventive measures. Shockingly, over three in ten rural women used prescription drugs to prevent cervical cancer37. In contrast, our studies baseline levels of knowledge of the women are lower than those reported in some earlier Bangladeshi studies, which found 57% and 52% recognition for avoiding multiple partners and condom use respectively30, and echo the need to address rural–urban and education-related disparities. Education plays as a key factor in improving awareness and understanding of cervical cancer. These findings underscore the need for further interventions to improve cervical cancer prevention. Thus, at the policy level, expanding national HPV vaccination programs to rural areas is essential, alongside awareness campaigns that promote preventive behaviors.

The study highlighted that in the pre-intervention stage, 40.3% and 22.8% of women received information about cervical cancer from relatives and healthcare providers, while in the post-intervention stage, eligible sources included family/friends (61%), doctors/healthcare providers (32.5%), and media (TV, social media). Previous studies focused on the pre-interventional stage, and majority gained knowledge about cervical cancer from books, family, neighbors, friends, healthcare professionals, and mass media like television21,30,34,35. These differences occurred because the majority of our participants lived in rural areas, and they had lower levels of education and financial stability compared to other study participants. Education and financial stability may greatly influence their level of knowledge. As a consequences, the rural women rely heavily on interpersonal networks, such as family, friends, and community members, due to barriers in accessing formal health information and limited trust or engagement with healthcare services38.

The study demonstrated that 68% of rural women accepted cervical cancer screening in the pre-interventional stage, while 70% in post-intervention. A previous study reported that despite 75% of women willingly having a screening, a significant majority did not believe the condition was as dangerous or at risk of contracting it36. Despite relatively high stated acceptance, actual screening status remained low. VIA was the most commonly practiced test, increasing from about 4% to 9.8%, whereas studies from Nigeria reported higher use of Pap smears (9.5–15.9%), reflecting differences in available services and cost36. The study showed that health education significantly improved rural women’s knowledge and perceptions of cervical cancer and its screening, consistent with evidence that peer health education can positively influence perceptions of seriousness, personal risk, and screening benefits, thereby enhancing awareness and utilization36,39.

The study found that rural women had a pre-test screening rate of 4.3%, with 9.8% of respondents having done so. This rate is lower than reports from Nigeria (10.5–17.3%)36, but higher than a previous Bangladeshi study (2.3%)30. Before the intervention, 81.8% of women were unaware of screening tests, and avoidance was linked to misconceptions such as believing they were not at risk, fear of results, absence of symptoms, and perceived cost. After the intervention, women learned that the government provides free VIA tests, which helped improve acceptance. These findings align with Nigerian studies highlighting limited awareness, fear, inadequate facilities, and socio-cultural barriers as key obstacles38,40. Our study found that women’s age, education, employment, and monthly family income influenced post-screening practice. An Nigerian study found that marital status, education, employment status, and parity strongly linked with post-screening practice36. Married women have higher screening rates than single people, possibly due to their spouses’ support36,41,42.

In our study, we observed significant changes in knowledge about cervical cancer screening methods among rural women. Specifically, knowledge of the VIA test increased from 16.3% to 99.5%, and knowledge of the Pap Smear test increased from 1.8% to 74.3% after the intervention. This may be due to the consultation rooms are often not private, which may lead to shyness among women and result in the underutilization of the VIA test, ultimately affecting the likelihood of women undergoing screening21. The overall findings are important for informing future research and are relevant to our current study and subsequent investigations.

Strengths and limitations

So far, this is the first study that explores the effectiveness of a health education intervention on the knowledge and perceptions of cervical cancer and cervical cancer screening among rural women. However, like all other studies, this one has some limitations. Firstly, it was a pre-post quasi-experimental study conducted solely among rural women, which means that urban women were not included in the research. Additionally, since the study was conducted in the Tangail district, the results may not be generalizable to the entire population of the country. Furthermore, this study had a small sample size and used a convenient sampling technique for data collection, which is prone to sampling bias. In addition, the absence of an external control group may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should involve a control group as well as larger population from diverse areas and populations, utilizing random sampling methods.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrated the effectiveness of a community-based health education campaign in improving rural women’s knowledge and perception towards cervical cancer and its screening. Prior to the intervention, participants had limited awareness of cervical cancer risk factors, symptoms, and prevention. However, post-intervention there was a significant improvement in knowledge, greater awareness of cervical cancer and the HPV vaccine, and increased uptake of cervical cancer screening. Cervical cancer information and prevention should be integrated into community health services, with routine village-level education programs to raise awareness and encourage screening. Policy initiatives must also prioritize cervical cancer awareness and screening to help reduce mortality in Bangladesh.
