La (NO3)3 substantially fortified Glycyrrhiza uralensis’s resilience against salt stress by interconnected pathways
Authors: Tingting Jia, Junjun Gu, Miao Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The taproot of Glycyrrhiza uralensis is globally appreciated for its medicinal and commercial value and is one of the most popular medicinal plants. With the decline of wild G. uralensis resources, cultivated G. uralensis has become a key method to ensure supply. However, soil salinization poses challenges to G. uralensis cultivation and affects the yield and quality of it. In this study, the inhibitory effects of NaCl and Na2SO4 on yield and quality of G. uralensis were comprehensively evaluated in a three-year large-scale pot experiment, and the alleviating effects of supplementation with lanthanum nitrate (La (NO3)3) on G. uralensis were further evaluated under salt stress. The findings indicate that La (NO3)3 significantly strengthened the plant's salt tolerance by enhancing photosynthetic capacity, osmolyte accumulation, antioxidant defenses, and cellular balance of ions, which led to a substantial increase in root biomass and accumulation of major medicinal components. In comparison to the NaCl-stress treatment, the 0.75 M La (NO3)3 + NaCl treatment resulted in a 20% and 34% increase in taproot length and biomass, respectively, alongside a 52% and 43% rise in glycyrrhizic acid and glycyrrhizin content, respectively. Similar improvements were observed with 0.75 M La (NO3)3 + Na2SO4 treatment, which increased root length and biomass by 14% and 26%, respectively, and glycyrrhizic acid and glycyrrhizin content by 40% and 38%, respectively. The combined showed that application of La (NO3)3 not only significantly improved the salt resilience of G. uralensis, but also had a more pronounced alleviation of growth inhibition induced by NaCl compared to Na2SO4 stress except in the gas exchange parameters and root growth. This study provides a scientific basis for high-yield and high-quality cultivation of G. uralensis in saline soils and a new approach for other medicinal plants to improve their salt tolerance. Introduction

Salt stress is one of the abiotic factors that seriously threaten crop yields globally [1–3], nd areas affected by salinization are found in almost all countries and regions, affecting about 8.31–11.73 million square kilometers of cultivable land resources worldwide [4, 5]. Exploring suitable salt tolerant and highly economical plants for cultivation in saline soils is an effective and rapid utilization strategy for the exploitation of salinized land resources [6–8].

Glycyrrhiza uralensis (Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch.), a plant species native to semi-arid and arid climes [9], exemplifies resilience in the face of environmental stresses, including salt [10]. This species has been recognized for its salt tolerance, and is particularly valuable in the arid regions of world [11]. Beyond its adaptability, G. uralensis holds a prominent position in the global medicinal plants due to its applications in nutraceuticals, cosmeceuticals, agri-foods and pharmaceuticals [12–14]. However, the unsustainable exploitation of wild G. uralensis resources has led to a critical diminution in natural populations [14, 15], there is a necessity to extend the cultivation of G. uralensis as an alternative to wild licorice.

While cultivation efforts is underway, the early growth stages of G. uralensis exhibit a weak salt tolerance [16], characterized by inhibited in seed germination and seedling vigor in salinized soils [17]. Hence, there is an urgent need for research to improve the salt tolerance of G. uralensis to ensure its survival and productivity in saline environments. Among the various strategies, the application of exogenous substances to enhance salt tolerance in cultivated G. uralensis has become the most effective method to increase its salt tolerance in a short period of time [18–20].

Rare earth elements, celebrated for their essential effects on plant physiology [21–23], have been integral to global agricultural practices since Qian Chongshu's seminal discovery in 1917 [24]. Lanthanum (La), in particular, has been utilized as a growth regulator in Chinese agriculture since the 1970s, owing to its capacity to enhance crop growth [25–28] and confer resistance to adverse environmental conditions [29–34]. The efficacy of the element is depended on the plant species and the concentration applied. Empirical evidence suggests that lanthanum may enhance plant adaptation to various environmental stresses by reducing peroxidation and ionic damage induced by environmental stresses through modulation of growth hormones [35], bolstering antioxidant systems [36], and refining photosynthetic efficiency [37].

Despite these insights, the role of lanthanum in enhancing salt tolerance in crops remains relatively uncharted. This study investigated how La(NO3)3 enhanced salt tolerance in G. uralensis by evaluating its effects on key physiological processes. Our analysis focused on photosynthetic performance, osmotic balance, ionic homeostasis, antioxidant response, and the structural and metabolic changes within the root system when exposed to saline conditions. The main emphasis of our research is to gain a solid understanding of the mechanism of La(NO3)3-induced enhancement of salt tolerance in G. uralensis and to provide a scientific basis for saline cultivation measures of G. uralensis.

Materials and methods

Test materials

The seeds of cultivated G. uralensis were obtained from the Institute of Licorice at Shihezi University (Shihezi, Xinjiang, China). Analytical standards for glycyrrhizic acid (Batch No.: C11654946) and glycyrrhizin (Batch No.: C11602211), as well as reagents including NaCl, Na2SO4, methanol, formic acid, anhydrous ethanol, were sourced from Macklin Biochemical (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Acetonitrile was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (LCMS-grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA), while nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide were purchased from Adamus Reagent (Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Assay kits for SOD, POD, and CAT were purchased from Solarbio Technology (SOD-BC0170, POD-BC0095, CAT-C0200, APX-BC0220, Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Plant growth conditions

The experimental site was located in the experimental field of the North Campus of Shihezi University (geo coordinates: 44°30′80″N, 86°05′66″E). The predominant climate type of Shihezi City is temperate continental, characterized by extended sunshine hours during summer, dry and hot conditions, and severe cold in winter, with an average altitude of 450.8 m. Despite significant temporal variability in rainfall, these weather conditions are considered typical for G. uralensis production in the area. Cultivation substrate collection

The cultivation substrate utilized in this study was designed to mimic the characteristic soil composition of the G. uralensis cultivation region, predominantly consisting of sandy soil (sand: loam mixture ratio of 3:7). The sand was obtained from the southern periphery of the Gurbantunggut desert, while the loam was collected from the experimental site of Shihezi University. The two materials were thoroughly blended and then evenly distributed into 36 plastic vats (diameter: 75 cm, height: 85 cm), each with an average weight of 180 kg. The proportion of sand, silt and clay components in the soil mixture was determined to be 62.2%, 32.5% and 8.8%, respectively, using a test based on the rate of settling of particles of different sizes in water, as specified in Wuddivira’s [38].

Seed pre-treatment

Healthy and uniform G. uralensis seeds were soaked in a 98% sulfuric acid solution for 30 min, followed by thorough rinsing under running water to remove any residual acid. The seeds were then soaked in distilled water at 25 °C for 8 h.

Experimental design

A randomized complete blocks design was implemented to mitigate the effect of environmental heterogeneity. The experimental layout, depicted in Fig. 2, was rearranged weekly to account for light and edge effects.Fig. 2Map of the experimental plot

Map of the experimental plot

The experimental design was a factorial arrangement with two factors: (1) salt, with three types of salt stress treatments based on the salinity components of saline soils in the nearby G. uralensis cultivation area—non-salt treatments, NaCl stress treatments (3 g/kg), and Na2SO4 stress treatments ( 3 g/kg)—to simulate non-salt areas and dominated by chloride and sulfate salts [9]; (2) lanthanum treatments, which were applied at concentrations of 0 mmol/L and 0.75 mmol/L, as referenced in our previous study. Salts were uniformly mixed into the soil, and La (NO3)3 solutions were sprayed onto the NaCl and Na2SO4 treated substrates, with an equal amount of distilled water applied to the control. Six replicates were established for each treatment.

On April 15, 2020, 30 imbibed G. uralensis seeds were sown 1 cm deep in each vat's substrate. The planting density was calibrated based on field data (6.5–7.0 × 105 plants/hm2), resulting in a final density of 26 plants with similar diameters and plant height per vat.

During cultivation from 2020 to 2023, except for the experimental treatments, fertilization and the other management measures used were the same as those.

Measurement of leaf gas exchange, relative chlorophyll content and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

On the 15th of August, 2023, among optimal meteorological conditions characterized by a clear, cloudless sky, leaf gas exchange parameters were measured between 09:00 and 11:00 BST on the fifth leaf from the shoot apex of 26 plants per treatment. The Li-6400 portable photosynthesis system (Li-Cor Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) was employed to assess the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), stomatal conductance (Gs), and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci). The photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was maintained at 1800 µmol m−2 s−1, as regulated by a Q-Beam light source (blue and red diodes). Throughout the measurements, the leaf temperature was maintained at 25.5 ± 0.4 °C, the ambient water vapor pressure at 1.30 ± 0.15 kPa, and the CO2 concentration at 400 ppm. Simultaneously, chlorophyll fluorescence was quantified using an LI-6400–40 integrating fluorescence leaf chamber fluorometer (Li-Cor Inc.). The parameters evaluated included the chlorophyll content index (SPAD), the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (PSII) (F v /F m), the photochemical efficiency of PSII under illumination (F v' /F m'), the actual quantum yield of PSII (Yield), the photochemical quenching coefficient (Qp), and the non-photochemical quenching coefficient (NPQ). The methodology for these measurements was adapted from the protocols established by Zhu et al. [39].

Measurement of morphological parameters of the root system

On October 10, 2023, above-ground organs were harvested, dried at 70 °C to a constant weight, and biomass determined. The whole soil and root systems were cautiously extracted from the plastic vats, ensuring the roots remained undamaged. Subsequently, the root systems were immersed in water and rinsed gently to remove all soil particles adhering to the vats, and separated the rhizomes from the roots.

The cleaned roots were scanned using an Epson digital scanner (Expression 11000XL; Epson, Suwa City, Japan). Root parameters, including taproot length (TL), maximum taproot diameter (MTD), and average diameter of taproot (ADT), were measured using Win RHIZO Pro2012b software (Regent Instruments Inc, Quebec City, QC, Canada).

For each treatment group, a precise aliquot of 10.00 g of the taproot and an equal mass of the fine root were excised. These root samples were rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen to facilitate immediate freezing, thereby preserving the integrity of the cellular constituents. Subsequently, the samples were stored at an ultra-low temperature of -80 °C for subsequent analysis. This cryopreservation method is critical for the accurate determination of key osmotic adjustment substances, malondialdehyde (MDA) content as a marker of lipid peroxidation, enzymatic activities indicative of antioxidant defense mechanisms, and the ionic composition of the root tissues.

Remainder roots of G. uralensis were bagged, desiccated at 70 °C to a constant weight, and their dry biomass was measured. The root-to-shoot ratio (R/S) was computed by dividing the underground biomass by the aboveground biomass.

Subsequently, the dehydrated above-ground organs and roots samples of G. uralensis roots were ground into a fine powder using a grinder (ISO-9001, Guangsha Trade Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and then standardized through an 80-mesh sieve for uniformity in subsequent assays.

Measurement of osmoregulatory substances, malondialdehyde content, reactive oxygen scavenging systems and ions analysis of the taproot

Concentrations of proline (Pro), soluble sugar (SS), soluble protein (SP), and malondialdehyde (MDA) in taproots were determined using the respective methods: acidic ninhydrin method [40], anthrone method [41], Coomassie brilliant blue (G-250) method [41], and thiobarbituric acid method [42]. The relative conductivity of the roots of Glycyrrhiza uralensis was determined with reference to Dionisio [42]. Enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) were measured using Solarbio kits. (SOD- BC0170, POD-BC0095, CAT-C0200, Beijing Solarbio Technology Co. Potassium (K+), sodium (Na+), calcium (Ca2+), and magnesium (Mg2+) concentrations were determined following Mohammadi [43].

Measurement of the concentration of medicinal components and lanthanum

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Barbara, CA) was employed to quantify glycyrrhizic acid and glycyrrhizin in taproots. Total flavonoid concentration was determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-1900, Shimadzu Corporation, Shanghai, China) at 490 nm, with extraction and quantification methods from He et al. [44]. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) ( Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Barbara, CA) was used to determine the concentration of lanthanum in G. uralensis taproots, and the concentrations were quantified using 0–1000 ppb lanthanum standards (GSBG62047-90), with methodological details referenced from my previous research [14].

Data processing

Statistical analysis of all experimental data was performed by IBM SPSS 19.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) software. Plotting of all experimental data was carried out in GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA), and all datasets were verified to be normally distributed using the D’Agostino and Pearson omnibus normality test. Variations in photosynthetic gas exchange parameters, chlorophyll fluorescence indices, osmotic substance concentrations, metal ion concentrations, antioxidant enzyme activities, medicinal component contents, root system morphological characteristics, the R/S, and taproot lanthanum concentrations across treatments were assessed using two-way ANOVA. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test to adjust for multiple comparisons, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was carried out using the "sem" and "DiagrammeR" software packages.

However, these treatments concurrently led to a pronounced decline in gas exchange efficiency, with the NaCl-stressed group exhibiting a 73.44% reduction in net photosynthetic rate, a 68.96% decrease in stomatal conductance, a 42.71% drop in intercellular CO2 concentration, and an 84.25% fall in transpiration rate. Similar trends were observed in the Na2SO4-stressed group, with reductions of 62.36%, 50.59%, 38.13%, and 50.59%, respectively (Table 1). The addition of La (NO3)3 significantly reduced the chlorophyll content under salt-stressed treatments. Interestingly, under non-salt treatments, lanthanum supplementation significantly bolstered chlorophyll content and enhanced photosynthetic parameters across the board. Specifically, the CK + La3+ group showed 26.04% increase in chlorophyll content, 9.45% rise in net photosynthetic rate, 26.54% improvement in stomatal conductance, and 17.07% elevation in intercellular CO2 concentration compared to the CK group.

In comparison with the NaCl-stressed group, the chlorophyll content in the La-NaCl group was diminished by 32.12%. Similarly, the chlorophyll content in the Na2SO4-stressed groups was reduced by 17.14% relative to the La-Na2SO4 group.

Under NaCl-stressed groups, the presence of lanthanum resulted in 70.14% increase in net photosynthetic rate, 23.66% enhancement in stomatal conductance, and 29.70% rise in intercellular CO2 concentration. Notably, compared with Na2SO4-stressed groups, the net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, intercellular CO2 concentration and transpiration rate of the La-Na2SO4 group increased by 96.25%, 35.49%, 35.49% and 37.39%, respectively.

Salt-stressed treatments, particularly in the absence of lanthanum, were found to significantly decrease photosynthetic efficiency, as evidenced by reductions in the maximum photochemical efficiency (F v /F m), actual photochemical efficiency (F v' /F m'), quantum yield of photochemistry (Y (II)), and electron transport rate (ETR) by 18.31%, 17.46%, 30.16%, and 23.93% in the NaCl-stressed group, and by 14.08%, 15.87%, 20.63%, and 15.87% in the Na2SO4-stressed group, respectively, compared to the CK group (Table 2). Discussion

Saline soils are often characterized by elevated concentrations of soluble salts, which, through the process of evaporation, are drawn towards the soil surface along with water, leading to accumulation [46]. The lack of water in the cells triggered by this condition causes the stomatal closure, reducing Gs and thereby limiting the intake of CO2 and the release of O2, which in turn disrupts the gas exchange essential for photosynthesis [47].

Furthermore, as the G. uralensis’s roots absorb water and nutrients, they also inadvertently assimilate excess salt ions, disrupting the intracellular ionic equilibrium. There is channel contention between the extra sodium ions (Na⁺) and essential nutrients such as potassium (K⁺), magnesium (Mg2⁺), and calcium (Ca2⁺), which can limit the absorption of these ions. Excess salt is harmful to G. uralensis and also reduces the plant's ability to absorb these key nutrients. Consequently, G. uralensis under salt stress faces a dual challenge of ionic toxicity and nutrient reduction, which severely perturbs their normal metabolism [48, 49].

Moreover, the toxic effects of excess salt ions upset the delicate balance of the reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism, leading to lipid and protein peroxidation within the cell membrane and an increase in membrane permeability, which causes the leakage of water-soluble cellular contents. Additionally, salt stress induces an overproduction of ROS [50–52], which can attack the electron transport chain, damage the structure of proteins and other biomolecules, and inflict systemic oxidative damage as well as PSII damage on G. uralensis. Notably, damage to PSII within the chloroplasts reduces the efficiency of photochemical processes, diminishes the G. uralensis's ability to capture and convert light energy, and ultimately affects the synthesis of photosynthetic products.

In our research, we saw how G. uralensis reacted to salty conditions, which supports the ideas about how it's affected by the salty conditions that we talked about earlier. that we talked about earlier. These responses were manifested in several ways: 1) A reduction in photosynthetic gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, with observed decreases in net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, intercellular CO2 concentration, transpiration rate, maximum photochemical efficiency (F v /F m), actual photochemical efficiency (F v' /F m'), the Y (II), and ETR; 2) An imbalance in ionic homeostasis, evidenced by an increase in Na⁺ concentration and a decrease in K⁺, Mg2⁺, and Ca2⁺ concentrations; 3) An increase in membrane permeability, as indicated by elevated levels of MDA and electrical conductivity; and 4) A suppression of root growth under salt stress, which significantly reduced the main root length, maximum and average diameter, and root biomass of G. uralensis.

Interestingly, in comparison to the NaCl-treated group, however, the soluble sugar content was lower in the Na2SO4-treated group, despite the higher photosynthetic rate, possibly related to the sugar content in the cytoplasmic solutes. We recognized that this is an area worthy of further exploration.

However, that different plant species exhibit varying degrees of adaptability in their photosynthetic characteristics, growth traits, and physiological processes to saline environments. The results of this experiment show that the response of G. uralensis gas exchange parameters and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters to salt stress were largely consistent, but the trend in SPAD values observed was contrary to that of the vast majority of previous studies [53–55]. Specifically, despite a decrease in photosynthetic capacity, an increase in chlorophyll content was observed. It is hypothesized that this may be a compensatory mechanism by which G. uralensis, under salt stress, increases chlorophyll content to enhance the capture and utilization of light energy when the light-harvesting capacity is diminished or the photosynthetic apparatus is compromised, thereby maintaining photosynthetic activity [56].

In our study, generally higher antioxidant enzyme activities were observed under NaCl treatment than Na2SO4 treatment, which may reflect the physiological response of plants to higher ROS levels under NaCl stress. Nevertheless, the reduction in ETR and qP suggests that the energy conversion efficiency of photosynthesis may still be affected despite the activation of the antioxidant system. This may be related to salt stress-induced damage to the photosynthetic apparatus or activation of photoprotective mechanisms, limiting the utilization of light energy and the rate of electron transfer.

Lower antioxidant enzyme activities under Na2SO4 treatment may be associated with reduced ROS production, but may also be related to differential plant responses to ROS signaling. This suggests that plants may rely on other non-enzymatic antioxidants or ROS scavenging mechanisms to maintain redox homeostasis.

We found that plant root development was limited under salt stress conditions, especially when discussing the negative effects of salt stress on plant growth, yield, and quality, so we hypothesised that this may be related to the reduced ability of the root system to absorb water and nutrients. However, the experiment revealed that the saline environment significantly increased the R/S in G. uralensis, which may represent a strategic adjustment in biomass allocation to adapt to salt conditions. Prior research has indicated that plants may reduce the proportion of root biomass to minimize the transport of salts to the aerial parts [57, 58] or increase the proportion of root biomass to enhance the absorption of water and nutrients, thus diluting the salts within the plants [59, 60]. In this study, the biomass allocation strategy of G. uralensis clearly falls into the latter category, demonstrating its capacity for complex physiological responses and adaptive adjustments under conditions of salt stress.

This study has elucidated the positive effects of exogenous La (NO3)3, when applied at an appropriate concentration, on the photosynthetic activity of G. uralensis under salt stress. The application of La (NO3)3 not only significantly alleviates the inhibition of photosynthesis by salt stress but also effectively mitigates the osmotic, ionic, and oxidative stresses induced by salt stress. Most importantly, it has been shown to significantly enhance the yield and quality of G. uralensis. In the realm of G. uralensis physiology, photosynthetic gas exchange parameters and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters serve as critical indicators for understanding the dynamic responses of G. uralensis to environmental changes. These parameters are instrumental in reflecting the alterations in photosynthetic capacity, which can be either beneficial or detrimental. In this study, the application of La (NO3)3 led to a significant amelioration of the photosynthetic gas exchange impairments caused by salt stress. The salt + La3+ treatment group exhibited a marked increase in net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, intercellular CO2 concentration, transpiration rate, maximum photochemical efficiency (F v /F m), actual photochemical efficiency (F v' /F m'), Y (II), and ETR compared to the salt-stressed treatment group. These findings suggest that La (NO3)3 may improve the photosynthetic efficiency of G. uralensis by enhancing the functionality of stomata, mitigating photoinhibition, and increasing the efficiency of PSII, thereby facilitating the conversion of light energy into chemical energy and improving the photosynthetic characteristics of G. uralensis. In this experiment, we observed that G. uralensis experienced a significant decrease in photosynthetic performance under salt stress. However, these parameters showed a tendency to rebound after the introduction of mitigants, suggesting that mitigants have a positive effect on restoring photosynthesis in plants. Nevertheless, the photochemical quenching coefficient (Qp) did not show a significant change in this process, which triggered deep thoughts.

We hypothesized that the stability of Qp might be related to the photoprotective mechanisms initiated by plants. Under salt stress, plants may optimize the absorption and utilization of light energy by adjusting the composition or content of photosynthetic pigments. Such adjustments not only help to improve the efficiency of conversion of light energy into chemical energy, but by increasing the photosynthetic pigment content, plants are able to scatter or reflect excess light energy more efficiently, thereby reducing the risk of photoinhibition. This adaptive change coincides with the increase in photosynthetic pigment content we observed and may explain why Qp remained stable in the presence of mitigants.

In addition, the stability of Qp may also reflect the intrinsic ability of plants to regulate the photochemical phase of photosynthesis and maintain a degree of photochemical efficiency even when stomatal conductance and other photosynthetic parameters are affected by stress. This regulation may involve the optimization of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, as well as the activation of photoprotective mechanisms, which together act to maintain the stability and efficiency of photosynthesis.

To counteract the reduced osmotic potential of the soil under salt stress conditions, G. uralensis produces osmotic regulatory substances such as soluble sugars, soluble proteins, and free proline to maintain osmotic balance and stabilize the structure of biomacromolecules, preventing excessive water loss from root cells.

Soluble proteins, predominantly enzymes involved in metabolism, reflect the metabolic vigor of the G. uralensis. Moreover, soluble proteins, with their strong hydrophilicity, can enhance the water retention capacity of cells, effectively preventing root dehydration. The results of this experiment indicate that the application of La (NO3)3 led to a significant increase in the content of soluble sugars, soluble proteins, and free proline in the salt + La3+ treatment group compared to the salt-stressed treatment group, suggesting that La (NO3)3 can enhance the osmotic regulatory capacity of G. uralensis and improve their tolerance to salt stress. In the study of G. uralensis salt tolerance, some researchers have posited that selective ion uptake and ionic balance may play a more critical role than osmotic regulatory substances like proline [57]. The accumulation of Na⁺ can cause damage to the cell membrane system, whereas K⁺, as a crucial inorganic solute, is essential for reducing the osmotic potential of G. uralensis cells and maintaining water balance [61, 62]. The experimental findings show that after the application of La (NO3)3, the K⁺, Ca2⁺, and Mg2⁺ content in the salt + La3+ treatment group significantly increased, while the Na⁺ content markedly decreased compared to the salt treatment group. Ca2⁺, acting as a second messenger in the salt stress signal transduction pathway, is significantly positively correlated with salt stress. La (NO3)3 may increase the Ca2⁺ content to activate the SOS pathway's sodium exclusion system, mitigating the damage caused by sodium. Mg2⁺ promotes the formation of chlorophyll and the activation of enzymes in photosynthesis and also regulates stomatal closure in the respiratory process, affecting ionic balance [63, 64]. La (NO3)3 may enhance the photosynthetic efficiency and stomatal function of G. uralensis leaves by increasing Mg2⁺ content, thus improving the salt tolerance of G. uralensis. Under saline-stress, a plethora of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated within G. uralensis. To prevent the damage caused by excessive ROS to the biomembrane system and cellular oxidative phosphorylation, G. uralensis relies on an antioxidant enzyme system to neutralize these ROS. Antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, POD, and CAT, reflect the metabolic and stress-resistant changes within the G. uralensis. The stronger the osmotic regulatory capacity and the higher the protective enzyme activity, the better the G. uralensis's adaptability to saline-alkali stress [65, 66]. However, the results of this experiment show that after the application of La (NO3)3, the antioxidant system in G. uralensis was effectively activated, with increased levels of SOD and CAT, but a significant decrease in POD content. This may reflect a specific regulatory strategy within the G. uralensis's antioxidant defense system. The effectiveness of this strategy may depend on the intensity, duration, and adaptability of G. uralensis to salt stress [67, 68]. SOD, a primary enzyme in the antioxidant system, catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide anions (O2−) into oxygen (O2) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). CAT is responsible for the decomposition of H2O2 into water (H2O) and oxygen, thus detoxifying the peroxide [69, 70]. An increase in SOD and CAT content aids in the G. uralensis's more efficient scavenging of ROS, safeguarding cells from oxidative damage. POD, an enzyme that utilizes H2O2 as a substrate, catalyzes the oxidation of various substrates. In certain scenarios, G. uralensis may reduce POD levels to minimize H2O2 consumption, ensuring an adequate supply of H2O2 for CAT, which helps maintain ROS homeostasis. The reduction in POD content may also represent a nuanced regulatory approach by the G. uralensis to control ROS levels, preventing an overabundance of H2O2 from being converted into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, which are highly destructive to cellular structure and function.

In the clinical applications of China, G. uralensis is extensively utilized in the form of slices. The length and thickness of the main root of G. uralensis, as well as the content of its medicinal components, are paramount indicators for assessing the quality grade of G-is explains the observed contradiction between yield and quality under high soil salt conditions in the experiment. Although salt stress can elevate the concentration of G. uralensis acid and glycyrrhizin in the main root, the high salt concentration of the soil inhibits the accumulation of biomass, leading to a decrease in the yield of individual G. uralensis. However, the addition of La (NO3)3, while reducing the concentration of these substances, significantly promotes the biomass of the main root and substantially increases the total content of G. uralensis acid and glycyrrhizin per plant, thereby effectively enhancing the yield and quality of G. uralensis.

In an exhaustive examination of the impact of exogenous lanthanum on G. uralensis tolerance to salt stress, we discerned notable variations in its efficacy against NaCl and Na2SO4-induced stresses. In our study, generally higher antioxidant enzyme activities were observed under NaCl treatment than Na2SO4 treatment, which may reflect the physiological response of plants to higher ROS levels under NaCl stress. Nevertheless, the reduction in ETR and qP suggests that the energy conversion efficiency of photosynthesis may still be affected despite the activation of the antioxidant system. This may be related to salt stress-induced damage to the photosynthetic apparatus or activation of photoprotective mechanisms, limiting the utilization of light energy and the rate of electron transfer.

Lower antioxidant enzyme activities under Na2SO4 treatment may be associated with reduced ROS production, but may also be related to differential plant responses to ROS signaling. This suggests that plants may rely on other non-enzymatic antioxidants or ROS scavenging mechanisms to maintain redox homeostasis.

We suggest that the mechanisms of ROS production and scavenging under NaCl and Na2SO4 stress involve complex physiological processes, including regulation of photosynthesis, activation of antioxidant systems, and plant signaling responses to oxidative stress. Although our study provides some insights, further studies are needed to explore these mechanisms in detail and how they affect plant adaptation under salt stress. In addition, Na2SO4 stress was not readily mitigated by lanthanum possibly because sulfate ions (SO4 2−) may affect soil pH, and consequently the efficiency of sulfate scavenging by G. uralensis. Collectively, these mechanisms may operate in unison or isolation, culminating in lanthanum's preferential mitigation of NaCl-induced stress. Nonetheless, the precise underpinnings of these effects warrant further experimental elucidation.

Conclusion

In summary, La (NO3)3 enhances the salt tolerance of G. uralensis through a multifaceted approach, which includes protecting the photosynthetic apparatus, bolstering osmotic regulation, improving ionic balance, activating the antioxidant system, and fostering root growth and development. These mechanisms collectively enable G. uralensis to sustain normal physiological functions, maintain high growth rates, and accumulate biomass under salt stress conditions, thereby improving their salt tolerance. Importantly, the addition of La (NO3)3 has been shown to be effective and significantly increases the yield and quality of G. uralensis.
