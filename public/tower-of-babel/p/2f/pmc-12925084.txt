Gut microbial metabolites link dietary history to appetite regulation
Authors: Emma C. Spillman, Andrew K. Shepherd, Meihua C. Kuang, In-Hwan Jang, Won-Jae Lee, Jing W. Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
All metazoan guts harbor commensal communities, from a dozen bacterial species in Drosophila to hundreds in humans. Here, we condition flies with diets containing varying levels of protein and sugar to investigate the impact of dietary history on the interaction between commensal gut bacteria and feeding adaptation in Drosophila. We find that appetite increases with dietary protein, dependent on total gut bacteria content, and enhanced by a drug that promotes the growth of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing gut bacteria. Lactiplantibacillus is a potential source of butyrate, while Acetobacter produces acetate. Mono-association with Acetobacter or Lactiplantibacillus increases food intake. Mutant strains unable to produce acetate or butyrate have lesser effects. Finally, adding acetate or butyrate to conditioning diets recapitulates the appetitive effect of Acetobacter and Lactiplantibacillus, respectively. Our findings suggest that protein-enriched diets enhance appetite by promoting the interaction between commensal bacteria and the host, with bacterial SCFAs as a conduit.

Introduction

There are competing theories on how the macronutrient composition of different diets regulates food intake. The hedonic override hypothesis suggests that while people typically balance their energy intake with expenditure, hedonic factors from high-fat or sugar content in food can disrupt this balance (Berridge et al., 2010; Berthoud, 2002; Hu et al., 2018). The protein leverage hypothesis suggests that animals, including humans, consume food to achieve a target intake of protein (Simpson & Raubenheimer, 2005). As such, when the dietary protein level is low, individuals increase their food consumption, resulting in excessive energy intake. However, these models have overlooked the potential long-term effects of dietary nutrients on gut microbiota.

Gut microorganisms inhabit an ecological niche formed by the interaction between the host and the microbes themselves (Ley et al., 2008). In different animal species, including humans, the type of food they consume profoundly impacts the composition of their gut microbiota (De Filippo et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2011). Conversely, gut microbiota play important roles in regulating host feeding behavior. Introducing the microbiota from an obese individual, whether human or mouse, to germ-free mice has been shown to confer the adiposity phenotype (Ridaura et al., 2013; Turnbaugh et al., 2006). However, germ-free mice, even when fed a diet rich in fat and sucrose, do not become obese, unlike their gut microbiota-containing, conventionally reared counterparts (Bäckhed et al., 2004). While these studies highlight the important role of gut microbiota in feeding regulation, little is known about the underlying mechanisms of host-microbiota interactions, partly due to the complex composition of mammalian gut microorganisms.

The fruit fly, with its genetic tractability and well-characterized, simple gut microbiota, has emerged as an attractive model for exploring the complex interactions between dietary macronutrients, gut microbiota, and host behaviors (Broderick & Lemaitre, 2012). The Drosophila gut typically harbors a dozen bacterial strains, predominantly Acetobacter and Lactiplantibacillus (Dodge et al., 2023; Obadia et al., 2017; Vandehoef et al., 2020; Wong et al., 2011). Studies have shown that dietary macronutrients influence the quantity and composition of gut microbiota. Diets containing high levels of either protein or carbohydrates alter the gut microbial composition, with the total gut bacterial load increasing in proportion to the level of dietary protein (Vandehoef et al., 2020). This suggests that dietary composition plays a crucial role in shaping the microbial landscape of the Drosophila gut.

On the other hand, the presence of gut bacteria itself significantly impacts feeding behavior. Flies with lower levels of gut bacteria often consume less food (Leitão-Gonçalves et al., 2017). Germ-free flies are also more sensitive to nutritional deficiency than their conventionally reared counterparts, as commensal bacteria can supply nutrients to the host, especially in poor nutritional conditions (Kim et al., 2021; Leitão-Gonçalves et al., 2017). These findings underscore the interplay between dietary macronutrients, gut microbiota, and host feeding behaviors, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms regulating appetite through host-microbiota interactions.

This study set out to investigate the long-term effect of dietary macronutrient composition on appetite using Drosophila as a model organism. To accomplish this, we fed flies a conditioning diet containing a mixture of protein and sucrose at different proportions and then measured their appetite by monitoring their consumption of a test food containing either protein or sucrose. We found that conditioning flies with protein-rich diets increased their appetite for both protein and sucrose. This change in appetite was abolished by depleting gut bacteria with antibiotics. Furthermore, the conditioning effect of the protein-rich diet was enhanced by metformin, which is known to alter the composition of the gut microbiota (Forslund et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2017). Again, the effect of metformin on food intake was abolished by antibiotics. These results suggest that feeding flies with protein-rich diets increases their appetite through modulating gut bacteria. Indeed, quantitative PCR (qPCR) experiments demonstrated that the total gut bacterial content increased with the protein level in the conditioning diets. We then investigated the effect of individual bacterial strains. We found that mono-association of germ-free flies with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum or Acetobacter pomorum increased food intake, with the highest intake observed in those associated with Lactiplantibacillus and conditioned by a high-protein diet. Additionally, mutant Acetobacter pomorum and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains, which are unable to produce acetate and butyrate, respectively, exerted lesser effects. Finally, we found that adding either acetate or butyrate to the conditioning diet increased appetite in germ-free flies, recapitulating the appetitive effect of Acetobacter and Lactiplantibacillus, respectively. This study provides new insights into how dietary macronutrients change Drosophila’s appetite. The appetitive effect of commensal bacteria is mediated by bacterial metabolites, acetate and butyrate, which are necessary and sufficient for Acetobacter and Lactiplantibacillus, respectively, to increase the host’s appetite.

Methods

Fly husbandry

Flies were raised on standard fly food (containing yeast, cornmeal, agar, and molasses) in a 12:12 light-dark cycle at 25 °C. Experimental flies were collected at eclosion as males and raised in same-sex groups of 10 per vial. All experimental flies were flipped to fresh vials daily.

Feeding assay

The excreta quantification experimental protocol was performed as previously described (Wu et al., 2020). In brief, flies were fed on standard fly food and conditioning diets as specified in each experiment. See Supplementary Table 1 for conditioning diet food recipes. Flies were then transferred to a standard fly vial (Genesee Scientific, catalog no. 32–116) with a 5 mm diameter food cup at the bottom of the vial containing tryptone (5%), sucrose (5%), or yeast (5%) and supplemented with 0.25% erioglaucine disodium salt (Sigma, catalog no. 861146) as a dye for quantification. Flies in groups of 10 were allowed to feed on the dye-containing food for 24 h before being fed with a sucrose (2.5%) without dye for 3 h to allow for all dye-containing food to be excreted. To quantify food intake, dye-containing excreta from each vial was dissolved in distilled water, and dye concentration was measured with a spectrophotometer (GENESYS 40 Vis, Thermo Scientific). Absorbance at 630 nm was converted to food volume consumed according to a standard curve.

Quantitative PCR

Flies were surface sterilized in ethyl ethanol dips and three sequential PBS dips. Each bacterial DNA sample was extracted from 4 flies per replicate for each condition with the QIAGEN DNeasy UltraClean Microbial Kit (Qiagen catalog no. 10196–4). A hand-held pestle drill was used during extraction. qPCR was performed with a Bio-Rad CFX machine using iQ SYBR Green Supermix from Bio-Rad. Bacterial DNA sequences were amplified using 5 μl of extracted bacterial DNA. Primer sequences: Universal 16S (forward primer - AGGAAGGTGGGGATGACG, reverse primer - CCCGGGAACGTATTCACC), Lactiplantibacillus 16S (forward primer - TGGAAACAGATGCTAATACCG, reverse primer - GTCCATTGTGGAAGATTCCC), and Acetobacter 16S (forward primer - TAGTGGCGGACGGGTGAGTA, reverse primer - AATCAAACGCAGGCTCCTCC) (Vandehoef et al., 2020). qPCR was performed with each primer pair in three technical replicates for each sample and CT values were recorded with Bio-Rad CFX Manager software (Version 2.0.885.0923).

Bacteria culturing

Acetobacter pomorum and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum were cultured from a single colony in 2 ml MRS broth media at 30 °C, shaking for 48 h. Bacteria from 10 μl of media were pelleted and resuspended in PBS. To ensure comparable quantities of each bacterial strain were used in the mono-association experiments, resuspended bacteria were loaded on a counting chamber (BlauBrand, BR717810), imaged with a light microscope and ImageJ for bacterial counting. The total number of cells and concentration were determined using a hemocytometer calculator at hemocytometer.org.103 (Figure 3) or 106 (Supplementary Figure 5) bacterial cells of each strain were plated on conditioning diets immediately before experiments.

Generation of an Acyl-ACP thioesterase deletion mutant of lactiplantibacillus plantarum WJL strain

To delete the Acyl-ACP thioesterase gene, 634 bp and 686 bp fragments encoding the upstream and downstream regions, respectively, of the Acyl-ACP thioesterase gene were amplified by PCR. PCR primers for the upstream fragment (Forward: 5′- CAG GTC GAC TCT AGA GGA TCC CCc tta gca caa gtt agc gat gcg-3′; Reverse: 5′-cac act agt gta aat acc aac cca gca tca aga ctc ctc atc gtt ggt a-3′) and for the downstream fragment (Forward: 5′-tac caa cga tga gga gtc ttg atg ctg ggt tgg tat tta cac tag tgt g-3′; Reverse: 5′-CAG TGA ATT CGA GCT CGG TAC CCg atc ttc gcc aat act gcc atc tg-3′) were used. The upstream and downstream fragments were assembled by PCR and finally inserted into the pGID023 vector (Hols et al., 1994). The resulting plasmid was subsequently electroporated into the Lactiplantibacillus plantarum WJL strain. Deletion mutants were generated using a method based on homology-based recombination with double cross-over. Briefly, transformants were selected at 37 °C on MRS agar plates with 5 μg/ml erythromycin for 2 days. After a single transformant was cultured at 37 °C in MRS liquid media for 1 day and plated on MRS agar media, each colony was replica-plated using MRS agar plates in the presence or absence of 5 μg/ml erythromycin. Erythromycin-sensitive colonies represent either mutant or revertant wildtype. Mutant strains were confirmed by PCR using the following primer set: Forward: 5′- ATG ACT TAT CGG GCA GTG GC −3′; Reverse: 5′-TTG GTT GCC CGG TAA CTT CA −3′. Deletions were further confirmed by sequencing analysis.

Statistical analyses

Prism was used for all statistical analysis (Version 9.4.1 for MacOS; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA test, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test, was used for food intake experiments. Two sample t tests were used for 16S rRNA gene levels.

Results

Protein-rich or sugar-rich diets lead to gradual appetite changes in Drosophila

To determine whether dietary history influences food consumption in Drosophila melanogaster, we fed flies for 1 to 5 days with conditioning diets containing different proportions of protein and sucrose, and gave them a test food of either protein or sucrose for one day to assess their appetite (Figure 1(A)). We used the excreta quantification (EX-Q) assay to measure food intake (Wu et al., 2020). We found that w1118 male flies conditioned on 8% tryptone for 3 or 5 days had higher food intake than those conditioned on a mixture of 4% tryptone and 4% sucrose, when they were tested with either 5% tryptone or 5% sucrose (Figure 1(B)). By contrast, male flies exposed for 3 or 5 days to a conditioning diet containing 8% sucrose decreased their food intake, regardless of whether the test food was 5% tryptone or 5% sucrose. Furthermore, feeding on the conditioning diets for a shorter time of one day had no impact on appetite. These results suggest that high-protein conditioning diets increase appetite, whereas high-sugar conditioning diets suppress appetite.

We next explored whether high-protein conditioning diets made from protein sources other than tryptone similarly influence food intake. We selected a low-dusting yeast extract with a significantly higher protein content (72%) and a lower carbohydrate level (1%) compared to the commonly used yeast extract. We fed male flies for three days with conditioning diets of different yeast-to-sucrose ratios and measured their appetite with either 5% yeast or 5% sucrose. Similar to our observations with tryptone, high-yeast conditioning diets increased appetite. In contrast, high-sucrose conditioning diets suppressed appetite (Figure 1(C)). Both our tryptone- and yeast-based conditioning diets demonstrate that protein content significantly influences appetite, irrespective of the specific amino acid profiles of the proteins.

Next, we addressed whether the observed changes in appetite are simply a reflection of caloric deprivation or excess during dietary conditioning. For example, flies might consume fewer calories on a high-protein diet, rendering them hungrier, or excess calories on high-sucrose diets, potentially confounding the interpretation of appetite changes. To test this, we measured intake of the conditioning diets. We found that flies gradually increased their consumption of high-yeast diets but decreased their consumption of sucrose-rich diets, which is opposite to the predicted pattern if caloric intake alone drove appetitive adaptation (Figure 1(D)). These results argue against nutritional state as the primary driver of the appetite shifts.

We then investigated whether another strain of wild-type males and w1118 females exhibit similar appetitive changes when conditioned on high-protein diets. Indeed, Canton-S wild-type male flies exhibited appetitive changes comparable to those of w1118 males in response to high-protein or high-sugar conditioning diets (Supplementary Figure 1 A). Female flies also showed an increased appetite similar to males in response to high-protein conditioning diets, compared to those conditioned on a mixture of 4% yeast and 4% sucrose. However, unlike males, they did not reduce their appetite when conditioned with the high-sugar diet for three days (Supplementary Figure 1B). Consistent with previous research, we also found that mating increased females’ food intake, but only when they were conditioned with the high-yeast diet (Hadjieconomou et al., 2020; Malita et al., 2022). Together, our results indicate that high-protein conditioning diets enhance appetite in both males and females. Therefore, we used w1118 male flies in all subsequent experiments.

Dietary nutrients alter the quantity and composition of gut bacteria

We employed a pharmacological approach to identify potential mechanisms by which high-protein conditioning diets can increase host appetite (Supplementary Figure 2 A). Specifically, we focused on rapamycin, wortmannin, and antibiotics. Rapamycin is known to inhibit the target of rapamycin (TOR), a protein kinase central to a highly conserved nutrient-sensing pathway that functions in organisms from yeast to mammals, regulating growth and metabolism (Bjedov et al., 2010; Saxton & Sabatini, 2017). Wortmannin is a potent inhibitor of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), a crucial downstream molecule involved in insulin’s control of gene transcription (Arcaro & Wymann, 1993). A previous study demonstrated that feeding flies food containing a cocktail of antibiotics—including tetracycline (50 μg/ml), ampicillin (500 μg/ml), and rifamycin (200 μg/ml)—effectively depletes their gut bacteria (Brummel et al., 2004). We found that supplementation of rapamycin or wortmannin in the conditioning diets did not affect food intake (Supplementary Fig. 2B). In contrast, depletion of gut bacteria in the adult stage by antibiotics markedly suppressed food intake and nearly eliminated the appetitive effect of protein-rich conditioning diets.

We then performed qPCR to measure the effect of conditioning diets on the total bacterial load (Figure 2(A)). 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene levels have been widely used as a measure of total bacterial load. Using previously validated primers, we measured 16S rRNA gene levels from flies fed different conditioning diets for three days (Vandehoef et al., 2020). We found that 16S rRNA gene levels increased with yeast concentration in the conditioning diets, showing a 78-fold difference between flies conditioned with 8% yeast and those with 8% sucrose (Figure 2(B)). These results suggest that total bacterial levels increase with the protein level in the conditioning diets.

We next searched for a pharmacological approach to augment gut bacteria. In mammals, metformin increases populations of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria such as Lactiplantibacillus (Forslund et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2017). Our qPCR experiments revealed that adding metformin at 5 mM to the 8% yeast conditioning diet further increased gut bacteria levels by 8-fold, albeit with no significant effect on the other two conditioning diets (Figure 2(C)). Conversely, the total bacterial content was significantly reduced when the cocktail of antibiotics was included in all foods used to feed adult flies. Furthermore, the antibiotics completely negated the effect of metformin.

Appetite increases with total gut bacteria

We next directly test the hypothesis that the quantity of gut bacteria regulates appetite. First, to increase the bacteria content, we used metformin, which promotes the growth of gut bacteria in flies conditioned with the high-yeast diet (Figure 2(C)), and found that indeed, it only increased food consumption in those conditioned with 8% yeast (Figure 2(D,E)). Second, the appetite enhancement by high-yeast conditioning diets was markedly reduced in flies treated with the antibiotics. While it is important to consider the possibility that metformin may have side effects in Drosophila unrelated to gut bacterial growth, the aforementioned antibiotics almost completely abolished the effect of metformin. Given this strong reduction in appetite when metformin was presented together with antibiotic treatment, the observed effect of metformin on appetite is most likely mediated by the gut bacteria. To determine whether the antibiotics used in our experiments affect flies’ appetite, we compared feeding behavior in flies given test food with or without antibiotics. We found no difference in appetite, confirming that antibiotic treatment does not alter feeding responses (Supplementary Figure 3). Finally, we plotted food intake against the relative 16S rRNA gene levels (Figure 2(F)). Linear regression analysis indicated that the 16S rRNA gene level is an excellent predictor for the intake of yeast and sucrose. Together, our results suggest that protein-rich conditioning diets promote the growth of gut bacteria, thereby increasing host appetite.

To characterize the persistence of diet-induced appetitive changes, we conditioned flies for three days. Then, we transferred them to antibiotic-containing standard food for 6 or 24 h before placing them on a test food that also contained antibiotics. The protein-rich diet’s effect on appetite remained significant after 6 h on the antibiotics-containing food (Supplementary Figure 4), demonstrating that microbiota-mediated appetitive adaptation persists beyond the conditioning period. This prolonged retention suggests that gut microbes and dietary history may interact to durably alter host gene expression or physiology.

Impact of two main commensal bacteria on appetite

To further understand the contribution of individual bacterial species to host food intake, we carried out experiments in which flies were given food containing antibiotics during the adult stage to deplete their gut bacteria (Figure 3(A)). These germ-free flies were then given conditioning diets containing either Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (Lp) or Acetobacter pomorum (Ap), the two dominant species of gut bacteria in laboratory Drosophila (Broderick & Lemaitre, 2012; Vandehoef et al., 2020). A previous study showed that either Lp or Ap can completely colonize the fly gut within 3 days (Dodge et al., 2023). Therefore, we expected to see the full effect of these bacteria on host feeding when flies were fed with the bacteria-containing conditioning diets for 3 days. We found that mono-association with either Lp or Ap increased food intake, regardless of which conditioning diets were given to the flies (Figure 3(B,C), middle panels). Interestingly, the food intake increase was greater with Lp under the high-yeast conditioning diet (Figure 3(C), middle panel). By contrast, the increase in food intake with Ap was similar under both high- and low-yeast conditioning diets (Figure 3(B), middle panel). These results suggest that the interaction between bacteria and host in regulating feeding differs between bacterial species. The appetitive effect of Lp appears to be amplified by the high protein levels in conditioning diets. Given that different gut bacterial strains have been found in laboratory Drosophila, we carried out the mono-association experiments with additional strains of Ap and Lp. Similarly, these other strains increased host appetite, corroborating the synergistic effect of Lp with high-yeast conditioning diets on host appetite (Supplementary Figure 5).

SCFAs such as acetate and butyrate are the most abundant metabolites produced by gut bacteria (Dalile et al., 2019). Ap is a member of the Acetobacter family known for its specialty in producing acetate from sugars or alcohols during fermentation. In contrast, some Lp strains are known to produce butyrate (Botta et al., 2017). Furthermore, our experiments showed that the significant impact of metformin on host appetite required the presence of gut bacteria (Figure 2(B)). Metformin is also known to increase the production of SCFAs (Forslund et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2017). We therefore designed experiments to test whether the production of acetate and butyrate by Ap and Lp, respectively, are required for their appetitive effect on the host.

We tested the ApP3G5 mutant strain, which is unable to produce acetate (Shin et al., 2011). We found that the mutant Ap strain was not as effective in increasing food intake as the wild-type Ap strain when flies were conditioned with a diet containing no yeast or a low level of yeast (Fig. 3B, right panel). However, for the conditioning diet containing a high level of protein, the ApP3G5 mutant strain was as effective as the wild-type Ap strain in increasing flies’ appetite, suggesting that dietary protein at a high level may substitute acetate in regulating host appetite.

To determine if butyrate production by Lp is essential for its appetitive effect, we engineered an Lp mutant strain by knocking out the medium-chain acyl-ACP thioesterase gene. This gene encodes the only terminal enzyme in the butyrogenic pathway (Botta et al., 2017). Indeed, the Lp mutants were not as effective in increasing food intake as the wild-type Lp strain (Figure 3(C), right panel). Together, these results indicate that acetate and butyrate, produced by Ap and Lp, respectively, play critical roles in their appetitive effect on the host.

Microbial metabolites can influence host appetite in the absence of gut bacteria

To test whether acetate and butyrate can replace their bacterial producers in enhancing host appetite, we gave germ-free flies the conditioning diets supplemented with different concentrations of acetate or butyrate (Figure 4(A)). The cocktail of antibiotics was added to all foods for the adult stage to deplete gut bacteria. Interestingly, regardless of the protein levels in the conditioning diets, acetate supplementation moderately increased flies’ food intake (Figure 4(B)). In contrast, the appetitive effect of butyrate supplementation was much stronger when the conditioning diets contained a high level of protein (Figure 4(C)). These results indicate that acetate and butyrate can replicate the appetitive effects observed with Ap and Lp, respectively, and that protein-rich conditioning diets can synergize with butyrate. Taken together, these results show that each SCFA has a unique effect on food intake in the absence of gut bacteria and could be viewed as a conduit of communication between the gut bacteria and host.

Discussion

Interactions between the host and its gut microbes create a unique ecological niche in the gut, allowing commensal microorganisms to proliferate and resulting in a composition distinct from that of environmental microbial communities (Ley et al., 2008). The food consumed by the host, serving as fuel and building blocks for the microbes, plays a critical role in shaping the gut microbiota (De Filippo et al., 2010; Vandehoef et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2011). Our study addresses the question of how gut bacteria alter food intake in Drosophila. We show that dietary protein levels significantly enhance gut bacteria in Drosophila. The amount of food intake is highly correlated with the total bacterial load in the fly gut. Furthermore, we demonstrate that gut bacteria, through their metabolites, acetate and butyrate produced by Acetobacter and Lactiplantibacillus, respectively, influence host feeding behavior. These findings reveal a diet-dependent interaction between the host and gut microbiota, with bacterial metabolites emerging as key regulators of appetite. Moreover, dietary history influences appetitive adaptation by altering the abundance and composition of gut microbes.

The concept of homeostasis is central to understanding feeding behavior, and various theories have emerged regarding the regulation of food intake in animals. Some propose that energy intake remains constant, while others suggest it is governed by a protein target (Simpson & Raubenheimer, 2005; Speakman & Hall, 2021). Our findings challenge the notion of a fixed set point because gut microbiota changes host food intake. Under the framework of homeostasis, the regulation of feeding must exhibit flexibility to account for the energy and protein requirements necessary to support the gut microbiota. Notably, our data highlights the crucial role of SCFAs produced by gut bacteria in this regulatory process. While there is an established role of SCFAs in mammals as appetite suppressors and signaling molecules through G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs) in enteroendocrine cells, there are no known homologous GPCRs in Drosophila (Brown et al., 2003; Le Poul et al., 2003; Nilsson et al., 2003). And yet, supplementation of SCFAs in conditioning diets can increase food intake in Drosophila after their gut bacteria have been depleted, suggesting other potential mechanisms for the appetitive effect of SCFAs in mammals. Future research will explore the molecular targets of SCFAs, especially in enteroendocrine cells, given their close proximity to the gut microbiota.

One limitation of our study stems from the method used to infer the role of commensal bacteria in regulating host feeding behavior through SCFA metabolites, which was primarily based on experiments utilizing antibiotics to deplete gut bacteria. It is important to note that, unlike axenic flies, which are raised in completely germ-free conditions, antibiotic treatment does not achieve total eradication of gut bacteria. However, axenic conditions can induce developmental changes, including alterations in the composition of gut epithelial cells, which could potentially affect the host’s feeding behavior in ways not accounted for in our study (Liu et al., 2022). Therefore, further research is needed to assess whether the appetite-regulating effect of commensal bacteria observed in our study extends to axenic flies, which would shed light on the scope of the microbial impact on host physiology. Additionally, while we employed qPCR to measure the levels of the 16S rRNA gene as a proxy for total bacterial content, this approach may not capture the full complexity of the gut bacteria or the specific contributions of each bacterial strain to host feeding behavior. Future studies incorporating high-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing to gain a deeper understanding of all strains present in the gut and metabolomic analyses to systematically investigate microbial metabolites are essential for a deeper understanding of the complex host-microbe interactions.

In this study, we explore how dietary history affects the host’s food intake and alters the quantity and composition of commensal gut bacteria by subjecting flies to conditioning diets with varying protein and sugar levels. Our study takes advantage of a simple gut bacterial community in Drosophila, which has only two main bacterial strains. With genetic manipulation, we can identify the key metabolites of the two bacterial strains, linking dietary history to appetitive changes in Drosophila. This investigation highlights the challenge of studying mammalian guts, which contain vastly more complex commensal communities, making it arduous to assess the impact of individual bacterial species on host feeding behavior. However, if the metabolites acetate and butyrate similarly regulate food intake in mammalian hosts, it could pave the way for addressing the commensal community’s effect on feeding regulation by measuring the overall levels of these metabolites, thereby offering a simplified approach to understanding the complex interactions within the gut microbiome and its influence on feeding behavior.
