Roles of Soil and Foliar Spray of Zinc Nutrition in Cadmium Reduction of Wheat Grain
Authors: Faruk OZKUTLU, Ayhan KOCAMAN, Ozlem ETE AYDEMIR
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the role of soil and foliar application of zinc (Zn) in reducing the accumulation of cadmium (Cd) in wheat grains grown on Zn-depleted and Cd-contaminated soils. In particular, the effects of increasing Cd doses (0.0, 0.2, 1.0, and 5.0 mg Cd kg–1 soil) on wheat grain quality were investigated. The results showed that the combined application of soil and foliar Zn significantly increased the grain yield of wheat and reduced Cd accumulation. Specifically, foliar Zn application increased grain yield by 49.75% in Cd-free soils and by 32.79% in soils with elevated Cd5 content (the highest dose of 5 mg Cd kg–1) compared to inadequate Zn fertilization. In addition, foliar Zn application reduced the Cd concentration in grains by 70.49% in Cd-free soils and by 9.31% in Cd5 soils. In terms of nutrient uptake, foliar Zn applications led to an increase in grain Zn concentration by 77.67% in Cd0 and 173.06% in Cd5. However, the concentration of phosphorus and manganese decreased with high Zn applications and increased Cd doses. These results underline the importance of Zn management in Cd-polluted soils and provide information on how to improve wheat quality and minimize Cd pollution of crops.

Introduction

Cadmium is a highly toxic heavy metal (HM), and due to increased concentration in agricultural soils due to anthropogenic activities, it causes an increased risk of toxicity in humans through the food chain from plants and animal products. − Cadmium is extremely poisonous and nonessential even at very low concentrations. In addition to contamination of soils with high levels of Cd from parent material, Cd enters soils from sources such as sewage sludge, aerial deposition, disposal of agricultural and mining wastes, application of fertilizers, and phosphate fertilizers to soils as a result of human activities. − Cultivated areas have a total Cd concentration limit of 3 mg kg–1 soil, while uncultivated areas have a limit of 0.1 mg kg–1. The average Cd concentration in cultivated areas is estimated to be 0.28 mg Cd kg–1.

The accumulation of Cd and other metals such as zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and manganese (Mn) in soil and plants is a subject of great interest in research because of its implications for agricultural productivity and environmental safety. Cadmium is usually present at very low concentrations compared with other trace metals in the environment. Zn, Fe, Cu, and Mn are essential and toxic metals that can affect plant growth and health. Although these micronutrients are essential for various metabolic functions such as photosynthesis, enzyme activation, and redox balance, their excessive accumulation in plant tissue can lead to toxicity. For example, high concentrations of Cu can inhibit root growth, damage cell membranes, and disrupt photosynthetic processes. Similarly, Fe toxicity can lead to oxidative damage and chlorosis due to excessive production of reactive oxygen species. Excess Zn can impair the uptake of phosphorus and manganese, disrupt the function of proteins by misbinding with thiol groups, and cause destabilization of membranes. Due to their concentration-dependent dual effects, the management of micronutrients in polluted soils is therefore particularly critical. −

However, Cd can inhibit plant growth in various plant species. In agricultural soils, especially when Cd reaches high concentrations, it can be easily taken up by plants. Cd, one of the toxic metals, has a high transfer factor from soil to edible products and has a particularly elevated transfer factor from soil to the edible parts of food products. , Accumulation of Cd in food crops, including potatoes, vegetables, and cereals, is widely being investigated. Approximately 799 million tons of wheat are produced worldwide each year, which provides the main food for over half of the world’s population. , Compared to other cereals, wheat has a greater capacity for Cd accumulation, principally through the roots, which can then move to the upper regions and eventually accumulate in the wheat grains. Wheat is an important staple food that can accumulate high concentrations of cadmium in its grain, posing a risk to human health. When grown on Cd-contaminated soils, wheat can easily accumulate Cd, leading to growth retardation, an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), impaired photosynthesis, and nutrient imbalance. − Because of the plant’s physiological deteriorations, there is a reduction in plant growth and grain yield. Cd accumulation may also differ depending on wheat species; durum wheat accumulates a higher Cd concentration as compared to bread wheat. − Zinc is an essential micronutrient for plant growth and development, but nearly half of the world’s cereal-growing land suffers from inadequate zinc availability for plant roots. Wheat grain is the most important source of calorie intake in several countries in the developing world. Wheat’s naturally low zinc concentration (about 20–30 mg kg–1 of wheat grain) prevents it from meeting the recommended daily requirement for zinc. Therefore, increasing the Zn concentration in wheat to 40–60 mg kg–1, especially in the grain, through agricultural practices (biofortification) is an important global situation. Such an improvement will not only improve the nutritional Zn status of millions of people but also contribute to the reduction of Cd accumulation in wheat grain, which is harmful to humans. Zinc and cadmium have a similar chemical structure, and zinc can compete with cadmium for uptake by plants. These metals can have antagonistic or synergistic interactions depending on their concentration and based on the type of plant. These interactions can affect wheat growth, nutrient uptake, and Cd accumulation in grain. Soil and foliar applications of Zn have been shown to reduce cadmium levels in wheat leaves and stems, but their effects on grain Cd are less clear. It is important to understand these interactions to develop effective strategies to minimize the adverse effects of these metals in agricultural soils and to ensure sustainable agricultural practices.

Therefore, contamination of agricultural soils with cadmium poses a significant problem for the environment and food safety due to its high toxicity and bioaccumulation potential. Wheat, which is a staple food for more than half of the world’s population, can accumulate significant amounts of Cd in its grains when grown on contaminated soil, posing a direct threat to human health. Effective management strategies are therefore essential to minimize Cd accumulation in wheat and ensure sustainable food production. In this context, Zn plays a crucial role as it has similar chemical properties to Cd and can compete for uptake into plants, which could reduce Cd accumulation. The main objective of this study is to evaluate the effect of combined soil and foliar Zn application on reducing Cd accumulation in wheat grains and improving nutrient balance under Zn-deficient and Cd-contaminated soil conditions. We hypothesize that the combined application of soil and foliar Zn will more effectively reduce Cd accumulation in wheat grains compared to soil Zn or foliar Zn application alone. This study specifically investigates the role of combined soil and foliar Zn application in reducing Cd uptake in wheat grown on Zn-depleted and Cd-contaminated soils. Furthermore, the study was conducted on calcareous soils where Zn availability is naturally limited due to high pH and CaCO3 content. Demonstrating the effectiveness of integrated Zn applications under such chemically constrained conditions provides valuable insights into nutrient management in challenging soil environments, which are common in arid and semiarid regions. This integrative approach highlights a novel perspective within the scope of HM–micronutrient interactions and aims to contribute to sustainable wheat production under multistress conditions.

Materials and Methods

Plant Growth Conditions and Experimental Setup

The experiment was conducted under greenhouse conditions in Turkey (37°03′21″ N, 35°21′18″ E, 127 m a.s.l.) from October to April. The average day/night temperatures were 21/10 °C, the relative humidity was 60% ± 5%, and the natural daylight duration was approximately 11/13 h (day/night). The experimental design was a completely randomized block trial with a factorial combination of three soil Zn levels (low: 0.5 mg Zn kg– 1, adequate: 5.0 mg Zn kg– 1, and high: 10.0 mg Zn kg– 1) and two foliar Zn treatments (control, 0.0%; and foliar Zn spray, 0.3% ZnSO4-7H2O). Four increasing Cd doses (0.0, 0.2, 1.0, and 5.0 mg Cd kg– 1 soil) were applied with four replicates per treatment (72 experimental units in total).

Preparation of the Soil and Chemical Analyses

Before potting, the soil samples were analyzed for pH (1:2.5 soil–water), organic matter (OM) content texture (hydrometer method), available phosphorus, and DTPA-extractable Zn and Cd concentrations according to ref . The experimental soil was classified as clay-textured, alkaline in reaction (pH 8.08), and with a high calcium carbonate content (14.2% CaCO3). The OM content was considerably low (0.70%). The DTPA-extractable Zn concentration (0.10 mg kg–1) indicated a severe zinc deficiency, while the DTPA-extractable Cd content (0.005 mg kg–1) represented a background concentration, suggesting slight contamination. The electrical conductivity of the soil was low (0.22 dS m–1), classifying it as nonsaline. These characteristics reflect a chemically challenging environment for the bioavailability of micronutrients, especially in the presence of HM contamination (Table S1 ).

Prior to sowing, basal fertilizers were thoroughly mixed with the soil: 100 mg P kg–1 as KH2PO4, 125 mg K kg–1 as K2SO4, 300 mg N kg–1 as Ca­(NO3)2, and 2.5 mg Fe kg–1 as FeEDTA. Cadmium (Cd), zinc (Zn), and the base fertilizers were homogenized evenly throughout the soil to ensure uniform nutrient distribution before the experiment began.

Planting and Growth

Ten seeds of durum wheat (Triticum durum cv. Diyarbakır 81) were sown per pot (diameter 15 cm, height 25 cm), which was filled with 3.25 kg of prepared soil. After emergence, the plants were thinned to five per pot. Daily irrigation was carried out with deionized water to maintain a field capacity of 60–70% throughout the growing period.

Foliar Zn Application

Foliar Zn treatments were performed using 0.3% (w/v) ZnSO4·7H2O solution, prepared in deionized water containing 0.01% Tween 20 as a surfactant to enhance leaf absorption. The spraying volume was standardized to 20 mL per pot, applied uniformly to the entire leaf surface using a hand-held sprayer. Applications were conducted at the flowering stage (51 days after sowing) and repeated twice at 5-day intervals (total of three applications), in the early morning to reduce evaporation losses. Control plants were sprayed with deionized water containing Tween 20.

Harvesting and Sample Preparation

At full maturity (149 days after sowing), plants were harvested. Spikes were manually separated, and grains were oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight for grain yield determination. Grain samples from each pot were finely ground using an agate mortar and pestle.

Elemental Analysis and Quality Control

For metal analysis, 0.25 g of ground grain sample was digested in a microwave digestion system with 2 mL of 30% H2O2 and 5 mL of 65% HNO3. Cd and micronutrient (Zn, Fe, Mn, and Cu) concentrations were measured by ICP-OES (Varian Vista-PRO, Australia). Total N was determined by the Kjeldahl method. A certified reference material (SRM 1567B, wheat flour) was used to verify accuracy, with recovery rates within 97–103%. Analytical blanks and triplicate samples were included for quality control.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted by using JMP (12.0.1) (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). The significance of treatment effects was evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA). Then, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test (p < 0.05) was used as a posthoc test to determine significant differences between means.

Results

Effect of Soil and Foliar Zinc on Grain Yield

There was an interaction between soil Zn and foliar Zn supplies on grain yield (p < 0.05) (Figure ). In soil with low Zn, increasing Cd contamination, grain yield of durum wheat was increased by both soil Zn and foliar Zn applications. Among the Cd treatments, the lowest grain yield was observed with Cd0 (4.29 ± 1.64 g plant–1), while the highest grain yield was obtained with a combination of a high Zn application to the soil and a foliar application (8.21 ± 0.78 g plant–1). In the Cd0.2 treatment, the lowest grain yield (4.26 ± 0.09 g plant–1) was obtained from a low soil Zn application, while the highest yield was obtained from a high soil Zn application supplemented with foliar Zn (8.14 ± 1.83 g plant–1). Similarly, grain yield in the Cd1 treatment was lowest (4.06 ± 0.30 g plant–1) with low soil Zn application, and the highest grain yield (7.64 ± 0.34 g plant–1) was obtained with both high soil and foliar Zn application. At the highest Cd concentration (Cd5), the lowest grain yield was obtained with insufficient soil Zn application (3.89 ± 0.21 g plant–1), while the highest grain yield (7.22 ± 0.38 g plant–1) was obtained with high soil Zn application supplemented by foliar Zn application (Table S2). As shown in Figure , despite the increasing Cd contamination of the soils, the highest grain yields were achieved with a high Zn soil application supplemented by a Zn foliar application. While there were no statistically significant differences within this group, significant differences in grain yield were observed when compared to low and sufficient Zn applications.

Zinc and Cadmium Concentrations in Grain

Under increasing Cd-contaminated conditions, Cd and Zn concentrations in grain were significantly affected (<0.05) by soil Zn and foliar Zn treatments. Based on the Cd doses applied to the soil, the Zn concentrations in the wheat grains in the Cd0 treatment were only reached at a low Zn soil application (21.50 ± 2.27 mg kg–1), while the highest Zn concentration in the grains was reached at an adequate Zn soil application combined with a Zn foliar application (56.43 ± 2.70 mg kg–1). For the Cd0 dose, although an increase was observed with a high Zn soil application and an additional Zn foliar application compared to a low soil and Zn foliar application, the Zn concentrations in the grains remained below those achieved with an adequate Zn application. In the Cd0.2 treatment groups, the lowest Zn concentration in grains was obtained with inadequate soil Zn application (19.92 ± 1.12 mg kg–1). In contrast, the highest Zn concentration was obtained with high soil and foliar Zn application (52.42 ± 5.44 and 52.35 ± 6.40 mg kg–1, respectively). In the Cd1 treatments, the lowest grain Zn concentration was obtained with insufficient soil Zn application (17.67 ± 1.49 mg kg–1), while the highest grain Zn concentration was obtained with high soil Zn application and additional foliar Zn application (54.64 ± 5.26 mg kg–1). In the group with the highest soil Cd concentration, Cd5, the lowest grain Zn concentration was found with insufficient soil Zn fertilization (14.04 ± 1.39 mg kg–1), and the highest grain Zn concentrations increased with increasing Zn application (Table S2). As Figure a shows, Zn concentrations in the grains increased statistically significantly with sufficient and high Zn fertilization in the soil and additional foliar Zn fertilization despite increasing Cd doses in the soil.

As shown in Figure b, the Cd concentrations in the grains showed statistically significant differences between the Cd doses in the soil and the Zn application levels (p < 0.05). In the Cd0 groups, the lowest Cd concentration in the grains was achieved with insufficient soil Zn fertilization (49.97 ± 4.26 μg kg–1). The highest Cd concentration in the grains was observed with sufficient Zn fertilization of the soil alone (45.29 ± 1.68 μg kg–1). The Cd concentrations in the grains varied depending on the increasing Cd concentration in the soil and the level of Zn fertilization. In the Cd0.2 dose group, the lowest Cd concentration in the grains was obtained with sufficient soil Zn fertilization and additional foliar Zn fertilization (118.31 ± 4.76 μg kg–1), while the highest Cd concentration was observed with high soil Zn fertilization alone (238.89 ± 24.84 μg kg–1). However, a decrease in grain Cd concentration (182.12 ± 6.64 μg kg–1) was observed when high soil Zn fertilization was supplemented with foliar Zn fertilization. One of the interesting findings of this study was that adequate and high soil Zn fertilization and supplemental foliar Zn applications resulted in a statistically significant reduction in grain Cd concentration when soil Cd levels increased. In wheat grown on soils treated with Cd1 and Cd5, the highest grain Cd concentrations were found with inadequate Zn fertilization (1224.32 ± 47.55 and 3153.72 ± 207.55 μg kg–1), while the lowest Cd concentrations in the grains were obtained with high Zn fertilization of the soil in combination with Zn foliar applications (631.48 ± 81.39 and 2460.93 ± 554.41 μg kg–1) (Table S2). Importantly, when compared to international food safety standards, these results show that Cd concentrations in cereals under Cd0 (49.97 μg kg– 1; 0.050 mg kg– 1) and Cd0.2 (118.31 μg kg– 1; 0.118 mg kg– 1) are below the maximum permissible level (PL) (0.20 mg kg– 1 = 200 μg kg– 1) set by both the Codex Alimentarius and the Chinese national standard GB 2762-2022. In contrast, the Cd levels in the cereal grains at the Cd1 and Cd5 doses significantly exceeded the PL (631.48 and 2460.93 μg kg– 1, respectively), even with combined soil and foliar Zn application. Nevertheless, additional foliar Zn significantly reduced the extent of exceedance compared to insufficient Zn fertilization. This shows that Zn management plays a crucial role not only in reducing Cd uptake but also in maintaining food safety.

Macronutrient and Micronutrient Concentrations in Grain

In plants grown on soils with different Cd concentrations, statistically significant differences were found in the N, P, and K concentrations of the grains in relation to the macronutrient concentration (p < 0.05) (Figure ). The lowest %N concentration of the grain was achieved with insufficient Zn fertilization of the soil, while the highest %N concentrations were obtained with high Zn fertilization in combination with foliar applications. The %N concentration decreased with increasing Cd concentration in the soil (Table S1). Treatments Cd0, Cd0.2, Cd1, and Cd5 had the lowest %N concentrations (2.06 ± 0.05, 1.93 ± 0.04, 1.72 ± 0.04, and 1.56 ± 0.07%, respectively), while the highest %N concentrations were recorded (3.38 ± 0.12, 3.11 ± 0.05, 2.95 ± 0.06, and 2.76 ± 0.09, respectively). For P and K concentrations, the highest values were obtained with insufficient soil Zn fertilization, while the values decreased with increasing soil Zn fertilization and foliar applications. The lowest P and K concentrations were found at the highest soil Zn fertilization in combination with foliar applications. The lowest P concentrations (0.22 ± 0.01, 0.23 ± 0.01, 0.21 ± 0.02, and 0.20 ± 0.02%, respectively) and the highest P concentrations (0.42 ± 0.02, 0.37 ± 0.02, 0.37 ± 0.02, and 0.38 ± 0.02, respectively) were found in the Cd0, Cd0.2, Cd1, and Cd5 treatments (Figure ). Similarly, the lowest grain K concentrations (0.32 ± 0.02, 0.32 ± 0.03, 0.32 ± 0.02, and 0.30 ± 0.02%, respectively) were obtained with high Zn additions, while the highest K concentrations (0.38 ± 0.03, 0.39 ± 0.01, 0.36 ± 0.02, and 0.35 ± 0.01%, respectively) were recorded with deficient Zn additions (Table S2). Statistically significant differences were observed in the macro- and micronutrient concentrations of wheat grain as a function of foliar Zn fertilization and soil Zn fertilization at different soil Cd levels (p < 0.05) (Figure ). While the Fe and Cu concentrations in the grain were insufficient at low Zn fertilization, the highest concentrations were achieved at high soil Zn fertilization in combination with foliar Zn fertilization. The Fe and Cu concentrations increased depending on the Zn fertilization and the additional Zn foliar fertilization (Table S2). In soil group Cd0, the lowest Fe and Cu concentrations (33.5 ± 1.29 and 4.29 ± 0.15 mg kg–1, respectively) were observed with insufficient Zn fertilization, while the highest concentrations (57.50 ± 1.29 and 7.13 ± 0.17 mg kg–1, respectively) were observed with high Zn fertilization in combination with Zn foliar fertilization. For soil groups Cd0.2, Cd1, and Cd5, the lowest Fe concentrations (32.5 ± 1.29, 30.75 ± 1.71, and 27.50 ± 1.29 mg kg–1, respectively) were observed with insufficient Zn fertilization, while the highest Fe concentrations were obtained with the highest Zn fertilization in combination with foliar Zn applications (55.75 ± 2.63, 57.00 ± 2.00, and 56.5 ± 1.29 mg kg–1, respectively). Cu concentrations were also lowest with insufficient Zn fertilization (4.06 ± 0.07, 3.88 ± 0.07, and 3.54 ± 0.06 mg kg–1, respectively), while the highest values were obtained with high soil Zn fertilization in combination with foliar applications (6.84 ± 0.11, 6.31 ± 0.05, and 6.07 ± 0.19 mg kg–1, respectively). With increasing Zn addition, a decrease in the Mn concentration in the grains was observed. The highest Mn concentrations in the grains were obtained with insufficient Zn fertilization of the soil, while the lowest Mn concentrations were recorded with high Zn fertilization of the soil in combination with Zn foliar applications. However, no statistically significant differences in Mg concentrations were observed between the different Cd applications and soil Zn fertilization and foliar Zn application.

Discussion

In this study, soil Zn and soil Zn+foliar Zn applications were shown to have positive effects on grain yield and reduction of Cd accumulation in the grain of durum wheat grown in calcareous soils contaminated with Cd and Zn deficiency. Moreover, this study also indicated that low levels of Zn application in soil were low (0.5 mg Zn kg–1) and that Zn deficiency was effectively corrected by foliar Zn application. The results of this study highlight the significant interaction of soil and foliar applications of Zn on durum wheat grain yield in the context of contamination. Specifically, the data indicate that both soil and foliar applications of Zn are essential for achieving optimum grain yields in Cd-contaminated soils, with the highest yields obtained when high levels of Zn were applied as both soil and foliar applications. For instance, in soils without Cd contamination, foliar application of Zn increased grain yield by 49.75% compared to inadequate Zn fertilization in soil alone. In addition, with increasing Cd5 concentration, additional foliar Zn fertilization led to a 32.79% increase in grain yield compared to inadequate Zn fertilization. With adequate Zn fertilization of the soil, the increase in grain yield in the Cd0 groups was 5.44%. In the group with the highest Cd application (Cd5), an additional Zn foliar application led to an increase in grain yield of 78.45% compared to a pure Zn fertilization in the soil. In the Cd0 group, Zn foliar fertilization led to an increase in grain yield of 91.17% compared to a high Zn fertilization in the soil. Interestingly, this effect was observed in the Cd5 group with an increase of 19.07%. When the Cd concentration of the grains was evaluated in relation to the yield increase, the Cd concentration in the Cd0 groups decreased by 70.49% compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization, while this reduction was 9.31% in the high Cd5 groups. Compared to adequate soil Zn fertilization, additional foliar Zn application in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups resulted in a 67.44 and 7.02% reduction in grain Cd concentration, respectively. Compared to a high Zn fertilization of the soil, additional foliar Zn application reduced the Cd concentration in the grain by 15.99 and 11.35% in the Cd0 and Cd5 soil treatments, respectively. In contrast, grain Zn concentration increased by 77.67 and 173.06% in the Cd0 and Cd5 foliar Zn treatments, respectively, compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization. Compared to adequate soil Zn fertilization, an additional foliar Zn application resulted in a 59.99% increase in Zn concentration in the Cd0 group, while an 8.49% decrease in grain Zn concentration was observed in the Cd5 group. Compared to high soil Zn fertilization, additional foliar Zn application resulted in a 5.72% decrease in Zn concentration in the Cd0 groups and a 1.43% increase in the Cd5 treatments. These results suggest that while the grain Zn concentration may be increased under Cd stress at appropriate Zn levels, additional foliar Zn applications at higher Cd levels did not result in the expected increases compared to appropriate and high Zn applications. Therefore, a correlation analysis was performed.

The correlation analysis revealed that both foliar treatments (R = 0.720**) and soil Zn treatments (R = 0.374**) showed a highly significant positive correlation with grain yield at a statistical level of p < 0.001, while they showed a negative correlation with cadmium concentration in grain. This negative correlation was statistically significant for Zn applications in soil (R = −0.086, p < 0.05). However, although foliar Zn application also showed a negative correlation (R = −0.048), it was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the Zn concentration in the grain showed a highly significant positive correlation with Zn application in the soil (R = 0.74, p < 0.001) and with Zn application via the leaves (R = 0.346, p ≤ 0.001). Cadmium can inhibit Zn uptake, leading to Zn deficiency and further exacerbating Cd toxicity. In addition, foliar application of zinc has been reported to be an effective strategy to mitigate Cd toxicity in plants, as it enriches Zn concentration in shoots and grains while reducing Cd concentration in these tissues. Experimental studies have shown that the chemical similarity between cadmium and zinc minerals in soil facilitates their uptake as divalent cations in plants. Depending on the concentration of the two metals, this uptake can be synergistic, additive, or antagonistic, depending on the concentration of the two metals. While low Zn concentrations help to mitigate Cd-induced oxidative stress, higher Zn concentrations can enhance Cd-induced stress due to synergistic effects. Conversely, studies also suggest an antagonistic relationship between the uptake of Cd and Zn ions in plants. , This is attributed to the similar chemical properties of cadmium and zinc, which allow Zn to act as a competing ion for Cd uptake by the roots and possibly interact with Cd in the plant’s transport system. , In summary, the presence of cadmium can reduce zinc uptake, potentially leading to zinc deficiency in plants. In particular, the results of this study show that the combination of high soil Zn application and additional foliar Zn application significantly increased grain yield even at high Cd levels, emphasizing the protective role of Zn against the detrimental effects of Cd on plant growth. Numerous studies have also reported that zinc reduces Cd uptake and translocation in wheat plants, ,− which supports the results of the present study. In addition, adequate Zn supply to plants can reduce Cd uptake by competing for transporters, thus reducing the amount of Cd transported to the shoots and grains. Zinc is important for the synthesis of phytochelatins, small peptides that bind Cd and are entrapped in vacuoles to prevent toxicity, which is critical for the detoxification of nonessential metals and metalloids such as cadmium and arsenic in plants and various other organisms. Zinc also plays a role in maintaining the structure and function of membrane systems in plant cells. When plants are exposed to Cd stress, foliar application of Zn can increase phytochelatin synthesis, thereby binding Cd and reducing its toxicity. Based on the study results, the lack of expected increase in Zn concentration in grains by additional foliar Zn applications, even with adequate and high soil Zn fertilization, could also be related to phytochelatin synthesis. The Zn buffer capacity of a cell is exceeded; Zn ions can bind abnormally to thiols or other functional groups and disrupt protein function. In addition, Zn ions can displace other essential metal ions from their binding sites. Thus, if the presence of Cd triggers excessive phytochelatin synthesis, then these chelating molecules may limit the uptake and transport of Zn and potentially prevent adequate Zn accumulation in wheat grains. In a hydroponic experiment with wild-type and two mutant plants exposed to Cd and Zn, the phytochelatin concentration was found to increase by 34% in roots, while it decreased to 28% in shoots. Similarly, in a cellular study, a significant reduction in Zn accumulation was observed, providing clear evidence that excess Zn2+ is bound by phytochelatins, with the in vitro formation of phytochelatin–Zn complexes documented. −

The Fe concentration of wheat grain increased under increased Cd stress with high Zn application. In the groups with inadequate Zn fertilization, the Fe concentration decreased with increasing Cd doses; however, additional foliar Zn application led to an increase in Fe concentration despite higher Cd doses in the soil. In the Cd0 groups, foliar Zn application resulted in a 23.13% increase in Fe concentration compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization, while this increase reached 69.09% in the Cd5 dose groups. With adequate soil Zn fertilization, the addition of foliar Zn resulted in an increase in Fe concentration of 51.49 and 61.19% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, compared to inadequate Zn fertilization. These increases were observed at 71.64 and 68.66% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, when foliar Zn application was combined with high soil Zn fertilization. The observation that inadequate Zn fertilization leads to a decrease in Fe concentration with increasing Cd dose is consistent with the literature. For example, Shoormij et al. (2022) have shown that combining Zn and Fe significantly increases micronutrient concentrations in the grain, suggesting that Zn plays a crucial role in improving Fe uptake, especially under stress conditions. This is consistent with the findings that additional foliar application of Zn can counteract the negative effects of Cd on Fe concentration. In addition, the synergistic effects of soil and foliar applications of Zn are well documented. Hafeez et al. (2021) found that applying Zn-based fertilizers improved wheat growth characteristics and grain quality, indicating that Zn not only increases the Fe concentration but also contributes to overall plant health. This is particularly important in the context of Cd stress, as Zn can mitigate the toxic effects of Cd on nutrient uptake.

The Cu concentration in wheat grain was highest in the Cd0 dose with insufficient soil Zn fertilization but decreased with increasing Cd doses. However, foliar Zn applications led to an increase in grain Cu concentration. With inadequate soil Zn fertilization, the grain Cu concentration decreased by 21.22% in the Cd5 group compared to the Cd0 group. However, when foliar Zn was applied, the grain Cu concentration increased by 35.56 and 36.56% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization. When adequate soil Zn fertilization was combined with foliar Zn application, increases of 46.85 and 24.68% were observed in the Cd0 and Cd5 doses, respectively, compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization. When high soil Zn fertilization was combined with foliar Zn application, these increases were 66.39 and 71.71% for the Cd0 and Cd5 doses, respectively. There was a statistically very significant (p < 0.001) and strong positive correlation (R = 0.715**) between Zn fertilization and Cu concentration in the grain. The statistically significant positive correlation (R = 0.715**) between Zn fertilization and Cu concentration in grain highlights the crucial role of Zn in regulating Cu concentration in wheat. This correlation is supported by studies that have shown that adequate Zn levels can improve the bioavailability of other micronutrients, including Cu, thereby improving plant health and productivity under stress conditions. Studies have shown, for example, that Cd can impair the absorption of essential micronutrients, including Cu, leading to reduced concentrations in plant tissues. , Particularly noteworthy are the results indicating that Zn foliar applications lead to an increase in Cu concentration in the grain. These results suggest that Zn plays a critical role in enhancing Cu availability and uptake, even under Cd stress. This is supported by research findings suggesting that Zn can mitigate the toxic effects of Cd and thereby improve the overall nutrient profile of crops. For example, Farhangi-Abriz and Ghassemi-Golezani (2022) have shown that Zn administration can improve the nutrient status and osmotic regulation of plants under HM stress, which could explain the observed increases in Cu concentration. The significant increases in Cu concentration when adequate soil Zn fertilization was combined with foliar Zn application for the Cd0 and Cd5 doses highlight the synergistic effects of these fertilization strategies. This result is consistent with the work of Ozkutlu (Ozkutlu, 2023), who reported that Zn application not only improved growth but also decreased Cd concentration in durum wheat, suggesting that Zn improves the plant’s ability to cope with Cd toxicity. The pronounced increases in Cu concentration at the Cd0 and Cd5 doses when high soil Zn fertilization was combined with foliar Zn application underline the importance of integrated nutrient management. This is in line with the findings of Dong et al. (2019), who found that supplements can significantly reduce Cd availability and uptake in plants, which increases their overall resilience to HM stress.

The results showed that foliar application of significantly increases the concentration in wheat grains, especially under stress, providing valuable insights into the role of micronutrients in improving crop quality. The increase in N concentration by 8.50% in the Cd0 group and by 10.77% in the Cd5 group with foliar Zn application compared to inadequate soil Zn fertilization highlights the importance of Zn for nutrient assimilation and plant metabolism. This result is consistent with previous studies emphasizing the role of Zn in increasing protein synthesis and amino acid concentration in wheat grains. The significant increases in N concentration observed with adequate soil Zn fertilization combined with foliar Zn application, 71.22 and 72.03% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, highlight the synergistic effects of these fertilization strategies. This is similar to the results of Arif et al., who reported that the interaction of Zn and nitrogen management significantly improved the grain quality and nutrient concentration of wheat. The combined application of soil and foliar Zn not only improves the N concentration but also contributes to overall plant health and productivity. In addition, the results show that the percentage N concentration increased by 64.34 and 77.65% when high soil Zn fertilization was combined with foliar Zn application in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, highlighting the crucial role of Zn in mitigating the negative effects of Cd on nutrient uptake. This is supported by the studies of Akram et al. who emphasized the synergistic relationship between nitrogen and zinc in improving nutrient uptake and nutrient turnover in wheat. The study suggests that the presence of Zn increases the efficiency of nitrogen-dependent proteins, which enables better N assimilation and improves overall grain quality. Also noteworthy is the positive correlation between Zn fertilization and %N concentration in the wheat grains. This correlation suggests that adequate Zn concentration is essential for optimizing N uptake and utilization in plants. Previous studies have shown that Zn deficiency can lead to impaired N metabolism, resulting in reduced protein synthesis and lower grain quality. The results of this study emphasize the importance of Zn for maintaining an optimal nutrient balance in wheat, especially under stress conditions.

The results of this study showed a statistically significant negative correlation between phosphorus (P) concentration in the wheat grains and fertilization in the soil and foliar, with correlation coefficients of R = −0.873 (p < 0.001) and R = −0.225 (p < 0.05), respectively. These results suggest that higher Zn levels, whether applied via soil or foliar, may affect P accumulation in wheat grains. The reduction in P concentration, 19.42 and 18.60% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, following foliar Zn application, is particularly noteworthy and is consistent with previous research indicating that excessive Zn levels can impair P uptake and metabolism in plants. The observed reductions in P concentration were even more pronounced when adequate soil Zn fertilization was supplemented with foliar Zn application, resulting in reductions of 72.92 and 80.43% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively. This significant decrease emphasizes the potential antagonistic relationship between Zn and P documented in various studies. For example, it has been reported that high Zn concentrations can inhibit P uptake by competing for binding sites in the root system, resulting in lower availability of P for plant uptake. , This phenomenon can be exacerbated in Cd-contaminated environments, where HM pollution can further complicate nutrient interactions. In addition, the results showed that high soil Zn fertilization combined with foliar Zn application resulted in an 88.64 and 96.15% reduction in P concentration in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, implying that excessive Zn application can lead to a nutrient imbalance that negatively affects crop yield and quality. Previous studies have also found that high Zn levels can lead to nutrient deficiencies, especially in P and other important micronutrients.

The results of this study showed that an increasing Cd load in combination with a Zn fertilization in the soil and an additional Zn foliar application significantly reduced the Mn concentration in wheat grains. The highest Mn concentration was observed in the Cd0 group with insufficient soil Zn fertilization, suggesting that lower Zn levels allow better Mn uptake under nonstressed conditions. This result is consistent with previous research indicating that micronutrient interactions can influence plants’ accumulation of essential elements. Singh and Sandhu (2021) reported that Zn fertilization promotes growth, which can indirectly support the uptake of other micronutrients, such as Mn. The significant reduction in Mn concentration was 72.90 and 37.11% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, when sufficient soil Zn was combined with foliar Zn application, illustrating the antagonistic relationship between Zn and Mn. This is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. (2021), who found that Cd stress can disrupt nutrient uptake mechanisms, resulting in reduced accumulation of essential micronutrients. The negative correlation (R = −0.873, p < 0.001) between soil-applied Zn fertilization and Mn concentration further supports the hypothesis that excessive Zn can inhibit Mn uptake, a phenomenon that has been documented in various studies. Under conditions of high soil Zn fertilization combined with foliar Zn application, the observed decreases in Mn concentration of 112.64 and 46.15% in the Cd0 and Cd5 groups, respectively, are particularly concerned. These results suggest that high levels of Zn may exacerbate the negative effects of Cd on Mn accumulation, potentially leading to severe nutrient deficiencies in wheat grains. This is corroborated by Alotaibi et al. (2023), which emphasized the importance of balanced micronutrient applications to avoid detrimental interactions that can compromise plant health and yield. Interestingly, while a negative correlation was also observed with foliar Zn application (R = −0.185, p > 0.05), this relationship was not statistically significant. This may indicate that the effects of foliar Zn on Mn concentration are less pronounced than those of soil-applied Zn, suggesting that soil nutrient status plays a more important role in determining Mn availability. This is consistent with the findings of Ni et al. (2018), who reported that exogenous application of micronutrients can mitigate the negative effects of Cd stress but cannot fully offset the negative effects of high soil Zn concentration.

The results of this study demonstrate the importance of integrated nutrient management strategies that combine soil and foliar applications of Zn to improve grain yield and nutrient quality of wheat, especially on Cd-contaminated soils. The protective role of Zn against Cd toxicity, as well as its ability to improve the uptake of other essential micronutrients, emphasizes the need for careful consideration of micronutrient interactions in agricultural practice. Future research should focus on optimizing Zn fertilization strategies to maximize crop resilience and nutrient quality in the face of environmental stressors. In addition, future studies are recommended to investigate the long-term effects of combined Zn applications on soil health, microbial community structure, and possible residual effects on subsequent crop rotations. Field trials are also needed to validate the results from the greenhouse under real agricultural conditions, including different climates and soil types. Research into the optimal timing and frequency of foliar Zn applications and interactions with other micronutrients will provide practical insights to improve plant nutrition and reduce the risk of Cd contamination of wheat grains. Nevertheless, it should be noted that although the Cd concentration in grain was significantly reduced by Zn foliar application, the values at higher Cd soil doses were still above the maximum level (0.20 mg kg– 1) specified in both Codex Alimentarius and Chinese standard GB 2762-2022. This indicates that Zn management alone may not be sufficient to ensure food safety under elevated soil Cd conditions. This emphasizes the need for additional integrated strategies such as soil amendments, crop selection, and pH management.

Conclusions

This study investigates the role of soil and foliar application of zinc in reducing the accumulation of cadmium in wheat grains grown on Zn-depleted and Cd-contaminated soils. Specifically, the effects of increasing Cd doses (0, 0.2, 1.0, and 5.0 mg Cd kg–1 soil) on wheat grain quality were investigated. The results showed that the application of Zn significantly attenuated Cd uptake, with the Cd concentration in the wheat grains being significantly reduced at all treatment levels. Thus, applying Zn in both soil and foliar at the highest Cd dose of 5.0 mg kg–1 resulted in a significant reduction in Cd concentration, which improved the nutritional quality and safety of the wheat for human consumption. Moreover, this result underlines the protective role of Zn against Cd toxicity and improves growth and yield even under stress conditions. Furthermore, the interaction between Zn and Cd illustrates complex nutrient dynamics. Zn application promoted an increase in essential micronutrients such as Fe and Cu, while the Cd concentration decreased the uptake of Zn and Fe, emphasizing the antagonistic relationship between these metals. The decreased P and manganese Mn concentration in the grains at high Zn treatments indicates a possible nutrient imbalance and highlights the need for balanced fertilization strategies. These results are consistent with previous research findings on the role of Zn in reducing Cd uptake by competing for root uptake, thus lowering Cd concentrations in the edible parts of grains and improving overall grain quality. This study confirms that the application of Zn can address nutrient deficiencies in wheat-dependent populations while contributing to public health by reducing Cd transfer in the food chain. Combining soil and foliar applications of Zn is a viable approach to mitigate HM stress in crops. Future research should focus on optimizing these nutrient interactions to ensure sustainable crop production and improved food safety in regions facing similar environmental challenges.

Importantly, the fact that this study was conducted under calcareous soil conditionswhere Zn bioavailability is naturally limited due to high pH and calcium carbonate contentmakes the demonstrated effectiveness of integrated Zn applications particularly valuable. These findings contribute to the development of micronutrient management strategies for calcareous soils, which are widely prevalent in arid and semiarid regions.
