Central Message

The hinotori-DRATS, using a specific 3-arm configuration with the hinotori system, enables safe, reduced-port lung resection by overcoming arm-collision challenges via vertical port separation.

The hinotori Surgical Robot System (Medicaroid Corporation) is the first Japanese-manufactured robotic platform. Although this system features 8-axis arms and a docking-free design, the narrower arm spacing (approximately 7 cm) compared with that of the da Vinci system (Intuitive Surgical) presents considerable challenges for reduced-port surgery. The primary issue is not merely the risk of physical collision but also the system's sensitive collision-avoidance mechanism (“error-induced freeze”), which frequently interrupts the workflow when the arms are in proximity.1 Although dual-portal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (DRATS) and its nonassistant operation variant (neoDRATS) have been established for the da Vinci system,2,3 adapting these for the hinotori system requires modification to address these spatial constraints. To address this problem, we developed “hinotori-DRATS,” a novel technique that uses a specific 3-arm configuration to facilitate safe anatomical lung resection.

This study was exempt from institutional review board approval according to the guidelines of Kindai University Hospital. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Port Placement

Patients are placed in the lateral decubitus position under general anesthesia with single-lung ventilation without carbon dioxide insufflation. To maximize assistant access, the hinotori-DRATS uses 2 “windows” (wound retractors).

1.First window (working port): A 4- to 5-cm incision is made in the fifth or sixth intercostal space (ICS) along the anterior-to-middle axillary line using an Alexis Wound Retractor XS (Applied Medical). This window accommodates the camera and the instrument.

Comment

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to present details of a reduced-port strategy, specifically designated as hinotori-DRATS, for application of the hinotori surgical system. Although DRATS and neoDRATS performed using the da Vinci Xi system can effectively use 4 arms, physical constraints associated with the hinotori system necessitate a modified approach.

We successfully performed anatomical resections, including lobectomies and segmentectomies, using hinotori-DRATS. Given the necessity for these adaptations, the question may arise as to why we wish to adapt DRATS for application using the hinotori system when the technique is already established for the da Vinci system. The answer lies in the unique benefits of the hinotori platform.

First, compared with existing systems, the hinotori system has distinct advantages, including 8-axis arms that mimic human arm movements for smoother articulation and a docking-free design that simplifies setup and reduces pressure on the patient's thoracic wall.1 Furthermore, Krauss and colleagues4 have demonstrated that the hinotori cockpit has superior ergonomic properties, specifically enabling a neck posture that falls within the recommended guidelines, thereby reducing surgeon fatigue.

However, to fully realize these benefits in a reduced-port setting, it will initially be necessary to resolve the issue of “error-induced freeze.” As noted by Suda and colleagues1, 4 and Krauss and colleagues,1, 4 the safety alarms of the system can lead to excessive stoppages during crowded 4-arm operations. The hinotori-DRATS addresses this problem by adopting a 3-arm configuration, thereby increasing external arm spacing and eliminating alarm-related interruptions, while maintaining the system's ergonomic and mechanical advantages.

An additional drawback is that the hinotori system currently lacks dedicated robotic staplers, and, accordingly, vascular division depends on the assistant. In this regard, we have adopted the “trocarless” technique described by Nakamura and colleagues,5 using soft wound retractors instead of rigid trocars. This provides the assistant with ample freedom to use standard staplers and retractors, effectively compensating for the absence of the robotic fourth arm.

We demonstrated that hinotori-DRATS is adaptable to both the right and left thoracic cavities, with the key technical modification being the placement of the second window (the seventh to eighth ICS on the right and the sixth to seventh ICS on the left). This adjustment accounts for anatomical differences (the position of the liver relative to the heart) and ensures optimal internal triangulation while maintaining external arm clearance. Regarding lower-lobe procedures, the lack of carbon dioxide insufflation can occasionally make visualization challenging in patients with a high-riding diaphragm. In such cases, the assistant plays a crucial role in retracting the diaphragm using the access provided by the 2 windows.

We acknowledge that this report is primarily a description of the surgical technique. A detailed comparison of clinical outcomes between the hinotori platform and existing robotic platforms will be included in future studies.

Conclusions

The hinotori Surgical Robot System is a promising platform that enables safe, effective surgeries comparable with those achieved with existing systems. The hinotori-DRATS is a viable reduced-port technique for both right and left lung resections. By adjusting the placement of the second window and using a 3-arm configuration, surgeons can take advantage of the system's docking-free design, while managing its spatial limitations.

Video 1

Modified dual-portal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (DRATS) for right segment 2 (S2) segmentectomy This video demonstrates a reduced-port anatomical lung resection using a Japanese-manufactured robotic platform. A 2.0-cm pure-solid lung nodule, suspicious for lung cancer, was identified in the right upper lobe. On the basis of the Japanese Oncology Group 0802 trial criteria, a segmentectomy was planned after careful preoperative evaluation using 3-dimensional computed tomography imaging. The surgical access uses a “2-window” approach: a 5-cm working port (first window) in the fifth intercostal space (ICS) and a 2-cm accessory port (second window) in the seventh ICS. This vertical port separation and a modified 3-arm configuration effectively overcome the robotic platform's spatial constraints and sensitive collision-avoidance mechanism. This setup ensures optimal triangulation and enables the bedside assistant to perform suction, retraction, and stapling through both windows, similar to the maneuvers in uniportal VATS. The procedure begins with the division of the fissure using electrocautery to expose the central vein. The ascending A2 artery is exposed, and its proximal side is ligated with a hinotori surgical clip before division. Peribronchial lymph nodes are dissected, and the B2 and B3 bronchi are identified peripherally from the apical side. Due to the current lack of dedicated robotic staplers for this platform, a standard video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) surgical stapler is used through the windows to divide the B2 bronchus. The intersegmental plane is clarified by identifying V2c, which runs between S2 and S3. The recurrent A2 artery is identified as it runs toward the distal B2 stump and is divided using a stapler through the first window. After the vascular and bronchial divisions, indocyanine green (ICG) is injected and visualized using the system's integrated fluorescence mode to confirm the intersegmental plane of S2. Finally, the intersegmental plane is divided with a surgical stapler, followed by an upper mediastinal lymph node dissection, which the robotic system performs with precision comparable to existing platforms. This 2-incision approach aims to minimize postoperative pain and facilitate rapid recovery. Video available at: https://www.jtcvs.org/article/S2666-2507(26)00303-2/fulltext.
