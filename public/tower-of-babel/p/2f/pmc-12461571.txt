Targeted Gene Sequencing in a Male Adult Diagnosed With X-Linked Osteoporosis Due to a Novel p.(Arg398Profs*2) PLS3 Variant
Authors: Sylvia Ye, Tashunka Taylor-Miller, Vivian Grill, Peter R Ebeling, Hanh H Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic loss-of-function variants in the plastin-3 gene (PLS3), encoding plastin-3 protein, are associated with early-onset X-linked osteoporosis. We present the case of a young adult male patient, with a history of multiple fragility fractures and blue sclerae, who was clinically diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) type 1 in childhood. He has been managed with intravenous bisphosphonate therapy, leading to an increase in bone density at the spine, stable bone density at the femoral neck, and a period free of fractures while on antiresorptive therapy. Two decades later, with a focus on reproductive family planning, a novel PLS3 variant was identified on genetic testing. This case report highlights an important role for genetic testing in patients with early-onset osteoporosis or a clinical diagnosis of OI. With the emergence of new targeted therapeutics and advanced reproductive options, such as preimplantation genetic testing, obtaining an accurate molecular diagnosis is key.Keywords: PLS3, X-linked osteoporosis, monogenic early-onset osteoporosis

Introduction

Osteoporosis in childhood and adolescence is defined as presence of ≥1 vertebral compression fractures occurring in the absence of systemic or local disease or high-energy trauma, or else the presence of both a clinically significant fracture history (≥2 long-bone fractures by age 10 years, or ≥3 long-bone fractures by 19 years), and a low age- and gender-matched bone mineral density (BMD) Z-score of ≤ −2.0 [1, 2].

Several monogenic forms of primary osteoporosis have been described [3] (see Table 1), the most common form of inherited osteoporosis being osteogenesis imperfecta (OI). Classically, OI is considered a disorder of collagen 1 synthesis, but currently more than 23 additional genes have been identified involved in collagen 1 synthesis, modification or trafficking pathways, osteoblast formation/function or F-actin bundle formation [4, 8]. The historical Sillence clinical classification of OI has now been replaced by the Nosology of Skeletal Dysplasia classification, which includes useful clinical descriptors, such as moderate vs progressively deforming form, as well as the causative gene [9]. In most cases, the inheritance pattern of OI is autosomal dominant [10]. X-linked osteoporosis (XLO) was first described in 2013, linked to pathogenic variants in the plastin-3 gene (PLS3), encoding the plastin 3 protein [11, 12]. Plastin 3, expressed in all solid tissues, is a protein involved in F-actin-binding and bundling [13]. Dysregulation of PLS3 has been associated with skeletal manifestations, such as early-onset osteoporosis and osteoarthritis, as well as having important roles in extraskeletal diseases, including cancer, infection, and neurodegenerative disorders [13].

We present a case of a young adult male individual who was diagnosed with Sillence type 1 OI in childhood, however diagnostic genomic testing subsequently identified XLO due to a novel PLS3 pathogenic variant. This case report highlights the implications of genetic testing to accurately diagnose an underlying monogenic bone disorder in early-onset osteoporosis, and discusses the genetic and phenotypic characteristics of XLO, a rare heritable cause for primary osteoporosis.

Case Presentation

The male consultand was 34 years of age, born to non-consanguineous parents. He had achieved age-appropriate developmental milestones and had no vision or hearing issues. His first long-bone fracture was at 21 months of age. By age 16 years, he had sustained approximately 15 fragility fractures, including 3 vertebral crush fractures, and he was noted to have blue sclerae. He received a clinical diagnosis of OI Sillence type 1 OI, likely due to an autosomal dominant collagen 1 variant, at age 11 years.

The family had received counseling that classical OI was due to a collagen 1 genetic variant that was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Family history was significant for a paternal history of multiple fractures sustained during high-impact sports, as well as adult-onset hearing loss, but white sclerae. His mother had cataracts. One sister had 5 fractures and blue sclerae, while the other sister had one fracture and white sclerae. A paternal aunt with osteoporosis and multiple fractures had received a clinical diagnosis of OI (see pedigree Fig. 1).

Diagnostic Assessment

The patient presented for adult endocrinology specialist management at the age of 21 years. A dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan at the time revealed low bone mass at the spine (BMD 0.757 g/cm2; Z-score −3.2) and bone mass within the expected range for age at the femoral neck (BMD 0.740 g/cm2, Z-score −1.9). His most recent minimal trauma fracture was of a first toe in the previous year. Height and weight were 175.3 cm and 82.1 kg, respectively.

Treatment

He was subsequently commenced on intravenous bisphosphonate antiresorptive treatment with pamidronate every 6 months for 6 years, followed by 3 intermittent doses of intravenous zoledronic acid up until the age of 30 years. At the end of the treatment course, spinal bone mass increased by 6.6% over 10 years but remained low, (BMD 0.802 g/cm2, Z-score −2.6), while BMD remained stable at the femoral neck (BMD 0.737 g/cm2, Z −1.2). Bone turnover markers were suppressed with procollagen-1 N-terminal peptide 12 μg/L (15-115 μg/L) and C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen 51 ng/L (100-750 ng/L). He was free of fractures during the ages of 21 to 30 years, so bisphosphonate antiresorptive therapy was ceased.

At age 34, for the purposes of family planning, he and his partner were referred for genetic counseling. The consultand had exome sequencing and curation of 39 genes associated with OI and bone fragility which identified a novel likely pathogenic PLS3 variant—c.1192dupC p.(Arg398Profs*2) (see Table 2 for genomic information). Familial segregation identified the PLS3 variant to be maternally inherited.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At the age of 38 years, 8 years following his last intravenous bisphosphonate treatment, he remains fracture-free and his bone density remains stable (spine BMD 0.816 g/cm2, Z-score −2.4; left femoral neck BMD 0.725 g/cm2, Z-score −1.1).

Discussion

We describe a case with a rare monogenic form of primary osteoporosis caused by novel loss-of-function pathogenic variant in PLS3 c.1192dupC. Hemizygous loss-of-function variants in PLS3 result clinically in XLO, with long-bone and vertebral fractures, and childhood-onset low BMD, as well as extraskeletal features, including facial dysmorphism, joint hypermobility, blue-gray sclera, waddling myopathic gait, and hearing loss [11, 13, 14]. There has been one case reported of an adolescent male with opalescent teeth [15].

Heterozygous female carriers typically present with a milder phenotype, later onset of fractures, increased fracture risk compared to noncarriers, and osteoporosis [11, 16], but may also present with severe childhood-onset osteoporosis [17]. The mechanism for the variable expressivity is not known; possibly skewed X-inactivation may be a factor [13, 17]. Polymorphisms in PLS3 have been postulated as genetic risk factors in postmenopausal osteoporosis [18].

The PLS3 protein is expressed in all solid tissue and displays tissue-specific expression and dosage effects [13]. The main function of plastin-3 is in F-actin-binding and bundling, which are important in processes such as cell motility, adhesion, cell division endocytosis, neurotransmission, and vesicle trafficking [13]. Its effect on bone homeostasis is possibly through controlling mechanosensing in osteocytes, as well as regulating osteoblast and osteoclast activity, although the exact molecular mechanisms are not well understood [14]. PLS3 knockout mice models have shown reduced cortical thickness and possibly increased osteoclastic bone resorption [14]. Trans-iliac bone biopsies in humans show reduced trabecular bone volume and thickness, variable levels of bone turnover, and variable mineralization distribution properties [14, 15, 19, 20]. In a recent high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography study, adults with PLS3 variants have low trabecular and cortical BMD, and severely impaired trabecular and cortical microarchitecture, compared to age- and sex-matched controls [21]. Bone microarchitecture differences included smaller trabecular number, increased trabecular separation and reduced cortical thickness [21]. Conversely, increased PLS3 levels have been linked to nonosteoporotic conditions such as susceptibility to osteoarthritis, malignancy (colorectal, prostate, breast, acute myeloid leukemia), thoracic aortic dissection in mice [22], and facilitating pathogen entry into cells [13, 14].

Treatment with oral or parenteral bisphosphonates has been associated with increases in BMD at both lumbar spine and femoral neck, and likely leading to decreased fracture frequency [11, 23, 24]. A small uncontrolled trial of an anabolic agent, teriparatide, showing increased BMD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, with no new fractures in the 24-month intervention period included PLS3-related XLO cases [25]. However, histomorphometry of iliac crest biopsies showed more varied results, with decrease in osteoid surface and volume found in 2 of 3 participants with XLO. In our case, intermittent intravenous bisphosphonate therapy led to increases in spinal bone mass and attenuation of fragility fractures.

This case highlights how correct molecular genomic diagnosis facilitates appropriate familial segregation and management of affected individuals. The family history in this case of paternal fractures and a paternal aunt with osteoporosis supported the expected autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. However, with the identification of an X-linked cause of primary osteoporosis, the reproductive planning counseling is dramatically changed. With increasing accessibility to advanced genomic sequencing techniques [14], genetic testing should become standard of care for early-onset osteoporosis with a strong suspicion of a heritable cause. A genetic diagnosis has implications for screening for associated extraskeletal manifestations across the lifespan, as well as familial and reproductive genetic counseling. Furthermore, characterization of rare diseases increases our understanding of pathophysiology and responses to available therapeutic agents (antiresorptive or anabolic therapy), and may lead to targeted, gene-specific treatment regimens.

In summary, we present a novel pathogenic PLS3 variant with an X-linked osteoporosis phenotype with wider familial implications. The consultand was misclassified as OI presumed secondary to a collagen 1 variant. A period of bisphosphonate therapy resulted in reduced fracture frequency and increased BMD. Such cases highlight the important role of genetic testing to make an accurate molecular genomic diagnosis in individuals with early-onset bone fragility. A molecular genetic diagnosis may have targeted therapeutic implications and guide family planning.

Obtaining an accurate family history, genetic testing and accurate molecular genetic diagnosis is central to appropriate management and reproductive counseling for patients with osteoporosis and bone fragility conditions.
