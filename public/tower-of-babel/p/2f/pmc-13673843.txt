Prevalence and determinants of low anterior resection syndrome following rectal cancer resection: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Khadije Gorgi, Maryam Aramesh, Seyed Vahid Hosseini, Matin Emami, Sara Shojaei-Zarghani, Mohammad Mohammadianpanah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow anterior resection syndrome (LARS) is a debilitating bowel dysfunction that frequently follows sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of LARS and identify its associated determinants in a cohort of rectal cancer survivors.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, patients who underwent sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer at least one year prior were recruited from the Shiraz Colorectal Cancer Surgery (SCORCS) registry. Based on symptom severity assessed via the LARS score, patients were categorized as having no, minor, or major LARS. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to identify determinants associated with any LARS (minor or major vs. none) and major LARS (vs. no and minor combined).ResultsThe prevalence of LARS was 53.7% (58/108), comprising minor LARS in 31.5% and major LARS in 22.2% of patients. Multivariable analysis identified older age (odds ratio [OR]: 1.04, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.08) and neoadjuvant radiotherapy (OR: 4.38, 95% CI: 1.54–12.45) as determinants for any LARS. For major LARS, independent determinants were older age (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10) and female sex (OR: 2.97, 95% CI: 1.02–8.60). There was no significant difference in quality-of-life domains across LARS severity groups.ConclusionThis study confirms a high prevalence of LARS among rectal cancer survivors. Neoadjuvant radiotherapy, older age, and female sex were identified as determinants of LARS in this cohort; however, due to the study’s limitations, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-04071-z.Keywords: Low Anterior Resection Syndrome, Fecal Incontinence, Diarrhea, Rectal Neoplasms

Introduction

Rectal cancer was diagnosed in 729,833 new cases and was responsible for 343,817 deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. Sphincter-preserving procedures, such as low anterior resection (LAR), are established as the gold standard for the curative treatment of mid to upper rectal cancer. Furthermore, due to innovations in surgical techniques and instrumentation, LAR is now also routinely performed for lower rectal tumors [2]. Despite the undeniable benefit of avoiding a permanent ostomy and its associated complications, a significant number of patients experience troublesome bowel symptoms, including increased stool frequency, urgency, and fecal incontinence. This constellation of symptoms is collectively termed Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS). Although LARS was thought to diminish by 12 months postoperatively, evidence indicates that symptoms frequently persist long-term [3, 4]. Approximately 41% of patients experience major LARS—the most severe form of the syndrome—one year after undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer [5]. This high prevalence underscores LARS as a major, yet understudied, health issue, particularly given the growing population of rectal cancer survivors. The syndrome profoundly impairs quality of life (QoL), with social functioning being notably affected. This impact necessitates identifying high-risk patients preoperatively and providing them with adequate postoperative support [6].

Previous studies have suggested several possible risk factors for LARS, including radiotherapy, low tumor height, anastomotic leak, and the use of a diverting stoma [7]. However, the relative contribution of these factors and the role of other potential determinants—such as patient demographics and body mass index—remain incompletely characterized, with some studies reporting conflicting findings [8–10]. Given the current inconsistent evidence, the present study aims to assess the prevalence of LARS in patients who have undergone rectal cancer resection and to investigate its associated determinants.

Methods

In this cross-sectional study, we included patients with rectal cancer who underwent LAR, very low anterior resection (VLAR), or Ultra-low anterior resection (ULAR) between March 2023 and March 2024 at Shahid Faghihi and Mother and Child Hospitals in Shiraz, Iran. LAR was defined as anastomosis between the distal anterior peritoneal reflection and the proximal anorectal junction, whereas VLAR referred to anastomosis located in the lower third of the rectum, between the anorectal junction and the proximal anus. ULAR was defined as coloanal anastomosis [11]. Patient data were sourced from the Shiraz Colorectal Cancer Surgery (SCORCS) registry, which is an ongoing registry database collecting perioperative and oncological data of colorectal cancer patients undergoing surgery since 2007 [12]. Eligibility criteria were age over 18 years, being alive at the time of the study, and providing consent to complete an additional study-specific questionnaire. Patients were excluded if they had recurrence or metastasis (confirmed by radiological imaging), inflammatory bowel disease, familial adenomatous polyposis, a current stoma, or if they could not be reached by phone after three attempts in three different days. To assess the potential for selection bias, we compared the baseline characteristics of study participants and non-participants (those who were unreachable or declined participation) using data available from the SCORCS registry. All eligible patients provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Approval No: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1403.703) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

A list of eligible patients and their associated data—including demographics, contact information, surgical details, neoadjuvant treatment, and tumor characteristics—was extracted from the database. Additional data on quality of life and low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) score were collected via direct patient contact by trained personnel between February 2025 and June 2025. The QOL was assessed using the validated Persian version of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-C30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) [13, 14]. This instrument includes a global health status scale, five functional scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social), several symptom scales (fatigue, nausea and vomiting, pain, dyspnea, loss of appetite, insomnia, constipation, and diarrhea), and a single item on the financial impact of the disease. The LARS questionnaire, which consists of five items, was also completed by each patient. Based on the total score, patients were categorized as having no LARS (0–20 points), minor LARS (21–29 points), or major LARS (30–42 points) [15, 16].

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25). The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parametric data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed data as median (range). Categorical data are reported as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons were conducted using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. To identify determinants of LARS, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Two models were constructed: one comparing any LARS (minor and major combined) to no LARS, and another comparing major LARS to no and minor combined. Ae, sex, and variables with a P < 0.1 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 147 eligible patients who underwent rectal cancer resection during the study period, 39 were excluded for the following reasons: disease recurrence and metastasis (n = 7), death (n = 6), permanent stoma (n = 5), refusal to participate (n = 8), non-contact due to being unreachable by telephone (n = 12), and incomplete data (n = 1). Consequently, 108 patients were included in the final analysis (54% male; mean age 60.23 years). Of these, 51.9% underwent LAR and the remaining underwent VLAR (25%) or ULAR (23.1%). Surgically, 92% of procedures were performed laparoscopically, four were open (laparotomy), and five were conversions from laparoscopic to open surgery. All patients had an end-to-end stapled colorectal or coloanal anastomosis without any pouch. The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Comparison of baseline characteristics between participants and non-participants revealed no significant differences in age, sex, body mass index (BMI), neoadjuvant treatment, tumor stage, or surgical details. The only observed difference was a slightly longer time since surgery in non-participants (21.0 months vs. 19.0 months; P = 0.029) (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

This study utilized data from the SCORCS registry, which prospectively collects information from patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery at two major tertiary referral centers in southern Iran. Our results indicate that over half (54.8%) of the included patients experienced either minor or major LARS. Furthermore, patients who received neoadjuvant radiotherapy had 4.4 times higher odds of LARS compared to their respective reference groups. Although this represents considerable effect sizes, the wide CIs primarily reflect the limited sample size, underscoring the need for caution when interpreting the magnitude of the association. Furthermore, older age and female sex were also identified as independent determinants of major LARS.

In this study, major LARS was observed in 22% of participants, a prevalence consistent with some prior reports. Dulskas et al. reported a comparable rate of major LARS (26.4%) within an overall LARS prevalence of 46.4% in a long-term follow-up study of 125 patients at a median of 7.5 years post-surgery [17]. This proportion, however, is lower than the pooled estimates reported in recent meta-analyses [5, 7]. The observed discrepancies may be attributable to heterogeneity in patient characteristics, cultural perceptions and reporting stigma, neoadjuvant therapy regimens, surgical techniques, and the interval since surgery or stoma reversal. To enhance the validity of our functional assessment, the current analysis was restricted to patients with a minimum of one year following their resection, a period generally considered sufficient for bowel function to stabilize.

Neoadjuvant radiotherapy is a cornerstone of rectal cancer management, conferring significant oncological benefits through tumor downstaging, which facilitates more effective resection, and reducing local recurrence rates [18]. Despite these advantages, its association with LARS remains a significant concern, with most [5], though not all [9], studies identifying it as a key risk factor. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying radiation-induced LARS are multifactorial, proposed to include pelvic tissue inflammation, anal sphincter impairment, pelvic neuropathy, and intestinal microbiota dysbiosis [19, 20]. Consequently, future research must prioritize the refinement of radiation techniques to minimize these detrimental functional sequelae.

In the present study, a lower anastomotic height was identified as a significant determinant of major LARS in univariate analysis; however, this association did not retain statistical significance in the multivariable model after adjusting for potential confounders. The observed association between lower anastomotic height and LARS may be attributable to the diminished capacity of the rectal reservoir and an increased risk of injury to the pelvic autonomic nerves and anal sphincter complex during construction of a low anastomosis [21]. Future large-scale prospective studies are warranted to further elucidate the independent contribution of anastomotic level, particularly in patients who do not receive neoadjuvant radiotherapy.

The observed association between age and the odds of LARS in the current study may be attributed to diminished functional rectal reserve and weakness of sphincter and pelvic floor muscles in older adults [22]. However, previous literature has suggested that younger age is a risk factor for major LARS, potentially due to higher patient expectations regarding postoperative bowel function and the performance of more radical resections with lower anastomoses [23, 24]. These conflicting findings indicate that age likely does not act in isolation but interacts with other clinical and psychosocial factors to influence LARS outcomes. Furthermore, the observed association between age and LARS in our cohort may be influenced by unmeasured confounders, such as baseline bowel function, and may not be generalizable to other populations with different demographic or clinical characteristics. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate these complex interactions.

Interestingly, despite the high prevalence of LARS in our cohort, we did not observe significant differences in any QoL domain across LARS severity groups. This finding contrasts with our previous meta-analysis, which demonstrated a relationship between LARS severity and QoL impairment as assessed by the EORTC QLQ-C30 [6]. The relatively small sample size and the short follow-up period in the current analysis may account for this discrepancy. Furthermore, cultural factors may influence symptom reporting and QoL perceptions; Iranian patients may prioritize survival outcomes over functional consequences or may underreport symptom burden due to social stigma.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Second, the relatively modest sample size, as reflected in the wide confidence intervals, limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses. For instance, the small number of patients without a defunctioning stoma may have precluded the detection of a significant association between stoma status and LARS. Third, the absence of preoperative bowel function data means that baseline dysfunction could have confounded the observed associations between surgical and demographic factors and postoperative LARS. Fourth, although anastomotic leak is widely recognized as a risk factor for LARS [25], incomplete documentation within the registry precluded its inclusion in our analyses. Fifth, despite data collection by trained interviewers, the potential for underreporting of bowel symptoms during telephone interviews cannot be excluded; future investigations may benefit from using self-administered instruments to reduce this bias. Sixth, while we compared participants and non-participants to assess selection bias and found largely comparable baseline characteristics, the possibility of residual selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the single-institution nature of the study.

In conclusion, this study confirms a high prevalence of LARS in rectal cancer survivors, with neoadjuvant radiotherapy, older age, and female sex identified as determinates of LARS. Our results highlight the critical need for targeted preoperative counseling for these higher-risk subgroups. However, given the limitations of the present study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, prospective studies.
