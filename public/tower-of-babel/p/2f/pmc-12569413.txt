Re: Comments on “Elevated Serum Amyloid A2 and A4 in Patients With Guillain–Barré Syndrome”
Authors: Yuzhong Wang
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We have carefully reviewed the letter discussing our article titled “Elevated Serum Amyloid A2 and A4 in Patients With Guillain–Barré Syndrome.”1 We sincerely appreciate the readers’ interest in our work and their thoughtful comments on our research. We have carefully read and comprehensively considered each point raised, and we address each of them in detail below.

The utility of serum amyloid A (SAA)—an acute-phase protein—as a biomarker in Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is significantly confounded by several clinical variables. Common comorbidities (e.g., diabetes and autoimmune disorders), concurrent infections (both preceding triggers such as Campylobacter jejuni as well as hospital-acquired pneumonia), and immunomodulatory therapies independently alter SAA expression. During participant enrollment, we excluded patients with metabolic disorders, hepatic impairment, and other conditions that might influence SAA levels. None of the included patients had received any medication before treatment. Treatment was initiated in all of the GBS patients at our hospital shortly after the diagnosis and disease evaluation, including the clinical evaluation of the GBS Disease Score (GBS-DS). As described in the Methods section, the serum samples were collected after diagnosis and before immunotherapy, and so there was no significant delay between GBS-DS determination and blood sampling.

Our study aimed to determine the potential of SAA as a biomarker for demyelinating injury in GBS. Patients with axonal GBS were the best internal control for the demyelinating subtypes in order to exclude other confounding factors such as antecedent-infection-related factors that might increase the SAA level. Concerning the composition of the disease control group, tension-type headache,2 peripheral vertigo,3 and somatic symptom disorder4 are definitely classified as neurological disorders. Patients with these conditions were specifically selected as disease controls because they have no documented association with SAA alterations, thus effectively minimizing potential confounding effects in our biomarker analysis. Notably, our data demonstrate that neither SAA2 (p=0.439) nor SAA4 (p=0.989) was significantly elevated in the disease control group relative to the healthy controls.

A Dutch cohort study found antecedent infections in approximately two-thirds of GBS patients.5 This is consistent with 23 (59%) of our 39 GBS patients having antecedent infections; the remaining 16 patients reported no identifiable triggering events such as infections, surgical interventions, or vaccinations. We compared SAA1, SAA2, and SAA4 levels between patients with and without antecedent infections, and found that SAA2 was significantly higher in those with antecedent infection (p=0.020).

While our study provides novel mechanistic insights into the role of SAA in GBS pathogenesis, we agree that the findings come from a relatively small sample and so further studies with larger samples are necessary to comprehensively validate the roles of SAA in this disease, including after incorporating multivariate adjustment for SAA-related factors.
