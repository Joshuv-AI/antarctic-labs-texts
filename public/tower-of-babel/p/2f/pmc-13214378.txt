Epidemiological and clinical profile of patients with liver hydatid cyst in an endemic region
Authors: Shorsh Hama Hussein Ameen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver hydatid cyst can be found anywhere in the world, but especially in endemic areas, including Iraq. However, its epidemiology and clinical patterns are different among various populations in other countries.ObjectiveTo find out the epidemiological and clinical profile of patients with liver hydatid cyst in our locality (endemic area). Patients and methodsThis observational cross-sectional study was conducted on a total of 250 patients diagnosed with liver hydatid cysts at multiple hospitals, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2022 to March 2025. All patients underwent partial cystectomy for liver hydatid cyst under general anesthesia. A self-created questionnaire was used to collect patients’ data on their sociodemographic characteristics, environmental factors, risk factors, and the recurrence of liver hydatid cyst. Then, correlations between some variables were determined and compared.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 39.49 ± 17.97 years. Majority of the patients were aged < 30 years (33.2%), females (60.4%), lived in rural areas (60.8%), had direct contact with livestock (55.2%) and dogs (59.2%), used spring water as their primary water source (56.8%), and did not have any family members diagnosed with hydatid cyst (92%). Among patients, only 19.6% experienced a postoperative bile leak that was significantly associated with cyst size (≥ 6 mm) (p = 0.004) and multiple cyst (p < 0.001) after adjustment of the data. More profound symptoms among patients was right upper quadrant pain (68.8%), while some individuals had jaundice, nausea and vomiting. Also, most patients (95.2%) did not experience any recurrence.ConclusionsPostoperative bile leak was significantly associated with cyst size, number of cysts, and affected liver segment. Hospital stay was longer among patients with postoperative bile leak.Keywords: Hepatic hydatid cyst, Endemic area, Epidemiology, Clinical profile, Risk factors

Introduction

The hydatid disease, also known as echinococcosis, is a non-contagious parasitic infection caused by metacestodes (larval stage) of Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato, which is a hermaphrodite flatworm with three stages of development [1]. Humans are accidental hosts in this zoonotic disease process, and hepatic infection accounts for over two-thirds of all cases [2]. The human incidence rates reach as high as 50 per 100,000 person-years [3]. The hydatid cyst is the actual fluid-filled sac that forms in an organ as a result of this infection. Large cysts may contain more than one liter of highly antigenic hydatid fluid, as well as millions of protoscolices (hydatid sand). Daughter cysts sometimes form in or outside primary cysts. If a cyst in the liver leaks or ruptures, infection can spread to the peritoneum [4].

Humans can get the infection after accidentally drinking or eating anything contaminated with the eggs of that parasite (especially vegetables). Dogs are the definitive hosts, which may have adult tapeworms in their gastrointestinal tract after eating infected meat. Whereas herbivores, including sheep, goats, swine, cattle, camels, horses, and deer or humans are intermediate hosts that develop cystic lesions in their organs [4]. The hydatid cyst is mainly found in the liver (75% of the cases) [5]; however, it can affect other organs, such as the brain, kidneys, lungs, spleen, bone, and muscles, especially in endemic areas [6]. Hydatid disease tends to occur more commonly in rural, poor, unsanitary or underdeveloped regions. Symptoms of the disease depends on the cyst’s size and its location (infected vital organ), as they are slow-growing and asymptomatic in most cases even for few years; however, patients may develop jaundice, coughing, shortness of breath, chest pain (when lungs are infected), nausea, vomiting (when liver is infected), skin rash, fever, and abdominal discomfort. It can lead to more serious health complications, such as anaphylactic shock and death, if it is not treated correctly and in cases of cystic rupture [7]. A hydatid cyst can be discovered accidentally on a routine abdominal ultrasound or an ultrasound performed for diagnosing other pathologies. In addition to these methods, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, serological tests, and faecal examinations can also be used for diagnosis [8]. The hepatic hydatid cyst therapy is multimodal, including medical (Albendazole), surgical, and, lately, minimally invasive techniques, such as cyst aspiration and instillation of a scolicidal agent [1]. However, surgery remains the mainstay of treatment for large, active, complicated or symptomatic hepatic cysts [9],The anatomical location, clinical status of the patient, surgical facilities, expertise of the surgical team, and quality of follow-up play a significant role in postoperative complications, including bile leaks. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the epidemiological and clinical profile of patients with liver hydatid cyst in Sulaimaniyah, Iraq.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This observational cross-sectional study was conducted on a total of 250 patients at Surgical Teaching Hospital, Teaching Hospital for Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Zhian Private Hospital, Naway Drwst Private Hospital, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2022 to March 2025, using a selective sampling method (patients diagnosed with liver hydatid cyst).

Inclusion criteria

Patients diagnosed with liver hydatid cysts, regardless of age, gender, or nationality.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with other types of hydatid cyst (brain, lung, bone, spleen, muscle, etc.), and those with chronic dreaded diseases and on intensive medications.

Questionnaire

A self-developed, validated (by five experts in the field of gastroenterology surgery) questionnaire was developed to collect patients’ sociodemographic data (age, gender, and residency) and environmental factors (livestock contact, dog contact, source of drinking water, and family member with hydatid cyst).

Study protocol

In the preoperative period, Albendazole was given at a dose of 10–15 mg/kg/day (400 mg twice daily). Then, 28-day cycles were separated by 14-days drug-free interval. Then, all patients underwent a surgical operation for liver hydatid cyst under general anesthesia (open surgery in 69.2% and laparoscopic surgery in 30.8%). Laparoscopic partial cystectomy was performed with the patient placed in a supine or slight reverse trendelenburg position, depending on cyst location. Pneumoperitoneum was established using a closed or open technique, and trocar placement was planned to allow optimal access to the involved hepatic segment. After diagnostic laparoscopy, the operative field was carefully isolated with gauze pads soaked in a scolicidal agent to prevent intraperitoneal spillage. The cyst was identified and punctured under direct vision, followed by controlled aspiration of hydatid fluid. Following decompression, the cyst wall was opened, and the germinal membrane along with daughter cysts were completely evacuated using suction and graspers. Partial cystectomy was achieved by excising the protruding portion of the peri-cyst, taking care to avoid injury to adjacent vascular and biliary structures. The residual cavity was thoroughly inspected for bleeding points and cystobiliary communications, which were managed with suturing or clipping as required. Depending on the size and location of the cavity, omentoplasty, capitonnage, or external drainage were performed. Hemostasis was secured, a drain was placed selectively, and the specimen was retrieved in an endoscopic bag to prevent contamination. This stepwise approach allows safe cyst eradication, while preserving maximal functional liver tissue.

Afterwards, patients were prescribed with third generation cephalosporin and metronidazole for one week and allowed to stay in the hospital until total recovery. Following surgery, risk factors for postoperative bile leak were collected, including cyst number, cyst size, liver segment location, intraoperative bile leak, and duration of hospital stay. Albendazole was also given postoperatively (400 mg twice daily) for 1–3 months, that might extend up to another three months with 14-days break, particularly if cyst was ruptured or spillage occurred during surgery. Liver function tests and blood counts were monitored every 2–4 weeks to detect hepatotoxicity or leukopenia, and the recurrence rate was reported after 6 months of surgery.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Science, version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and 95% Confidence Interval (CI). The Chi-square test was used to assess the association between categorical variables. A p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant, while a p ≤ 0.001 was set as highly significant.

Results

Epidemiological profile of patients with liver hydatid cyst

The mean age of the patients was 39.49 ± 17.97 years, with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) ranging from 37.25 to 41.73 years. Most patients were aged < 30 years (n = 83, 33.2%), females (n = 151, 60.4%), resided in rural areas (60.8%, n = 152). Regarding livestock contact, 44.8% (n = 112) of the patients reported no contact with livestock, whereas 55.2% (n = 138) had direct contact with livestock. Similarly, 40.8% (n = 102) of the patients did not have contact with dogs, while 59.2% (n = 148) had contact. Furthermore, 56.8% (n = 42) of patients reported using spring water as their primary source, while 43.2% (n = 108) relied on groundwater. The majority of patients (n = 230, 92%) did not have any family members diagnosed with hydatid cyst or previous history of hydatid cyst (n = 225, 90%). More profound symptoms among patients was right upper quadrant pain (n = 172, 68.8%) and the most affected liver lobe was the right lobe (n = 170, 68%). Majority had the cyst in the single liver lobe (n = 241, 96.4%) and only 9 patients (3.6%) had the cyst in both right and left lobes (Table 1).

Risk factors for postoperative bile leak in liver hydatid cyst surgery

Among patients, 19.6% (n = 49) experienced a postoperative bile leak, while 80.4% (n = 201) did not. Cyst size emerged as a significant risk factor for postoperative bile leak. Among patients with cysts of < 6 cm, only 3.8% (n = 2) experienced a postoperative bile leak, while 96.2% (n = 50) did not. In contrast, among patients with cysts of ≥ 6 cm, 23.7% (n = 47) developed a postoperative bile leak, while 76.3% (n = 151) did not develop a postoperative bile leak. The association between larger cyst size and postoperative bile leak was significant (p = 0.001). Among patients with single cyst (84.1%, n = 212), only 14.6% (n = 31) experienced a postoperative bile leak, while 85.4% (n = 181) did not. For those with multiple cysts (15.9%, n = 38), 47.4% (n = 18) developed a postoperative bile leak, while 52.6% (n = 20) did not. The higher incidence of postoperative bile leaks in patients with multiple cysts was significant (p < 0.001). Regarding the liver segment location, 56.8% (n = 142) of patients had cysts in the central lobe, and 43.2% (n = 108) had cysts in the peripheral lobe. Among those with central lobe cysts, 23.9% (n = 34) experienced a postoperative bile leak, compared to 13.9% (n = 15) in the peripheral lobe group. The difference in postoperative bile leak incidence between the two groups was significant (p = 0.047). In terms of intraoperative bile leaks, 18.8% of patients experienced it during surgery, of which, 10.6% developed a postoperative bile leak, while 89.4% did not. Among patients without an intraoperative bile leak, 21.7% had a postoperative bile leak, while 78.3% did not (p = 0.086) (Table 2). Patient with bile leak had significantly longer hospital stay, as 94.3% of them had hospital stay of < 4 days and only 5.7% of them have hospital stay of > 4 days (p < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis of risk factors for postoperative bile leak

A multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors associated with the occurrence of postoperative bile leak following hepatic hydatid cyst surgery. Patients with cystic lesions of ≥ 6 cm had approximately 9.65-fold higher odds of developing a postoperative bile leak compared with those with smaller cysts (aOR = 9.65, 95% CI = 2.03–45.76, p = 0.004). Similarly, patients with multiple cysts demonstrated a 5.91-fold increase in risk (aOR = 5.91, 95% CI = 2.35–14.86, p < 0.001). On contrary, liver segment location (central vs. peripheral lobe; aOR = 1.59, 95% CI = 0.72–3.53, p = 0.253) and type of surgery (laparoscopic vs. open; aOR = 1.30, 95% CI = 0.47–3.57, p = 0.618), were not significantly associated with postoperative bile leak. The intraoperative bile leak was inversely associated with postoperative leakage (aOR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.07–0.83, p = 0.023), suggesting that early intraoperative identification and management may reduce the risk of postoperative complications (Table 3).

Recurrence of liver hydatid cyst

The recurrence of hydatid cysts was observed in 4.8% (n = 12) of the patients after 6 months of surgery, while the remaining 95.2% (n = 238) did not experience any recurrence.

Discussion

The prevalence of human hydatidosis, a neglected disease, is recognized as a critical issue by the public health domain and educational programs. To improve recognition of the disease’s symptoms and identify infection sources, it is essential to focus on high-risk populations, including this locality [10]. Thus, for the first time in Iraq, we designed this study to find out the sociodemographic characteristics, clinical patterns, and potential epidemiological factors associated with the postoperative bile leak among patients with liver hydatid cyst. The mean age of the patients in this study was 39.49 ± 17.97 years, and the age distribution highlights a wide range of ages within the cohort, with the highest proportion of participants aged < 30 years (33.2%). The fact that broad age groups, including children, young adults, and the elderly, were represented in the confirmed hepatic hydatid cyst cases (ranging from < 30 to 70 years) suggests that all age groups are susceptible to infection. Regarding the gender distribution, females reported a higher proportion (60.4%) that resulted in a female-to-male ratio of 1.5:1. This outcome indicated a higher prevalence of liver hydatid cysts among females in our locality, as most females in this locality are housewives and have the highest chance of contact with sources of infection, such as dogs, soil, and vegetables. Also, a significant proportion of the studied patients were from rural areas (60.8%). This distribution reflects the geographical characteristics of the study population, with a greater number of cases reported from rural regions, which may be attributed to environmental or occupational risk factors associated with living in such areas. These findings (age, gender, and residency) are aligned with other international studies, such as Botezatu et al. in Hungary [1], Işık et al. in Turkey [11], Mihmanli et al. [12], Shahriarirad et al. in Iran [13], and Inaebnit et al. in Spain [14].

More than half of the studied patients had direct contact with livestock (55.2%). This exposure is known as a risk factor for hydatid cyst infection, highlighting a potential link to the endemicity of the disease in this region. Hydatid disease is highly prevalent in Iran, one of the most endemic areas in the Middle East, which is considered a serious public health issue in that country. The majority of cystic hydatidosis cases reported from Iran are of the liver and lungs [15], with an overall seroprevalence of 4.2% in human hosts [13]. Sulaimaniyah city in the Kurdistan region of Iraq has its closest border with Iran and imports > 90% of sheep from Iran, which might be a potential cause of endemic hydatid disease in the region. Whereas our patients have high contact with dogs (59.2%), the definitive host of the hydatid tapeworm emphasizes the role of zoonotic transmission in the spread of hydatid disease, as contact with dogs is considered a significant risk factor for infection. These results align with those in Bulgaria by Jordanova et al., who reported that 90% of the affected children with hydatid disease had sustained contact with dogs [16], and also with another study in Iran [13]. However, Khazaei et al. in Iran stated that contact with a domestic dog (49%) was not a common risk predictor among patients with liver hydatid cyst [10].

Humans are accidental intermediate hosts that become infected by faecal-oral transmissions, that is, unintentional consumption of food or water contaminated with feces-containing eggs or by direct contact while handling a definitive infected host. In this study, more than half of the operated patients used spring water as their primary source (56.8%). Thus, this outcome is parallel to those found by other studies, which reveal that the use of groundwater may be more prone to contamination and contribute to the transmission of Echinococcus granulosus, especially in rural and semi-rural areas [17]. Additionally, the majority of the patients had no family members diagnosed with hydatid cysts (92%), or previous history of hydatid cyst (90%) as the disease is not contagious and not transmitted from infected individuals to others. Hence, there is no documented correlation in the medical literature between having family members diagnosed with hydatid cysts and an increased risk of postoperative bile leak following surgery for hydatid disease. More profound symptoms among this study patient was right upper quadrant pain (68.8%), right lobe was mostly affected (68%). The main reason for the right lobe’s higher susceptibility is portal circulation. After ingesting the parasitic eggs, the larvae enter the portal circulation, and the right lobe receives a larger portion of this blood flow, increasing the likelihood of larvae lodging in this lobe [18, 19]. These results are in parallel to that of Ufuk et al [20].

Furthermore, in the present study, the postoperative bile leakage rate was 19.6%, which is almost close to that of Özdemir et al. in Turkey (19.2%) [21]. On the contrary, it was lower than that of a national study by Sawady et al. in Basrah, Iraq (57%) [22], and Gurana et al. in India (31.8%) [23]. Generally, abscess formation, recurrent disease, and larger cysts may contribute to anatomical distortion or delayed healing, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative biliary leakage among patients [24]. Also, some factors were examined to determine their association with the occurrence of postoperative bile leak and their corresponding significance. Consequently, postoperative bile leak was significantly associated with large cyst size of ≥ 6 cm (p = 0.004), multiple cysts (p < 0.001). All these factors emerged as significant risk factors for postoperative bile leak. However, cyst in the central lobe and type of surgery (laparoscopy) have no significant correlation to postoperative bile leak, which means these 2 factors may not be a strong predictor of postoperative bile leaks in this cohort as the surgeon deal with the leak intraoperatively. Similarly, Vâlcea et al. in Romania found the same results, especially those who underwent partial cystectomy with large or recurrent cysts [25]. Another study indicated that the largest cyst being located in the perihilar region, the mean diameter of the cyst, the diameter of the largest cyst, biliary tract dilatation on the preoperative images, and infection were associated with increased postoperative bile leakage after multiple liver hydatid cyst surgery [21]. Finally, most patients (95.2%) did not experience any recurrence of the liver hydatid cyst after six months of follow-up, which indicate that the surgeries were performed by an experienced surgical team, using advanced technique, with effective hospital care in this locality that was offered to the patients in the surgical rooms. In this regard, Velasco-Tirado et al. stated that the likelihood of recurrence was higher when the cyst was located in organs other than the liver and lung (22.6% versus 14.2%) [26]. Also, a recent meta-analysis reported a recurrence rate of 8% after primary surgical resection. Interestingly, there was a marked variation in recurrence rates across different regions, as Europe had the highest recurrence rates, whereas parts of Asia reported the lowest recurrence rates [27]. The limitations of this study were not using Gharbi classification, having no control group to compare the data with, nature of the study (cross-sectional), potential selection bias from hospital-based sampling, not determining the correlation between patients’ sociodemographic characteristics and prevalence of postoperative bile leakage, and not following up the patients for longer duration (up to one year) due to their transportation issues, as they were lived in far places from the city center.

Conclusions

Hydatid cyst is more commonly reported among middle-aged females who live in rural regions and have direct contact with livestock and dogs. Postoperative bile leak was significantly found among patients with large cyst size (≥ 6 cm) and multiple cysts. Also, patients with postoperative bile leak had prolonged hospital stay (< 4 days). Consequently, we recommend using more advanced diagnostic tools to detect cysts at the initial stage and conducting regular follow-ups to prevent the cysts from developing into an advanced stage, which can lead to cystic rupture and dissemination to other organs.
