Non-adaptive Radiation Promotes Phenotypic Diversification and Convergent Evolution of Aposematic Mimicry in a Highly Diverse Genus of Megaloptera
Authors: Yuezheng Tu, Xuankun Li, Fumio Hayashi, Feng Zhang, Ding Yang, Fabien L Condamine, Xingyue Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvolutionary radiations are considered key processes underlying the origin of biodiversity. Notably, the mechanisms driving these radiations can vary across organisms and often involve a complex interplay of abiotic and biotic factors. Empirical studies on evolutionary history are crucial for the validation of multiple hypotheses regarding the mode of evolutionary radiations. Within the aquatic insect order Megaloptera, the genus Protohermes is the most speciose clade with 90 described species, accounting for around 22% of the total ordinal diversity. Protohermes species are featured by the limited dispersal ability, primarily occurring across the Oriental region, and a range of diversified phenotypes, e.g., highly divergent genital characters, and mimetic coloration alongside shifts in biological rhythm—from nocturnal to diurnal activities. Here we infer the spatiotemporal mode of diversification and associated driving factors of the Protohermes radiation as a test case for exploring the processes and potential mechanisms of evolutionary radiations. We present the first time-calibrated phylogeny of Protohermes using genome-scale data of ultraconserved elements (UCEs) and mitochondrial genes with a comprehensive taxon sampling. Our results reveal a mid-Cretaceous stem age of Protohermes, followed by a recent and steady diversification during the Neogene. Estimation of historical biogeography suggests the genus likely originated from a broad range including the Himalayas-Hengduan Mountains + Indochina + Borneo, with the first two areas serving as the center of early diversification. Our results further suggest that vicariance events, likely attributed to the Cenozoic Himalayan orogeny as well as climate change in East Asia, triggered speciation that coincided with the accumulation of genital divergence. Further enhancement of genital and phenotypic diversification may have been promoted by secondary contacts of allopatric or parapatric lineages following the build-up of species richness, likely facilitating species coexistence and lineage accumulation. We argue that the current species diversity of Protohermes likely resulted from a non-adaptive radiation. Our results highlight the role of geographic vicariance and sexual selection in driving the species and phenotypic diversification in insects.

Evolutionary radiations have long fascinated evolutionary biologists because of the impressive biological diversity they have produced. These phenomena often occur after the emergence of new ecological opportunities, such as the colonization of unexplored areas, the extinction of competitors, or the evolution of key traits. The most fascinating examples include the Darwin’s Finches in the Galápagos Islands (e.g., Grant and Grant 2006; Lamichhaney et al. 2018) and the Lake Victoria’s cichlids in Eastern Africa (e.g., Ronco et al. 2021), both widely known as classic cases of the “adaptive radiations.” Despite debates on its definition and the range of underlying processes (e.g., Ginvish 2015; Simões et al. 2016; Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019), adaptive radiation generally refers to as rapid diversification of lineages accompanied by ecological divergence, which is often characterized by an initial expansion in clade’s fitness and niche breadth (Gavrilets and Losos 2009; Glor 2010; Martin and Richards 2019). In contrast, evolutionary radiations can also occur without significant ecological divergence across lineages, a scenario referred to as the “non-adaptive radiations.” In such cases, non-ecological speciation may be driven by factors such as polyploidy, specific forms of sexual selections, geographic vicariance, or a combination of these mechanisms (Rundell and Price 2009; Givnish 2015; Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019; Martin and Richards 2019). Adaptive and non-adaptive radiations can lead to similar outcomes in taxon and trait diversification. In other words, the present diversity may have formed through different processes and mechanisms (Table 1). To determine whether species richness and trait diversity observed in a given lineage arises from adaptive or non-adaptive processes, it is crucial to comprehensively infer the evolutionary history and test potential factors underlying the radiation.

As the most diverse macro-organisms on Earth, insects provide a vast number of biological systems for testing hypotheses on the formation of biodiversity and evolutionary radiations (Misof et al. 2014; Stork 2018), including both the adaptive (e.g., the Heliconiini butterflies; Cicconardi et al. 2023) and the non-adaptive ones (e.g., Blepharoneura fruit flies; Winkler et al. 2018). Within insects, the dobsonfly genus Protohermes van der Weele, 1907 stands out as an ideal model for studying the processes and mechanisms of evolutionary radiations. The genus belongs to the subfamily Corydalinae of the family Corydalidae and is the most species-rich group of the holometabolous insect order Megaloptera, with 90 described species, representing 22.3% of the world megalopteran fauna ( Martins et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024). Despite the high species richness, most Protohermes species share similar life styles: their larvae are exclusively aquatic and generalist predators highly relying on montane streams, while the adults are mostly short-lived and inhabit the forests adjacent to larval habitats (New and Theischinger 1993; Yang and Liu 2010). Nevertheless, Protohermes also exhibits remarkable phenotypic disparity in coloration and marking patterns (Fig. 1b), which are sometimes associated with ecological divergence. For example, some lineages have undergone shifts in adult lifestyle from nocturnal to diurnal, accompanied by darkened coloration. These species are among the most colorful species in Megaloptera, characterized by deeply dark bodies and wings interspersed with striking yellow or white markings (Fig. 1a–c; Supplementary Fig. S1), which is believed to represent Batesian mimicry of some co-distributed diurnal toxic moths with warning coloration (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3; Hayashi 1995). From the perspective of adaptive radiation (i.e., rapid diversification accompanied by ecological divergence), it is worth investigating whether and how the diverse coloration might have enhanced ecological fitness and facilitated species diversification in Protohermes.

Besides the diversification of body and wing coloration, Protohermes species are also notable for their highly divergent genitalia, particularly the varied shapes and sizes of male genital sclerites among different species. These genital characters play a crucial role in the alpha-taxonomy of Protohermes as the basis for classifying most species into 16 species groups (Liu et al. 2024). More importantly, the divergence of these genital characters may reflect remarkable sexual selection in this genus. The mating behavior of Protohermes is complex with insemination occurring indirectly through an external gelatinous spermatophore as a “nuptial gift” produced by male, rather than through direct genital contact (Hayashi 1992, 1993; Liu et al. 2015a; also see Supplementary Fig. S6). Thus, the genital structures of both sexes are specialized to tightly grasp the spermatophore during copulation (Supplementary Fig. S6a). As sexual selection is regarded as one of the key drivers of lineage diversification and plays a critical role in lineage accumulation and the subsequent coexistence of species, particularly during the process of non-ecological diversification (Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019; Martin and Richards 2019; Gómez-Llano et al. 2021), rapid speciation can be promoted and sustained by the diversification of complex sexual traits related to a wide range of mechanisms, including the mechanical mechanisms (often driven by genitalia and related traits; Hosken and Stockley 2004). Therefore, it is essential to test the role of sexual selection in facilitating the lineage diversification of Protohermes.

Considering the abiotic factors, diversification could be temporarily promoted during periods of environmental and geological changes (Moen and Morlon 2014). Protohermes may be particularly susceptible to these factors due to their reliance on specific microhabitats and limited capacity in long-distance dispersal (New and Theischinger 1993; Liu et al. 2010; Yang and Liu 2010). Currently, all extant species of Protohermes are endemic to Asia and occupy a wide range from East, North, South to Southeast Asia, including the major continental and insular systems (Fig. 1d). Most of the species diversity is confined to the Oriental realm, particularly the Southern Himalayas, Southern China, Indochina, and Borneo (Liu et al. 2009b; Yang and Liu 2010). It has been hypothesized that historical biogeography and between-species divergences within several species groups may be associated with past vicariance during Cenozoic tectonic events (e.g., Liu et al. 2009b, 2013, 2015b).

The phylogenetic relationships and divergence times within Protohermes remain unresolved, which impede the investigation of the evolutionary processes such as the phenotypic traits or the biogeographic patterns underlying the formation of their prominent diversity. In this study, we present the first well-resolved phylogenetic timeframe of Protohermes with a comprehensive taxon sampling, that covers close to 80% of the total species diversity, using genomic data from ultraconserved elements (UCEs) and mitochondrial genes. Based on this phylogenetic framework, we investigated historical biogeography, trait evolution, and macroevolutionary dynamics of the genus to address whether the diversification process in this genus is adaptive or non-adaptive with the following questions: (1) How have lineages and traits diversified? (2) What are the spatiotemporal patterns of the diversification? and (3) What are the drivers of the diversification? Our study provides new insights into the role of geographic isolation and sexual selection in driving the non-adaptive radiation in insects, which further lead to the formation of species and phenotypic diversity.

Material and Methods

The workflow of the study is summarized in Figure 2. Phylogenetic analyses, dating analyses and some of the biogeographic analyses were conducted on the High-performance Computing Platform of China Agricultural University.

Flowchart showing the analytical pipeline in the present study.

Taxon Sampling, Sequencing, and Data Assembling

A total of 89 taxa were included in the present phylogenetic study, of which 86 were newly sequenced. Detailed information on species names, collecting information and institutes of deposition are listed in Supplementary Table S1 for all samples. Our ingroup taxon sampling includes 74 taxa of Protohermes, reaching nearly 80% of the known species and all 16 species groups of the genus. We selected 15 outgroup taxa that include seven genera of Corydalinae and one species each from Chauliodinae (Megaloptera: Corydalidae), Sialidae (Megaloptera) and Chrysopidae (Neuroptera). We extracted genomic DNA from muscle tissue of ethanol-preserved or pinned specimens with Magen UniPure Universal DNA Kit (Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech Co., Ltd.), respectively. Genomic DNAs were sent to Berry Genomics Co., Ltd. for library preparation and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform or BGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads and an insert size of around 350 bp. Raw reads were quality-trimmed with fastp 0.23.4 (Chen et al. 2018) and genomes were de novo assembled in minia 3.2.4 (Chikhi and Rizk 2013; Chikhi et al. 2016) following the pipeline described by Zhang et al. (2019).

UCE Probe Design, Sequence Extraction, and Dataset Construction

We used Phyluce 1.6.6 (Faircloth 2016) to design a lineage-specific probe set for the present phylogenomic analysis of Protohermes (Supplementary Appendix S1). The UCE loci were subsequently extracted with these probe sets from the de novo assembled genomes in Phyluce. Loci were aligned in MAFFT 7.52 (Katoh and Standley 2013) and were trimmed in ClipKit 1.1.5 with the “smart-gap” model (Steenwyk et al. 2020). We used TAPER to exclude errors within the alignments (Zhang et al. 2021). To remove possible external contamination, we used Treeshrink 1.3.8b (Mai and Mirarab 2018), a long-branch detection algorithm to detect the outlier long branches and remove their corresponding sequences. Finally, we generated matrices with different taxon occupancy in PhyKit 1.11.1 (Steenwyk et al. 2021). The above-mentioned steps for sequence obtaining and processing were carried out using the scripts in PLWS (Zhang et al. 2019; Du et al. 2023).

In addition, we recovered mitochondrial (mt) genes to reach a more comprehensive taxon coverage (i.e., the addition of P. karubei and P. sonus, whose materials are only available for obtaining mt genes) as well as to test the performance of these markers since they have been widely used in previous phylogenetic studies of Megaloptera (Chang et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2015a; Yang et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2021, 2022). The mt COI gene of P. karubei was sequenced following the method in Chang et al. (2013). Besides, we assembled and processed 13 mt protein-coding genes (PCGs) of the remaining species following the pipeline described in Breinholt et al. (2018).

We assembled the locus alignments into two series of datasets based on different strategies of taxon sampling (Supplementary Table S1). First, we constructed a dataset with 30 taxa representing all genera in Corydalinae as ingroup taxa to investigate the feasibility of our newly designed probe sets as well as the evolutionary timescale of Corydalinae. This dataset is named the matrixCORY series. In this dataset, we selected Chrysopa pallens (Neuroptera: Chrysopidae), Sialis melania (Megaloptera: Sialidae), and Parachauliodes buchi (Megaloptera: Corydalidae: Chauliodinae) for calibration in the later molecular dating analyses (Supplementary Table S1). We retained those UCE loci with > 90% taxon coverage in the final matrix (uceCORY90; 3,416 loci).

Second, we constructed a series of matrices for the phylogenomic inferences of Protohermes (namely matrixPRO series): (1) a matrix with 72 sampled taxa of Protohermes as ingroup and 12 taxa of the rest Corydalinae as the outgroup, with UCE loci > 90% taxon-coverage (ucePRO90; 2,109 loci); (2) a matrix using the same taxon sampling as (1) but with loci > 70% taxon-coverage, to test the potential impact of more loci but higher missing data (ucePRO70; 4,381 loci); (3) a matrix with UCE loci in ucePRO90 and mt COI sequences, to reach the most comprehensive taxon sampling (74 taxa) of Protohermes (ucePRO90 + coi); and (4) a matrix with 13 mt PCGs with the same taxon sampling as in (3) (mtPRO). Detailed information for all the datasets is listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Phylogenetic Analyses

Phylogenetic analyses were performed based on concatenated and coalescent approaches. For the concatenated method, we first concatenated all aligned loci in FASconCAT-G-1.05 (Kueck and Longo 2014). We conducted maximum likelihood (ML) analyses in IQ-TREE 2.2.2.6 (Minh et al. 2020b) for our concatenated datasets with the partition model (“-p”) (Chernomor et al. 2016). The concatenated matrices were partitioned by locus. To further test the impact of different partitioning strategies, we used the method SWSC-EN for the uceCORY90 and ucePRO90 matrices. SWSC-EN is a method designed based on the characteristics of UCE loci and can split each UCE into a conserved middle part and two variable flanking regions by entropy, which is suggested to better handle the heterogeneity within each UCE locus (Tagliacollo and Lanfear 2018). We merged the possible partitions to identify the best partitioning schemes (“-MFP+MERGE”) and determined the best scheme under the Bayesian information criterion (“-merit BIC”). Nodal support was computed with 1000 ultrafast bootstraps (UFBoot; “-bb” option) replicates (Minh et al. 2013; Hoang et al. 2018) and 1000 SH-aLRT (“-alrt”) (Guindon et al. 2010). Detailed parameters for IQ-TREE are described in Supplementary Appendix S1.

The gene-tree reconciliation (also known as coalescent-based) method was conducted in ASTER 1.1.5 (Zhang et al. 2018). We first calculate ML trees for each gene respectively in IQ-TREE with Model Finder (“-MFP”) for model selection, then summarize all the gene trees into a “species tree” based on the multi-species coalescent (MSC) model (Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). We applied the weighted method in ASTER (Zhang and Mirarab 2022) to improve the utility of tree reconciliation.

We further evaluated the robustness of our phylogenetic results using different approaches. First, we investigated the concordance and discordance among genes and sites in our alignments (uceCORY90 and ucePRO90 matrices) by estimating the gene concordance factors (gCF) and site concordance factors (sCF) in IQ-TREE (Minh et al. 2020a; Mo et al. 2023). We also used Quartet Sampling (Pease et al. 2018) to further examine branch support across the tree. Finally, we compared controversial topologies using the statistics-based method Approximately Unbiased (AU) test (Shimodaira 2002). Trees with alternative topologies were constrained according to the different results that occurred in the present analyses or those relationships proposed in previous studies (Glorioso 1981; Penny 1993; Yang and Liu 2010; Contreras-Ramos 2011; Liu et al. 2015a, 2016; Jiang et al. 2022). Thereafter, all trees were tested using the “-au” option with 1000 replicates (“-zb 1000”).

Divergence Time Estimation

Due to the scarce fossil record of Corydalinae and the lack of fossils of Protohermes, we designed a two-step strategy to estimate the divergence time within this group. First, a genus-level timescale for Corydalinae was inferred based on matrixCORY using fossil calibrations. Upon obtaining these results, we then recovered the 95% credibility intervals (CI) of age estimates for key nodes and used them as secondary calibrations to estimate the divergence times within Protohermes based on the matrixPRO.

We used SortaDate (Smith et al. 2018) to reduce our matrix to a computationally tractable subset for Bayesian inferences of divergence time by selecting 50-locus subsets from the uceCORY90 and ucePRO90 datasets respectively (Supplementary Appendix S1). We added the mt COI gene along with the UCE 50-locus subset to cover all the sampled taxa in the dating analyses. We investigated the partitioning schemes and best-fitting models for these matrices in PartitionFinder 2.1.1 (Lanfear et al. 2017), using the greedy algorithm and the BIC scores across all models included in BEAST ("models = beast").

We estimated divergence times with BEAST 2.6.1 (Bouckaert et al. 2014) for Corydalinae and Protohermes. We used the uniform age prior with the minimum ages set based on the age of fossil occurrence. We first made a survey of the known fossil record of the group in the Paleobiology Database (https://paleobiodb.org/classic). We set a total of three calibrations with carefully selected fossils in the analyses (Supplementary Appendix S1). After inferring the evolutionary timescale of Corydalinae, we selected three secondary calibrations for the divergence time estimation of Protohermes (Supplementary Table S13).

For both dating analyses, we applied an uncorrelated log-normal relaxed clock as the clock model. We evaluated the fit of the birth-death model and Yule model as the branching process prior to our dataset under the path sampling (Bouckaert et al. 2014). Accordingly, the birth-death model was suggested to be the preferred tree prior for both datasets (Supplementary Table S12) and was applied in all analyses. Tree topologies were constrained following the ML tree inferred by IQ-TREE based on the ucePRO90 + coi matrix throughout the analyses. For both analyses, we set Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) with 400 million generations in a single run. We evaluated the convergence and mixing of MCMC in Tracer 1.7.1 (Rambaut et al. 2018) to ensure the effective sample sizes (ESS) of most parameters exceeded 200. Tree files were resampled with a frequency of 5000 in LogCombiner (BEAST package), and a burn-in of 30% was applied. The subsampled trees were summarized into the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree using TreeAnnotator with node ages estimated with median heights.

Estimation of Trait Evolution

We estimated the evolutionary pattern of the phenotypic traits of Protohermes with different strategies. As the color in museum specimens could easily fade, we used qualitative discrete characters to define colorations (dark or pale) for Protohermes species. We tested the fitness of different models using the R package geiger 2.0.11 (Pennell et al. 2014; Supplementary Appendix S1). Thereafter, we carried out ancestral state estimation (ASE) analyses using the R package phytools 2.0.3 (Revell 2012) based on the best-fitting model and 1,000 stochastic character mappings (make.simmap function).

We further estimated the evolution of body and wing marking patterns based on quantitative measurements. We measured the relative size of cephalic, pronotal, and forewing markings (Fig. 3f) in comparison to the corresponding head, pronotum, and forewing, respectively (Supplementary Appendix S1). Measurements were obtained using Fiji 2.15.1 (Schindelin et al. 2012) based on uniform dorsal-viewed photos of the specimens. Depending on the ability of materials, we measured 1 to 10 individuals per taxon, reaching a total of 573 individuals measured for the 74 taxa sampled in the present phylogenetic study (nearly 8 individuals per taxon). We reduced the dimensions of the morphometric dataset using the phylogenetic principal components analysis (pPCA) implemented in phytools (phyl.pca function; Revell 2009, 2012). Finally, we explored the temporal dynamics of the phenotypic disparity using the relative subclade disparity-through-time (DTT; Harmon et al. 2003) with the R package geiger 2.0.11. DTT plots were estimated based on the first ordinated axis yielded from pPCA (pPC1).

To investigate the mode of genital evolution, we coded 86 categorized morphological characters based on both male and female genitalia. We used the ordinate_cladistic_matrix function in the R package Claddis 0.6.3 (Lloyd 2016) to calculate the Maximum Observable Rescaled Distance (MORD) matrix and perform the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to reduce the dimensions (Supplementary Appendix S1). DTT analysis was performed based on the first ordinated axis (PCo1).

To estimate the evolution of ecological traits, we collected climate records from WorldClim 2.1 (Fick and Hijmans 2017) based on the species occurrence records (details in the following section), to represent the ecological preference of different Protohermes species. Mean values of each climate variable were subsequently calculated for each species (details in Supplementary Appendix S1). After performing pPCA, we estimated DTT of the ecological traits using the pPC1, following the method described above.

Biogeographic Analyses

We collected 866 occurrence records for all the 90 taxa of Protohermes from the published literature in the Lacewing Digital Library (LDL; Oswald 2022) and our unpublished records from specimen examinations (Supplementary Table S2). Ambiguous records were excluded from the present analysis. The distribution of species richness was calculated and displayed based on a 1°×1° fishnet grids using ArcMap 10.4. Based on the species occurrence records, we performed the Age-Range-Correlation (ARC) analysis (Fitzpatrick and Turelli 2006) to assess the dominant mode of geographic speciation (sympatric versus allopatric/parapatric; Supplementary Appendix S1). The ARC analysis was carried out using the R package phyloclim 0.9.5 (Heibl and Calenge 2018).

Ancestral range estimation (ARE) was performed using the R package BioGeoBEARS 1.1.3 (Matzke 2013). We defined nine areas for the analyses: (A) Himalayas and Hengduan Mountains (Mts.); (B) Indochina; (C) southern China (including the mainland and Taiwan Island); (D) northeastern mainland Asia; (E) Japanese Archipelago; (F) Ryukyu Islands; (G) Hainan Island; (H) Malay Peninsula, Java and Sumatra; (I) Borneo (Fig. 5c). The areas were defined by integrating the zoogeographic regions proposed in different studies (Zhang 1999; Meng and Song 2023), with adjustments based on the species composition of Corydalidae in East Asia (Yang and Liu 2010). Southern India, from which only P. maculiferus is distributed (Liu et al. 2015b), was not included in our biogeographic analyses as we failed to include this species. We utilized the DEC (dispersal-extinction-cladogenesis; Ree and Smith 2008) model for all the analyses. We did not apply the “+j” option (a model accounting for founder-event speciation) due to its theoretical flaws (Ree and Sanmartín 2018). To minimize potential artificial influence, we limited the maximum number of ancestral areas to nine in all analyses, which is also the maximum number of all defined areas.

We also estimated the historical dynamics of speciation and dispersal of Protohermes following the event-based approach in Li et al. (2022a), which is based on biogeographic stochastic mappings (BSMs) (Dupin et al. 2017) implemented in BioGeoBEARS. We randomly extracted 100 topologies from the BEAST posterior distribution and created 10 simulated trees from each topology by adding 16 missing taxa randomly into their affiliated species groups respectively using the R package phytools 2.0.3 (Revell 2012). Besides P. maculiferus mentioned above, we excluded P. vitalisi and P. montanus in the analyses for their doubtful taxonomic status. We carried out 100 BSMs for all simulated trees. In total, 100,000 pseudoreplicated biogeographic histories were simulated to investigate rates for four types of biogeographic events: (i) in-situ speciation, represented by the inferred speciation events within each defined biogeographic area; (ii) colonization from area X to Y, represented by inferred dispersal events from X to Y; (iii) emigration from each defined biogeographic area, and (iv) immigration events to each area.

Macroevolutionary Dynamics

We investigated the diversification process of Protohermes under different approaches. We inferred branch-specific diversification rates within Protohermes using ClaDS (Maliet et al. 2019) implemented in the julia package PANDA.jl. Unlike approaches that only recognize major clade shifts, models in ClaDS assume that changes in diversification rates are pervasive in an evolutionary process and are supposed to better account for the diverse sources of variation in diversification rates (Maliet et al. 2019; Maliet and Morlon 2022). We used the ClaDS2 model, which assumes the extinction rate can vary across lineages but with a constant turnover value. We used sample probabilities for each tip to account for incomplete taxon sampling, with missing species assigned to their species groups or clades.

We estimated diversification rates through time using the Episodic Birth-Death Model (EBD) model (Höhna 2015) implemented in RevBayes 1.1.1 (Höhna et al. 2016). We applied a Horseshoe Markov random field (HSMRF) prior distribution on log-transformed rates (Magee et al. 2020) with 100 time intervals. We accounted for incomplete sampling using the clades-specific sampling probabilities (Höhna 2014) with the unsampled taxa assigned to their species groups or clades.

Testing Potential Drivers of Diversification

Hypothetically, the complicated body coloration and wing markings are related to the ecological fitness of Protohermes (Hayashi 1995; Chang et al. 2013) and might have contributed to the speciation of this group. Therefore, we used the R package hisse 2.1.12 (Beaulieu and O’Meara 2016) to test whether the diversification of Protohermes was influenced by such phenotypic traits. We created two sets of binary characters, i.e., the presence (state 1) or absence (state 0) of wing markings, and the darkened (state 1) or pale (state 0) color forms. Following Li et al. (2022b), a total of 32 models were tested in our present study (Supplementary Table S16). Missing taxa were added by setting the sampling fraction parameter.

To test whether the diversification is dependent on a specific geographic area, we set and tested a total of 33 GeoHiSSE models using the R package hisse 2.1.12. Four areas with high species diversity were tested: (A) Himalayas-Hengduan Mts., (B) Indochina, (C) South China, and (I) Borneo. Species were coded as endemic to the target area (state 1), absent from the target area (state 2) or distributed in both the target area and the other area (state 0). Missing taxa were accounted for by setting the sampling fraction parameter for each state (Supplementary Appendix S1).

We tested the impact of paleoenvironmental variables on the diversification rates (Condamine et al. 2013, 2019) as implemented in the R package RPANDA 2.3 (Morlon et al. 2016). We designed a total of four paleoenvironment-dependent models (Supplementary Appendix S1) to test the potential impact of time-continuous global cooling that was accompanied by aridification, as well as East Asian monsoon activities on the diversification of the genus. We applied the past global temperatures and East Asian monsoons as paleoenvironmental variables based on the data from Westerhold et al. (2020) and Clift et al. (2014), respectively. In addition, we set two rate-constant models as null models, and four time-dependent models (Supplementary Appendix S1). We finally optimized the fit of all the models with maximum likelihood (ML) and we evaluated model performance based on the AICc and ∆ AICc. To investigate the difference in performance of ML and Bayesian frameworks, we tested the paleoenvironment-dependent EBD model implemented in RevBayes (Palazzesi et al. 2022). We applied the HSMRF prior with 100-time intervals for the analysis (Supplementary Appendix S1).

Finally, we tested the direct and indirect correlations between the observed species richness, trait divergence, and different abiotic factors as their potential drivers using structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis (Pugesek et al. 2003). The SEM analysis was carried out based on the 1°×1° fishnet grids as our geographic units. We calculated species richness and the ratio of darkened species per grid. Besides, we obtained abiotic variables including temperature, precipitation, and forest coverage for each grid. We also calculated the topographic heterogeneity (based on the elevation data) and ecological heterogeneity (based on temperature, precipitation, and forest coverage data) as the indices of environmental heterogeneity (Stein and Kreft 2015). Trait divergences were represented by the functional divergence indices (FDiv), which were calculated using the R package FD 1.0.12.3 (Laliberté et al. 2014). The hypothetical causal dependencies among variables were examined using the R package piecewiseSEM 2.3.0 (Lefcheck 2016). Details for the SEM analysis are described in Supplementary Appendix S1.

Results

Phylogenetic Inference

Both coalescent and concatenated approaches generated robustly supported and highly consistent topologies based on different UCE matrices (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. S7–S12; Supplementary Tables S4–S9), supporting the division of two subclades within the genus. These two subclades can also be morphologically separated by the immaculate wings (clade A) and the wings with pale whitish or yellowish markings (clade B) (Fig. 3). Most of the morphology-defined species groups proposed in previous studies are well supported by both UCEs and mt matrices (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. S9–S12). Based on the present results together with further examination of morphological characters (Supplementary Appendix S2), we merge the P. sabahensis species group into the P. dichrous species group, the P. changninganus species group into the P. assamensis species group and the P. xanthodes species group into the P. costalis species group. P. dulongjiangensis is recovered sister to all remaining species of the clade A, except the P. xingshanensis species group. Considering its unique morphology, we exclude P. dulongjiangensis from the P. davidi species group. P. ishizukai with its closely related species was recovered sister to the P. differentialis species group and should be placed into this species group considering their morphological similarity. In sum, the present phylogenomic analyses resulted in a division of 13 species groups (four in the clade A and nine in the clade B; Fig. 3). Only three species, P. dulongjiangensis, P. tingtingae and P. hainanensis, are not affiliated with any species group. The monophyly of most species groups was robustly supported by the mtPRO dataset, however, the relationships among them were generally unresolved (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Notably, our results from the UCEs recovered the phylogenetic positions of the species or species groups with dark-colored bodies and wings. For example, the P. tonkinensis species group (all species dark-colored) is sister to the P. assamensis species group (most species pale-colored), the P. differentialis species group (most species dark-colored) is sister to P. hainanensis (a pale-colored species) and P. infectus (a dark-colored species) is sister to the P. guangxiensis species group (all species pale-colored).

Divergence Time Estimation

Our two-step molecular dating provided a phylogenomic-based and more precise timescale for Corydalinae (Supplementary Fig. S14; Supplementary Table S13) as well as the first diversification timeframe for Protohermes (Supplementary Fig. S15; Supplementary Table S14). Our results dated the divergence of crown Corydalinae during the mid-Cretaceous (104.84 Ma, 95% CI: 126.03–90.88 Ma), followed by the origin (stem) of Protohermes (92.17 Ma, 95% CI: 108.92–75.79 Ma). Despite the Cretaceous origin, the diversification within the genus (crown) took place more recently in the mid-Miocene (14.76 Ma, 95% CI: 18.91–11.36 Ma). The temporal evolution of Protohermes is further divided into two phases. Most of the species groups diverged during ca. 13–10 Ma, while most extant species originated after ca. 5 Ma (Fig. 3).

Trait Evolution

The phylomorphospace analysis of genital characters reveals significant divergence both among and within species groups, characterized by the distinct distances, with only minor overlaps observed between several groups (Fig. 4b and c). The evolution of genital morphology experienced a substantial increase in among-clade disparity ca. 10.5 Ma, indicating the expansion of morphospace resulting from rapid diversification during its early phase (Fig. 6f).

Our ASE on color evolution suggests that the common ancestor of Protohermes was pale-colored, so as ancestors of early-branching lineages. Our results further indicate that the evolution of darkened coloration likely took place independently in seven subclades within clade B (Fig. 3). These shifts from pale to dark coloration were not temporally synchronized but mostly occurred after the two peaks of speciation events, characterized by increases in CTT rates that lag behind the increase in net diversification rates (Fig. 6e; Supplementary Fig. S20). Following the rapid divergence between most species groups (ca. 10 Ma), the initial shift to dark coloration took place in P. spectabilis, P. tonkinensis species group, P. fruhstorferi species group, and P. differentialis species group during the middle to late Miocene. Meanwhile, the phenotypic DTT curve began to deviate above the 95% expectation of the simulated null model under a BM, showing a delayed accumulation of disparity after the speciation events (Fig. 6g).

Subsequent transitions between binary coloration states mostly took place following the species-level diversification, such as the shifts from pale to dark coloration in P. karubei, P. curvicornis, and P. hunanensis after the Pliocene (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S20). Notably, the only secondary reversal from dark to pale coloration was observed in the P. differentialis species group during this period (Fig. 3). Concurrently, the phenotypic DTT curve experienced a sharp increase, suggesting a prominent rise in the within-clade phenotypic disparity (Fig. 6g).

The evolution of ecological traits in Protohermes demonstrates a pattern of niche conservatism. The DTT curve falls within the 95% distribution of the null model under a BM throughout most of the evolutionary history, with a notable increase in within-clade disparity only occurring after ca. 2 Ma (Fig. 6h).

Biogeographic Analyses

Our ARC analyses, conducted using different buffer sizes (diameters of 20 km and 50 km), yielded similar results with minor intercepts (0.11 for the 50 km buffer and 0.09 for the 20 km buffer) and positive slopes (Supplementary Fig. S16). Furthermore, comparisons between sister species pairs indicate that most of the sister species are allopatric, with the exception of several pairs within the P. dichrous species group (Borneo) and the P. sinuolatus species group (Indochina) (Supplementary Fig. S16; Supplementary Table S16).

Our results suggest the ancestral range of Protohermes to be Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. + Indochina + Borneo. Subsequently, the common ancestor of Protohermes in different areas split into two subclades during ca. 15 Ma, through vicariance events between the clade A in Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. and the clade B in Indochina plus Borneo (Fig. 5a). The P. dichrous group represents the only lineage from Borneo, being diverged due to an estimated vicariance event, i.e., the isolation of Borneo from the Asian continent and other islands.

Two distinct phases in the spatial diversification were also detected for Protohermes. During the first phase (ca. 15–6 Ma), vicariance events resulted in the divergence of the genus in the ancestral range into three different lineages, followed by intensive speciation events within the ranges of Indochina and Himalayas-Hengduan Mts., as well as subsequent emigrations to their adjacent areas (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. S17). In the second phase (after ca. 5 Ma), in-situ speciation rates increased again within these two areas as well as in Borneo and Southern China (Fig. 5e). Meanwhile, vicariance events between different biogeographic areas were detected under the most likely scenario of the DEC model, which mainly account for speciation events of the insular fauna. Our results highlight Indochina as the center for diversification and the largest source of dispersal, followed by the Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. (Fig. 5c). These in-situ speciation events, together with continuous dispersals, likely contributed to the diversity and the current distribution pattern of the genus.

Macroevolutionary Dynamics

Our macroevolutionary analyses were conducted using different approaches assuming rates of diversification can behave slightly differently. Based on the results from EBD in RevBayes, a negligible extinction rate is recovered through time (Supplementary Fig. S19). The net diversification rate of Protohermes displays repeated increases during ca. 15–10 Ma and ca. 6–2 Ma (Fig. 6e). A similar pattern is also indicated by the branch-specific speciation rate in ClaDS. Furthermore, the result inferred from the ClaDS2 model shows a high speciation rate during the early history of the genus (i.e., divergence of species groups) and a subsequent decrease in many subclades, except several subclades (i.e., the P. davidi species group and the P. parcus species group in the clade A, as well as the P. costalis species group in clade B) experienced a secondary increase in speciation rate (Fig. 6a).

Testing Different Drivers of Diversification

Testing for the trait-dependent models in HiSSE, we found that two CID-2 (2-state character-independent) models were preferred among a set of 32 models (Supplementary Fig. S21; Supplementary Table S18), which assume the evolution of a binary trait to be independent of the diversification process (Beaulieu and O’Meara 2016). Thus, the presence of complex wing markings or darkened coloration may not have directly promoted the diversification of Protohermes. Similar results were obtained with GeoHiSSE, where the best-fitting models for each target area belonged to the CID model series (Supplementary Fig. S22; Supplementary Table S19). This suggests that the diversification process of Protohermes is independent of any specific geographic area.

Our likelihood-based time-continuous environment-dependent analyses using RPANDA suggest the constant pure-birth model (BCST) is the best-fitting model, followed by the temperature-dependent model (BTempVar; Supplementary Appendix S2; Supplementary Table S20). Considering the statistical equivalence of these two models (ΔAICc < 2), the paleotemperature may still have a substantial impact on the diversification of Protohermes. From this result, the speciation rate of the genus is negatively correlated with the past temperature, while no extinction rate is detected. Besides, the best-fitting model among the monsoon-dependent models suggests a weak correlation between speciation and monsoon strength (Fig. 6c; Supplementary Table S20). On the other hand, the Bayesian autocorrelated model implemented in RevBayes indicates weak dependencies of both the speciation and extinction rates on the examined paleo-environmental variables (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Appendix S2).

Our structural equation model suggests that temperature likely had a positive effect on both the species richness and the ratio of darkened species (RD). Additionally, species richness may have also been positively influenced by the forest coverage and topographic heterogeneity. In contrast, both ecological and topographic heterogeneity were found to negatively affect RD. Species richness showed positive effects on both genital FDiv and RD but might have negatively influenced divergence of marking patterns within an area (phenotypic FDiv). No direct influence of environmental factors on the genital or phenotypic FDiv was detected (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Spatiotemporal Diversification of Protohermes

According to the most likely diversification scenario, the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Protohermes once ranged from the Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. to Borneo during the middle Miocene, which were connected by land at that time (Hall 2013). The genus initially split into one subclade in the Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. and another in Indochina plus Borneo (Fig. 5a), which may result from the vicariance events during the lateral extrusion of Indochina (Li et al. 2024). Since then, these two subclades have experienced different spatiotemporal evolutionary trajectories.

The species diversity of clade A (MRCA in Himalayas-Hengduan Mts.) was mainly shaped through the in-situ speciation events within the Himalayas and adjacent regions. Some of the late-derived lineages, such as species of the P. xingshanensis species group and P. davidi species group, immigrated into Southern China or Indochina and experienced subsequent speciation. A similar pattern has also been reported for other lineages in animals or plants (Xing and Ree 2017; Zhao and Li 2017; Ye et al. 2019).

For clade B (MRCA in Indochina and Borneo), the split between the Bornean lineage (i.e., the P. dichrous species group) and its sister lineage in Indochina took place in the late Miocene (ca. 10 Ma). The early diversification within this subclade took place rapidly in Indochina, with subsequent increased colonization events to the Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. and Southern China (Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18). These northward dispersals are notable for the diversification of many lineages in clade B, such as the P. assamensis species group and the P. costalis species group. Likewise, the divergence times of many species of clade B within Southern China appear to take place after the immigration from Indochina.

Besides the Bornean-endemic species, all remaining insular species also affiliate with clade B. These species might have originated from an array of different immigration events from the mainland during the late Miocene (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18), which were predominantly accompanied by allopatric speciation. For instance, species of the P. tonkinensis species group might have immigrated from mainland Asia to Sumatra and Java, subsequently diverging into three allopatric species due to transgression and island isolations. Similarly, species from the Japanese Archipelago and Ryukyu Islands might have diverged following different dispersal events of the mainland lineages of the P. costalis species group, which took place from the late Miocene to the Pliocene. During this period, Taiwan, Ryukyus, and the western part of the Japanese Archipelago were intermittently connected (Sibuet and Hsu 2004; Tojo et al. 2017), which could have facilitated these dispersals. Subsequent transgression events could further lead to the allopatric speciation of these species. Interestingly, P. hainanensis, a species endemic to Hainan Island, may have an Indochina origin as suggested by our ARE analysis. A similar scenario of Indochina-to-Hainan dispersal has also been discovered in other groups of animals and plants, which has been hypothesized to be linked to the southward rotation of Hainan from Indochina during the middle Cenozoic (Zhu 2016; Luo and Li 2017). Nevertheless, this hypothesis has been questioned by geographic evidence (Ali 2018) and contradicted by the estimated ages from both our present results (ca. 9.92 Ma, 95% CI: 12.48–7.47 Ma) and other studies (Shih et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2021b).

Abiotic Factors Underlying the Non-adaptive Diversification of Protohermes

All Protohermes species share a similar immature lifestyle, which is highly dependent on microhabitats in montane streams or rivers. This indicates a relatively low level of niche divergence (New and Theischinger 1993; Hayashi 1995; Yang and Liu 2010), consistent with the limited ecological divergence among clades revealed by the DTT analysis (Fig. 6h). The ARC analyses further suggest that allopatric or parapatric speciation events were likely dominant throughout the diversification of Protohermes (Supplementary Fig. S16). These factors contradict one of the key characteristics of adaptive radiation, where rapid speciation events are the result of ecological divergence and often take place sympatrically across a broad geographic area (Gavrilets and Losos 2009; Glor 2010). In contrast, abiotic factors such as the geographic or environmental vicariance may have played a significant role in driving the diversification in our present case of Protohermes.

Our biogeographic analyses reveal a remarkable asymmetric pattern of diversification across space, supporting the hypothesis that Indochina and Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. served as the diversification centers for Protohermes (Yang and Liu 2010). Nevertheless, we did not detect accelerated diversification depending on a specific biogeographic area with GeoHiSSE (Supplementary Table S19), which may be attributed to the lack of a single dominant mode in the spatiotemporal diversification of Protohermes. Our ARE and BSM analyses also suggest that the diversification of Protohermes may vary distinctly across different subclades, with different geographic areas together contributing to the formation of the diversity.

The Cenozoic orogeny of the Himalayas and adjacent areas caused by the India-Eurasia collision is widely recognized as a significant driver of biological diversification in this area (Averyanov et al. 2003; Favre et al. 2015; Lei et al. 2015; Condamine et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2021a; Xia et al. 2022). The rapid orogeny during the Miocene aligns closely with the timeline of the first peak of radiation (ca. 10 Ma) in Protohermes, which is characterized by the sharp increase in diversification rates with the ClaDS2 model and the EBD model, as well as high in-situ speciation rates from BSMs. Orogenetic events likely enhanced environmental heterogeneity, forming numerous geographic barriers that could inhibit the gene flow among populations. These processes could have further facilitated numerous allopatric speciation events (Rahbek et al. 2019; Hrivniak et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021; Boschman and Condamine 2022). Given the mountain-dwelling habit and limited capacity of long-distance dispersal, speciation in Protohermes is highly likely to have been influenced by vicariance associated with orogeny. This hypothesis is also supported by the positive correlation between species richness and topographic heterogeneity in our SEM analysis. Regarding the secondary acceleration of diversification after ca. 5 Ma, the most notable increases in speciation rate occur in the lineages of P. davidi + P. parcus species groups and the P. costalis species group (Fig. 6a). These lineages exhibit a congruent pattern where the early speciation mainly took place in the Hengduan Mts., followed by subsequent colonization in Southern China (Figs. 5e and 6a; Supplementary Fig. S18). The rapid uplift of the Hengduan Mts. after the late Miocene (Kirby et al. 2002; Clark et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2012) might have acted as a major driver of diversification for various organisms in this region (Sun et al. 2017; Xing and Ree 2017; Ye et al. 2019; Shi et al. 2024). This important orogenic event might have a persistent influence on the allopatric speciation of Protohermes. Furthermore, the subsequent colonization of these lineages into new areas, where unoccupied niches and abundant resources were available, might have further promoted these secondary diversification accelerations.

Borneo harbors a remarkably high insular species diversity of Protohermes, particularly for its limited land area. Ten species belonging to the P. dichrous species group are found on this island, constituting ca. 10% of the total species richness of the genus (Fig. 1d). In comparison, other major islands of Asia harbor no more than 11 species of Megaloptera each (i.e., Honshu: 11, Taiwan: seven, Hainan: seven, Sumatra: six, Java: four), with only one species of Protohermes on each of these islands (Fig. 1d). Although we did not detect accelerated diversification events in Bornean lineages from the branch-specific diversification rates (Fig. 6a) and GeoHiSSE, the BSM simulations show a high in-situ speciation rate in Borneo throughout the Pliocene (Fig. 5e). The earlier origin and colonization of Bornean lineage, compared to those on the remaining islands likely provided a longer history of residence and speciation—a phenomenon known as “time-for-speciation” (McPeek and Brown 2007). Throughout the diversification history of the P. dichrous species group, the widespread evergreen rainforests in the Sundaland may have acted as a “wet refugia”, supporting its sustained speciation by providing abundant niches and resources to harbor the high diversity (de Bruyn et al. 2014). Other factors, such as the orogeny of Mt. Kinabalu during the late Miocene (Hall 2011), may have also promoted the diversification.

Rapid changes in the paleoenvironment could be another factor that promoted the diversification of Protohermes. As suggested by the paleoenvironment-dependent birth-death models implemented in RPANDA, the speciation rate of Protohermes increased under the influence of continuous global cooling (Fig. 6c). Since the late Miocene, the global cooling accompanied by aridification has been suggested to promote the reorganization of terrestrial ecosystems (Potter and Szatmari 2009; Herbert et al. 2016). Consequently, a stepwise expansion in grass-dominated habitats led to the restriction and fragmentation of the rainforests (Heaney 1991; Pound et al. 2012; Jacques et al. 2013; Morley 2018). For the organisms which highly reliant on forest and aquatic environments, such as our present case of Protohermes, habitat fragmentation could have facilitated the isolation between populations in different forest refuges, which may further result in accelerated allopatric speciation.

Genital Divergence Facilitated Lineage Diversification

Our results reveal a remarkable disparity between species groups in genital phylomorphospace (Fig. 4b and c). Spatially, the genital morphological functional divergence (FDiv) of Protohermes was significantly positively influenced by the species richness (Fig. 7), coinciding well with the pattern from reproductive character displacement (RCD). A notable example is the P. dichrous species group in Borneo, which shows similar external characters and unusually high range overlaps among some closely related species. In contrast, the genitalia of these species are highly divergent (Fig. 4; Liu et al. 2008, 2013a). RCD is one of the key outcomes of assortative mating, featured by more divergent genital characters between sympatric populations than allopatric populations. Moreover, RCD is one of the reinforcement mechanisms of the speciation process, which could contribute to the enhancement of prezygotic reproductive barriers, further facilitating species coexistence and lineage accumulation within a limited area. However, genital divergence can also accumulate as a by-product of allopatric speciation, with secondary contact and lineage accumulations within an area potentially resulting in a similar pattern (Hollander et al. 2018). Our result from DTT shows a distinct rapid diversification in genital morphology in Protohermes generally congruent with the early phase of lineage diversification (Fig. 6f). Based on our results from biogeographic analyses, which highlights the dominant role of allopatric/parapatric speciation in lineage diversification of Protohermes, it is more likely that a substantial proportion of genital divergence initially accumulated in isolated areas as a by-product of speciation events. Following the speciation process, persistent diversification of genital characters may result from natural selection or various types of sexual selection, or may even be promoted by other mechanisms such as founder effect (Matute 2013).

Among the possible scenarios, genital morphology might diverge rapidly through natural selection favoring species isolation, leading to mechanical or sensory incompatibilities in heterospecific pairings—a concept commonly referred to as the “lock-and-key” theory (Eberhard 2015). Nevertheless, copulation in Protohermes does not involve direct contact between male and female genitalia. In addition, female genital morphology is generally conserved and almost identical across species despite the divergence in male genitalia. Both pieces of evidence suggest that mechanical or sensory incompatibility mechanisms likely play a minor role in the species isolation of this group. Moreover, this mechanism requires range overlap among closely related species, which appears uncommon in Protohermes (Supplementary Fig. S16a). A notable exception within the genus is the P. differentialis group from northern Indochina and adjacent areas, which stands out for the highly divergent genital structures in both sexes (Liu and Yang 2006, Liu et al. 2009a, 2024). The significant genital divergence in both sexes might represent a pattern of RCD, in which these reproductive traits may have experienced enhanced selective pressure in areas with higher species diversity, potentially serving as a stronger reinforcement of species barriers within this group. Alternatively, rapid genital divergence in this genus is more likely to have been driven by sexual selection, particularly through post-copulatory mechanisms such as cryptic female choice or male-male competition (Eberhard 2015; Langerhans et al. 2016; Hollander et al. 2018). Distinguishing the specific mechanism underlying such a phenomenon remains challenging given the current evidence. However, considering the mating behavior of Protohermes, their genital diversification may have been driven by one of these selective pressures, co-evolving alongside the size and complex structures of spermatophores. Male genitalia, which grasp the dorsal part of the spermatophores, may be diversified in response to divergence in size and external shape of the spermatophores. As a consequence, genital divergence may have further promoted the process of lineage diversification by facilitating species coexistence and lineage accumulation (Hosken and Stockley 2004; Eberhard 2015; Langerhans et al. 2016).

A Late Burst in Phenotypic Disparity with Convergent Evolution of Darkened Coloration

Our results highlight a phenotypic diversification within Protohermes after the acceleration of speciation events, with a remarkable pattern of convergent evolution in darkened coloration in bodies and wings. The color evolution in Protohermes occurred following either the first or second peaks of radiation in our time-calibrated phylogeny (Fig. 6e and f), with non-simultaneous transitions from pale to dark coloration taking place in seven different lineages of clade B (Fig. 3). Notably, body and wing marking patterns also underwent a substantial increase in within-clade disparity after the second peak of speciation, largely aligning with the second phase of coloration changes (Fig. 6g; Supplementary Fig. S20). As suggested by the SEM (Fig. 7), the diversified marking patterns may not possess direct biological functions and thus were likely accumulated from the speciation events (Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019). However, they may have contributed to the further phenotypic divergences, particularly with the occurrence of darkened coloration.

The evolution of darkened coloration in Protohermes is remarkable for its association with the shift in adult biological rhythms (Figs. 1a–c; Hayashi 1995). From field observations, some species in the P. tonkinensis species group (Fig. 1b and c; Supplementary Fig. S1) are active during the daytime and co-occur with some toxic or unpalatable moths bearing similar coloration and wing markings, such as some species of Eterusia (Lepidoptera: Zygaenidae) and Macrobrochis (Lepidoptera: Erebidae) (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). The distinct appearances of these Protohermes species—characterized by blackish coloration, prominent whitish wing markings, and orange pronotum—exhibit significant divergence from most other species in the genus but closely resemble the patterns found in these toxic lepidopteran species. As these Protohermes species exhibit limited unpalatability in laboratory experiments, their specialized phenotypic traits may represent aposematic Batesian mimicry, likely serving to deter predation during their daytime activities (Hayashi 1995). Notably, similar aposematic appearances are also found in other unpalatable insect groups, such as the butterfly genus Delias (Lepidoptera: Pieridae) (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5; Müller et al. 2013). In areas such as Sumatra and Java, where the formerly mentioned models (i.e., the Eterusia and Macrobrochis species) are absent, Delias butterflies might serve as potential mimetic models for the darkened Protohermes species. The convergent evolution of aposematic mimicry might have been accelerated by the interspecific interactions and competitions after the build-up of species diversity (Fig. 7; Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019; Folk et al. 2019). Consequently, these coloration divergences have likely facilitated niche partitioning within the genus and further enabled the coexistence of more species of Protohermes in the same area.

On the other hand, the darkened coloration in some nocturnal species (Fig. 1b), such as P. niger from the Hengduan Mts. and P. spectabilis from Borneo, may serve different functions compared to the diurnal darkened species. The darkened coloration is restricted to males of these species, while the females exhibit much paler coloration, displaying distinct sexual dimorphism (Supplementary Fig. S1; Liu et al. 2008; Chang et al. 2013). Such specialized phenotypes are hypothesized to function as signals for species recognition, potentially having evolved under sexual selection (Chang et al. 2013). Studies on various animal groups have suggested that coloration traits associated with sexual signals tend to evolve following the build-up of species richness, likely playing a role in species recognition and reinforcement of prezygotic reproductive barriers (Martin et al. 2010; Iyengar et al. 2014; Gómez-Llano et al. 2021; Puissant et al. 2023).

Conclusions

Here, we take the dobsonfly genus Protohermes, the most diverse genus of the holometabolous insect order Megaloptera, as a case study to investigate the complex processes and mechanisms of evolutionary radiations. Based on the genome-scale datasets of UCEs and mitochondrial loci, we provide the first time-calibrated phylogeny based on comprehensive taxon coverage. Our results revealed Indochina and Himalayas-Hengduan Mts. as the diversification center of the genus, while the rapid orogeny as well as paleoenvironmental changes could have facilitated geographic vicariance and further promoted the allopatric/parapatric speciation within the genus. The remarkable genital divergence among lineages may have occurred accompanying lineage diversification and was possibly further enhanced as a form of speciation reinforcement, which likely facilitated species coexistence and lineage accumulation. The diversification of phenotypic traits, such as coloration and body markings, occurred later than the increases of speciation rates and might have been promoted after the build-up of species richness and secondary contact after allopatric/parapatric speciation. Consequently, these phenotypic disparities have likely contributed to niche partitioning and assortative mating, further enabling more species to coexist within a limited range. Our present study emphasizes the role of geographic vicariance and sexual selection in driving non-adaptive radiations, which have further shaped the taxonomic and phenotypic diversity of insect lineages.
