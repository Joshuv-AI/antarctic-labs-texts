d-Dimer levels during pregnancy and postpartum: non-applicability of regularly used cut-offs for diagnosis of suspected pulmonary embolism
Authors: E Ranieri, W Korte, G Brandi, S Kalimeris, N Ochsenbein, C Haslinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess d-dimer levels during pregnancy and postpartum period and evaluate the appropriateness of commonly used cut-offs for ruling out pulmonary embolism (PE) in this population.MethodsSecondary analysis of the prospective PPH 1300 study conducted at the University Hospital Zurich, including 1309 women between 25 + 0 and 42 + 3 weeks of gestation without acute thromboembolic events. d-Dimer levels were measured at admission for delivery and 24–48 h postpartum, stratified into four gestational age groups.ResultsMedian d-dimer values were 1.56 mg/L (IQR 1.20–2.12) antepartum and 1.78 mg/L (IQR 1.25–2.84) postpartum. Antepartum, 99.3% of women had levels ≥ 0.5 mg/L and 88.8% ≥ 1.0 mg/L; postpartum, 98.8% and 88.1% exceeded these cut-offs, respectively. Antepartum values showed an increasing trend with gestational age, whereas postpartum values remained uniformly elevated. No clinically relevant thromboembolic events occurred during the index hospitalization.ConclusionsNearly all pregnant and postpartum women exceed conventional d-dimer thresholds also in the absence of thromboembolism. Standard cut-offs of 0.5 and 1.0 mg/L are therefore unsuitable in obstetric practice, limiting the applicability of general diagnostic algorithms, such as YEARS and Geneva, unless specifically adapted. Trimester- and postpartum-adjusted thresholds or alternative biomarkers are needed to reduce unnecessary imaging and improve clinical management.Keywords: Venous thromboembolism, Thrombosis risk, Hypercoagulability, Biomarkers, Clinical decision rules, Diagnostic algorithms, Pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm, Geneva score

What does this study add to the clinical work

D-dimer levels are markedly elevated in nearly all women during the pregnancy and postpartum period, limiting their diagnostic value and reliability in guiding imaging decisions for suspected pulmonary embolism.

Introduction

d-Dimer is a fibrin degradation product commonly used in clinical practice as a diagnostic marker for the exclusion of thromboembolic events, particularly pulmonary embolism (PE). In the general population, elevated d-dimer levels are often indicative of ongoing coagulation and fibrinolysis, thus may suggest the presence of PE or other thrombotic conditions [1–5].

However, the interpretation of d-dimer levels in pregnant and postpartum women remains a complex issue due to physiological changes that occur during these periods. Pregnancy induces a hypercoagulable state, characterized by increased levels of clotting factors and fibrinogen, as well as changes in the fibrinolytic system. These alterations result in elevated d-dimer concentrations, making it difficult to distinguish between normal pregnancy-related changes and the presence of thromboembolic events [6]. Similarly, postpartum changes in the coagulation system can result in persistently elevated d-dimer levels, further complicating the diagnostic process [7].

The use of conventional d-dimer cut-offs to rule out PE in pregnant and postpartum women can lead to false-positive results (i.e., failure to rule out PE), potentially prompting unnecessary and invasive diagnostic investigations such as CT pulmonary angiography, which carries risks, including exposure to radiation and contrast agents. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that clinical assessment of typical signs of pulmonary embolism (such as tachycardia, dyspnea) is also difficult to distinguish from physiological symptoms in late pregnancy. These issues raise significant concerns about the clinical applicability of standard d-dimer thresholds in these specific patient populations. Consequently, it is critical to reassess the appropriateness of existing diagnostic criteria and develop more suitable guidelines tailored to the unique physiological context of pregnancy and the postpartum period.

The ARTEMIS study [8], which focused on the management of suspected acute pulmonary embolism in pregnant patients, introduced the Pregnancy-Adapted YEARS Algorithm [1]. This algorithm proposes that CT pulmonary angiography should be performed in case of suspected PE, when d-dimer values are greater than or equal to 1 mg/L. Additionally, the algorithm recommends performing CT pulmonary angiography in the presence of at least one YEARS criterion (such as clinical signs and symptoms suggestive of PE) when D-dimer values exceed 0.5 mg/L. Accordingly, the algorithm allows CT scans to be omitted if D-dimer levels are below the corresponding cut-off values.

Similarly, the revised Geneva score [9], another widely used clinical decision rule for PE diagnosis, incorporates d-dimer levels as part of its algorithm. In this revised version, the d-dimer threshold is set at 0.5 mg/L, a value that is more sensitive but potentially less specific when applied to pregnant and postpartum women.

Both the YEARS algorithm and the revised Geneva score represent important steps toward adapting PE diagnostic protocols for pregnant women, yet these adaptations have not fully addressed the challenges presented by the physiological alterations in coagulation during pregnancy and the postpartum period. The aim of this study was to analyze d-dimer levels in a prospective cohort of 1309 women without thromboembolic events shortly before and after delivery to better characterized the physiological values in pregnancy and postpartum.

Methods

This was a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study, the “PPH 1300 study [10]”, which was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics at the University Hospital Zurich, Zurich, Switzerland, between October 2015 and November 2016. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Kantonale Ethikkommission Zürich, KEK-ZH 2015-0011, Clinical Trials.gov registration NCT02604602) and was in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects.

This study included pregnant women between 25 + 0 and 42 + 3 weeks of pregnancy. They were stratified by gestational age at the time of delivery into 4 groups, labeled A, B, C, and D. Group A included women between 25 + 0 and 29 + 6 weeks of pregnancy, group B between 30 + 0 and 34 + 6, group C between 35 + 0 and 36 + 6, and group D between 37 + 0 and 42 + 3. Blood samples for d-dimer measurement were taken at admission at birth (“antepartum blood sample”) and 24 to 48 h after delivery (“postpartum blood sample”). It is important to mention that d-dimer measurements were performed only after the mother study (PPH-1300 study) had stopped, so there was no possibility for any treatment bias. In the total patient cohort (n = 1309) and in each group (A, B, C, D), the median d-dimer value (with interquartile range, IQR) and the percentage of women with a d-dimer value ≥ 0.5 mg/l and ≥ 1 mg/l, both in the antepartum and postpartum period, were calculated.

None of the patients were hospitalized for suspected venous thromboembolism. All women underwent routine clinical surveillance for signs and symptoms of venous thromboembolism during their hospital stay. Electronic health records were reviewed for any diagnostic imaging or therapeutic anticoagulation initiated for suspected thrombosis. As our institution functions as the tertiary referral center for the canton of Zurich, postpartum PE cases are typically referred back to our hospital, allowing identification of events occurring after discharge. No symptomatic PE was recorded during the index hospitalization or the subsequent six-week postpartum period.

To identify any venous thromboembolic events during follow-up, we cross-referenced clinical records with diagnostic coding for PE and manually reviewed the medical files of all participants flagged through this search.

d-Dimer levels were measured on stored blood samples of the primary study [10], using the Hemosil d-Dimer Assay for ACL TOP (Werfen).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of the prospective “PPH 1300” cohort [10], we sought to clarify how routinely used d-dimer cut-offs would perform in the obstetric population by quantifying ante- and postpartum d-dimer levels across different gestational ages. Among 1,309 women delivering between 25 + 0 and 42 + 3 weeks, we found that 99% of all samples exceeded the conventional rule-out threshold of 0.5 mg/L and nearly 90% lay above 1.0 mg/L. Even the earliest gestational subgroup (25–29 weeks) showed markedly elevated values, with concentrations rising further as gestation advanced and remaining uniformly high 24–48 h postpartum.

The clinical diagnosis of pulmonary embolism (PE) in pregnancy is notably complex. Physiological pregnancy-related symptoms, such as dyspnea, tachycardia, and leg swelling, frequently overlap with manifestations of acute PE, markedly lowering diagnostic specificity [11, 12]. In addition, pregnancy induces profound hemostatic changes, including increases in fibrin turnover and thrombin generation, which drive d-dimer levels far beyond non-pregnant adult ranges. These physiological elevations erode the specificity on which standard PE diagnostic algorithms rely. Imaging strategies are further complicated by concerns about fetal radiation exposure and variability across institutions, as highlighted by recent guidelines.

d-dimer-guided strategies, such as the YEARS algorithm [1] and the revised Geneva score [9], have reshaped adult PE diagnostics by combining clinical pre-test probability with fixed thresholds (0.5 mg/L for standard rule-out and 1.0 mg/L for very low pre-test probability). The prospective YEARS trial (n = 3465) avoided thoracic imaging in 13% of adults with low 3-month PE rates, and Geneva cohorts demonstrated comparable performance. Subsequent head-to-head studies—such as ADJUST-PE [2] (age-adjusted thresholds), PEGeD [3] (three-level Wells with a 1.0 mg/L threshold in low-probability patients) and the 2024 HEAD-TO-HEAD multicenter comparison [13]—consistently showed that probability-adapted cut-offs outperform the traditional 0.5 mg/L threshold in the general population.

However, pregnancy and the puerperium remain outliers. Several studies [14–16] demonstrate that standard d-dimer thresholds have limited diagnostic performance in pregnancy. Nearly all pregnant women, particularly in the third trimester, exceed the 0.5 mg/L cut-off, often surpassing 1–3 mg/L even in the absence of disease [17]. The pregnancy-adapted YEARS model (van der Pol et al., NEJM 2019, ARTEMIS) permitted a uniform 1.0 mg/L threshold irrespective of pre-test probability and safely avoided imaging in up to 65% of cases, with a 0% three-month venous thromboembolism rate. We note that the YEARS algorithm is most effective in the first trimester, with reduced specificity in the third trimester as d-dimer levels rise physiologically.

Our large, real-world prospective cohort reinforces these findings. Almost all women exceeded 0.5 mg/L, and 88% exceeded 1.0 mg/L both before and after delivery, yet no clinically apparent PE occurred during the index episode and no events of deep vein thrombosis were recorded either. This mirrors observations from EPIPHANY [18], DiPEP [14] and the PEGeD [3] pregnancy subgroup, all of which reported nearly universal “positive” d-dimers despite extremely low venous thromboembolism incidence. When almost every pregnant or postpartum patient surpasses standard thresholds, the test loses discriminatory capacity and risks prompting unnecessary computed tomography pulmonary angiography or ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy with associated radiation and contrast exposure.

A major strength of this study is its size: to our knowledge, it represents one of the largest prospective real-life obstetric cohorts with systematically collected d-dimer measurements under routine-care conditions. The data of this study may be utilized to establish pregnancy-specific values for d-dimers in a general obstetrics population, as the blood samples were drawn in all women of the primary study, in a standardized way (antepartum at admission to delivery and 24 to 48 h postpartum) independently of the clinical situation of the woman. Of note, the treating physicians were not aware of the d-dimer levels, as these were measured only after the finalization of the study. The absence of clinically apparent acute venous thromboembolism supports our hypothesis that conventional 0.5 mg/L and 1.0 mg/L thresholds are not suitable in pregnancy or the puerperium to rule out a PE. The main limitation is the lack of systematic PE screening and long-term follow-up; delayed events occurring after the six-week postpartum window, when the postpartum checkup was performed, cannot be excluded. However, as a tertiary referral center for the canton of Zurich, women developing postpartum PE are typically redirected to our institution, making it unlikely—though not impossible—that cases were missed. Additionally, both postpartum inflammation and immobilization tend to diminish over time, further reducing the risk of late thromboembolic events. However, it must be clearly stated as a possible limitation that we did not screen actively every woman for a postpartum PE and thus PE events could have been missed.

In summary, standard d-dimer thresholds show limited diagnostic specificity in pregnant and postpartum women due to physiologically elevated baseline levels. Future diagnostic pathways must incorporate pregnancy-specific thresholds or alternative biomarkers to balance safety with the imperative to reduce unnecessary imaging.
