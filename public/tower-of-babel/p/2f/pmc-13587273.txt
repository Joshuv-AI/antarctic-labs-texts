Large aneurysm of the right atrial and the tetralogy of fallot: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Ehsan Aghaei Moghadam, Mohammadreza Mirzaaghayan, Sadatinejad Seyyed Mohsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCongenital right atrial (RA) aneurysm is a rare cardiac anomaly, and its association with complex cyanotic heart disease, such as tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), is exceptionally uncommon. This combination poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationWe report the case of a 22-month-old boy with uncorrected TOF who was found to have a giant RA aneurysm (approximately 3 × 5 cm) causing significant compression of the left ventricle. The diagnosis was confirmed by transthoracic echocardiography and computed tomography angiography. The patient underwent complete single-stage surgical repair, including TOF correction (ventricular septal defect closure and right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction) and RA aneurysmectomy with patch closure of a large communication between the RA and the aneurysmal sac.OutcomeDespite a technically successful operation, the patient developed catastrophic hemodynamic collapse several hours postoperatively and could not be resuscitated. Post-mortem examination revealed re-expansion of the aneurysm with thrombus within the pericardial space, adjacent to the left ventricle. While the exact mechanism of hemodynamic collapse remains uncertain, the post-mortem findings suggest that the distended aneurysmal sac may have contributed to impaired cardiac filling or reduced ventricular preload. We hypothesize that residual vascular communications to the aneurymal cavity may have contributed to this fatal complication.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare and fatal association between a giant RA aneurysm and TOF. It underscores the critical importance of meticulous intraoperative evaluation for potential coronary-cameral fistulae or additional venous drainage into the aneurysmal sac. We recommend direct intraoperative testing, such as cardioplegic injection, to identify and ligate these vessels, which may be crucial for preventing fatal postoperative re-expansion in similar cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04371-8.Keywords: Right atrial aneurysm, Tetralogy of Fallot, Congenital heart disease, Case report

Introduction

Right atrial (RA) aneurysms are rare congenital abnormalities, reported far less frequently than left atrial aneurysms [1, 2]. Patients may be asymptomatic or present with atrial arrhythmias, thromboembolism, or heart failure [2]. Dyspnea and palpitation are the most common clinical presentations [2]. Transthoracic echocardiography is the primary diagnostic modality, with mean reported aneurysm size of 8.83 × 6.05 cm² [2]. CT or MRI may be used for confirmation [3]. Surgical resection is indicated for symptomatic patients or those at risk of complications [2, 3]. In this article, we report a case of RA aneurysm associated with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a giant RA aneurysm in a patient with unrepaired TOF where post-mortem examination was performed.

Case presentation

A 22-month-old boy was referred to our center for surgical management of congenital heart disease. He was born at term following an uneventful pregnancy and delivery. Prenatal cardiac screening had not been performed. At one month of age, he developed feeding difficulties and was diagnosed with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) by transthoracic echocardiography. Definitive surgical treatment was delayed until 18 months of age due to limited healthcare access. He was medically managed with oral propranolol and captopril during this period. At presentation to our center, the patient was asymptomatic at rest but demonstrated mild cyanosis with feeding.

On physical examination, a systolic ejection murmur was auscultated. Vital signs were within normal limits, while oxygen saturation ranged between 70% and 80%. Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with extreme axis deviation. Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly and a boot-shaped heart consistent with TOF. Repeat echocardiography revealed a giant RA aneurysm causing significant compression of the left ventricle (LV), a newly identified finding (Fig. 1). Additional echocardiographic findings were consistent with tetralogy of Fallot, including a large perimembranous ventricular septal defect (VSD) with aortic override; severe subvalvular, valvular, and supravalvular pulmonary stenosis (peak gradient: 70 mmHg); moderate tricuspid regurgitation (peak gradient: 80 mmHg); a small left atrium; a compressed LV with acceptable longitudinal dimension; normal pulmonary venous drainage; a roofed coronary sinus; and a double superior vena cava. Preoperative CT angiography confirmed the transthoracic echocardiographic findings (Fig. 2) and additionally revealed an associated horseshoe kidney. CT angiography demonstrated a RA aneurysm measuring approximately 3 × 5 cm in diameter; frame-by-frame review confirmed that all four pulmonary veins drained normally into the left atrium. No evidence of a common pulmonary venous chamber or anomalous connection to the coronary sinus was identified. Laboratory investigations were within normal limits, with no evidence of anemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulation abnormalities, or other laboratory disorders.

After obtaining written informed consent from the parents, surgical intervention was performed on the first day of 2023. The planned surgical approach included deep hypothermia and total cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) via median sternotomy. Following initiation of CPB, cardioplegic solution was administered and cardiac arrest was achieved. Intraoperatively, RA aneurysmectomy was performed. The RA aneurysm appeared as a large pulsatile chamber located posterior to the left and right ventricles (LV and RV), which decreased in size after initiation of CPB. The aneurysmal chamber was anatomically separate from the RA appendage. Upon opening the RA, a large atrial septal defect (ASD) was identified. Additionally, a small defect measuring approximately 3–4 mm was noted, which communicated directly with the aneurysmal cavity. This defect was located between the inferior vena cava (IVC) orifice, the coronary sinus (CS) ostium, and the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve. The aneurysmal sac and its communication with the RA are visible on preoperative CT imaging (Fig. 2). The coronary sinus orifice was visualized separately and was not closed during the procedure.The defect at the RA aneurysm opening was closed using a 1 × 1 cm double-layer autologous pericardial patch, and the ASD was partially closed. Complete repair of TOF was performed using a transannular patch, including pulmonary valvectomy and RV outflow tract muscle resection. Reconstruction of the pulmonary valve leaflet was performed using tissue from the RA appendage. The VSD was closed with a Gore-Tex patch. Additional procedures included aneurysmorrhaphy, tricuspid valve repair, completion of ASD closure, and patent ductus arteriosus ligation.

Approximately five hours after surgery, the patient developed progressive hypotension in the intensive care unit. Aggressive resuscitation was immediately initiated, including volume expansion, inotropic support (epinephrine), and mechanical ventilation optimization. Despite these measures, metabolic acidosis progressed and cardiac arrest occurred. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was promptly initiated following ACLS protocol but was unsuccessful. The patient expired. The rapid deterioration (approximately 30 min) left no window for reoperation.

Post-mortem mid-sternotomy revealed a distended RA aneurysm filled with thrombus and hemorrhagic fluid, located in close proximity to the left ventricle. Whether this finding represented true mechanical compression of the LV or an alternative mechanism such as impaired venous return or unrecognized pulmonary vein obstruction could not be definitively determined. We acknowledge that from a physiological standpoint, a low-pressure RA chamber would not be expected to compress a high-pressure LV. Therefore, the exact cause of post-operative cardiogenic shock remains speculative.

The precise mechanism of postoperative collapse remains unclear. Post-mortem revealed a thrombus-distended aneurysmal sac adjacent to the LV. However, a low-pressure RA chamber is unlikely to directly compress a high-pressure LV. Impaired venous return (reduced preload) or unrecognized pulmonary vein obstruction (see differential diagnosis) are alternative possibilities. We therefore caution against overinterpreting the post-mortem findings as definitive evidence of LV compression. For future similar cases, our surgical team recommends cardioplegic injection into the coronary vessels to identify a potential coronary–cameral fistula or additional Thebesian veins communicating with the aneurysmal cavity, followed by suture ligation of these vessels if present.

Discussion

Atrial aneurysms are rare congenital abnormalities, and their occurrence in children is even less frequent. RA aneurysm was first described by Bailey in 1955 and first surgically excised by Morrow and Behrendt in 1968 [3].

Several recent case reports in adults have highlighted potential complications of RA aneurysm that should be considered. Barberato et al. reported a 23-year-old man with a RA aneurysm measuring 15 × 8 cm, which caused RV compression. The patient had experienced two episodes of tachyarrhythmia (atrial flutter), which were treated with amiodarone, and he refused surgical intervention [1]. Shyamadeep et al. reported successful surgical resection of a RA aneurysm in a 22-year-old asymptomatic woman. On examination, her heart sounds were normal, and ECG showed no abnormalities. Echocardiographic evaluation revealed a large aneurysm measuring 8.7 × 11.7 cm. Histologically, the aneurysm wall often demonstrated lipomatous degeneration and loss of muscular elements, and a thrombus was present within the aneurysm. The patient remained well at the 2-month postoperative follow-up [4]. Sato et al. reported a 28-year-old man with a RA aneurysm. ECG was normal, while chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio of 70%. Echocardiography demonstrated posterior displacement of the RA and RV by the aneurysmal mass. The aneurysm was successfully excised surgically, and the patient had a favorable postoperative outcome [5].

Several pediatric cases of RA aneurysm have been reported. Chatrath [6] described a 5-year-old with an asymptomatic giant RA aneurysm initially misdiagnosed as Ebstein’s anomaly who underwent successful reduction and valve repair. Imren [7] reported a 15-month-old with atrial fibrillation and giant RA aneurysm who normalized rhythm after resection. Jonavicius [3] reported a 2-month-old who developed heart failure and tamponade at 14 months, successfully treated with urgent aneurysmectomy. Lee [8] operated on a 2-week-old neonate with a giant RA aneurysm compressing the RV. Tunks [9] and Zhang [10] described neonatal and pediatric cases with arrhythmia successfully managed by resection. Li [11] reported two adult cases managed conservatively. Bandyopadhyay [12], Shakerian [13], Belegrinos [14], and Imanishi [15] described various neonatal and infant cases, with outcomes ranging from successful resection to long-term conservative follow-up. More recently, Ariaka [16], Amansakhatova [17], Zhou [18], Luo [19], Bhat [20], and Wang [21] have reported additional pediatric cases, most with good outcomes after surgical resection. Congenital RA aneurysm is a rare cardiac defect that may be asymptomatic or present with a variety of clinical manifestations, including supraventricular arrhythmias, intracardiac thrombus formation, and severe atrial dilatation, which can lead to compression of adjacent cardiac chambers or, rarely, atrial wall rupture.

There is no single standardized approach for the management of RA aneurysm. Two main strategies are currently described: conservative and surgical. A conservative approach is typically recommended for asymptomatic patients with small aneurysms, who may receive low-dose aspirin and undergo regular follow-up. In contrast, patients with severe aneurysmal dilatation or clinical symptoms should undergo surgical intervention. The primary indications for surgery include atrial arrhythmia, thrombus formation, severe atrial enlargement, and compression of other cardiac chambers. Early surgical correction may prevent further dilatation of the RA and associated complications.

In the present case, the aneurysm was adjacent to the LV and raised concern for impaired cardiac filling; therefore, surgical management was indicated [3]. If no other cardiac defects are present, resection of a RA aneurysm can be performed without cardiopulmonary bypass. However, open-heart aneurysmectomy using cardiopulmonary bypass allows safe inspection of the aneurysmal sac and simultaneous repair of any associated cardiac anomalies. We suggest that in cases where other congenital heart defects coexist with an atrial aneurysm, the morphology and location of the aneurysm should be carefully evaluated before surgical intervention.

Among previously reported cases, the association of congenital heart disease with RA aneurysm has been uncommon. As noted above, our patient presented with TOF concomitant with RA aneurysm. The surgical procedure was performed without technical complications. The precise cause of death could not be definitively established; however, re-expansion of a huge aneurysm can cause hemodynamic instability through coronary arterial or venous fistula pathways, potentially leading to dilation or compression. For subsequent cases, we recommend cardioplegic injection to identify potentially additional small vessel drainage into the aneurysm, followed by suture ligation.

Complete excision of the aneurysmal sac is the recommended approach in the literature. However, at the time of surgery, the operating surgeon opted for patch closure due to concerns about the sac’s proximity to the coronary sinus. In retrospect, complete excision or subtotal resection with plication would have been a superior strategy. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our surgical approach. For future similar cases, we strongly recommend complete or subtotal resection of the aneurysmal sac rather than simple patch closure when anatomy permits. As case report authors, we present this case honestly, including its shortcomings, so that others may learn from our experience.”

A possible differential diagnosis in this case is total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC) to the coronary sinus. We acknowledge that our CT angiography lacked thin-section reconstruction, which can occasionally obscure complex venous anatomy. However, the following evidence argues against TAPVC: [1] frame-by-frame CT review showed all four pulmonary veins draining into the left atrium; [2] intraoperative direct inspection confirmed normal pulmonary venous connections; [3] the left superior vena cava drained unobstructed into the right atrium. The small left atrium and left ventricle, while typical of TAPVC, can also be seen in long-standing TOF with right-dominant physiology. Nevertheless, because TAPVC cannot be excluded with absolute certainty, we recommend intraoperative coronary sinus injection in future similar cases to rule out this diagnosis before closing any defect in this region.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare and fatal association between a giant right atrial aneurysm and tetralogy of Fallot. The exact mechanism of postoperative hemodynamic collapse remains uncertain, but post-mortem findings demonstrated a thrombus-distended aneurysmal sac adjacent to the left ventricle. For future similar cases, we recommend complete or subtotal resection of the aneurysmal sac rather than simple patch closure, and intraoperative coronary sinus injection to rule out residual communications or unrecognized TAPVC.
