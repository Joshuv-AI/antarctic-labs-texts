Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices of Nurses Regarding Pressure Injury Prevention and Management in Nablus, Palestine: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Loai M Zabin, Baraa F Shayeb, Shorouq H Draedi, Sa'ed H Zyoud, Abdullah Abdullah, Mohammad Faisal Al Ali, Mohammed F Hayek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPressure injuries are largely preventable yet remain common in hospital care. This multi‐hospital study assessed nurses' knowledge, attitudes and practices for prevention in governmental, private and charitable hospitals in Nablus, Palestine, and examined links with professional characteristics. A descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted in six hospitals in Nablus city, Palestine. A total of 231 registered nurses were recruited using convenience sampling and completed a content‐validated, self‐administered questionnaire assessing demographics, knowledge (20 items), attitudes (11 items), and practices (17 items). Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, one‐way analysis of variance with Tukey post hoc tests, and binary logistic regression for adequate knowledge and practice (≥ 80%). Overall, 64.5% demonstrated adequate knowledge, and 58.0% expressed a favourable attitude. Practice adherence averaged 70.9 ± 16.3 out of 100. Only 31.6% achieved ≥ 80%, indicating suboptimal practice in most participants. Key gaps involved massage avoidance and the use of lifting devices. Knowledge was higher with recent PI training (1–2 years vs never: OR = 4.110, 95% CI 1.622–10.414, p = 0.003; 2–3 years vs never: OR = 3.047, 95% CI 1.208–7.687, p = 0.018). Practice adequacy was higher with training < 1 year ago vs never (OR = 4.146, 95% CI 1.636–10.512, p = 0.003). Other adjusted associations were imprecise and treated as exploratory. Nurses showed generally adequate knowledge, whereas routine preventive practice remained less consistent. Recent training was associated with better knowledge and practice, suggesting that targeted education, supervised skills training, unit‐level protocols and audit with feedback may support improvement.Keywords: attitude, knowledge, nurses, Palestine, patient safety, practice, pressure injury, prevention

Introduction

Pressure injuries (PIs) are common, harmful and largely preventable in acute care. International guidelines recommend systematic risk assessment, regular repositioning, skin care, nutrition optimization and appropriate support surfaces [1]. However, implementation gaps persist, especially in resource‐constrained settings. In Palestine, available evidence is fragmented across units and hospitals, limiting system‐level insight. This study addresses that gap by examining nurses' knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) for PI prevention across governmental, private and non‐governmental organization (NGO) hospitals to identify priorities for improvement.

Implementation is frequently hindered by well‐described individual and organizational barriers, including workload/time pressure, limited access to pressure‐relieving devices, documentation burden/competing priorities, and training and leadership gaps, as consistently shown in recent systematic reviews [2, 3].

PIs are associated with pain, infection risk, prolonged hospital stay, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare costs, while prevention is generally less costly than treatment [2, 3, 4]. In Palestinian hospitals, resource constraints and staffing pressures may further hinder implementation of recommended preventive care [5, 6, 7]. Accordingly, understanding current nursing knowledge, attitudes and practices is essential for identifying realistic improvement targets.

Nurses play a pivotal role in PI prevention and management. Their responsibilities include risk assessment, repositioning, moisture and nutrition management, and use of pressure‐relieving devices in line with contemporary guidance [1, 8]. However, translating formal knowledge into consistent practice remains challenging. Recent evidence suggests that nurses often demonstrate mixed or adequate knowledge but suboptimal practice and that the relationships among knowledge, attitudes and practice are variable rather than linear [9, 10]. This pattern has also been reported in regional studies, including Saudi Arabia and Rwanda, where practice gaps persisted despite generally positive attitudes or acceptable knowledge levels [11, 12]. These findings reinforce the need for localized research to identify context‐specific gaps within Palestinian hospitals.

In the Arab world, including Palestine, PIs remain a growing concern among patients with limited mobility or chronic illness, and regional studies from Jordan and Saudi Arabia continue to report gaps in nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices [13, 14, 15]. Within Palestine, recent studies have provided useful but still partial evidence, often focusing on a single domain or specific population, such as nursing students, intensive care settings, or medical device‐related PIs [8, 16, 17, 18]. However, to our knowledge, prior Palestinian research has rarely integrated all three domains: knowledge, attitudes and practices, across multiple hospital settings. This underscores the need for a comprehensive multicentre study assessing all three KAP domains across a wide range of clinical departments; however, we recognize that generalizability is constrained by the use of non‐probability sampling.

The primary objective of this research was to assess the level of knowledge, attitudes and practices of nurses regarding pressure injury prevention and management across multiple hospital departments in Palestine. Additionally, the study compared outcomes between governmental and private hospitals and examined associations between demographic/professional characteristics (e.g., age, education, years of experience) and KAP indicators. We did not directly measure organizational barriers; rather, implications about potential barriers are contextualized using contemporary syntheses.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted to assess registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding pressure injury prevention and management. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [19]. The completed STROBE checklist has been provided in Supporting Information File 1. Data were collected in Nablus, Northern West Bank, Palestine, across six hospitals: two governmental, three private, and one NGO facility. Hospital capacity ranged from approximately 100 to 250 beds. Participating units included surgical, medical, intensive care, emergency, paediatric, day care and operating room services. These sites were selected to ensure representation across ownership types and major clinical departments, capturing variation in staffing models, case mix and resource availability.

Study Population and Sampling

The study population consisted of registered nurses with at least 1 year of direct clinical experience in bedside patient care. Nurses were eligible if they were employed in various wards and directly involved in the prevention or management of PIs. Nurses working exclusively in administrative/educational roles, on extended leave, or with less than 1 year of clinical experience were excluded. These criteria were consistent with the eligibility definitions applied in similar KAP studies [20, 21, 22].

The estimated study population across the six participating hospitals was approximately 800 nurses. The target sample size was 260, calculated using the Raosoft online calculator with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error [23]. To ensure feasibility across multiple hospitals with varying staffing levels, a non‐probability convenience sampling approach was employed. This method is widely used in nursing research conducted in resource‐constrained contexts [8, 24, 25, 26]. Although practical, convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings beyond the participating hospitals.

Ultimately, 231 fully completed questionnaires were retained for analysis from 260 distributed/targeted responses (response rate: 88.9%). Non‐completion reflected routine service constraints (e.g., shift patterns, leave, workload) and a small number of partial or duplicate submissions that did not meet inclusion for analysis. Although the final sample was slightly below target, it remained adequate for the planned descriptive and exploratory analyses.

Instruments

Data were collected via a structured, self‐administered questionnaire adapted from previously validated tools used in these studies and tailored to the Palestinian nursing context [22, 27]. The questionnaire assessed nurses' knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding pressure injury prevention and management (historically referred to as pressure ulcers). The final instrument comprises four sections with a total of 54 items:

Sociodemographic characteristics (13 items): age, sex, marital status, educational qualification, country of certification, department, workplace (governmental/private/NGO), years of professional and departmental experience, weekly working hours, night shifts per week and recency of education on PIs (last lecture/article).

Knowledge (20 items): assessed with True/False/‘I don't know’ questions covering risk factors, prevention, interventions and management. Items were dichotomously scored (1 = correct; 0 = incorrect or ‘I don't know’). Reverse‐worded items were recoded so that higher values consistently indicate correct knowledge. Item means are interpretable as proportion correct; totals ranged 0–20 and were also scaled to 0%–100%. Knowledge levels followed McDonald's learning‐outcome criteria: very low (< 20%), low (20%–39%), moderate (40%–59%), high (60%–79%), and very high (≥ 80%) [28]. For inferential analyses, scores were dichotomized into adequate knowledge (high/very high) versus inadequate knowledge (very low/low/moderate).

Pilot Study

The questionnaire was piloted with 30 registered nurses from Palestinian hospitals to assess clarity, comprehensibility and content validity. Pilot feedback led to minor wording clarifications and re‐ordering of two items for flow; no items were added or removed. To prevent bias, pilot data were excluded from the main analysis, and nurses who participated in the pilot study were not included in the final sample.

Instrument Validity and Reliability

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from previously validated tools and further reviewed by a panel of three nursing experts specializing in wound care and clinical education to ensure content validity and cultural relevance to the Palestinian context. Modifications were made to improve clarity, eliminate ambiguity and align with international guidelines on pressure injury prevention. Content validity was supported through expert review and pilot testing. Reliability testing demonstrated Cronbach's alpha coefficients of 0.76 for knowledge, 0.65 for attitudes and 0.93 for practice. The lower alpha for the attitude domain was considered acceptable for a brief exploratory scale but was taken into account during interpretation.

Data Collection

Following institutional and hospital approvals, data were collected between October 2024 and March 2025. Questionnaires were distributed both electronically (via a secure online survey link) and in paper format during nurses' shifts in participating departments to ensure accessibility and minimize disruption to clinical duties. To minimize missing data, the electronic form enforced completion of primary KAP items and applied basic range/logic checks; paper questionnaires were double‐entered and reconciled after piloting for clarity. The electronic and paper versions were identical in wording, item order and instructions to reduce mode effects. Partial questionnaires lacking any primary KAP outcome were excluded per protocol, and completed questionnaires were retrieved promptly to preserve data integrity.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of of An‐Najah National University, reference number (Med. Sept. 2024/23). Permissions were also granted by hospital administrations. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically or in writing before data collection. The respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity, with all the data stored securely and reported in aggregate form.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 [32]. Only fully completed questionnaires were included; cases missing any primary KAP outcome were excluded listwise. Descriptive statistics (frequencies/percentages; means ± SD) summarized sociodemographic and KAP outcomes. All tests were two‐tailed with α = 0.05.

Using the domain scores defined in the Instruments section, we compared mean knowledge, attitude and practice scores across sociodemographic groups using one‐way ANOVA. Levene's test was used to assess homogeneity of variance. When this assumption was violated, Welch's ANOVA was reported. Significant omnibus tests were followed by Tukey's HSD for pairwise comparisons. Item‐level practice and attitude frequencies are presented descriptively. For adjusted associations and comparability with prior work [12, 20, 22], binary logistic regression modelled knowledge adequacy and practice adequacy (cut‐point ≥ 80%). Sex, department, recency of education on PIs, total years of experience, weekly working hours, and night‐shift frequency were entered as a priori covariates based on clinical relevance and prior literature. To maintain parsimony, the model was restricted to this conceptual set, and collinearity and model fit were verified. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and standard model diagnostics were reported. Given the limited number of outcome events relative to the number of categorical predictors, adjusted results were treated as exploratory. Because data were collected in multiple hospitals within one city, clustering by hospital or unit was not modelled, which may widen uncertainty when interpreting confidence intervals and p‐values. Attitudes were not modelled with logistic regression to avoid information loss from dichotomization; emphasis remained on continuous and item‐level findings.

Results

Of 260 eligible nurses, 231 provided complete questionnaires for analysis (88.8% completion); 29 were excluded due to unavailability, refusal or partial/duplicate responses. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Most were female (53.2%) and married (62.8%); 74.0% held a bachelor's degree, 97.8% qualified locally. Departmental distribution was led by ICU (30.3%) and Emergency (22.5%). Employment settings were government hospitals (56.3%), private (42.0%) and NGOs (1.7%). Regarding training, 32.9% had attended a lecture/read an article on PIs in the past year, whereas 25.1% had never received such training. Most participants were aged 20–30 years (56.3%), with 1–10 years' total nursing experience (69.7%) and 1–10 years in their current department (84.8%). Weekly hours were typically 30–40 (71.9%), 21.2% worked > 40 h, and 87.4% reported 1–2 night shifts/week.

Item‐level results showed a clear pattern: recognition of key risk conditions and mechanisms was excellent, while epidemiology and misconceptions lagged. Highest correct response rates were for predisposing diseases/conditions (97.8%), shear/handling risks in overweight patients (95.7%), and immobility due to stroke/head injury (94.8%). In contrast, notable gaps included incorrectly linking male sex to higher risk (18.6% correct), global burden/prevalence (48.5%), and understanding that PIs are typically not sterile wounds (43.7%). Overall, the pattern suggests strong foundational knowledge of risk identification with more limited accuracy in epidemiology and selected misconceptions (see Table S1).

Nurses' Attitudes Toward Pressure Injury Prevention

On the 11‐item scale, nurses showed a generally positive orientation (mean 1.98 ± 0.27; observed range 1.27–2.91) (Table S2), equivalent to a total of 21.80 ± 3.00 (range 14–32). As a secondary descriptor for comparability, Table 2 presents the attitude total score (theoretical range 11–44; observed 14–32) and the favourable/unfavourable split (58.0%/42.0%), but this dichotomy should be interpreted cautiously. Item‐level results revealed notable misconceptions: 77.0% endorsed that a daily bath/sponge bath prevents PIs, 71.4% agreed that obese patients are at lower risk and 74.8% agreed that a heel blister is ‘not of concern’. In contrast, attitudes aligned strongly with best practices: 97.4% supported documenting non‐blanchable erythema, 95.7% recognized immobility/neurologic compromise as high risk and 87.4% acknowledged friction/shear during repositioning.

Nurses' Practices in Pressure‐Related Injury Prevention

Overall adherence to the primary 0–100 index was 70.89 ± 16.29 (range 13.73–100.00). McDonald bands were 19.9% very low, 27.7% low, 20.8% moderate, 19.9% high and 11.7% very high; using the prespecified ≥ 80% threshold, 31.6% met adequate practice (Table 2). Item‐level results (Table S3) highlighted priority gaps: only 14.7% always used a lifting device rather than dragging, 16.5% always avoided massage over bony prominences, and 21.2% always repositioned at the stated interval. In contrast, several behaviours were more commonly reported, including encouraging frequent position changes (44.2% always), advising patients/caregivers before discharge (45.5% always), and offloading heels with pillows (46.8% always for immobile patients; 41.1% always for bed‐bound patients). Taken together, these findings indicate partial adherence across many preventive practices, with particularly important gaps in safe handling and avoidance of contraindicated actions.

Associations Between Knowledge and Sociodemographic Characteristics/Professional Characteristics

On the continuous knowledge scale, recency of education and weekly working hours were associated with mean differences (education: ANOVA F (3227) = 4.58, p = 0.004, η 2 = 0.057; working hours: F (2228) = 6.89, p = 0.001, η 2 = 0.057; Levene tests p > 0.05). Tukey post hoc tests showed that nurses who received education 1–2 years ago scored higher than those who never received education (mean difference = 1.82, p = 0.002); other pairwise education contrasts were not significant. For working hours, nurses working 30–40 h/week scored higher than those working > 40 h/week (mean difference = 1.55, p = 0.001); other pairwise comparisons were not significant. An omnibus difference by marital status was observed (F (2228) = 4.69, p = 0.010), but Tukey contrasts involved a very small divorced subgroup (n = 3) versus single (p = 0.039) and married (p = 0.015), and should be interpreted cautiously. No significant differences were found for the rest of the variables (all p > 0.05; see Table 3).

Discussion

This multi‐site study shows a familiar yet troubling pattern in PI prevention: nurses display generally adequate knowledge and broadly positive professional attitudes, yet routine bedside practices remain suboptimal. In other words, knowing and believing do not reliably translate into doing. Similar knowledge–practice gaps are well‐documented across the region and beyond, including Jordan [13, 33] and Saudi Arabia [11], and have also been described within Palestinian settings [5, 16, 34]. This pattern is also reflected in international syntheses, which found only modest, variable links among knowledge, attitudes and practice—implicating implementation constraints rather than knowledge alone [9, 10]. Contemporary multicentre and national studies likewise document gaps in practice despite awareness [14, 25]. Our findings, therefore, align with a wider body of evidence indicating that structural cultural, and workflow constraints frequently interrupt the pathway from evidence to practice.

A first encouraging signal is that risk recognition is comparatively strong: participants consistently identified immobility, shear and neurological compromise as salient hazards. This mirrors prior work in Palestine and neighbouring Jordan, in which nurses tend to score best on basic risk concepts while struggling with detailed protocol adherence [5, 34, 35]. However, several misconceptions persist and are clinically consequential. Parallel gaps also extend to medical device–related PIs in Palestine [17]. Notably, belief in massaging bony prominences remains prevalent despite explicit contraindication in the (per 2025 International Guideline) [1]. Likewise, daily bathing per se does not prevent PI; guideline‐consistent skin care prioritizes moisture/microclimate management, risk‐based repositioning, heel off‐loading, and appropriate pressure‐redistribution surfaces [1]. Although the attitude scale theoretically spans a wide range, the observed pattern occupied a narrower band; taken together with the frequent endorsement of a contraindicated item, this argues against over‐interpreting a dichotomized ‘favourable attitude’ label—item‐level beliefs provide a more actionable clinical signal than an aggregated median‐split score.

Attitudinally, nurses overwhelmingly endorsed the importance of early recognition and documentation—for example, non‐blanchable erythema—and correctly prioritized high‐risk groups. This attitudinal positivity is consistent with regional studies [11, 35] and suggests that resistance to prevention is not ideological; rather, implementation barriers are the binding constraint.

Education emerged as a modifiable lever. In adjusted models, training within the last year was associated with higher odds of adequate practice, and training 1–2 and 2–3 years ago was associated with higher knowledge; effects for other predictors were imprecise and should be viewed as exploratory. Recent training correlated with better knowledge and more favourable attitudes in our cohort, consistent with regional evidence showing gains after targeted education [33, 36]. Crucially, how education is delivered matters. Evidence indicates that simulation and hands‐on training reliably improve competence and decision‐making, whereas effects on pressure‐injury incidence alone are uncertain without broader implementation supports (e.g., multicomponent bundles and audit–feedback), consistent with contemporary systematic reviews of simulation‐based PI education [37, 38, 39]. In settings like ours—where practices diverge most sharply from guidelines—embedding myth‐busting within simulation, return‐demonstration, and supervised bedside coaching is essential.

Some associations warrant cautious interpretation. Given wide confidence intervals for several predictors, we emphasize the training‐related pattern over isolated odds ratios and treat non‐training signals as hypothesis‐generating. Although department‐level heterogeneity appeared on omnibus testing, the absence of consistent pairwise differences suggests broad system‐level shortfalls rather than isolated unit effects.

Policy Implications

The observed pattern—generally adequate knowledge and favourable attitudes alongside weak routine practice and specific misconceptions—points to implementation rather than information as the core challenge. Accordingly, policy actions in Palestinian hospitals should be framed as quality‐improvement priorities, not causal remedies. First, embed targeted myth‐correction within recurring, guideline‐aligned refresher training, with explicit prohibition of contraindicated actions and emphasis on early recognition/documentation (e.g., non‐blanchable erythema) ‘in line with EPUAP/NPIAP/PPPIA recommendations’. Second, institute routine process surveillance for prevention behaviours (repositioning, skin assessment, moisture/microclimate management) using unit dashboards and rapid feedback to close gaps (Cochrane audit‐and‐feedback evidence shows small–moderate practice gains, maximized with actionable feedback and co‐interventions). Third, conduct local audits of device availability and accessibility (e.g., pressure‐redistribution surfaces, safe‐handling aids) and escalate shortfalls through existing QI channels. Finally, when organizations wish to understand whether operational constraints contribute to practice gaps, they should assess workflow and staffing conditions using validated tools as part of a diagnostic phase—without presuming specific staffing solutions based on the current data. These steps align with standard patient‐safety and accreditation frameworks, translating the study's findings into concrete, monitorable improvement targets.

Recommendations

To operationalize these implications, implement a unit‐level improvement package that is practical and measurable: (1) run skills‐focused education cycles with return‐demonstration and simulation scenarios that explicitly correct prevalent myths and drill core behaviours; (2) maintain concise unit protocols and bedside cues that prohibit contraindicated practices and prompt timely documentation; (3) perform plan–do–study–act (PDSA) pilots to fit the education‐protocol bundle to local workflow before scaling and (4) use audit‐and‐feedback to track process measures and device accessibility, displaying results on dashboards to reinforce behaviour change. Where leaders aim to evaluate the role of staffing or workload, treat this as a separate, prospective evaluation using validated measures linked to process outcomes, rather than inferring such effects from the current study.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, self‐reported practice is prone to social desirability and recall biases; direct observation would likely yield more accurate estimates of routine care. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference regarding factors associated with knowledge, attitudes or practice. Because exposure and outcome variables were measured at the same time, temporal relationships cannot be established; therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of directionality or effect. With respect to measurement, the practice domain was scored using a clearly specified algorithm; nevertheless, any composite index can mask domain‐specific behaviours, and categorical thresholds derived from it are descriptive rather than inferential. For attitudes, the scale showed modest internal consistency and a restricted observed range, which may attenuate associations; accordingly, item‐level findings should be interpreted alongside summary scores. At the contextual level, organizational and resource determinants (e.g., staffing patterns, equipment availability, local workflow policies) were not measured, so some gaps likely reflect system factors beyond individual control. Although data were collected from multiple hospitals within a single city, clustering by hospital/unit was not modelled, which may limit generalizability and slightly underestimate standard errors. In multivariable analyses, the combination of a limited number of outcome events and many categorical predictors likely reduced events‐per‐variable and produced wide confidence intervals; estimates should therefore be viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Finally, voluntary participation may introduce self‐selection bias.

Despite these constraints, the multi‐hospital sampling frame, transparent scoring procedures and convergence between item‐level results and guideline‐consistent prevention priorities support the robustness and practical relevance of the main findings.

Conclusions

This single‐city, multi‐hospital study found that nurses generally recognize key pressure‐injury risks and endorse prevention, yet routine practice lags, and specific misconceptions persist—most notably endorsement of contraindicated actions. Recent training aligned with adequate knowledge and more favourable attitudes, suggesting a practical target for improvement. Immediate priorities grounded in our data include targeted myth correction, skills‐focused training with supervised practice, clear unit‐level protocols that prohibit contraindicated practices and promote early recognition/documentation, and routine audit‐and‐feedback to sustain behaviour change. Organizational contributors are plausible but were not measured here and should be evaluated prospectively alongside observed care processes.
