Greater alterations in atherogenic lipids and inflammatory markers in women during type 2 diabetes development and risk of coronary heart disease—Findings from two population-based studies
Authors: Yilin Yoshida, Danting Li, Xiang Li, Anand Rohatgi, Lydia Bazzano, Vivian A. Fonseca, Lu Qi, Franck Mauvais-Jarvis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Diabetes confers a greater risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) in women than in men, potentially due to women’s antecedent of metabolic risk factors. We examined circulating metabolites and their associations with CHD risk in men and women across glycemic statuses.METHODS:We analyzed data from 97,271 CHD-free UK Biobank participants. Metabolomic profiling was performed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of baseline plasma samples. We used linear regression models to examine the association between sex and log-transformed metabolites in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes (T2D), prediabetes, and euglycemia, adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, income, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, body mass index, and medication use (for diabetes, lipid-lowering, and hypertension). Cox regression models were used to evaluate associations between metabolites and CHD risk (fatal or nonfatal myocardial infarction) stratified by sex and adjusted for the same covariates. We further considered menopausal status, and all analyses were adjusted for false discovery rate. The analyses were replicated in 6199 participants of the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) with NMR data.RESULTS:With worsening glycemic status, women exhibited significantly higher atherogenic lipid/lipoprotein markers, fatty acids, and glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) than men, as well as lower albumin and lactate (all P-values <.0001). Sex differences persisted regardless of menopausal status. In adjusted Cox regressions, 1 SD increase in triglyceride (TG), saturated fatty acids (SFA), and GlycA was associated with a greater risk of CHD in women with T2D than men over a 10-year follow-up (hazard ratios 1.2–1.5 in women and 0.9–1.04 in men, P-interaction <.05).CONCLUSIONS:With worsening glycemic status, women exhibit higher levels of atherogenic lipid and inflammatory markers. TG, GlycA, and SFA are more strongly associated with CHD risk in women with T2D than in men.

Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) confers a 50% excess risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) in women compared to men. 1 We and others reported that, during the transition from euglycemia to T2D, women experience a more rapid and pronounced worsening of metabolic risk factors, such as atherogenic lipids and inflammatory markers, than men, which potentially contributes to their elevated CHD risk.2,3

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics enables comprehensive profiling of metabolic markers, including lipoprotein particle concentrations, compositions, size, and constituent lipids from lipoprotein subclasses, as well as fatty acids, inflammatory markers, amino acids, ketones, and metabolite intermediates of glycolysis or fluid balance.4 This approach provides critical insights into sex differences in the CHD risk during T2D development. Understanding these differences is essential for designing tailored prevention and treatment strategies aimed at reducing disparity in diabetic CHD.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting of the study

Leveraging blood metabolomics data from 2 large prospective cohorts, the UK Biobank Study (UKB) and the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) in the US, we sought to compare levels of lipids, lipoproteins, and other metabolites in men and women across glycemic status, including euglycemia and prediabetes (pre-DM) to newly diagnosed T2D. We examined the associations between metabolites and incident CHD in women and men across glycemic status. Because estrogens improve metabolic risk profiles and may underpin sex differences in these profiles, we compared sex differences in metabolites by menopausal status.

Characteristics of participants

This study primarily utilizes data from the UKB, a large prospective cohort study of over 500,000 adults across 22 centers throughout the United Kingdom.5 Participants aged 40 to 69 were enrolled between 2006 and 2010, attended the baseline assessment, and provided blood samples.5 We included a total of 97,271 cardiovascular disease (CVD)-free participants with known glycemic status and baseline plasma biomarker measurements by NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Figure 1 for sample selection). We validate the analysis in MESA, a population-based cohort that recruited community-dwelling men and women 45 to 84 years old of African Americans, Hispanics, Whites, and Chinese Americans from 6 field sites in the United States. A detailed description of the study design and objectives has been previously provided.6

Measurements

Metabolite quantification was prepared by the UKB as previously described.4 Only samples and biomarkers that passed the quality control were used in this study. Each sample included 168 metabolites in absolute level (mmol/L), spanning lipids, lipoproteins, fatty acids, glycolysis metabolites, ketones, and amino acids, and 81 in ratio measurements.4 During the physical examination at MESA study enrollment, blood was drawn after a 12 hour-fast, and samples were stored at −70 °C with EDTA. NMR spectroscopy was conducted at the MESA baseline, as described previously.7

Glycemic status:

Participants self-reported their medical history of diabetes, including age at first diagnosis of diabetes and the use of diabetic medications. We defined newly diagnosed T2D as those with a duration of T2D ≤2 years from baseline. Pre-DM was defined by hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) 5.7% to 6.4% without a T2D diagnosis or antidiabetic medication.

Outcome:

Incident CHD was defined using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes for fatal or nonfatal myocardial infarction. Outcomes were adjudicated by linking hospital admissions data from England, Scotland, and Wales with the national death register to identify the date of the first myocardial infarction following baseline assessment.8 Follow-up began at UKB inclusion and ended on January 31, 2021, date of death, or upon occurrence of the outcome.

Covariates:

Age, sex, and ethnicity were obtained at baseline. Townsend deprivation index was obtained from local National Health Service Primary Care Trust registries and the name of the recruitment center. Average household income and use of medications (antidiabetic, lipid-lowering, antihypertensive) were assessed using the touch screen questionaire. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire provided data on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, converted to metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes/day, with a cut-off of 150 m/wk for recommended activity.9 Alcohol intake was self-reported as never, special occasions only, 1 to 3 × per month, once or twice per week, 3 to 4 × per week, and daily or almost daily. Self-reported menopausal status or use of hormone replacement therapy were used to define postmenopausal (vs pre or perimenopausal) status. Smoking status was classified as a current smoker or non-smoker (former or never). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by squared height in meters (kg/m2). Serum lipid traits (apolipoprotein [Apo] B, Apo A1, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol [LDL-c], high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol [HDL-c], triglycerides [TG], and total-cholesterol [total-c]) were measured using immunoturbidimetric analysis on a Beckman AU5800, while serum albumin levels were estimated using a colorimetric method on the same analyzer.10

In MESA, assay methods for glucose and common lipids, such as total-c, LDL-c, and HDL-c, can be found in previous reports.11 We used the same algorithm as in UKB to define glycemic categories, except for pre-DM where fasting glucose was used as HbA1c was unavailable at MESA baseline. Covariates in the MESA analysis were consistent with UKB analysis.

Statistical analysis

Multivariable linear regressions were employed to analyze the associations between sex and metabolites, adjusting for age, race, income, deprivation index, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, and medications for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. Metabolites were log-transformed prior to analysis, and β coefficients for sex were reported for each biomarker, with positive values indicating higher relative levels in women. The association between each biomarker and sex was inferred from the absolute values of β coefficients. We calculated adjusted means for each metabolite by sex and glycemic status, evaluating the influence of menopausal status by comparing pre and postmenopausal women with age-matched men. We conducted a sensitivity analysis in those without lipid-lowering medications at baseline. We also replicated the analysis of NMR data using common assay-measured biomarkers, including total-c, LDL-c, HDL-c, TG, Apo B, Apo A1, and serum albumin. The false discovery rate (FDR) method was used for multiple comparisons. We used Cox proportional hazard models to evaluate the association between each individual metabolite and CHD risk in men and women, respectively, adjusting for the same covariates. The same analysis for linear and Cox regressions was replicated in MESA using available lipid/lipoprotein markers.

Results

The primary analysis included 83,315 UKB participants with euglycemia (56% women; mean age 56 years old), 12,706 with pre-DM (56% women; mean age 59 years old), and 1250 with newly diagnosed T2D (40% women; mean age 59 years old). Participants in the T2D group had a higher proportion of Non-Whites from low-income backgrounds, along with greater percentages of lifestyle risk factors, such as obesity, being physically inactive, and higher percentages of participants having a history of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive medication use (Table 1). The MESA analysis included 4992 participants with euglycemia (55% women; mean age 61 years old), 939 with pre-DM (44%; mean age 64 years old), and 268 with newly diagnosed T2D (52% women; mean age 63 years old). Similar to UKB participants, MESA participants with newly diagnosed T2D also were more likely to be Non-White and from low-income backgrounds, and had more lifestyle risk factors and antidiabetic, antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering medication use (Supplementary Table 1).

Lipids, lipoproteins, and fatty acids

In linear regressions adjusted for age, race, deprivation index, income, obesity, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, lipid-lowering medications, antihypertensive medications, and antidiabetes medications, women exhibited significantly higher concentrations of total-c, non-HDL-c, remnant-c, LDL-c, total esterified cholesterol, total free cholesterol, and total phospholipids than men across all glycemic groups (Fig 1 and Supplementary Table 2). These sex differences were most striking in the pre-DM and new T2D group (Fig 1). In the euglycemia group, women exhibited lower concentrations of total TG and constituent TG from lipoprotein particles (ie, very low-density lipoprotein [VLDL]-TG, LDL-TG, and HDL-TG) and lower concentrations of Apo B and Apo B/Apo A1 ratio than men (Fig 1, Fig 2, and Supplementary Table 2). However, in the pre-DM and T2D groups, this trend was reversed and women exhibited higher concentrations of LDL-TG, HDL-TG, and Apo B than men (Fig 1 and Fig 2). Women also exhibited higher concentrations of HDL-c, Apo A1, and constituent lipids from HDL, such as HDL-cholesteryl esters, HDL-free cholesterol, HDL-total lipids, and HDL-phospholipids; however, the favorable lipid traits became less pronounced with advancing glycemic status (Fig 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Women exhibited smaller VLDL particles but larger HDL particles than men across all glycemic status. However, the higher levels of larger HDL particles in women (vs men) gradually diminished with worsening glycemia. In the T2D group, women exhibited higher total concentrations of lipoprotein particles than men (Fig 1 and Supplementary Table 2B). Women exhibited higher concentrations of most fatty acids and fatty acid ratio measures across all glycemic groups than men. Notably, women had higher concentrations of total fatty acids, omega-6 fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), saturated fatty acids (SFA), and linoleic acid compared to men, and these sex differences became more pronounced with the glycemic advancement. However, women had a significantly lower level of omega-6/omega-3 fatty acids ratio across glycemic status (Fig 1, Supplementary Table 2, and Fig 2 A-C).

We replicated the linear regression analysis for sex differences of common assay-measured lipid markers, including total-c, LDL-c, HDL-c, TG, Apo B, Apo A1, and serum albumin markers. This analysis yielded consistent findings with the NMR analysis, showing women had more pronounced increases in atherogenic lipids with worsening of glycemic status compared to men (Supplementary Table 3).

Fluid balance, glycolysis, inflammation, amino acids, and ketones

Fluid balance markers, albumin and creatinine, were consistently lower in women than men across glycemic groups, which was more pronounced in the T2D group. Women exhibited higher glycolysis- and tricarboxylic acid cycle-related metabolites, including glucose, pyruvate, and citrate, across glycemic groups, while lactate was lower, especially in the T2D group (Fig 1, Supplementary Table 2, and Fig 2). Glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA), a new inflammatory marker of early CHD risk, 12 was lower in women than men in the euglycemia group but became higher in women of the pre-DM and T2D groups (Fig 1, Supplementary Table 2, and Fig 2). Women with euglycemia or pre-DM exhibited lower concentrations of alanine, glutamine, and histidine than men, while no sex differences were observed in the T2D group. Women exhibited higher glycine concentrations across glycemic statuses (Supplementary Table 2, Fig 1). Women also exhibited significantly lower concentrations of branched-chain amino acids (BCAA) such as isoleucine, leucine, and valine. No sex differences were found in ketone levels across glycemic statuses.

In the MESA analysis, women (vs men) exhibited a higher level of total-c and LDL-c, particularly in the T2D group. Total TG and VLDL-TG were lower in women (vs men) in the euglycemic or pre-DM groups, but there was no sex difference in the T2D group. HLD-TG remained consistently higher in women than in men across glycemic status. HDL markers, such as HDL size and concentrations of large or median HDL particles, were higher in euglycemic women; however, this sex difference was not apparent in the T2D group. On the other hand, the concentrations of TG-rich VLDL particles and small LDL were lower in women during the euglycemic and/or pre-DM group; however, in the T2D group, no sex difference was observed in these atherogenic markers (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig 2).

Analyses stratified by menopause

Stratifying by menopausal status revealed similar sex differences in lipids, lipoproteins, and other metabolites across glycemic statuses in postmenopausal women compared to all individuals (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Figure 3). In comparisons between premenopausal women and age-matched young men, euglycemic premenopausal women exhibited lower levels of atherogenic lipid markers—such as total-c and non-HDL-c, remnant-c, VLDL-c, TG, Apo B, and lipid content in various lipoprotein particles—as well as lower levels of fatty acids and amino acids, except for glycine, than age-matched men. However, men and women in the premenopausal T2D group had similar atherogenic lipid traits.

In another sensitivity analysis, excluding participants on lipid-lowering medications, the results were consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table 6).

Association between lipids and metabolites and risk of CHD in men and women across glycemic groups

We evaluated the sex-specific association between lipids and metabolites and the risk of CHD. During a mean follow-up of 10 years, a total of 6124 CHD cases were identified. Adjusted Cox proportional hazard regression showed that in the T2D group, total TG, VLDL-TG, LDL-TG, and HDL-TG were positively associated with increased risk of CHD in women but not in men (hazard ratio [HR] of CHD per SD of TG, 1.46, 95% CI: 1.01–2.11 in women and 1.04, 0.84–1.28 in men, respectively, P-value for interaction .03), VLDL-TG (1.46, 1.00–2.13 in women and 1.04, 0.84–1.29 in men, P-interaction .03), LDL-TG (1.34, 0.96–1.86 in women and 1.04, 0.85–1.28 in men, P-interaction .11), and HDL-TG (1.43, 0.99–2.05 in women and 0.99, 0.88–1.21 in men, P-interaction .03 (Fig 3, Supplementary Table 7). In the euglycemia group, total TG, VLDL-TG, LDL-TG, and HDL-TG were all positively associated with the risk of CHD in both men and women (point estimates for HRs ranged from 1.08 to 1.18), and the magnitude of the associations was stronger in women than in men (P-values for interaction ranged from .03 to .25, Fig 3, Supplementary Table 7).

In the euglycemic group, increased diameter of HDL was inversely associated with risk of CHD, with a stronger association in women than in men, with HR per 1SD increase in HDL diameter of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73–0.84) in women, and 0.91 (0.87–0.95) in men (P-value for interaction = .004, Fig 3, Supplementary Table 6). In euglycemic women, there was also a stronger inverse association between the concentrations of very large HDL particles (XL_HDL), large HDL particles (L_HDL), and risk of CHD, than among their male counterparts (P-values for interaction <.0001 and .017, respectively, Fig 3, Supplementary Table 6). Among individuals who already have T2D diabetes, the associations between HDL markers and CHD risk was no longer significant in either sex group (Fig 3, Supplementary Table 7).

Among individuals with T2D, the association between SFA and risk of CHD was stronger in women than in men, with an HR per 1SD increase in SFA of 1.46 (1.03–2.08) in women vs 0.97 (0.78–1.20) in men, P-value for interaction of .01 (Fig 3, Supplementary Table 6). Similarly, the association between SFA to total fatty acid ratio and risk of CHD was stronger in women than in men in both T2D (HR: 1.71, 1.15–2.57 in women vs 0.94, 0.75–1.18 in men, P-interaction .008) and euglycemic groups (HR: 1.1, 1.04–1.18 in women vs 1.03, 0.99–1.08 in men, P-interaction .03, Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 6). In women in the T2D or euglycemic group, ratios of omega-6 fatty acids/total fatty acids, PUFA/total fatty acids, and linoleic acid/total fatty acids all showed a stronger inverse association with risk of CHD compared to men, respectively (Supplementary Table 7, not shown in Figure).

The inflammatory marker GlycA was associated with a greater risk of CHD in euglycemic women compared to men, with an HR of 1.24 (1.16–1.33) in women vs 1.14 (1.09–1.19) in men, P-interaction .046. GlycA conferred a similar greater risk of CHD in women of the T2D group (P-interaction .048) (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 7).

In the sensitivity analysis, excluding participants on lipid-lowering medications, the results remained similar to the main analysis (Supplementary Table 8).

Among MESA participants (Supplementary Table 9), during a follow-up of 16 years, a total of 689 incident CHD cases were identified. We found that HDL measurements, including increased concentrations of total or large HDL particles and HDL particle size, appeared to exhibit more substantial protective effects in women for incident CHD than in men within the euglycemic group; however, this sex difference diminished in the T2D group. One SD increase of total TG and VLDL-TG appeared to show a stronger increased risk of CHD in women than men in the T2D group. Overall, the MESA findings based on available lipid/lipoprotein markers were consistent with the UKB findings, despite the absence of statistical significance (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Previous studies, including ours, showed that women have more atherogenic lipid profiles than men at or before the diagnosis of T2D.2,3,13 However, these studies were based on traditional lipid panels and did not provide insights into lipoprotein characteristics during T2D development. The present study is one of the first studies that comprehensively investigated lipoprotein subclass particle concentration, size, and composition, which are more informative of atherogenicity than traditional lipid markers.14–17 We found that LDL, VLDL, and lipid concentrations (eg, TG) in these lipoprotein particles were significantly higher in women than men in the new T2D group. Constituent TG from VLDL, LDL, and HDL increases the inflammatory and atherogenic properties of these particles and was reported to be independently associated with CHD risk.18,19 We observe that women’s advantage in exhibiting more HDL antiatherogenic properties, such as higher concentrations of total and large HDL particles and greater HDL particle size compared to men, diminishes with T2D development. In contrast, women exhibit higher levels of atherogenic lipoprotein markers, such as higher levels of TG-rich VLDL and small LDL particles, compared to men during T2D development. Our prospective analysis demonstrates the associations between lipoprotein composition (TG content), particle size, and CHD risk in women with new T2D but not in their male counterparts. The augmented lipoprotein alterations in women during T2D development may provide a compelling explanation for the greater excess risk of CHD in women with T2D.14,20

Women with T2D had higher levels of fatty acids, including SFA and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA). This is consistent with previous research showing women have higher fatty acid species than men.21 We observed that women with T2D exhibited enhanced CHD risk associated with SFA and MUFA compared to men with T2D. In contrast to the well-recognized link between SFA and increased risk of CHD, findings on the association between MUFA and the risk of CHD remain inconsistent.22 There is, therefore, no unanimous rationale for MUFA recommendations in a therapeutic regimen.22 Sex-disaggregated examinations of SFA or MUFA and associated cardiovascular risk are limited, which requires further evaluations.23

Another finding is that GlycA, a new serum biomarker for systemic inflammation12 amplifies the risk of CHD in women. Evidence suggests that greater systemic inflammation in women contributes to their higher CVD risk and more severe manifestations.24 Inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein, interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α are preferentially elevated in women than men with diabetes.25 Our study adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that chronic inflammation may predispose women with T2D to a higher risk of CHD than men.

Women exhibited lower serum albumin and lactate levels than men, and the advancement of glycemic status augments the sex differences. Serum albumin has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticoagulant properties. Low albumin levels independently predict CVD risk.26 In diabetes, albumin synthesis and secretion are decreased due to insulin deficiency. In our study, men have preserved serum albumin with increased glycemia compared to women. A higher serum albumin level in men compared to women was also reported in populations with or without T2D.27 Differences in nitric oxide metabolism and oxidative stress between women and men, and the influence of sex hormones, have been proposed as explanations for the sex differences in albumin.28

Lactate, was higher in men than in women in the new T2D group. Although the findings are limited, men may exhibit higher lactate levels than women due to their greater muscle mass and more frequent participation in intense exercise. Intense exercise promotes increased anaerobic glycolysis, which in turn raises lactate levels.29 People with T2D have increased lactate production due to increased glycolysis and impaired lactate clearance.30 However, sex differences in lactate level change during T2D development have been ill-defined and warrant further investigations.

Previous studies indicated that diabetes nullifies the protective effects of estrogens on women’s cardiovascular health.31,32 Our study supports this concept by showing the attenuating effect of menopausal status on a comprehensive metabolic risk profile in premenopausal women with T2D. Diabetes impairs endothelial response more detrimentally in women than in men by interrupting the hemodynamic regulation of estrogen through complex interactions between insulin and estrogen signaling.33 Estrogens are also known for their role in regulating lipid metabolism in women via estrogen signaling pathways.33 Hyperglycemia significantly disrupts estrogen action, accelerating the atherogenic process in women with T2D. In this study, we found that estrogen’s beneficial effect on lipoprotein and lipid metabolism was attenuated in premenopausal women with T2D, indicating the detrimental interaction between T2D and estrogens in young women. However, the causality of this observation needs to be further evaluated.

Beyond the synergistic adverse effects of estrogen deprivation and T2D on women’s metabolic profiles and CHD risk, several additional factors that disproportionately affect women may further contribute to their heightened metabolic burden and poorer cardiovascular outcomes compared to men. Women with diabetes often face more adverse social determinants of health, including financial constraints that limit access to healthy food, medications, transportation, and quality healthcare, all essential for effective diabetes management.34 Moreover, women with diabetes have been reported to experience greater psychosocial stress, diabetes-related distress, depression, and caregiving responsibilities, all of which can hinder adequate disease self-management and are linked to worse cardiovascular outcomes.1 Clinical disparities also play a role: women are more likely to be underdiagnosed and undertreated, receiving less aggressive risk factor screening and delayed interventions compared to men.1, 35, 36 Taken together, these factors, combined with more pronounced metabolic deterioration and the loss of estrogen’s protective effects associated with T2D, collectively contribute to women’s elevated risk of CVD in the context of T2D. The study’s strengths include the largest sample size of individuals with NMR metabolome profiling to examine these associations, a prospective design, and extensive phenotypic details on the participants of 2 longitudinal cohorts. However, several limitations of the study merit mentioning. First, no causation or mechanism can be made due to the study’s observational nature. Diabetes was self-reported in the UK Biobank, so some undiagnosed cases were likely excluded. However, having confirmed diagnosis status allows for better control of medication use in the analysis. Furthermore, due to data limitations, we cannot examine the type (> 90% of the medications in our sample are statins), dosage, and duration of lipid medication use in the analysis. Additionally, the analysis is subject to potential residual confounding. We were unable to account for dietary factors, as more than half of the study sample lacked 2-day dietary recall data. UKB participants are predominantly White and middle-aged; therefore, generalization to other diverse populations is unclear. Further, when conducting the analysis, metabolomics data were only available for baseline plasma samples, precluding the analysis of sex differences in metabolite changes over time. Future studies that incorporate multiple metabolomics assessments could further validate our findings.

Conclusions

In this observational study, we demonstrated sex differences in atherogenic lipid profiles and other metabolites across glycemic status. Overall, women have shown progressively worse metabolic risk profiles with worsening glycemic status, represented by increased atherogenic lipids and inflammation markers, and lower serum albumin. The protective effects typically associated with estrogen appeared attenuated in premenopausal participants with T2D. Further, TG, GlycA, and SFA are differentially associated with the risk of CHD in men and women with T2D. These findings suggest that, compared to men, women may experience earlier and more pronounced metabolic changes around the onset of T2D that could contribute to their elevated risk of CHD. Despite lifestyle changes and statin-optimized LDL, effective approaches to modifying CHD risk in diabetes remain lacking.37 Our study underscores the need for sex-specific screening and treatment strategies in people with T2D.
