Obstetric anaesthesia for caesarean section in Central Europe: a prospective population-weighted binational audit (OBAAMA-COV Study)

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuraxial anaesthesia is recommended as the preferred technique for caesarean section, and the proportion performed under general anaesthesia is increasingly regarded as a quality indicator. Previous national audits in the Czech Republic and Slovakia demonstrated a reduction in general anaesthesia rates between 2011 and 2015; however, contemporary population-level data are lacking.MethodsOBAAMA-COV was a prospective, cross-sectional national audit conducted in November 2022 across maternity units in the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Consecutive caesarean sections were recorded using a structured electronic registry. Population weighting based on official national statistics was applied to generate representative estimates of anaesthetic technique distributions and reported indications. The analyses were descriptive.ResultsA total of 2,270 caesarean sections were recorded from 94 centres. Population-weighted estimates corresponded to 2,068 procedures in the Czech Republic and 1,205 in Slovakia. Overall general anaesthesia rates were 33.9% and 31.3%, respectively. In elective procedures, general anaesthesia was used in 23.0% (95% CI 19.9–26.4) in the Czech Republic and 24.9% (20.6–29.7) in Slovakia; in emergency procedures, rates were 45.4% (42.1–48.8) and 36.8% (31.8–42.1), respectively. Maternal preference was the leading reported indication in elective cases (approximately 60–63% across audit cycles), whereas urgency predominated in emergency settings. Rapid sequence induction with tracheal intubation was used in most cases; difficult intubation occurred in approximately 2% of cases, and no aspiration was recorded. Umbilical arterial pH values were comparable between general anaesthesia and neuraxial techniques.ConclusionsThese population-weighted data demonstrate a persistent divergence from contemporary European standards. General anaesthesia for caesarean section remains common in both countries, plateauing rather than declining since 2015. Background

Caesarean section is the most frequently performed major surgical procedure worldwide, and its incidence continues to rise across Europe [1, 2]. International guidelines and European consensus statements uniformly recommend neuraxial anaesthesia as the preferred technique for both elective and emergency caesarean section, primarily to reduce airway-related complications and optimise maternal safety [3, 4]. Consequently, the proportion of caesarean sections performed under general anaesthesia (GA) is increasingly regarded as a system-level quality indicator in obstetric anaesthesia [4, 5], with several Western European countries reporting elective general anaesthesia rates below 1% [5].

In Central Europe, previous national audits conducted in the Czech Republic and Slovakia (OBAAMA 2011 and 2015) documented substantially higher general anaesthesia rates compared with Western European benchmarks [6, 7]. A marked reduction in the use of general anaesthesia was observed between 2011 and 2015. However, without contemporary population-level data, it is unclear whether this positive trend has continued, and what specific clinical or organisational factors currently drive the ongoing use of general anaesthesia in elective and emergency settings.

The OBAAMA-COV project was designed as a prospective, population-weighted national audit of obstetric anaesthetic practice. Following publication of the labour analgesia component [8], the present analysis focuses on anaesthesia for caesarean section. The primary objective was to describe current practice in the Czech Republic and Slovakia, using population weighting to generate representative national estimates [9]. Secondary objectives were to compare elective and emergency procedures, to analyse reported indications for general anaesthesia—including maternal preference—and to determine whether previously observed improvements have successfully closed the gap with current European standards.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

OBAAMA-COV was a prospective, cross-sectional national audit of obstetric anaesthesia practice conducted in the Czech Republic and Slovakia between 1 and 30 November 2022. It represents the third phase of the OBAAMA programme, following national audits performed in 2011 and 2015 [6, 7]. The “COV” phase reflects continuation of the programme during the COVID-19 era; however, the study was not designed to assess pandemic-related effects on anaesthetic practice.

In the Czech Republic, the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of University Hospital Brno, and informed consent was waived. In Slovakia, approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Louis Pasteur University Hospital, Košice, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants.

Setting and participants

All maternity units in both countries were invited to participate, including university, regional, and local hospitals. In Slovakia, centres are formally classified into levels I–III + according to the complexity of perinatal care. Each participating centre prospectively recorded all consecutive caesarean sections performed during the study period. The audit was purely observational and did not influence clinical management.

Centre recruitment

All maternity units were invited via emails to the respective heads of department. Non-responding centres received targeted follow-up emails. In cases of continued non-participation, members of the steering committee contacted the departmental heads personally by telephone. The project was formally endorsed by the Czech Society for Anaesthesiology, Resuscitation, and Intensive Care Medicine (CSARIM) and the Slovak Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Medicine (SSAIM).

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured web-based electronic case report form (CLADE-IS, Masaryk University, Czech Republic). The registry captured centre-level characteristics and detailed procedural data.

Data completeness and internal consistency were verified prior to analysis. Mandatory fields and logical range checks were implemented within the electronic case report form to minimise missing or implausible entries. The database structure was aligned with previous OBAAMA audits to ensure longitudinal comparability [6, 7].

Indications for general anaesthesia were selected from predefined registry categories and reported prospectively by the attending anaesthesiologist at the time of the procedure; no external adjudication was performed.

Classification of urgency

Caesarean sections were classified as elective or emergency. The emergency category comprised Lucas' urgency categories 1–3 [10, 11]. These categories were analysed as a single aggregate group to preserve longitudinal comparability with previous OBAAMA audits. Furthermore, this approach minimises inter-centre classification bias, given the absence of a uniform national triage system in either country. The study was not designed to evaluate urgency-specific performance indicators or decision-to-delivery intervals.

Representativeness and population weighting

Participation rates varied across centre types. To obtain nationally representative estimates, population weighting was applied using official national statistics from the Institute of Health Information and Statistics of the Czech Republic (ÚZIS) and the National Health Information Centre of Slovakia (NCZI). Centres were weighted by maternity centre type and the corresponding national caesarean section volumes. Population weighting was applied exclusively to national estimates of anaesthetic technique distribution and reported reasons for GA. Detailed procedural characteristics are presented as unweighted registry data to reflect actual reported practice within participating centres.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were descriptive. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Confidence intervals reflect the precision of population-weighted estimates and were not used for formal hypothesis testing. For strata with complete national coverage (100% participation), applying the finite population correction yielded zero sampling variance for specific estimates, resulting in identical lower and upper confidence interval bounds. No formal hypothesis testing or between-country comparisons were performed. Analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). LLM-assisted tools were used for language editing. The authors reviewed, edited, and approved all text and take full responsibility for its content.

Results

Study population and national representativeness

A total of 94 maternity centres participated in the audit: 67 of 87 in the Czech Republic (12/12 university, 6/15 regional, and 49/60 local centres) and 27 of 53 in Slovakia (3/6 level III +, 7/8 level III, 10/18 level II, and 7/21 level I). Participating centres recorded 1,581 caesarean sections in the Czech Republic and 689 in Slovakia during the study period.

This corresponded to 76.5% of the national caesarean section volume in the Czech Republic and 57.2% in Slovakia between 1 and 30 November 2022.

Median maternal age was 32 years (IQR 28–35) in the Czech Republic and 31 years (IQR 27–35) in Slovakia. Median BMI was 29.4 kg/m2 and 28.7 kg/m2, respectively. Detailed maternal and obstetric characteristics are presented in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Discussion

Our binational audit shows that general anaesthesia remains disproportionately used for caesarean section in both the Czech Republic and Slovakia, accounting for approximately one-third of all procedures and one-quarter of elective cases. After the marked reduction observed between 2011 and 2015 [6, 7], current data indicate stagnation rather than further decline. Elective general anaesthesia rates of 23–25% contrast with reported Western European rates below 1% [5]. This divergence is clinically relevant because contemporary guidelines and European consensus recommendations define neuraxial anaesthesia as the preferred technique for caesarean section whenever feasible and emphasise the importance of structured obstetric anaesthesia services, including timely access to neuraxial analgesia and anaesthesia [3, 4, 12]. Medical contraindications to neuraxial techniques accounted for only a minority of cases, highlighting a persistent gap between international recommendations and Central European clinical practice.

Socio-cultural barriers and maternal preference

The leading reported contributor to elective general anaesthesia was maternal preference, which has remained remarkably stable across audit cycles, accounting for approximately 60% of elective general anaesthesia cases over more than a decade [6, 7]. This temporal stability suggests a multifactorial, deeply embedded regional pattern rather than a simple failure of antenatal preparation, inadequate counselling, or a lack of anaesthesiologist expertise. Although qualitative data from Central Europe are lacking, studies from other settings indicate that preference for general rather than neuraxial anaesthesia may be influenced by preoperative anxiety, fear of intraoperative pain or being awake during surgery, concerns about neuraxial needle placement, back pain, spinal cord injury or paralysis, previous negative obstetric or anaesthetic experience, psychiatric comorbidity, and non-medical sources of information [13–17]. Maternal preference should therefore be interpreted not as a single causal category, but as a complex construct shaped by psychological, experiential, informational, and sociocultural determinants. Importantly, this pattern should not be regarded as an optimal standard, but as a persistent phenomenon requiring structured multidisciplinary antenatal counselling and future qualitative research. In this context, anaesthesiologist involvement should occur early enough to support informed consent, address misconceptions about neuraxial techniques and general anaesthesia, and distinguish persistent informed preference from modifiable fear or misinformation.

Emergency caesarean sections, urgency, and neuraxial infrastructure

In emergency procedures, “urgency” was the predominant reported reason for general anaesthesia. However, this finding requires cautious interpretation. Emergency caesarean sections in this audit included Lucas categories 1–3 [10, 11], and the indication “urgency” reflected real-world documentation by the attending anaesthesiologist rather than externally adjudicated criteria. It therefore does not necessarily correspond to an objective category 1 caesarean section requiring immediate delivery under general anaesthesia.

A key systemic barrier in both countries is the absence of a uniformly implemented national urgency classification. In routine practice, the obstetric team usually declares the urgency of delivery, whereas the anaesthesiologist remains responsible for selecting and safely administering the anaesthetic technique. Without an objective urgency framework, the anaesthesiologist may have limited practical ability to challenge whether sufficient time exists for a de novo neuraxial block, particularly under high-stress team conditions. This decision-making asymmetry may contribute to general anaesthesia being chosen even in some cases where neuraxial anaesthesia might have been feasible with clearer urgency stratification and multidisciplinary communication.

This issue is amplified by the low utilisation of labour epidural analgesia, reported as 20% in the Czech Republic and 30% in Slovakia [8]. European data show substantial variation in labour epidural use across countries, with several Western and Northern European systems reporting substantially higher rates [18]. This difference is important for caesarean section practice: in systems with high labour epidural utilisation, a functioning epidural catheter is often already in place and can be rapidly extended to surgical anaesthesia. In contrast, when no catheter is available, the anaesthesiologist must choose between de novo spinal anaesthesia under perceived time pressure and general anaesthesia. Thus, limited labour epidural infrastructure may contribute to higher general anaesthesia use in emergency caesarean sections independently of anaesthesiologists’ technical ability to perform neuraxial anaesthesia.

Labour epidural underuse should not be viewed as a uniform national cultural phenomenon, because marked heterogeneity exists between individual maternity units and is not explained simply by hospital size or level of perinatal care. Local staffing models, availability of a dedicated 24/7 epidural service, engagement of obstetricians and midwives, persistent outdated concerns regarding labour prolongation or caesarean delivery despite contrary evidence [19], and non-medical narratives favouring unmedicated birth may all contribute. These organisational factors are directly relevant to the European minimum standards for obstetric anaesthesia services, which emphasise reliable access to neuraxial techniques and structured departmental organisation [4].

Pharmacological practice and postoperative recovery

The audit also identified pharmacological and organisational barriers that may reduce the full benefit of neuraxial techniques. In epidural anaesthesia, adrenaline was not consistently recorded as part of the local anaesthetic mixture. Its absence may reduce the reliability, duration, and quality of epidural extension and could contribute to insufficient anaesthesia or conversion to general anaesthesia. As this was an audit, we cannot determine whether this reflected deliberate practice, incomplete documentation of the epidural mixture, or local protocol variability. Nevertheless, standardisation of epidural top-up regimens represents a relevant target for improvement.

The low use of intrathecal morphine is another important finding. Long-acting neuraxial opioids are a key component of contemporary post-caesarean analgesia and Enhanced Recovery After Caesarean Delivery pathways [20, 21]. In our region, preservative-free morphine suitable for intrathecal administration is not commercially available in routine clinical practice. In the Czech Republic, it can be prepared only as a magistral formulation by selected hospital pharmacies, predominantly in university hospitals, and its availability for other maternity units is limited. This restricted access may reduce one of the major advantages of spinal anaesthesia for caesarean section: simple, effective, opioid-sparing postoperative analgesia that supports early mobilisation, maternal comfort, breastfeeding, and early maternal–newborn bonding. Wider implementation of high-quality neuraxial anaesthesia should therefore be viewed not only as a strategy to reduce exposure to general anaesthesia, but also as a pathway to improve postoperative Quality of Recovery and maternal experience.

These patient-centred postoperative outcomes were not captured in the present audit. Length of stay, opioid consumption, postoperative pain trajectories, maternal satisfaction, breastfeeding, validated Quality of Recovery measures, ERAC-related endpoints, and early bonding should therefore be included in future studies. A dedicated follow-up analysis focusing on determinants of general anaesthesia use and patient-centred postoperative outcomes is currently being planned.

Safety profile and neonatal outcomes

Neuraxial anaesthesia remains the preferred technique for caesarean section whenever feasible, because it avoids airway manipulation and provides important benefits for postoperative analgesia, maternal experience, and recovery. However, historical mortality estimates comparing general and neuraxial anaesthesia should be interpreted cautiously in contemporary practice. The often-cited excess maternal mortality associated with general anaesthesia is largely derived from historical observational data collected before current obstetric airway algorithms, the routine availability of supraglottic rescue devices, the wider use of videolaryngoscopy, and contemporary monitoring standards [22, 23]. Moreover, such comparisons are highly vulnerable to confounding by indication, because general anaesthesia is preferentially used in the most urgent and highest-risk cases. Our audit was not designed to estimate causal differences in maternal morbidity or mortality between anaesthetic techniques. Therefore, the aim should not be to frame contemporary general anaesthesia as uniformly unsafe, but to reduce potentially avoidable general anaesthesia through better counselling, neuraxial infrastructure, urgency classification, and team communication, while maintaining the ability to deliver safe general anaesthesia when clinically indicated or strongly requested after informed counselling.

In our cohort, airway management was consistent with contemporary obstetric standards [24]. Rapid sequence induction with tracheal intubation was used in most general anaesthesia cases; difficult intubation occurred in approximately 2%, and no aspiration events were recorded. These findings suggest a high degree of airway safety, but should be interpreted with the limitations of registry-based reporting and the short audit period in mind.

Neonatal outcomes are presented in Additional file 1: Table S6. Lower Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were observed in the general anaesthesia group; however, these differences should be interpreted descriptively and not as causal effects of anaesthetic technique. General anaesthesia was used more frequently in emergency and higher-risk procedures, creating substantial confounding by urgency, indication, and underlying foetal status [25]. Umbilical arterial pH values were comparable between anaesthetic techniques, and Apgar scores largely improved by 10 min in both groups. These findings are consistent with transient neonatal depression and differences in case mix rather than demonstrating clinically meaningful hypoxic injury attributable to general anaesthesia. Because the audit was not designed or powered as a neonatal outcome study, these data should be considered hypothesis-generating only.

Financial incentives are unlikely to explain the observed pattern. In both the Czech Republic and Slovakia, there are no direct payments to obstetricians, anaesthesiologists, or institutions based on the type of anaesthesia used for caesarean section. Anaesthetic care is reimbursed as part of the broader DRG-based hospital payment system; therefore, there is no direct financial incentive favouring general anaesthesia over neuraxial techniques.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is its prospective binational audit design combined with population weighting based on official national statistics [9]. This approach mitigates selection bias inherent in voluntary surveys and generates representative national estimates. Alignment with previous OBAAMA audits further permits a unique longitudinal comparison over more than a decade [6, 7].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The observational and descriptive design precludes risk adjustment and causal inference. Reported reasons for general anaesthesia were selected by attending anaesthesiologists from predefined registry categories and were not externally adjudicated. Urgency categories were analysed as a single aggregate group, and no case-level data were collected on decision-to-delivery interval, time of day, or individual provider seniority; therefore, the contribution of out-of-hours practice, anaesthesiologist experience, and obstetrician experience could not be directly assessed. The audit also did not collect qualitative data on maternal preferences, antenatal counselling, decision-making processes, or local reasons for low epidural utilisation during labour. Neonatal outcomes were reported descriptively and were not adjusted for urgency, indication, or foetal status. While population weighting was applied, voluntary participation may still introduce residual selection bias, particularly given Slovakia's lower participation rate. Finally, the one-month audit period limited the capture of rare complications and patient-centred postoperative outcomes.

Conclusion

These population-weighted data show that contemporary anaesthetic practice for caesarean section in the Czech Republic and Slovakia continues to differ substantially from recommended European standards. General anaesthesia remains common, particularly in elective cases, attributed to maternal preference and in emergency cases, attributed to urgency. These findings should not be interpreted as reflecting a single cause, but rather a stable interaction between sociocultural expectations, antenatal counselling pathways, labour epidural availability, non-standardised urgency classification, interdisciplinary decision-making under time pressure, and regional pharmacological and organisational constraints.

Reducing potentially avoidable general anaesthesia will require structured antenatal counselling with earlier anaesthesiologist involvement, expansion of labour epidural infrastructure in line with European obstetric anaesthesia service standards [4], standardised epidural top-up protocols, improved access to neuraxial opioid analgesia, nationally accepted urgency classification, and multidisciplinary communication pathways that allow anaesthesiologists to make technique decisions within an objective and shared framework.
