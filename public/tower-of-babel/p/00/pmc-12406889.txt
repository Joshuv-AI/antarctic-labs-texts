Improving tuberculosis treatment adherence: a qualitative study of patients’ perspectives from a pragmatic trial of the tuberculosis treatment support tools intervention
Authors: Javier Roberti, Daniela Moraes Morelli, Omar Alfonso Aguilar-Vidrio, Agnes Suyanto, Priscilla Carmiol-Rodriguez, Alejandra Tolentino, Enrique Chen-Liang, Jennifer Sprecher, Fernando Rubinstein, Sarah Iribarren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore patient perspectives on using a digital adherence technology (DAT) for tuberculosis (TB) treatment, specifically, the TB Treatment Support Tools (TB-TST) intervention, which integrates a mobile app designed to enhance patient-centred support, monitoring and communication, alongside a drug metabolite test.DesignQualitative study conducted as part of a pragmatic randomised controlled trial.Setting and participantsFour public reference hospitals in Argentina. All patients in the intervention group were invited to participate; 33 patients in the intervention group and five treatment supporters were included.Data collection and analysis: semistructured interviews were conducted. The normalisation process theory guided analysis to understand factors that enable or hinder the intervention’s integration into routine practice for TB treatment medication adherence.ResultsPatients identified medication reminders, educational messages and direct communication with treatment supporters (TSs) as the most helpful components of the intervention. Many reported using the app to ask TSs questions they felt uncomfortable raising with physicians in person. Initially, many patients did not fully understand the purpose and use of the metabolite test. Over time, their understanding of the app improved, though some continued to misinterpret the test results. Motivation to adhere to TB treatment was primarily driven by a desire to protect family members and resume normal daily activities. Reported barriers to app use included time constraints due to work, technical issues, limited internet connectivity and the burden of medication side effects. While the intervention was generally perceived as supportive and user-friendly, patients suggested improvements such as faster response times from TSs, expanded availability and better technical reliability and internet access.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of tailoring digital adherence interventions to meet the diverse needs of patients and reinforce the pivotal role of the TS as a trusted and accessible source of guidance throughout TB treatment.Trial registeration numberNCT04221789; https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04221789.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Medication Adherence, Health informatics, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Digital Technology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, causing 1.5 million deaths annually, making it the world’s leading infectious disease.1 Despite being preventable and curable, adherence to TB treatment, which requires daily medication for a minimum of 6 months, remains a significant barrier to successful outcomes. To achieve the WHO goal of reducing TB incidence by 90% by 2035, a treatment success rate of 90% is targeted. However, current success rates remain below this target.1 The complexity and duration of the TB treatment regimens contribute to non-adherence,2 which can lead to treatment failure, ongoing transmission, the development of drug-resistant TB and increased mortality. Drug-resistant TB, resulting in part from non-adherence, is considered a threat to global health security, requiring effective interventions.1

To address the issue of non-adherence, the WHO has recommended patient-centred strategies,3 4 including the integration of digital adherence technologies (DATs) into healthcare services.3 4 Research suggests that DATs can improve treatment outcomes for high-risk patients by reducing travel-related costs, strengthening patient-provider interactions and promoting patient-centred care when combined with health behaviour interventions.5,8 Mobile Health (mHealth) applications, a type of DAT, have been tested across various chronic diseases and have shown promise in improving treatment adherence.9 Evidence suggests that mHealth app-based interventions can be effective in enhancing medication adherence,9,12 a benefit that may extend to TB treatment.13

Aligned with the WHO’s focus on patient-centred care4 and the integration of DATs, we codeveloped the TB Treatment Support Tools (TB-TST) intervention with patients and experts in Argentina, where treatment success rates have been historically low, from 44% to 66%, and treatment is often self-administered.14,16 TB-TST offers a suite of features, including comprehensive TB education, adherence tracking, side effects monitoring, an anonymous patient forum and interactive communication with treatment supporters via a dedicated dashboard.17 The intervention was iteratively developed, refined and piloted in preparation for a pragmatic, multisite randomised clinical trial (RCT).16 18 TB-TST aims to address the challenges of the TB treatment and contribute to improved success rates.2 19

While previous studies have examined the general acceptability and effectiveness of DATs in TB care, fewer have explored participant experiences with interactive app features and metabolite testing.20 This study, conducted as part of a larger pragmatic clinical trial, focuses on participant experiences using and engaging with the TB-TST intervention during treatment, understanding the use and support of all components of the intervention, including their recommendations for improvement. Effectiveness outcomes, including treatment success rates, are reported elsewhere. Using the normalisation process theory) as an analytical framework, we examined the social processes that shaped how the intervention was integrated into patients’ daily routines and whether it supported adherence practices. Understanding these dynamics is essential for enhancing patient engagement, improving adherence and optimising treatment outcomes. This process evaluation aimed to explore how patients used the TB-TST intervention and identify the barriers and facilitators influencing its use.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was embedded within the larger pragmatic RCT titled ‘Mobile Tuberculosis Treatment Support Tools to Increase Treatment Success in Patients with Tuberculosis in Argentina’ (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04221789).16 The trial evaluated the effectiveness of the TB-TST intervention, aiming to improve treatment adherence and success rates among patients with drug-susceptible TB. This manuscript presents findings from the qualitative component of the study, which sought to explore the experiences of patients from the intervention group participants only and treatment supporters with the intervention to inform iterative improvements. As part of our pragmatic trial, we conducted semistructured indepth interviews with individuals randomised to the intervention arm.16 Additionally, we interviewed five healthcare providers from the study sites (three physicians and two social workers) who served as treatment supporters and study coordinators, focusing on their perceptions of the patient engagement with the intervention. Although not designed to produce generalisable findings, this qualitative study offers rich insights into how and why patients engaged with the DAT, complementing the trial’s quantitative findings and informing future refinement and implementation. This manuscript was prepared following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.21

Setting

This study was conducted at four public hospitals in Buenos Aires, Argentina, which collectively manage over 65% of the country’s annual TB cases. These hospitals were selected based on their geographic diversity within areas identified as high TB burden by Argentina’s National TB Program. At these hospitals, patients with TB undergo self-administered treatment and receive, in general, a 1-month supply of medication, manage their medication at home and are asked to return to the hospital for monthly follow-up visits or earlier if necessary.

Sampling

Participants in their final month of treatment or who had completed treatment within a 6-month data collection window were invited to participate in an exit interview. The target sample included a minimum of 20 participants, with recruitment guided by data saturation and diversity in age, gender and geographic location. Eligible participants were those enrolled in the RCT and randomly assigned to the intervention arm, as well as all the health workers participating in the study as treatment supporters. Inclusion criteria were: age of 16 years and older, a new diagnosis of drug-susceptible TB, regular access to a smartphone and the ability to operate the phone independently or with assistance. Exclusion criteria included severe illness requiring hospitalisation, residing in the same household as another participant and known drug-resistant TB. The sampling approach aligns with qualitative research standards that prioritise depth and variation over statistical representativeness.

Participants were invited to interviews during their final month of treatment regardless of their duration of app use. Treatment supporters provided information about the purpose of the interview and obtained consent to share their contact details with the interviewer, who then scheduled the interviews. The interviewer was blinded to the participants’ treatment outcome and level of app engagement to minimise bias.

Data collection

Semistructured interview guides were collaboratively developed by the research team, drawing on existing literature on DATs, prior experience with similar interventions and findings from the pilot study.17 The guides were piloted with one patient and one treatment supporter, allowing refinement of question flow, wording and contextual relevance. They included open-ended questions designed to elicit participants’ experiences with the intervention, perceptions of its usefulness, motivations for treatment adherence and suggestions for improvement. Interviews were conducted remotely via WhatsApp or telephone due to COVID-19–related restrictions. A registered nurse with TB expertise and training in qualitative research, with no prior relationship with the participants, conducted all interviews. Sessions lasted between 15 min and 45 min and were audio-recorded. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, with no new insights in successive interviews.

Data analysis

Interview audio recordings were transcribed verbatim using Whisper-GPT (OpenAI, California, USA) and reviewed by the research team. A team member cleaned each transcript, standardising formatting, assigning speaker labels and flagging unclear sections for further review. We conducted a theory-informed, abductive analysis,22 iteratively engaging with the data and drawing on key constructs from the NPT, described below, to identify patterns and generate explanatory insights.23,27 Before formal coding, all team members independently read the same three transcripts to identify initial themes. Based on these discussions, a preliminary codebook was developed collaboratively. Four bilingual team members (DMM, AT, EC-L, JR) then applied the codebook to the same three transcripts using Atlas.Ti (Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Coding results were compared and refined through discussion until consensus was reached to ensure consistent coding practices. The remaining transcripts were coded in pairs (PCR-JR, AT-DMM, JR-DMM, AT-JR), with each member coding independently before reviewing and reconciling results with their partner. Regular team meetings supported ongoing refinement of themes, resolution of discrepancies and alignment of interpretations with study objectives. These discussions also fostered reflexivity and consistency throughout the analytic process.

TB-TST intervention

The intervention integrates a progressive web app with a direct urine-based drug metabolite test to monitor medication adherence (figure 1). The app (V.2.0+) is designed to support patients with drug-susceptible TB through medication tracking, educational resources and communication with treatment supporters. Key app features include reporting daily medication intake and side effects, accessing educational content via written materials and videos, receiving weekly messages tailored to treatment phase, messaging treatment supporters, viewing treatment progress via a calendar and visual indicators and participating in an anonymous peer forum. The drug metabolite test detects isoniazid adherence by turning blue-purple in the presence of its metabolites, which remains detectable in urine for approximately 24 hours, making it a reliable biomarker for daily adherence. Offline features enabled patients to complete reports, which were automatically uploaded once connectivity was restored.

Intervention components. TB, tuberculosis.

Treatment supporters play a central role by monitoring reports, providing support and fostering trust to encourage treatment completion. The case management dashboard allows treatment supporters to respond to issues in real time. At enrolment, patients received assistance downloading and activating the app, a live demonstration of its features, instructions for using the metabolite test and submitting test photos and a step-by-step informational trifold. The app includes an onboarding walkthrough accessible at any time.

Theoretical framework

NPT is widely used in healthcare to assess the implementation and sustainability of innovations in real-world settings (figure 2).23,27 NPT comprises four key constructs to determine whether a new practice becomes successfully integrated: ‘‘coherence’ (how well the intervention is understood and is perceived beneficial), ‘cognitive participation’ (extent to which individuals engage with the intervention), ‘collective action’ (effort required to implement or sustain the intervention) and ‘reflexive monitoring’ (how users and providers evaluate impact).27 These constructs guided our analysis of participants’ understanding of TB and the app, their motivations for engagement, the interventions’ impact on routines and assessment of barriers, facilitators and treatment progress.23,27

Understanding TB and the intervention (coherence building)

TB diagnosis meaning: participants described feelings of guilt, isolation and stigma following their TB diagnosis. Many had limited prior knowledge of the disease and expressed a strong sense of responsibility for protecting the health of those around them. Restrictions on daily activities, such as work, school and recreation, exacerbated their feelings of isolation. Stigma was reported not only by the public but also by some healthcare professionals.

Understanding the app: patients generally appreciated the app for creating a secure, anonymous space to share symptoms and concerns without fear of discrimination. It fostered a sense of companionship and connection during treatment. Features such as medication reminders, alarms, appointment tracking and side effect reporting were seen as useful tools for managing and adhering to their treatment. While most participants found the app informative and supportive by facilitating communication with treatment supporters, two viewed it as a constant reminder of their illness. For those who had prior family histories with TB, the app offered a more personalised and distinct treatment experience. Some users struggled to navigate certain intervention features, such as completing the metabolite test and submitting the test pictures; also, the appointment feature was less frequently mentioned compared with other features.

Role of treatment supporters. Treatment supporters were viewed as essential to their treatment experience. They valued their prompt, reliable advice and described the chat feature as the most beneficial aspect of the intervention. They were able to seek reassurance or instruction during episodes of new symptoms, side effects or uncertainty. However, some patients noted delayed response times or hesitated to reach out, fearing they might be a burden. A few patients considered the treatment supporter a replacement for inperson physician visits, valuing their role in reducing the need for frequent visits with doctors. Patients and treatment supporters reported that patients often asked questions via the app that they might not have felt comfortable asking during inperson visits.

Public forum: most participants appreciated the anonymous forum as a space to share experiences and learn from others undergoing TB treatment. It helped them anticipate potential side effects and feel less alone. While some participants actively contributed, others preferred to read the posts passively. Those who did not engage cited outdated posts or a desire to solely focus on their recovery. The anonymity provided by the forum was highly valued, as it allowed for open discussion without the fear of stigma. Treatment supporters also noted that anonymity encouraged more open participation.

Drug metabolite test: initially, most participants did not fully understand the purpose of the drug metabolite test, which is designed to detect isoniazid metabolites in urine and confirm medication adherence. Some mistakenly believed it was used to monitor medication tolerance or renal function. As a result, they often undervalued the importance of submitting test photos to their treatment supporter and interpreted a positive result simply as being ‘on track’ or ‘doing well.’ A few participants found the colour change on the strip confusing or intimidating, unsure of its meaning. Treatment supporters also noted that the colour changes sometimes caused anxiety among patients, leading to concerns about their health.

Engagement and commitment (cognitive participation)

Motivation: patients were primarily motivated to complete their treatment by a desire to be cured and to protect their loved ones from TB. Many viewed adherence to treatment as a way to safeguard their families, especially those who had witnessed severe illness or death from TB in the past. The app played a significant role in maintaining motivation, with features like reminders and a sense of companionship helping them stay accountable and focused on completing treatment. Treatment supporters emphasised that explaining the app’s communication features early encouraged regular engagement. While some patients became preoccupied with their illness, most prioritised medication adherence, even if they occasionally forgot to report it in the app during difficult times. Treatment supporters acknowledged that daily life demands sometimes made consistent app use difficult, as it required time, effort and regular attention.

Implementing the intervention (collective action)

Practising adherence: many participants struggled to establish a routine early in treatment due to medication side effects and lifestyle changes, such as changes to diet or exercise. Most relied on the app’s alarm or automatic reminders, with some supplementing them with personal strategies like phone alarms or associating medication with specific routines. However, some found the reminders and notifications overwhelming or irrelevant. Reporting medication intake was a common challenge; some forgot, while others encountered technical issues with the app, and a few relied on family members to assist with reporting. The calendar feature was frequently used to track progress and check if reports were submitted, though many reported logging entries every few days rather than daily. The drug metabolite test posed the most frequently cited challenge. It required patients to be at home, have a stable internet connection and coordinate hospital visits to collect test strips or medication. Educational messages and public forum discussions were appreciated, although the frequency with which patients used these features varied. Few participants sought additional information about their condition, symptoms or treatment beyond what the app provided. Patients said that limited mobile data, even with provided data plans, obstructed consistent use.

Appraising the intervention (reflexive monitoring)

Barriers to value: time constraints due to work, school, childcare and occasional forgetfulness were significant barriers to daily app use. Technical issues, particularly with older phones, were common and some participants faced challenges related to power outages or insufficient mobile data. Although most participants did not face consistent internet access issues, some relied on public Wi-Fi or WhatsApp, which could be used without data. Treatment supporters reported that patients often used up their data plans, hindering app use. Inconvenient timing, test requests and symptom burden or side effects also contributed to sporadic reporting. Additional reported technical challenges included difficulties with photo capture, app registration and password resets when credentials were forgotten, often requiring assistance from the treatment supporter. The app’s timed logout feature, standard in most applications for security purposes, was reported by some users as disruptive, as it required additional time to log back in. Patients also mentioned that they found it difficult to transfer the app to new devices.

Enablers of value: participants appreciated features such as alarms, calendars, educational messages, reminders and the anonymous peer forum, which were considered helpful in promoting adherence and engagement. These features were well-received and enhanced the app’s usability, making it more intuitive and user-friendly. The onboarding process and support from the healthcare professionals were also seen as valuable. Both patients and treatment supporters agreed that the app became easier to use after the initial learning curve. Although the app included offline functionality, a stable internet connection was considered essential for effective navigation and management.

Tracking progress: the app helped patients monitor their treatment progress, report symptoms and track side effects and overall well-being through their messages to treatment supporters. Some saw the metabolite test as a measure of treatment response rather than adherence, which reinforced their perception that the app tracked their treatment. Participants acknowledged a general lack of public awareness about TB, and many admitted to initially having limited knowledge themselves. While some found the app’s educational content helpful, others suggested it could be more detailed. Treatment supporters noted that the communication feature was instrumental in monitoring treatment progress and addressing patient concerns.

While overall feedback was positive, participants suggested several areas for improvement (table 3). Common requests included: more customisable (eg, sounds) notifications, faster response times from treatment supporters, improved visibility of resources and information and clearer explanations of medications and the metabolite test.

Discussion

This study explored experiences of patients and treatment supporters in using a DAT for TB care. Patients expressed feelings of guilt, stigma and discrimination following their TB diagnosis, and many found comfort and support through the app. The platform enabled open discussion of TB-related issues and concerns without fear of judgement. Overall, participants valued the app for its reminders, treatment guidance and a sense of community. While some noted occasional delays in treatment supporter responses, the treatment supporter feature was widely appreciated. Opinions on the public forum were mixed, with some finding it helpful, while others felt it added little value.

Patient motivation to adhere to treatment was primarily driven by a desire to be cured and protect loved ones, with the app’s reminders and sense of companionship reinforcing this commitment. This study contributes to the literature by offering a theory-informed, qualitative account of patient and health provider experiences with a smartphone-based DAT implemented within a pragmatic RCT in Latin America, an underrepresented region in digital health research. The use of NPT provided a structured lens to understand how the intervention was embedded into practice, aligning with calls for systematic evaluation of implementation outcomes.29 By focusing on relational, emotional and contextual dimensions of technology use, our findings provide insights that may be less accessible through large-scale quantitative studies and complement broader evaluations of implementation and effectiveness.30 31 In resource-constrained settings, where sustained human support may be limited, findings suggest that maintaining interpersonal elements within digital platforms is critical for fostering user motivation and trust.

Ensuring adherence to treatment is essential for successful TB treatment and preventing drug resistance.32 33 Challenges such as medication side effects, managing multiple medications and long treatment duration can hinder adherence.34 35 Additional factors such as depression, socioeconomic status, gender discrimination and healthcare accessibility also impact an individual’s ability to adhere to their treatment.36 37 The reminder function was the most frequently used feature, helping patients establish routines. Notably, some patients reported reduced app usage after integrating medication adherence into their daily lives, an indication that the app helped normalise treatment adherence behaviour rather than a sign of disengagement.

Although evidence remains mixed, DATs show promise in improving medication adherence.735 38,42 Studies across diverse settings have demonstrated that mobile apps and DATs can improve TB treatment outcomes and reduce treatment dropout rates. For example, a study in rural Morocco found that a medication event monitoring system increased treatment success and reduced loss to follow-up compared with standard care.43 Similar benefits have been observed in other chronic conditions, highlighting the potential of digital interventions.9 10 44 45 Remote digital healthcare can also reduce the burden on a patient’s time and energy.46 47 However, for these tools to be effective, they must accommodate patients with limited digital access or literacy and address financial, language, cognitive, physical and social support barriers. A tailored, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach, is essential for patient-centred care.35 Failure to address these issues risks exacerbating digital inequalities and placing an additional burden on patients.48

Education and counselling on TB treatment have been shown to improve adherence, reduce loss to follow-up and increase treatment completion.49 50 Several studies support integrating education into digital health interventions to promote patient acceptance and engagement.3951,53 In our study, participants highlighted the educational component of the app as essential for understanding and managing their treatment. While many knew TB was treatable, few understood the treatment process.

Another important finding was the role of treatment supporters in providing guidance and support throughout the treatment process. Patients valued the ability to monitor their progress independently through the app while having access to healthcare professionals’ support when needed. The COVID-19 pandemic amplified the need for remote monitoring and support where treatment is often self-administered.34 54 55 The treatment supporter feature helped bridge gaps in standard care, offering a more personalised and patient-centred experience.49 50 However, expectations around the level of support varied among patients, underscoring the need for digital interventions to accommodate different user preferences and needs.

Patients often assumed treatment supporters were continuously monitoring and reviewing their inputs and messages, an expectation that may not align with health system capacity. As Madden et al noted, integrating DAT into routine workflows is complex, and provider capacity to review data or messages varies significantly across settings.31 In settings with limited human resources, relying exclusively on human treatment supporters may constrain scalability and sustainability. Exploring complementary solutions, such as artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools, could extend support while easing system burdens. A recent proof-of-concept study demonstrated that large learning models can classify medication adherence from video with high sensitivity and precision.56 Our findings suggest that large language models used in such contexts must be designed to respond with empathy and contextual awareness to preserve the quality of human communication and should maintain humans-in-the-loop for instances where they are not appropriate.

Despite positive findings, this study has several limitations. Participants were purposively selected to reflect diversity in age, gender and location, but the sample may not capture the full range of user experiences. The sample skewed younger, reflecting the age distribution of regional TB demographics.57 Given its qualitative design, the study does not allow for statistical comparisons between subgroups, focusing instead on capturing diverse individual experiences with the digital adherence tool. We did not collect clinical data such as comorbidities, which may influence treatment adherence. Additionally, remote interviews, conducted due to pandemic-related restrictions, may have affected data quality, potentially leading to shorter responses compared with inperson interviews.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of DATs to enhance patient-centred care through tailored support, interactive communication and comprehensive features. The app helped patients better understand their condition, manage treatment and navigate stigma by providing a secure and supportive space. Treatment supporters played a critical role in sustaining engagement and promoting adherence. To strengthen the intervention, efforts should focus on addressing barriers, improving app functionality and enhancing overall user experience. Exploring complementary solutions, such as AI-powered tools, may help extend support, reduce system burdens and improve reach and sustainability. These tools could also improve patient satisfaction by enabling quicker response times and more personalised, context-sensitive interactions.
