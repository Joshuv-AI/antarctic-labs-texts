Beyond campaigns: a national mixed-methods study on advancing routine HPV vaccination in Lesotho
Authors: Mesfin Zbelo, Jethro M Chakauya², Selloane A Maepe, Malefaso Anicia Mosese, Masebeo Koto, Mathaha Makone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLesotho has historically delivered human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination through school-based campaigns, achieving high uptake of the first dose but lower completion of subsequent doses. This study assessed HPV vaccination coverage, dropout rates, documentation practices and the behavioural and social drivers (BeSD) of uptake to inform the transition towards routine HPV vaccination.MethodsA nationally representative mixed-methods survey was conducted in 2024. The quantitative component involved visits to 5111 households and identifying 279 eligible girls aged 9–17 years. Data were collected on receipt of HPV dose 1 and dose 2, availability of vaccination cards and caregiver knowledge. Additionally, 15 key informant interviews were conducted with adolescent girls, caregivers and service providers in Butha-Buthe and Quthing to explore barriers to communication, trust and access. Weighted survey estimates and logistic regression were used for quantitative analysis, while qualitative data were analysed thematically using the WHO BeSD framework.ResultsHPV dose 1 coverage was 85%, while dose 2 coverage was 52.9%, reflecting a 32% dropout rate. In-school girls had higher completion rates than out-of-school girls. Caregiver education and trust in health workers were strong predictors of vaccine uptake. Only 43% of vaccinated girls were able to present a vaccination card. Qualitative findings highlighted communication gaps in follow-up doses, limited access for out-of-school girls due to school-based delivery, and the significant role of trust in health workers as a key factor influencing vaccine acceptance.ConclusionHigh uptake of HPV dose 1 demonstrates strong acceptance; however, substantial dropout and inequities persist, particularly among out-of-school girls. Strengthened communication, improved documentation and expanded delivery strategies are needed. Integrating HPV vaccination into routine primary healthcare and adopting the WHO-recommended single-dose schedule could enhance equitable coverage and support cervical cancer elimination efforts in Lesotho.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains one of the most preventable yet deadly cancers affecting women globally, with an estimated 90% of cervical cancer deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 2 In sub-Saharan Africa, it ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women, largely due to late diagnosis, limited access to screening and treatment, and low uptake of preventive measures. Human papillomavirus (HPV), a common sexually transmitted infection, is the primary aetiological agent and the availability of safe and effective HPV vaccines has made cervical cancer prevention both feasible and cost-effective.1 3 4

In 2020, the WHO launched a global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem, anchored in the ‘90–70–90’ targets: 90% of girls fully vaccinated with the HPV vaccine by age 15; 70% of women screened by age 35 and again by 45; and 90% of those with cervical disease treated.2 HPV vaccination, particularly among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years, is a cornerstone of this strategy.

Lesotho, a lower-middle-income country in southern Africa, introduced the HPV vaccine through school-based campaigns beginning in 2010, supported by Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance.5 6 The country administers the bivalent HPV vaccine (Cervarix) as part of its national HPV immunisation programme.6 Following a period of programme interruption, the country resumed HPV vaccination in 2022, continuing with a similar campaign-based approach targeting selected age cohorts. While these campaigns have achieved promising first-dose coverage, they have not yet been integrated into the national routine immunisation platform.79 As a result, challenges persist, including dropout between doses, limited follow-up mechanisms and exclusion of out-of-school girls, undermining the programme’s equity and sustainability.911

In response to high dropout rates between HPV dose one and HPV dose 2, even in high-coverage settings,12 WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts updated its guidance in 2022 to recommend a single-dose schedule for girls aged 9–14 years, citing comparable protection, improved cost-effectiveness and simplified delivery.2 13

While countries such as Rwanda and the United Republic of Tanzania have demonstrated that strong routine service delivery platforms and community engagement can help achieve high and equitable HPV vaccine coverage,14 15 many LMICs, including Lesotho, are still transitioning from campaign-based to routine service delivery. There is limited national evidence on vaccine coverage, dropout rates and the behavioural and system-level factors influencing vaccine uptake in these settings.1619

In 2024, Lesotho’s Ministry of Health, in collaboration with partners, conducted a nationally representative HPV vaccination coverage survey to generate evidence on coverage levels, equity gaps and implementation challenges.10 18 The study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative estimates of coverage and dropout with qualitative insights from adolescents, caregivers, health workers and community stakeholders. This manuscript presents key findings from that national survey and discusses the policy and programme implications for transitioning from a campaign-based model to a sustainable, routine and equity-oriented HPV vaccine delivery system in Lesotho. The evidence generated is intended to support national policy dialogue and contribute to the global knowledge base on effective cervical cancer prevention strategies in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study setting

Lesotho is a landlocked country in southern Africa, with an estimated population of 2.2 million. It is administratively divided into 10 districts, each served by a network of public and private health facilities. The national HPV vaccination programme primarily uses the bivalent HPV vaccine (Cervarix), which is delivered mainly through school-based campaigns,9 targeting adolescent girls. Efforts are underway to transition this campaign-based delivery model into a routine immunisation programme, integrated within the expanded programme on immunisation and primary healthcare (PHC) platforms.8 9

Study design

A nationally representative, cross-sectional, mixed-methods study was conducted from February to April 2024. Quantitative household surveys were combined with qualitative interviews to assess HPV vaccination coverage, dropout rates, equity in delivery and behavioural and social drivers (BeSD) of uptake.20

Quantitative component

A multistage, stratified cluster sampling design was used to ensure national and subnational representativeness. In the first stage, 268 enumeration areas (EAs)—standard geographical units used in national censuses—were selected as primary sampling units using probability proportional to size, stratified by district and urban/rural classification. In the second stage, households were randomly selected within each EA. A total of 5111 households were visited, with 4884 completing the household interview. From these households, 279 eligible girls aged 9–17 years were identified and included in the survey. The lower-than-expected number of eligible girls reflects the demographic profile of Lesotho, where adolescents represent a small proportion of the population, and many households had no girls in this age group.

Although WHO recommends HPV vaccination primarily for girls aged 9–14 years, this study included girls aged 9–17 years to allow for comprehensive analysis of current and historical coverage. Girls aged 15–17 years may have received the vaccine through earlier or catch-up campaigns conducted between 2012 and 2022. Including this broader age range enabled assessment of historical programme reach, age-related coverage trends and dropout patterns to inform future routine and catch-up strategies.

Sample size determination

The sample size was estimated using the standard single-population proportion formula for HPV vaccination coverage, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and an expected coverage of 50% to maximise the required sample size. A design effect of 2 was applied to account for cluster sampling and a 10% non-response rate was allowed. This resulted in a target of approximately 845 eligible girls per stratum. Based on these assumptions, it was estimated that about 5111 households would have to be visited. In practice, 279 eligible girls aged 9–17 years were identified, reflecting the relatively small proportion of girls in this age group within the general population. The low yield of eligible girls reflects Lesotho’s demographic structure, where the 9–17 year age group constitutes a small proportion of the total population. Additionally, many households did not include girls in this age range.

Qualitative component

To complement the quantitative findings, a qualitative component was conducted using key informant interviews (KIIs) to explore contextual, behavioural and social factors influencing HPV vaccination uptake. A total of 15 KIIs were carried out, comprising 5 adolescent girls (3 in-school and 2 out-of-school), 5 caregivers of eligible girls and 5 service providers (comprising nurses, teachers and village health workers).

Interviews were conducted in Butha-Buthe and Quthing, purposively selected based on preliminary HPV vaccination performance data, with Butha-Buthe representing a high-performing district and Quthing a low-performing district. This sampling strategy ensured variation in contextual factors relevant to service delivery and access.

Interviews were conducted in Sesotho by trained qualitative researchers using a semi-structured interview guide that explored awareness of HPV vaccination, experiences with service delivery, perceptions of benefits and risks, communication and trust, and barriers to access. The qualitative interview guides are provided in online supplemental file 1. Interviews were held in private settings at schools, health facilities or participants’ homes according to participant preference. They lasted approximately 30–45 min and were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent.

Recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English and subjected to thematic content analysis. Two independent coders reviewed and coded all transcripts, employing both inductive (to capture emergent concepts) and deductive coding guided by the WHO BeSD of immunisation framework.20 NVivo 12 software (QSR International) was used to facilitate coding, data management and theme development. Any coding discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus through iterative review.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected by trained field teams using a structured, tablet-based Measles Rubella Post Campaign Coverage survey administered to eligible participants. The survey questionnaire used in this study is provided in online supplemental file 2. Vaccination status was verified by reviewing home-based records where available; otherwise, caregiver recall was recorded. Interviews were conducted in Sesotho and lasted approximately 30–45 min. They were supervised by district coordinators to ensure data quality. Daily data checks and field validations were conducted. The questionnaire included demographic details, awareness of HPV and cervical cancer, receipt of HPV dose 1/HPV dose 2 and reasons for non-vaccination. Documentation refers to the presence of a home-based vaccination card confirming receipt of HPV doses.

Qualitative transcripts were coded by two independent researchers using thematic content analysis. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion. NVivo 12 was used to organise data. Themes were derived deductively using the WHO BeSD framework and inductively from emerging patterns.20 Qualitative data were collected in Sesotho, audio-recorded, transcribed and translated into English.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Coverage estimates were weighted to reflect the national population structure. Descriptive statistics were generated for HPV dose 1 and HPV dose 2 coverage, dropout rates, vaccination card availability and caregiver knowledge. Bivariate analyses, including χ² tests, were used to assess associations between vaccination outcomes and sociodemographic variables such as school attendance and caregiver education. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of HPV vaccination uptake, with adjusted ORs and 95% CIs reported. All analyses were conducted using Stata V.18.5 for Windows, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

Qualitative analysis

A thematic content analysis approach was used. Transcripts were coded inductively and deductively using a framework informed by the WHO BeSD of immunisation model.20 Two researchers independently coded transcripts, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. NVivo 12 software supported data organisation and theme development. Qualitative findings were triangulated with quantitative results to contextualise vaccination coverage patterns.

Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was maintained by removing personal identifiers and restricting data access to authorised research personnel. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient and public involvement

None. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Sociodemographic profile of survey respondents

A total of 279 eligible girls aged 9–17 years consented and were included in the analysis (table 1). These girls were identified from 4884 completed household surveys drawn from 5111 households visited nationally. The mean age was 13.2 years (± 2.1 SD), with a majority (84.7%) attending school at the time of the survey. About 15.3% were out of school, highlighting a subpopulation at risk of being missed by school-based vaccination efforts.

In terms of residence, 61.1% of participants lived in rural areas, while 38.9% resided in urban settings. This reflects Lesotho’s largely rural population and underscores the importance of equitable rural health service delivery.

Regarding caregiver education, 55.2% of girls were cared for by someone with primary education or less, while 44.8% had caregivers with secondary education or higher—a factor known to influence health-seeking behaviour and vaccine uptake. Nearly half of all participants (48.3%) came from female-headed households, indicating the potential importance of gender dynamics in health decision-making within families.

HPV vaccination coverage by age group

HPV dose 1 and HPV dose 2 coverage increased steadily with age, peaking at 92.9% and 81.6%, respectively, among girls aged 15–16 years (figure 1). In contrast, younger girls (aged 9–10 years) showed lower coverage levels.

HPV dose 1 and dose 2 vaccination coverage and dropout rate by age group among girls aged 9–17 years in Lesotho. HPV, human papillomavirus.

The dropout rate—defined as the proportion of girls who received HPV dose 1 but not HPV dose 2—also declined with age, from 52.5% among 9 years old to 12.2% among 15–16 years old. These trends suggest that older adolescent girls were more likely to complete the vaccination series, possibly due to stronger participation in school-based campaigns and greater health awareness. Younger age groups may require more targeted outreach and follow-up to ensure full vaccination.

HPV vaccination cascade

Among girls aged 9–17 years (n=279), 85% received the first dose of the HPV vaccine, while 52.9% received the second dose, corresponding to a dropout rate of approximately 32%. This coverage cascade highlights substantial attrition between doses (see online supplemental figure 1).

HPV dose 1 and HPV dose 2 coverage by district and place of residence

HPV vaccination coverage showed notable variation across Lesotho’s 10 districts, reflecting differences in campaign performance, outreach effectiveness and accessibility—particularly in remote or mountainous areas. Butha-Buthe achieved the highest HPV dose 1 coverage at 90%, followed by Leribe (88%) and Berea (85%). In contrast, Quthing recorded the lowest HPV dose 1 coverage at 78%, while Mokhotlong, Mohale’s Hoek and Qacha’s Nek also reported coverage below 82% (figure 2).

HPV dose 1 and dose 2 vaccination coverage in Lesotho by district and place of residence. HPV, human papillomavirus.

HPV dose 2 coverage followed a similar but consistently lower pattern, with the highest in Butha-Buthe at 68% and the lowest in Quthing at 45%. These disparities highlight the need to tailor interventions to local contexts, strengthen district health team capacity and ensure consistent support to underserved areas to achieve equitable and comprehensive coverage nationwide.

HPV coverage by urban and rural residence

Urban girls had slightly higher coverage for both doses and lower dropout compared with rural girls (figure 2). This suggests the need to strengthen rural outreach and improve equitable access to vaccination services.

HPV vaccination coverage by school attendance

HPV dose 1 and dose 2 coverage, as well as dropout rates, disaggregated by school attendance status among girls aged 9–17 years in Lesotho are shown in figure 3. Among in-school girls, HPV dose 1 coverage was 86.5% (95% CI 84.4% to 88.3%) and HPV dose 2 coverage was 65.0% (95% CI 60.4% to 69.6%), resulting in a dropout rate of 21.5% (95% CI 18.2% to 24.9%).

In contrast, out-of-school girls had lower coverage: HPV dose 1 at 71.8% (95% CI 63.0% to 80.0%) and HPV dose 2 at 40.8% (95% CI 32.1% to 47.9%), with a dropout rate of 43.7% (95% CI 35.6% to 51.8%).

HPV coverage by caregiver education

Girls whose caregivers had secondary or higher education had significantly higher coverage of both HPV dose 1 and dose 2 and lower dropout rates, underscoring the role of caregiver awareness and empowerment in vaccination decision-making (see online supplemental figure 2).

Reasons for non-vaccination

The most common reasons for non-vaccination included lack of information about return dates (18.9%), unawareness of the need for immunisation (8.0%) and absence during campaigns. Minor but notable concerns included fears related to fertility and vaccine rumours (figure 4).

Statistical analysis findings

χ² tests were conducted to examine associations between key categorical variables, such as school attendance, caregiver education and place of residence, as well as HPV vaccination outcomes. Binary logistic regression was also employed to identify significant predictors of vaccine uptake, including behavioural and sociodemographic factors. All analyses accounted for sampling weights and clustering using Stata V.18.5.

Significant bivariate associations were observed between school attendance and both HPV dose 1 and dose 2 vaccination coverage, as well as between caregiver education and vaccine completion. Girls attending school were significantly more likely to receive both doses, while higher caregiver education was associated with higher completion rates and lower dropout rates (p<0.001 for all comparisons). In addition, girls residing in rural areas had significantly lower HPV dose 2 coverage, highlighting persistent geographical disparities in access to services. Detailed results of the chi-square analyses are presented in online supplemental table 1.

The logistic regression analysis reinforced these associations. Girls whose caregivers had secondary education or higher were nearly twice as likely to complete the vaccine series (OR=1.9, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.4). Trust in health workers and awareness of the HPV vaccine were also strong predictors of HPV dose 1 uptake (ORs=2.4 and 2.1, respectively), highlighting the critical roles of health literacy, trust and effective communication (table 2).

These findings underscore the need for equity-driven interventions that prioritise outreach to out-of-school girls, girls from less-educated households and those in rural areas, through routine immunisation platforms and community-based strategies.

Qualitative findings

The qualitative component explored behavioural and contextual factors influencing HPV vaccination uptake across different groups. Three major themes emerged from the 15 KIIs with adolescent girls, caregivers and service providers.

Theme 1: communication gaps and limited awareness of HPV vaccination

Participants consistently reported gaps in communication regarding the purpose of the HPV vaccine and the timing of follow-up doses. Caregivers described uncertainty about when vaccinators would return to schools or communities, which contributed to missed opportunities for completion of the vaccination schedule.

I did not know she needed another dose; no one told us when they would return. – Caregiver, rural district.

Some adolescent girls also noted limited understanding of the importance of completing the full vaccine series, especially when information at school was brief or unclear.

Theme 2: barriers to accessing vaccination for out-of-school girls

Interviewees highlighted that out-of-school girls faced substantial barriers to vaccination access. Because HPV delivery platforms relied heavily on schools, girls not enrolled in school were commonly missed unless outreach teams specifically targeted communities.

Girls who are not in school are usually missed because vaccination only happens at the school. – Village health worker.

Health workers and teachers noted that these gaps were more pronounced in rural and mountainous areas where household dispersion made community outreach more difficult.

Theme 3: trust in health workers as key influencers of vaccine acceptance

Caregivers consistently emphasised trust in health workers as a major factor shaping their decision to allow their daughters to be vaccinated. Participants reported that explanations from nurses and village health workers increased their confidence and willingness to consent.

When the nurses explain the vaccine, I feel comfortable allowing my daughter to receive it. – Caregiver, urban district.

Service providers also highlighted the importance of their community relationships in countering misinformation and encouraging vaccine uptake.

Supporting quantitative observations

Qualitative insights helped contextualise several quantitative findings, including: lower vaccination completion among out-of-school girls, caregivers’ limited awareness of the need for a second dose and documentation gaps, with many participants reporting never receiving or later losing vaccination cards.

These insights complement the quantitative results by highlighting structural and informational barriers that influence completion of HPV vaccination.

Discussion

This nationally representative mixed-methods study provides critical insights into the current state of HPV vaccine delivery in Lesotho. While the country has made commendable progress in reaching 85% coverage for the first dose (HPV dose 1), a substantial 32% dropout before the second dose (HPV dose 2) reveals systemic gaps—particularly in follow-up mechanisms, communication and documentation. These challenges are consistent with patterns seen in other sub-Saharan African countries using campaign-based delivery models.11 12

Our findings reaffirm the effectiveness of school-based platforms in reaching in-school girls, especially those aged 11–16 years. However, the persistent exclusion of out-of-school girls—often from marginalised, rural or less-educated households—highlights significant equity concerns. These girls face limited access to health services and fewer opportunities to receive vaccine information, increasing their vulnerability to HPV-related cervical disease.

The global literature supports this trend and emphasises similar barriers, including weak reminder systems and caregiver unawareness.13 14 17 19 21 WHO’s recent endorsement of a single-dose HPV schedule offers a timely opportunity to reduce dropout, simplify delivery and improve completion rates—particularly in resource-constrained contexts.2 4 Had this policy been implemented, Lesotho could have potentially avoided a significant portion of its observed dropout.

Transitioning from campaign-based delivery to a routine, integrated approach—leveraging PHC, school health programmes and community outreach—is now essential. Additional strategies, such as mobile clinics and adolescent wellness services, will be vital to reaching out-of-school girls and sustaining immunisation momentum.2125

Behavioural insights from this study further enrich the analysis. Although vaccine hesitancy was minimal, gaps in communication and myths about fertility were reported. The strong trust placed in health workers, teachers and local leaders offers an opportunity to enhance social mobilisation and vaccine literacy.

Digital tools can play a pivotal role in strengthening immunisation systems. Lesotho’s 2024 National Digital Health Strategy26 provides a platform for innovations such as electronic immunisation registers, SMS reminders and real-time dropout tracking—essential components for a resilient and responsive immunisation system.

These findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, vaccination status was partly based on caregiver recall in the absence of vaccination cards, which may have introduced recall bias. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding determinants of vaccine uptake and dropout. Third, while the survey was nationally representative, subnational estimates in some strata may have wider CIs, consistent with data quality challenges and follow-up limitations noted in other evaluations.2528 Fourth, the number of eligible girls identified during household visits was lower than anticipated, reflecting the small proportion of girls aged 9–17 years in the general population. This reduced sample size may have limited the precision of some age-specific and subgroup estimates. Despite these limitations, the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods enhances the robustness of the findings and provides actionable insights for policy and programme design.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence for Lesotho to transition from campaign-based to routine and equity-focused HPV vaccination. While high HPV dose 1 coverage reflects strong community acceptance and operational readiness, dropout before HPV dose 2 and challenges in reaching out-of-school girls highlight areas in need of urgent attention.

Adopting the WHO single-dose policy would significantly simplify implementation and reduce missed opportunities.4 Integrating HPV vaccination into PHC and school health systems, while expanding outreach to underserved populations, will be key to sustaining and accelerating progress.

Investments in digital tracking, health worker capacity and multisectoral coordination will further strengthen the delivery system. Aligning HPV efforts with broader adolescent and cervical cancer prevention strategies will help ensure that no girl is left behind.

With the right policy choices and implementation focus, Lesotho is well positioned to achieve the WHO 90–70–90 cervical cancer elimination targets and contribute to global health equity, in line with the WHO Global Cervical Cancer Elimination Initiative.2830

Implications for advancing routine HPV vaccination

The findings of this study have several implications for strengthening HPV vaccination delivery in Lesotho. Transitioning to the WHO-recommended single-dose HPV schedule for girls aged 9–14 years could simplify delivery, reduce dropout rates and lower operational costs while maintaining strong protective efficacy.22931 Evidence from this study suggests that a simplified schedule may help address the substantial second-dose dropout observed.

Integrating HPV vaccination within the national routine immunisation programme—anchored in PHC and school health services—may enhance sustainability and continuity beyond periodic campaigns. Complementary delivery approaches, such as outreach services and adolescent health platforms, will be essential for reaching out-of-school girls who currently face the greatest access barriers.

Improving documentation and follow-up systems, including strengthened home-based records and digital immunisation registries, may reduce gaps in vaccination card retention and enhance monitoring. The 2024 National Digital Health Strategy offers a foundation for boosting information systems and accountability.20 21

Strengthening provider communication and community engagement remains critical. Health workers, teachers and local leaders continue to play key roles in building confidence and addressing misconceptions. Multisectoral coordination across health, education and gender sectors will be important in advancing routine HPV vaccination and supporting Lesotho’s cervical cancer elimination goals.
