Cardiovascular and renal outcomes of combined SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists versus monotherapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Min-Hsiang Chuang, Cheng-Wei Ho, Hsien-Yi Wang, Heng-Chih Pan, Vin-Cent Wu, Yu-Kang Tu, Jui-Yi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Whether using both sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) is more effective than using either alone in type 2 diabetes mellitus remains uncertain. We sought to assess cardiovascular and kidney outcomes of combined SGLT2i and GLP-1RA therapy versus monotherapy using network meta-analysis.Methods:We identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of SGLT2i or GLP-1RA in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus through a comprehensive search of MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library until Aug. 15, 2025. We compared patients receiving SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, both SGLT2i and GLP-1RA, or neither medication in a systematic review and network meta-analysis. Primary outcomes were major adverse cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and stroke) and major adverse kidney events (decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate, kidney failure, and death due to kidney failure). Secondary outcomes included heart failure–related hospital admissions, serious adverse events, and hypoglycemia.Results:We included 12 RCTs with 99 683 participants. The risk of major adverse cardiovascular events did not differ significantly with combined SGLT2i and GLP-1RA therapy compared with SGLT2i (risk ratio [RR] 0.86, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.74 to 1.01; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA alone (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.12; very low certainty). Similarly, the risk of major adverse kidney events did not differ significantly with combined therapy from SGLT2i (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.49; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.21; very low certainty). However, combined therapy was associated with a lower risk of heart failure–related hospital admission than SGLT2i (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.99; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.86; very low certainty). Risks of serious adverse events or hypoglycemia did not differ significantly between combined therapy and monotherapy.Interpretation:Compared with SGLT2i or GLP-1RA alone, combined therapy did not significantly reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular or kidney events but was associated with lower risks of heart failure–related hospital admission. Randomized controlled trials directly comparing combined therapy with monotherapy are needed to clarify the comparative effectiveness of combined therapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.Protocol registration:PROSPERO — CRD42024605727

Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) lower blood glucose by reducing renal glucose reabsorption, preventing glucose toxicity and oxidative stress in kidney cells.1 They also enhance tubuloglomerular feedback by increasing sodium delivery to the macula densa, which lowers glomerular pressure and blood pressure, thereby protecting kidney function.2 On the other hand, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) promote insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release, while lowering blood glucose by reducing liver glucose production and slowing gastric emptying.3

The combination of SGLT2i and GLP-1RA has garnered considerable attention for its potential to improve cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.4,5 Recent evidence, including findings from a large cohort study, suggests that combining these 2 drug classes may provide superior benefits for metabolic, cardiovascular, and renal outcomes compared with monotherapy.5 The distinct and complementary mechanisms of action of SGLT2i and GLP-1RA result in synergistic effects that reduce both macrovascular and microvascular complications, improve cardiovascular risk factors, and prevent diabetic nephropathy, making their combination an appealing therapeutic option.6

Meta-analyses that have evaluated combined therapy versus monotherapy report varying results. A recent meta-analysis by the SMART-C (SGLT2i Meta-Analysis Cardio-Renal Trialists Consortium) collaboration found that SGLT2i consistently reduced the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, heart failure–related hospital admission (henceforth, hospitalized heart failure), and progression of chronic kidney disease, regardless of whether patients were receiving a GLP-1RA at baseline.7 Similarly, another meta-analysis, which analyzed randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of GLP-1RA, evaluated cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in patients with and without concurrent SGLT2i use.8 The study concluded that GLP-1RA provide consistent cardiovascular and renal benefits, irrespective of background SGLT2i therapy.8

However, both meta-analyses included a relatively small number of trials and participants using combined therapy at baseline, which limited the generalizability of their findings. The comparisons were limited to pairwise analysis, which precluded further insights into the comparison between various treatment arms. To address these gaps, we sought to incorporate data from a wider range of RCTs in a network analysis to evaluate the cardiovascular, kidney, and safety outcomes of combined SGLT2i and GLP-1RA therapy versus monotherapy or placebo. By leveraging both direct and indirect comparisons, we aimed to provide more comprehensive evidence on the clinical efficacy and safety of combined therapy compared with SGLT2i or GLP-1RA monotherapy to guide clinical practice in real-world scenarios.

Methods

Identification and selection of studies

This systematic review and network meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 checklist,9 along with the PRISMA extension for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA).10 The PRISMA-NMA checklist is included in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250369/tab-related-content. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024605727). We conducted a comprehensive search for RCTs in Ovid (Embase, MEDLINE) and the Cochrane Library without any date or language restrictions, with the final search completed on Aug. 15, 2025. Search terms included a combination of free text and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms. Two reviewers (M-H.C. and C-W.H.) independently screened citations based on predefined selection criteria. We examined the references of included articles and relevant systematic reviews for additional eligible studies. The full search strategies are provided in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 2.

Eligibility criteria

We included RCTs of adults (aged ≥ 18 yr) with type 2 diabetes who received GLP-1RA, with or without baseline use of SGLT2i, and those who received SGLT2i with or without baseline GLP-1RA. All included trials evaluated either GLP-1RA or SGLT2i monotherapy versus placebo. No trial directly compared combined SGLT2i and GLP-1RA therapy with placebo. We excluded trials that compared GLP-1RA or SGLT2i with other glucose-lowering medications, studies that did not specifically target the population with type 2 diabetes, trials in which the use of GLP-1RA and SGLT2i could not be distinguished, studies that did not report cardiovascular or kidney outcomes, and studies published in abstract form or without peer review.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of the current study were major adverse cardiovascular events (the composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and stroke) and major adverse kidney events (the composite of ≥ 40% or 50% decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], kidney failure [eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2, chronic dialysis, or kidney transplantation], or death due to kidney failure). Trials in which the components of these composite outcomes differed were not included for the corresponding outcome. Secondary outcomes included hospitalized heart failure, eGFR slope, serious adverse events, and hypoglycemia (blood glucose level ≤ 70 mg/dL or any severe episode requiring the assistance of another person). Serious adverse events were defined by individual trials. A detailed list of serious adverse events for each trial is provided in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 3.

Data extraction

For each included study, we recorded study and patient characteristics, including the year of publication, the medications used in the intervention arm, sample size, mean age, sex, history of cardiovascular disease or heart failure, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, mean baseline eGFR, glycated hemoglobin, and the proportion of patients under renin-angiotensin system blockade at baseline. We recorded the number of patients with primary and secondary outcomes, the total number of patients in each group, and changes in eGFR during treatment. Trial-level sample sizes and the number of major adverse cardiovascular events and major adverse kidney events are provided in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 4. We categorized patients into treatment groups (i.e., SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, or placebo) if they had been randomized to it or if they had been using it at baseline in an included study. Accordingly, patients randomized to the SGLT2i group who also used GLP-1RA at baseline or those randomized to the GLP-1RA group who also used SGLT2i at baseline were categorized as the combined therapy group. We considered patients taking one of the medications at baseline and subsequently randomized to placebo, as well as those not taking it and subsequently randomized to receive the medication, to be part of the same treatment group. We extracted data for the subgroups of patients who did or did not use SGLT2i or GLP-1RA at baseline separately. To avoid violating treatment randomization, in each trial, patients receiving the noninterventional medication at baseline (e.g., GLP-1RA in SGLT2i trials) were compared only with those who were also receiving it at baseline and vice versa. Two independent examiners (H.-Y.W., H.-C.P.) extracted data, which a third examiner (V.-C.W.) subsequently reviewed and adjudicated.

Quality assessment of evidence within studies and across networks

We evaluated the risk of bias in the included studies using Cochrane’s Risk-of-Bias tool 2.0 for RCTs, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, loss of outcome data, and selective reporting of results.11 Two investigators (V.-C.W., H.-C.P.) independently carried out the assessments. In instances where the reviewers disagreed on the risk-of-bias rating, a third reviewer (J.-Y.C.) was consulted for discussions to reach a consensus. Based on these assessments, we classified studies as low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias. We assessed the transitivity assumptions according to the clinical and methodological characteristics of the included studies, as well as by a comparison of the treatment effects among treatment arms. We proceeded with data synthesis if the transitivity assumptions were met, confirming validity of indirect comparisons.

We evaluated the certainty of evidence for each outcome across the network using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. This approach assessed within-study risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias for direct and indirect evidence in a stepwise manner, along with intransitivity, incoherence, and imprecision, providing a final grading for the network evidence categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.12

Statistical analysis

In the networks of treatment comparisons, a node represented each treatment, and randomized comparisons between therapies were shown as links between nodes. We performed statistical analyses for all outcomes based on 4 nodes: combined therapy, GLP-1RA alone, SGLT2i alone, and placebo. We conducted frequentist network meta-analysis using random-effects models. We reported continuous data as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) and dichotomous data as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. We evaluated overall heterogeneity and inconsistency with I2 statistics. We estimated the between-study variance τ2 using the restricted maximum likelihood method and calculated the CIs based on the standard normal distribution. We performed sensitivity analyses based on the t distribution to make statistical inferences using multivariate random-effects meta-analysis. We assessed publication bias with funnel plots and Egger tests. We calculated P-scores to evaluate the relative ranking of the probability that a treatment modality was superior to others. These scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater likelihood that a treatment is superior to other treatments in the network. The league tables displayed the relative efficacy of all possible pairwise comparisons, with the point estimates presented as RRs along with their 95% CIs. We evaluated incoherence by a design-by-treatment interaction test and node-splitting analysis. Subgroup and dose–response analyses were not pre-specified and not conducted because of the inconsistent availability of arm-level data.

To ensure that treatment effects were not systematically different between SGLT2i and GLP-1RA trials, we performed proportional meta-analyses to assess between-group heterogeneity for pooled proportions of patients with outcomes from SGLT2i trials versus GLP-1RA trials. In the absence of significant heterogeneity, we deemed treatment effects consistent across trial designs, and we proceeded with a network meta-analysis.

All tests were 2-tailed, with the statistical significance level set at a p value less than 0.05. We conducted statistical analysis using R version 4.2.2 with packages netmeta (version 3.2–0), meta (version 8.2–0), metafor (version 4.8–0), and ggplot2 (version 3.5.2).

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was not required for this study, as it was a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published, publicly available aggregate data and did not involve individual patient-level data or human participants.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Through systematic search of the electronic database, we identified a total of 13 798 records. After removing duplicates (n = 2108), we screened 11 690 records based on their titles and abstracts, among which 298 were assessed in full text for eligibility. We included 12 studies published between 2015 and 2025 in the current study, comprising 6 SGLT2i trials (EMPA-REG OUTCOME,13 CANVAS Program,14 DECLARE-TIMI 58,15 CREDENCE,16 VERTIS CV,17 SCORED18) and 6 GLP-1RA trials (ELIXA,19 LEADER,20 Harmony,21 AMPLITUDE-O,22 FLOW,23 SOUL24). We extracted data from the original reports and post hoc analyses of the ELIXA,25 Harmony,26 AMPLITUDE-O,27 FLOW,28 and SOUL29 trials, supplemented by 2 meta-analyses7,8 for data extraction and crosschecking. A flowchart for the selection process is provided in Figure 1. The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants ranged from 60.3 to 69.0 years, and the proportion of male participants ranged from 55.1% to 71.4%. Three studies included patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease,16,18,28 with a mean eGFR ranging from 44.6 to 56.2 mL/min/1.73 m2, while 9 other trials included patients with type 2 diabetes regardless of chronic kidney disease status, with a mean eGFR greater than 70 mL/min/1.73 m2.

The risk-of-bias assessment of the included trials is summarized in Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 1. All studies were deemed to have low risk of bias. Evaluation of treatment effects between SGLT2i and GLP-1RA trials revealed no significant heterogeneity for major adverse cardiovascular events, major adverse kidney events, hospitalized heart failure, severe adverse events, and hypoglycemia (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 5). Data on eGFR changes were unavailable for some treatment arms in the included trials, so heterogeneity could not be assessed, and network meta-analysis was not performed for this outcome.

Primary outcomes

Among 4 treatment arms from 11 studies with 21 comparisons, the median incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events was 8.6% in the combined therapy group (interquartile range [IQR] 6.5% to 11.0%), 10.6% in the GLP-1RA group (IQR 7.7% to 12.5%), and 10.0% in the SGLT2i group (IQR 8.4% to 11.4%). Combined therapy was not associated with a significant difference in the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events compared with SGLT2i (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.01; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.12; very low certainty). Therapy with GLP-1RA alone was associated with a lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events compared with SGLT2i alone (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.99; very low certainty) (Figure 2A and Table 2). Combined therapy, GLP-1RA, and SGLT2i were all judged as maybe effective according to P-score ranking, treatment effect, and certainty of evidence (Table 3). The overall heterogeneity and inconsistency were low (I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.8) and we did not detect asymmetry on the funnel plot (Egger test p = 0.3; Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 2A). The certainty of evidence was low for comparisons between GLP-1RA and placebo and very low for comparisons between combined therapy and SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, or placebo, as well as for comparisons between SGLT2i and GLP-1RA and between SGLT2i and placebo (Table 2 and Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 6A).

Among 4 treatment arms from 8 studies with 16 comparisons, the median incidence of major adverse kidney events was 2.6% in the combined therapy group (IQR 0.9% to 5.3%), 2.8% in the GLP-1RA group (IQR 2.2% to 5.1%), and 2.3% in the SGLT2i group (IQR 2.0% to 5.3%). The risk of major adverse kidney events did not differ significantly with combined therapy than with SGLT2i (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.49; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.21; very low certainty), whereas GLP-1RA therapy was associated with a higher risk than with SGLT2i (RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.49; very low certainty) (Figure 2B and Table 2). According to the P-score ranking and certainty of evidence, SGLT2i may be among the most effective in reducing major adverse kidney events, whereas combined therapy and GLP-1RA may be of intermediate efficacy (Table 3). The overall heterogeneity and inconsistency was low (I2 = 10.3%, p = 0.3), and we did not detect apparent asymmetry on the funnel plot (Egger test p = 0.8; Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 2B). The certainty of the evidence was low for the comparison between SGLT2i and placebo, and very low for other comparisons because of potential intransitivity and imprecision (Table 2 and Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 6B).

Node-splitting analysis showed no significant incoherence between direct and indirect evidence for all comparisons of major adverse cardiovascular and kidney events (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 7A and Table 7B, and Supplementary Figure 4A and Figure 4B). Inconsistency for all direct comparisons was considered not severe, as indicated by overlapping CIs (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 5A and Figure 5B).

We judged the transitivity assumption to be valid, considering the similar demographics of the included participants. Although the proportion of patients with underlying chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease varied between studies, the treatment effect sizes and their relative order were generally consistent across the included studies (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 3), thereby providing transitable estimates in relative terms.

Secondary outcomes

The risk of hospitalized heart failure was significantly lower with combined therapy than with GLP-1RA (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.86; very low certainty) or SGLT2i (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.99; very low certainty), but was significantly higher with GLP-1RA compared with SGLT2i (RR 1.15; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.32; very low certainty) (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 6A and Figure 7A). According to the P-score ranking and certainty of evidence, combined therapy and SGLT2i may be among the most effective in reducing hospitalized heart failure, whereas GLP-1RA may be of intermediate efficacy (Table 3). The funnel plot showed no apparent asymmetry (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 8A); certainty of evidence ranged from very low to low (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 6C and Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 7A).

Data on eGFR changes during treatment were collected but effect estimates were unavailable for some treatment arms in the included trials. A summary of eGFR changes is provided in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 8. In the included trials and patient subgroups, the treatment arm generally showed less eGFR decline or a null effect compared with placebo, with a mean difference ranging from −1.70 to 5.82 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year.

The risk of serious adverse events with combined therapy did not differ significantly from SGLT2i (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.01; very low certainty) or GLP-1RA (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.02; very low certainty) (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 6B and Figure 7B). Based on the P-score ranking and certainty of evidence, combined therapy, GLP-1RA, and SGLT2i may be less harmful than placebo with respect to serious adverse events (Table 3). The funnel plot for this outcome showed no apparent asymmetry (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 8B). The confidence of evidence is listed in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 6D.

No significant difference was observed in the risk of hypoglycemia between the combined therapy with SGLT2i (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.32; very low certainty) and GLP-1RA (RR 1.31, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.75; very low certainty), while GLP-1RA was associated with a lower risk of hypoglycemia than SGLT2i (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.93; very low certainty) (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 6C and Figure 7C). The P-score ranking and certainty of evidence indicated that GLP-1RA may be the least harmful therapy with respect to hypoglycemia, while the risk of hypoglycemia with SGLT2i and combined therapy may be no different from placebo (Table 3). Funnel plots for this outcome demonstrated no apparent asymmetry (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 8C). The confidence of evidence for hypoglycemia is listed in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 6E.

Node-splitting analysis showed no significant incoherence between direct and indirect evidence for all secondary outcomes (Appendix 1, Supplementary Tables 7C to 7E, and Supplementary Figures 4C to 4E). Inconsistency for direct comparisons was considered severe for the comparison between GLP-1RA and combined therapy regarding hospitalized heart failure (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 5C), and between SGLT2i and placebo regarding severe adverse effects (Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 5D), and certainty of evidence was down-rated accordingly.

Sensitivity analyses of treatment effects based on the t distribution revealed similar results as our main analyses (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 9) with slightly wider CIs. The upper bound of the 95% CIs for the risk ratio of combined therapy for major adverse kidney events increased from 0.98 to 1.02 and crossed the null but remained within the range of equivalence; therefore, the interpretation of a potential benefit or null effect was not altered.

Interpretation

Our network analysis, involving 12 large RCTs with 99 683 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, found that combined therapy with SGLT2i and GLP-1RA did not significantly reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events compared with either agent alone. All 3 treatments were likely more effective than placebo. The risk of major adverse kidney events was not significantly different with combined therapy than with SGLT2i or GLP-1RA. Combined therapy reduced the risk of hospitalized heart failure compared with GLP-1RA or SGLT2i alone, with very low certainty of evidence. In addition, combined therapy did not increase the risk of severe adverse events or hypoglycemia compared with monotherapy.

The rationale for combining SGLT2i and GLP-1RA lies in their distinct mechanisms of action, with synergistic effects that reduce both macrovascular and microvascular complications by improving glycemic control, promoting renal sodium excretion, and reducing blood pressure.6 Previous studies indicate that the cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2i are likely owing to hemodynamic effects and the reduction of cardiac fibrosis, while GLP-1RAs offer cardiovascular protection through antiatherogenic and anti-inflammatory properties.6,30 These 2 classes of medications may therefore provide additive and enhanced cardiovascular benefits when used together. Furthermore, both SGLT2i and GLP-1RA reduce systemic oxidative stress by modulating overlapping oxidase and antioxidant pathways, potentially enhancing levels of circulating provascular progenitor cells and mediating vascular repair.31 Three other meta-analyses have also demonstrated results favouring combined therapy over monotherapy for major adverse cardiovascular events.7,8,30

Regarding kidney-related outcomes, recent studies have shown different results for combined therapy compared with SGLT2i and GLP-1RA alone, indicating that combined therapy has a lower risk of major adverse kidney events than GLP-1RA but showing no significant difference when compared with SGLT2i.7,8,32 Studies have shown SGLT2i to provide direct renal benefits by reducing sodium reabsorption in the proximal tubule through its osmotic diuretic effect and activation of tubuloglomerular feedback.33 Different from SGLT2i, kidney benefits from GLP-1RA are primarily driven by the reduction of urinary albumin excretion and enhancement of natriuresis.34,35 Whether these differing mechanisms account for the variation in findings of major adverse kidney events between SGLT2i and GLP-1RA is uncertain. Moreover, the modest kidney protection observed with GLP-1RA may be influenced by differences in the medications tested, patient populations, and follow-up durations in GLP-1RA trials.36 This suggests that different combinations of SGLT2i and GLP-1RA may have varying effects on major adverse kidney events, which remained to be examined in more detailed, stratified RCTs.

Considering the differing profiles of adverse effects, SGLT2i and GLP-1RA are unlikely to cause negative interactions when used together.6 Their distinct mechanisms of action provide additive cardiorenal benefits, potentially reducing the risk of serious adverse events. Previous studies have shown that combined therapy is not inferior to monotherapy in terms of the risk of serious adverse events,7,8,37 consistent with the results observed in our analysis. Moreover, combined therapy did not increase the risk of hypoglycemia compared with monotherapy. This outcome may be attributed to the glucose-dependent action of GLP-1RA, which minimizes the risk of hypoglycemia while providing additional benefits through its high efficacy in glucose control.38,39 On the other hand, SGLT2i does not increase the risk of hypoglycemia, likely because it blocks glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules, promoting glucose excretion without stimulating insulin secretion.40 The combination of these 2 drug classes may further reduce the need for other hypoglycemia-prone medications.

Limitations

Some patients in the GLP-1RA trials started SGLT2i not given at baseline and vice versa during the follow-up period. These post-randomization changes may have biased our results toward the null since the definition of combined therapy was based on baseline antidiabetic prescriptions. Because of differences in the outcomes reporting by each study, the number of studies included for each outcome varied, and we could not analyze the risk of all-cause death. Our analyses relied on subgroup data and are therefore methodologically weaker than trials directly randomizing patients to combined therapy versus monotherapy. This is why, although we found that monotherapy was more effective than placebo, we rated the evidence generated in our study as low or very low certainty.

Patients in the combined therapy group were using 1 medication at baseline before being randomized to a second medication; thus, they were likely already tolerating or benefiting from the initial treatment, and this population may differ from patients who were not using either GLP-1RA or SGLT2i at baseline. Furthermore, although we categorized both patients initiating medication after randomization to the intervention arm and those on stable baseline therapy randomized to placebo into the same exposure group, their clinical characteristics (e.g., treatment tolerance) may have differed. Consequently, caution is warranted when interpreting our findings, particularly the indirect estimates comparing combined therapy to placebo.

Although we did not observe asymmetry in the funnel plots on visual inspection or the Egger test, the statistical tests may be underpowered, particularly when the number of data points was fewer than 10. Variability in the availability of arm-level dose data across the included trials precluded a dose-specific analysis. Finally, as most evidence comparing SGLT2i and GLP-1RA comes from indirect assessments through network meta-analysis, more direct clinical trials comparing SGLT-2i and GLP-1RA are needed to draw definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

Combined therapy did not significantly reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events but was likely more effective in reducing hospitalized heart failure than either SGLT2i or GLP-1RA alone, with very low certainty of evidence. All 3 treatments were likely more effective than placebo, with low certainty of evidence for GLP-1RA alone, and very low certainty of evidence for combined therapy and SGLT2i alone. For major adverse kidney events, combined therapy did not differ significantly from SGLT2i or GLP-1RA, with very low certainty of evidence. Combined therapy was not associated with an increased risk of severe adverse events or hypoglycemia. Given the absence of significant differences between combined therapy and monotherapy and the serious imprecision of available evidence, RCTs directly comparing combined therapy with monotherapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus are needed to provide guidance for clinical practice.
