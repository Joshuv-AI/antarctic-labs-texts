The effect of high intensity interval training on body composition, metabolic, inflammatory and oxidative stress markers in adolescents with obesity
Authors: Gulten Atabay Arslan, Ozgur Kasimay, Ilknur Bingul, Asuman Gedikbasi, Ani Agopyan, Meral Kucuk Yetgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of the study is to evaluate the effects of 12-week high-intensity interval training (HIIT) on body composition, metabolic hormones, insulin resistance, lipid profile, inflammation, and oxidative stress markers in adolescents with obesity.MethodsThirty-two (female = 19; male = 13) adolescent volunteers with obesity were divided into two groups: training and control. Participants underwent health checks with resting electrocardiogram (ECG) and blood pressure monitoring. Anthropometric measurements, body composition components, and biochemical tests were performed in pre and post-tests of all participants. Serum fasting glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglyceride, high and low-density lipoproteins, leptin, adiponectin, High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), hs-CRP, TNF-alpha, IL-6, prooxidant, and antioxidant parameters were analyzed biochemically. Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated. The training group participated in a HIIT program for 12 weeks, 3 days a week, for a total of 36 sessions, after 3 weeks of pre-adaptation studies. Heart rate was monitored with a heart rate monitor. The control group was not included in any exercise program during this period.ResultsThe mean age of the adolescents with obesity included in the study was 15.28 ± 2.93 years and Body Mass Index (BMI) was 33.21 ± 4.98 kg/m2. Group, sex, and time interaction effects showed that HOMA-IR and triglyceride levels had reduced in the HIIT group (p < 0.001). The group-time interaction effect was observed in the parameters related to body composition, metabolism, inflammation, and pro-oxidant status (p < 0.05). BMI, waist and hip circumferences, body fat (%), insulin, glucose, total cholesterol, LDL, triglyceride, and leptin were decreased in the HIIT group (p < 0.05). While lean mass, muscle mass, body water and adiponectin were increased in the HIIT intervention group (p < 0.05). The inflammation parameters (hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6), and pro-oxidant indicator Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs) were decreased in the HIIT group.ConclusionA 12-week HIIT program in adolescents with obesity has positive effects on body composition, metabolic parameters, inflammation, and oxidative stress markers. These benefits may contribute to healthy growth and developmental processes. HIIT represents a time-efficient, practical, and effective strategy to mitigate obesity-related metabolic and inflammatory disturbances while strengthening antioxidant defenses.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic metabolic disease arising from sedentary lifestyle, excessive energy intake, and environmental or genetic factors, characterized by an increasing prevalence a low-grade systemic inflammatory, and an association with various chronic diseases. Adolescent obesity is a serious public health problem facing the world in the twenty-first century [1].

Obesity has also been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction mediated by oxidative stress and inflammation, which adversely affects energy metabolism [2]. Exercise is an effective strategy to reduce obesity-induced oxidative stress. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has proven to be an effective exercise protocol for reducing fat mass in a shorter time and is widely used in the field of chronic disease prevention [3, 4]. HIIT is an effective exercise strategy for improving cardiometabolic health, particularly in children and adolescents with obesity, by enhancing cardiorespiratory fitness and improving risk factors such as cholesterol, insulin resistance, and blood pressure [5]. Furthermore, HIIT has been reported to elicit greater enjoyment compared to moderate-intensity exercise among adolescents, supporting its feasibility and acceptability as an intervention for this population [6].

Although this health evidence supports the practice of HIIT, it has mostly focused on the adult population and its effects on adolescents have been shown in a limited number of studies [7].

Adipose tissue, an endocrine organ, shows continuous volume variability throughout life in terms of cell number and size, depending on energy demand and consumption and hypertrophy of adipocytes gives the first signal of obesity [8]. Adipocytes produce a variety of biologically active molecules collectively known as adipocytokines or adipokines, including TNF-α, leptin, and adiponectin. The infiltration of macrophages into adipose tissue causes a low-grade chronic inflammation. Infiltration of inflammatory cells into the expanding white adipose tissue (WAT) is well known to cause adipocyte dysfunction and metabolic dysfunction such as glucose intolerance and insulin resistance [9]. In humans, fat accumulation has been shown to be associated with systemic oxidative stress and impairs glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue, reduces insulin secretion from pancreatic β cells [10]. The increase in adipokines and free fatty acids in response to insulin signaling, as well as the increase in triglycerides (TG), contribute to oxidative stress and insulin resistance by exceeding antioxidant capacity (lipid peroxidation). Increased oxidative stress suppresses antioxidant systems in adipose tissue and creates a vicious cycle. Obesity-related oxidative stress, an imbalance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants, is linked to both metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [10].

Exercise improves antioxidant mechanism, lowers fat mass, provides glycemic control and may reduce oxidative stress by regulating blood lipids [11]. HIIT may be a feasible and effective exercise model against obesity and chronic low-grade inflammation in adolescents. Compared to traditional long-duration aerobic exercises, it can provide effective results in a shorter time [12].

However, the evidence regarding the effects of HIIT on adolescents with obesity is still limited, and comprehensive studies examining anthropometric, metabolic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress parameters in this age group are scarce. Addressing this gap is important since adolescence represents a critical developmental stage where effective interventions may support long-term health and prevent adulthood obesity.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of HIIT on anthropometric measurements, body composition, metabolic status, inflammation, oxidative stress and antioxidant defense system markers in adolescents. The limited studies on the effects of HIIT on adolescents and the importance of the adolescent period in the transition to a healthy adulthood were seen as the main necessity for this study.

Methods

Study participants

The research group consisted of belonging to the Caucasian race volunteer adolescents (13–18 years old, n = 32) who applied to a private sports center and were classified as obese [(Body Mass Index (BMI) > 85th Percentile)] according to international standards [13]. Power analysis was performed using G*Power (3.1.9.2) programme to determine the sample size. To obtain 95% power at α = 0.05 level, the effect size was found to be 1.78 and at least 8 people in each group [14]. The power analysis was conducted to determine the overall sample size and was not specifically designed to detect sex-specific effects; therefore, sex-based analyses should be considered exploratory. The 32 volunteers who met the health control conditions were divided into two groups HIIT (male n = 5; female n = 11) and control group (male n = 8; female n = 8). For the allocation to either group, a sequential number was given according to enrolment within the study. Boys and girls were randomized separately. The randomization process was completed using the website randomizer.org.

Study design

In the study, experimental research method with control group including pre-test/post-test model was used. Before starting the study, the participants were informed about the principles of volunteering, their rights, training model, planning and processes. One week before the start of the training programme, anthropometric measurements (waist circumference, hip circumference, waist and hip ratio, body weight, height, BMI) and body composition components (body fat percentage, body fat mass, body muscle mass, lean body mass) of the volunteers were analysed and BMI percentiles according to age were determined. The compliance of the study volunteers with the HIIT protocol was evaluated by a health check-up. For this purpose, risk analyses were performed, ECGs were performed at rest and blood pressure was monitored. In addition, blood pressure and blood pressure was monitored at rest in the morning and evening for one week. All participants in both the control and training groups were instructed not to follow any specific dietary pattern and not to to engage participate in any additional exercise programme during the 12-week intervention period, otherwise, they would be excluded from the study.

For biochemical analyses, blood samples were collected in the morning following a 12-h fasting period fasting period, centrifuged and serum samples were stored under appropriate conditions until analysis.

After the pre-tests, to ensure participant adaptation in the training group, the aerobic program (3 days/week; 30 min/day) at 40–60% heart rate reserve (HRR) during in the first 2 weeks and 70–80% HRR in the 3rd week was implemented, followed by the application of the high-intensity interval training model 3 days per week totaling 36 sessions, over 12 weeks. In the following week, body composition and anthropometric measurements were repeated and serum samples collected and were stored again for biochemical analyses. Throughout the study, all procedures were performed by the same same trained experts in their respective fields.

Anthropometric measurements

All the anthropometric parameters of the participants were measured by a specialist according to the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry [15] protocol under fasting conditions, without shoes and with underwear. Height measurements were taken barefoot using a stadiometer (SECA, Germany) with a precision of 0.01 mm. Waist circumference (WC-cm) was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib cage and the upper iliac crest in the horizontal plane, and hip circumference (HC-cm) was measured at the level of the femoral trochanters. Both circumference measures were taken to the nearest 0.1 cm at the end of a normal expiration in standing position with a flexible non-elastic tape measure (Lufkin Steel Anthropometric Tape; W606PM; Lufkin; Apex; USA). Waist /Hip ratio: Waist circumference (cm) / Hip circumference (cm) was calculated using the formula.

Assessment of body composition

Body weight, body fat percentage (BF) [%], muscle mass (MM) [kg], fat mass (FatM) [kg], lean tissue content (FFM) [kg], total body water (TBW) [kg] and total body water percentage (TBW) [%] were analyzed by a bioelectrical impedance method using a standardized body composition analyzer (Tanita SC 330-S, London, UK). To minimize body composition measurement error, the intake of food, beverage, alcohol, caffeine, etc., and vigorous physical activity were restricted for 8 h before measurement. The body mass indexes (BMI) of the participants were calculated by dividing the body weight (kg) by the square of the height length (m). BMI = Body weight (kg) / [Height (m)]2 [16]. BMI classification was made according to age and gender. In both sexes and at each age, BMI values in the 85th-95th percentile were classified as high risk group for obesity (overweight) and those in the 95th percentile and above were classified as obese [13].

Determination of target heart rate

A physician measured blood pressure manually from the left arm using a stethoscope and a sphygmomanometer (Erka, Bad Tölz, Germany) in a resting position. A 12-lead ECG (Mason-Likar lead placement) was continuously monitored and recorded using a computerized device (Case12, Marquette Medical Systems, Milwaukee, USA). The target heart rate of the training group was calculated with the Karvonen formula:

Target heart rate = ((220-age-resting heart rate) x target percentage%) + resting heart rate [17].

Heart rate monitoring

Following calculation of target heart rate according to the Karvonen formula, a personalised HIIT protocol was applied based on the HRR of each participant in the training group. Accordingly, data were recorded in the Polar application as loading with 90–95% HRR (working phase) and 40–60% HRR as active rest. Heart rate was monitored using a heart rate analyzer Polar S810i (Polar ®, Finland) with a recording strap positioned on the distal third of the sternum and a receiver placed on participants’ wrist [18]. These intervals were personalized according to the HIIT protocol, and exercise was monitored within the target heart rate range.

Biochemical analyzes

Peripheral blood samples from each subjects were taken into two 5 ml vacutainer tubes (Becton Dickinson, BD, Juiz de Fora, MG, Brazil) containing EDTA for plasma separation and into one 5 ml dry vacutainer tube for serum separation. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4◦C, and plasma and serum samples were stored at − 80◦C until biochemical analyses.

Serum fasting glucose, total cholesterol (TC) triglyceride (TG), low density lipoprotein (LDL) high density lipoprotein (HDL) and high sensitive C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels were measured using a Beckman Coulter AU680 autoanalyzer using original kits (Beckman Cuolter, Inc. California, USA). Insulin levels were detected by using UniCel DxI 800 autoanalyzer (Beckman Coulter, Inc. California, USA). The Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) index was used to evaluate insulin resistance (IR) and was calculated using the following formula: fasting insulin concentration (µU/mL) × fasting glucose concentration (mg/dL) / 405. Higher HOMA-IR scores indicate greater insulin resistance [19].

Serum Leptin (Human Leptin Quantikine #DLP00, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Canada) and adiponectin (Human Adiponectin Platinum #BMS2032, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) levels were measured using ELISA (enzyme linked immunosorbent assay) kits in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Tumor necrosis factor alpha (Human TNF-alpha Quantikine #DTA00C, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Canada) and interleukine-6 (Human IL-6 Quantikine #D6050, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Canada) were detected in serum samples by sandwich ELISA method.

Determination of prooxidant parameters

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (Saint‐Louise, MI, USA). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was determined by the method described by Wang and Joseph [20]. Serum samples were diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4, and were incubated with 100 µmol/L 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate at 37 °C for 30 min. The fluorescence was determined using a microplate fluorometer (Fluoroskan Ascent FL, Thermo Scientific Inc, MA, USA) with an excitation at 485 nm and emission at 538 nm. Results were reported as relative fluorescence units (RFU).

Lipid peroxidation was measured by the determination of thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) according to Buege and Aust [21]. 0.2 mL serum, 0.2 mL 0.15 M KCl and 0.8 mL Buege-Aust reagent were added to each tube and heated in a boiling water bath for 15 min. After cooling and centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min, absorbance of supernatans were measured spectrophometrically. Results were reported as using the 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane standard curve and expressed as µmol malondialdehyde/L.

To determine advanced glycation end products (AGEs), serum samples were diluted with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4, and fluorescence intensity was measured (λemission: 440 nm; λexcitation: 350 nm). Results were expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU) [22].

Determination of antioxidant parameters

Serum antioxidant power was evaluated by ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay. A ferric-tripyridyltriazine complex is reduced to the ferrous form by the action of electron donating antioxidants present in serum. The reaction is monitored by measuring the change in absorption at 593 nm. Results were reported as µmol Fe2+ equivalents/L [23].

Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity was measured using cumene hydroperoxide as substrate [24]. The assay mixture contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH:7.0), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM sodium azide, 0.2 mM NADPH, 1 mM GSH, 0.5 IU/mL GSH reductase, 1.2 mM cumene hydroperoxide, and 0.04 mL of serum samples in a total volume of 0.2 mL. The reaction was followed spectrophotometrically (340 nm) at 37 °C after the addition of cumene hydroperoxide. Results were calculated using the extinction coefficient of NADPH (6.22 × 103 M−1 cm−1) and expressed as nmol/mL/min.

Total SH levels were performed according to the method of Sedlak and Linsay with the use of 5,5-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoate) at 412 nm [25]. Results were calculated using the extinction coefficient of 13.6 × 103 M−1 cm−1) and expressed as µmol/L.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were detected by spectrophotometric-colorimetric method and expressed as U/mL in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit #706,002, Cayman Chemical, Michigan, USA).

High ıntensity ınterval training (HIIT) protocol

For HIIT protocols, all sessions were supervised and were conducted 3 days per week (Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays), each day for 12–16 min each session over 12 weeks, totaling 36 sessions. In this study, a progressive HIIT protocol with was applied with gradually increasing loading intensity: 12 min (30 s load, 60 s rest × 3 repetitions) at 90–95% HRR during weeks 1–4, 14 min (30 s load, 60 s rest × 4 repetitions) during weeks 5–8, and 16 min (30 s load, 60 s rest × 5 repetitions) during weeks 9–12. Rest intervals between sets were performed at 40–60% HRR, with 60 s of low intensity (speed 17–20). At the beginning of each session, a 3-min pedaling warm-up (speed 13–17 / 34–40 rpm) and a 3-min pedaling cool-down (speed 13–17 / 34–40 rpm) of pedalling cool-down at the end of each session were included in the total training time.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency (%) for categorical variables, mean ± SD for normally distributed data, or median (IQR) for nonparametric data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Participant characteristics were compared with Pearson’s chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U test. The effect of HIIT intervention, sex and time was investigated with Repeated measures three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Aligned Rank Transformation (ART) was applied conducting nonparametric three-way ANOVA. The correlation between participant age and changes in parameters was analyzed with Spearman’s rank correlation. Statistical analysis were performed with R (ARTool package) [26] and IBM SPSS version 25.0, and the statistical significance was set at 0.05 level.

Anthropometric measurement and body composition parameters

Height, waist, waist circumference, waist-hip ratio, body fat (%), body mass, lean mass, muscle mass, body water, and body water (%) differed significantly between sex (p < 0.05). Waist circumference (p < 0.01) and waist-hip ratio (p < 0.05) were significantly decreased with HIIT intervention. The main effect of HIIT intervention was not seen in BMI and hip circumference parameters, but the group-time interaction effect on reduced BMI and hip circumference was significant (p < 0.05). The repeated-measures analysis revealed significant group × time interactions for multiple body composition outcomes, indicating that the 12-week HIIT intervention elicited distinct temporal responses compared with the control condition. Specifically, participants in the HIIT group demonstrated favorable longitudinal changes in body fat percentage, body fat mass, lean mass, muscle mass, and total body water across the intervention period, whereas these parameters remained largely unchanged in the control group. The individual-level data further supported these findings by demonstrating consistent within-group trends in the HIIT group, alongside comparatively stable distributions in the control group.

Metobolic parameters

Metobolic parameters affected by group, sex and time was presented in Table 2. The reduction in glucose and triglycerid levels were significantly affected by sex (p < 0.05) and interaction effect of group-time (p < 0.05). Adiponectin level was significantly increased with HIIT intervention, time and interaction effect of group-time (p < 0.05). HIIT intervention and time interaction effect was significant for all metobolic parameters (p < 0.05), excluding HDL. Triglycerid level was significantly affected by the three-way interaction of group-sex-time (p < 0.05). The two-way effect of group and time showed that triglycerid (T: p = 0.023, ES = 0.16; GxT: p < 0.001, ES = 0.37) and HOMA-IR (T: p < 0.001, ES = 0.37; GxT: p < 0.001, ES = 0.58) were significantly affected by the main effect of time and the interaction effect of group-time. Inflamation, pro-oxidant and antioxidant parameters

The effects of Group, sex, and time effect on parameters related to inflammation, pro-oxidant, and antioxidant status was shown in Table 3. IL-6, ROS, and FRAP levels differed significantly by the HIIT intervention, with a significant group × time interaction (p < 0.05). The effect of HIIT intervention was not as significant as the main effect on CRP, TNF alpha, and AGE; however, the the group × time interaction for these parameters was significant (p < 0.05). ROS levels were affected were affected by the group × sex × time interaction (p < 0.01). Correlation analysis

Males’ age was negatively correlated with the weight change (rho = -0.656, p = 0.015) and the change in BMI (rho = -0.577, p = 0.039), while it was positively correlated with the change in insulin (rho = 0.559, p = 0.047). Females’ age was inversely associated with the change in lean mass (rho = -0.463, p = 0.046), the change in body water (rho = -0.456, p = 0.05), and the change in HDL (rho = -0.491, p = 0.033).

Discussion

The main result of this study was that the 12-week HIIT program (3 days/week) with progressively increasing intensity every 4 weeks with had a positive effect on body composition, metabolic profile, inflammation and oxidative stress markers in adolescents with obesity. It was revealed that BMI, waist and hip circumference, body fat percentage, insulin, glucose, total cholesterol, LDL, triglycerides, and leptin HOMA-IR levels decreased in the HIIT group. In addition, while lean body mass, muscle mass, and adiponectin levels increased in the HIIT group, inflammation markers (CRP, TNF alpha, and IL-6) and prooxidant indicator AGE levels decreased. Overall, the study hypothesis was partially supported.

Anthropometric measurement and body composition

In line with previous findings, this study demonstrated that HIIT positively affects key anthropometric and body composition parameters—including reductions in BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, and body fat percentage—even without dietary intervention (Table 2). These findings support meta-analytical evidence suggesting that HIIT is an effective exercise modality for improving health indicators [5]. According to a recent meta-analysis, the impact of HIIT on BMI in adolescents is limited however, it significantly improves body composition and cardiovascular health in overweight adolescents by increasing VO₂peak, raising HDL levels, and lowering SBP [27].

Notably, the approximately 6 cm reduction in waist circumference observed in the participants underscores the clinical significance of the HIIT intervention. Discrepancies in the literature where some studies report significant reductions [28, 29] while others observe no effect [30, 31] may reflect differences in training duration, frequency, intensity, periodization, and participant characteristics such as age and sex. Additionally, HIIT may exert immediate effects on carbohydrate metabolism and muscle glycogen stores, whereas measurable changes in adipose tissue may necessitate longer intervention periods.

Discussion of metabolic parameters

HIIT is not clinically superior in terms of body fat loss and reduction of body mass; however, it provides significant cardiovascular improvements in the context of obesity. Our study suggests that HIIT has beneficial effects on key metabolic parameters in adolescent obesity, including insulin, glucose, HOMA-IR, leptin, adiponectin and blood lipids. Delfan et al. [32] demonstrated significant reductions not only in adipokine levels but also in glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR in males with obesity following a 12-week HIIT protocol and underlined its cardiometabolic health enhancing role. Similarly, the main findings of the study conducted by Saeidi et al. on 68 obese men reported that a 12-week HIFT program improved adipokine profiles and insulin resistance in obese men [33]. Additionally, it has been reported that different resistance training protocols in obese men produce measurable improvements in gremlin-1, macrophage migration inhibitory factor, as well as cardiometabolic and anthropometric parameters [34]. The results of another study demonstrated that short-term circuit resistance training reduced muscle damage indices reflecting cardiovascular risk in untrained young subjects [35]. Babraj et al. [36] proposed HIIT as a time-efficient alternative to traditional aerobic exercise for individuals at metabolic risk. Racil et al. [37] reported significant reductions in total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and LDL, along with an increase in HDL after a 16-week HIIT program with severe obesity and moderate obesity adolescents boys with obesity. These findings align with our results, particularly regarding TC and LDL reductions. They concluded that HIIT enhances lipid oxidation, reduces TG, increases HDL, and supports cardiovascular improvements in adolescents with obesity. In our study, adiponectin levels significantly increased in the HIIT group, and no statistical correlation was found between changes in adipokines and body composition. These results suggest that HIIT may improve metabolic parameters independently of changes in body composition. Another study reported that a 3-week HIIT program in overweight/obese young women had acute but not permanent effects on leptin and adiponectin levels [38]. The molecular pathways mediating this effect remain an active area of current research. Exercise is a potent physiological stressor that disrupts cellular homeostasis and triggers a complex network of adaptive reactions. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and inflammation-related signaling pathways are key regulators of this stress response [39]. AMPK is the primary regulator that senses energy status, supports metabolism for glucose and fatty acid utilisation, and mediates beneficial cellular adaptations in many vital tissues and organs [40]. Changes in the AMP/ATP ratio during exercise activate AMPK, a metabolic master switch, to maintain energy balance and support mitochondrial biogenesis [41]. Peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), a master transcriptional coactivator, regulates mitochondrial biogenesis by interacting with various transcription factors. A deficiency of PGC-1α has been associated with impaired glucose homeostasis. The observed increases in expression levels of PGC-1α protein produced by HIIT have been supported by many studies in obese rats, diabetic mice, and humans [41]. Research indicates that manipulation of adipocyte AMPK may influence body fat composition, glucose homeostasis, lipolysis, and beige fat formation, revealing its potential to serve as a therapeutic target in the treatment of various metabolic disorders [42]. Exercise-induced cytokines exert profound effects on adipose tissue metabolism, influencing energy expenditure, thermogenesis, fat loss, and adiposity [43]. For example, IL-6 promotes fatty acid oxidation and reduces lipogenesis by activating AMPK. IL-15 stimulates fatty acid catabolism by upregulating the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta (PPARδ) and suppresses lipogenesis. HIIT may lead to downregulation of leptin and upregulation of adiponectin by affecting the transcriptional activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ), a central regulator of fat cell differentiation and adipokine gene expression. Furthermore, improvements in fat tissue insulin sensitivity after HIIT may contribute to a transition toward a healthier adipokine profile by alleviating metabolic stress and inflammatory signaling within fat cells. Taken together, these mechanisms provide a plausible biological framework for the observed decrease in leptin and increase in adiponectin after HIIT; however, they remain speculative in the context of the current study and require direct molecular investigation in future studies.

Age and gender spesific differences

Our study revealed several significant age and sex-related differences in outcomes. Males’ age was negatively correlated with the weight change and the change in BMI, while it was positively correlated with the change in insulin. Girls’ age was inversely associated with the change in lean mass, the change in body water, and the change in HDL. The relationship between cardiorespiratory fitness and age (both chronological and biological) has been examined multiple times by gender. A study evaluated the role of biological age in the relationship between pre-intervention fat mass and cardiorespiratory fitness effects after a HIIT intervention in adolescents [44]. The results indicated that HIIT is an effective way to achieve positive cardiorespiratory changes in adolescents, and these changes are more pronounced in boys than girls. Cardiorespiratory fitness was significantly higher in younger girls compared to older girls and in early adolescence compared to late adolescence. In boys, cardiorespiratory fitness was higher in younger adolescents, with the greatest increase occurring at the age of peak height velocity. Age at peak height velocity also plays an important role in girls, and HIIT should be adapted based on their maturity. Another study investigating the relationships between sex hormones and muscle mass and strength in male adolescents at different stages of puberty investigated the interactive effects of testosterone and exercise on muscle strength. The results suggest that exercise interventions in boys in early- to mid-adolescence may have a greater effect than in boys in late-to-post-adolescence on muscle strength. [45]. A meta-analysis reported that gender-specific differences in leptin levels, characteristic of normal-weight pre-pubertal children, disappeared in obesity. Therefore, the greater acceleration of puberty in girls compared to boys due to obesity cannot be explained by leptin levels alone; it may be a combination of other factors [46]. Although our study was not designed to assess these physiological pathways directly, we acknowledged their relevance and emphasized the need for future studies that incorporate hormonal profiling and a more precise assessment of pubertal stage. We believe that this provides a clearer framework for developing sex-specific, personalized exercise interventions in adolescents.

Inflammatory and oxidative stress markers

Our study demonstrated that long-term HIIT intervention significantly reduced CRP levels in adolescents with obesity, suggesting its beneficial impact on chronic low-grade inflammation commonly associated with adiposity. This aligns with findings by Buchan et al. [47] who reported no post-exercise increase in CRP in the HIIT group, whereas the control group experienced a rise. A significant inverse correlation between waist circumference and CRP levels supports the inflammation-reducing effects of fat loss [48]. Despite reviews [49, 50] reporting that high-intensity or anaerobic exercise induces acute increases in IL-6 and TNF-α levels due to metabolic stress as part of the post-exercise inflammatory response, our study found no significant acute-phase inflammatory elevation; instead, TNF-α and IL-6 levels significantly decreased after 8 weeks of HIIT, demonstrating an anti-inflammatory effect [51]. Although there are clear links between adiponectin and cytokine (particularly TNF-α and IL-6) alterations and body composition/metabolic improvements [52], we observed metabolic improvements independent of body composition changes. These findings highlight HIIT’s potential to modulate inflammation beyond mere fat loss in the adolescent with obesity.

With respect to glyco-oxidative stress, our findings are consistent with those reported by Farinha et al. [53]. In their study, significant reductions in circulating AGEs, were observed following HIIT in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Similarly, we found notable decreases in AGEs in adolescents with obesity, despite the absence of dietary modification, suggesting that this effect may be specific to the HIIT-induced metabolic adaptation rather than changes in nutritional intake. Considering that AGEs serve as long–half-life biomarkers of chronic carbonyl and glyco-oxidative stress, the reduction observed over the 12-week period indicates a meaningful decline in metabolic stress.

The oxidative stress response to HIIT appear nuanced. Some animal studies have reported that following short-term HIIT sessions, the lipid peroxidation marker and oxidative stress indicator TBARS decreased by approximately 17.75% in rats, while no change was observed in creatine kinase levels, a marker of muscle damage [54]. In contrast, some studies have reported increases in ROS and TBARS levels after HIIT due to high anaerobic stress [55, 56]. In our study, no significant change was detected in TBARS levels; however, whereas an elevation in ROS levels was observed, reflecting the increased mitochondrial oxygen flux and metabolic demand in muscle tissue during repeated high-intensity efforts. Remarkably, a significant increase was noted in FRAP levels, indicating an enhancement in total antioxidant capacity. Moreover, while the modest increase in ROS levels is an expected consequence of heightened oxygen turnover during intense exercise, the concurrent rise in FRAP suggests that HIIT may stimulate an adaptive upregulation of endogenous antioxidant systems. This pattern aligns with previous findings indicating that chronic HIIT enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, attenuates lipid peroxidation, and improves overall redox homeostasis [57, 58]. The simultaneous elevation of ROS and FRAP further supports an acute hormetic response-consistent with the hormesis theory described by Ristow and Zarse and Radak et al., in which mild oxidative stress enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses [59, 60].

The lack of significant change in TBARS, a lipid peroxidation marker, indicates that lipid oxidative damage may not have increased despite elevated ROS, supporting the notion of an adaptive response rather than harmful oxidative stress. The post-HIIT pattern characterized by decreased AGEs, increased FRAP, elevated ROS, and unchanged TBARS suggests both adaptive redox signaling and a concurrent reduction in actual oxidative damage. Briefly, in the present study, the increased ROS levels were observed alongside increased antioxidant capacity, thereby unchanged TBARS levels were evident.

While acute ROS elevations are expected during early phases of HIIT, the consistent changes observed after 12 weeks suggest stable and beneficial chronic adaptations in redox homeostasis Although standardized clinical thresholds for oxidative stress biomarkers in adolescents are limited, the magnitude of change observed in our study (generally within the biologically relevant range of 10–20%) is indicative of improved oxidative balance in youth with obesity [61, 62].

When considered alongside previous findings, these results suggest that HIIT enhances the endogenous antioxidant system while preventing excessive oxidative damage, thereby contributing to healthier redox physiology during adolescence.

Limitations

One of the major limitations of this study is that, although participants were instructed not to change their diets, dietary intake was not directly monitored or reported. Research has demonstrated that energy balance and macronutrient distribution can significantly influence metabolic, inflammatory, and adipokine responses. For example, low-carbohydrate diets have been shown to reduce CRP and IL-6 levels and exert beneficial effects on inflammatory markers, and these improvements appear to be associated with weight loss; however, low-carbohydrate diets are not more effective than low-fat diets in reducing TNF-α [63]. Furthermore, evidence from overweight and obese individuals indicates that combining exercise with caloric restriction is more effective than caloric restriction alone in reducing inflammatory cytokines and CRP [64]. In addition, scientific evidence shows that normocaloric, carbohydrate-moderate, low-glycemic-index, protein-balanced diets, rich in monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids, omega-3, and low in saturated fats, have favorable effects on inflammatory status [65]. Healthy fats (particularly omega-3), polyphenols, low-glycemic-index carbohydrates, and high dietary fiber contribute to increases in adiponectin levels [66], and various dietary patterns, energy restriction strategies, and specific nutrients have been shown to significantly modulate adipokine concentrations. Therefore, the lack of dietary monitoring in our study may represent a potential confounding factor influencing metabolic and inflammatory outcomes. Future studies would benefit from incorporating at least basic dietary recall methods or food frequency questionnaires to better isolate the independent effects of HIIT on metabolic outcomes.

Moreover, the relatively small sample size and the restriction to adolescents with obesity may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the absence of a long-term follow-up period makes it unclear whether the observed improvements can be sustained over time. Only selected inflammatory and oxidative stress markers were assessed, and other potentially important biomarkers were not examined. Lastly, pubertal status was not assessed in this study, which should be considered a limitation. Pubertal maturation involves hormonal and physiological changes that may differ between male and female adolescents and could have influenced the observed sex-specific findings. Future studies including standardized assessments of pubertal development may help clarify these effects.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a 12-week high-intensity interval training (HIIT) intervention in adolescents with obesity produced significant improvements in body composition (reductions in waist circumference and waist-hip ratio; BMI and hip circumference decreased via group × time interactions; increases in fat-free mass, muscle mass, and total body water), metabolic function (enhanced glucose regulation, insulin sensitivity, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, lipid profile, and adiponectin levels), systemic inflammation (decreased CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6), and oxidative stress balance (improved AGEs, ROS, TBARS, and FRAP), accompanied by enhanced antioxidant capacity. These effects were observed independently of dietary interventions, highlighting the training-specific impact of HIIT. The study’s strengths include the structured chronic HIIT protocol with pre-adaptation, comprehensive assessment of multiple health markers, and consideration of sex-specific responses. Importantly, these adaptations may contribute to healthy growth and developmental processes during adolescence. HIIT therefore represents a time-efficient, practical, and effective strategy to improve metabolic health, mitigate obesity-related low-grade inflammation, and bolster antioxidant defenses in youth. Future research should employ larger and more diverse cohorts, extended follow-up periods, integrated lifestyle interventions, and mechanistic investigations to elucidate the molecular pathways and age- and sex-specific responses underlying HIIT’s effects.
