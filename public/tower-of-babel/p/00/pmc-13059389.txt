The yield of computed tomography scan in the emergency department, tertiary care center, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Zyad E. Raffah, Maan Jamjoom, Wed Mazin Ismail, Bsaim Abdulsalam Altirkistani, Noura Ehab Aljehani, Lama H. Alsubhi, Mohammed Ismail Turkestani, Ammar Mohammad Alhindi, Alaa Althubaiti, Abduljalil Adnan Alsafi, Nesreen H. Abourokbah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCT is widely used in emergency departments, but overuse and variable diagnostic yield remain concerns. This study assessed CT yield and factors associated with scan outcomes.MethodsA retrospective review of emergency department patients who underwent CT imaging between July and December 2023 was conducted using electronic health record data. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with CT scan results.ResultsA total of 1380 patients were included (mean age: 50.9 years; 57% male). Positive CT findings were identified in 43.1% of scans, most commonly fractures (16.1%), hematomas (7.6%), and renal calculi (7.2%). Patients with positive CT results were more likely to be hospitalized than those with negative findings (59% vs. 31.5%). In the adjusted analysis, CT scan timing was the only factor independently associated with scan outcomes (p < 0.001). Scans performed during the daytime and early morning hours were more likely to yield positive findings than those performed in the evening.ConclusionCT imaging demonstrates a substantial diagnostic yield in the emergency department. Based on these findings, departments should consider reviewing CT ordering protocols, providing targeted education on optimal scan timing, and monitoring utilization trends to optimize diagnostic outcomes and resource allocation.

Background

Computed Tomography (CT) is a non-invasive imaging modality that provides crucial information about internal organs [1]. Therefore, the use of CT has been increasing dramatically, particularly in the Emergency Department (ED), for its ability to provide rapid assessment and robust diagnostic evaluation [1–4]. Moreover, CT plays a vital role in the fast management of ED patients and assists ED physicians in determining the most appropriate treatment options, particularly during challenging times when the diagnosis is uncertain or the condition is life-threatening [3, 4]. However, this increase does not occur without additional negative impacts on CT and patients. These include overuse of the machine, time management and constraints, and unnecessary radiation exposure. For every 100 patients seen in the ED in 2019, an estimated 27 CT scans were ordered, compared with 18 in 2006 [5]. According to previous studies conducted over 5 years, ED physicians have increased the number of ordered or performed CT scans by 200–400%, including both adult and pediatric populations [6, 7]. Many factors contribute to the overuse of CT scans in emergency settings, some of which are physician-oriented. These include inadequate information about radiation dose and its potential risks, failure to adhere to international guidelines, and insufficient knowledge of the patient’s full history [8]. Additional causes involve avoiding malpractice lawsuits due to incorrect or missed diagnoses, especially in trauma patients, which, in a study, accounted for 57% of all CT scans in the ED. This is attributed to physicians’ uncertainty about the cause of injury in poly-trauma patients, along with mandatory scans to rule out trauma as a cause of loss of consciousness in intoxicated patients [3]. Some studies also suggest that physician shortages and ER jams could lead to over-utilization of CT scans [9]. In a systematic review, cases of excess CT utilization are labeled as “overuse” because of the insignificant yield of new information, delays in necessary interventions and management, excess radiation that harms patients, and increased healthcare costs [9]. However, in some cases, negative CT findings that rule out the need for expensive surgery may be cost-saving [3]. Although improper utilization of resources and time may lead to blocked ED access and prolonged waiting times, guidelines for practical imaging should not compromise diagnostic accuracy or patient safety [10]. In a study in which 36% of total hospital CT scans were ordered from the ED, the overall positive yield was 53.8%, with the majority of patients aged 60–80 years. Moreover, trauma patients, mostly males, had higher ratios of CT orders (head CTs, specifically). Another study conducted in Malaysia recorded a positive yield of 70.5% [3]. On the other hand, a study revealed that only 26% of total emergency CT scans were abnormal, of which 74% were unnecessary [8]. Despite multiple international studies addressing similar topics, these studies lack data regarding the CT yield rate. Many studies include trauma patients only, take place in tertiary centers, or address physicians’ ordering habits. Therefore, these results cannot be generalized or objectified. Some studies do not account for patients’ symptoms or acuity of presentation, which is an important factor that influences physicians’ decisions to order a CT. Our study aims to assess the yield and outcomes of all CT scans in the emergency department at KAMC-Jeddah. Given the wide range of results, standards need to be set to enhance protocol implementation and reduce the overuse of CT.

Materials and methods

Study settings and participants

This retrospective study was conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City in Jeddah, focusing on CT scans ordered from the emergency department between July and December 2023. According to the Raosoft calculator, the minimum sample size required to represent the selected population was 371. Therefore, taking into account a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval, the final sample size was set at 1380. The study employed a consecutive sampling technique, including all CT scans ordered from the emergency department and reported by the radiology department during July-December 2023. CT scans ordered by physicians in non-emergency departments and those of pediatric patients under age 14 were excluded.

Data collection process. Data were extracted from the Best Care System and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for aggregation. Variables included age group, gender, presenting symptoms, comorbidities, and patient outcomes. CT scans ordered by other physicians rather than the ER physician were excluded.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations (SDs) and assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t-test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically Significant. Multivariable regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with CT scan outcomes, with results reported as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. All statistical tests were two-sided, and analyses were conducted using JMP® Pro version 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

CT scan results and findings

Among the 1380 cases, 595 (43.1%) had positive CT scan results, while 785 (56.9%) had negative results. The CT reports showed a variety of abnormalities, with fractures the most prevalent (16.1%). The next most common findings were hematomas (7.6%), renal calculi (7.2%), and hemorrhages (6.1%). Ureteric stones, appendicitis, sinus abnormalities, strokes, and tumors occurred in approximately 4–5% of cases. Bowel obstruction (3.9%), hernias (3.5%), pulmonary embolism (1.8%), pericardial effusion (1.7%), pleural effusion (1.2%), and brain lesion (1.2%) were among the less common findings. The least common conditions were hydrocephalus, pulmonary nodules, cholelithiasis, and pneumonia, each occurring in fewer than 1% of cases. Trauma versus non-trauma presentations

Most patients presented with non-traumatic complaints (n = 991, 71.8%), while trauma-related presentations accounted for 389 cases (28.2%). Among patients with non-traumatic presentations, 421 (42.5%) had positive CT scan findings and 570 (57.5%) had negative findings. In the trauma group, 174 patients (44.7%) had positive CT findings, while 215 (55.3%) had negative results. There was no statistically significant difference in CT scan outcomes between trauma and non-trauma presentations (p = 0.44).

CT scan timing and multivariable analysis

A significant association between CT scan timing and CT results (Fig. 1) was observed. Among those who had a CT scan from 4:00 pm to 11:59 pm, 63.6% had negative results, compared with 53.8% and 52.2% for those scanned from 8:00 AM to 3:59 PM and 12:00 AM to 7:59 AM, respectively. In the multivariable nominal logistic regression model, CT scan timing was the only variable independently associated with CT results (likelihood ratio χ2 = 15.17, p < 0.001). Compared with scans performed between 4:00 PM and 11:59 PM, CT scans Scans conducted between 8:00 AM and 3:59 PM were associated with higher odds of positive findings (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.16–1.97, p = 0.002), as were scans performed between 12:00 AM and 7:59 AM (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.23–2.06, p < 0.001).Fig. 1Association between the time of the CT scan and the CT scan results

Discussion

This study assesses the use of CT imaging as a diagnostic modality in emergency settings, its yield rate, and the subsequent clinical outcomes, with consideration of patients’ demographic data, clinical presentation, and timing of imaging. In our study, male participants constituted 57% of the total study sample, indicating a slight male predominance. This aligns with previous reports by Swartzberg K and Goldstein LN, in which 64.3% of participants were male, and with Khosravi et al.’s results, in which male patients accounted for 64% of participants [3, 8]. The predominance of male patients visiting emergency settings may be associated with the prevalence of motor vehicle accidents, traumatic injuries, and occupational hazards among males, as they are more likely to be involved in hazardous activities and exertional work [11]. Past studies have also shown that men are more likely to seek medical care in emergencies, whereas females tend to defer medical treatment because of societal norms and influence [12]. Furthermore, stroke, a common presentation in the emergency department, is reported to be higher in males than in females [13].

Comparatively, as in Swartzberg and Goldstein’s study [3], the head is reported to be the most frequently scanned anatomical region, accounting for more than half of the total number of CT scans performed in the ER. These results align with universal statistics showing that head CT is the investigation of choice for the appraisal of acute conditions presenting to the ED, such as traumatic head injuries, seizures, stroke, and confusion [14]. The significant rise in the use of CT for cranial imaging by ER physicians demonstrates high reliance on CT as a diagnostic modality and discloses the profound nature and severity of cranial abnormalities. The reliance on CT imaging in the management of head injuries may also reflect the need for immediate intervention to treat life-threatening conditions, including intracranial hemorrhages [15]. Following head CT, abdominal and pelvic CT scans accounted for 20.9% and 20.6%, respectively. The high volume of abdominal and pelvic imaging performed in emergency settings suggests the possibility of underlying critical conditions in patients presenting with general symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. For example, traumatic abdominal injuries, appendicitis, and perforation are some abdominopelvic emergencies that necessitate rapid medical intervention. Nevertheless, the occurrence of these conditions does not justify the overuse of CT scans in emergency departments, as physician expertise and prophylactic precautions are sufficient to reach an accurate diagnosis and manage such patients. The prevalence of presentations involving these anatomical regions in EDs may also suggest inadequate coverage in outpatient settings [16].

Only 43.1% of CT scans performed in emergency settings revealed abnormal findings, most of which were classified as “other” (42.2%). This suggests a wide range of symptoms presenting to the ED. Fractures were found in 16.1% of CT scans, followed by renal calculi, which accounted for 7.2% of scans. This indicates that fractures and renal calculi are the most common reasons for ER visits. Similarly, traumatic insult and acute renal colic were among the most common causes of ER visits, as reported in another study [17]. In addition to fractures, hematomas (7.6%) and hemorrhages (6.1%) were also frequently reported among patients in the ED. These findings are common in trauma patients, indicating that trauma cases constitute 28.2% of scans, in which only 29.2% were positive. Unlike a previous study in which the majority of negative findings were recorded in non-trauma patients [3], 72.6% of negative findings in our study were non-traumatic. These results may indicate potential misuse of CT scans due to physician overestimation of non-critical conditions presenting to the ER that can be approached conservatively [4]. Poor clinical assessment skills and misjudgment of the probability of a serious underlying abnormality that requires detailed imaging could contribute to the high percentage of negative CT scans in non-trauma cases. To prevent such causes of CT overuse in emergency settings, it is recommended to follow evidence-based clinical guidelines to eliminate unnecessary exposure to radiation, minimize resource overuse, and ensure precise evaluation of patients for better patient outcomes. Examples of these guidelines include the Ottawa Ankle Rules for fractures and the Canadian CT Head Rule for head trauma [18]. The variability of CT scan results between trauma and non-trauma ER presentations may suggest differences in the methods physicians use to approach patients and in their choice of investigation. It may also be affected by patient factors such as age, concurrent chronic conditions and presenting complaints [19].

The association between CT scan findings and patient outcome was statistically significant (p-value < 0.001). Among hospitalized patients, 59% had abnormal CT scan findings, whereas most discharged patients had negative scans. Hospitalized patients were most often admitted for fractures (17.9%) and appendicitis (6.3%). This underscores the essential role of CT imaging in recognizing life-threatening emergencies that require immediate surgical management, such as appendectomy and open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) [20]. Among the 9 deceased patients, only one CT scan was negative. This indicates the validity of CT scan as a diagnostic modality in patients with severe diseases/conditions to administer the appropriate treatment and prevent further deterioration and mortality [21]. Given that negative findings were mostly recorded among discharged patients, this suggests that CT imaging is advisable to rule out critical underlying conditions. Moreover, negative CT scans may comfort patients and prevent redundant hospital admissions, avoiding unnecessary medical expenditure [20]. Regardless of its significant role in detecting serious underlying pathologies, CT utilization should be monitored to avoid overuse of this modality in mild presentations to reduce avoidable radiation exposure. Therefore, the above findings, along with previous research, emphasize the effectiveness of CT scans in patient management, treatment, and outcome. For example, the use of CT imaging as a diagnostic modality in high-risk acute conditions, including bowel obstruction and stroke, is effective in narrowing the decision-making window, ensuring an appropriate management approach, and enhancing patient survival [15, 22].

Although headache is commonly associated with serious underlying conditions, such as subarachnoid hemorrhage and cerebral venous thrombosis [23], our study showed a significant association between headache and negative CT scan findings. This possibly indicates that ER physicians could be overestimating the role of CT scan in dealing with headache, similarly to dizziness, altered mental status, or vision complaints [24]. Regarding these findings, adherence to guidelines such as the Canadian Headache Society’s recommendations is highly recommended [25]. In a systematic review, the allocation of Multidisciplinary teams were proven to be the most effective method of reducing CT scans in the ER [10]. On the other hand, constipation and abdominal pain were significant findings and were commonly found in patients diagnosed with bowel obstruction, renal calculi, and appendicitis. A study showed that CT is highly sensitive and specific in diagnosing acute abdominal conditions [16].

A significant relationship was also established between CT timing and results. Negative CT scans were most common among patients visiting the ER between 4:00 and 11:59 PM. Positive CT scans were most common between 12:00 and 7:59 AM. A study revealed that lack of nighttime sleep could affect physicians’ decision-making, as physicians on evening and night shifts often lack sleep. Thus, they are more prone to take inaccurate actions and have a higher risk of committing medical errors [26]. Moreover, patients presenting to the ER between 12:00 and 7:59 AM are more likely to have serious trauma presentations; therefore, this period had mostly positive CT scans [27].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As this is a single-center study, its results may lack generalizability compared with multicenter studies. Additionally, being a trauma center mandates the use of standardized trauma protocols that include whole-body CT imaging to rapidly assess patients and minimize the risk of missed injuries. This may have contributed to a higher proportion of negative CT scans. It could also have influenced observed diagnostic Yield and patient outcomes, potentially limiting the precision of yield estimates. Additionally, the retrospective design relies on the accuracy of documentation in electronic health records, which may not fully capture clinical decision-making factors that influenced imaging requests. Additionally, the potential misclassification of interpreted CT scans and the lack of correlation with the final diagnosis should be considered areas for improvement in future studies to achieve more accurate results. Future multicenter, prospective studies over longer time frames are warranted to improve generalizability and better define optimal CT utilization strategies in emergency settings. Moreover, an assessment of clinical appropriateness may be included.

Study strengths

This study discusses an important topic that may contribute to future improvements in CT utilization in critical settings, thereby reducing the overuse of CT. It also included a wide range of data that can be used to conduct future in-depth studies. The large sample size used in this study aids in producing more generalized results. Finally, being a trauma center means accepting a wide range of cases, therefore the study results are not limited to a specific population or background.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into CT scan utilization and diagnostic yield in the emergency department. Less than half of CT scans showed clinically significant findings, with head CT the most frequently performed examination. Fractures, hematomas, and renal calculi were the most commonly identified abnormalities. Positive CT scan results were significantly associated with patient disposition, particularly hospital admission. Variations in CT scan positivity by imaging time were also observed, suggesting that scan timing may influence diagnostic yield. These findings underscore the importance of targeted, symptom-driven CT use to enhance diagnostic efficiency while reducing unnecessary imaging in emergency care.
