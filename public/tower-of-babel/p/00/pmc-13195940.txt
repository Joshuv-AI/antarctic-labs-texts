Evolving determinants of implementing low-risk childbirth quality improvement in Japan: a longitudinal qualitative study
Authors: Mai Takeshita, Kayo Ueda, Ayako Kohno, Taichi Hatta, Yoshimitsu Takahashi, Takeo Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternational evidence suggests that reducing user costs increases maternity care utilization but not necessarily care quality, underscoring the need for transparent, comparable measurement. In Japan, evidence-based maternity care for low-risk childbirth has not been systematically evaluated, and limited claims-data capture hinders assessment, benchmarking, and sustained quality improvement. We aimed to explore how determinants (facilitators and barriers) of implementing a multifaceted quality improvement intervention for low-risk childbirth emerge and evolve over time.MethodsThis longitudinal qualitative study was performed at four facilities in the Kinki region of Japan using a process-evaluation approach. The intervention included quarterly performance-based audits and feedback to healthcare providers caring for low-risk women, multidisciplinary team-based quality improvement efforts, and educational sessions addressing care quality and organizational culture. Healthcare providers involved with low-risk childbirth participated in semi-structured interviews at the beginning and end of the intervention. Data were analyzed using directed content analysis, following the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), to identify facilitators and barriers of the intervention, observe changes over time, and compare variations across facilities. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University (R2342 and R2344), and all interview participants provided written informed consent.ResultsAcross 66 semi-structured interviews with 41 participants, four CFIR constructs—Champions, Key Stakeholders, Structural Characteristics, and Networks and Communications—transitioned from serving as facilitators to barriers during the intervention. In contrast, the Knowledge and Beliefs about the Intervention construct remained stable within each facility over time, although its valence differed across facilities. Facilities where Champions exhibited participatory leadership and effectively disseminated the intervention’s positive value experienced greater stakeholder engagement and smoother implementation. In contrast, in facilities where the intervention was perceived as having low value, various context-driven factors hindered implementation.ConclusionsThe role-related CFIR constructs, such as Champions and Key Stakeholders, exhibited more dynamic change over time, whereas perceptions of the intervention’s value remained relatively constant. Consistently positive perceptions of value facilitated sustained participation despite the presence of additional barriers. These findings highlight temporal fluctuations in implementation determinants and underscore the importance of supporting key roles and offering phase-specific, contextually responsive implementation support over time.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14502-5.Keywords: Implementation science, Health care quality assurance, Process evaluation, Qualitative research, Multiple-case study, Low-risk childbirth, Quality improvement

Background

Implementation of evidence-based maternity care for low-risk childbirth varies across settings, and sustaining consistent practice remains a significant challenge. International studies have shown that reducing user costs for maternity care can increase service utilization; however, such measures alone do not necessarily lead to improvements in care quality or outcomes [1]. Similarly, the World Health Organization guidance emphasizes that expansions in health coverage are effective only when combined with clear frameworks for quality improvement [2]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of making care quality visible and assessing it in ways that are transparent and comparable across facilities. In Japan, the extent to which evidence-based maternity care is delivered during low-risk childbirth has not been systematically evaluated, and quality improvement efforts differ across facilities. Although comprehensive evidence-based guidance for perinatal care is available, adoption of recommended practices and related quality improvement activities remains inconsistent in routine care [3]. A key contextual factor is that low-risk childbirth has historically not been covered by the universal health insurance system. Therefore, data on low-risk childbirth cases have not been consistently captured in claims-based administrative databases, making objective assessment of care quality difficult. These constraints make it difficult to monitor, benchmark, and standardize care quality for low-risk childbirth and to translate quality measurement into sustained quality improvement. In this context, the planned expansion of coverage under the universal health insurance system to include low-risk childbirth from fiscal year 2026 represents an important turning point [4]. Going forward, establishing transparent and comparable systems for measuring care quality and standardized approaches to evaluating and improving care across facilities will become increasingly important.

Summative evaluation, which focuses solely on intervention outcomes, cannot fully explain why implementation succeeds in some settings yet weakens or erodes in others [5, 6]. Process evaluation addresses this gap by examining how and why implementation varies across settings and identifying determinants, contextual influences, and adaptations that shape its delivery and sustainment [5, 6]. Because these determinants may evolve over time and differ across settings, longitudinal process evaluations that incorporate a temporal dimension are particularly valuable; however, such approaches remain limited in practice [7, 8]. In this study, we used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to organize determinants and compare how they changed across facilities and over time [9]. Additionally, we drew on Clinical Performance Feedback Intervention Theory (CP-FIT) to interpret mechanisms through which audit and feedback may influence implementation [10].

Accordingly, an urgent need exists to generate evidence supporting the standardization of care quality for low-risk childbirth. To fulfill this need, we developed a multifaceted quality improvement intervention using quality indicators (QI; plural QIs) for low-risk childbirth in Japan, incorporating audit and feedback components [11–14]. While the intervention was designed to strengthen evidence-based intrapartum care, the mechanism of implementation, alongside the evolution of determinants influencing implementation over time and across facilities, remains insufficiently understood.

The overall aim of the current longitudinal qualitative process evaluation is to explore how determinants (facilitators and barriers) of implementation emerge and evolve throughout the multifaceted quality improvement intervention. The primary study objectives are to: (1) identify key determinants influencing implementation; (2) examine how and why these determinants shift between the early and post-intervention phases; and (3) compare similarities and differences among facilities to identify context-specific factors shaping the intervention’s implementation for low-risk childbirth.

Methods

Study design

This longitudinal qualitative study was conducted as a process evaluation for a quality improvement intervention targeting low-risk childbirth. A longitudinal design was selected in anticipation of evolving implementation determinants—both facilitators and barriers—as teams gain experience with the intervention, participate in successive audit-and-feedback cycles, and refine their local quality improvement activities. This design was grounded in implementation theory: CFIR highlights that determinants may be transient and vary across implementation phases, and CP-FIT conceptualizes audit and feedback as a cyclical process in which responses to feedback and local adaptations may change over successive cycles [9, 10]. To effectively capture changes and adaptations throughout the implementation period, semi-structured interviews were conducted during the early and post-intervention phases in the same facilities and with the same participants, thereby facilitating comparative analysis over time. This study was designed as a pre-scale-up implementation in four motivated facilities to generate implementation learning—practical insights to refine the intervention and its delivery and to inform the evaluation approach prior to wider scale-up. It also provides groundwork for future large-scale evaluations of the effectiveness and implementation of quality improvement interventions for low-risk childbirth, particularly in anticipation of the forthcoming integration of low-risk childbirth care into Japan’s health insurance system and the associated need to strengthen quality measurement and improvement on a broader scale.

Components of the intervention

The multifaceted intervention included (1) audits and feedback using QIs developed in Japan for low-risk childbirth, (2) quality improvement activities led by multidisciplinary teams, and (3) training sessions focused on healthcare quality and organizational culture. Healthcare providers caring for women with low-risk pregnancies and childbirth entered relevant information into the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system [15] alongside routine medical documentation. The research team aggregated the QI data, generated facility-specific reports, and delivered structured feedback to healthcare providers. Additionally, a goal-setting form was distributed to encourage reflection on QI outcomes and support action planning; this form was completed REDCap system at the unit level. After each feedback report, units formulated action plans, and the progress of these activities was reviewed at the conclusion of each quarterly audit-and-feedback cycle.

The effectiveness of the intervention was assessed using an interrupted time series analysis of 12 QIs [14]. This analysis quantified changes in performance on these indicators associated with the intervention over time. The current qualitative process evaluation complements this effectiveness evaluation by examining how the intervention was implemented in practice and identifying determinants that may help explain variation across facilities and phases. To evaluate implementation, contextual facilitators and barriers were explored through interviews and assessment of implementation outcomes. Interview data were organized by facility (considered as individual cases) and compared across cases to identify commonalities and differences in contextual factors [16].

Theoretical approach

The CFIR [9] and CP-FIT [10] were employed as theoretical frameworks. The CFIR is a comprehensive framework designed to systematically assess implementation determinants during the introduction of new interventions or programs in healthcare and public health settings. It comprises five domains—Intervention Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Process—with 39 associated constructs. The CFIR encompasses intervention design, pre-implementation planning, active implementation, and post-implementation evaluation, making it comprehensive and flexible. CFIR guided this study’s intervention development, the creation of interview guides for both the early and post-intervention phases, and data coding and analysis. The CP-FIT, developed by synthesizing 30 pre-existing theories of audits and feedback, conceptualizes effective feedback as a continuous, cyclical process. Accordingly, it guided the development and interpretation of the intervention. By combining the CFIR and CP-FIT, the intervention was designed and evaluated in a theoretically informed manner.

Setting and interview of participants

The intervention was implemented in four obstetric care facilities in Japan’s Kinki region, each handling approximately 100–1,000 deliveries annually. The facilities were selected using purposive sampling based on feasibility and willingness to implement the quality improvement intervention and participate throughout the study period. The four participating facilities were referred to as Units 1–4 throughout the manuscript. Access to participating units was obtained through different routes. Units 1 and 2 had an established collaboration with the second author (KU) since the development phase of QIs for low-risk childbirth [11–13] and continued to support the present research, with the midwife manager serving as the primary contact person for coordination within each unit. Unit 3 was approached through an introduction to the unit director by an acquaintance of KU who previously worked in the unit. Unit 4 had no prior relationship with the research team; MT and KU introduced the intervention to the midwife manager, and the unit agreed to participate.

To ensure facility-specific focus while enabling cross-facility comparison of implementation processes, the selection of interview participants focused on those with experience representative of typical implementation experiences at their respective facilities. Using purposive, typical-case sampling, individuals directly involved in the intervention and positioned to articulate the details of quality improvement activities were selected. Both managerial staff overseeing the intervention (e.g., head midwives) and frontline healthcare providers caring for low-risk women were included to ensure a diverse range of perspectives [17].

Sample size determination prioritized conceptual coverage of the theoretically important CFIR constructs. Qualitative sample sizes are often justified by saturation or practical considerations; however, theory-based analyses emphasize adequate representation of core constructs [18]. Accordingly, approximately 8–10 participants were sampled per facility to ensure coverage across the CFIR constructs and roles. To capture the temporal dynamics of facility-specific contextual factors, two interviews with the same participant were conducted during early and post-intervention stages whenever possible. If original participants were unavailable due to factors such as maternity leave, transfers, or resignations, replacements occupying equivalent positions were interviewed.

Data collection

Semi-structured interview guides for early and post-intervention phases were developed based on the CFIR. In accordance with recommendations from the CFIR authors to limit the number of constructs assessed in a single study [9], 21 constructs were selected for early intervention interviews and 22 for post-intervention interviews through team discussion and pilot interviews.

These constructs were identified as most relevant to the study’s objectives (Additional file 1). Given the staggered initiation of the intervention across four facilities, interview time points were defined relative to each facility’s start date. Early intervention interviews were conducted at month 5 (January–August 2021), while post-intervention interviews occurred at month 14 (October 2021–May 2022) across all sites. At each interview time point, facility-specific audit-and-feedback results were shared with staff, allowing one month for review and confirmation before conducting interviews during the subsequent month (i.e., months 5 and 14).

Interviews were conducted either in person or online, according to participant preference. Regardless of modality, interviews were conducted in a private setting to protect privacy (e.g., an available consultation room or patient room for in-person interviews and a private space for online participation). All interviews were conducted in Japanese by the lead author (MT), a clinically experienced midwife trained in qualitative research methods and semi-structured interviewing through graduate coursework and workshops. Prior to the main data collection, MT conducted pilot interviews to further refine the interview technique and procedures. To minimize interviewer bias, MT used a standardized semi-structured interview guide (Additional file 1) that included prespecified probing questions and neutral prompts while avoiding leading questions. The guide was refined through pilot interviews and team discussions. Interviews were conducted reflexively, with attention to maintaining neutral phrasing and minimizing undue influence on participants’ responses.

Each interview was scheduled for approximately 1 h, with adjustments made as necessary to accommodate participant availability. Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, using a digital voice recorder for in-person interviews and Zoom’s recording function for online sessions. All recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim in Japanese by MT, and data analysis was conducted using these transcripts. Field notes were recorded immediately after each interview to capture key contextual details, a brief non-verbatim summary of the discussion (including salient statements), and initial reflections of the interviewer.

Data analysis

A directed content analysis was performed using the CFIR as the guiding theoretical framework [19]. This systematic, theory-informed approach facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of implementation factors and enabled assessment of how empirical data aligned with established theoretical constructs. Additionally, we identified emerging elements not fully captured by the existing framework, thereby informing potential refinement or expansion of the CFIR. This methodology facilitated exploration of the dynamic changes in facilitators and barriers throughout implementation, as well as an appraisal of the CFIR’s explanatory capacity regarding these changes.

Initially, a holistic review of the data was conducted to gain an overall understanding. Subsequently, deductive coding was applied using 39 CFIR constructs as the preliminary coding framework. When new meaning units emerged from data that did not fit the existing constructs, supplementary codes were developed inductively and considered for integration into the established constructs. Two researchers (MT and KU) independently coded transcripts from four information-rich early intervention interviews and held group discussions to consolidate, revise, and refine the codebook, resolving discrepancies as needed. MT then coded the remaining transcripts using the finalized codebook. Each facility was analyzed as an independent case. For each CFIR construct, summary memos were created for early and post-intervention interviews, followed by comparative analyses across time points. To depict facility-level changes, matrix tables were constructed to indicate the evolution of each construct and facilitate cross-case comparisons. After coding all interviews and constructing facility-by-phase matrices for all identified CFIR constructs, we selected a subset of “key” constructs for in-depth reporting using data-driven criteria. Specifically, we prioritized constructs that (1) were salient and appeared likely to strongly facilitate or act as barriers to implementation, (2) were supported by recurrent and information-rich excerpts (i.e., repetition and volume of relevant narrative), (3) were observed across all facilities, and (4) showed meaningful temporal patterning (i.e., constructs that shifted across phases or remained stable within facilities while differing in valence across facilities). Candidate key constructs were identified by MT and KU through review of the matrices and supporting excerpts and were finalized through team discussion. We also consulted the CFIR’s published rating criteria to support transparent summarization of construct valence and strength (see Additional file 2). However, we did not rely on numeric ratings as the primary basis for reporting because they risk oversimplifying the qualitative evidence and obscuring facility-specific nuances (e.g., differences in the richness and content of excerpts that can yield the same score). Therefore, we used the facility-by-phase matrices and supporting excerpts as the primary evidence base for describing construct patterns and transitions, while using the rating criteria only as an interpretive aid. To summarize construct valence and change by facility and phase, constructs were coded as facilitators (F) or barriers (B), mixed (X) when facilitative and inhibiting evidence co-occurred without clear predominance, and neutral (0) when evidence was descriptive without clear directional influence; “+” (F+) indicates a facilitator that was strengthened in the post-intervention phase compared with the early phase. When data were lacking for a phase, the construct was coded as “missing (M)” and not interpreted further. For longitudinal interpretation, we examined overall directional patterns in the facility-by-phase matrices. “Declining implementation” was operationally inferred when the matrices indicated a shift over time toward less supportive implementation conditions (e.g., facilitators becoming weaker or changing to mixed evidence [X] or barriers becoming stronger), considering the five key constructs reported in the main text and the full set of constructs summarized in Additional file 3.

By employing a longitudinal qualitative design, we identified the evolution of facilitators and barriers using the CFIR and investigated their underlying causes [20]. After re-examining all transcripts, passages evidencing changes in facilitators or barriers were highlighted and coded according to the initial CFIR-based categories. Where original constructs proved too abstract to fully capture causal mechanisms, subcategories reflecting context-specific details were introduced. Interpretative differences during coding and interpretation were discussed and reconciled by the research team. The same procedural rigor was applied to post-intervention interviews. Although the CFIR offers a comprehensive implementation science framework, no entirely novel theoretical categories emerged; however, certain constructs demonstrated greater temporal variability than others. This observation may further refine the framework by emphasizing the variable sensitivity of individual constructs over time. For reporting, the findings were translated into English and reviewed by the research team to ensure conceptual equivalence and fidelity to the intended meaning.

To ensure the credibility of the qualitative findings, multiple strategies were implemented. Formal member checking was not conducted because it was not feasible to request additional input from participants given increased clinical workloads during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Instead, we employed several strategies to enhance trustworthiness. Researcher triangulation strengthened credibility by involving team members beyond those directly engaged in the intervention during coding and interpretation. Reflexivity was fostered by recording the authors’ perspectives and assumptions in reflective journals and discussing them with the team. Thick description was utilized to detail the study context, participant backgrounds, and analytical process, supporting transferability assessments. An audit trail was maintained to record analytic decisions, code revisions, and methodological notes, thereby reinforcing consistency and transparency throughout the research process. Field notes were not treated as primary analytic data; they were used to support reflexive interpretation and contextualize participants’ accounts during analysis.

Researcher position and reflexivity

The lead author, a clinically experienced midwife, has been engaged in perinatal care and quality improvement activities. The research team comprised experts in implementation science and qualitative methodology, each possessing substantial knowledge of both the intervention content and healthcare context. To address potential biases, the researchers engaged in critical reflection on their preconceptions about the intervention’s value, as well as on their interpretations of its facilitators and barriers. Bias was further minimized through systematic code cross-checking and collaborative team discussions during analysis.

Additionally, the study considered reflexivity by examining relationships between researchers and participants. One team member had previously worked as a clinical educator during student internships at one participating facility, establishing a prior professional relationship. This connection was transparently disclosed and discussed within the team to mitigate its influence on data interpretation. The researcher in question did not conduct interviews to preserve neutrality. MT conducted all interviews and had no prior relationship with any participants before this study. However, participants were aware that MT was a member of the research team involved in delivering the intervention. MT’s clinical background as a midwife was explained at the start of each interview. Other team members did not have prior relationships with participants, supporting transparency and reflexive interpretation during data collection and analysis.

Ethics

All participating facilities agreed to participate in the quality improvement initiative and confirmed they would not conduct competing studies during the implementation period. Comprehensive written information about the study was distributed to the midwives, nurses, and obstetricians at each facility, and explicit consent was obtained for participation in the intervention. Interviewees received verbal and written explanations of the study’s objectives and procedures, with written informed consent collected before participation. All data were anonymized and securely managed.

To mitigate potential power hierarchies within facilities, the voluntary nature of the interview was emphasized, clarifying that refusal or withdrawal would not carry any disadvantages. Interviews were conducted individually, without supervisors present, and participants were assured that responses would remain confidential and not be shared with managers or staff members.

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with Japan’s Ethical Guidelines for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. The Ethics Committee of Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine and Kyoto University Hospital approved the study (R2342 and R2344).

Reporting guidelines

This study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [21] and Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies [22].

Results

Overview of intervention completion and implementation status

All planned intervention components were delivered by the research team to all participating facilities, including four quarterly audit-and-feedback cycles. A total of 66 interviews with 41 participants were conducted: 37 during early intervention and 29 at post-intervention. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participating facilities. The participants included midwives and obstetricians (Table 2). The average interview duration was 43 min (46 min during early implementation and 40 min during post-intervention). Some participants who completed the early-phase interview did not participate in the post-intervention interview, and the reasons differed by unit (Table 2). In Unit 2, COVID-19–related organizational changes and increased workload limited availability for post-intervention interviews. In Unit 4, follow-up interviews with the same individuals were sometimes not feasible due to maternity/parental leave or transfers.

So I didn’t feel right pushing those who weren’t keen to do more. (Champion, post)

I regularly check what’s missing, and if something hasn’t been entered, I quietly input it myself. The staff are already swamped, and if I push them too hard, they’ll burn out. So I just try to handle things myself whenever I can. (Champion)

The head midwife never forced the intervention on the staff. But somehow, naturally, we were all moving in the same direction. It was kind of top-down, sure, but she explained why it was necessary, and I think that made a big difference. Not just saying ‘we’re doing this,’ but also explaining why it mattered, what kind of outcomes we could expect, and how it might change things for us. I think everyone was able to buy into it because of that. (Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders)

To be honest, I didn’t really understand the study. I didn’t actually do anything for it in practice. (Key Stakeholder)

Even when I tried reaching out to everyone, they didn’t seem to take it personally. It was hard to get their cooperation … I started to wonder if they just didn’t see it as their responsibility. (Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders)

There was a really strong sense of resistance to doing anything extra. People would immediately focus on how hard it was going to be, and I kept hearing them say no over and over. (U2, Champion)

If we’re going to take part, I want it to be something positive we can use. But honestly, people outside the implementation team just felt like it was being forced on them. I even heard—indirectly—that some staff members felt they couldn’t ask us to help with other work while we were entering data for the intervention. (U3, Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders)

When the head midwife said, ‘Looks like there are some missing entries,’ that kind of made us think, ‘Okay, we need to be careful not to forget.’ We also knew she really valued getting feedback from women, and that made us want to take it seriously. I remember thinking, ‘This matters to her, so I need to make sure I don’t forget.’ (Key Stakeholder)

Compared to the beginning, I could feel everyone becoming more aware over time. People started saying things like, ‘What about this person’s QI?’ or ‘Do you remember how it went with her?’ I think it became more embedded in everyone by the second half. (Key Stakeholder)

Every time a state of emergency was declared, we had to ban visitors and family attendance at birth, and explain these restrictions carefully to the women. It wasn’t just the outpatient department—we also had to coordinate with the ward and the infection control office. Non-obstetric patients also started being admitted to the ward, which made things busy in a different way than usual. (U1, Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders)

With every wave of COVID-19, things changed, and we were constantly being pushed around by those external shifts. (U2, Champion)

I got the feeling that some low-risk women were going to clinics instead, maybe because their families weren’t allowed to be there during childbirth. Actually, deliveries by low-risk women only made up about 13% of all births here. (U1, Champion)

Because of our role as a university hospital, we started asking low-risk women to go to nearby hospitals. We told them we could only take high-risk cases that needed care specifically at the university. So we saw a lot of high-risk patients and rarely handled low-risk births. (U2, Key Stakeholder)

Absolutely impossible. It was beyond my workload. I didn’t actually spend that much time on the intervention, but all I could see was how much extra burden the implementation team was carrying.

Champions

Champions were central figures who promoted intervention implementation. Although all Champions actively supported the implementation in the early phase of the intervention, the specific content of their activities varied slightly across the facilities.

In Unit 1, the Champion was the head midwife who oversaw the in-hospital midwifery system and the obstetric outpatient department. She made the decision to adopt the intervention, served as a liaison with the research team, disseminated information within the facility, selected the implementation team (comprising Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders), and regularly monitored their work progress. Furthermore, the Champion allocated duties to staff by appropriately matching roles and skills, including appointing individuals with excellent interpersonal skills to recruit low-risk women and assigning QI data entry tasks to trusted, respected staff with detail-oriented, meticulous personalities. The Champion also listened to the Key Stakeholders’ intervention-related concerns, provided reassurance, and motivated them to engage. She promoted implementation with a neutral attitude, serving as a champion.

I explained the intervention documents to the core staff, but they found the theme difficult and did not understand what they were supposed to do. Since it was something we had never done before, a lot of anxiety arose. I reassured them while reading through the materials together, encouraging them by saying, ‘You all will take the lead. (U1, Champion, early intervention)

Toward the end of the intervention, the Champion’s absence due to external training became a barrier, as it prevented their involvement.

In Unit 2, the Champion was the head midwife of the obstetric ward. She made the adoption decision, acted as a liaison with the research team, disseminated information internally, and monitored the activities of the implementation leaders. Furthermore, the Champion promoted implementation using top-down authority to address the concerns and resistance among key stakeholders.

I was the final decision-maker for adopting the intervention. The others—senior midwives who became implementation leaders—had been involved in previous QI development for in-hospital midwifery and birth centers, so they understood the research better than I did. Since I had previously worked in a different department, I did not fully understand how this study was supposed to proceed. I may have used some coercive pressure, thinking that we needed to cooperate because of the ongoing nature of the project. (U2 Champion, post-intervention)

As the intervention progressed, there was a decline in the Champion’s motivation due to the limited ability to improve care during the COVID-19 pandemic and the discouraging QI feedback.

In Unit 3, the Champion was an obstetrician and a facility administrator. She requested that Key Stakeholders cooperate with the intervention. The Organizational Leader (clinic director) decided to adopt the intervention and Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders communicated with researchers and entered QI data.

There wasn’t really anyone who strongly led or had great influence over the intervention… but if I had to say someone, I guess it would be me. (U3 Champion, early intervention)

As a director, the Champion was reluctant to require the entire staff’s participation due to concerns about increased workload. Although the obstetrician was recognized as a Champion during the early phase, implementation leaders stated that the obstetrician had effectively become a nominal champion as the intervention progressed.

In Unit 4, the Champion was the head midwife in the obstetric ward. She considered it necessary to improve the quality of care to increase the facility’s birth rate. The Champion made the adoption decision, coordinated with the research team, disseminated information internally, selected the implementation team, and monitored its progress. Unlike other champions, the Champion not only checked for missing entries but also entered unrecorded QI data, supporting the implementation team and key stakeholders. The Champion explained the intervention’s purpose to staff, shared QI feedback with other departments, and set facility-wide quality-improvement goals. The Champion maintained participatory leadership and consistently facilitated its implementation through active engagement (Table 4).

I shared the QI results with obstetricians and the clinical committee to highlight the efforts of our team. We also submitted a report to the head of the nursing department to describe what our staff was working on. We incorporated QI evaluations into our ward’s quality goals; therefore, instead of seeing it as a research project, we treated it as part of our annual quality improvement plan. (U4, Champion, post-intervention)

Key stakeholders

Key Stakeholders are individuals whose engagement is essential for the implementation process. In this study, Key Stakeholders included healthcare providers—midwives, nurses, and obstetricians—who directly cared for low-risk women, excluding Champions, Opinion Leaders, Organizational Leaders, and Formally Appointed Internal Implementation Leaders.

In Unit 1, the implementation team completed most of the tasks for the intervention. Although the Key Stakeholders provided care to low-risk women, they did not enter the data into the QI collection system. Their involvement was limited to distributing questionnaires to postpartum women to evaluate the care received.

I only give the questionnaire to the mothers before discharge and collect and store them in the designated place. (U1, Key Stakeholder, post-intervention)

Although they were aware of the intervention, their limited involvement led to a lack of stakeholder ownership, which contributed to decreased engagement.

In U2, the Key Stakeholders who provided care to low-risk women were responsible for entering the QI data into the system.

Everyone knows that if they’re involved in a low-risk birth, it’s their job to enter the QIs. (U2, Key Stakeholder, early intervention)

In contrast, in U3, individuals besides the Implementation Leaders were largely unengaged with minimal involvement in the intervention.

My stance toward the rest of the staff was: if someone wanted to handle the intervention-related tasks, they could go ahead and do it. That was the compromise we reached with the rest of the staff just to get the study implemented. (U3, Implementation Leader, early intervention)

In both facilities, incorporating quality improvement activities into routine duties led to resistance toward the intervention and decreased engagement.

In U4, Key Stakeholders entered the QI data into the system, and shift managers monitored the status of data collection.

The staff who attended the birth always entered the intrapartum QI data. Anything extra was done when we had the time. Each shift manager monitored the input status using a checklist we created. (U4, Key Stakeholder, post-intervention)

In this unit, the emergence and consolidation of stakeholder ownership occurred, as Key Stakeholders empathized with the Champion’s commitment to the intervention and provided encouragement, leading to improved engagement.

Structural characteristics

Structural Characteristics refer to the organizational and institutional frameworks of the implementation environment, including social structure, organizational tenure, maturity, and size.

In Units 1 and 2—tertiary care institutions—changes in structural characteristics became barriers to implementation. Although both facilities had in-hospital midwifery systems for low-risk childbirth, they focused on the care of high-risk pregnant women due to their institutional nature. During the COVID-19 pandemic, both units not only accepted patients with COVID-19 but also prioritized care for high-risk women, resulting in organizational restructuring induced by the COVID-19 pandemic (Table 4). In Unit 1, the midwifery system remained operational but was functionally weakened. In Unit 2, the midwifery space was closed and repurposed for COVID-19 care. Consequently, both units experienced a decrease in the number of visiting low-risk women, reducing opportunities to deliver care to the primary intervention target group.

In Unit 3, compared with other units, the limited familiarity with research practices in the facilities hindered implementation. This resulted from the institutional structure and the lack of an educational or research-oriented culture.

In contrast, in Unit 4, alignment between the facility’s main patient population and the intervention target group (alignment between the intervention and target population) facilitated implementation.

Networks and communications

Networks and Communication refer to the nature and quality of formal and informal information exchanges and interpersonal relationships within an organization.

In Unit 1, the lack of voluntary collaboration among Key Stakeholders hindered the implementation. In Unit 2, growing frustration toward the intervention emerged due to staff capacity constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic. In Unit 3, conflicts developed between the implementation team and Organizational Leaders, Opinion Leaders, and Key Stakeholders who resisted the intervention, leading to deterioration in interpersonal relationships.

I couldn’t talk openly with others about the research. So honestly, I don’t really know what they were thinking. (U3, Implementation Leader, post-intervention)

At the start of the implementation in Unit 3, there was no formal head midwife position, a rarity in healthcare facilities. Midwives with approximately 20 years of clinical experience unofficially performed the duties of head midwives, and a senior midwife served as the Opinion Leader. Midway through the intervention, the facility established formal leadership positions. One of the midwives who had unofficially carried out head midwife duties—and who had consistently expressed negative attitudes toward the intervention—assumed this formal leadership role. This shift affected the relationships and communication between the implementation team and clinical staff. The four midwives who comprised the implementation team had over 20 years of clinical experience but had only worked at the facility for 1–3 years, making them relatively new to the organizational culture.

Several factors led to the deterioration of networks and communications (Table 4): the uncooperative stance of Opinion Leaders, resistance to implementation driven by newcomers, and the perceived disruption to routine work among staff opposed to the intervention.

In contrast, in Unit 4, a shared sense of urgency emerged among Key Stakeholders following perceived implementation failures, leading to the emergence of peer-led collaboration that facilitated implementation.

Knowledge and Beliefs about the intervention

This construct refers to an individual’s level of understanding of an intervention and their perceptions of its effectiveness and value.

In Unit 1, although stakeholders considered the intervention a catalyst for care improvement, there were concerns regarding an inconsistent understanding between the Champions or implementation team and key stakeholders, which limited the program’s utilization. Consequently, this construct exhibited facilitative and inhibitory perceptions of the intervention’s value.

In Unit 2, the small number of eligible cases reduced the number of opportunities for application.

In Unit 3, there was a mismatch between clinical judgment and QI evaluation criteria. For example, even when providers intentionally omitted certain types of care due to patient-specific factors, the QI criteria still evaluated them as “not performed.” These limitations raised doubts about the value of the intervention in both units, thereby inhibiting its implementation.

In Unit 4, despite the perceived burden of the intervention, stakeholders consistently considered it a valuable opportunity to determine whether appropriate care was being provided and identify areas for improvement. This consistent positive perception of the value of the intervention served as a sustained facilitator throughout implementation.

These findings suggest that perceptions of the intervention appeared to be shaped early in the implementation period and showed limited change between the early and post-intervention interviews. Across units, there was minimal change in overall valence of this construct between phases, although it differed between facilities. Rather than being shaped primarily by prior information or education, these perceptions may have reflected internal factors such as facility-specific contextual conditions, past experiences, and pre-existing values and priorities.

Therefore, a lack of change in Knowledge and Beliefs about the Intervention should not be considered merely as a static or passive phenomenon. Instead, it may indicate a relatively stable orientation of either acceptance or resistance within each facility over the interview intervals in this study. Recognizing this relative stability provides valuable insights for identifying where targeted support should be directed in future implementations.

Facility-level patterns of sustained versus declining implementation

The cross-case analysis revealed two distinct facility-level patterns regarding implementation progress. Unit 4 maintained or strengthened implementation engagement throughout the intervention, whereas Units 1, 2, and 3 experienced varying degrees of decline in engagement and implementation activity (i.e., a shift toward less supportive implementation conditions in the facility-by-phase CFIR matrices). In the sustained implementation group (Unit 4), the Champion remained continuously engaged; Key Stakeholders developed a sense of shared responsibility; the intervention was incorporated into the facility’s quality improvement plan; and there was strong organizational alignment with the intervention’s purpose. These factors enabled the organization to maintain and institutionalize the implementation.

In contrast, the facilities with declining engagement (Units 1–3) experienced disruptions in leadership continuity or motivation, lack of ownership among Key Stakeholders, and contextual mismatches such as a low proportion of eligible cases or competing priorities. These factors gradually eroded engagement and weakened implementation capacity.

These findings suggest that successful and sustained implementation requires not only initial readiness but also continuous reinforcement of leadership, stakeholder ownership, and organizational alignment over time.

Discussion

This study evaluated the implementation of a quality improvement intervention for low-risk childbirth in four Japanese obstetric facilities. The temporal dimension of the analysis revealed that certain CFIR constructs shifted, acting as either facilitators or barriers at different times, while other remained relatively stable. The cross-case comparison revealed two distinct implementation patterns: Unit 4 maintained or strengthened implementation engagement throughout the intervention period, whereas Units 1, 2, and 3 showed varying degrees of decline. These differences suggest that perceived value may represent a foundational determinant, appearing to interact with contextual conditions across facilities in shaping implementation trajectories. Facility circumstances—such as shifts in service priorities during the pandemic, differences in eligible case volume, and changes in leadership continuity—unfolded alongside implementation, and we interpret perceived value as potentially shaping how these conditions were navigated in practice.

Perceived value of the intervention and healthcare provider engagement

The results indicated that the perceptions of Organizational Leaders, Champions, and Key Stakeholders may be important for advancing the intervention’s implementation. A previous study suggested that even when Organizational Leaders support the adoption of an intervention, implementation may fail if it conflicts with frontline staff’s values [23]. Therefore, successful implementation may be supported not only by top-level endorsement but also by shared understanding and recognition of the intervention’s value among Key Stakeholders [24]. For example, a study on the implementation of the Maternal Newborn Dashboard in Ontario, Canada, reported higher success rates among facilities where the intervention aligned with organizational priorities and teams understood its value and were actively engaged in its implementation [25, 26]. Similarly, in Unit 4, Organizational Leaders and Key Stakeholders consistently acknowledged the value of the intervention. Implementation progressed steadily alongside other barriers.

Meanwhile, in Units 1 and 2, the emergence of COVID-19 pandemic-related competing demands led tertiary care facilities to prioritize high-risk care, which may have contributed to a perceived decrease in the importance of low-risk care and reduced engagement with the intervention. In these settings, healthcare providers were unable to provide the level of care they considered desirable, as demonstrated in feedback from the QIs. Particularly in Unit 2, this sense of compromised care was described alongside feelings of powerlessness and a decline in self-efficacy, consistent with previous findings on the psychological impact of the pandemic on healthcare providers [27].

In our study, although COVID-19 represented an extraordinary contextual disruption, the mechanism observed may be relevant beyond pandemic conditions. Fluctuating service demands and organizational re-prioritization (e.g., staffing shortages, surges in clinical volume, or other system pressures) may reduce opportunities to implement the intervention, undermine the perceived credibility of feedback, and weaken providers’ self-efficacy and sense of belonging. Accordingly, future scale-ups and evaluations should incorporate strategies to safeguard audit-and-feedback cycles and sustain the engagement of key stakeholders under changing service pressures. Potential approaches include simplifying feedback, protecting time for review and discussion, and ensuring continuity of key roles (e.g., deputy Champions or shared leadership).

In addition, some providers believed that the QIs used in the intervention did not reflect their actual clinical practice. This misalignment between clinical judgment and the intervention’s content emerged as a barrier to its implementation. Notably, the QIs in this study were developed through a consensus among researchers, healthcare providers, and women and were intended to visualize care quality. Nevertheless, the interviews revealed that some providers believed that the most meaningful aspects of their care were not captured by the QIs. This finding is consistent with prior research indicating that feedback is most effective when it aligns with providers’ expertise and values [10, 28, 29].

Attributes of champions and influence of organizational characteristics

The presence and attributes of Champions appeared to be important for successful implementation of the intervention. In Unit 4, the Champion had a strong physical presence in the setting and was internally motivated to improve care quality for increased birth volume (ownership), make the decision to adopt the intervention (influence), and build a shared vision with formally appointed internal implementation leaders and key stakeholders (credibility). In addition, the Champion adopted a participatory leadership style by personally entering the QI data. The Champion embodied many of the qualities considered important for the successful implementation of the intervention and was described by participants as a driving force throughout the implementation period [30].

However, in other units, the role of the Champion became symbolic or temporarily absent, which may have constrained implementation. Sustained engagement with the Champion is essential to facilitate implementation. During the pre-intervention phase, the research team explained this role to healthcare providers at each facility and asked them to identify an appropriate individual for the role. Eventually, in all units except Unit 3, the head midwife assumed the role. When the Champion was the head midwife, their physical presence in the clinical setting, combined with a deep understanding of care practices and the authority to allocate resources, may have served as a strong facilitator of the intervention. However, in Japanese clinical settings, head midwives are often reassigned to or required to attend offsite training, which makes consistent engagement difficult. Regarding the sustainability of the intervention, organizational support—such as succession planning and the development of future champions—is essential to ensuring the continued and substantive engagement of champions in the implementation process [31, 32].

In addition, organizational characteristics affected receptivity to interventions and stakeholder engagement. Providers in academic or university-affiliated hospitals, where educational and research activities are routine, were more receptive of the intervention. Meanwhile, providers in clinics demonstrated limited familiarity with research activities, which may have contributed to perceptions of the intervention as burdensome or lacking personal and professional relevance.

Thus, ensuring the quality and continuity of Champions and providing context-specific support structures tailored to each institution are likely to support the more stable and sustainable implementation of quality improvement interventions.

Building relationships and addressing resistance in implementation

Conflicts arose between individuals promoting the intervention, such as the implementation team or researchers, and those resisting it, such as Organizational Leaders, Opinion Leaders, or Key Stakeholders. These conflicts may pose major challenges to the sustainability of the intervention. In Unit 3, discrepancies in the perceived value of the intervention between the research or clinical implementation team and Opinion Leaders were associated with strained relationships and deteriorated communication. This aligns with earlier findings suggesting that such conflict is more likely to emerge when an intervention is not recognized as an organizational priority or its value is not shared across roles [33, 34].

From the perspective of community-based participatory research, research teams may need to identify and co-construct the value of a program with Organizational Leaders and foster communicative relationships that can improve the relative priority of the intervention within an organization [32, 35]. Hence, building trust-based relationships and sharing a common understanding of the intervention’s value among all involved parties is likely to support the sustainability of implementation.

Theoretical implications for CFIR application

Although the CFIR was developed as a comprehensive framework to assess implementation-related factors, most empirical studies have applied it cross-sectionally to identify static facilitators and barriers at a single point in time. As previously highlighted, the CFIR is often used retrospectively, while its temporal dimension remains underexplored [8, 36], limiting the understanding of how implementation constructs may evolve—or remain stable—throughout an intervention.

The longitudinal design of this study enabled tracking the CFIR constructs’ temporal trajectories across four facilities. Certain constructs, such as Champions and Key Stakeholders, underwent substantial changes during implementation. In contrast, the Knowledge and Beliefs about the Intervention construct was relatively stable within each facility during the study period, although its valence differed across facilities. This observation may contribute to the theoretical refinement of the CFIR by highlighting that the constructs may differ in their temporal change.

Specifically, certain constructs may enter a relatively stable orientation during the early intervention phase through pre-existing values, organizational alignment, or initial experiences. These constructs may be less responsive to further change despite ongoing implementation activities. Identifying these stable states—distinct from constructs that gradually change—may enhance the explanatory power of the CFIR and support the development of more targeted, time-sensitive implementation strategies. This approach aligns with recent evidence showing that implementation strategies vary in effectiveness across contexts and settings, underscoring the importance of tailoring support to local needs and the implementation phase [37].

Utility of process evaluation in visualizing implementation dynamics

Limited studies have conducted evaluations at multiple time points using the CFIR [38]. Process evaluations can help identify emerging barriers and support timely responses, which may facilitate a shared understanding among individuals involved in implementation. For example, in Units 1 and 2, where the value of care for low-risk women was often underestimated, online meetings, phone calls, and email communications were performed to emphasize that care for low-risk women included foundational elements essential across all risk levels. In Unit 3, informal meetings and communications with Opinion Leaders, Implementation Leaders, and Key Stakeholders were conducted to clarify concerns and explain the purpose and benefits of the intervention. Multilevel leadership support and shared organizational vision are important factors for successful implementation [23, 24]. While these strategies may have improved understanding in this study, their overall effect may have been restricted since only a small number of providers received support. Similar discrepancies between recommended implementation processes and actual team practices, as well as the need for capacity-building, have been noted in maternal–newborn hospital settings [39].

Alongside this process evaluation, an interrupted time-series analysis was conducted to quantitatively evaluate the same intervention [14]. Although the analysis showed gradual improvements in spontaneous vaginal delivery and uterotonic use rates during the third stage, no significant changes were observed in most other indicators. The qualitative findings of the current study complement the contextual background of the observed quantitative effects. For example, we interpreted the positive outcomes of the intervention as possibly associated with factors such as Champions fostering shared recognition of the intervention’s value and strong alignment between the intervention and the organizational context. Conversely, in facilities where implementation was challenging, barriers included a lack of recognition of the value of the intervention and the absence of Champions. These findings highlight variations in implementation processes across facilities and provide contextual depth for interpreting quantitative outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Two interview rounds were conducted with most participants, excluding those who were unavailable, enabling a fuller understanding of contextual changes and deeper insights at each facility.

Some study limitations must also be addressed. First, in Units 2 and 4, follow-up interviews with the same individuals were not always feasible due to COVID-19–related workload and organizational changes (Unit 2) and maternity/parental leave or transfers (Unit 4), necessitating some participant substitutions across phases. Consequently, individual-level trajectories could not be comprehensively evaluated, and comparisons across phases were primarily based on role- and facility-level patterns rather than within-person changes. This may have increased the risk of bias due to incomplete longitudinal follow-up. Nonetheless, post-intervention participants included Champions and Organizational Leaders who provided valuable information. Second, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers could not provide on-site implementation support; instead, support was delivered via phone and email, as needed. Third, as the findings of this study are context-dependent, their applicability to other settings with different organizational structures or cultural backgrounds may be limited. In addition, because this study was conducted as a pre-scale-up implementation in four motivated facilities, the identified determinants may reflect conditions in settings with relatively high readiness and engagement. Transferability to less-engaged settings may, therefore, be limited. Fourth, obstetricians were underrepresented in our interview sample; given their role as key stakeholders in maternity care, this may have limited the breadth of perspectives captured regarding implementation determinants. Fifth, formal member checking was not conducted, and selected quotations were translated from Japanese to English for reporting purposes. Although alternative strategies were adopted to enhance trustworthiness and team reviews of the translations were conducted, interpretive bias and nuance loss may remain. Interviews included both positive and critical perspectives; however, social desirability bias may have influenced the responses. Finally, as a qualitative process evaluation, the findings reflect changes in perceptions over time based on interpretation; they do not demonstrate causal effects or measure the effectiveness of the intervention quantitatively.

Conclusions

This study, conducted in Japanese obstetric settings, found that constructs related to individuals involved in the implementation, such as Champions and Key Stakeholders, were more dynamic over time, whereas perceptions of the intervention’s value remained relatively stable within each facility over time, despite its differing valence across facilities. This stable, facility-specific perception may have supported sustained engagement despite other barriers, particularly in the facility where perceptions remained positive. These findings highlight temporal variations in changes to the CFIR constructs and can inform refinement of the framework and development of more phase-specific implementation strategies. From a practical perspective, the findings suggest the importance of supporting key roles, such as Champions and Key Stakeholders, fostering shared value and alignment between QIs and clinical priorities, and providing phase-specific support as determinants evolve.
