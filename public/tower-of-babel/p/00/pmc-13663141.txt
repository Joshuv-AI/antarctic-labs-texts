Beyond BMI: body composition phenotypes and their systemic inflammatory profiles in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture
Authors: Qing Wang, MingJiao Liao, ShuXing Xing, Yulin Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBody mass index (BMI) cannot distinguish fat from muscle mass, masking metabolic heterogeneity. We aimed to characterize body composition phenotypes based on the intersection of BMI and low muscle mass in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF), and to compare systemic inflammatory profiles across phenotypes.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 245 hospitalized OVCF patients aged ≥ 50 years were consecutively enrolled. Appendicular muscle mass was measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and low muscle mass was defined per the 2025 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia criteria. Four phenotypes were delineated: normal weight/normal muscle (NW/NM), overweight/obese/normal muscle (OW/OB/NM), normal weight/low muscle (NW/LM), and overweight/obese low muscle (LMO). Systemic inflammatory markers were compared across groups: platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) as positive markers, and prealbumin and albumin as negative markers.ResultsThe overall prevalence of low muscle mass was 53.47% (95% CI: 47.22%–59.61%). Phenotype distribution was: NW/NM 11.02%, OW/OB/NM 35.51%, NW/LM 38.78%, and LMO 14.69%. Significant overall differences were observed for all systemic inflammatory markers: PLR (P < 0.001, ε²=0.059, small), CRP (P = 0.022, ε²=0.028, small), prealbumin (P = 0.007, ε²=0.037, small), and albumin (P = 0.015, ε²=0.031, small). For albumin, prealbumin, and PLR, the OW/OB/NM phenotype consistently exhibited the most favorable profile, while the most adverse values were split between the two low-muscle phenotypes. For CRP, the NW/NM reference group had the lowest median concentration, and all three non-reference phenotypes exceeded 3 mg/L. The key findings persisted in the female-only subgroup and after age adjustment.ConclusionsLow muscle mass is highly prevalent in hospitalized OVCF patients, and body composition phenotypes beyond BMI display modestly differentiated systemic inflammatory profiles. Effect sizes were small across all comparisons. The NW/LM phenotype, hidden to BMI screening, represents the largest subgroup. When benchmarked against clinically validated risk thresholds, albumin and prealbumin levels remained above high-risk cut-offs across all groups, whereas PLR exceeded the sarcopenia risk threshold in both low-muscle phenotypes and CRP exceeded the cardiovascular risk threshold in all non-reference phenotypes. Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) is the most common type of osteoporotic fracture, with its incidence rising steadily as populations age globally [1].

Beyond the acute pain and functional impairment, OVCF initiates a cascade of adverse events, including chronic residual back pain, spinal deformity, and substantially elevated mortality [2]. Despite optimal surgical management with vertebral augmentation techniques, a subset of patients continues to experience poor outcomes, with residual back pain affecting up to 16% of cases and refracture rates reaching approximately 13% [3]. These observations suggest that factors beyond bone mineral density and vertebral morphology, particularly those related to systemic metabolic status, play an important role in modulating post-fracture recovery.

In clinical practice, body mass index (BMI) has long been utilized as a convenient proxy for nutritional status. However, its fundamental limitation is that it cannot distinguish between fat and lean muscle mass, thereby masking substantial heterogeneity in body composition that may exist among individuals with identical BMI values [4]. This limitation has given rise to the controversial concept of the “obesity paradox,” wherein higher BMI appears paradoxically protective against mortality in various chronic conditions including cardiovascular disease and even among elderly patients with hip fractures [5, 6]. Distinct metabolic phenotypes have been recognized, including metabolically healthy obesity and metabolically unhealthy normal weight, underscoring the critical importance of assessing not just body weight, but body composition [7]. By integrating BMI with an objective measure of muscle mass, four distinct phenotypes can be delineated: normal weight/normal muscle (NW/NM), overweight/obese/normal muscle (OW/OB/NM), normal weight/low muscle (NW/LM), and overweight/obese low muscle (LMO) [4]. Notably, low muscle mass, the core component of sarcopenia, has been independently associated with inferior clinical outcomes across a range of settings, from rotator cuff repair to hip fracture surgery [6, 8, 9].

Chronic low-grade inflammation has been identified as a key mediator in the pathogenesis of age-related muscle loss, sometimes termed “inflammaging” [10, 11].

Among clinically accessible systemic inflammatory markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) are positive markers that rise with inflammation, while prealbumin and albumin are negative markers that decrease in response to systemic inflammation [12]. CRP can directly suppress muscle protein synthesis through inhibition of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, providing a mechanistic link between systemic inflammation and muscle catabolism [13]. PLR, the ratio of platelet to lymphocyte counts, has been associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults [14]. Prealbumin has a short half-life of approximately two days [15], and albumin has a longer half-life [16]; both are strongly modulated by the inflammatory state. Despite these well-established interrelationships, a critical knowledge gap persists regarding patients with OVCF.

Specifically, the distribution of body composition phenotypes defined by the intersection of BMI and objectively measured low muscle mass has not been systematically characterized in this population. It remains unknown whether these distinct phenotypes exhibit divergent profiles of systemic inflammation.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to: first, characterize the distribution of four pre-defined body composition phenotypes based on BMI and low muscle mass status in hospitalized OVCF patients; second, compare systemic inflammatory markers across these phenotypes, specifically the positive markers PLR and CRP and the negative markers prealbumin and albumin; and third, explore whether the OW/OB/NM phenotype is associated with a distinct systemic inflammatory profile relative to the NW/NM reference group. These cross-sectional comparisons are intended to provide a foundational description of systemic inflammatory heterogeneity among OVCF patients and to generate hypotheses for future prospective investigations.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study consecutively included patients aged ≥ 50 years admitted for OVCF to Chengdu Fifth People’s Hospital between October 2023 and October 2024. Data on demographics, body composition and bone mineral density (BMD), laboratory markers (PLR, CRP, prealbumin, albumin, total protein, uric acid, creatinine), and comorbidities were extracted from the electronic medical record system. All patients had sustained their vertebral fracture within 3 days before admission. OVCF was confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Fifth People’s Hospital (Lun Audit 2023-013-Ko01).

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following: age ≥ 50 years; BMD T-score ≤ -2.5 at the spine or femoral neck; acute vertebral compression fracture confirmed by MRI; and availability of complete clinical data as specified above. We excluded patients with severe hepatic dysfunction (aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase > 3 × upper limit of normal) [17, 18], severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) [19], high-energy trauma, concomitant malignancy [20], systemic infection at admission [21, 22], recent use of medications affecting body composition (e.g., glucocorticoids, anti-osteoporotic agents) [23], or contraindications to dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or MRI. Body composition and BMD assessment

Body composition and BMD were measured using a single DXA scanner (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare; enCORE v18.41.005). BMD was assessed at the lumbar spine (L1–L4) and femoral neck. Appendicular lean mass (ALM, kg) was summed from the limbs, and the appendicular skeletal muscle index (ASMI) was calculated as ALM/height² (kg/m²). Daily quality control was performed per manufacturer specifications.

Diagnostic criteria and phenotype classification

Low muscle mass

Low muscle mass was defined in accordance with the 2025 consensus update of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) [24]: for men aged 50–64 years, ASMI < 7.2 kg/m²; for men aged ≥ 65 years, ASMI < 7.0 kg/m²; for women aged 50–64 years, ASMI < 5.5 kg/m²; and for women aged ≥ 65 years, ASMI < 5.4 kg/m². It must be emphasized that this study identified low muscle mass, not the full sarcopenia syndrome, as data on muscle strength (handgrip) and physical performance (gait speed) were unavailable.

Overweight and obesity

Height and weight were collected at admission via patient self-report, as acute vertebral fracture precluded standardized standing measurements. BMI was computed as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Overweight/obesity was defined using the World Health Organization (WHO) Asian-specific criteria: BMI ≥ 23.0 kg/m² [25].

Body composition phenotypes

Based on BMI category and low muscle mass status, all eligible participants were assigned to one of four mutually exclusive groups:

NW/NM (normal weight/normal muscle): BMI <23.0 kg/m² and absence of low muscle mass.

NW/NM (normal weight/normal muscle): BMI <23.0 kg/m² and absence of low muscle mass.

OW/OB/NM (overweight/obese/normal muscle): BMI ≥23.0 kg/m² and absence of low muscle mass.

OW/OB/NM (overweight/obese/normal muscle): BMI ≥23.0 kg/m² and absence of low muscle mass.

NW/LM (normal weight/low muscle): BMI <23.0 kg/m² and presence of low muscle mass.

NW/LM (normal weight/low muscle): BMI <23.0 kg/m² and presence of low muscle mass.

LMO (overweight/obese low muscle): BMI ≥23.0 kg/m² and presence of low muscle mass.

LMO (overweight/obese low muscle): BMI ≥23.0 kg/m² and presence of low muscle mass.

Laboratory measurements

Fasting venous blood was collected on the morning of the second hospital day; the maximum interval from fracture to blood draw was approximately 5 days. Complete blood counts and serum markers (CRP, prealbumin, albumin, total protein, uric acid, creatinine) were measured using automated analyzers in the hospital’s central laboratory.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) because of non-normal distributions, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Proportions are reported with 95% Wilson confidence intervals.

Baseline characteristics were compared across the four body composition phenotypes using the Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables (effect size: ε²) and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For markers with a significant omnibus test (P < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Dunn’s test and Benjamini-Hochberg correction; pairwise effect sizes are reported as the rank-biserial correlation coefficient r with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1000 resamples).

The two low-muscle phenotypes (NW/LM vs. LMO) were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Because the NW/NM reference group contained no men, all primary analyses were repeated in the female-only subpopulation (n = 191). To account for age differences among phenotypes, quantile regression (τ = 0.5) was performed with age as a covariate and NW/NM as the reference group; model fit was assessed with pseudo-R² (Koenker & Machado).

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted: exclusion of patients with CRP > 50 mg/L (a validated cut-off for pyogenic spinal infection [26]) and reclassification of overweight/obesity using the international BMI threshold of ≥ 25.0 kg/m² [27].

Post-hoc power was estimated via 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations based on observed effect sizes.

An exploratory six-phenotype analysis further stratified the BMI ≥ 23.0 kg/m² category into overweight (BMI: 23.0–27.4 kg/m²) and obesity (BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m²) according to WHO Asian criteria [25], yielding six phenotypes:

normal weight/normal muscle (NW/NM: BMI <23.0 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

normal weight/normal muscle (NW/NM: BMI <23.0 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

overweight/normal muscle (OW/NM: BMI 23.0–27.4 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

overweight/normal muscle (OW/NM: BMI 23.0–27.4 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

obesity/normal muscle (OB/NM: BMI ≥27.5 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

obesity/normal muscle (OB/NM: BMI ≥27.5 kg/m², no low muscle mass),

normal weight/low muscle (NW/LM: BMI <23.0 kg/m², low muscle mass),

normal weight/low muscle (NW/LM: BMI <23.0 kg/m², low muscle mass),

overweight/low muscle (OW/LM: BMI 23.0–27.4 kg/m², low muscle mass),

overweight/low muscle (OW/LM: BMI 23.0–27.4 kg/m², low muscle mass),

obesity/low muscle (OB/LM: BMI ≥27.5 kg/m², low muscle mass).

obesity/low muscle (OB/LM: BMI ≥27.5 kg/m², low muscle mass).

The results were presented descriptively because of small subgroup sizes.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analytic sample comprised 245 patients (191 women, 54 men). Among the 290 initially eligible patients, 33 (11.38%) were excluded because of missing variables, most commonly prealbumin (n = 25, 8.62%), DXA-derived limb muscle mass (upper limb: n = 5, 1.72%; lower limb: n = 5, 1.72%), BMI (n = 4, 1.38%), hypertension (n = 4, 1.38%), and diabetes (n = 3, 1.03%). Several patients had missing values for multiple indicators; therefore, the sum of individual missing counts exceeds the number of excluded patients.

The overall prevalence of low muscle mass (AWGS 2025 criteria) was 53.47% (95% CI: 47.22%–59.61%). Half of the cohort (50.20%) was classified as overweight or obese. The distribution of the four phenotypes was: NW/NM, 27 patients (11.02%; 95% CI: 7.69%–15.56%); OW/OB/NM, 87 (35.51%; 95% CI: 29.78%–41.68%); NW/LM, 95 (38.78%; 95% CI: 32.89%–45.01%); and LMO, 36 (14.69%; 95% CI: 10.81%–19.67%).

Age-adjusted analyses

After age adjustment by quantile regression (Table S2, overall cohort), the key findings persisted. The OW/OB/NM group remained significantly lower in PLR than both NW/LM (β = 26.78, P = 0.023) and LMO (β = 43.66, P = 0.002), and significantly higher in prealbumin (β = −18.33, P = 0.018) and albumin (β = −1.67, P = 0.014) than NW/LM. All three non-reference groups became significantly higher in CRP than the NW/NM group after age adjustment (NW/NM vs. OW/OB/NM: β = 3.07, P = 0.009; NW/NM vs. NW/LM: β = 3.45, P = 0.006; NW/NM vs. LMO: β = 7.00, P = 0.005). Notably, the CRP difference between NW/NM and OW/OB/NM (or NW/LM), which was non-significant in the primary analysis, became significant after age adjustment, suggesting a suppression effect of age. All pseudo-R² values were low (Table S3), indicating that age accounted for only a small proportion of the variability in inflammatory markers. Residual diagnostics showed no systematic trend.

Female subgroup analysis

In the female subgroup (n = 191; baseline characteristics in Table S4), only PLR showed a significant omnibus difference across phenotypes; post-hoc comparisons were therefore restricted to PLR, and The OW/OB/NM group was significantly lower than NW/LM (P = 0.006, r = 0.31, medium). No significant differences between the two low-muscle phenotypes were observed in women: PLR (P = 0.611), CRP (P = 0.225), prealbumin (P = 0.833), and albumin (P = 0.450), with uniformly small effect sizes (Table 2, lower panel).

After age adjustment in the female subgroup (Table S5), the OW/OB/NM–NW/LM difference for PLR persisted but did not reach the conventional significance threshold (β = 25.56, P = 0.054). For CRP, the NW/NM group was significantly lower than OW/OB/NM (β = 3.13, P = 0.008) and NW/LM (β = 3.08, P = 0.019); the NW/NM–LMO difference was not significant. For prealbumin and albumin, the OW/OB/NM–NW/LM differences did not reach significance (prealbumin: β = −20.32, P = 0.053; albumin: β = −0.66, P = 0.359). But for albumin, the OW/OB/NM group was significantly lower than LMO (β = −1.89, P = 0.016).

Sensitivity analyses

Excluding patients with CRP > 50 mg/L did not alter the conclusions: overall differences remained significant for PLR (P = 0.001), prealbumin (P = 0.013), and albumin (P = 0.048), whereas CRP differences were attenuated to non-significance (P = 0.131).Re-classifying overweight/obesity using BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² yielded consistent results for PLR, prealbumin, and albumin (all P < 0.05); for CRP, the overall difference was no longer significant (P = 0.068).

Exploratory six-phenotype analysis

Baseline characteristics and inflammatory marker profiles for the six phenotypes are presented in Table S6 and Figure S2. When the overweight/obese category was split into overweight (BMI: 23.0–27.4 kg/m²) and obesity (BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m²), the OB/LM subgroup (n = 2) exhibited the highest median PLR (230.53), the highest CRP (50.80 mg/L), the highest prealbumin (276.00 mg/L), and the lowest albumin (34.60 g/L). Formal statistical testing was precluded by small subgroup sizes.

Post-hoc power analysis

Post-hoc power for the Kruskal-Wallis H tests was 0.95 for PLR, 0.84 for prealbumin, 0.78 for albumin, and 0.75 for CRP. In contrast, power for the Mann-Whitney comparisons between NW/LM and LMO was low, ranging from 0.05 to 0.16.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional investigation of 245 elderly patients hospitalized with OVCF, we characterized the distribution of four body composition phenotypes and compared their systemic inflammatory profiles. Three principal findings were observed. First, low muscle mass was highly prevalent, affecting over half the cohort, with the NW/LM phenotype representing the single largest subgroup. Second, the OW/OB/NM phenotype exhibited lower PLR and higher prealbumin and albumin than the NW/LM phenotype, while its systemic inflammatory profiles were statistically comparable to the NW/NM reference group. Third, direct comparison of the two low-muscle phenotypes revealed no significant differences in any systemic inflammatory marker, although this null finding should be interpreted with caution given the low statistical power for these comparisons (0.05–0.16; Table 2). These cross-sectional associations provide a foundational description of metabolic heterogeneity among OVCF patients, yet the interpretation of these findings requires careful consideration of effect sizes, potential confounders, and the inherent constraints of the study design.

The overall prevalence of low muscle mass in our cohort was 53.47%, a figure substantially higher than the 5% to 16% estimates reported in community-dwelling older populations [8, 28], and aligning more closely with rates observed in chronic inflammatory or malignant conditions [28, 29]. This high prevalence likely reflects a convergence of advanced age, acute fracture-related catabolic stress, and pre-existing vulnerabilities. Of particular concern, the NW/LM phenotype constituted nearly 40% of the cohort. This phenotype is entirely invisible to BMI-based screening and has been described in other populations as a state of hidden metabolic vulnerability [4]. Its prominence underscores the fundamental limitation of relying solely on BMI in the perioperative evaluation of OVCF patients.

A central finding requiring cautious interpretation concerns the magnitude of the observed inter-phenotype differences. According to Cohen’s conventional benchmarks [30] (ε²: 0.01 = small, 0.06 = medium, 0.14 = large), the overall differences for CRP, prealbumin, and albumin were small, while that for PLR (ε² = 0.059) approached a medium effect. Effect sizes, unlike P values, are independent of sample size and convey the magnitude of an association rather than merely its statistical detectability [31]. The small effect sizes observed here suggest that while the inflammatory profiles do differ among body composition phenotypes, the degree of differentiation is modest. This is not unexpected in a cross-sectional study of an acutely hospitalized population, where the superimposed effects of fracture-related stress on circulating markers may partially obscure the more subtle differences attributable to chronic body composition status. Furthermore, as emphasized by Tomczak and Tomczak [32], the interpretation of effect size must consider the specific research context; in exploratory clinical studies where measurement is inherently noisy, even small effect sizes can provide meaningful leads for hypothesis generation, provided they are not overstated.

Beyond the statistical interpretation of effect sizes, it is informative to benchmark the observed marker concentrations against clinically validated risk thresholds. For albumin and prealbumin, the median levels in all four phenotypes (albumin: 38.50–40.10 g/L; prealbumin: 184.00–210.00 mg/L) remained above their respective high-risk thresholds (albumin: <35 g/L for mortality risk [33]; prealbumin: <150 mg/L for poor one-year survival [34]), indicating that even the most metabolically compromised groups had not crossed into established danger zones. Nevertheless, the OW/OB/NM group consistently showed the highest concentrations, and the OW/OB/NM–NW/LM difference was statistically significant for both markers. For PLR, both low-muscle phenotypes (NW/LM: 155.00; LMO: 168.38) exceeded the ≥ 155 threshold for sarcopenia risk [14], whereas the OW/OB/NM group median (130.43) approximated the population mean [35]; the OW/OB/NM–NW/LM difference was significant. For these three markers (albumin, prealbumin, and PLR), the OW/OB/NM phenotype consistently exhibited the most favorable profile, while the most adverse values were split between the two low-muscle phenotypes: NW/LM for albumin and prealbumin, and LMO for PLR. For CRP, the pattern differed: the median concentration was lowest in the NW/NM reference group (2.80 mg/L) and exceeded 3 mg/L in all three non-reference phenotypes [36], with LMO showing the highest level (9.65 mg/L) and NW/LM the second highest (6.70 mg/L). The OW/OB/NM group, despite its preserved muscle mass, had a numerically higher median CRP than NW/NM, and this difference became statistically significant after age adjustment (β = 3.07, P = 0.009). This acute-phase response likely obscures the more subtle inter-phenotype differences attributable to chronic body composition status in two ways: by raising the baseline inflammatory signal across all groups, thereby compressing the relative differences between phenotypes, and by introducing time-sensitive variability that a single cross-sectional measurement cannot disentangle from the chronic inflammatory background.

The differential behavior of the inflammatory markers merits separate discussion. PLR demonstrated the largest overall effect size (ε²=0.059) and remained statistically significant across all sensitivity analyses, including after exclusion of patients with CRP exceeding 50 mg/L. This pattern is consistent with the notion that PLR may be less susceptible to acute perturbation than CRP and may therefore serve as a more stable indicator of the underlying chronic inflammatory milieu in this acutely ill population. Supporting this interpretation, elevated PLR has been associated with a greater risk of sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults [14] and with increased all-cause mortality in a large population-based cohort [35]. However, it must equally be acknowledged that this apparent “stability” of PLR may also indicate a relative insensitivity to acute inflammatory fluctuations. In the setting of acute vertebral fracture, where the inflammatory milieu represents a composite of chronic background and acute-phase response, the clinical interpretation of PLR requires caution; an unchanging PLR value in the acute phase may fail to capture the dynamic nature of the systemic response to fracture. In contrast, the CRP differences among phenotypes were attenuated to non-significance in the sensitivity analysis excluding patients with markedly elevated CRP, reinforcing that this acute-phase reactant is heavily influenced by fracture-related stress. The low pseudo-R² values from the age-adjusted models further support the interpretation that the observed inter-phenotype differences in systemic inflammatory markers were largely independent of age, reinforcing the role of body composition itself in driving metabolic heterogeneity.

The negative systemic inflammatory markers prealbumin and albumin, as emphasized by the ASPEN 2021 consensus [12], exhibited significant inter-phenotype differences, with the highest concentrations observed in the OW/OB/NM group and the lowest in the low-muscle phenotypes. Prealbumin, with its short half-life of approximately two days, responds rapidly to changes in the systemic inflammatory state [37]. Albumin, owing to its longer half-life, reflects more chronic inflammatory burden and has been consistently linked to adverse outcomes in surgical and orthopedic populations. In a large-scale study of non-cardiac surgical patients, preoperative serum albumin was the strongest predictor of operative mortality and morbidity [38], and in geriatric hip fracture patients, hypoalbuminemia was a powerful independent risk factor for mortality and postoperative complications [33]. Serum albumin has also been shown to correlate positively with skeletal muscle mass in elderly individuals, independent of age and adiposity [39]. The lower prealbumin and albumin concentrations observed in the low-muscle phenotypes are therefore consistent with a greater systemic inflammatory burden, a phenomenon well documented in critically ill patients [40].

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference regarding the direction of the observed associations between body composition and systemic inflammatory profiles. Given the inherent constraints of this design, the present study is limited to describing associations and cannot establish causal relationships or predict long-term clinical outcomes. Any suggestion of prognostic value or clinical utility remains untested and must await validation in longitudinal studies with functional endpoints. Second, a notable sex imbalance existed across phenotypes: the NW/NM reference group comprised exclusively female patients, whereas the LMO group contained 52.8% men. This distribution precluded adjustment for sex in multivariable models and raises the possibility that some of the observed inter-phenotype differences are partially attributable to sex-specific biological variation rather than to body composition per se. We sought to address this limitation by replicating all primary analyses in the female-only subpopulation; the key findings persisted in women, lending support to the interpretation that at least part of the observed metabolic heterogeneity is attributable to body composition. Nonetheless, residual confounding by sex cannot be fully excluded, and future studies with balanced sex distributions across phenotypes are warranted. Third, we combined overweight and obesity into a single BMI ≥ 23.0 kg/m² category for the primary analysis. Our exploratory six-phenotype analysis provided a key insight: the OB/LM subgroup (n = 2) displayed a markedly elevated inflammatory profile, with the highest median PLR, CRP, and prealbumin, and the lowest albumin. Given the extremely small subgroup size, these extreme values must be interpreted with caution; they may reflect individual patient characteristics rather than a stable phenotype pattern. Nevertheless, when viewed alongside meta-analytic evidence that sarcopenic obesity confers a substantially increased risk of all-cause mortality [41], this finding suggests that the OB/LM phenotype may represent a distinct and particularly high-risk metabolic state that warrants dedicated investigation in larger cohorts.

Fourth, data on vertebral fracture severity, number of fractured vertebrae, and pre-fracture functional status were unavailable, representing a source of residual confounding. Fifth, DXA-derived appendicular lean mass includes non-skeletal muscle components; therefore, the ASMI may overestimate true skeletal muscle mass.

Sixth, height and weight were self-reported because standing measurements were infeasible in patients with acute vertebral fractures; self-reported height tends to be overestimated, potentially leading to BMI underestimation and phenotype misclassification. Seventh, important covariates such as diabetes, hypertension, and other comorbidities were not included in multivariable-adjusted analyses and may have influenced the observed associations. Eighth, this study employed post-hoc rather than a priori power calculations, and the low power for comparisons between NW/LM and LMO indicates that these null findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study of hospitalized OVCF patients demonstrates that body composition phenotyping beyond BMI reveals distinct but modest systemic inflammatory profiles. Low muscle mass affected over half the cohort, and the NW/LM phenotype, undetectable by BMI-based screening, was the largest subgroup.

The small effect sizes across all comparisons indicate that these metabolic differences, while statistically discernible, are limited in magnitude.

When benchmarked against clinically validated risk thresholds, the OW/OB/NM phenotype exhibited the most favorable profile for albumin, prealbumin, and PLR, while the two low-muscle phenotypes carried the greater inflammatory burden. For CRP, the NW/NM reference group showed the most favorable profile, with all three non-reference phenotypes exceeding the cardiovascular risk threshold.

These cross-sectional associations do not support standalone clinical action in the acute setting but provide a rationale for incorporating body composition assessment into perioperative evaluation. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether phenotype-guided risk stratification can improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
