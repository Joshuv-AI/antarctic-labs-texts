Introduction

Chylous ascites is an uncommon condition in infancy and childhood resulting from the accumulation of milky chyle rich in triglycerides into the peritoneal cavity [1,2].

Chylous ascites in the pediatric population can be primary or secondary, depending on the underlying pathological factors. Primary congenital chylous ascites is rare and is mainly due to congenital lymphatic malformations in neonates. Secondary chylous disorders are commonly observed in older children and may follow trauma, malignancy, surgical injuries, and infections such as tuberculosis [3].

The most common clinical manifestations of chylous ascites is abdominal distension, hydrocoele, chylothorax, or peritonitis with other nonspecific symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, fever, night sweats, poor feeding, and symptoms of respiratory distress [4,5].

Ultrasonography (USG) and computerized tomography (CT) aids in diagnosis by confirming the presence of fluid in abdominal cavity and excluding other causes, and the final diagnosis of CA is dependent on paracentesis [2,5].

Usually conservative treatment is initially recommended, including abdominal paracentesis, a high-protein low-fat diet with medium-chain triglycerides (MCT), total parenteral nutrition (TPN), and more recently somatostatin therapy. In cases not responding to conservative treatment after a several weeks – two months surgery is recommended [6].

The association between congenital hypothyroidism and congenital CA rarely described in the literature. In this case report, we will discuss the occurrence of congenital CA and congenital hypothyroidism in a child who was successfully treated surgically.

Case presentation

A 3-week-old infant presented with rapidly increasing abdominal distention noticed within the first week of life. The physical examination showed a severely distended abdomen without any palpable abdominal mass, lower limbs edema and bilateral scrotal hydrocele. Additionally, coarse facial features, a large anterior fontanelle, and a truncal shortening were noted, raising suspicion of hypothyroidism. Thyroid function tests revealed a low free T4 of 9.7 pmol/L and elevated TSH level of 23.4 mIU/L. Abdominal ultrasound (USG) revealed disseminated ascites with small pleural effusion. The paracentesis was performed, and the ascites fluid showed 86 % lymphocytes and triglycerides of 3265 mg/dL. Based on previous findings, the infant was diagnosed with congenital CA and congenital hypothyroidism.

The baby was initially treated conservatively with TPN, a medium-chain milk diet for 3 months, multiple abdominal paracentesis and thyroid hormone supplementation (L-thyroxine, 10 μg/kg/day) was given.

After nearly a month, the patient showed no signs of improvement and presented with worsening of his overall health status. Serum electrolytes, urea, and creatinine levels were normal, whereas hemoglobin was low of 7.6 g/dL. Abdominal CT showed a huge amount of intraperitoneal fluid with no structural changes, and ultrasonography showed bilateral scrotal hydrocele. The paracentesis was performed, the ascitic fluid revealed elevated triglycerides of 836 mg/dL, cholesterol 49 mmol/L, LDH 308 U/L, and bacterial cultures were positive for Pseudomonas. Urine and blood cultures showed no bacterial growth.

Octreotide was initiated at a dose of 1.6 μg/kg/h for 8 days, and only breast feeding was allowed with continuing levothyroxine treatment. Additionally, he received a unit of blood transfusion, and antibiotics were prescribed, with a good response. The patient was discharged a month later and was prescribed to take octreotide, omega-3, and fat-soluble vitamins.

In the following months, the patient presented to the emergency department due to abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, and poor general condition, including serious malnutrition and severe respiratory distress, with rapidly increasing abdominal distention, indicating the failure of the conservative treatment. USG revealed a large amount of intraperitoneal fluid with floating bowel loops (Fig. 1).

Pre-operation: severely distended abdomen.

Surgical intervention was decided after stabilizing the general condition. He was fed by high-fat milk with methylene blue 6 h before surgery to detect the leak site. Laparotomy revealed milky fluid leaking in the peritoneal cavity; about 3 L of milky fluid was accumulated in the peritoneal cavity, the intestines were tightly adherent as a single mass, and there were dilated lymphatics at the root of the mesentery, but the exact point of leakage was not clear, with no other abnormalities. The retroperitoneal aorta was exposed, the adhesions were released and the entire area of lymph was ligated on the left side of the inferior vena cava on the base of superior mesenteric vessels, whereas on the right side, it was partially ligated to prevent lower limbs edema, and a drainage tube was placed in the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 2).

On Laparotomy exploration: severely adhered and thickened bowel.

Postoperative, the patient received oral clear fluids, and on the third day he started milk feeding. The fluid output from the drains consisted of serous fluid and progressively decreased. He was discharged from the hospital a week later in good condition. Follow-up abdominal USG at 1st, 3rd, and 6th months after discharge showed gradually decreasing in ascitic fluid, and the child has normal growth (Fig. 3).

post-operation: noticeable regression of abdominal distension.

Discussion and conclusion

Congenital hypothyroidism, a disease occurring with a frequency of 1/3000–4000, is often detected early through routine screenings. While it rarely presents with ascites, there may be other accompanying congenital abnormalities. Previous studies have linked congenital hypothyroidism with congenital chylothorax [2].

In our case, we observed both congenital chylous ascites (CA) and congenital hypothyroidism. The question that arises is whether fetal hypothyroidism can lead to CA. It is known that thyroid hormone exerts its effects through adrenergic receptors, and it is hypothesized that a deficiency in thyroid hormone leads to reduced adrenergic stimulation of the lymphatic system. This, in turn, can result in sluggish lymph flow, lymphatic system engorgement, and leakage of lymph into the peritoneal cavity, potentially causing CA. Animal studies have shown a direct link between the rate of lymphatic flow, lung liquid clearance, and adrenergic receptor activity in the lymphatic system [2,5].

Congenital chylous ascites is a rare condition characterized by the accumulation of chyle, a milky fluid rich in triglycerides and lymphocytes, in the abdominal cavity of infants under three months old. Primary chylous disorders are generally caused by primary congenital dysplasia, while secondary disorders are typically the result of tumors, trauma, inflammation, or abdominal surgery [7].

Approximately 39 % of cases of chylous ascites are attributed to congenital malformations of the lymphatic system, such as congenital lymphangiomatosis, congenital chylous cysts, and atresia of the lymphatic ducts. An additional 30 % of cases are considered idiopathic, characterized by “leaky lymphatics” which is thought to occur due to delayed maturation or underdevelopment of lacteals, allowing chyle to escape and enter the peritoneal cavity [7,8].

Symptoms of chylous ascites may include abdominal distension, discomfort, pain, nausea, loss of appetite, and weight loss. The primary life-threatening symptom in chylous ascites often involves respiratory difficulties, occurring when the massive abdominal distention applies pressure on the diaphragm [4,5].

The true challenge lies in determining the underlying cause of the chylous ascites. USG and CT help by confirming the presence of fluid in the abdominal cavity and excluding other causes, and the final diagnosis of CA depends on the paracentesis. Paracentesis is a vital diagnostic tool and also used for therapeutic management. Where the ascites fluid is milky and rich in lymphocytes, proteins, triglycerides, and chylomicrons [2,5].

Lymphangiography is avoided due to technical difficulties in small children and the potential for serious side effects [9].

The management of chylous ascites may vary depending on the underlying cause, individual factors and the severity of the condition. It usually begins with conservative treatment, including diet management. A low-fat diet is often recommended, and medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) supplements may be added to the diet to reduce new chyle production. If there is no improvement with the administration of an MCT-based formula, Total parenteral nutrition may be used to provide essential nutrients in severe cases where oral intake is not sufficient. Paracentesis may be performed to alleviate symptoms, reduce abdominal pressure, and can be repeated as necessary [4,10].

The aim of conservative management is to provide time for the leaky lymphatics to get obliterated and for new lympho-venous channels to get established [5].

Somatostatin analogs have been employed as a treatment option for chylous ascites, particularly when conservative measures have been unsuccessful. Many case reports have demonstrated the effectiveness of using somatostatin analogs in reducing lymph production. Several successful cases have been reported in newborns with chylothorax and chylous ascites, showing the benefits of somatostatin analogs in these conditions. The mechanism by which somatostatin reduces lymphatic drainage is not fully understood. It is believed to work by decreasing fat absorption, reducing triglyceride levels in the thoracic duct, and decreasing lymphatic flow in most lymph channels [5,10].

In cases that are refractory to conservative treatment, in case of worsening clinical symptoms, we resort to surgical treatment. Successful surgical treatment is dependent on pinpointing the area of ascites leakage to ligate the appropriate lymphatic duct or to excise congenital mesenteric chylous cyst. in pediatric patients it is difficult and rare to pinpoint the area of chylous leakage [6].

The association between congenital hypothyroidism and congenital chylothorax has been described in the literature. We found no reports of a similar association between congenital hypothyroidism and chylous ascites. However, we did come across two previous case reports describing a case of CA accompanied by congenital hypothyroidism, which resolved following thyroid hormone supplementation, octreotide treatment, and a diet containing MCT.
