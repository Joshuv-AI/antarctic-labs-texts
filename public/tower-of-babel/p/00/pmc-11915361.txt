Glaucoma Tube Shunt Revision with Scleral “Turtle-Plast”
Authors: Sylvia Edoigiawerie, Peter Weber, Madhu Gorla, Giovanni Campagna, Arsham Sheybani, Mary Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis article contains two cases with accompanying surgical videos, illustrating the use of a Tutoplast plug to create a watertight seal in the corneoscleral fistula formed after tube shunt removal.Case descriptionThese cases demonstrate that Tutoplast can be cut into a strategic geometric shape to both plug the fistula formed at the tube entry site and reinforce adjacent areas of scleral thinning during tube removal or repositioning surgery. Specifically, a piece of dehydrated scleral Tutoplast was shaped into a small rectangle with an attached larger rectangle. The smaller rectangle or “head” would plug the fistula, while the attached larger rectangle or “body” could be used to reinforce the adjacent area of scleral thinning. The patch graft was sutured to the sclera at the four corners and resembled a turtle with four sutures for legs, with its head in the fistula. Hence, this technique is called the scleral “Turtle-Plast.”Conclusion and clinical significanceThis “Turtle-Plast” technique is advantageous over direct suturing of the tube track because the head of the turtle provides an astigmatically neutral, watertight seal for the short anterior fistula, which can often be technically challenging to secure. Meanwhile, the body of the turtle can serve as a more substantive piece of patch graft to suture down to the underlying sclera that may be susceptible to thinning.How to cite this articleEdoigiawerie S, Weber P, Gorla M, et al. Introduction

Tube erosion is a serious complication of glaucoma tube shunt surgery, which can lead to endophthalmitis and vision loss. Ocular inflammation and topical steroid use have been associated with tube exposure.1 Postoperative nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use has been associated with conjunctival retraction and wound leak.2 Surgical management of tube erosion may involve repositioning or removing the tube, and the prior sclerotomy track is typically sutured shut.3 However, achieving a watertight closure may be challenging if the sclerotomy is very short and anterior, and suturing these tracks too tightly may induce significant astigmatism. There are two reports in the literature describing plugging the corneoscleral fistula at the time of tube repositioning or removal with pericardial or scleral Tutoplast.4,5 A video example of this technique can be found on Dr Keith Barton's YouTube channel. Herein, our adaptation of this surgical technique is described through two illustrative cases and accompanying surgical videos. These case studies complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the University of Chicago and Washington University in St Louis Institutional Review Board.

Case Description

Case 1

The first case (referred by MG to MQ) is a pseudophakic 82-year-old woman with uveitic glaucoma who had undergone a prior failed trabeculectomy and subsequent superotemporal Ahmed FP7 12 years prior to presentation with tube erosion in the setting of chronic topical steroid and NSAID use (Fig. 1A and supplement Video 1). The erosion was in her left eye. Initially, her visual acuity (VA) was 20/80, and IOP was 10 mm Hg on only IOP drops. The tube was eroded from the tube-plate junction to the sclerotomy site into the anterior chamber (AC). There was no associated AC cell, hypopyon, or vitritis. The fellow eye had already undergone tube removal for recurrent tube erosion. She underwent complete removal of the Ahmed FP7 and concurrent SION goniotomy (Sight Sciences, Menlo Park, CA) by MQ for IOP control.

(A) Case 1 (from MQ) Ahmed FP7 tube erosion from AC entry site to endplate; (B) SION goniotomy; (C) Ahmed FP7 tube, plate, and capsule removal; (D) Healon Pro refluxing from anterior to short tube entry site; (E) Rigid dehydrated Tutoplast cut to shape, inserted in tube entry site, hydrated, and sutured to sclera for a watertight seal; (F) A capsule autograft from the prior Ahmed's capsule was used to assist with the closure, and the conjunctiva and Tenon's were re-approximated to the limbus; (G) Case 2 (from GC to AS) Baerveldt-250 tube erosion from AC entry site to endplate, also with anterior plate erosion; (H) Baerveldt-250 tube and plate removal; (I) Prehydrated Tutoplast cut to shape with a thin tip protruding; (J) The hydrated Tutoplast could not be inserted into the old tube track, so the fistula was first widened with a side port blade; (K) The anterior corners of the Tutoplast were then anchored to the sclera so the Tutoplast's tip could be inserted into the old tube track; (L) After the conjunctiva and Tenon's were advanced to the limbus, an area of bare sclera remained, so an amniotic membrane graft was used to cover the bare sclera temporally

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia as per surgeon and patient preference. A temporal paracentesis was made, lidocaine was injected into the AC, followed by Miostat to constrict the pupil and Healon Pro to stabilize the AC. A nasal goniotomy was performed, and the paracentesis was sutured. A conjunctival peritomy was initiated temporally, and careful sharp and blunt dissection was performed to separate scarred conjunctiva from the underlying sclera. A 6-0 polyglactin scleral traction suture was placed superotemporally to rotate the eye inferonasally. The tube was removed from the AC and amputated from the plate. The dissection was continued to mobilize the conjunctiva superiorly beyond the tube entry site. A second scleral traction suture was placed at the tube-plate junction to rotate the eye further. A radial relaxing incision was made in the superior conjunctiva, and blunt and sharp dissection were performed to separate the conjunctiva from Tenon's capsule. The capsule was incised, and a block of capsule tissue was excised and stored for later use as a patch graft. The Ahmed plate was removed, and the deep side of the capsule tissue was dissected off the bare sclera.

Since the tube entry site was very anterior and short, it would have been difficult to achieve a watertight seal with direct closure. Instead, a piece of dehydrated scleral Tutoplast with dimensions of 0.6 × 1 cm was used to plug the fistula (Fig. 1B). When it was rehydrated, the Tutoplast expanded to create a watertight seal of the fistula. This piece of Tutoplast was shaped like a small rectangle with an attached larger rectangle, so the smaller part could be used to plug the fistula and the attached larger part could be used to reinforce the adjacent area of scleral thinning. The patch graft was sutured to the sclera at the four corners and resembled a turtle with four sutures for legs, with its head in the fistula; hence, this is called the scleral “Turtle-Plast” (Fig. 1C). When balanced salt solution was used to rinse Healon Pro out of the AC, there was no leak at the prior tube sclerotomy site.

Postoperatively, the patient was started on prednisolone four times a day and moxifloxacin four times a day in addition to the intraocular pressure (IOP) drops as described above. At postoperative week 2, preservative-free tafluprost and timolol were started in her left eye, which had initially been managed with no IOP-lowering agents. At that time, an IOP of 21 mm Hg was recorded. Throughout the postoperative course, there was no leak from the corneoscleral fistula, the conjunctival closure remained intact, and there was negligible induced corneal astigmatism. By her most recent follow-up at postoperative month 4 (supplemental Fig. 1), the VA was two lines better at 20/60, and the IOP was controlled at 14 mm Hg on the same regimen of preservative-free tafluprost and timolol.

Case 2

The second case (from GS to AS) is a 58-year-old with secondary open-angle glaucoma who underwent removal of a Baerveldt-250 implant following tube erosion (Fig. 1D). The preoperative plan was to remove the entire Baerveldt endplate and to perform cyclophotocoagulation in the future as necessary for further IOP control. The surgery was done under monitored anesthesia care with a retrobulbar block of 5 mL of 2% preservative-free lidocaine.

After the Baerveldt-250 was removed, a piece of already-hydrated scleral Tutoplast was also cut into a geometric shape with a protruding thin tip and anchored down to the sclera with polyglactin suture (Fig. 1D). The tip of the Tutoplast was then inserted into the tube entry site to create a watertight seal of the fistula (Fig. 1E). The conjunctiva and Tenon's were rotated from superonasal to superotemporal, but a small area of bare sclera remained temporally, which was covered with an amniotic membrane graft (Figs 1F to L).

At postoperative year 1 (supplemental Figure 2), the patient's vision in her left eye is 20/50 (better than baseline 20/80), and her IOP is 15 on preservative-free timolol, preservative-free tafluprost, and fluorometholone once a day. She has not needed any subsequent IOP-lowering surgery.

Discussion

This “Turtle-Plast” technique is advantageous over direct suturing of the tube track because the head of the turtle provides an astigmatically neutral, watertight seal of a short anterior fistula, which can often be technically challenging to secure. Meanwhile, the body of the turtle can reinforce an adjacent area of scleral thinning in addition to being a more substantive piece of patch graft to suture down to the underlying sclera. Variations on this technique may be implemented in a variety of clinical scenarios where plugging, rather than covering up or suturing shut, a fistula may be useful. Further variations, such as prehydration of the Tutoplast prior to plugging, may also be implemented. For example, case 2 featured a different surgical team who prehydrated Tutoplast prior to fistula plug. Notably, in both cases, scleral Tutoplast hydrated with balanced salt solution was used. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, previously published reports of the use of a Tutoplast entry site did not include a surgical video. Here, two surgical videos were included to clarify the technique.

Although prehydrated Tutoplast was used in the second case, we recommend that the Tutoplast not be hydrated until after fistula insertion, where it can then be hydrated and swell to plug the track fully. The second case video demonstrates that when the Tutoplast is rehydrated prior to insertion, it is softer and more difficult to insert. If a surgeon desires to use a Tutoplast plug in tube revision surgery where the preexisting tube track may be difficult to suture shut, a dehydrated and thus more rigid Tutoplast might be easier to maneuver supplement video 2.

Conclusion

Using two illustrative cases, this article demonstrates that Tutoplast can be cut into a strategic geometric shape with a thin protruding tip, which can be used to plug a tube entry site during tube removal or repositioning surgery, as well as to reinforce adjacent areas of scleral thinning if needed. Additionally, while the Tutoplast can be inserted if it has already been prehydrated, it may be more easily inserted into the tube entry site if it is kept dehydrated and rigid.
