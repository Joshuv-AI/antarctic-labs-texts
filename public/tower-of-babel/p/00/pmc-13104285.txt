Coral color morphs exhibit distinct microbial and proteomic profiles linked to stress and immune mechanisms in a changing ocean
Authors: Nathalia Delgadillo-Ordoñez, Adrian Schwarzenberg, Huoming Zhang, Laura Beenham, Dalila Bensaddek, Inês Raimundo, Tullia Terraneo, Francesca Benzoni, Raquel S. Peixoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoral phenotypic plasticity facilitates acclimation and adaptation to environmental variability. Coral species often display a variety of color morphs, yet key biological and ecological implications of such phenotypic variation remain underexplored. Here, we present the first proteomic and untargeted lipidomic and metabolomic survey to explore the biological characteristics and potential ecological significance of different color morphs (pink and brown) of healthy Pocillopora verrucosa sampled along a latitudinal gradient.ResultsOur multi-omic approach elucidated distinct mechanisms associated with these dominant color morphs. We discovered bacterial indicators specific to each morph: putative pathogens such as Salmonella, Escherichia-Shigella, and carotenoid-producing Gemmatimonas were notably associated with the pink morph, whereas the brown morph was associated with potentially beneficial bacteria, such as Lysobacter, Acinetobacter, and Endozoicomonas. Despite these microbiome differences, the lipidome and metabolome of P. verrucosa were surprisingly homogeneous across colors and locations, suggesting similar metabolic performances during summer conditions. Key polar and apolar lipid classes, such as fatty acids, glycerophosphocholines, and retinoids, were prevalent. Notably, our proteomic analysis revealed morph-specific expressions, with pink morphs exhibiting enhanced levels of GFP-like proteins, Ankyrin, and the enzyme pullulanase, suggesting novel putative protective roles. In contrast, the brown morphs showed a higher abundance of heat shock proteins, indicating putative differential stress response capabilities.ConclusionThis comprehensive study provides the first proteomic survey of P. verrucosa and identifies key physiological pathways and trade-offs linked to color morphs, which can further contribute to enhancing our understanding of coral resilience in the face of climate change.Significance statementUnderstanding the phenotypic plasticity of corals is crucial for uncovering mechanisms of resilience in warming oceans, yet the biological significance of coral color morphs still needs to be explored. Using an innovative multi-omic approach (proteomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics), we provide the first comprehensive analysis of differences between pink and brown morphs of Pocillopora verrucosa. Our data reveal key taxa, potentially pathogenic or beneficial, associated with each morph, and suggest different strategies for each color morph to cope with heat stress, either expressing proteins involved in UV protection and heterotrophic activity or enhanced levels of heat stress resilience and DNA repair. These findings offer insights into the phenotypic plasticity of coral color morphs and their differential responses to climate change. Introduction

Scleractinian corals exhibit phenotypic plasticity, a phenomenon characterized by natural morphological variations among colonies of the same species and their adaptability to environmental changes [1]. This plasticity manifests at multiple levels, including colony morphology, color morphs, and even flexible associations with microorganisms, across diverse environmental conditions [2–6]. Investigations into phenotypic plasticity among coral color morphs have been limited and less understood [7–13], leaving the biological roles and ecological implications of such variation in response to environmental change largely unexplored [10, 11]. Some studies have reported tradeoffs in susceptibility to bleaching and disease among coral morphs of the same species [9, 11, 14, 15], highlighting the significance of understanding intraspecific variation and its implications for coral ecophysiology.

Coral species exhibit different color morphs, arising mainly from polymorphism (genotypic differences leading to phenotypic traits) or polyphenism (variations in phenotypic traits due to differences in gene expression or post-translational mechanisms) [16, 17], or a combination of both [17]. For instance, genetic variation in coding sequences, gene copy number, and regulatory regions can provide a heritable basis for stable color differences among coral genets [17, 18]. However, pigment expression is highly sensitive to external conditions, including light regime, thermal stress, and developmental stage, and color intensity can vary across ontogeny or environmental change [19–21]. Color phenotypes may therefore reflect both fixed genetic architecture and environmentally mediated regulation of gene expression, allowing distinct morphs to coexist in sympatry. Pigments synthesized by corals contribute to their coloration, primarily due to green fluorescent proteins (GFP-like pigments), photoactivatable fluorescent proteins (PAFPs) [22], and photosynthetic pigments from symbiotic zooxanthellae, and carotenoids produced by Symbiodiniaceae and coral associated-bacteria [23–28]. These pigments constitute a substantial portion of the soluble proteins in coral tissues and play significant roles in coral biology, contributing to photoprotection and light enhancement, particularly in shallow-water corals [17, 24, 25, 27, 29–32]. Coral pigments fulfill various roles in coral ecophysiology, including thermal dissipation, antioxidant activity, and attraction of symbionts [33–36], as well as facilitating symbiont re-colonization during post-bleaching recovery [24]. In addition to GFP-like proteins, other compounds, including heat shock proteins, antioxidant enzymes, and photoprotective pigments, contribute to differential susceptibility to thermal bleaching among coral morphs [26, 31, 32, 37].

Additionally, exploring corals’ metabolic and proteomic profiles proves particularly valuable in uncovering intraspecific differences among color morphs, potentially revealing varying health statuses and physiological responses to environmental factors. Overall, corals exhibit differences in metabolic (metabolome) and proteomic (proteome) signatures, which represent the collection of molecules produced by cells, ultimately reflecting the coral’s physiological state and responses to the environment [38]. In this sense, the coral metabolome and proteome reflect the physiological and metabolic processes of the host, associated Symbiodiniaceae, and microbiome, offering a holistic view of coral health [13, 39, 40]. Metabolomics and proteomics enable the identification of metabolite biomarkers indicative of functional metabolic processes that collectively help underpin coral’s health and response to environmental change [40, 41]. Hence, metabolomic approaches have been increasingly applied in coral studies [13, 41–46], revealing metabolites involved in cellular processes, growth, and development [46, 47]. Extensive proteomic work has been conducted in the cnidarian model Exaiptasia, which has provided major insights into host–symbiont interactions and metabolic integration [48–52], as well as earlier foundational symbiosis proteomic studies [53]. On the contrary, coral proteomics research is still limited and has primarily focused on predictive genomic or transcriptomic studies [12, 40, 54, 55]. Very few studies have conducted protein extraction and identification within coral holobionts [6, 13, 45, 56–58], and none have explicitly evaluated intraspecific phenotypic plasticity at the proteomic level.

Color morphs may exhibit differential bleaching responses linked to different GFP-proteins expression profiles [15] and also harbor distinct microbiomes and Symbiodiniaceae photosymbionts, reflecting distinctive associations with specific members of Symbiodinium or bacterial groups [10, 59–61]. Even corals lacking Symbiodiniaceae may exhibit morph-specific microbiomes, as observed in the cold-water coral Desmophyllum pertusum (formerly Lophelia pertusa) [62]. Unveiling further differences in proteomes from different coral morphs and connecting them with other distinctive traits may contribute to understanding the functional mechanisms expressed by different phenotypes.

The zooxanthellate and reef-builder scleractinian coral Pocillopora verrucosa, recently re-assigned as Pocillopora favosa [63] (family Pocilloporidae) represents a good model organism to study intraspecific variation, as it is a common and locally abundant component of shallow water tropical coral communities, displays high levels of phenotypic plasticity [4, 6, 64], and occurs in different color morphs, including beige, pink, and brown [65]. This coral genus has an extensive distribution across the Indo Pacific and Eastern Pacific Oceans [66]. The actual diversity and distribution of species in the genus have been addressed by several studies in the last decade, evidencing a complex situation that has only partially been formalized in systematic revisions [67, 68]. In the Red Sea, Pocillopora is commonly found in shallow water reefs [6, 69], and different studies have found an absence of genetic differentiation of P. verrucosa along the well-described latitudinal gradients of this semi-enclosed and saline sea [70], evidencing the presence of a single species in the basin [4, 63, 69, 71].

Previous studies investigated the bacterial communities (as reviewed in [72]), algae symbionts (Symbiodiniaceae) [6, 71, 73], and physiological responses of this coral to different environmental conditions, including to some extent lipid and protein content analysis, both in the Red Sea [4, 6, 74] and other geographical locations [75–79]. However, the intraspecific differences between color morphs of P. verrucosa are currently unknown as, to our knowledge, no study has addressed them so far. Moreover, available predictive proteomic and metabolic data of this coral has been obtained from genome assembly and functional annotation [54], although no studies have assessed its metabolome and proteome, using high-resolution mass spectrometry untargeted omics.

The Red Sea serves as a natural laboratory for studying coral responses and acclimation strategies amidst extreme environmental conditions unique to this ocean basin, such as high seawater temperature and salinity, surpassing those found in other oceans [70, 80]. This context presents an exceptional opportunity to explore the phenotypic plasticity of corals adapted to these challenging conditions, revealing differential responses among distinct color morphs of prominent reef-building corals.

In this study, we employed a multi-omics approach to investigate the in situ physiological status (assessed through metabolomics and proteomics), bacterial and Symbiodiniaceae communities of two color morphs of P. verrucosa along a latitudinal gradient on the Central Red Sea’s Saudi Arabian coast in October 2022, at the end of the summer season. Our investigation marks the first comprehensive survey of this coral holobiont at the intraspecific level, yielding novel insights into their microbiome, lipidome, metabolome, and proteome. These findings unveil color-morph-specific traits, shedding light on potentially differential acclimation strategies in response to a changing ocean, which could be used to enhance coral health and restoration using microbial-based or other innovative strategies [81–84].

Results

We investigated intraspecific traits of coral color morphs of the major reef building coral P. verrucosa along a latitudinal gradient in central the Red Sea (Fig. 1a). A total of 49 coral fragments of the pink and brown morphs of healthy P. verrucosa (visually assessed with the Coral Health Chart) [85] were collected by Scuba diving in six studied locations (reefs), spanning more than 200 km of coastline between Thuwal and Yanbu, Saudi Arabia, in October 2022. The central Red Sea experienced a widespread bleaching event in 2020, two years before our sampling took place. Monitoring surveys conducted in October of 2020 and 2022 reported variable bleaching impacts across coral taxa, with P. verrucosa among the less affected species in the region [86]. Importantly, we did not observe bleaching in this species during our sampling campaign. Sampling depths were between 4 and 7 m depth (Fig. 1b). Bacterial community and Symbiodiniaceae

Microbial communities (bacteria and Symbiodiniaceae) associated with P. verrucosa color morphs were investigated, to evaluate intraspecific differences at the microbiome level. Notably, the bacterial community structure was remarkably homogeneous between color morphs (Adonis (color) R2 = 0.01654, F = 0.8238, Pr(> F) = 0.544) and remained consistent across locations (Adonis (color*location) R2 = 0.10734, F = 1.0690, Pr(> F) = 0.381). This finding was supported by alpha-diversity metrics (Supplementary Fig. 1), which revealed no statistical differences in diversity between color morphs within each location (Wilcox test, p-values > 0.05). The summary and results of alpha diversity metrics are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Despite the overall homogeneity (Fig. 2a), the species indicator analysis identified 13 and 20 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) significantly associated with the pink and brown morphs, respectively (Multipatt test, p-values ≤ 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2). Excluding the unclassified ASVs (n = 5 for the pink and n = 13 for the brown), eight ASV indicators associated with the pink morph belonged to seven families and eight genera, while seven ASVs associated with the brown morph belonged to six families representing seven different genera (Fig. 2b). Bacterial indicators of the pink morph included families and genera such as Enterobacteriaceae (Salmonella & Escherichia-Shigella), Comamonadaceae (Comamonas), Xanthomonadaceae (Luteimonas), Gemmatimonadaceae (Gemmatimonas), Phycisphaeraceae (Phycisphaera), Burkholderiaceae (Ralstonia), and Lachnospiraceae (Blautia). The brown morph indicators comprised families including Xanthomonadaceae (Lysobacter), Moraxellaceae (Acinetobacter), Beijerinckiaceae (Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum), Nocardioidaceae (Aaeromicrobium), Endozoicomonadaceae (Endozoicomonas), and Lachnospiraceae (Roseburia & Lachnoclostridium). The Symbiodiniaceae community was remarkably dominated by the genus Symbiodinium, from which five different ITS2 type profiles were identified. Post-med ITS2 type profiles were evaluated to account for statistical differences by color overall and within each location. Similarly to the homogeneity observed in the bacterial communities, the two color morphs presented very similar Symbiodinium ITS2 type profiles, and no significant differences were found within reefs (Adonis (color) R2 = 0.01449, F = 0.7235, Pr (> F) = 0.486); Adonis (color*location) R2 = 0.07937, F = 0.7928 Pr (> F) = 0.665) (Fig. 2c). Interestingly, the brown morph had an affiliation with one ITS2 type profile (A1.A1c.A1j.A1cc.A1h), which was not present in the pink morph (Fig. 2d). However, this ITS2 type profile was only found in one studied location (Shark reef) in the brown morph, and absent in the others.

Metabolomics profiling

Untargeted lipidomics and metabolomics were employed to screen apolar (lipidome) and polar (metabolome) compounds in the P. verrucosa color morphs. A total of 3458 and 997 apolar features were detected in positive and negative polarity modes, respectively, with 863 annotated compounds in available mass spectral libraries. These compounds were classified into 15 classes, with fatty acid esters and glycerophosphocholines (GFC) being the most abundant, constituting approximately 25% of the total annotated lipidome. Other major classes included O-phosphatidylcholines (O-PC), ceramide lipids, steroid esters, and dyradiglycerols, while less abundant classes comprised saturated fatty acids (Saturated-FA), retinoids, quinone and hydroquinone lipids, and dihydroceramides, among others (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3Lipidomics and metabolomics profiles of Pocillopora verrucosa. a–b Pie charts depicting classes of apolar (lipidome) and polar (metabolome) compounds respectively. c–f PCA score plots of lipidomics (c & d) and metabolomics (e & f) profiles (detected in the positive and negative polarity modes, respectively) of the pink and brown morphs. Similarly, 1306 and 1199 polar features were detected in the positive and negative runs, respectively, with 300 annotated in available databases, classified across 15 classes. The main metabolites included retinoids, glycerophosphocholines, estrane steroids, and carbonyl compounds, along with carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates, collectively representing over 50% of the annotated metabolome (Fig. 3b). The full list of annotated apolar and polar compounds is provided in Supplementary Table 3a–d.

Analysis between color morphs revealed a homogeneous composition of the lipidome and metabolome in pink and brown morphs of P. verrucosa across the studied reefs in the Central Red Sea (T-test, q-values ≤ 0.05). The overall composition of both color morphs’ lipidome and metabolome remained similar across reefs, with no observed clustering in principal component analysis (PCA), neither by color morph nor by location (Fig. 3c&d; Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, high variation in compound abundance was noted among samples between color morphs and reefs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Given the stability of quality control samples and randomized injection order, the variability observed among morphs and reefs is unlikely to be driven by analytical artefacts and more likely reflects underlying biological heterogeneity.

Clustering analysis via PCA did not reveal clustered patterns by color morphs overall nor by locations (Supplementary Fig. 4). However, 1517 proteins had significantly differential abundance between color morphs (fold change (or ratio) ≥ 1.5, q-value ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 4a). From those proteins, 1347 were annotated in available databases while 170 corresponded to uncharacterized proteins (Supplementary Table 5). Noteworthy, proteins most significantly enriched in the pink morph included five GFP-like fluorescent chromoproteins (GFP), notably amFP486 (Fig. 4a). Additionally, enriched proteins encompassed Pullulanase (Alpha-dextrin endo-1,6-alpha-glucosidase) (Pullulan 6-glucanohydrolase) (bPTLP), several Ankyrin repeat domain 28 proteins (Ankrd28), bacterial universal stress protein (UspA), and Fibronectin type III domain (Fn3). Conversely, proteins significantly enriched in the brown morph included Chymotrypsin-C (bPTLP), Histone H3 (His3), G-protein signaling modulator (Gpsm1), Kinesin family member 2A (kif2a), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RNF213 (Ring finger protein 213) (RFN213), hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 10 (Mhbd), and gamete and reproduction-related proteins such as Spermatogenesis-associated protein 5-like protein 1 (AWC38_SpisGene139) and the doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 3. Other enriched proteins included creatine kinases (Creatine Kinase B, Cpk-B), as well as proteins involved in the negative regulation of the apoptotic process, such as the apoptosis and caspase activation inhibitor (AVEN_104647_1) (Fig. 4a).

The five enriched GFP proteins exhibited notably higher intensity ratios (pink/brown), indicating enrichment (with ratios ≥ 1.5) in the pink morph, while four GFP-identified proteins showed no significant differences between morphs (Fig. 4b) and five were slightly less abundant in the pink morph vs the brown morph. In addition, three GFP-identified proteins were not quantified probably due to low abundance. Amino acid sequencing alignment revealed that all five enriched GFP proteins in the pink morph were clustered separately from other non-significantly identified GFP proteins (Fig. 4c).

Interestingly, seven heat shock proteins, including Heat shock protein Hsp-16.48/Hsp-16.49, Heat shock 70 kDa protein 12 A, Heat shock 70 kDa protein 12B, and Heat shock protein 75 kDa-mitochondrial (HSP 75), consistently showed enrichment in the brown morph (Fig. 4d).

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the differentially abundant proteins (both up- and downregulated) identified between the two morphs (Supplementary Table 5). The analysis revealed various biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and membrane functions (MF) associated with the proteomic profiles of P. verrucosa, which were differentially enriched in the pink and brown morphs (Fig. 4e&f). In the pink morph, significant enrichment was observed in biological processes such as the regulation of complement activation of the lectin pathway and cellular defense response, as well as negative regulation of angiogenesis, cell adhesion processes (including cell–matrix adhesion and extracellular matrix organization), and signaling transduction (Fig. 4e). Cellular components included proteins related to nematocyst cells, the extracellular space, and cellular membrane, among others. Molecular functions enriched in the pink morph comprised fucose binding, extracellular matrix binding, serine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, NAD + nucleoside activity, chitin binding, toxin activity, heparin-binding, serine-type endopeptidase activity, and various functions associated with oxidative stress response (such as scavenger receptor activity and oxidoreductase activity), as well as zinc, calcium, metal, and ATP binding.

Conversely, the brown morph exhibited significantly higher abundance of proteins associated with nucleic acid metabolic processes, including enzyme-directed rRNA pseudouridine synthesis, as well as positive regulatory activity of transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter, DNA recombination, and DNA integration. Others included metabolic processes of cellular macromolecules and nucleosides. Cellular components encompassed the chromatin assembling mediating RCAF complex, the proton transporting V type ATPase, the kinesin complex, and integral membrane components, among others. Differentially expressed molecular functions in the brown morph included transposase activity, N-acetyltransferase activity, S-adenosyl-L-methyonine binding, and hydrogen ion transmembrane transporter activity. Additionally, enriched molecular functions comprised those associated with oxidative stress and antioxidant activity, such as oxidoreductase activity and scavenger receptor activity (Fig. 4f).

Discussion

Pocillopora verrucosa color morphs showed distinct associations with putative pathogenic and putative beneficial bacterial taxa in the Red Sea.

This study revealed intraspecific differences of two dominant color morphs of P. verrucosa in the Central Red Sea, using a multi-omics approach. Our findings showed overall consistent bacterial communities and Symbiodiniaceae photosymbionts in both pink and brown morphs, across the studied reefs. P. verrucosa is considered a “microbiome regulator,” maintaining stable microbiomes, even under environmental stress [88, 89], although recent studies have demonstrated successful in situ reshaping of the microbiome of healthy P. verrucosa using bacterial probiotics [90]. Microbial communities are likely influenced by both host genotype and environmental factors such as temperature [71]. Since the studied reefs in the Central region of the Red Sea had similar environmental conditions here represented as seawater temperature, salinity, and pH (although other environmental variables not measured in this study could influence microbial dynamics), such environmental similarity has likely contributed to the marked stable bacterial communities in P. verrucosa. Despite the overall similar community structure, specific bacterial indicators were found for each morph, indicating subtle microbiome differences. The pink morph was associated with genera that include opportunistic taxa, such as Salmonella and Escherichia-Shigella, previously linked to coral disease and thermal stress [91–94], as well as Gemmatimonas, which includes carotenoid-producing and ROS-scavenging bacteria - which are beneficial traits for coral-associated bacteria [95–101]. In contrast, the brown morph was associated with genera including Lysobacter, Acinetobacter, and Endozoicomonas, taxa reported in healthy corals and encompassing strains with antimicrobial activity and roles in nutrient cycling [102–106]. While functional roles cannot be inferred directly from taxonomy alone, these consistent morph-specific associations indicate subtle but consistent differences in microbial composition that may contribute to distinct holobiont physiological states.

Although the indicator ASVs represented a small proportion of the total bacterial community (0.03% in pink morphs and 0.01% in brown morphs), low-abundance taxa can contribute disproportionately to ecosystem function, as described under the “rare biosphere” concept [107–109]. In coral holobionts, rare microbial members may act as functional reservoirs or respond rapidly to environmental change [110, 111]. Low abundance microbes are also associated with key changes in the holobiont’s phenotypic response to stress, through the use of probiotics [112–116]. Therefore, despite their low abundance, the consistent association of specific taxa with each morph suggests potential ecological relevance.

Symbiodiniaceae communities of both color morphs were dominated by the genus Symbiodinium evidencing the consistent association of this algal symbiont at the intraspecific level in P. verrucosa, in the central Red Sea region. This result aligns with previous studies that found the pervasive dominance of Symbiodinium in P. verrucosa, along the Red Sea [71, 73], which seems an even more predominant association in the Central region, where no particular clustering has been observed when accounting for different reef types (i.e., in-shore, off-shore reefs) or by depth [4, 117]. In general, a low diversity of ITS2-type profiles of Symbiodinium associated with P. verrucosa has been reported, with an increasing number of ITS2-type profiles towards the northern Red Sea reefs [71], reflecting adaptation to regional environmental conditions. It is worth noting that the brown morph presented a unique association with one ITS2 type profile (A1.A1c.A1j.A1cc.A1h) that was absent in the pink morph. However, this was only detected in one studied reef (Shark reef), and absent in all other locations. Hence, this could represent a stable association specific to brown morphs of this particular location, as Symbiodiniaceae associations with coral hosts do not always demonstrate host specificity or stability over time, and may vary upon environmental change [71, 117].

Pocillopora verrucosa color morphs display similar lipidomes and metabolomes.

The untargeted approach used in this study allowed a high-resolution detection of a myriad of metabolites from which the vast majority remain uncharacterized due to a lack of databases or mass spectral libraries, posing a current challenge for coral’s metabolomics studies [41]. The lipidome and metabolome profiles of the pink and brown morphs of P. verrucosa were overall highly homogeneous and did not change across the studied locations. Recent investigations showed marked differences in metabolic profiles between coral genera that varied across locations in the Pacific Ocean, whereas differences between genotypes of the same species were not detected [46]. Another study found intraspecific differences in metabolic profiles of polar and apolar compounds in the threatened Caribbean coral Acropora cervicornis, suggesting that differences in metabolome structure could contribute to phenotypic variation among genotypes in this coral species [118]. The observed homogeneity of the P. verrucosa color morphs lipidome and metabolome provides evidence of similar metabolic responses both intraspecifically and regionally that align with previous findings of the physiological plasticity of this coral in the Red Sea [4]. Nonetheless, broader investigations including even more samples and areas could further investigate any possible subtle intraspecific differences.

Among the major apolar and polar compounds detected, several lipid classes were in high abundance in the P. verrucosa metabolome. Lipids are a key component of cellular membranes and are the main energy reserve in corals [119], playing a central role in cellular functioning, response, and acclimation to environmental stress (e.g., higher temperatures) [120], therefore serving as biochemical and physiological biomarkers of coral health [119, 121–124]. Fatty acids, (FAs) (including FA-esters, and saturated FAs) were detected in high abundance in our samples. Fatty acids are the major lipid compounds in corals [125] and have been well documented [78, 126], including in P. verrucosa [74]. FAs are precursors of the synthesis of other lipids [127], as well as membrane construction, energy metabolism, reproduction, and growth. Corals acquire fatty acids either by heterotrophic assimilation via consuming prey directly, such as zooplankton, or by translocation from their autotrophic algae symbionts [128]. FAs also serve as reliable trophic biomarkers for corals [79, 125, 129]. Glycerophosphocolines (GPCs) were also detected in high abundance in both color morphs. These lipids are crucial for cellular structure and the regulation of cellular processes [130], serve as vital nutrients involved in key metabolic pathways [131], and exert significant influence on organismal growth and metabolism [132]. Tang and colleagues [133] have documented the presence and differential expression of glycerophosphocholine in Pocilloporid corals, emphasizing their roles in maintaining the cell membrane properties and structure, as well as playing an important role in coral growth and cellular aging processes. In addition, retinoids were the major class of polar metabolites, which have several biological functions including growth, differentiation, development, reproduction, vision, and tissue maintenance [134]. In other cnidarians, retinoids (i.e., retinoic acid) are involved in epithelial nervous cell specification in adults [135] and could also be beneficial for corals.

A previous study assessed the total lipid content, wax ester, triglyceride content, and fatty acid composition of P. verrucosa in the Red Sea [74]. Our study further expanded the database of untargeted apolar and polar metabolites, also providing insights into their homogeneous abundance intraspecifically along the Central Red Sea. The fact that the two color morphs did not present significant differences in the metabolic profiles suggests similar functional nutritional strategies and energy reserves.

Proteomic profiles reveal color-morph intraspecific differences linked to stress and immune response

Despite the overall metabolic similarities, comparative proteomics of pink and brown morphs of P. verrucosa revealed significant differences in protein abundance, with over 600 proteins identified. Notably, Green-Fluorescent-like chromoproteins (GFP-proteins) were highly abundant in the pink morph (Fig. 5). GFP-proteins are known for their photoprotective roles against UV radiation and light adaptation [17, 24, 29, 32], mitigating light-induced damage and supporting coral recovery during bleaching [136]. In coral growth and survival, optimizing internal light conditions is key, with pigments like fluorescent proteins and chromoproteins regulating symbiotic activities [29, 36, 37]. Chromoproteins from the green fluorescent protein-like pigment category offer photoprotection to symbionts in shallow water by absorbing light. This photoprotection mechanism assists in reducing the light-amplifying impact caused by coral tissue scattering and skeletal reflection during stressful events such as bleaching, thus aiding in the overall survival of the coral holobiont [24, 32]. Our data provide additional insight into the differential abundance of GFP-proteins in the prominent reef-building coral P. verrucosa, highlighting their significance in environmental adaptation across different color morphs. Different coral color morphs have been previously associated with different responses to thermal stress [11, 136, 137]. Another highly abundant protein in the pink morph corresponded to the Pullulanase enzyme (Pullulan 6-glucanohydrolase) (Fig. 5), a debranching enzyme of bacterial origin, widely used in the food industry for its hydrolyzing activity to break down polysaccharides and oligosaccharides such as starch, amylopectin, and pullulan, into less complex carbohydrates [138, 139]. This enzyme facilitates the transformation of complex polysaccharides with branching structures into less complex sugars. Pullulanases have been identified in thermophilic marine bacteria and archaea associated with deep-sea hydrothermal vents [140], and in metagenomic studies from coral-associated microbiomes and symbiotic zooxanthellae, and are likely associated with enzymatic activity of photosynthetic organisms [141–143]. Hence, Pullulanase enzymes are potentially involved in carbohydrate metabolism in coral holobionts, facilitating the degradation of starch produced by the photosynthetic algae partner Symbiodiniaceae [143]. Therefore, its upregulation may indicate the increase in the availability of Symbiodianiaceae-derived carbohydrates from starch, as a response to fulfill energy demands during environmental stress, such as elevated seawater temperature. Carbohydrates constitute the main energy source utilized by corals during thermal stress [42]. In this sense, studies have shown increased levels of translocation from the photosynthetic Symbiodiniaceae-derived carbohydrates to the coral host during heat stress [144], along with carbohydrate reprogramming in response to elevated temperatures [42]. Nonetheless, it is important to consider that enzyme levels do not necessarily correlate directly with steady-state metabolite pool sizes. Metabolite concentrations reflect the balance between production, degradation, and downstream utilization, and increased enzyme abundance may indicate enhanced metabolic flux rather than accumulation of intermediates [145]. In coral holobionts, Symbiodiniaceae-derived carbohydrates are rapidly translocated and metabolized, particularly under thermal stress, when carbohydrate reprogramming supports host energetic demands [144, 146, 147]. Therefore, elevated Pullulanase abundance may reflect increased carbohydrate turnover without necessarily resulting in detectable differences in bulk carbohydrate pools.

Additionally, a major set of proteins that were majorly enriched in the pink morph was associated with the immune system response (Fig. 5). For instance, the Ankyrin repeat domain 28 (Ankrd28), which belongs to the family of Ankyrin-domain proteins, mediates protein–protein interactions and cell migration [148]. In corals, this protein group plays roles in the innate immune system, activating host defenses, and stress response [149, 150] and cell signaling transduction pathways [151, 152]. Ankyrin repeat proteins are also produced by bacteria present in the coral microbiome [153–155] and have been identified to have roles in bacterial infections of host cells, suppressing the host antimicrobial-response [156]. These proteins can also be used by pathogenic or mutualistic bacteria to modulate the host’s immune response through epigenetic mechanisms [157].

Bacterial universal stress protein was also upregulated on pink morphs (Fig. 5). This protein is widely present in bacteria, and eukaryotic organisms can be upregulated in exposure to thermal stress, and is linked to resistance to DNA-damage agents [158]. In corals, however, their roles are not yet known, but they likely play similar roles in protection against high temperatures and oxidative stress [159]. Fibronectin-like protein (Fibronectin type III domain (Fn3) was also highly abundant in this morph. It has been predicted in Symbiodinium transcriptomes [160] and it has functions with ligand binding and protein–protein interactions [161]. Also, it has been associated with wound healing repair mechanisms [150]. Additionally, it was found in association with mucins-glycoproteins in anemones, acting as a protective barrier against pathogens and large molecules [162].

In contrast, the brown morph presented a significantly increased abundance of serine-proteinases such as Chymotrypsin-C (Fig. 5), one of the main digestive enzymes in mollusks and crustaceans [163] and also found in the digestive cavity of the hard coral Euphyllia ancora [164]. Therefore, its enriched abundance might be indicative of increased digestive enzymatic activity in the brown morph, possibly evidencing heterotrophic activity. In addition, Histone H3, a protein involved in chromatin structure and DNA packing, whose structural changes are associated with dysbiotic states (i.e., bleaching) in corals [165], was also found enriched in this morph. It plays vital functions in the regulation of development and growth in multicellular organisms [166]. It was shown that H3 histones are involved in DNA-methylation regulation in cnidarians, which has important implications in establishing, maintaining, and disrupting the critical cnidarian-dinoflagellate symbiotic relationship through epigenetic means, and therefore might contribute to coral resilience by adjusting transcription in response to environmental stress [167, 168]. Similarly, proteins associated with cell apoptosis processes including G-protein signaling modulator (Gpsm1) and Kinesin family member 2A (kif2a) were found to increase. The upregulation of such proteins is linked to pathobiological processes of several types of cancer in humans [169, 170], and hence, may be indicators of physiological stress in corals, involved in cell apoptosis. Another upregulated protein was the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RNF213 (Ring finger protein 213) (Fig. 5), which plays important roles as an antimicrobial effector against pathogens including bacteria and viruses in a host organism, including humans [171, 172] and recently found in bivalves [173]. Recent studies found that RNF213 induces antibacterial autophagy [174], and is involved in several biological processes and regulatory functions in human diseases [175].

Furthermore, a higher abundance was observed of proteins involved in sexual reproduction and spermatogenesis in the brown morph, such as hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 10 (Mhbd), Spermatogenesis-associated protein 5-like protein 1, and doublesex- and mab-3-related transcription factor 3 (Dmrt) (Fig. 5). These proteins are involved in gametogenesis and spawning in corals and other marine organisms such as fish [176, 177]. The latter has been identified in stony corals (e.g., Acropora millepora; Lophellia pertusa) [178]. It was found that A. millepora had a higher prevalence of Dmrt in adult colonies during October [179]. As samples in this study were collected also in October, the observed Dmrt abundance pattern may reflect seasonal reproductive activity in P. verrucosa in the Central Red Sea, which coincides with documented seasonal reproductive patterns in broadcast-spawning corals [180], warranting further investigation. According to this data, Dmrt protein is differentially expressed in color morphs of P. verrucosa, suggesting potential variation in reproductive or developmental activity. Interestingly, the brown morph had increased abundance of several Heat shock proteins, which are widely involved in defensive response mechanisms against thermal stress [181–184]. Such differential abundance may indicate a different physiological response to cope with high seawater temperature conditions during the summer, which can surpass 33 °C during summer months in the Central Red Sea [80, 185]. The upregulation of heat shock proteins observed in this study likely indicates a protective mechanism for heat stress prevention and cell maintenance [4]. Moreover, beyond stress responses, Heat shock proteins also function as molecular chaperones involved in protein folding and stabilization during periods of elevated protein synthesis (e.g., developmental stages and morphological changes) and cellular activity [186, 187]. Therefore, their upregulation may reflect either enhanced protective mechanisms in response to elevated seawater temperatures, or increased cellular and reproductive activity in the brown morph. As we did not measure growth rates, skeletal extension, or gamete output, we cannot disentangle whether these proteomic patterns indicate heightened stress responsiveness, increased reproductive investment, or a combination of both. Nevertheless, the coordinated expression of reproductive proteins and Heat shock proteins suggests distinct physiological states between morphs during the sampling period.

Gene Ontology functional annotation reveals color-morphs differential activation of cell defense and immune responses

The GO enrichment analysis revealed several BPs associated with cellular defense and immune response that were majorly expressed in the pink morph of P. verrucosa. For instance, the regulation of complement activation via the Lectin pathway and regulation of cellular defense response were the two BPs with major activity. They are associated with the activation of the coral immune response to pathogens and symbionts [188]. The complement activation of the lectin pathway is activated when pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) bind to carbohydrate structures that are expressed in the cell surface of microorganisms such as bacteria, yeast, parasites, and photosynthetic symbionts [188–190]. In scleractinian corals, this immune response plays a major protective role in host defense against pathogenic agents and infections and regulation of photosynthetic endosymbionts and activates a cascade of immune responses that are linked to cell aggregation, phagocytosis and destruction of pathogens, but also recognition and acquisition of Symbiodinium [188–190]. This observed immune response may be related to a defensive mechanism in the pink morph possibly associated with a greater susceptibility to pathogenic infection. It may also be a response to uptake/retain Symbiodiniaceae and maintain the symbiotic balance. This has important implications for understanding susceptibility to bleaching and pathogens. For instance, the activation of lectin-pathways for zooxanthellae recognition and pathogenic bacteria can be affected by temperature, and are reduced at high temperatures (36 °C) in the coral Pocillopora damicornis [191]. An increase in cellular components (CC) associated with the extracellular matrix and extracellular space was also observed in the pink morph. This could increase during inflammatory and tissue repair processes, in corals [192]. This increase could also occur because lectin-carbohydrate binding proteins associated with the complement lectin-pathway are generally found dissolved within the extracellular matrix, playing roles in cell adhesion [193], which was a cellular function enriched in this morph. Other functions included the negative regulation of angiogenesis, which may have a role in thermal adaptation [194], fucose-binding proteins, which play roles in immune-cell recognition and microbial pathogenesis control [195], and recognition of photosynthetic symbionts through fucose-binding recognition in the cell surface of Symbiodiniaceae [196]. Additionally, the enriched quintin-binding activity may be related to a defensive mechanism against pathogens, as chitinolytic enzymes play a role against pathogen-induced stresses [197].

In contrast, biological functions and cellular processes that were enriched in the brown morph in comparison to the pink morph included the synthesis of RNA structural constituent nucleosides including pseudouridine. It is associated with several biological roles for regulation of RNA translational processes, including fidelity of protein biosynthesis and accuracy of translation, rRNA folding and ribosome assembly, [198], and mitochondrial protein synthesis and cell viability [199]. Several other biological processes and molecular functions were associated with DNA replication, transcription, recombination, and integration (e.g., RCAF complex, which mediates chromatin assembly during DNA replication and repair [200]), indicating a major biological activity allocated to nucleic acids’ metabolic processes, and genomic plasticity [201] (e.g., transposase activity) and possibly DNA repair against damage (e.g., UV radiation [40]). The high activity of nucleosomes (nucleosomal DNA binding, nucleosome assembly) that play a fundamental function in DNA repair mechanisms and chromosome structure, being key for DNA replication and gene expression [202], consistent with an overall activation of DNA repair mechanisms. Conversely, given the significantly reduced abundance of GFP-chromoproteins in this color morph, which is known to confer photoprotective and antioxidant roles, it could be inferred that DNA-repair mechanisms are more active. This heightened activity may serve to counteract the detrimental effects of high solar irradiance and temperature stress on coral physiological performance. The increased expression of heat-shock proteins in this morph may be part of its defensive mechanisms to acclimate and thrive under the high temperature and UV irradiance conditions of the Red Sea environment.

Lastly, the enrichment of oxidative stress processes that included oxidoreductase activity and scavenger receptor activity were active in both morphs. This enrichment does not imply identical protein expression patterns, but rather that distinct redox-associated proteins were differentially abundant in each morph (e.g., Thioredoxin-like protein, sulfiredoxin, and peroxiredoxin-4, in the pink vs NADPH:adrenodoxin oxidoreductase and nucleoredoxin and in the brown), indicating distinct redox regulatory pathways between morphs. Redox reactions are fundamental to cellular metabolism and homeostasis [203] and are not exclusively indicative of oxidative stress or bleaching. Nevertheless, elevated temperatures can increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in corals, triggering antioxidant responses and potentially contributing to symbiosis destabilization [183]. Oxidative stress in corals has been documented extensively as a physiological indicator of thermal stress and bleaching [159, 204, 205], which triggers the activation of the antioxidant system, to detoxify the holobiont from reactive oxygen species (ROS) that when accumulated leads to expelling the Symbiodiniaceae and subsequent dysbiotic state [206–208]. The observed enrichment of distinct redox- and stress-associated proteins in each morph suggests differential physiological regulation of oxidative balance under summer temperatures, without necessarily implying bleaching-related damage.

Conclusions

This study provides novel insights into color-morph-specific traits of proteomics differentially abundant profiles, alongside specific bacterial associations and homogeneous metabolomes and lipidomes of two dominant color morphs of P. verrucosa, a major reef-building coral in the Red Sea. Major differences were observed in regard to immune and stress response, as well as epigenetic and DNA repair and replication processes, all possibly related to a differential susceptibility or strategies to cope with pathogens and environmental stress as evidenced by the proteomic data.

The higher expression of GFP-proteins and the pullulanase enzyme in the pink morph shows a color-morph-specific trait, which could be linked to the coral’s acclimation and adaptation to UV radiation and a specialized adaptation in carbohydrate metabolism, possibly enhancing energy availability through the breakdown of complex polysaccharides produced by symbiotic algae, respectively. These traits could be crucial during periods of environmental stress, facilitating photoprotection, energy transfer and utilization within the coral holobiont.

The brown morph was mostly associated with an increased expression of proteins like serine proteases, heat shock proteins, and several nucleic acids’ metabolic functions, including DNA repair mechanisms, which suggest the activation of a physiological response to cope with thermal stress during the summer season in the Red Sea.

These findings provide novel insights into the complex biological mechanisms associated with coral phenotypic plasticity, and offer a valuable multiomics dataset for understanding the potential ecological roles and evolutionary strategies of coral color morphs in response to environmental pressures.

Materials and methods

Coral fragment collection and study sites

Coral fragments of healthy Pocillopora verrucosa of the pink and brown morphs were collected by Scuba diving from 6 reefs (Fig. 1a), from Thuwal to Yanbu (22° 18′ 18.4″ N 38° 57′ 52.5″ E to 24° 00.357″ N 37° 53.093″ E), covering a latitudinal gradient in the Saudi-Arabian central Red Sea coast, in the summer period in October 2022. The two morphs are sympatric and can occur on the same reef [65]. The study was conducted on reefs displaying similar environmental conditions (Table 1). Coral colonies were assessed with a coral health chart in vivo [85], for their natural color pigmentation at the moment of fragment collection and assigned to the pink and brown morphs categories (Fig. 1b). Only colonies that presented a well-defined visible color morph (coral chart categories E4–E6 and C4–C6, for the pink and brown morphs, respectively) and were at least 3 m apart from each other, to minimize the collection of clonal genotypes, were chosen. Coral fragments were collected from four different colonies of each color morph at similar depths between 4 and 7 m per studied site. Coral fragments of approximately 5 cm in length were cut from the tip of the coral’s branch using a plier and stored immediately in individual Whirl–Pak® dark collection bags and processed following the protocol described in Voolstra et al. [209]. The fragment collection was performed in the morning at similar hours (between 9 and 11 am approximately), to reduce environmental variability to exposure to light intensity and seawater temperature. Right after collection on the boat, coral fragments were cut into smaller pieces using sterile gloves and pliers for each color morph, thoroughly disinfected with 70% ethanol and 10% bleach, in between samples, and placed in sterile 5-ml cryovials, that were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for approximately 10 days on board (Fig. 1b). Once in the laboratory, samples were stored at − 80 °C for downstream analysis, without adding preservation buffers. Study sites and in situ physicochemical parameters during sample collection

In situ physicochemical parameters of the study sites

Physicochemical parameters were registered with a CTD multi-parameter sensor, to record the in situ water temperature, salinity, and pH of each sampling site, at the moment of sample collection (Table 1). For all the study sites (except Al Fahal CPV), the environmental data was collected using a CTD (Ocean Seven 310 Plus cast), and for Al Fahal CPV, a Multiparameter CTDs (Ocean Seven 310 Multiparameter CTD, Idronaut) was implemented.

Genomic DNA extraction and sequencing of bacterial communities and Symbiodiniaceae

DNA from coral fragments was extracted using the DNeasy® Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen), with the Gram-positive bacteria pre-treatment according to manufacturer instructions, with the following modification: Coral fragments were cut into smaller fragments and weighed to have approximately 0.5 g of the coral fragment used directly for the extraction. The lysis incubation step after adding the proteinase K was carried out for 4 h, at 56 °C, with a constant agitation of 650 rpm in a Thermomixer (ThermoFisher®). DNA concentration was verified using the Qubit™ dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen™) high-sensitivity reagent and Nanodrop™ 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™). To assess the bacterial and Symbiodiniaceae composition (Fig. 1c, right panel), libraries and sequencing of the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene and ITS2 amplicons were performed in a NovaSeq platform, at Novogene Company, in China.

Sequencing of the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using the universal primers 341F 5′ CCTACGGGNGGC WGCAG 3′ and 785R 5′ GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA TCC 3′ for the genomic DNA. PCR mixtures contained 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 10 ng of the samples’ genomic DNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: a first denaturation step at 98°C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension of 5 min at 72°C.

For Symbiodiniaceae communities, sequencing of the Internal Transcribed Spacers 2 (ITS2) rRNA gene was performed using the primers (overhang adapter sequences underlined) SYM_VAR_5.8S2: 5′ TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGAATTGCAGAACTCCGTGAACC 3′ and SYM_VAR_REV: 5′ GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCGGGTTCWCTTGTYTGACTTCATGC 3′ for the genomic DNA [210]. PCR mixtures contained 15 µl of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 10 ng of the samples’ genomic DNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: a first denaturation step at 98°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 56°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension of 5 min at 72°C.

PCR products of both bacteria and Symbiodiniaceae amplicons were verified and quantified by mixing their equal volume with 1X loading buffer (containing SYB green) and performing electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels. For the library preparation, PCR products were purified using a Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sequencing libraries were generated with a NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat No. E7645). The library quality was evaluated on a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific™) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq platform (Illumina), and 250 bp paired-end reads were generated. The raw data from the sequencing of 16S rRNA and ITS gene-based amplicons are available at the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under the study accession numbers PRJEB75194 and PRJEB75195, respectively.

Bioinformatic processing and analysis of bacteria and Symbiodiniaceae

The DADA2 pipeline [211] was used to process the 16S rRNA gene-based amplicon libraries. In short, the raw reads were decontaminated of phiX and adapter-trimmed using the BBDuk tool from the BBMap suite (Bushnell B, http://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/). PCR primers were then removed from the reads using the cutadapt tool [212]. After performing concatenation of the forward and reverse reads via the mergePairs function of DADA2, the sequences were analyzed under the pseudo-pooling mode by following the standard DADA2 (version 1.22) workflow and by using the SILVA database, version 138.1 [213]. The extraction blank did not present DNA at the limit of detection; therefore, no decontamination step was performed. Analysis of read counts, including calculations of relative abundance of ASVs, alpha diversity, and beta diversity, was analyzed in R studio [214] using the Phyloseq [215] and Vegan [216] packages. After excluding ASVs belonging to Archaea, Mitochondria, and Chloroplast, and further removing singletons, a total of 9454 ASVs were obtained. ASV counts were rarefied to the minimum library depth after generating rarefaction curves using the rarecurve function in Vegan (Supplementary Fig. 5). Alpha diversity of the coral bacterial community associated with each color morph was calculated for each reef using the rarefied ASV counts with the function “estimate.diversity” from Phyloseq, with the default diversity indices (Observed S, Shannon H’, Simpson, and Chao1). Statistical comparisons between color morphs for alpha diversity metrics were calculated by implementing the Wilcoxon test, and previous testing of the null hypothesis for normal distribution of the data (Shapiro–Wilks, p value < 0.05).

ASV counts were transformed to relative abundance before performing statistical comparisons. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS) analyses were generated from Bray–Curtis distances of Wisconsin-square root transformed ASV counts using the vegdist and metaMDS functions in Vegan. The homogeneity of variance was calculated for “color” and “location,” using the betadisper and permutest functions in Vegan, from Bray–Curtis distances and 999 permutations (Betadisperse (color), df = 1, F = 0.7717, Pr (> F) 0.4); (location) df = 5, F = 0.596, Pr (> F) 0.741). Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to determine statistical differences between color morphs (pink and brown) and studied reefs (location), implementing the Adonis function in Vegan, using “color” and “location” as fixed factors, from generated Bray–Curtis distances and 999 permutations. As no statistical differences were detected by color morphs, location, or by the interaction between the two factors (Adonis, p-values > 0.05), we used the Species indicator analysis [217] to determine indicator ASVs associated overall with the pink and brown color morphs (p-value < 0.05), by implementing the function Multipatt in the indval package.

ITS2-type profiles of Symbiodiniaceae were defined by submitting paired fastq.gz files to SymPortal analytical framework [218], which identifies specific sets of defining intragenomic ITS2 sequence variants (DIVs) to define ITS2-type profiles [218]. Results were downloaded and analyzed in R [214] studio (version 4.3.2), using Vegan (2.6–4) [216]. The relative abundance data was transformed using the Wisconsin-square root double transformation and visualized using nMDS from Bray–Curtis distances, implementing the metaMDS function in Vegan. Statistical differences between color morphs overall and per study site were assessed using a two-way ANOVA, with “color” and “location” as factors, previous testing for homogeneity of variance (Levene test, p-value = 0.9685), implementing the functions in Vegan and lawstat [219] version 3.6. To assess for differences between color morphs by reef (location), one-way ANOVA tests were conducted for each of the six studied locations.

Overall, no color morph comparisons were conducted between reefs. Instead, the focus was on evaluating differences within color morphs in each location. While the studied reefs share similar environmental conditions (such as seawater temperature, pH, and salinity), other environmental factors that could influence microbial group differences were not examined in this study.

Extraction of lipids, metabolites, and proteins from coral samples

To assess the metabolic profiles of P. verrucosa, coral fragments of 2–4 cm were processed by macerating the entire fragment in liquid nitrogen, using a sterile mortar and pestle. Approximately 100 mg of the macerated paste was weighed and stored at − 80 °C in a sterile 2-ml Eppendorf tube until the extraction process. We implemented the metabolomics/lipidomics sample extraction protocol from the Analytical Chemistry Core Labs (KAUST) to extract lipids, metabolites, and proteins from the entire holobiont, with some modifications: First, three metal beads (Biospec) (2.8 mm diameter) were added to each sample, and were placed on a grinder (Retch, Germany) for agitation for 3 min at 30 Hz frequency, until the sample was completely pulverized. Right after, 1 ml of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE): Methanol (MeOH) (3:1 vol: vol) solution was added to each sample and placed for 1 min at 30 Hz frequency in the same grinder for homogenization. Right after, samples were incubated at 4 °C on a thermomixer (Benchmark Scientific, USA) at 600 rpm for 45 min agitation. Then, samples were placed on foam floaters in a sonicator bath (Branson, USA) with a mixture of ice and water, for 20 min. Once the sonication was completed, the Eppendorf tubes containing the samples were placed on ice, and 700 µl of water: Methanol solution (7:3 vol:vol) was added, then vortexed for 1 min. Lastly, samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 15,000 rpm to separate the apolar, polar, and pellet phases, containing the lipids, primary and secondary metabolites, and proteins, respectively (Fig. 1c, left panel). Aliquots of 400 µl of lipids and metabolites, respectively, were taken from the corresponding apolar and polar phases in the Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 30 °C for downstream analyses. The pellets containing proteins were centrifuged at 10,000 g to remove any remaining organic phases and metal beads. Approximately 100 µl of protein extraction buffer containing 5% SDS + 2 M urea in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate was added to each sample. They were subjected to intermittent sonication three times with each for 10 s at 4 °C and two cycles of freezing–thawing (between − 80 °C and room temperature) to efficiently extract proteins. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g to remove insoluble debris. To purify the proteins before tryptic digestion, the supernatant was subjected to methanol/chloroform protein precipitation.

Eppendorf tubes containing the apolar and polar phases corresponding to the apolar (lipids) and polar metabolites, respectively, were placed on a speed-vac (Labconco™, USA) for approximately 2 h to completely evaporate the solvents. Right after, 250 µl of acetonitrile/isopropanol 7:3 was added to the lipid samples and vortexed on a bench mixer (Benchmark, USA) for 4 min at 2500 rpm. Samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C. Then, 200 µl of each sample was placed on vials and kept at − 80 °C until running in the tims-TOF Pro 2 (Bruker Daltonics, Germany). A pooled QC containing all the samples was added, by mixing 30 µl of each sample into a vial and mixing well by vortexing for 1–2 min. For the metabolomics samples, 250 µl methanol:water 8:2 v/v was added to the samples and vortexed for 1 min. The samples were centrifuged for 3 min at 15,000 rpm. The pooled QC was prepared in the same way as for the lipid samples. Finally, 220 µl of each sample was carefully pipetted into vials for untargeted lipidomics and metabolomics in the LC–MS tims-TOF.

Untargeted lipidomics and metabolomics by UHPLC—timsTOF MS and data analysis

Samples for lipidomics were analyzed using a Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) at the Analytical Chemistry Core Lab (KAUST, Saudi Arabia). Sample extracts were separated on a CSH C18 UPLC column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters) using a mobile phase consisting of (A) acetonitrile/water (60:40), 10 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid, and (B) isopropanol/acetonitrile (90:10), 10 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid. The gradient used for the analysis can be found in Supplementary Table 6a. The injection volume for all samples was 10 µl, and the flow rate was set at 400 µl min−1. The column oven and auto-sampler temperatures were held at 55 °C and 5 °C, respectively.

Samples for metabolomics were analyzed using a Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) at the Analytical Chemistry Core Lab (KAUST, Saudi Arabia). Sample extracts were separated on a CSH C18 UPLC column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters) using a mobile phase consisting of (A) H2O with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient used for the analysis can be found in Supplementary Table 6b. The injection volume for all samples was 10 µl, and the flow rate was set at 400 µl min−1. The column oven and auto-sampler temperatures were held at 40 °C and 5 °C, respectively.

For both lipidomics and metabolomics analyses, the mass spectrometer was operated in positive and negative polarity modes, with the capillary voltage of the electrospray (ESI) source set at 4500 V and 3500 V for positive and negative modes, respectively. The dry temperature was maintained at 220 °C, while the nebulizer gas pressure and dry gas flow rate were set to 2.2 Bar and 10 l min−1, respectively. The acquisition was performed in a mass range of 100 to 1350 m/z for lipidomics, and 20 to 1200 m/z for metabolomics, using Parallel Accumulation-Serial Fragmentation (PASEF). MS/MS was acquired with a fixed collision energy of 30 eV and 40 eV for positive and negative modes, respectively, for lipidomics, whereas for metabolomics, MS/MS was acquired with a stepped collision energy of 20 to 40 eV, for positive and negative modes. To ensure analytical robustness, the pooled sample was injected 10 times at the start of the sequence to equilibrate the chromatographic system. Subsequently, it was injected after every 10 samples to monitor the stability of the analysis over time. Raw data were processed using Metaboscape software (version 2023b, Bruker Daltonics). MetaboScape T-Rex 4D algorithm was used to extract data automatically from the four-dimensional space, namely, m/z, retention time, ion mobility (CCS), and intensity. The software also correlated the MS2 data acquired with PASEF to each extracted feature. Recalibration of measured masses was performed using sodium formate and the ion mobility data were recalibrated using ESI L-low concentration tuning mix (Agilent Technologies, Germany). Annotation was performed using an in-house MS2 spectral library, commercial (NIST 23, MetaboBase), and public available (LipidBlast, GNPS) mass spectral libraries. Databases (LipidMaps) and fragmentation-based rules tools (Lipid species, Bruker Daltonics) were employed for identification. Data post-processing and statistical analyses were performed using the Metaboanalyst platform (version 5.0) (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca) [87]. Data was processed using the median absolute deviation (MAD) for the variance filter, and the abundance filter was done using the median intensity value. Data normalization was performed by selecting the normalization by median, then log-transformed and Pareto-scaled. Principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were used to detect overall differences in the metabolic profiles between color morphs (pink/brown) and by studied location. A volcano plot was conducted to pinpoint metabolites showing significant variations among the treatment groups. Significant metabolites were identified based on a false discovery rate (FDR) corrected significance threshold (q value ≤ 0.05). Pie charts were generated using the enrichment analysis and principal component analysis graphs (PCA) were generated on the Metaboanalyst 5.0 website and customized in Inkscape (Free version 1.4. Available at https://inkscape.en.softonic.com/mac).

Protein digestion, diaPASEF MS acquisition, and MS data analysis

The dried pellets from methanol/chloroform precipitation were solubilized into 5% SDS in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate with the aid of sonication. The protein content was determined using a nanodrop (Thermo ScientificTM). Approximately 20 µg of proteins were then digested using the STrap approach following the published protocol [220].

Approximately 200 ng of peptide mixture per sample was analyzed using a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer coupled with a nanoElute liquid chromatography system (Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Germany). The sample was injected directly into an RP-C18 Aurora emitter column (75 µm i.d. × 250 mm, 1.6 μm, 120 Å pore size) (Ion Opticks, Australia) using a one-column separation method. An 80-min gradient was established using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in H2O) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in Acetonitrile): 2–25% B for 60 min, 25–37% for 10 min, ramping 37 to 95% in 5 min, and maintaining 95% B for 5 min. The column temperature was set at 50 °C and the flow rate was constant at 250 nl/min. The sample eluting from the separation column was introduced into the mass spectrometer via a CaptiveSpray nano-electrospray ion source (Bruker Daltonik GmbH) with an electrospray voltage of 1.5 kV. The ion source temperature was set to 180 °C and a dry gas of 3 l/min. Samples were analyzed using the diaPASEF scheme [221] consisting of 24 cycles including a total of 48 mass width windows (13 Da (m/z) from m/z 400 to 1000 and TIMS scan range from 0.63 to 1.35 Vs cm−2 (1/K0). The collisional energy increased linearly from 20.01 eV at 0.6 (1/K0) to 52.00 eV at 1.35 Vs cm−2 (1/K0). The scan range for MS and MS/MS spectra was set to 100–1700 m/z. TIMS ramping time and accumulation time were set to 100 ms.

The diaPASEF data were analyzed by the directDIA approach using SpectronautⓇ software (v18.6) as described earlier [222]. Briefly, the MS data (.d file) and coral P. verrucosa proteome sequences (27,439 entries) [54] were loaded into Spectronaut. The default settings for the database match consisted of the following parameters: full trypsin cleavage, peptide length of between seven and 52 amino acids, and the maximum missed cleavage of two. In addition, lysine and arginine (KR) were used as special amino acids for decoy generation, and N-terminal methionine was removed during the pre-processing of the protein database. In the directDIA, data extraction was based on maximum intensity in both MS1 and MS2 spectra with relative mass tolerances of 10 and 20 ppm, respectively. The retention time window of the extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) was set dynamically but with a correction factor of 0.5 to enhance specificity. Biognosys default settings were applied for both identification and quantification. To determine the differential abundance between samples, an unpaired t-test assuming equal variance, group-wise testing correction (Benjamini–Hochberg applied per comparison), and clustering was carried out. Proteins with a fold-change of higher than 1.5 and a q-value of less than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed proteins.

Functional annotation of the candidate proteins was carried out using the web‐based platform of the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) [223] (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/). The differentially expressed proteins (up- and downregulated) were uploaded separately for gene ontology enrichment analysis with the total identified proteins as background. Protein sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega [224] (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo). Figures were generated using in-house scripts written in Python (version 3.9) [225] executed within the Jupyter Notebook interactive environment (version 6.4.8) [226].
