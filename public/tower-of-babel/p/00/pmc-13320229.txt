Mechanistic insights into cellular deformation enable enhanced extensional-flow cytometry for label-free classification and sorting
Authors: Huasheng Zhuo, Tanhe Wang, Jianxin Wang, Xian Jiang, Fan Li, Chengxu Lin, Xufan Si, Chunhua He, Zhiyong Liu, Lei Nie, Yimin Huang, Huaqiu Zhang, Guanglan Liao, Tielin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular biomechanics have emerged as powerful, label-free indicators for stratifying heterogeneous cell populations across disease contexts. However, existing deformability-based cytometry techniques often suffer from limited specificity, low interpretability, and poor compatibility with real-time sorting, limiting their translational utility in functional screening. Here, we demonstrate an extensional-flow cytometry platform for tumor cell profiling and sorting, enabled by a mechanistic reinterpretation of cellular deformation dynamics. Fluid–structure interaction simulations facilitated a mechanistic reinterpretation of strain-induced morphological transitions, allowing deformation phenotypes to be reliably captured and interpreted through image-derived features. Then we developed a novel lightweight detection algorithm incorporating auto-localization filters and a normalized block attention module to enhance spatial precision and morphological sensitivity, and achieved a mean average precision upon 96.8%. The final sorting is accomplished through a fully integrated pipeline comprising droplet encapsulation, electrostatic charging, and voltage-controlled deflection, yielding a sorting purity of 90.2% ± 4.4% while maintaining cell viability above 95%, which approaches the state-of-the-art technology for image-driven, label-free deformability-based systems. Our work establishes a robust and scalable cytometry platform that bridges mechanistic insight with real-time, label-free sorting, offering an interpretable solution for mechanophenotyping and functional cytometric decision-making.

Introduction

Precise cell sorting is essential for biomedical research and clinical applications1–4. From distinguishing stem cell subtypes5,6 to isolating rare circulating tumor cells7,8, this technique has consistently driven progress in mechanistic interrogation9–11, diagnostic therapeutics12–14, and personalized medicine15,16. Traditional stratification and sorting technologies, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting and magnetic cell sorting, rely heavily on biochemical markers17. This reliance introduces labeling procedures, which are not only complex but also ill-suited for real-time workflows18,19. In this context, cellular biomechanics, including deformability and stiffness, have emerged as powerful, non-invasive indicators of cytoskeletal remodeling, metastatic potential, immune activation, and stemness20–22. These biomechanical traits offer a universal, label-free dimension for stratifying heterogeneous cell populations across disease models and species23,24.

Extensive research has laid a solid foundation for biomechanical analysis, yielding important discoveries across diverse cell types and disease contexts25,26. As the field continues to evolve, the integration of real-time sorting capabilities emerges as a critical next step to translate mechanical insights into functional outcomes. Conventional cell mechanics measurement techniques, such as atomic force microscopy27, micropipette aspiration28, optical29, acoustic30, and magnetic31 tweezers, have been developed to probe cellular mechanics with high precision, but these methods are often constrained by low throughput, complex operation, and limited compatibility with downstream sorting modules25. This disconnect between mechanical characterization and functional sorting has constrained the translational utility of deformability-based cytometry. To bridge this gap, there is an urgent need for integrated platforms that combine biomechanical measurement with active sorting, enabling both the identification and isolation of mechanically distinct subpopulations for downstream functional analysis. Fortunately, microfluidics provides a uniquely suitable architecture for such integration32–34. Its inherent scalability, programmability, and compatibility with real-time control make it the dominant modality for deformability-based cytometry35. Indeed, nearly all successful implementations of biomechanical cell sorting have leveraged microfluidic designs36–38.

Within this domain, existing microfluidic cell deformation measurement methods can be broadly categorized into three mechanistic modalities based on flow geometry and deformation mode: constriction-based deformability cytometry39,40, shear flow deformability cytometry41,42, and extensional-flow deformability cytometry (xDC)43,44. In constriction-based deformability cytometry, cells are forced through narrow constrictions, enabling direct quantification of mechanical parameters45. In shear flow deformability cytometry, cells are deformed by hydrodynamic shear forces at high speed46. Both approaches offer valuable insights into cellular mechanics, yet their practical deployment can be constrained by channel design limitations, imaging speed requirements, and potential impacts on cell viability35. xDC offers a distinct advantage. Leveraging homogeneous strain fields, it induces gradual and uniform deformation, facilitating the capture of continuous mechanical dynamics47. However, xDC remains largely confined to imaging-only platforms. Their specialized channel geometries are difficult to integrate with downstream sorting modules, limiting compatibility with established cytometry systems35. Furthermore, accurate interpretation of extensional-flow deformation typically depends on fluid-structure interaction modeling, which complicates calibration and hinders direct application in large-scale functional screening48. At present, cell sorting based on xDC imaging remains unachieved, and the biophysical basis of cell deformation under extensional flow remains incomplete. These gaps underscore a critical barrier to the translation of mechanical phenotyping into actionable cytometric decisions.

Here, we demonstrate an extensional-flow deformability cytometry sorting platform designed for label-free cell profiling and sorting enabled by mechanistic reframing for the first time. By coupling extensional flow with high-speed imaging, the platform enables continuous observation of cell deformation under well-defined strain fields. These deformation phenotypes reflect intrinsic cell states and gain mechanistic interpretability through fluid-structure interaction simulation. To support real-time detection and sorting, the lightweight object detection algorithm is improved by incorporating novel self-localizing region-of-interest filters and attention-enhanced feature extraction. Downstream, the platform integrates droplet generation, electrostatic charging, and voltage-controlled deflection modules to achieve cell sorting. This work addresses key limitations of existing deformability-based cytometry by introducing a scalable, biocompatible platform grounded in extensional-flow deformation mechanics, bridging biophysical insight with real-time, phenotype-driven sorting.

Methods

Cell culture and preparation

In this study, human glioblastoma cells (U87) and normal human astrocytes (NHA) were selected as representative models to evaluate the performance of our microfluidic chip. U87 cells, as the tumor-derived glioblastoma cells, exhibit lower stiffness, higher deformability, and enhanced migratory behavior. In contrast, NHA cells possess a more stable cytoskeletal architecture and greater morphological resilience. These distinct mechanical profiles provide a robust contrast for validating the chip’s ability to discriminate cell types based on biomechanical properties.

U87 and NHA were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% carbon dioxide (CO₂). For subculturing, cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (trypsin-EDTA; Gibco), followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4), and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Before experiments, cells are stained with trypan blue for viability assessment and counted using a hemocytometer. Only suspensions with viability above 95% are used for microfluidic assays. For fluorescence-based identification, U87 cells are labeled with a green fluorescent dye. NHA cells are labeled with a red fluorescent dye, enabling a clear distinction between the two populations during imaging and downstream analysis. For U87-NHA sorting experiments, U87 and NHA suspensions were combined at a 1:1 ratio and subsequently diluted 10-fold with PBS before loading into the microfluidic device.

Data analysis and statistical evaluation

The collected emulsions spontaneously separated into an upper oil phase and a lower aqueous phase containing the released cells after sorting. The lower aqueous phase was aspirated and transferred to hemocytometers for fluorescence imaging and manual counting. Purity was defined as the proportion of target cells among all cells collected in the target outlet. The recovery rate was calculated as the proportion of target cells collected in the target outlet relative to the total number of target cells observed across both outlets.

Sorting performance was evaluated across multiple independent experiments. After phase separation, cell images were acquired from 10 randomly selected regions per outlet. All clearly identifiable single cells in these regions were manually annotated, resulting in 100–120 analyzed cells per outlet. The average purity and recovery rates were calculated across 3 biological replicates, with error bars representing the standard deviation. Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Due to the limited number of biological replicates, the results are presented as mean ± SD without overinterpretation of statistical significance.

Chip design and platform working principle

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the extensional-flow deformability microfluidic chip integrates multiple functional modules into a unified workflow for label-free sorting. In Fig. 1a, the sample solution is introduced into the microfluidic chip, where inertial focusing stabilizes the sample flow and concentrates cells along the central streamline, ensuring consistent entry into the downstream deformation zone. As cells enter the cross-shaped microchannel region (Fig. 1b), the channel geometry generates an extensional flow field that subjects cells to extensional stress. This configuration enables rapid and reproducible deformation as cells traverse the high-strain zone. The deformation responses of U87 and NHA are compared in Fig. 1c. Under identical stress conditions, U87 cells exhibit more pronounced elongation, reflecting their lower stiffness and higher deformability. Although our study does not directly adopt axis-based elasticity quantification, this principle offers a biophysical foundation for interpreting deformation phenotypes. This conceptual foundation informs the design of our image-based classification and sorting strategy. As cells pass through the cross-shaped deformation zone, their mechanical responses are captured via high-speed imaging, and an optimized object detection algorithm processes the acquired images to detect and identify cells. The resulting classification signal is transmitted to a signal generator, which selectively charges droplets in response. At the flow-focusing junction (Fig. 1e), target cells are simultaneously encapsulated into droplets and charged in real time. In Fig. 1f, charged droplets are deflected under a deflection electric field and flow into designated outlets. This closed-loop control architecture supports label-free sorting while preserving cell viability, enabling real-time mechanical phenotyping with direct relevance to functional cytometry.

Imaging and object detection algorithm

As described earlier, cells undergo continuous deformation within the cross-shaped deformation zone, which serves as the primary region of interest for imaging acquisition (Fig. 1b). To support robust and scalable detection, we introduced a self-localizing filtering strategy that dynamically detects this region of interest without relying on fixed spatial coordinates. This is accomplished through an object detection algorithm that simultaneously locates the deformation zone and extracts image patches containing candidate cells. The rationale for adopting object detection stems from the observation in Fig. 1c that cell orientations vary significantly during deformation, with long and short axes often deviating from the canonical x-y alignment. Traditional approaches for axis-based elasticity measurement typically rely on segmentation followed by ellipse fitting, which can be sensitive to orientation and boundary irregularities. In contrast, object detection enables direct localization of candidate cells while preserving complete image context, thereby facilitating downstream analysis without requiring strict geometric assumptions. Implementation details are provided in Section 2 of the Supplementary Information.

To further enhance detection algorithm accuracy, we developed the normalized block attention module (NBAM), a plug-and-play design that enhances discriminative feature learning through normalization-based weighting. By stabilizing the training process, NBAM improves model robustness. Its modular architecture allows flexible deployment across layers and integrates seamlessly into existing convolutional networks without requiring structural modification. To support effective placement, we conducted a hierarchical analysis to identify optimal insertion points for attention modules. Implementation details and experimental configurations are provided in Section 3 of the Supplementary Information.

Results and Discussion

Mechanistic insights into cellular deformation and elasticity

The cross-shaped microchannel is a pivotal structure within our chip, governing key cellular deformation processes. We compared two outlet configurations: a dual-outlet design (Fig. 2a), which retains the canonical symmetry of traditional cross-shaped deformation cytometry (xDC), and a modified single-outlet design (Fig. 2c). The dual-outlet configuration, widely adopted in prior studies, was included to validate simulation fidelity and establish a performance baseline. However, in the dual-outlet configuration, cells may bifurcate unpredictably at the junction, resulting in increased ambiguity in classification. Upon completion of the simulations, a comparative analysis of mechanical deformation under single-outlet and dual-outlet configurations revealed a high degree of consistency. As shown in Fig. 2b&d, both equivalent deviatoric strain (EDS) and von Mises Stress (VMS) distributions exhibit similar magnitudes across configurations. Interestingly, the peak stress values in the single-outlet case were slightly lower than those in the dual-outlet design, suggesting that the enforced unidirectional flow does not intensify mechanical loading. In addition to preserving mechanical equivalence, the single-outlet configuration was found to shorten the duration of particle transit through the deformation zone. The time-resolved curves of EDS and VMS (Fig. 2e, f) further support these observations. Particle trajectories exhibited higher velocities and reduced residence times in the deformation zone. This temporal compression is expected to reduce the time required for mechanical feature extraction for increasing throughput, offering practical advantages for high-speed cell sorting applications. The mean value curves represent volume-averaged mechanical metrics across each particle domain at successive time points, while the corresponding standard deviations quantify the spatial dispersion of stress and strain within individual particles. Notably, the standard deviation curves of VMS and EDS also revealed a subtle difference: particles in the single-outlet configuration exhibited higher intra-particle stress variance compared to those in the dual-outlet setup. This suggests that although the overall mechanical loading remained comparable, the spatial distribution of stress within individual cells may be less uniform under unidirectional flow.

A notable observation emerged from the simulated stress–strain relationship: despite the adoption of a nonlinear visco-hyperelastic constitutive model, VMS and EDS exhibited an approximately linear correlation across the entire deformation range. This result is striking given the inherent nonlinearity of the constitutive formulation. It confirms that, under the present experimental conditions, cells remain within a moderate deformation regime where linear elastic behavior dominates the mechanical response. Another aspect that warrants consideration is the potential for Dean-flow-induced, size-dependent lateral offsets, which may cause cells of different diameters to enter the deformation zone from shifted transverse positions. The baseline simulations assume a centered entry position, which represents an inherent simplification of the flow field. To evaluate the extent to which this assumption may bias the deformability measurement, additional simulations were performed varying both the lateral entry position and particle diameter. The results show that, although off-center trajectories and size-related positional shifts slightly modify the local shear environment, the resulting changes in peak stress, strain, and deformation trends remain small relative to the dominant elastic response. These indicate that the turning-based deformability metric is robust against moderate variations in lateral position and cell size. Sorting algorithms and phenotypic classification

The lightweight detection model was designed for fast and efficient classification by focusing on key feature regions instead of increasing model complexity. Specific layers were fine-tuned to improve recognition accuracy for different detection tasks. In Fig. 4, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) visualizations are presented to illustrate the spatial attention patterns across five layers: Layer 7, 8, 9, 16, and 19. These heatmaps reveal how distinct structural cues in the input data are differentially emphasized by various layers. In Layer 7 and Layer 16, visible and localized activation around cellular contours is observed, indicating effective capture of cell morphology. Rounded boundaries and internal gradients are particularly well represented, suggesting that mid-level convolutional filters have been tuned to detect cellular features with minimal interference from background structures. In contrast, pronounced activation at crossing regions and junctions is exhibited by Layer 8, Layer 9, and Layer 19. Linear intersections and edge discontinuities are preferentially highlighted, reflecting the model’s capacity to distinguish structural crossings from cellular bodies. Notably, focused activation is retained in Layer 19 despite its depth, confirming that specificity is preserved even in later stages of the lightweight design. The Grad-CAM facilitates this layer-wise specialization, through which class-specific gradients are backpropagated to identify regions contributing most to the prediction. By constraining the receptive field and avoiding overparameterization, distinct spatial priors are allowed to emerge in each layer, aligned with its functional role. From a broader perspective, it is demonstrated that feature disentanglement can be achieved without increasing model complexity, provided that layer selection and training supervision are carefully controlled. To enhance detection performance while maintaining computational efficiency, we evaluated four representative attention mechanisms. These include coordinate attention (CA), convolutional block attention module (CBAM), global attention mechanism (GAM), and NBAM. Each mechanism was individually inserted into activation-sensitive regions identified in earlier analysis to assess its contribution to feature refinement and detection accuracy. These mechanisms were not integrated throughout the entire network. Instead, they were selectively applied at key locations to avoid unnecessary increases in parameter count and to preserve model speed.

Figure 5a includes ablation experiments designed to evaluate the layer-specific responsiveness of each attention mechanism. In these studies, attention modules were selectively removed from individual activation sites to observe the resulting impact on model performance. The degree of performance degradation following each removal reflects how effectively the attention mechanism interacts with that particular layer. The results reveal that different attention types exhibit varying sensitivity to specific activation layers. For example, CA and CBAM show strong dependence on early or mid-level layers, while GAM tends to concentrate its effect on deeper layers. In contrast, NBAM demonstrates consistent responsiveness across multiple activation sites. Its removal from any of the optimized layers leads to a noticeable drop in performance, indicating that NBAM contributes meaningfully at each location. This layer-wide compatibility explains why NBAM achieves the highest overall performance among all tested configurations. As shown in Fig. 7a, bright-field and dark-field microscopy images show particle distributions under five inlet velocities. At low velocities (Vi = 0.017 and 0.033), particles exhibit sedimentation near the channel floor and tend to accumulate close to the walls, indicating insufficient inertial lift to maintain suspension. Starting from Vi = 0.033 m/s, directional alignment and partial clustering begin to emerge, suggesting the onset of inertial migration. As the flow rate increases, the focusing effect becomes more pronounced, with Vi = 0.083, 0.166 m/s, and 0.25 m/s producing visibly narrower focusing bands. To provide a deeper explanation of the principle of inertial focusing, a flow field velocity simulation was performed for this structure (Fig. 7b). The velocity distribution map of the microchannel shows a non-uniform flow profile, with high-speed regions concentrated near the channel center and low-speed zones near the walls. The structured geometry induces periodic shear gradients, which are essential for generating lateral lift forces that drive inertial migration. These gradients explain why particles near the walls experience reduced velocity and delayed migration, contributing to the sedimentation observed at low flow rates. To interpret this behavior, the classical inertial lift force expression is considered:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{L}\propto \rho \cdot {U}^{2}\cdot \frac{{a}^{4}}{{D}_{h}^{2}}$$\end{document}FL∝ρ·U2·a4Dh2where ρ is fluid density, U is average flow velocity, aa is particle diameter, and Dh is the hydraulic diameter of the channel. This relationship shows that lift force increases quadratically with flow velocity and strongly with particle size. At low velocities, the lift force is too weak to overcome wall-induced drag or gravitational effects, leading to delayed migration and potential sedimentation. Only when the flow velocity exceeds a critical threshold do particles begin to escape the boundary layer and migrate toward equilibrium positions.

The simulation experiments in Fig. 7c reproduce the experimental trends and provide mechanistic insight into particle behavior at low inlet velocities. The experiments employed elastic rebound conditions for particles at the channel walls, ensuring no energy loss or adhesion upon contact. Despite this, particles near the top and bottom boundaries exhibit significant velocity attenuation. This effect arises not from settling or surface interactions, but from proximity to boundary layers where fluid velocity approaches zero. Within these regions, particles experience reduced hydrodynamic lift and prolonged residence times, which delay lateral migration and contribute to the apparent sedimentation observed (Fig. 7a). The simulation confirms that a minimum flow velocity is required to overcome boundary-layer interference and initiate stable inertial migration. Figure 7d shows that at intermediate flow rates, Vi = 0.04 m/s, particles form well-defined focusing bands, indicating optimal inertial migration. In contrast, Fig. 7e demonstrates that further increasing the flow rate to Vi = 0.05 m/s leads to flow instability and loss of focusing precision, likely due to excessive shear and secondary flow disturbances. Additionally, the focusing position along the channel exhibits velocity-dependent behavior: higher flow rates shift the focusing onset downstream, requiring longer channel lengths to achieve full migration. This observation suggests that optimizing channel periodicity and length is essential for achieving high-quality focusing at elevated flow rates.

Assuming water as the working fluid (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, μ = 1×10−3Pa·s), a particle diameter of 10 μm, and a channel hydraulic diameter of 100 μm, the Reynolds number threshold for focusing onset is typically cited as Re = 10. This corresponds to a theoretical velocity threshold of 0.1 m/s. However, our experiments demonstrate that focusing begins at Vi ≈ 0.04 m/s, significantly below the predicted threshold. This discrepancy can be explained by the structured geometry of the channel, which induces strong shear gradients and secondary flows that enhance lateral migration even at low Reynolds numbers. Recent studies have shown that inertial focusing can occur at Re < 10 in geometrically optimized channels due to amplified lift forces and streamline curvature effects52,53. Additionally, the nonlinear scaling of lift force with particle size (a4) further reduces the required flow velocity for larger particles such as the 10 μm beads used in this study.

As the other critical site for flow convergence and interface deformation, the constriction region measuring 50 μm × 150 μm at the junction between the continuous and dispersed phases was designed, enabling controlled droplet generation across a range of flow conditions. All subsequent experiments and simulations were conducted with the dispersed phase flow rate fixed at 20 μL/min to investigate the droplet formation behavior and charging processes of our microfluidic system.

As shown in Fig. 8a, experimental observations revealed distinct transitions in droplet formation mechanisms as the continuous phase flow rate increased. At low flow rates (25 μL/min), droplet generation was governed by pressure-driven squeezing, producing large and irregular droplets with slow necking dynamics. As the flow rate increased to 50–75 μL/min, the system entered a dripping regime, where interfacial tension and shear stress reached a balance that enabled periodic and more uniform droplet detachment. At the highest flow rates tested (125–150 μL/min), the continuous phase exerted strong shear forces that elongated the dispersed phase into a thin jet before breakup, resulting in smaller and more frequent droplets, though with reduced monodispersity. These regime transitions reflect the interplay between viscous shear, interfacial tension, and flow rate ratio, all modulated by the geometry of the constriction region. Importantly, these distinct droplet formation modes were only observed due to the constriction structure. Without this geometric constriction, the dispersed phase would be continuously stretched into a jet across all tested flow rates, resulting in persistent jetting behavior and loss of discrete droplet formation. In all cases corresponding to the squeezing and dripping regimes (25–75 μL/min), droplet pinch-off consistently occurred within the constriction region. The narrowing segment imposes a localized increase in shear stress and pressure gradient, which accelerates interface deformation and promotes timely droplet detachment. More importantly, the constriction effectively suppresses jetting behavior by modulating the Capillary number (Ca), a dimensionless parameter that governs the balance between viscous forces and interfacial tension. The Ca is defined as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Ca}=\frac{\mu U}{\gamma }$$\end{document}Ca=μUγwhere μ is the dynamic viscosity of the continuous phase, U is the local flow velocity, and γ is the interfacial tension between the two phases. Although increasing flow rate tends to raise U and thus increase Ca, the constriction geometry counteracts this effect by reducing the characteristic length scale of the flow. Specifically, the narrow width (50 μm) of the constriction leads to a smaller hydraulic diameter, which in turn reduces the effective velocity gradient experienced by the dispersed phase. This geometric confinement limits the extent of elongation and delays the transition to jetting. In other words, shrinking the constriction lowers the local Capillary number, keeping the system within the squeezing or dripping regime even at flow rates that would otherwise induce jetting in an unconstrained channel. This principle is supported by prior studies on flow-focusing geometries, which demonstrate that smaller orifices stabilize droplet formation by enhancing interfacial tension dominance54–56. In our system, the 50 × 150 μm constriction acts as a passive regulator of Ca, ensuring that droplet breakup remains discrete and periodic across a broad range of flow conditions. The consistent localization of pinch-off within this region confirms its role as a geometric trigger for controlled droplet generation.

Quantitative analysis of droplet generation dynamics further supports these observations. As shown in Fig. 8b, droplet generation frequency increased sharply with flow rate, indicating accelerated pinch-off cycles. In parallel, droplet area decreased consistently, consistent with enhanced shear-induced fragmentation of the dispersed phase. Notably, both curves exhibited nonlinear transitions, with inflection points aligning with the regime shifts observed in the experimental images. This suggests that droplet formation is highly sensitive to flow conditions and that specific operating windows exist for achieving optimal size and throughput. It is important to note, however, that in the jetting regime (125–150 μL/min), droplet formation becomes less predictable. The dispersed phase is elongated into a continuous jet before breakup, and the resulting droplets exhibit significant variability in size and spacing. As a result, the measured droplet area and frequency in this regime should be interpreted as approximate indicators rather than precise metrics. The loss of monodispersity and the stochastic nature of jet breakup limit the reliability of quantitative characterization under these conditions. Therefore, while the data trends are informative, they primarily reflect the behavior of the system within the squeezing and dripping regimes, where droplet generation remains discrete and controllable.

To complement the experimental findings, we performed simulations of the current density distribution within the microfluidic channel during droplet formation (Fig. 8c). The results revealed localized regions of high current density near the necking zone, where fluid interfaces undergo rapid deformation. These concentrations indicate active charge separation at the droplet interface, consistent with charging mechanisms. The current density exhibits directional asymmetry in its X- and Y-components. Laterally, the X-component aligns with the channel geometry, reflecting flow-guided charge distribution. Vertically, the Y-component indicates transport along the elongation axis, especially near the pinch-off region. These patterns confirm that the forming droplets acquire net charge as a result of conductivity gradients and interface dynamics, particularly under high shear conditions.

Together, these results demonstrate that the constriction structure effectively modulates droplet formation behavior through geometric confinement and shear enhancement. By tuning the continuous phase flow rate, the system transitions through distinct generation regimes—squeezing, dripping, and jetting—each with characteristic frequency, size, and electrical response. The constriction not only stabilizes the pinch-off location and suppresses premature jetting but also enables consistent droplet production across a broad range of flow conditions. Importantly, while this geometric feature strongly influences mechanical breakup dynamics, it does not interfere with the charging process.

Because cellular stiffness can span an order of magnitude across physiological and pathological states, it was essential to establish whether the hydrodynamic loading and imaging conditions used here can reliably resolve cell deformations across cells of varying rigidity. To further evaluate the applicability of our platform across cells with different mechanical phenotypes, an analysis to determine the operational stiffness window of our system was conducted. The operational stiffness window of the platform was quantified by combining finite-element simulations with the experimentally determined imaging-resolution limit. Because deformation must exceed the minimum resolvable axial strain imposed by the optical system, this threshold defines the lower stiffness boundary at which hydrodynamic deformation remains detectable. In addition to the resolution-limited lower bound, the upper bound of the operational stiffness window is constrained by the temporal sampling of the imaging system. Very soft cells can undergo rapid deformation and relaxation, and the insufficient camera frame rate may fail to capture their peak deformation before they exit the junction. This effect can theoretically lead to an underestimation of the deformability of extremely soft cells, but the resulting error does not materially affect the conclusions of this study under the present flow and imaging conditions. Nevertheless, it represents a practical limitation that need warrant further consideration in future system refinements. Under the present flow and channel conditions, the resulting stiffness window fully spans the mechanical spectrum reported for most mammalian cell types, enabling reliable discrimination between soft malignant cells and their stiffer non-transformed counterparts. To further investigate the factors contributing to the observed sorting errors, the sorting results were shown in Fig. 11b, c. From a biological perspective, cell misclassification arises primarily from three reasons: First, our control process is to charge the identified U87 cells so as to sort them out. Due to the cellular heterogeneity of glioma populations67,68, the morphologically regular or weakly deformable U87 cells may exhibit deformation signatures similar to the NHA. Second, our chip is configured with two outlets, so that all particles that are not identified as U87, including debris, subcellular fragments, and abnormally small variants, will be directed to the NHA outlet. This design can ensure efficient sorting accuracy of U87 cells, because most impurities do not enter the U87 outlet. However, during routine culture and handling, U87 cells are prone to generating small fragments and detached particles due to their weaker adhesion and susceptibility to mechanical perturbation69,70. These particles may retain red fluorescence to enter the NHA outlet, which are counted as false-positive events in the NHA purity analysis. These two factors major impact on the NHA purity. Third, fluorescence signal overlap and background noise in densely populated regions may compromise the accuracy of ground‑truth annotation. Beyond these biological and signal‑related factors, engineering limitations also contribute to reduced sorting precision. Physical phenomena such as cell aggregation and multicellular encapsulation within droplets distort the imaging and deformation profiles, making it difficult to resolve individual cell identities. In particular, multicellular encapsulation introduces composite mechanical responses that limit the achievable purity for U87 classification. These constraints are inherent to droplet‑based sorting systems and will be systematically addressed in future work.

Building on these observations, several broader limitations of the current system should also be acknowledged, together with potential directions for future development. One limitation is that current validation is restricted to single cell type pairs, and the generalizability of the method to broader biological contexts, such as leukocyte subpopulations, activated immune cells, or sepsis-associated phenotypes, has yet to be established. Future work will expand the platform to clinically relevant primary cells to assess its applicability across diverse mechanical phenotypes. Another limitation is that the present sorting strategy relies primarily on image-derived morphological and deformation features extracted at a single time point. This constrains the system’s ability to capture the dynamic viscoelastic response of cells, which is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of cellular state and function. A natural extension of this work is to incorporate time-resolved deformation trajectories and construct a multimodal feature space that integrates mechanical signatures with high-content imaging descriptors. Ultimately, linking mechanical phenotypes to downstream biological functions remains an unresolved challenge. Constructing a multimodal cellular information map connecting deformability features to proteomic or genomic profiles would enable quantitative mapping between mechanical states and cellular functional programs. Such integration would elevate deformability-based sorting from a physical separation tool to a mechanically guided biological discovery platform.

Conclusion

This study establishes and validates a label-free cytometry platform for tumor cell detection and sorting, grounded in a mechanistic reinterpretation of cellular deformation under extensional flow. To accommodate downstream sorting requirements, we designed a single-outlet deformation structure to accomplish the extensional-flow imaging. Fluid-structure interaction simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanistic basis of cellular deformation under extensional strain. Building on these insights, an improved lightweight object detection algorithm was developed by integrating self-localizing region-of-interest filters with normalized-based attention modules. Attention insertion sites were pre-determined, and ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate architectural contributions. The resulting model achieved a mean average precision of 96.8%, offering both high accuracy and interpretability. Cell sorting was implemented through a closed-loop platform comprising droplet encapsulation, electrostatic interaction, and voltage-guided deflection. Each module was engineered from principles and refined through iterative experimental trials. The evaluations using mixed glioma and astrocyte populations, each outlet demonstrated specificity: the glioma outlet achieved 90.2% ± 4.4% purity and 83.0% ± 5.3% recovery rate, the astrocyte outlet purity was 72.2% ± 6.1% with a recovery rate of 78.0% ± 5.8%. Cell viability remained consistently high, with pre- and post-processing rates of 96.8% ± 1.2% and 95.6% ± 1.4%, respectively. Time-resolved observation of cell deformation in both normal and malignant cell populations provides a novel observational approach to support and refine existing hypotheses regarding the interplay between cellular mechanics, physiological properties, and genomic expression. This platform offers a mechanistically interpretable, scalable, and biocompatible solution for real-time phenotype-driven cytometry, enabling direct integration into biomedical diagnostics and downstream functional workflows.
