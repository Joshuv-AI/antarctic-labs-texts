Association of Suspected Aspiration During of Out‐Of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest With Development of Early‐Onset Pneumonia
Authors: Karoliina Yli‐Luukko, Paula Mäki, Sanna Hoppu, Piritta Setälä, Joonas Tirkkonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly‐onset pneumonia is a possible complication after out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) resuscitation. Whether or not a suspected aspiration during prehospital care is associated with the development of early‐onset pneumonia has not been studied.MethodsWe conducted a single‐centre prospective observational study among comatose out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest survivors treated in the intensive care unit (ICU). Both prehospital emergency physicians and nurse‐paramedics completed a structured survey on possible aspiration after each successful resuscitation. The primary outcome was being diagnosed with early‐onset pneumonia within the first 96 h of ICU stay.ResultsA total of 55 patients were included, and 11 patients were suspected of having aspirated during their out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest. Patients with suspected aspiration were younger, had a lower Charlson comorbidity index, and their out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest was more often due to non‐cardiac aetiology with a non‐shockable primary rhythm. Patients with suspected aspiration had comparable oxygenation, ventilation, and ventilator parameters to the patients without suspected aspiration. Pneumonia findings on chest X‐rays, CT imaging, and infection parameters were similar across the patient groups. Early‐onset pneumonia during the first 96 h was as common in both groups (91% vs. 68%, p = 0.130). Prehospital suspicion of aspiration was not associated with early‐onset pneumonia in multivariable regression analysis (odds ratio 2.8, 95% confidence interval 0.29–28).ConclusionSuspicion of aspiration during out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest was not associated with early‐onset pneumonia. However, this study was possibly underpowered to detect clinically relevant differences.Editorial CommentThis study presents associations between post‐cardiac arrest and resuscitation early pneumonia and suspicion of pulmonary aspiration at the time of the out of hospital cardiac arrest and first treatment. In this cohort, there did not seem to be a clear association.Keywords: aspiration, early‐onset pneumonia, out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest

Introduction

One of the most common causes of global mortality is out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), with ischemic heart disease being the leading cause of death with cardiac aetiology [1, 2]. In Europe, the incidence of EMS‐treated OHCA is 55 per 100,000 inhabitants, and the survival is on average 7.5% (range 3.1%–35%) [1]. In Nordic countries, the annual estimated incidence of EMS‐treated OHCA has been reported to be between 47.9 per 100,000 inhabitants (Norway) and 72.0 (Denmark), while survival at 30 days ranges between 12.3% and 15% [1, 3].

In Finland, the annual incidence of OHCA is 126 per 100,000, with resuscitation attempted in 51 cases/100,000 inhabitants annually [4, 5]. Mortality at year after OHCA varies from 85% to 91% depending on the type of municipality of the patient [5]. Considering just comatose cardiac out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest survivors, the Finnish incidence of OHCA patients treated in the intensive care units is 13/100,000 inhabitants annually, with a 90‐day mortality of 54% [6].

In case of successful cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), one of the possible adverse events is the development of pneumonia. Pneumonia can be caused either by aspiration of gastric contents during the resuscitation event or as a later complication related to ventilator treatment (ventilator associated pneumonia; VAP) [7]. In post‐resuscitation care the incidence of gross aspiration is 9.1%, and the incidence of VAP for OHCA patients in post‐resuscitation care is approximately 35% [8, 9]. Different definitions for early‐onset pneumonia and VAP exist, and there is no golden standard for differentiating between aspiration induced pneumonia, early‐onset pneumonia and VAP [7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Current European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines do not recommend prophylactic antibiotics to prevent pneumonia, but they can be considered in cases of clear infiltrates on the chest X‐rays [17]. However, the ERC guideline is based on a study that excluded patients with gross aspiration during resuscitation [9, 17].

There is a lack of contemporary data in the incidence of early‐onset pneumonia in OHCA patients [16]. One systematic review found the incidence of early‐onset pneumonia after OHCA to be 24%–65% in the eight studies reviewed [14]. There is no data on whether suspected aspiration during prehospital care is associated with the development of early‐onset pneumonia.

The objective of this prospective study was to investigate the association between suspected gastric aspiration during OHCA resuscitation on scene and later confirmed early‐onset pneumonia in the intensive care unit (ICU).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A single centre prospective observational study.

Ethics

Our study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tampere University Hospital on June 12, 2018 (Approval no: R18100). Although purely of observational design, informed consent from the patient's next of kin was obtained where computed chest tomography was done for study purposes only (not clinically deemed necessary).

Study Setting

The county of Pirkanmaa has approximately 550,000 citizens. The most advanced care is provided in Tampere University Hospital, which is one of the five university level tertiary hospitals in Finland. The hospital has approximately 550 beds and 65.000 annual somatic admissions. The mixed surgical‐medical intensive care unit (ICU) has 20 beds with annual 1600 admissions.

Prehospitally, first advanced life support (ALS) providers at OHCA scene are nurse paramedics, who use supraglottic airway (SGA) (i‐gel) for airway management. If an SGA functions appropriately, ALS continues with the inserted SGA after the helicopter emergency medical service with prehospital physician arrives. Comatose cardiac arrest survivors who are not intubated during ALS are intubated prehospitally before hospital transfer. Prehospital emergency physicians utilise a standard operating procedure for endotracheal intubation, which includes the use of a video laryngoscope and a bougie.

Patients and Data Collection

Data from all OHCA patients in the County of Pirkanmaa, Finland, were prospectively collected in 2022. As part of this study, suspected gastric aspiration during resuscitation was documented by both the nurse‐paramedics and the prehospital emergency physicians to a separate structured study form after each successful resuscitation attempt (Appendix 1). In addition, prehospital emergency physicians described in detail the airway management and possible soiled airway. If either nurse paramedics or prehospital physicians had suspected gastric aspiration, this was recorded as suspected aspiration of gastric content.

In short, all comatose cardiac arrest survivors were treated—and neurological prognostication conducted—according to ERC 2021 guidelines during the study period [17]. This included routine deep sedation (Richmond Agitation‐Sedation Scale −4) for the first 36 h after the cardiac arrest [18]. Since the guidelines were updated from targeted temperature management to temperature control in late 2021, OCHA patients' body temperature was controlled to ≤ 37°C in our hospital during the study period [19, 20].

Detailed data on OHCA survivors' ICU care were collected. This included chest X‐rays at admission and once a day thereafter with radiology reports, inflammatory biomarkers, microbiological cultures and ventilatory settings. Data on ventilatory parameters were collected in detail for the first 36 h of ICU treatment, as during this period all the patients were sedated and mechanically ventilated. Measurements related to oxygenation and ventilation included the first eight blood gas analyses for each patient (routinely analysed at ICU admission, at 8.00 a.m., at 12.00 a.m., at 18.00 p.m. and at 22.00 p.m.), and ventilator parameters were recorded simultaneously as blood gases were obtained. Patients were followed for 90 days. Survival with good neurological outcome, defined as a cerebral performance category of 1–2, was noted from the patient records.

As part of the study protocol, we aimed to conduct early chest computed tomography (CT) for all OHCA survivors. Although purely of observational design, informed patient consent was obtained from the patient's next of kin, because early computed chest tomography was done for study purposes in cases where not clinically deemed necessary.

As this was a pragmatic cohort study, ICU physicians were not blinded to prehospital treatment and were aware if aspiration was suspected by their prehospital colleagues.

Outcome Measure

The primary outcome was being diagnosed with early‐onset pneumonia during first 96 h after the OHCA; meaning a diagnosis of early‐onset pneumonia was set in multidisciplinary rounds including ICU physicians, a radiologist and an infectious disease specialist within 96 h after the cardiac arrest. Chest CT scans were first assessed by two separate radiologists, and then both the CT scans and chest X‐rays were re‐evaluated in the multidisciplinary rounds by a radiologist provided with the clinical context in detail. All radiologists and infectious disease specialists were senior consultants, and ICU physicians were both senior consultants and senior residents.

Secondary outcomes were related to radiological findings, inflammatory markers, severity of illness scores, and respiratory parameters during the first 96 h. A cerebral performance category of 1–2 at 90 days after OHCA defined a good neurological recovery.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients under 18 years of age, patients with treatment limitations at ICU admission, and patients in involuntary treatment (meaning convicts and psychiatric patients under involuntary treatment) were excluded. OHCA patients who woke up immediately after the return of spontaneous circulation and required no airway management were also excluded.

Statistics

Data are presented as counts (percentages) and continuous variables as medians (Q1, Q3). The Chi–square test and the Mann–Whitney U‐test were used for the comparisons between groups. No sample size calculations were conducted. Based on previous data on the incidence of ICU‐treated OHCA patients and 20‐year‐old data on the incidence of suspected aspiration of gastric content prehospitally in our region, we estimated that a one‐year prospective study would include 50–60 ICU‐treated comatose OHCA patients, of whom 15–20 would have had aspiration suspected during their prehospital care [5, 6, 21]. A multivariable logistic regression model using the ‘enter’ method was used to investigate possible independent associations of suspected aspiration with later early‐onset pneumonia. Bag valve mask (BVM) ventilation only during resuscitation and non‐cardiac aetiology for OHCA were introduced as confounding factors. BVM ventilation only, as compared with intubation during resuscitation, has been shown to increase the risk of aspiration, thus introducing a possible confounding factor [22]. Similarly, common non‐cardiac OHCA aetiologies, such as hypoxemia resulting from primary airway/pulmonary problems, frequently lead to non‐shockable rhythms, making it a possible confounding factor [23]. Two‐sided p‐values are reported. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals are reported where appropriate. The SPSS Statistics software (version 27 for Windows, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used.

In 11 patients, the nurse‐paramedics and/or the prehospital emergency physician suspected gastric aspiration during the resuscitation. The prehospital suspicion of gastric content was induced by ‘gastric content leaking out of mouth during resuscitation’ in four cases and ‘gastric content observed in larynx during airway management’ in seven cases.

Patients with suspected aspiration were younger (median age 47 vs. 69 years, p = 0.012), had lower Charlson comorbidity index (0.0 vs. 1.0, p = 0.002) and clinical frailty scale (2.0 vs. 3.0, p = 0.002). Their cardiac arrests were more often due to non‐cardiac aetiologies (54% vs. 9%, p = 0.002) and the primary rhythm was more often non‐shockable (73% vs. 34%, p = 0.038). Patients with suspected aspiration were more often intubated during the resuscitation attempt (18% vs. 2.3%), but this difference was not statistically significant.

Antibiotics were more often prescribed at ICU admission for those patients with suspected aspiration.

Discussion

Prehospital suspected aspiration during OHCA by the prehospital emergency physicians and nurse‐paramedics was not associated with being diagnosed with early‐onset pneumonia. However, a statistically non‐significant trend towards association between suspected aspiration and the development of early‐onset pneumonia was observed in multiple secondary parameters, such as positive fiberoptic bronchoscopy finding for aspiration at ICU admission, subsequent radiological findings of early‐onset pneumonia, as well as positive sputum and blood cultures. Thus, this single centre prospective study may have been underpowered to detect clinically relevant differences between the groups.

Aspiration of gastric content was more often suspected among the patients with non‐cardiac reasons for OHCA, and their primary rhythms were more often non‐shockable. In line with this, endotracheal intubation during the resuscitation seemed to be more frequent among the patients with suspected aspiration. Higher body mass index was not associated with suspected aspiration. On the contrary, there was a trend towards lower body mass index among the patients with suspected aspiration, although obesity is a known risk factor for complications in airway management [24].

One previous Finnish study found the incidence of regurgitation signs during out‐of‐hospital resuscitation to be the same (20%) as in our study. In this study, the writers compared the prehospital suspicion of aspiration of gastric contents just to the early chest X‐ray findings. They found that 46% of the patients with suspected aspiration had chest X‐rays suggestive of early‐onset/aspiration pneumonia, whereas 19% of the patients without suspicion of aspiration had early‐onset/aspiration pneumonia in their chest X‐rays. These findings are in line with ours regarding the plain chest X‐ray findings, taking into account that most patients in this study had only one chest X‐ray analysed [21].

The overall incidence of early‐onset pneumonia was over 70%, which is higher than the average overall incidence of early‐onset pneumonia after OHCA in the previous studies [8, 9, 14, 16]. However, we included all comatose cardiac arrest survivors, and the different inclusion/exclusion criteria in the previous studies make further comparisons fragile. Further, due to the lack of a clear definition for early‐onset pneumonia/VAP, previous studies have used different development timelines for their diagnostic criteria, ranging from 3 to 7 days [7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Perhaps one French study, which found the incidence of early‐onset pneumonia within the first three ICU days to be 65%, provides the best comparison with our data [7]. Moreover, they used multiple indicators to establish the early‐onset pneumonia diagnosis, such as radiological findings and micro‐organism cultures [7]. Notably, however, they had more aggressive hypothermia management, with the target temperature being from 32°C to 34°C—a known risk factor for the development of early‐onset pneumonia [7, 14, 25].

Thus, while our current data seems somewhat comparable to the previous literature regarding both the incidence of suspected aspiration of gastric contents prehospitally and the incidence of early‐onset pneumonia after OHCA, our hypothesis that these two factors are associated was not confirmed. Considering the non‐significant trends in the primary outcome and many secondary outcomes, however, it is possible that the prehospital suspicion of gastric aspiration is associated with being diagnosed with early‐onset pneumonia. Fully comparable parameters related to oxygenation, ventilation, ventilator settings and patient survival suggest that the possible higher incidence of early‐onset pneumonia did not impact patient outcome.

The strengths of this study are the prospective design, beforehand planned recording process of airway management and possible signs of gastric aspiration, multidisciplinary definition of early‐onset pneumonia, systematic chest CT imaging and meticulous ICU‐level data gathering. However, our study has several limitations. Due to the pragmatic study setting, the ICU physicians were not blinded from the prehospital suspicion of aspiration. Single hospital district and thus single EMS system left the patient cohort size small. Thus, type two error appears probable, with multiple results indicating an association between prehospital suspicion of aspiration and later diagnosis of early‐onset pneumonia. We suggest this study provides data on a clinically relevant question that warrants further, larger prospective trials.

Conclusion

Suspicion of aspiration during OCHA was not associated with later diagnosis of early‐onset pneumonia. However, this study was possibly underpowered to detect clinically relevant differences.
