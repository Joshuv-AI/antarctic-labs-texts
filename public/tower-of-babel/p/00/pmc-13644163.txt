Risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure in tubo-ovarian abscess: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Minmin Zhao, Rong Chen, Liting Fan, Haiyan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) is a severe complication of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). Conservative antibiotic therapy is the preferred first-line treatment; however, treatment failure necessitates surgical intervention in a subset of patients. This study aimed to identify risk factors associated with antibiotic treatment failure in TOA through a systematic review and meta-analysis, thereby providing evidence for clinical decision-making.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library and Web of Science to identify controlled studies on risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure in TOA published between database inception and December 2025. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of the literature. Mean differences (MDs) or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Q test.ResultsThirteen studies involving 1,759 patients were included. The meta-analysis revealed that patients in the antibiotic treatment failure group were significantly older (MD = 3.61 years, P < 0.0001) and had significantly larger abscess diameters (MD = 18.43 mm, P < 0.0001) than those in the success group. Furthermore, higher gravidity (MD = 0.59, P = 0.0001) and parity (MD = 0.32, P = 0.002) were identified as significant risk factors for treatment failure. No statistically significant associations were found between treatment failure and history of smoking, history of PID, intrauterine device use, palpable pelvic mass or positive pathogen culture.ConclusionAdvanced age, larger abscess diameter and multiparity are potential risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure in TOA. For such high-risk patients, more aggressive monitoring or early combined minimally invasive drainage strategies are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04534-8.

Introduction

Tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) represents one of the most severe and potentially life-threatening late complications of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), primarily characterised by acute purulent inflammation and inflammatory mass formation involving the fallopian tube and ovary [1]. It has been reported that the incidence of TOA is approximately 15%–35% among patients hospitalised for PID [2]. Tubo-ovarian abscess not only manifests as an acute abdomen with symptoms such as high fever, severe pelvic pain and signs of peritoneal irritation but may also lead to abscess rupture if not managed promptly or appropriately. Rupture can subsequently precipitate diffuse peritonitis, septic shock and even death, with mortality rates reaching 5%–10% in cases of ruptured abscesses [3]. Furthermore, the long-term impact of TOA on female reproductive health cannot be overlooked, as it may result in adverse outcomes, including chronic pelvic pain, destruction of tubal anatomy, ectopic pregnancy and secondary infertility [4, 5].

Currently, the therapeutic strategy for TOA is primarily determined by the patient’s clinical severity and abscess characteristics. For the majority of patients who are haemodynamically stable and show no signs of abscess rupture, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic therapy combined with close clinical monitoring is recognised as the standard first-line treatment [1, 6]. This conservative strategy aims to control infection and reduce inflammation, thereby avoiding surgical trauma and maximising preservation of ovarian function and fertility potential. Existing data indicate that the success rate of antibiotic conservative treatment alone ranges from 70% to 87% [7]. However, a considerable proportion of patients (approximately 25%–30%) respond poorly to antibiotic therapy, manifesting as persistent high fever, worsening abdominal pain or enlargement of the abscess volume, which is defined as ‘antibiotic treatment failure’ [8]. For these patients, conversion to invasive intervention, such as percutaneous/transvaginal drainage, laparoscopic exploration or laparotomy, is often required. Notably, due to the frequent presence of pelvic tissue congestion, oedema and severe adhesions at this stage, delayed surgical intervention not only significantly increases surgical difficulty and the risk of complications (e.g. bowel or ureteral injury) but also greatly elevates the likelihood of necessitating salpingo-oophorectomy or even total hysterectomy, causing irreversible damage to the fertility of women of reproductive age [8].

Therefore, early identification of high-risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure is crucial for optimising the stratified management of patients with TOA. If populations at high risk of antibiotic treatment failure can be accurately screened based on clinical characteristics at the time of admission, clinicians can promptly adopt more aggressive interventions (e.g. early minimally invasive drainage) rather than miss the optimal window during ineffective antibiotic therapy. Although multiple observational studies have previously explored potential factors influencing TOA treatment outcomes, such as advanced age, large abscess volume, elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], white blood cell count) and intrauterine device (IUD) use [9–11], the conclusions across studies remain inconsistent or even contradictory. For example, regarding whether IUD use increases the risk of antibiotic treatment failure, different studies have yielded diametrically opposite conclusions; similarly, there is currently a lack of consensus on the impact of a history of PID and parity on prognosis. Moreover, the majority of existing studies are single-centre, small-sample retrospective analyses with limited statistical power, making it difficult to provide high-level evidence-based medical guidance.

In view of this, the present study comprehensively synthesises existing literature evidence using rigorous systematic review and meta-analysis methods to quantitatively evaluate the strength of the association of demographic characteristics (e.g. age), lifestyle factors (e.g. smoking), past obstetric and gynaecologic history (e.g. parity, history of PID, IUD use) and clinical imaging features (e.g. abscess diameter) with antibiotic treatment failure in TOA. The study aims to identify reliable predictors to provide a scientific basis for clinicians to formulate individualised treatment decisions, thereby improving the cure rate of TOA and reducing the risk of unnecessary surgical trauma and related complications.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement [12], a systematic search was conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library and Embase. The search period extended from database inception to 10 December 2025. The literature search strategy included the following keywords: ‘tubo-ovarian abscess’, ‘abscess’, ‘antibiotic therapy’, ‘failure’, ‘surgery’ and ‘surgical therapy’. The detailed literature search strategy is provided in the Supplementary File. Additionally, to expand the scope of the literature search, target literature was further identified by reviewing the reference lists of the included studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study participants were female patients with a confirmed diagnosis of TOA (diagnostic criteria were required to be based on clinical presentation [e.g. fever, pelvic pain] combined with imaging examination [ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging] or surgical exploration); (2) exposure/intervention was patient groups initially receiving conservative antibiotic therapy (including antibiotics alone or antibiotics combined with minimally invasive drainage) but defined as treatment failure (generally defined as lack of symptom improvement, clinical deterioration, abscess rupture or the need for conversion to surgical treatment); (3) the comparator was patient groups initially receiving conservative antibiotic therapy and achieving treatment success (no need for surgery, clinical cure); (4) outcomes included data on at least one risk factor associated with treatment failure or providing data available for calculating effect sizes (e.g. odds ratio [OR], relative risk or raw contingency table data); (5) studies published in English or Chinese in peer-reviewed journals, with a study design of observational studies, including cohort studies, case–control studies and secondary analyses of randomised controlled trials.

The exclusion criteria included (1) patients with PID alone without abscess formation or special populations (e.g. pregnant women, patients with pelvic malignancies); (2) patients proceeding directly to surgical treatment upon diagnosis (without an attempt at conservative antibiotic therapy); (3) conference papers, case reports, systematic reviews and other study types; (4) studies for which the full text could not be obtained or key data were missing and could not be acquired by contacting the authors; and (5) duplicate publications.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initial screening was conducted by reading the titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reading of studies that potentially met the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement between the two researchers, the opinion of a third researcher was sought, and a discussion was held to reach a consensus. Upon completion of the literature screening, the two researchers independently extracted data using a standardised data extraction form. The extracted information included basic study characteristics, baseline data of study participants, interventions and effect-size data for each potential risk factor. For studies that did not directly report effect sizes but provided raw contingency table data, the raw values were extracted for subsequent calculation. If key data were missing, attempts were made to contact the corresponding author to obtain them.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [13] was used to assess the quality of observational studies. This scale evaluates eight items across three categories, including the representativeness of the study population, the comparability between groups, the adequacy of outcome assessment, the sufficiency of follow-up time and the integrity of follow-up. The maximum score is 9 points; a total score of 7 points or higher indicates high-quality literature, whereas a score of 3 points or lower indicates low-quality articles. Two researchers independently conducted the literature quality assessment. In cases of disagreement between the two researchers, the opinion of a third researcher was sought, and a discussion was held to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 software. Effect sizes for dichotomous data were expressed as ORs, and for continuous data as mean differences (MDs), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) used to estimate the effect-size range. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Q test. If I2 < 50% or P > 0.1, the included literature was considered homogeneous, and a fixed-effects model was used for analysis; if I2 >5 0% or P ≤ 0.1, the homogeneity among included studies was considered poor, and a random-effects model was used for analysis. If heterogeneity was significant, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to explore its sources. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s test and Egger’s test. Unless otherwise specified, the significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics and quality of included studies

In the initial systematic search, a total of 3,392 relevant literature records were identified from the databases. After excluding 411 duplicate records and 2,938 studies that clearly did not meet the research topic or design requirements, 43 articles were retained for the full-text eligibility assessment phase. Ultimately, 13 studies met al.l criteria [8–11, 14–22] and were included in the quantitative synthesis of this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study selection

The basic characteristics of the 13 included studies are detailed in Table 1. All studies were observational, with publication years ranging from 2003 to 2025. The geographic distribution covered multiple countries across Asia (Israel, Turkey, Japan, Singapore), Europe (the UK), Oceania (New Zealand), and North America (the USA). Study sample sizes ranged from 50 to 450 cases, with a total sample size of 1,759 patients diagnosed with TOA who received initial conservative antibiotic therapy. The age range of the patients was 31.5–46 years. Antibiotic treatment regimens were predominantly intravenous administration; common regimens included clindamycin combined with gentamicin, ceftriaxone combined with doxycycline and metronidazole or broad-spectrum antibiotic monotherapy. For patients who failed initial antibiotic therapy, the surgical interventions employed were diverse, primarily including transvaginal or image-guided abscess drainage and laparoscopic or open surgery (e.g. abscess drainage, salpingo-oophorectomy, adnexectomy or total hysterectomy). This study utilised the NOS scale to assess the quality of the included literature (Supplementary Table 1). All included studies were of high quality, with no significant publication bias (P values for Begg’s and Egger’s tests > 0.05).

Demographic and lifestyle factors

A pooled analysis was performed on age data reported in 13 studies (Fig. 2). The results showed that patients who achieved antibiotic treatment success were significantly younger than those who failed antibiotic therapy and required conversion to surgery (MD: −3.61; 95% CI: −5.23, − 1.99; P < 0.0001). This indicates that age may be an important factor influencing antibiotic treatment failure in patients with TOA, with younger patients potentially having a higher success rate with conservative treatment. However, heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 80%), suggesting that the strength of the association between age and treatment outcome varied across studies, which may be attributable to differences in population characteristics, treatment regimens or study designs. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis did not identify significant sources of heterogeneity or significant changes in the pooled effect size.

A pooled analysis was conducted on four studies reporting patients’ smoking history (Fig. 3). The results showed that the proportion of smokers in the antibiotic treatment success group was lower than that in the antibiotic treatment failure group (pooled OR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.40, 1.10; P = 0.11; I2 = 0%), but the difference was not statistically significant. This result suggests that within the studies included in this analysis, a history of smoking may not be a significant predictor of antibiotic treatment failure in TOA. However, due to the limited number of included studies, this conclusion requires further verification through more high-quality studies.

Reproductive and gynaecologic history

A pooled analysis of seven studies (Fig. 4) showed that the number of previous pregnancies in patients with antibiotic treatment success was significantly lower than in those who failed treatment and required surgical intervention, with an MD of − 0.59 (95% CI: −0.90, − 0.29; P = 0.0001). This result was statistically significant, suggesting that lower gravidity may be a protective factor for the success of conservative antibiotic therapy. Furthermore, the heterogeneity analysis results did not show significant heterogeneity.

Meta-analysis results based on eight studies (Fig. 5) indicated that parity in the treatment success group was also significantly lower than that in the treatment failure group (MD = − 0.32; 95% CI: −0.53, − 0.12; P = 0.002). This finding is consistent with the gravidity analysis results, collectively suggesting that nulliparity or lower parity may be associated with a higher success rate of antibiotic therapy, reflecting the potential impact of reproductive history on disease prognosis.

Seven studies reported the impact of a history of PID (Fig. 6). Pooled analysis showed that the proportion of patients with a history of PID in the antibiotic treatment success group was lower than that in the antibiotic treatment failure group (pooled OR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.22, 1.44; P = 0.23); however, this difference was not statistically significant, and a history of PID may be unrelated to antibiotic treatment failure. Sensitivity analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed that after excluding one study [17], heterogeneity decreased to 25%, but the pooled effect size was not significantly altered (OR = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.73–1.36).

Pooled analysis of seven studies (Fig. 7) showed that IUD use might reduce the risk of antibiotic treatment failure (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.58, 1.06). However, this difference was not statistically significant, and IUD use may not be a critical risk factor leading to antibiotic treatment failure.

Clinical manifestations

A pooled analysis of maximum abscess diameter data reported in 12 studies (Fig. 8) showed that abscess size in patients with antibiotic treatment success was significantly smaller than in patients who failed treatment and required surgical intervention (MD: −18.43; 95% CI: −22.44, − 14.43). This result was statistically significant, suggesting that smaller abscess volume is a consistent and reliable predictor of success with conservative antibiotic therapy on clinical imaging. Sensitivity analysis did not identify significant sources of heterogeneity or significant changes in the pooled effect size, indicating good robustness of the analysis results.

Forest plot of the association between abscess size (mm) and antibiotic treatment failure in patients with tubo-ovarian abscess

Meta-analysis results from three studies (Fig. 9) indicated that the proportion of patients with palpable pelvic masses on physical examination in the antibiotic treatment success group was lower than in the antibiotic treatment failure group (pooled OR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.02, 3.13). However, due to the limited number of included studies, this difference was not statistically significant, and further verification through more high-quality studies is required. Sensitivity analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 2) showed that after excluding one study [17], heterogeneity decreased to 46%, but the pooled effect size was not significantly altered (OR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.39–2.04).

Meta-analysis results based on four studies (Fig. 10) indicated that the proportion of patients with positive pathogen culture from abscess specimens in the antibiotic treatment success group was markedly lower than that in the antibiotic treatment failure group (pooled OR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.29, 1.90). Sensitivity analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed that after excluding one study [8], heterogeneity decreased to 46%, and the results for pooled effect size were OR = 0.62 and 95% CI = 0.40–0.97. However, due to the limited number of included studies, this difference warrants further verification with additional high-quality studies.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 13 observational studies that reported on 1,759 female patients diagnosed with TOA, aiming to comprehensively evaluate potential risk factors for failure of conservative antibiotic therapy. Through quantitative synthesis of multidimensional data, including demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, obstetric and gynaecologic history and laboratory tests, this study found that advanced age, larger abscess diameter and higher gravidity and parity are associated with antibiotic treatment failure and subsequent conversion to surgical intervention. Conversely, no statistically significant associations were found between treatment failure and smoking history, history of PID, IUD use, palpable pelvic mass on physical examination or positive pathogen culture. These findings provide an important evidence-based medical reference for clinicians to identify high-risk patients early and optimise treatment decisions.

Among all risk factors identified in this study, the impact of abscess volume was the most significant and consistent. Meta-analysis results showed that the mean abscess diameter was significantly smaller in the antibiotic treatment success group than in the failure group (MD: −18.43 mm). This result holds high statistical and clinical significance, and sensitivity analysis confirmed its robustness. Analysing from pathophysiological and pharmacokinetic perspectives, an abscess is a purulent cavity encapsulated by fibrin, with an internal environment characterised by low pH, low oxygen tension and high protein binding rates; this not only reduces the activity of antibiotics such as aminoglycosides but also impedes drug penetration through the thick abscess wall [23]. As the abscess diameter increases, its surface-area-to-volume ratio decreases, and the difficulty of antibiotic penetration to the centre increases exponentially, making it difficult to achieve effective minimum inhibitory concentrations [24]. Furthermore, large abscesses are frequently associated with more severe surrounding tissue adhesions and anatomical distortion, rendering complete resolution via drug absorption alone extremely difficult [25]. Therefore, our study results support the current clinical consensus that for abscesses with larger diameters (e.g. ≥5.5 cm), combined minimally invasive drainage (such as ultrasound- or CT-guided aspiration) should be considered at the initiation of antibiotic therapy to reduce bacterial load and breach the drug penetration barrier, thereby improving cure rates [4, 7]. Current evidence supports that for large abscesses in which antibiotic penetration is limited, early minimally invasive drainage – such as ultrasound- or CT-guided aspiration – serves as a pivotal intermediate strategy between conservative medical management and radical surgery [26]. By mechanically reducing the purulent volume and bacterial load, this approach facilitates better antibiotic efficacy and alleviates local pressure symptoms, potentially preventing abscess rupture. Consequently, consistent with recent clinical recommendations, for patients identified with significant risk factors, such as large abscess diameter or failure to improve within 48–72 h, prompt image-guided drainage should be considered to optimise outcomes while minimising surgical morbidity [4, 7].

This study found that age is another key factor influencing TOA outcomes. The mean age of patients in the treatment success group was significantly lower than that in the failure group (MD: −3.61 years). This finding may be related to multiple biological mechanisms. First, with advancing age, the immune system function gradually declines (immunosenescence), leading to a decreased ability to clear infections and resulting in prolonged inflammatory processes [27]. Furthermore, recent evidence underscores that dysregulation of inflammatory dynamics, characterised by persistent elevation of systemic markers and impaired resolution pathways, is significantly associated with disease severity and poor therapeutic response [28]. This aligns with the concept of immunosenescence, in which the ageing immune system fails to clear infections effectively, leading to prolonged inflammatory processes that perpetuate tissue damage and antibiotic resistance. Second, the microcirculation in pelvic tissues of older women may be poorer than in younger women, affecting the local tissue distribution concentration of antibiotics. Additionally, older patients often have a higher proportion of chronic comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension; these comorbid states may further weaken the physiological reserve capacity of the body to combat severe infections [29]. Although the heterogeneity of age data in our analysis was relatively high, suggesting potential differences in age distribution across study populations, the overall trend was highly consistent. This suggests that for perimenopausal or older patients with TOA, clinicians should maintain higher vigilance, and initial treatment regimens should be more aggressive. Future research should prioritise developing comprehensive predictive models that integrate multiple clinical variables, high-resolution imaging features and dynamic laboratory markers. Such multidimensional algorithms would enable more precise risk stratification and guide individualised treatment strategies, moving beyond the limitations of single-factor associations to optimise patient outcomes.

An interesting, less well-explored finding of this study is that increases in gravidity (MD: −0.59) and parity (MD: −0.32) are significantly associated with an increased risk of antibiotic treatment failure. While clinical evidence in this area remains sparse, several hypothetical anatomical and physiological mechanisms might be considered. Recurrent pregnancies and deliveries could potentially induce cumulative structural alterations in the pelvic environment, such as pelvic varicosities, ligamentous laxity, or subtle modifications in the endometrial microenvironment [30]. It is theoretically possible that these changes influence pelvic hemodynamics or the localized diffusion of inflammatory mediators, thereby potentially complicating the resolution of infection through pharmacological intervention alone. However, these biological explanations remain exploratory, and the confounding effect of advanced age—often correlated with multiparity—cannot be entirely excluded. This finding suggests that when taking medical history, we should not overlook the patient’s reproductive history, as multiparous women may belong to a potentially refractory population.

Regarding whether IUD use should be considered a risk factor for antibiotic treatment failure in TOA, controversy currently exists. Traditional views hold that, as an intrauterine foreign body, an IUD may promote the colonisation of pathogens (especially Actinomyces), thereby aggravating infection [31–33]. However, the pooled data from this study (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.58–1.06) did not find a significant statistical association between IUD use and treatment failure, suggesting that once a TOA has formed, the presence of an IUD itself may no longer be the dominant factor determining antibiotic efficacy. In other words, although an IUD may increase the risk of developing PID, it does not significantly worsen the responsiveness of TOA to antibiotics. This suggests that when managing patients with TOA fitted with an IUD, although removal is standard practice, clinicians should not prejudge failure solely due to the presence of an IUD and prematurely abandon attempts at conservative therapy.

Similarly, a history of PID (OR = 0.56) did not show a statistically significant influence. This may be because patients with recurrent PID are often more sensitive to pelvic pain and seek medical attention earlier, thus receiving intervention in the early stages of abscess formation. Alternatively, it may be due to extensive pelvic adhesions resulting from recurrent inflammation, which form a ‘walling-off’ effect that limits the acute spread of inflammation and, although increasing surgical difficulty, may also limit the risk of acute abscess rupture.

Although sensitivity analyses helped identify specific studies driving heterogeneity for certain risk factors, substantial variation persisted for continuous variables such as age and abscess diameter. This residual heterogeneity is likely multifactorial and reflects the inherent diversity of observational clinical practice. First, the measurement of abscess dimensions is highly dependent on the imaging modality used (e.g. ultrasound vs. CT) and the operator’s technique, leading to inevitable measurement bias across institutions. Second, the retrospective nature of the included studies implies that baseline patient characteristics – such as variations in ethnicity, socioeconomic status and prior antibiotic exposure before admission – were not uniformly controlled, creating clinical heterogeneity that statistical adjustments alone cannot eliminate. Furthermore, the definition of treatment failure itself varied across included studies, ranging from subjective clinical deterioration to objective indications for surgery. This heterogeneity in outcome ascertainment may have introduced classification bias, potentially influenced the magnitude of observed associations and contributed to the residual statistical variation. Finally, the lack of standardised cutoff values for these continuous variables across primary studies further complicated data pooling, suggesting that these results should be interpreted as general trends rather than precise predictive thresholds.

Moreover, considering that antibiotic treatment failure often necessitates surgical intervention (e.g. salpingectomy or drainage), the potential impact on ovarian reserve and long-term fertility cannot be overlooked. For patients of reproductive age who fail conservative therapy, the focus must shift towards fertility preservation, a paradigm already well-established in the management of gynaecological malignancies such as early-stage endometrial cancer [34–37]. Recent literature suggests that a multidisciplinary approach, potentially incorporating oocyte vitrification and tailored assisted reproductive protocols (e.g. advanced techniques such as embryo culture supernatant injection), may be indicated to safeguard future reproductive potential [34, 38–41]. Additionally, it is crucial to recognise that pelvic inflammation may also involve male partner factors, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation to prevent recurrence. Emerging evidence also highlights the role of nutraceutical supplementation, such as myo-inositol, in improving oocyte quality and hormonal balance, which could be relevant for optimising outcomes in patients recovering from severe pelvic inflammatory conditions [42]. Thus, counselling regarding these fertility-sparing options and long-term follow-up should be integral to the management plan for patients with TOA at high risk.

Although this study followed rigorous systematic review procedures, certain limitations remain. First, all included studies were observational, and the retrospective design inevitably introduced selection bias and information bias. Second, some outcome indicators (e.g. age) exhibited high statistical heterogeneity; although we employed a random-effects model, due to the lack of sufficient characteristic information for subgroup analysis, the sources of heterogeneity (e.g. differences in antibiotic regimens and pathogen types) could not be completely eliminated. Due to limitations in the primary study data, we were unable to perform stratified analyses for specific antibiotic regimens (e.g. monotherapy vs. combination therapy) or specific pathogens (e.g. anaerobes vs. aerobes), which limits the guidance significance for treatment details. Moreover, the assessment of publication bias is limited by the reduced statistical power of Begg’s and Egger’s tests when fewer than 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis, warranting a cautious interpretation of the absence of detectable asymmetry in our results. Furthermore, the evaluation of potentially significant predictors, including inflammatory biomarkers (e.g. CRP, white blood cell count), symptom duration and detailed abscess characteristics, was limited by inconsistent reporting and heterogeneous data formats across the included studies. This constraint precluded quantitative synthesis, thereby preventing the assessment of their independent prognostic value and highlighting the urgent need for standardised reporting of these variables in future research.

Conclusions

In summary, this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that larger abscess diameter, advanced age and multiparity are significant risk factors for failure of conservative antibiotic therapy in patients with TOA. Conversely, smoking, IUD use and history of PID did not show clear predictive value. Based on these findings, we recommend that clinicians comprehensively assess patients’ age, reproductive history and imaging characteristics when managing patients with TOA. For patients with advanced age, multiparity and large abscess volume, the failure rate of antibiotic therapy alone is higher, and collaborative diagnosis and treatment by a multidisciplinary team is recommended to avoid treatment delays, reduce the risk of complications and maximise patient prognosis. Future studies should focus on developing clinical prediction models based on the aforementioned risk factors and conducting prospective studies to assess the benefits of early intervention strategies across risk-stratified populations.
