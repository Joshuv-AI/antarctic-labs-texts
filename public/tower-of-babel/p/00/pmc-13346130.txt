Corticosteroids in asthma and COPD: an inflammopharmacological perspective on molecular and genetic determinants
Authors: Subhajit Dutta, Shanmugam Ramaswamy, Aritra Dutta, K. Yamuna, L. Priyanka Dwarampudi, G. Mirunalini, M. V. N. L. Chaitanya, M. Laharipriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corticosteroids have broad anti-inflammatory effects to treat asthma and COPD. They do not alter the disease’s progression, but they do improve the function of lung, symptoms, and quality of life. Additionally, they also reduce the exacerbation of both disorders. In asthma, they reduce mortality, but not in COPD. After penetrating the cytoplasm of the cell, the corticosteroid binds to an inactive glucocorticoid receptor complex. Thus, the activated glucocorticoid receptor attaches to DNA at the glucocorticoid response element sequence, promoting the development of anti-inflammatory proteins (transactivation) and suppressing the transcription and secretion of various proinflammatory cytokines (transrepression). The available corticosteroids differ regarding their therapeutic index and potency. All age groups utilize corticosteroids, but because younger and smaller children can get larger mg/kg doses of corticosteroids than older children, they may be more susceptible to adverse systemic effects. Corticosteroids are most beneficial when taken at low to medium doses. While greater doses may help certain patients, there is little additional improvement shown with them. The benefits of corticosteroids for COPD are more debatable, even though they are the recommended treatment for chronic asthma in people of all ages. When taken as instructed, at low to medium dosages, ICS adverse effects are rare however, the risk increases with greater dosages. Even though many kinds of novel treatments have been invented and analyzed, it is unclear that any of them will take the position of ICSs as the first, long-term controller medication for asthma. However, a better initial control treatment for COPD might be established. This chapter focuses on the role, mechanisms, including glucocorticoid receptor binding and modulation of pro-inflammatory gene expression, steroid resistance, clinical applications, challenges, and limitations of corticosteroids in the management of asthma and COPD.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The adrenal cortex synthesizes various corticosteroid hormones, such as mineralocorticoids (e.g., aldosterone), which play a role in fluid and electrolyte balance, and glucocorticoids (from this point on referred to as CSs), which are essential for maintaining homeostasis and possess anti-inflammatory properties. This final characteristic is frequently used in medicine. According to US prospective cohort research with over a million participants, 21% had CS treatment in the previous 12 months. A significant portion of the CS prescriptions were for respiratory conditions. Regrettably, using CS is linked to frequent and serious side effects [1].

When treating severe viral pneumonia in clinical settings, the World Health Organization (WHO) recently advised against routinely using systemic corticosteroids if COVID-19 is suspected. This recommendation was made on March 13, 2020. This is because regular corticosteroid treatment is ineffective and poses a risk of injury, as documented in earlier research on viral pneumonia outbreaks, such as SARS. SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19, is very closely related to SARS-CoV-1, which caused the last SARS outbreak [2].

Inflammation serves a major part in the onset and course of each condition, asthma and COPD. Therefore, in some phases of the formation of these obstructive airway illnesses, anti-inflammatory measures are therapeutically beneficial and may stop the condition from progressing. Reducing inflammation should enhance airway function and lower bronchial hyperresponsiveness (BHR). On the clinical level, anti-inflammatory treatment lessens symptoms and flare-ups of the illness, which ought to be the shared objectives of treatment for both conditions. Knowing which clinical characteristics and whether pharmacogenetic factors play a significant role in anti-inflammatory innovations could help us comprehend the pathophysiology of COPD and asthma. The necessity of tracking lung inflammation has prompted research into emitted air and condensation that could help with pulmonary illness differential diagnosis, disease severity assessment, and therapy response evaluation. This chapter focuses on the mechanisms involved in the corticosteroids that treat asthma and COPD, and their limitations [3].

Pathophysiology of asthma and COPD overlap

Asthma and COPD have distinct manifestations of inflamed airways [4]. The overlap showed a combination of asthma-COPD inflammatory patterns with Th2 (IL-5 and IL-13) and non-Th2 cytokine production, according to a systemic inflammatory networking study [4–6]. According to the study, asthma had the greatest average values of IL-5 and IL-13, both had intermediate values, and COPD had the lowest values [4]. Remarkably, people who have previous instances of smoking (COPD and both) had greater levels of IL-8 than those with asthma, which attracts neutrophils and macrophages [4, 5].

According to conventional wisdom, asthma is a condition characterized by fluctuating airflow restriction brought on by exposure to allergens and hyperresponsive airways. Therefore, it is simple to see that a tobacco-using asthmatic eventually acquires a fixed restriction to airflow as a result of an elevated inflammatory response and ensuing COPD [6, 7]. Cigarette smoking in asthma is associated with higher lung neutrophilia, an occurrence similar to COPD, according to sputum and endobronchial biopsy investigations [7–9]. This is likely due to the expression of cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, and IL-17 A [8, 9]. Neutrophil chemotaxis in asthmatic smokers has been linked to these cytokines [8]. Furthermore, IL-17 is important in neutrophil-driven bronchial asthma and is probably implicated in promoting macrophage production of MMP-9 in COPD [9, 10]. Additionally, a prior investigation shows that across the airway pathophysiology of cigarette users with asthma discovered a rise in macrophages, CD8 + T cells, and COPD-like epithelial remodeling found in the bronchial tubes [7, 8]. Remarkably, there was no difference in neutrophil counts between people who smoke with asthma and those who don’t, indicating that CD8 + and macrophages are the most noticeable inflammatory cells in an asthmatic smoker [7].

In addition to cigarette smoking, air pollution has a variety of other effects on those suffering from asthma, such as raising cellular oxidative stress, releasing cytokines and chemokines, inbuilt immune cell activity via DAMP receptors like TLR-2 and TLR-4, altered DNA methylation, and damaged regulatory T-cells [11]. There is a wealth of information supporting the SNPs in potential asthma genes. Only a small number of these genes, including TNF, ADAM33, IL-4RA, MMP9, IL-12B, C3, and IL-13, have been replicated, though, due to problems with replication [12, 13]. Furthermore, SNPs of GATA3, MUC5AC, KIAA1109, HLA-DR, IL-33, IL-1RL1IL-18R1, SMAD3, ORMDL3/GSDMB, and IL-2RB have been linked to asthma in GWAS of both adults and kids with the condition [13, 14]. The earliest stage of asthma is specific to the ORMDL3/GSDMB gene on chromosome 17q21 [14, 15]. APOBEC3B and APOBEC3C are linked to the worsening of asthma in children receiving inhaled corticosteroids [14]. Asthma-related abnormalities in epithelial barrier function and innate and adaptive immune responses are linked to these genes [13, 15] (Fig. 1).

Corticosteroid responsiveness in asthma-COPD overlap syndrome

Asthma-COPD overlap syndrome (ACOS) is a unique clinical syndrome with both similar inflammatory, physiological, and molecular patterns of asthma and COPD. In contrast to classical COPD, in ACOS, patients often have a high number of eosinophils, more type-2 cytokine activity, and are more responsive to corticosteroid treatment. There is emerging evidence that ACOS could be a corticosteroid-responsive endotype in the continuum of obstructive airway diseases. Eosinophils in blood, FeNO, periostin, and serum IgE have been shown to be potentially useful biomarkers to predict patients with ACOS that are most likely to respond to inhaled corticosteroids. The therapeutic implications of these findings are significant since corticosteroid responsiveness seems to be more about inflammatory endotype than conventional disease naming [16].

Molecular endotypes of ACOS

More recent multi-omics research has shown that ACOS is not a homogeneous disorder but rather exists as several inflammatory endotypes each with different extents of eosinophilic, neutrophilic, and mixed granulocytic inflammation. Corticosteroid responsiveness is usually better in patients with eosinophilic biomarkers of increased IL-5, IL-13, and type-2 immune activation. Conversely, IL-17-, TNF-a-, and IL-8-neutrophilic ACOS phenotypes are less steroid sensitive. These findings indicate that the management approach in the future needs to be centered on molecular endotyping and not traditional disease classification, thus allowing individualized corticosteroid treatment [17].

Mechanism of corticosteroids

Understanding the cellular mechanisms by which corticosteroids reduce inflammation is essential to comprehending the different ways that asthma and COPD patients develop steroid resistance [18]. In the process of suppressing numerous genes associated with inflammation and activating other anti-inflammatory genes, corticosteroids can also modify inflammation through other posttranscriptional pathways [19].

Activation of genes

Diffusing over the cell membrane, corticosteroids attach to the cytoplasmic GR [20]. The GR undergoes a conformational shift upon ligand attachment, is liberated from several chaperone proteins, including heat shock protein 90, and attaches to nuclear import proteins, such as importin α, before entering the nucleus [20, 21]. GRα has a ligand-binding site, where corticosteroids bind to it. Rβ is also a splicing variant of GR that does not bind to corticosteroids but does bind to DNA; thus, disrupting GR’s DNA binding can limit corticosteroid activity [21]. The GR associates and homodimerizes with a DNA detection region called a GRE that is found in the promoter portion of genes that respond to steroids [22]. By connecting to the transcriptional coactivator molecule, such as CREB-binding protein, it has basic histone acetyltransferase activity. When the GR binds to a GRE, it acetylates core histones that are physically attached to DNA, activating (or rarely inhibiting) gene transcription. Genes are activated by remodeling engines like RNA polymerase II and SWI/SNF, which attract to tagged histones. A gene’s transcription may be lowered by GR interacting adversely with GREs or GREs that cross the transcriptional activation site, which might trigger steroid adverse effects such as the reduction of osteocalcin, which helps in bone formation [23] (Fig. 2).

Suppression of genes

Corticosteroids primarily control inflammatory responses by inhibiting several triggered genes linked to inflammation that produce adhesion molecules, cytokines, chemokines, inflammatory enzymes, and receptors. In asthma and COPD patients, the proinflammatory transcription factors, including NF-κB and activator protein 1, are regularly present in their airways, which stimulate these inflammatory genes. Identical to the above-described process for GR-mediated gene stimulation, reactions occur when transcription coactivator subunits trigger genes that promote inflammation [24].

Histone acetylation and chromatin alteration processes are reversed when energized GRs attach to corepressor elements to reduce NF-κB–associated coactivator activation. More significantly, active GR attaches HDAC2 to the stimulated inflammation gene complex, which reduces histone acetylation and suppresses several stimulated inflammatory genes. This likely explains why corticosteroids are so successful in reducing inflammatory processes in people with asthma when a large number of inflammatory genes, likely over 100, are triggered, but also why they are comparatively safe because they do not affect genes that control structure, ion channels, or receptors. When corticosteroids attach to the GR, it becomes acetylated, which seems to be required for connecting GREs. In contrast, HDAC2 must deacetylate the GR for it to attach to the NF-κB complex to suppress inflammatory genes [25] (Fig. 3).

Additional mechanisms

There are several mechanisms by which corticosteroids reduce inflammation. Corticosteroids significantly block MAPK signaling pathways by activating MKP-1, which may prevent the proliferation of several inflammation-related genes in asthmatic and COPD victims. Certain RNAses quickly break down the unstable mRNA of some inflamed genes, like TNF-α, but when cells are activated by inflammatory agents, the mRNA becomes stable. The impact is reversed by corticosteroids, which result in decreased release of protein from inflammation and quick mRNA breakdown. This can be accomplished by upregulating the production of proteins that degrade inflammatory protein mRNAs, like tristetraprolin, a zinc finger protein that attaches to the 3′ adenylate-uridylate-rich unaltered area of mRNAs [26, 27].

Emerging molecular determinants of corticosteroid responsiveness

The phenomenon of corticosteroid responsiveness has become widely regarded as a multi-faceted molecular process which is controlled by the epigenetic, transcriptomic and post-transcriptional processes. Recent research has shown that DNA methylation patterns, histone modifications and remodeling of the chromatin greatly affect the accessibility and transcriptional activity of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR). Abnormal methylation of inflammatory-related genes can disrupt corticosteroid-mediated transrepression and thus, be part of the inconsistent therapeutic outcomes in patients with asthma and COPD. The significance of histone acetylation and deacetylation is especially notable since corticosteroids need sufficient levels of histone deacetylase-2 (HDAC2) to effectively suppress the expression of inflammatory genes. The decrease in HDAC2 levels, which is typical of severe asthma and COPD, has become a decisive factor in steroid resistance [28].

Besides epigenetic control, non-coding RNAs have lately become the focus of attention as the regulators of corticosteroid responsiveness. Some microRNAs such as miR-21, miR-146a and miR-155 are involved in regulating glucocorticoid-related inflammatory pathways. Unregulated expression of these microRNAs has been linked to decreased steroid sensitivity, chronic airway inflammation and severity of disease. Moreover, long non-coding RNAs like MALAT1 and NEAT1 have been detected in regulation of the production of inflammatory cytokines and the activation of immune cells. These new molecular regulators give us an understanding of the heterogeneity of corticosteroid responses and are a possible therapy target in order to overcome steroid resistance [29] (Table 1).

Corticosteroid for asthma

Dose–response relationships with ICS

The majority of asthmatic patients are advised to use ICS. If necessary, the dosage is raised to keep things under control. The British Thoracic Society suggests 400–1,000 µg/day of fluticasone propionate and 800–2,000 µg/day of budesonide or beclomethasone, administered with a large-volume spacer, whereas the British National Formulary suggests a therapeutic dosage range of 200–2,000 µg/day. The National Asthma Education Expert Panel suggested adjusting the drug amount according to the asthma phase in the original guidelines [39]. But it seems like far smaller dosages might be sufficient. Recently, some researchers investigated the relationship between fluticasone propionate dosage and reaction in adolescents and asthmatic patients. The primary outcome indicators were significant exacerbations, β-agonist usage, nocturnal awakenings, and two measurements of PEFR (morning and evening), as well as FEV1. This meta-analysis comprised 8 trials with 2,324 subjects. At doses of 100–200 µg/day, the dose–response curve began to plateau, and at 500 µg/day, it reached its peak. Ninety per cent of the 1,000 µg/day effect was obtained with these dosages. A dose of around 600 µg resulted in a decrease in β-agonist utilization of 2.36 puffs per day, a modification in FEV1 of 0.71 L, and morning and evening PEFRs of 50 L/minute and 51 L/minute, respectively [40].

The latest research has found that minimal and moderate amounts of ICS were sufficient. Both medium-dose beclomethasone (672 µg/day) and minimal-dose fluticasone propionate (88 µg/day) produced nearly maximum increases in FEV1. Similar to this, medium-dose beclomethasone and low-dose fluticasone propionate administered via metered-dose inhaler both produced near-maximal improvement in hyperresponsiveness of the bronchi as determined by methacholine challenge. Systemic effects were increased, but neither FEV1 nor the stimulating concentration that result in a 20% decrease in FEV1 (PC20) was increased by high-dose ICS treatment (704 µg/day of fluticasone propionate or 1,344 µg/day of beclomethasone). In a similar research, lung function quickly improved in those with mild to extreme chronic asthma who administered an ICS. However, there was no clear dosage-response relationship [41, 42].

On the other hand, a meta-analysis of 25 studies revealed that higher dosages of ICS, more than 1000 µg, improved bronchial hyperresponsiveness more than lower dose studies. Two investigations of asthmatic patients with OCS dependence found that larger fluticasone propionate dosages permitted a higher reduction in the prednisone dosage [43]. Some studies have developed a technique to investigate the dosage reaction concerning Equi systemic cortisol suppression by comparing the systemic effects of six ICS formulations. At one-week intervals, 4 progressively larger dosages were administered. The only medications that did not exhibit an important dose-response impact were fluticasone propionate administered by a dry powder inhaler and a placebo [44].

Inhaled corticosteroid for asthma

In the case of an emergency, adding ICS to a β-agonist may be helpful. Adding an ICS to standard treatment has been shown in multiple randomized controlled trials to enhance pulmonary function outcomes and reduce the hospitalization rate by 50% [45]. Within a double-blind study, 400 µg of salbutamol and for three hours, either a placebo or an equal amount of salbutamol + 1 mg of flunisolide was given every ten minutes, to 94 patients who arrived at the emergency room with asthma flare-ups. The group undergoing combination therapy showed significantly higher FEV1 and PEFR at 90, 120, 150, and 180 min compared to the control group [46]. The researchers came to the conclusion that an ICS acts far more quickly than systemic steroids and that this could be because of localized airway reactions like vasoconstriction [46, 47]. In addition, 40 patients with acute asthma who were admitted to the hospital for treatment were compared to those who received systemic corticosteroids and a high-dose ICS. Following eight intravenous corticosteroid doses, participants were given either continuous systemic corticosteroids or inhaled eight puffs of flunisolide twice a day at a dose of 250 µg each. By Day 7, the flunisolide group FEV1 had increased by 1.6 to 2.3 L, while the systemic corticosteroid group had increased by 1.4 to 2.1 L [48].

The latest versions of ICS inhalers with metered doses use hydrofluoroalkane (HFA) propellants instead of chlorofluorocarbon (CFC) propellants [49]. In contrast to CFC-based devices, which administer particles with a mass median aerodynamic diameter of 3.8 μm, the HFA devices give particles with a mass median of 1.2 μm [50]. Compared to 19.7% with CFC, lung accumulation is 68% with an HFA propellant. Particles delivered by HFA devices should, in theory, enter the lung more deeply and enter the small airways (those with a diameter of less than 2 mm) [51]. Spirometry is not very good at detecting changes because the pulmonary airways only make up A tiny portion of the overall airway resistance. Other metrics, like final volume, can indicate obstruction in small airways, and patients with mild asthma who experience frequent flare-ups have higher closing volumes [52].

Oral corticosteroid for asthma

OCS works well for severe asthma. Between 6 and 12 h, lung function gradually improves, and OCS therapy minimises the rates of hospitalization and relapse. Nevertheless, using ever-higher doses has not been associated with an equivalent improvement in lung function, and there is no difference in the duration, magnitude, or onset impact between OCS and IV corticosteroids. In the event of an emergency room, systemic corticosteroids have benefits over intravenous corticosteroids [53]. One hundred youngsters diagnosed with acute severe asthma whose FEV1 was less than 60% randomly assigned to receive either 2 mg/kg of prednisone or 2,000 µg of fluticasone propionate via a metered-dose inhaler using a spacing device in a double-blind study. Four hours after therapy, the improvement in FEV1 with fluticasone propionate was 9.4%, while the improvement with prednisone was 18.9%. FEV1 was reduced in 25% of patients receiving fluticasone propionate, but not in any of the OCS subjects. In the groups administered fluticasone and prednisone, respectively, 16 (31%) and 5 (10%) of the children were admitted to the hospital [54]. A group of 365 adults living in inner cities, roughly separated into thirds based on the severity of their condition, participated in a study by Coyle and colleagues to assess the efficacy of acute asthma care. During the 2- to 3-week follow-up appointment, systemic corticosteroids given upon hospital release to patients significantly increased PEFR in all groups (β = 26.1; 95% CI: 1.8–50.5; p = 0.04) [55]. It is unknown how long OCS treatment should last. 44 adults with acute asthma who were admitted to the hospital were given prednisolone orally, a daily dose of 40 mg for five days. They were then given either a placebo or prednisolone for five days. By Day 21, patients who had taken five days of prednisolone had a PEFR that was just six liters per minute less than those who got treatment for ten days [56].

Precision medicine and biomarker-guided corticosteroid therapy in asthma

Treatment of asthma has increasingly changed an approach that is symptom-driven to precision medicine where corticosteroid therapy is customized based on specific inflammatory profiles and molecular endotypes. The biomarkers, including blood eosinophil count, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), serum periostin, and immunoglobulin E (IgE) have proven to be very useful in the predictability of corticosteroid responsiveness. High blood eosinophils and FeNO concentrations have a strong correlation with type-2 airway inflammation and tend to be a positive response to inhaled corticosteroid therapy. In contrast, non-type-2/neutrophilic asthma patients tend to have a diminished corticosteroid sensitivity and could need other treatment methods [57].

Recent developments in molecular phenotyping have demonstrated that there is also a significant heterogeneity in patients with asthma. Transcriptomic investigations have revealed that different corticosteroid-responsive and corticosteroid-resistant gene signatures can be used to aid personalized treatment choices. Moreover, IgE, IL-5, IL-4/IL-13 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) biologic therapies have corticosteroid-sparing effects in severe asthma. Such changes point to the fact that the further management of asthma will be more dependent on the decisions on the use of biomarkers, which will help the therapeutic process to be as efficient as possible and minimize the exposure to corticosteroids and their side effects [58] (Fig. 4).

Corticosteroid for COPD

Inhaled corticosteroid for COPD

According to early evaluations, ICS may help slow the decrease of FEV1 in COPD. Now, a number of sizable, controlled trials spanning three years, along with meta-analyses of those studies are have unequivocally shown that the average reduction in FEV1 is not significantly impacted by ICS [59]. Some researchers performed a meta-analysis of twelve ICS placebo-controlled studies for COPD [60, 61]. According to short-term research, ICS slightly raised FEV1 (mean 96 ml after 1 to 6 months) [62]. According to long-term research, FEV1 was only 51 ml greater in steroid-treated participants than in control volunteers after 1 to 3 years of continuous therapy [63]. Sin and associates observed a baseline through FEV1 increase of just 45 ml (95% CI, 22–69 ml) in a meta-analysis of six major trials [64]. Irrespective of whether individuals had received systemic steroid treatment beforehand, A meta-analysis of nine placebo-controlled, randomised trials found that ICS had a favourable effect on COPD exacerbations but a mixed effect on lung function. In some, but not all, research, tobacco smoking has simultaneously reduced the enhancement in FEV1 [65].

Concerns regarding the various ICS formulations, dose responsiveness, treatment duration, and clinical advantages and disadvantages are being discussed. Numerous controlled short-term research investigations using fluticasone propionate, budesonide, or beclomethasone have shown mixed results regarding their advantages for pulmonary function; compared to a placebo, beclomethasone significantly increased FEV1, FVC, and PEFR. Budesonide has been shown to improve FEV1 and reduce dyspnea in β-agonist responders, but it failed to enhance FEV1 in COPD patients who were assessed for six weeks to six months. Fluticasone propionate 1,000 µg/day for six months significantly increased mean baseline FEV1 by 150 ml and reduced the slow decline in pulmonary function from 110 to 40 ml/year when compared to a placebo. These gains were significantly greater than those found in most studies [66, 67].

Subsequently, the effectiveness of salmeterol and fluticasone propionate together has been contrasted with that of the two drugs alone. Compared with placebo, ICS and LABA combination therapy was generally successful in increasing FEV1. In a study, predose FEV1 increased by 156 ml (14%) when patients received a bid of 500 µg fluticasone propionate + 50 µg salmeterol, by 105 ml when they received fluticasone propionate alone, and by 107 ml when they received salmeterol alone. When given a placebo, it dropped by 4 ml. FEV1 increased by 261, 138, 133, and 0 ml two hours after the dose for the combination, salmeterol alone, fluticasone propionate alone, and placebo, respectively [68]. The findings of the Spencer study, which compared the effects of inhaled steroids with long-acting β2-agonists. Pretreatment FEV1 was 133 ml higher with combination therapy than with a placebo [69]. Budesonide plus formoterol has been shown to improve lung function in COPD patients in a comparable way. For the management of COPD, 250 µg fluticasone propionate + 50 µg salmeterol BD is a more effective combination than 36 µg ipratropium bromide + 206 µg albuterol QID. Results from a sizable perspective study on the function of ICS in COPD should be accessible in 2,006. Thus far, there have been only slight improvements in pulmonary function. Decision-making regarding this group of agents will undoubtedly benefit more from other outcomes, such as death rates, hospitalization rates, exacerbations, and health-related standards of life [70].

Compared to treating acute asthma attacks, there are fewer studies on the utilization of ICS for treating acute exacerbations of COPD. In a double-blind test, 199 patients who were hospitalization due to COPD exacerbations were randomized to receive nebulized budesonide, oral prednisone, or a placebo. Budesonide increased FEV1 by 0.10 L compared to placebo, prednisone increased FEV1 by 0.16 L compared to placebo, and prednisone increased FEV1 by 0.06 L compared to budesonide at 72 h. In the above case, the variance was not statistically significant. Thus, inhaled budesonide 2 mg every 6 h might replace 30 mg of prednisone in the treatment of nonacidotic acute exacerbations of COPD; however, more research is required [71].

12 hypercapnic COPD patients on ventilators experienced a bronchodilator response after a short trial of fluticasone propionate; their baseline FEV1 was predicted to be 13%. A reduction in airway resistance seems to be the main cause of this effect. Significant modifications in maximal airflow resistance, minimal airflow resistance, and static intrinsic end-expiratory pressure that were positive demonstrated the response, while changes in FEV1 markedly understated the impact. In people suffering from acute respiratory failure who are hospitalized and intubated, the use of nebulized ICS has not been sufficiently investigated [72].

A single-centre, double-blind study examined the effects of stopping 1,000 µg of fluticasone propionate per day for four months. Even though there was only a 38 mL variance in FEV1, stopping fluticasone propionate was linked to a greater chance of exacerbations, an earlier beginning of exacerbations, and a decline in health-associated quality of life. Older patients with severe, irreversible COPD were given a placebo and beclomethasone for six weeks each in a crossover study. The mean FEV1 was significantly lower during the placebo stage than during the beclomethasone treatment [73].

Oral corticosteroid for COPD

Limited research has been done on how OCS affects respiratory function among stable patients with COPD. For 16% of people, taking 30–60 mg of prednisone for 7–21 days was linked to a ⩾ 20% increase in FEV1. According to an observational study, FEV1 was stabilized by taking 10 mg of prednisone daily for 20 years. After six to twenty-four months, the improvement appeared. In a randomized, prospective trial, the effects of budesonide alone or a placebo were examined over 2 years, as well as prednisone 5 mg/day + budesonide 600 µg/day. FEV1 decreased less in patients receiving active corticosteroid treatment than in those receiving a placebo. According to certain studies, patients with comparable levels of fixed airflow obstruction from COPD or asthma have different levels of airway inflammation. A 15-day treatment involving 50 mg of prednisone per day was administered to both groups along with an inhaled bronchodilator. While participants with COPD showed developments of 122 and 100 ml, consequently, those with previous episodes of asthma showed developments of 251 ml using the bronchodilator and 320 ml using an OCS. In 21 COPD patients, the supplement of 30 mg of prednisolone daily to beclomethasone did not further enhance FEV1 or FVC. The benefits of OCS for stable COPD are not balanced by an increase in adverse effects, and the enhancement in FEV1 is, at best, modest and inconsistent [74, 75].

Systemic corticosteroid use over acute exacerbations of COPD is strongly supported by the available data. When intravenous methylprednisolone was administered for three days to manage acute exacerbations, the FEV1 increased more quickly than FVC, but not FVC. In a different study, 27 patients with COPD who experienced acute exacerbations received tapering doses of either prednisone or a placebo over a period of nine days. After 3 and 10 days, the prednisone-treated group’s FEV1 was higher. At 5 days, FEV1 improved by 90 ml when 30 mg of prednisone was taken once daily as opposed to 30 ml when a placebo was used. But at 6 weeks, there was no change in FEV1. In 96 patients having acute exacerbations of COPD who were admitted to the emergency room, a single 100 mg IV dose of methylprednisolone did not modify spirometric parameters [76].

The SCCOPE trial, which was carried out at U.S. Veterans’ Hospitals, found that oral prednisone and intravenous methylprednisolone administered for either two or six weeks increased FEV1 by a maximum of 0.120 L. There was no discernible respondent or nonrespondent group on the first day, and the distribution was unimodal. At two weeks, there was no difference between the prednisone-treated and placebo-treated groups, although the increase in FEV1 lasted for three days. The hospital stay for the steroid arm was shorter. There was no extra advantage to using prednisone for six weeks, and the frequency of steroid-related side effects was higher after six weeks of treatment than after two weeks [77].

In terms of the ideal duration of treatment, 34 patients with COPD who were hospitalized with acute exacerbations showed better improvements in FEV1 and FVC after ten days of systemic corticosteroids than after 3 days. Oral prednisone (40 mg for 10 days) administered as patient therapy produced a substantially greater improvement in FEV1 than a placebo in a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study of 147 COPD patients being discharged from the emergency department. Additionally, prednisone treatment reduced dyspnea, relapse rate, and time to relapse. OCS was evaluated in randomized, placebo-controlled trials for acute exacerbations of COPD. They came to the conclusion that during the initial three days of treatment, the FEV1 increased by 100 ml. In conclusion, the majority of research indicates that OCS improves respiratory activity during therapy of acute exacerbations of COPD, preferably during the initial stage, and lowers the risk of relapse [78].

Steroid resistance developed by asthmatic patients

Low dosages of ICSs may manage the signs of a large number of asthmatic patients, and they have transformed care and are now typically used as the first line of therapy. In contrast, a small percentage of people show up to be completely corticosteroid resistant, as indicated by the lack of significant clinical enhancement following excessive amounts of oral corticosteroid drugs (40 mg/d prednisone or prednisolone for two weeks). About 10% of individuals require the maximal ICS dose, and about 1% need typical oral corticosteroids (corticosteroid-dependent asthma). It now appears likely that a range of corticosteroid responsiveness ends with corticosteroid resistance. While just one injection of a depot steroid (triamcinolone acetonide) or an experiment of oral corticosteroids is helpful for recognizing individuals who have total steroid resistance, it is challenging to measure the level of corticosteroid responsiveness among particular participants because there is no clear method to measure medical steroid responsiveness [79].

Over forty years ago, 6 individuals with steroid-resistant asthma were reported to have either decreased blood eosinophilia or failed to respond medically to large dosages of oral corticosteroids [80]. Following oral prednisolone (20 mg/d for at least 7 days), a larger cohort of 58 patients with persistent asthma whose pulmonary function failed to improve (FEV1 < 15%) was later documented. These steroid-resistant patients were significantly different from the typical responsiveness patients in that they had an elevated level of airway hyperresponsiveness, an impoverished morning lung function, an extended duration of signs and symptoms, and a more common family record of asthma. The sex hormone abnormalities linked to the extremely uncommon familial glucocorticoid resistance are not present in steroid-resistant patients, nor are they cortisol-deficient (Addisonian). People with steroid-resistant asthma typically experience the usual steroid adverse reactions with peripheral corticosteroids, including normal plasma cortisol and adrenal inhibition in reaction to exogenous corticosteroids [81]. The absence of reaction to oral steroids cannot be attributed to pharmacokinetic factors or decreased gastrointestinal absorption. This implies that instead of corticosteroids’ metabolic or endocrine impacts, there is a selective flaw in their anti-inflammatory properties. Despite receiving significant amounts of corticosteroids, respiratory biopsy samples from individuals who have corticosteroid-resistant asthma exhibit an identical pattern of inflammation, with elevated eosinophil and lymphocyte numbers in comparison to those observed among individuals with sensitive asthma, and the TH2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-5 are not suppressed [82].

Steroid resistance developed by COPD patients

The majority of COPD patients react inadequately to even high dosages of ICSs or orally administered corticosteroids, in stark contrast to those who have asthma. While there is a slight decrease in exacerbations, although this has been inquired about, ICSs do not influence the development of the disease or death rates [83]. ICSs don’t decrease the number of inflammatory cells and mediators in COPD patients’ sputum or airways, and they don’t stop alveolar macrophages from releasing cytokines in vitro. About 10% of COPD patients exhibit some therapeutic reaction to ICSs; these patients are distinguished by higher eosinophil counts in the lungs and greater reversibility to bronchodilators, which may indicate that these patients also have asthma [84].

Emerging mechanisms underlying corticosteroid resistance in COPD

Recent findings indicate that corticosteroid insensitivity in COPD is mediated by various interrelated molecular pathways on top of the traditional inflammatory pathways. Activation of phosphoinositide-3-kinase delta (PI3K δ) caused by oxidative stress caused by cigarette smoke and environmental contaminants results in decreased HDAC2 expression and activity. The loss of HDAC2 is critical to the ability of glucocorticoids to repress inflammatory gene expression and therefore the loss of HDAC2 is an important factor that reduces the effectiveness of corticosteroids. Moreover, hyperactivation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) also leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation and sustained stimulation of pro-inflammatory transcription factors like NF- KB and AP-1 [85].

Others are the activation of inflammasomes, especially the NLRP3 pathway which facilitates corticosteroid-insensitive neutrophilic inflammation. The role of cellular senescence, modification of autophagy, and metabolic reprogramming in the maintenance of steroid-resistant inflammatory responses is also emerging. The results indicate that the restoration of HDAC2 activity, the regulation of oxidative stress responses, and the regulation of the inflammasome signaling pathway can be promising treatment options to overcome corticosteroid resistance in COPD [86].

Adverse effects and limitations

The most effective treatment for asthma and COPD is still corticosteroids. In order to control inflammation and prevent flare-ups, both systemic and inhaled (ICS) compositions are crucial. But they have serious side effects and important limits that doctors need to think about very carefully when treating patients [87].

There are two types of corticosteroid side effects: local effects, which are primarily associated with inhaled formulations, and systemic effects, which are more obvious with oral or parenteral corticosteroids. Local adverse effects include coughing and throat irritation, voice changes or dysphonia, which affect approximately 37% of patients, and oral candidiasis (thrush), which affects up to 16% of COPD patients using ICS [88]. These adverse effects are brought on by the drug’s direct deposition in the larynx and oropharynx. One of the more concerning systemic side effects is suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which can lead to adrenal insufficiency even after short-term use [89]. According to studies, up to 89% of patients may experience both immediate and long-term HPA suppression after just 14 days of systemic corticosteroid treatment. Osteoporosis and a higher risk of fractures are examples of bone-related problems [90]. Hyperglycemia, weight gain, and worse diabetes are examples of metabolic problems. Immunosuppression is another big worry. ICS doubles the risk of pneumonia in people with COPD, and fluticasone has a higher risk of pneumonia than other ICS molecules. Cataracts (affecting 27% of long-term ICS users), high blood pressure, mental health problems, muscle weakness, and, in children, growth suppression of about 0.48 cm per year during the first year of treatment are some other systemic effects [91].

There are several important restrictions on the use of corticosteroids in respiratory conditions. Steroid resistance is significantly higher in COPD than in asthma, primarily due to neutrophil-dominated inflammation that is resistant to corticosteroids. The mechanistic explanation for this resistance is decreased expression and activity of histone deacetylase-2 (HDAC2), an essential enzyme regulating steroid responsiveness; in severe cases, COPD patients have HDAC2 levels less than 5% of normal [92]. The inflammatory profile of COPD includes bacterially induced neutrophil recruitment and macrophage IL-8 production, both of which are primarily insensitive to corticosteroids. Some asthmatic patients exhibit steroid-resistant disease, particularly those with neutrophilic inflammatory patterns. In combination with period and dose-dependent side effects, long-term systemic use of steroids is linked to accumulative toxicity that may exceed benefits. The overall, non-specific immunosuppressive effect of corticosteroids makes people more prone to infections, and their overuse or improper prescription can conceal the advancement of underlying illnesses [93]. Development suppression is a particularly worrying side effect in young people because ICS results in a noticeable decrease in peak velocity that increases during their initial year of treatment. Last but not least, some patients develop a dependence on corticosteroids, making it difficult to stop using them. This is especially true when there is HPA axis suppression, which necessitates careful tapering and close monitoring to avoid an adrenal crisis and can last for months after stopping. When used responsibly, with proper evaluation, patient awareness, and adherence to evidence-based dosing strategies that minimize risks while maximizing effectiveness, corticosteroids remain vital therapies despite their side effects and limitations [94] (Table 2).

Future perspectives

The concept of integrating multi-omics technologies is revolutionizing the concept of corticosteroid responsiveness in respiratory diseases. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS), transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics and single-cell RNA sequencing have also found new molecular signatures that relate to therapeutic response, disease progression and steroid resistance. Through these methods, the broad characterization of airway inflammatory endotypes can be achieved and patient-specific biomarkers that could inform corticosteroid choice and dosing can be identified [108].

The emerging technologies in artificial intelligence and machine learning also contribute to the creation of predictive models that can combine clinical, molecular, and imaging data to predict treatment outcomes. These technologies can aid in detecting patients that can respond to corticosteroids and those that need alternative treatment. Individualized treatment plans, which combine biomarkers profiling, molecular phenotyping, and computer-based prediction models, will probably enhance clinical outcomes and reduce the unnecessary exposure to corticosteroids in the future. The developments signify a major paradigm change of the traditional empiric treatment methods to precision respiratory medicine [109].

Conclusion

When assessing therapies, FEV1 is an accurate indicator of effectiveness. When asthma patients are using corticosteroids, their lung function, as shown by FEV1, has been improved. The outcome is different with COPD, as there is a poor correlation between the FEV1 response and functional testing. Lung function is not always improved by OCS in stable COPD outpatients. The combination of fluticasone propionate and salmeterol and the combination of budesonide and formoterol appear to enhance FEV1 over therapy with the individual components, whereas individual ICS do not have a significant impact. Additionally, there is strong evidence that OCS should be used during acute COPD exacerbations. There is evidence that people with COPD who react to corticosteroids have characteristics of an asthma phenotype, including eosinophilic inflammation.

Precision medicine including molecular biomarkers, genetic determinants, and inflammatory endotypes have the potential to shape future developments in corticosteroid therapy. New opportunities to find corticosteroid-responsive groups of patients and overcome steroid resistance can be revealed by emerging evidence based on genomics, transcriptomics, epigenetics and artificial intelligence-driven predictive models. Combination of these innovative methods can help provide personalized treatment plans in asthma, COPD, and asthma-COPD overlap, which will eventually lead to better treatment outcomes and minimize the harm.
