Assessment of Nasal Morphology within various Sagittal Skeletal Growth Patterns—A Cross-sectional Cephalometric Study
Authors: Romilkumar Shah, Aakash Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:The present research aims to assess the nasal morphology within various Sagittal skeletal growth patterns like Classes I, II and III.Material and Method:The present research assesses various nasal parameters within equally distributed 150 lateral cephalograms having Skeletal Classes I, II, and III by manual tracing. The study identified differences in nasal morphology across different skeletal classes.Result:Naso-facial angle and Profile angle were greater in Class II cases, whereas Naso-mental angle was greater in Class III cases.Conclusion:Nasal morphology must be considered for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and facial reconstruction.

INTRODUCTION

Beauty, as the ultimate expression of human emotion, primarily resides in symmetrical and harmonious proportions.[1] A well-proportioned face underscores the seamless balance among its various features.[2] Enhancing facial aesthetics has swiftly emerged as a key goal in successful orthodontic treatment. Shifting from a focus on hard to soft tissue, treatment plans can now be tailored to improve patients’ facial appearance by carefully assessing the soft tissue contours.[3] Deviations in facial proportions can be corrected through various methods, including dentofacial orthopedics,[45] orthognathic surgery,[67] and aesthetic surgeries targeting both soft and hard tissues[8910] either individually or in combination.[111213] The nose, positioned centrally on every face, is a pivotal feature that demands attention. Nasal growth progresses steadily throughout adolescence, typically concluding around 16 and 18 years for girls and boys, respectively.[14151617181920] Nonetheless, long-term studies by Behrents[21] had shown significant nasal growth continuing into adulthood. Regarded as a cornerstone of facial aesthetics, the nose, in conjunction with the lips and chin, contributes to each individual’s distinctive facial identity. While orthodontic treatment may not directly impact the nose, its adjacent tissues are affected, suggesting a need for orthodontists to carefully assess nasal morphology. Despite the majority of cephalometric analyses focusing on the relationship between the nasal tip, lips, and chin, comprehensive assessment of nasal morphology is often ignored. Therefore, it is crucial for orthodontists to possess thorough knowledge and understanding of nasal morphology and its relationship with various sagittal skeletal growth patterns for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning.

AIM

The present research aims to assess the nasal morphology within various Sagittal skeletal growth patterns like Class I, II and III.

METHODOLOGY

After taking approval from the institutional ethical committee, a cross-sectional cephalometric study was carried out including high-quality and true-size lateral cephalograms having age ranges between 18 and 30 years. Cephalograms indicating congenital defects, trauma, and earlier orthodontic/surgical treatment were excluded from the study. A total of 150 lateral cephalograms were equally divided into Skeletal Classes I, II, or III using the ANB angle, Beta angle, and Wit’s Appraisal, in accordance with established norms. A total of nine nasal parameters (five angular and four linear) were measured by a single operator through manual tracing. To reduce error, interobserver and test–retest reliability was considered. The mean Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.96 indicated near-perfect agreement.

DISCUSSION

Facial beauty hinges on achieving a harmonious balance among the various components of the face. Enhancing facial aesthetics has long been considered a fundamental aspect of effective orthodontic treatment.[22] The nose holds particular significance in facial esthetics due to its central and prominent position. An ideal nose is one that complements the favorable features of an individual’s face. Research by Czarnecki et al.[23] highlighted the significant impact of nasal form on the perception of facial appearance and its interplay with other soft tissue structures. Consequently, assessing nasal morphology and its relationship to the face should be integral to orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. There has been a notable lack of cephalometric studies focusing on the soft-tissue profile, particularly the nasal region.[24] The present study utilizes lateral cephalograms, which are routinely obtained as part of essential diagnostic aids for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. This approach offers advantages in terms of less X-ray dose and price compared to alternative diagnostic methods such as CBCT. In the present research, when comparing nasal parameters within Class I, II, and III using the one-way ANOVA test. Several parameters showed statistically significant differences between the classes. Specifically, Naso-facial angle, Naso-mental angle, and Profile angle exhibited significant variations between the classes. Naso-facial angle and Profile angle displayed the highest mean values in Class II compared to Class I and Class III. Conversely, the Naso-mental angle demonstrated the highest mean values in Class III compared to Class I and Class II. These findings align with the observations by Kothari et al.,[25] who also noted an increase in Naso-facial angle in Class II cases. Similarly, Jafarpour et al.[26] also found greater angular nasal parameters in Class II cases. Gulsen et al.[4] reported that Class III subjects had the widest Naso-mental angle, while Class II subjects tended to have increased soft tissue facial convexity. Bharadwaj et al.[27] reported nasal length to be greater in Class III subjects, contrary to the findings of the present research. On the other hand, Genecov et al.[28] did not find any linkage between the amount of nasal development and the sagittal growth pattern of an individual, contradicting the present research’s observations. Thus, further in-depth understanding of the nose–face morphology in all three dimensions warrants future research.

CONCLUSION

The study identified differences in nasal morphology across different skeletal classes. For instance, Naso-facial angle and Profile angle were greater in Class II cases, whereas Naso-mental angle was greater in Class III cases. These findings underscore the importance of considering nasal morphology alongside other facial features in orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning, and facial reconstruction. Understanding these variations across different skeletal classes can aid orthodontists in developing personalized treatment strategies to achieve optimal facial aesthetics and function.
