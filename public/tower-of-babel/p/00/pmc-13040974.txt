Exploring the reflective practice training needs of Master of Nursing Specialist students: a qualitative study
Authors: Yuning Wang, Panpan Zhang, Hongmei Zhang, Yaru Li, Wenqiong Lin, Songyao Li, Xiaoxiong Sun, Liyuan Xing, Liu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is well established in the nursing field that reflective practice training constitutes a key component of nursing education, and reflective practice can exert positive impacts on nursing students across multiple dimensions. However, existing studies still exhibit significant gaps regarding the differentiated training needs of master’s nursing students, the long-term effectiveness of training programs, the underlying influencing mechanisms, and relevant qualitative research, which fail to fully align with the training characteristics and practical needs of master’s nursing students.AimThis study explores the need for reflective practice training among Master of Nursing Specialist degree candidates, aiming to provide a basis for developing training programs.MethodsA total of 16 full-time enrolled Master of Nursing Specialist students undergoing rotation in a Grade A tertiary hospital in Henan Province were selected through purposive sampling for face-to-face semi-structured in-depth interviews. Data analysis was performed using content analysis, following the thematic analysis framework developed by Braun and Clarke (2006).ResultsThrough analysis, five key themes were identified: scientifically designing training plans, rationally formulating training content, emphasizing faculty selection, diversified training and assessment methods, and institutionalizing training management alongside support system development.ConclusionMaster of Nursing Specialist students exhibit a strong demand for reflective practice training. It is recommended that regions tailor their approaches based on local conditions, optimizing reflective practice training programs for nursing master’s students in aspects such as training objectives, curriculum design, teaching methods, institutional support, and faculty development. These efforts will enhance their professional competence and cultivate high-quality nursing talents.Keywords: Nursing, Reflective practice, Postgraduates, Needs, Training, Qualitative research

Background

The global healthcare landscape is undergoing profound transformation, driven by the accelerated advancement of medical technologies worldwide [1]. Within this evolution, the smart city concept has emerged as a key focal point in the healthcare sector [2]. Centered on digitalization, integration, and convenience, smart cities are driving more efficient and refined public services, which has raised public expectations for the experience of healthcare services [3]. Within this context, healthcare constitutes a key application domain of smart cities, where nursing services play a vital role in ensuring the accessibility and continuity of care [4]. Consequently, the provision of high-quality nursing care—an indispensable component of smart city infrastructure—has become a central imperative globally, with public demand emerging as the primary driver of industry advancement [5, 6]. Master of Nursing Specialist postgraduates, as high-level, application-oriented, and specialized nursing professionals with both research capabilities and teaching potential, are entrusted with core roles as clinical experts, industry leaders, and promoters of evidence-based practice [7]. They have thereby become a critical force in addressing healthcare challenges in the new era. However, these advanced nursing roles demand not only proficient operational skills but also higher-order cognitive and affective competencies, including critical thinking, clinical judgment, ethical decision-making, and adaptive leadership [8]. Although higher-order competencies are introduced at the undergraduate level, their development often remains inadequately systematized and in-depth due to pedagogical and practical constraints [9–11]. Consequently, establishing a scientific, systematic, and supportive reflective practice framework is of paramount importance in master’s nursing programs, where educational objectives are more advanced.

Reflective practice constitutes a cyclical learning process wherein professionals consciously engage in critical examination of their lived experiences, professional actions, and contextual factors within clinical settings, with the aim of generating novel insights and informing evidence-based future practice [12]. In the long term, reflective practice has gradually emerged as an effective tool for fostering nursing students’ comprehensive competencies, including critical thinking and interpersonal communication skills [13]. It demonstrates significant potential in enhancing students’ self-awareness, prompting them to reflect on personal values and beliefs, and expanding their influence within the healthcare system [14]. As a cognitive process oriented toward professional growth, reflective practice emphasizes that individuals maintain critical questioning of uncertainties in clinical contexts [15]. Through continuous, cyclic reflective activities, they explore solutions and ultimately achieve behavioral transformation and practice innovation [16]. This theory profoundly reveals the dialectical relationship between knowledge and practice, and aims to cultivate specialized professionals capable of responding calmly in uncertain situations [17]. By fostering nursing students’ self-discovery and self-awareness, it bridges the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice, driving their professional development [18]. Studies indicate that systematic reflective training can effectively alleviate anxiety and stress among nursing students, strengthen their emotional resilience, and thereby comprehensively enhance their overall competencies—establishing itself as a critical pathway to ensuring the quality of nursing talent development [19]. While the value of reflective practice has garnered widespread consensus, and most Master of Nursing programs have initially integrated relevant content—encompassing common training modalities such as reflective journals, clinical case deliberations, and group reflection presentations—notable limitations persist in the current implementation of reflective practice courses, including fragmentation, arbitrariness, and a lack of systematic design [20]. Specific manifestations include: the absence of mechanisms to establish reflective competence as an explicit instructional objective and conduct effective evaluation [21]; a widespread lack of professional training among clinical preceptors and academic faculty in guiding in-depth reflection [22]; and insufficient student access to protected time for reflection, a safe psychological environment, and structured feedback support. These shortcomings fail to support graduate-level adult learners in engaging in critical reflection—an essential process for interpreting the meaning of their contexts, developing new beliefs or behaviors, and achieving transformative outcomes—thereby further constraining educational effectiveness [23].

Notably, existing research has predominantly focused on the perspective of reflective model construction, with most studies remaining at the theoretical level [24, 25]. There is still a lack of in-depth qualitative exploration into the lived experiences, real-world dilemmas, and developmental needs of Master of Nursing Specialist postgraduates in reflective practice training. Therefore, this study intends to systematically collect and analyze the reflective practice-related training needs of this population via qualitative research methodologies, with the purpose of providing empirical evidence and strategic guidance for the development of a scientific and standardized reflective practice training program.

Aim

This study aims to explore reflective practice training needs among Master of Nursing Specialist students, to inform the development of relevant curricula.

Methods

Design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative approach to investigate and understand the reflective practice needs of Master of Nursing Specialist students. The entire research process was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

Interview subjects

This study utilized purposive sampling to select Master of Nursing Specialist students undergoing clinical practice at a Grade-A tertiary general hospital in Henan Province between November 2024 and February 2025 as participants for semi-structured interviews. The inclusion criteria were: (1) full-time enrolled Master of Nursing Specialist students; (2) familiarity with the interview content; and (3) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to participate due to medical leave or physical conditions; and (2) discontinuation of clinical practice during the study period. All study procedures were approved by the Zhengzhou University Ethics Committee (Approval No.: ZZUIRB2024-85), and written consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 16 Master of Nursing Specialist students were ultimately included.

Development of interview guide

The interview guide was collaboratively developed by the research team, whose core members comprised three professors with over a decade of experience in nursing education and two clinical nursing specialists holding master’s degrees. Drawing on a systematic review of literature pertaining to reflective practice and Master of Nursing Specialist education, and aligned with the research objectives, an initial interview guide incorporating open-ended questions was drafted. Subsequently, two Master of Nursing Specialist students were invited to participate in pilot interviews. Based on insights derived from the pilot interviews, minimal adjustments were made to the final version of the interview guide solely regarding question sequencing, with the intent of optimizing the interview process. The final interview guide included the following questions: (1) What are your perspectives on reflective practice training for Master of Nursing Specialist students? (2) How do you think reflective practice training will impact you? (3) What personal attributes do you believe influence your level of reflective practice? (4) What factors, issues, or circumstances do you perceive as barriers to engaging in reflective practice? (5) What would your ideal training program look like? (6) What outcomes do you expect the training to help you achieve? (7) Do you have any additional suggestions or comments regarding reflective practice training for Master of Nursing Specialist students?

Researcher’s positionality

Researcher bias constitutes a prevalent challenge in qualitative research [26]. To mitigate its potential impact, all interviews in this study were conducted by the first author, who had received specialized training in qualitative interview methodologies prior to data collection. Notably, the interviewer maintained no hierarchical, supervisory, or teacher-student relationships with the participants, thereby minimizing bias induced by potential power differentials. Throughout the entire research process, the investigator employed reflexive strategies—including maintaining a reflective journal and engaging in regular deliberations with the research supervisor—to continuously examine their own positionality and strive for neutrality and openness during both data collection and analysis phases.

Data collection

A thorough explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures was provided prior to the interview to ensure participants fully understood the research process. Participants retained the right to pause or withdraw from the study at any time. All collected data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality, used exclusively for academic purposes.

Prior to formal data collection, two graduate students were selected via purposive sampling for preliminary interviews. The interview guide was promptly refined based on insights gained from these pre-interviews. With participants’ consent, semi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted in a quiet, independent setting. Each interview lasted 30–40 min, during which techniques such as repetition, summarization, probing, and responsive feedback were employed. The researcher attentively listened to participants’ narratives while observing non-verbal cues. All sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented with field notes.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim from audio recordings and accompanying notes within 24 h after completion. The transcripts were then returned to participants for verification to ensure accuracy. Following confirmation, the textual data were imported into NVivo 14.0 software for management and analysis. Sample size was determined based on data saturation [27]. Interviewing ceased when no new themes emerged from the data. After the 14th interview, thematic saturation was attained, as evidenced by the absence of new themes. To further validate saturation, two additional interviews were conducted, which yielded no novel themes. Consequently, data collection was finalized after a total of 16 interviews.

Data analysis

Following data collection, two researchers (the second and third authors) independently analyzed the interview transcripts. The analyses were subsequently compared and discussed until consensus was achieved. Preliminary themes were then reviewed against the full set of interview data and field notes to ensure consistency and validity.

This study employed a qualitative content analysis approach, guided by Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase framework for thematic analysis [28]. Content analysis is an inductive method that involves systematic preparation, organization, and interpretation of textual data, with the aim of deriving rich, nuanced insights into participants’ lived experiences [29]. This approach is particularly suitable when prior knowledge in the area is limited or fragmented [30]. The study followed an inductive approach. The analytical process involved repeatedly reading the textual data to achieve immersion and a holistic understanding; extracting meaningful core information to generate codes; comparing and clustering related codes to form sub‑themes and themes; reviewing the coherence and accuracy of the themes; clearly defining each code, sub‑theme, and theme; and presenting the findings accompanied by representative quotes from the raw data as supporting evidence for the themes. To illustrate the coding process, the initial code “accounting for individual differences” was developed from statements such as the following. P2, “Individualized needs of students should be taken into account, and training should be tailored to the specific characteristics of each person, such as personality and habits.” P4, “I hope individual differences among different groups will be considered during training, as not everyone prefers the same educational approach.” As all participants expressed similar views on individual variation, these segments were systematically marked and assigned the corresponding code.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, we adopted Lincoln and Guba’s framework, evaluating credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [31]. Credibility was strengthened by engaging Master of Nursing Specialist students who had completed at least ten months of clinical practice during their undergraduate studies, along with a minimum of six months of multi-departmental clinical experience in the Master of Nursing Specialist program. This ensured participants brought authentic, experience-based perspectives to the study. Dependability was established through maintaining a detailed audit trail of the research process. Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of the research context and participant profiles, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other settings. Confirmability was achieved by having two authors independently analyze and code the data, followed by collaborative discussion to resolve discrepancies and reach consensus.

Results

Participants were recruited from three universities in Henan Province, with 56% from Zhengzhou University, 38% from Henan University, and 6% from Henan University of Medicine. The mean age of the participants was 24 years. Among them, 25% were male and 75% female; 25% were in their second year of study, while the majority (75%) were in their first year. Participants shared their views and needs concerning reflective practice training within the Master of Nursing Specialist program. Following the organization of transcribed interview data, thematic analysis revealed five core themes and thirteen corresponding subthemes, as summarized in Table 1.

Themes and subthemes generated in this study

Theme 1: scientifically designing training plans

Subtheme 1.1: setting practical training objectives

Establishing practical training objectives represents a core component of reflective practice training for Master of Nursing Specialist students. Participants consistently emphasized that training should be practice-oriented, a goal attainable only when clearly defined, practical objectives are in place. Specifically, such objectives should guide training content to reflect the complexity of clinical environments while also offering actionable strategies for addressing challenges in patient care and research. Furthermore, participants highlighted that practical training objectives should focus on integrating research competencies with clinical skills. As one student explained, “As postgraduate students, we are expected to develop both research capabilities and the ability to manage clinical responsibilities. I hope this training enhances my critical thinking and equips me with practical strategies to address challenges in both research and clinical settings.” (P1) Participants highlighted that practical training objectives should define the focus of the curriculum, with learning through real-world cases being an essential means to achieve them. A participant noted how instructors demonstrated problem-solving through case-based teaching, “During the training, instructors share how they approach and resolve specific clinical or research problems through concrete cases. By reflecting on their experiences, we can identify and internalize effective problem-solving approaches.” (P6) The participants’ core demands for training further underscore the importance of realistic training objectives. They strongly expressed a desire for training closely aligned with clinical practice, which is exactly what practical training objectives should prioritize. One participant voiced concerns about overly theoretical instruction, “I hope the training remains closely tied to clinical realities rather than staying at an abstract, theoretical level. When we encounter problems during clinical rotations, the training should guide us in addressing similar issues and help clarify systematic ways of thinking—this is what truly matters to us.” (P7) The participants clearly stated that the ultimate value orientation of postgraduate education should be integrated into the training objectives. As one expressed, “More importantly, the training should enable us to solve real-world nursing problems. After all, the purpose of pursuing postgraduate studies is to investigate and address pressing issues in nursing practice. Our ultimate focus should be on patients—to alleviate their suffering, optimize nursing processes, reduce workload, and enhance the quality of care they receive.” (P14)

Subtheme 1.2: defining training priorities based on clinical rotation stages

Participants noted that differences in the duration of clinical rotations and levels of clinical proficiency influenced students’ perceptions of reflective practice and their associated training needs. Some interviewees suggested that reflective practice training should be tailored to students’ stage of clinical exposure, their familiarity with clinical environments, and their level of knowledge mastery. One participant proposed a staged approach to training in reflective practice, “I believe this training should consist of three stages. First, before starting clinical practice, we should proactively learn about potential challenges that may arise in clinical settings and enhance relevant competencies in a targeted manner to prevent or minimize these issues. Second, during the middle stage of training, we should reflect on the shortcomings identified in the initial phase and develop plans for improvement in subsequent practice. Third, after completing clinical rotations, we can engage in group discussions where each student shares the challenges encountered and the solutions applied during their placements. Through mutual learning, we can enhance our reflective and practical abilities in a more comprehensive way.” (P4) Another participant emphasized the importance of preparing students prior to clinical placements, “For students who are about to start clinical rotations, basic nursing procedures and common workflows should be introduced in advance as part of reflective practice training. This would help reduce anxiety and tension caused by unfamiliarity with the clinical environment.” (P10)

Theme 2: rationally formulating training content

Subtheme 2.1: delivering theoretical knowledge

The structured design and delivery of reflective practice training are critical to ensuring learners achieve systematic understanding and sustained professional development. Several participants emphasized the importance of providing foundational knowledge prior to engaging in reflective activities, suggesting that adequate orientation can enhance students’ comprehension of reflection and foster active engagement. One participant highlighted the need for guided instruction, “I hope instructors first provide theoretical grounding in the concept of reflective practice. If possible, integrating concrete clinical cases into the training would be highly beneficial—these could serve as a basis for guiding us through the process of reflection, formulating questions, and receiving feedback on our inquiries. This approach would help us develop a deeper and more practical understanding of reflective practice.” (P14) Another participant also expressed the desire to learn about reflective practice-related knowledge, “I have not been exposed to such a course before, so I look forward to learning the relevant theoretical knowledge.” (P16).

Subtheme 2.2: emphasizing practical education

Regarding training content, participants commonly emphasized that reflective practice training should be closely aligned with a practical orientation, integrating the development of reflective competence with clinical applicability. One participant highlighted the importance of applying reflective competence in clinical contexts, “The training should focus on cultivating reflective practice competence and then encourage its application in clinical work. This enables students to draw inferences from one situation to another, formulate their own questions, and attempt to solve problems independently.” (P3) Drawing on personal experience, another participant emphasized that the training content should reflect the interplay between reflection and action, and highlighted the central role of practice in the training process, “First, one needs to engage in deliberate thinking. After reflection, it is equally important to test those thoughts through practical application.” (P7) The third participant articulated a specific demand for the practical orientation of the training content, noting the current disconnect between theory and practice as a key concern, thereby further underscoring the need for training to remain practice-focused, “At this stage, our biggest challenge is applying theoretical knowledge to real clinical situations. We hope that during training, instructors can use concrete clinical cases to guide us in applying theory, cultivating critical thinking, and improving our ability to manage emergency scenarios in clinical practice.” (P11)

Theme 3: emphasizing faculty selection

Subtheme 3.1: implementing strict faculty screening

While most participants focused on curriculum content, a few emphasized the importance of instructor qualities, suggesting that those leading reflective practice training should themselves demonstrate active reflection, critical thinking, and strong implementation skills to provide meaningful guidance. One participant stressed the need for instructors who model reflective practice, “There needs to be a teacher with highly active thinking—someone who regularly reflects on their own practice, actively explores new approaches, and is able to integrate these insights into real-world teaching and clinical contexts.” (P2) Another highlighted the value of diverse faculty selection criteria, “In the selection process, we could include younger teachers or those with higher academic qualifications, strong learning abilities, divergent thinking, and demonstrated reflective capacity. Teacher selection should consider multiple dimensions; seniority may reflect experience, but it represents only one aspect and cannot substitute for other essential competencies.” (P10)

Subtheme 3.2: providing pre-training orientation for instructors

Several participants highlighted the need for formal training for educators, noting that unexamined teaching habits may inadvertently constrain student growth. One participant emphasized the risks of non-reflective instruction, “Instructors also need training. If a teacher adheres rigidly to established routines and subconsciously assumes their approach is correct simply due to long-standing work habits, this mindset can hinder students’ development.” (P9) Another underscored the importance of experiential learning for faculty, “Instructors should receive training on reflective practice content. After completing the training, they should first apply what they have learned within their own teaching practices. Subsequently, based on students’ individual differences, they can evaluate key indicators of reflective practice to assess training effectiveness.” (P12)

Theme 4: diversified training and assessment methods

Subtheme 4.1: combining online and offline teaching methods

Most participants expressed a preference for blended learning formats that integrate online and offline components, emphasizing that this modality could better accommodate the demands of clinical work, reduce scheduling conflicts, and minimize disruptions to rest and research responsibilities. One participant reflected on the trade-offs of in-person training, “Face-to-face sessions can be effective, but given the intensity of clinical duties, frequent offline training often leads to fatigue and disengagement. I hope a combination of online and offline formats is adopted, and that training does not excessively encroach on personal rest time.” (P4) Another highlighted the need for flexibility during active research periods, “Since some of us are currently occupied with data collection and questionnaire distribution, it would be helpful if the training format were flexible—blending online and offline components to better fit our clinical and academic schedules.” (P5)

Subtheme 4.2: enhancing teacher-student interaction

When discussing learning motivation, participants consistently expressed expectations for interactive engagement with instructors. One participant emphasized the importance of active engagement during sessions, “I hope instructors interact with us as much as possible during training—through eye contact, asking questions, or inviting responses. These small interactions help me stay focused throughout the session.” (P8) Another expressed a desire for more dialogic teaching approaches, “I hope instructors do not remain on the podium delivering lectures solely through PowerPoint slides. Instead, they should engage with us directly to assess how well we have understood the content, so they can provide more personalized guidance.” (P11) A third highlighted the emotional and educational value of relational teaching, “I prefer instructors who genuinely seek to understand students and build meaningful connections. I know this requires considerable effort, but when an instructor takes the time to communicate with me and seriously address my concerns, it has a profound impact on my learning experience.” (P13)

Subtheme 4.3: accounting for individual differences

Some participants emphasized the importance of individualized training approaches, suggesting that educational programs should account for differences among students in terms of their learning needs, career orientations, and background characteristics. They argued that a one-size-fits-all model may limit the relevance and effectiveness of training. One participant highlighted the need to differentiate training based on professional focus, “The emphasis should differ between research and clinical practice training. Offering homogeneous content for all students should be avoided. Instead, the training focus should be aligned with students’ developmental trajectories and career goals.” (P15) Another advocated for data-informed stratification to enable tailored instruction, “Students’ individual needs should be considered, but rather than treating everyone separately, we could group them according to shared characteristics—such as personality traits, place of origin, or educational background—using methods like latent profile analysis. Training can then be customized for each subgroup to improve its appropriateness and impact.” (P2)

Subtheme 4.4: conducting instruction within clinical contexts

Participants highlighted the need for reflective practice training to be closely integrated with authentic clinical settings in terms of both learning context and instructional methods. Participants emphasized that teaching should not be detached from real-world contexts, and called for greater efforts to incorporate clinical scenarios into instructional design. One participant highlighted the value of grounding theory in real events, “It would be more effective if theoretical content were delivered through actual clinical events or realistic scenarios.” (P10) Another underscored the importance of learning in genuine clinical settings, “While classroom-based training is feasible, when it comes to clinical application, learning within a real clinical environment is likely to be more impactful.” (P12) A third expressed the need for expert-guided reflection in context, “If possible, I hope instructors with strong reflective practice skills can guide us directly during clinical work, helping us reflect on and think critically about specific cases.” (P14)

Subtheme 4.5: diversifying assessment methods

When asked about assessment methods, many students expressed dissatisfaction with the uniform theoretical evaluations typical of traditional learning processes and advocated for more comprehensive forms of assessment. Participants expressed a clear preference for moving beyond traditional paper-based examinations toward assessments that capture holistic development and individual progress. One participant emphasized the need for multi-faceted evaluation, “If assessments are to be conducted, I would prefer that changes in our abilities be evaluated from multiple perspectives—such as our clinical performance, the depth of our reflections, and how we approach problems. Although this may be more challenging to implement, it offers a more comprehensive picture than scores on written exams.” (P6) Another suggested integrating simulation into assessment design, “We could combine theoretical assessments with practical evaluations. For example, we might be asked to manage simulated clinical scenarios involving patients, with scoring based on our decision-making, communication, and overall performance throughout the process.” (P12)

Theme 5: institutionalizing training management alongside support system

Subtheme 5.1: development improving institutional guarantees

When asked about the greatest obstacles to participating in reflective practice training, participants provided feedback concerning hospital management and institutional systems. Participants noted that heavy clinical workloads often leave little time or energy for reflection, and may even lead to disengagement from additional educational activities. They called for clinical rotation schedules to better align with postgraduate training goals, including dedicated time for reflection and academic development. They also emphasized the need for organizational openness—departments should value student input and provide opportunities to apply reflective insights in practice. One participant stressed the need for institutional space to innovate, “This requires a certain degree of implementable space. I hope relevant departments or the hospital can provide institutional or capacity-building support so that everyone has more opportunity and room to apply new ideas. When we propose reflections or innovations, the department should not dismiss them simply because they haven’t been done before—we need encouragement and structural support.” (P8) Another described the impact of workload on reflective capacity, “At times, rotations are extremely demanding, and it feels like we’re repeating the same tasks daily without pause. We’re also occasionally assigned non-clinical duties such as preparing presentations or organizing documents. By the end of the day, we feel physically exhausted and mentally drained, with no motivation to engage in self-reflection, let alone review literature or pursue self-improvement. What we need is systemic support to better balance clinical, academic, and personal demands.” (P13)

Subtheme 5.2: providing resource support

Some participants acknowledged that while they often reflect on challenging or confusing issues in clinical practice, they frequently lack systematic methods and accessible channels to explore these concerns further. Additionally, hesitancy to approach instructors—due to embarrassment or concerns about burdening others—means their reflections often remain unexamined and isolated. One participant emphasized the need for learning resources to support reflection, “I hope reference materials or recommended readings can be provided during training. Without supporting resources, it’s difficult to sustain focus or deepen understanding.” (P7) Another called for structured opportunities to share and discuss reflections, “I have been reflecting consistently, but for various reasons, I feel uncomfortable raising my questions with instructors, and I don’t want to take up my classmates’ time. I hope there could be a dedicated forum or session where we can openly discuss our thoughts and seek guidance. Only through mutual learning can we make meaningful progress.” (P14)

Discussion

The findings of this study on the reflective practice training and needs of master’s degree nursing students are categorized into five themes: scientifically designing training plans, rationally formulating training content, emphasizing faculty selection, diversifying training and assessment methods, and institutionalizing training management alongside the development of support systems. Consistent with the research by Stevenson [32], our results reveal that the training of master’s degree nursing students must be an integrated system that encompasses objectives, content, methods, support systems, and collaborative relationships, rather than merely a simple accumulation of practical courses. The findings of this study correspond with international trends in advanced nursing education and are supported by existing theoretical literature in several key aspects. Firstly, the systematic training needs identified in this study—such as integrating research and clinical competence, teaching in clinical contexts, and engaging in reflection during practice—are consistent with the core competencies highlighted in global advanced nursing curricula (e.g., professional leadership, complex clinical decision‑making, and evidence‑based practice improvement) as reported by Walker and Goemaes [33, 34]. Together, these point to higher‑order competency development that transcends basic skill training and underscores the integration of knowledge, practice, and reflection. Secondly, a central theme underpinning these training needs is the systematic cultivation of critical thinking. The literature widely recognizes that critical thinking cannot be taught in isolation through didactic instruction but must be nurtured within authentic, complex learning environments [35, 36]. Participants in this study emphasized the importance of clinical teaching, case‑based guidance for critical thinking, and the development of systematic thinking through reflection. These demands align with the contextual, case‑based, and reflective pedagogical principles advocated in the critical‑thinking literature, supporting the position that reflective practice can serve as an effective approach to fostering critical thinking among Master of Nursing Specialist students.

The scientific design of training programs and the establishment of practical training objectives not only clarify the learning content but also guide master’s students’ learning orientations and motivations, thereby deepening their reflective practice abilities. Williams provided a key theoretical framework for this in their work, noting that traditional nursing education tends to focus on performance goals (e.g., achieving high scores, passing assessments), which often fosters a fixed mindset among students and hinders the development of deep reflection [37]. Therefore, training objectives should incorporate the concepts of mastery goals and growth mindset advocated in the literature, guiding Master of Nursing Specialist students to develop professional competencies through reflective practice, view setbacks as valuable opportunities for growth, and cultivate the resilience and lifelong learning skills essential for nursing graduate students. According to Lazcano, although nursing students generally exhibit a learning orientation driven by professional mission, a lack of critical thinking and reflective capacity may undermine their learning motivation and lead to passive learning goals [38]. Consequently, assisting students in establishing clear learning objectives and systematically cultivating their reflective thinking will be an effective approach to enhancing their motivation and learning outcomes. Meanwhile, participants’ expectations for phased training objectives closely integrated with clinical rotations resonate with the essence of Kolb’s (1984) experiential learning cycle, wherein learning advances iteratively through concrete experience and reflective observation [39]. This connection carries direct implications for theoretically grounding the structure of training pathways in future curricula.

When constructing the content for reflective practice training in master’s level nursing programs, it is essential to adhere to the principle of balancing theoretical knowledge with practical skills. The findings of this study suggest that participants stressed a cyclical process of “reflection” and “action” (e.g., P7), which corresponds closely to Schön’s concept of reflection-in-action. Schön argued that professionals continually adapt their approaches through in-the-moment reflection amid uncertain situations [12]. Therefore, training should prioritize timely feedback and iterative learning within clinical settings, moving beyond an exclusive focus on post-event analysis or retrospective evaluation. This concept has been well substantiated in internationally recognized research on palliative care nursing education. While student nurses acquire extensive knowledge during their university studies, they may struggle to connect theory with nursing practice [40]. A systematic review by Pesut et al. corroborates that integrating theory with practice in training can effectively enhance nurses’ knowledge, confidence, and clinical competence [41]. Similarly, Andersson cautioned that theoretical instruction detached from practice seldom leads to meaningful outcomes, underscoring the need for educational models that bridge theory and practice [42]. This is further supported by Wang, whose implementation of 5G‑based remote ultrasound training demonstrated that a model combining systematic theory with clinical application markedly improves participants’ practical skills—offering direct methodological insights for enhancing reflective practice training in master’s‑level nursing education [43]. Together, these studies suggest that training programs should be grounded in clinical contexts and designed to foster continuous theory‑into‑practice translation. Specifically, training ought to incorporate authentic clinical cases, provide timely guidance, and establish responsive reflective‑support mechanisms aligned with clinical rotations. Such an approach can enable Master of Nursing Specialist students to genuinely develop higher‑order competencies that integrate critical thinking and actionable skills within complex clinical environments.

Research findings indicate that students expect guidance and instruction from high-quality educators who possess advanced reflective practice skills. Educators play a crucial role in supporting students’ development of reflective skills, providing the necessary support and guidance to ensure students can effectively engage in reflective practice [44, 45]. The study by Botelho highlights that the teacher-student relationship is a central link in the educational process, with positive teacher-student interactions stimulating students’ motivation to learn and promoting their overall development [46]. Exceptional educators are more likely to earn students’ trust and respect, which not only enhances teaching effectiveness but also serves as a positive role model for interpersonal relationships. Therefore, hospitals should select outstanding clinical educators with strong reflective practice skills to provide high-quality bedside teaching and personalized guidance. To ensure the quality of reflective practice training for master’s level nursing students, it is essential to prioritize the selection and training of mentors. This can draw from the successful experiences of Australia’s National Simulation Educator Training Program (AusSETT), which emphasizes that competent educators do not inherently possess the necessary skills, and clinical experience alone is insufficient to make effective simulation instructors or reflective practice facilitators [47]. Thus, establishing a systematic and standardized mechanism for faculty selection and training is crucial. Tomokawa have constructed a health workforce training system that includes legal protections, pre-service training, and on-the-job supervision, providing a valuable reference for systematic faculty training [48]. We should learn from these experiences by integrating a reflective practice curriculum into nursing education programs, establishing a standardized certification system for “Reflective Practice Teaching Demonstration Bases-entity platforms that are systematically accredited and equipped with standardized teaching and supportive functions,” developing specialized resources for reflective practice teaching, implementing a qualification certification system for “Reflective Practice Teaching Mentors,” and systematically advancing the implementation and quality monitoring of reflective practice teaching programs. This approach aims to enhance the overall reflective practice capabilities of nursing professionals.

To ensure the effectiveness of reflective practice training for master’s level nursing programs, it is essential to adopt diverse training and assessment methods that meet the demands of comprehensive competency development in complex clinical contexts. This perspective is supported by multiple lines of evidence. Rybarczyk, in a review of approximately 100 international collaborative training programs, found that successful initiatives consistently adopted a mixed‑methods design integrating theory, case studies, simulation, and practice [49]. Similarly, Mianehsaz observed that the systematic combination of diverse learning methods can produce synergistic effects and improve learning outcomes [50]. Cohen’s work further suggests that students tend to prefer interactive and experiential pedagogical approaches [51]. Collectively, these methods reflect a teaching pathway centered on experiential learning-a cyclical process involving concrete experience, reflection, conceptualization, and action. Through real‑world cases and contextually embedded instruction, learners are guided to build new knowledge upon prior understanding, ultimately achieving cognitive and behavioral change via reflection on personal experience [52]. Such diversified training models align closely with the goal underscored in reflective practice—namely, the integration of knowledge, skills, and attitudes across varied contexts—and together facilitate the effective translation of theory into clinical practice.

Evidence suggests that structured reflective clinical learning can enhance clinical competence, strengthen confidence in clinical judgment, and improve patient care outcomes. The needs articulated by participants in this study—for instance, learning and receiving guidance in authentic clinical settings and promoting personal growth through reflection—not only represent an ideal form of reflective clinical learning but also, from the learner’s viewpoint, affirm its role in fostering cognitive and behavioral transformation. Therefore, from a demand perspective, this study reinforces that creating a supportive environment for reflective clinical learning is a crucial pathway to achieving the educational aims of advanced nursing training. The findings of this study indicate that students expect hospitals and relevant departments to optimize the scheduling system for rotating graduate students, allowing for flexible work hours that provide opportunities for autonomous learning and professional development. This expectation may be linked to the significant time pressures faced by students, who often juggle clinical rotations with research responsibilities, resulting in challenges in balancing training tasks with clinical work [53].

Lieow’s research emphasizes the importance of ongoing management support in translating theoretical knowledge into stable clinical practice [54]. Active participation and support from nursing managers can enhance the effectiveness of reflective practice. Additionally, the study by George highlights that the successful implementation of training programs requires ensuring that trainees have sufficient time; excessive workloads can hinder staff from balancing work with other life aspects, thereby reducing their motivation [55, 56]. Consequently, it is essential for national policies or regulations to be enacted prior to training to optimize the clinical rotation training plans for graduate students, ensuring adequate support for reflective practice training.

Managers should adhere to a people-centered management philosophy, tailoring positions to meet needs and managing roles based on employee characteristics. By adjusting departmental staffing configurations, managers can maximize the potential of their workforce. Given that graduate students represent a pool of high-quality talent, departments should take into account the student attributes of this group when planning schedules. Clinical departments should establish an institutionalized system to support reflective practice, including scheduled reflective sessions, structured preceptor supervision, regular case debriefing platforms, and positive feedback incentives. With clear institutional safeguards, consistent resource allocation, and sustainable organizational support, a safe, inclusive, and critical thinking‑encouraging clinical learning environment can be fostered for master’s nursing students. Only through a reasonable combination of systematic guidance and resource allocation can a stable, supportive, and effective ecosystem be created for the reflective practice of nursing master’s students, ultimately internalizing reflective abilities as a core professional competency.

Limitations

While this study explored the reflective practice training needs of Master of Nursing Specialist students and offers valuable implications for developing corresponding training programs, several limitations should be noted. The sample was drawn solely from a single tertiary hospital in Henan Province and consisted predominantly of first‑year graduate students, which may limit the representativeness of the findings across different regions, hospital levels, and cultural contexts. Additionally, although the interviewer endeavored to remain neutral, her background in nursing education may have influenced the framing of questions and interpretation of the data. Despite these constraints, this preliminary work aims to lay a foundation for future research involving more diverse participant groups.

Conclusion

This study provides a basis for developing a reflective practice training program for Master of Nursing Specialist students. Needs were identified across various aspects, including curriculum design, faculty expertise, instructional methods, and supportive policies. The development of such a training program should fully consider these dimensions. It is recommended that different countries and regions, based on their specific contexts, construct and adapt training plans for Master of Nursing Specialist students in terms of setting reflective practice training objectives, building curriculum systems, diversifying teaching approaches, establishing institutional support mechanisms, and strengthening faculty capacity. These efforts will help enhance the reflective practice competence of Master of Nursing Specialist students and contribute to the cultivation of high-quality nursing professionals.
