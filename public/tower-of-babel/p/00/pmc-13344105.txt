Views on the distribution of consciousness influence ethical judgements toward brain organoids as biological computers: an exploratory study
Authors: Jonathan Lomax Boyd, Eric Allen Jensen, Aaron Michael Jensen, Nethanel Lipshitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological computers, or ‘biocomputers’, are being engineered with brain organoids integrated with in silico hardware and software components. This technology promises to advance our mechanistic understanding of cognitive processes by embodying in vitro neural systems within virtual learning environments. Yet, biocomputers raise ethical and moral questions, such as whether evidence of consciousness indicates that embodied neural systems have moral status. To better understand the relationship between signs of consciousness and ethical concerns, we conducted an exploratory study to investigate public views toward biocomputers. We examined whether the public’s tendency to attribute consciousness to human and non-human entities guides moral reasoning about biocomputers. We discovered a latent structure underlying public views on the distribution of consciousness. Specifically, we identified three cluster groups, characterized by opinions on the scope of consciousness. These cluster groups were highly predictive of views toward the cognitive capacities and moral status of biocomputers. Respondents in our sample exhibited strong support for research on and creation of biocomputers, and for some, support increased as biocomputers were perceived to be more conscious—a relationship that appears to conflict with conventional reasoning in moral philosophy. Future studies will help determine the prevalence of these views among stakeholders and demographically representative publics at local, national, and global scales whose ethical judgements will inform regulation, oversight, and governance of engineered neural systems.Keywords: bioethics, public, synthetic biological and organoid intelligence, biological computer, brain organoids, consciousness, moral status

Introduction

The neural mechanisms of cognition remain a central focus of neuroscience research. Advances in engineering synthetic biological and organoid intelligence (SB/OI) have the potential to inform our understanding of cognitive systems through bottom-up design. SB/OI represents an emerging field that embodies human neurons, or human brain organoids, within virtual learning environments using feedback from in silico hardware and software components (Kagan et al. 2023; Smirnova et al. 2023; Alam El Din et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2023). One of the ethical issues raised by scholars in bioethics and neuroethics is the possibility of these entities acquiring some level of consciousness, and consequently, moral status (Sawai et al. 2019; Lavazza 2021; Zohny et al. 2021). Although brain organoids currently generated in the laboratory are unlikely to possess human-level consciousness or other advanced reasoning abilities, they exhibit neural responses that have been used as indicators of consciousness in humans. For instance, neuronal cell cultures can exhibit critical dynamics–phase transitions between highly order and disordered states of neural activity–often attributed to conscious human cognitive performance (Habibollahi et al. 2023; Müller et al. 2025). Neuronal cultures can also exhibit positive scores on the perturbational complexity index, a proxy measure of consciousness inferred from integrated information theory and tested in humans under anesthesia (Lavazza and Massimini 2018; Colombi et al. 2021; Lavazza 2021; Dong et al. 2023). These observations raise the concern that researchers are creating novel entities with intrinsic worth and dignity (Kagan et al. 2024). Thus, understanding factors that underlie attribution of consciousness to non-paradigmatic and unfamiliar entities become a critical component of ethical analysis.

If biocomputers are conscious, the actions of researchers could harm or benefit them by influencing their wellbeing or welfare. Relatedly, if biocomputers are conscious, they can be wronged, i.e. they may have certain moral rights, such as the right-to-life, that can be violated. These issues become pressing when biocomputers develop conscious states that can be experienced as positive or negative, i.e. valenced conscious states (Carruthers 2018; Niikawa et al. 2022). Sentience is sometimes used to refer to this kind of consciousness (Zohny et al. 2021). If biocomputers become sentient, the possibility that they will be harmed or wronged would have to shape the actions of researchers and other users.

To use terminology prevalent in bioethics, consciousness, and especially sentience, could ground moral status in biocomputers. An entity that has moral status matters in its own right from a moral point-of-view. If an entity has moral status, we have certain moral duties to this entity, and it has moral claims against us (Warren 1997; DeGrazia 2008). A common philosophical view is that consciousness or sentience are necessary and sufficient for moral status (Singer 1986; DeGrazia 1996; Zohny et al. 2021; Peña-Guzmán 2022). Relatedly, consciousness and sentience have often been mentioned as the decisive factors in determining whether human brain organoids have moral status (Lavazza and Massimini 2018; Sawai et al. 2019; Hyun et al. 2020; Mollaki 2021; Sharma et al. 2021; Niikawa et al. 2022). Thus, the attribution of consciousness or sentience to biocomputers has direct implications for the ethics of their development and for their use in research, medicine, and business. This raises a dilemma. For research purposes, we may want biocomputers that resemble human brains as much as possible, as they are most likely to yield scientific insights. But the more biocomputers resemble the brains of human beings, the higher the probability that they are conscious or sentient, reintroducing many of the ethical concerns we hoped to avoid in creating them (Greely 2021). Thus, a fuller understanding of the factors that guide attribution of consciousness in novel contexts is critically important.

Debates continue among philosophers about whether consciousness alone or conscious valenced experiences are necessary and sufficient for moral status (Lee 2019). Thought experiments comparing paradigmatic humans with ‘zombie’ duplicates have been used to ground intuitive claims about the connection between consciousness and moral status (Siewert 2001). Yet, to what extent do the intuitive judgements of philosophers cohere or diverge from those of the public? In order to infer which characteristics may be relevant to folk conceptions of moral status, Ota and colleagues conducted an experimental bioethics survey of public attitudes toward the moral permissibility of using human brain organoids with varying capacities, including the capacity for having visual or painful experiences (Ota et al. 2025). They found that moral permissibility of research was negatively correlated with organoids exhibiting visual or painful experiences, compared to controls without those capacities. Notably, respondents in this study were not asked to assess whether they attribute the capacity for valanced experiences to brain organoids, even though these capacities were a central feature of the informational vignettes. In other words, we do not know whether respondents attributed the capacity for valenced experience to brain organoids nor whether these attributions correlate with moral permissibility. Evans (2023) asked survey participants to indicate whether they approve of research using human brain organoids with a level of awareness equivalent to an insect, pig, or human fetus (Evans 2023). Respondents’ support for research was not impacted by the level of awareness that brain organoids were said to have. Yet again, the degree to which respondents attribute different levels of consciousness to brain organoids was not measured. If we assume that moral permissibility and support for research are correlated, as morally impermissible research should not be supported, it is unclear how to reconcile the results of Evans (2023) and Ota et al. (2025). A partial explanation may reside in qualitative interviews, which suggest that the ‘moral value of cerebral organoids … depended much on the respondents’ imagination of what cerebral organoids look like and what sort of function they could have … They [survey respondents] found it difficult to imagine what type of entities they were’ (Haselager et al. 2020). Across these studies, the relationship between consciousness and moral judgements remains unclear. We sought to identify factors that inform whether the public will perceive biocomputers as conscious.

We speculated that individuals may call upon antecedent beliefs about the distribution of consciousness among familiar beings when asked about novel entities, such as biocomputers. This hypothesis, however, requires knowledge about the scope of consciousness and its moral significance. An underlying structure in the attribution of consciousness could act to guide moral judgements. The influence of such a structure, if it exists, would have significant implications for the moral imagination that ethicists argue will be needed in the age of biocomputers (Diner and Gaillard 2023). By understanding the public’s tendency to attribute consciousness, we can gain a clearer understanding of factors that guide moral reasoning.

We collected survey responses from a convenience sample of online participants to investigate factors that guide moral views toward biocomputers. We found that variations in the description of biocomputers, either in application or in cognitive features, had limited effect on public views. However, we discovered a latent structure underlying the public’s tendency to attribute consciousness to diverse entities, which appear to guide moral reasoning toward biocomputers.

Methods and materials

Study rationale and scope

This study was designed as an exploratory and experimental comparison of factors that influence public views toward biocomputers. We collected data on public views toward the consciousness of diverse entities and deployed an experimental approach to bioethics research where participants are randomly assigned to informational vignettes that are predicted to vary morally-salient features of biocomputers to assess their impact on ethical decision-making (Earp et al. 2020). This approach has been used in previous studies to investigate the relationship between cognitive attributes and moral permissibility of brain organoid research (Ota et al. 2025). Our study applied this general approach to the study of biocomputers by analyzing the impact of additional context-specific factors, including different areas of technological application and descriptions of baseline cognitive features of moral relevance (Boyd and Lipshitz 2024). For instance, our study does not describe biocomputers as capable of ‘painful experience’, but rather capable of general evaluative-decision making necessary for agency or autonomy. Data was obtained from a convenience sample recruited online to enable comparison of different informational vignettes and discovery of latent factors that influence public views. The data may contain demographic biases. Thus, our analyses focus on comparison of experimental effects and the identification of latent factors that characterize patterns in the attribution of consciousness rather than claims about generalized public attitudes. As with prior studies, our experimental vignettes intentionally use framing language to evaluative the plasticity of opinions and moral views toward biological computing systems (Evans 2023; Ota et al. 2025).

Ethics and data collection

This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins University Homewood Institutional Review Board (Protocol/FSA Number: HIRB00018078). The online software platform Qualia was used to build and host the survey instrument. Survey participants were recruited through online advertisements via posts on social media, community forums (e.g. Reddit), and paid social media advertisements on Meta. Individuals who clicked on the survey link from online ads and completed less than 20% of the survey were considered bounce hits and not considered further. A detailed description of the attrition rates and procedures for removing respondents from the survey dataset are provided (Supplementary Document 1). Analysis of attrition rates revealed that 92.4% of all respondents removed from the final dataset progressed through less than 5% of the survey instrument, which indicated very limited engagement from those respondents with the survey instrument. Thus, attrition rates among these initial respondents are likely not due to participant fatigue or condition-specific framings provided later in the survey. We sought to procure a high-quality convenience sample by ensuring survey participants successfully responded to attention checks and affirmed participation through the consent form. The prevalence of potential duplicate entries were assessed based on several criteria, which included usage of the same name and contact information, submission of non-relevant responses from the same IP address with different names/emails but same demographic information, submission of nonsensical open-ended responses, very short survey fill-time and poor internal consistency, e.g. directly opposing answers to two items measuring similar outcomes, entries with identical survey responses but varied metadata and demographic information. Our procedure yielded 350 initial respondents who completed at least 20% of the survey. Respondents who failed the attention checks (N = 17), did not consent (N = 52), submitted duplicate names/emails (N = 6), were tagged with identical IP addresses (N = 65), or provided incoherent responses to open-ended questions (N = 15) were removed from the dataset. A total of N = 190 survey responses, or 54.3% of respondents completing more than 20% of the survey, were used for subsequent analysis. Our final dataset could be over-representative of individuals with particularly high levels of interest in biocomputers, or enriched for respondents with more pro-science views, compared to the general U.S. population. We investigated the over-representation of individuals with pro-science views in the results section. The study builds on prior pilot testing and incorporates refined or rotated items based on those earlier rounds to improve clarity of questions and accessibility. Future studies should assess the influence of recruitment framing, prior domain familiarity, and demographic stratification to validate and extend the present findings.

Our analyzed analysis focused on 190 high-quality entries collected between March 13 to June 12, 2024. Our sample included the following demographic characteristics: Female = 53%, Male = 41%, Non-binary = 6%; Political affiliation: Democrat = 52%, non-Democrat = 48%; Age: mean = 37.7 years, SD = 17.5; Education level: Below bachelor = 61%. These demographic statistics are similar, but not identical, to weighted estimates from the 2023 five-year American Community Survey.

Procedure

Survey participants were provided information about the aim of the study, instructions, procedure, right to discontinue participation at any time, confidentiality of data, and provided contact information for the Principal Investigator of the study. This study reports the results of a subset of questions contained in a larger survey questionnaire that will be published elsewhere. The questionnaire items included in this study are available in supplementary materials (Supplementary Document 2). Respondents were provided an operational definition of consciousness: “Consciousness is the capacity to perceive, think about, feel and experience the world. Scientifically, it’s believed to vary across different species based on the inner workings of their brain structure and function”. Next, respondents were asked to estimate the degree to which various entities are conscious on a 5-point Likert-scale from ‘Not at all conscious’ (1) to ‘Extremely conscious’ (5) with the option to have ‘No opinion’ (0). The order of entities presented was randomized to minimize the influence of respondents inferring an implicit rank order. While most of the entities were likely familiar to the participants (e.g. adult humans, monkeys, cats, etc.), we described several unconventional entities in more detail. For instance, in vitro planer cultures of human neurons were described as ‘A petri dish full of living human neurons’, and human brain organoids were described as ‘A living miniature organ resembling a human brain’. Next, participants watched a 5-minute video that provided general information about biocomputer components (human neurons and computer chips), how they have been used in prior research, and what scientists think they could do in the future. In pilot testing, two versions of the video were used that changed subtle visual motifs, which were found to have no impact on survey responses. After the video, participants were asked to complete a knowledge comprehension question about biocomputers. Participants were then exposed to an additional text-based description of biocomputers using an experimental 2×3×2 between factor design. Participants were randomly assigned to an informational vignette that described biocomputers used in a specific application (e.g. develop new medicines) and capable of exhibiting a specific cognitive-like behavior (e.g. sensitivity to environmental stimuli) (Supplementary Document 2). We varied specific components of each vignette to empirically evaluate how cognitive traits and application areas of biocomputing systems influence moral judgements. The text described biocomputers in one of two Scenarios (2×; biocomputers ‘Consciously’ perform an activity or merely ‘process information’) using one of three cognitive attributes (3×; biocomputers demonstrating sensory perceptiveness, preferences/evaluative decision-making, and self-awareness) in one of two application areas (2×; medical treatments verse information technology). The variation in cognitive features exhibited by biocomputers in the informational vignette was based on prior work describing morally-relevant dimensions of consciousness in brain organoids (Boyd and Lipshitz 2024). Variation in the application area enables studies of moral encroachment were epistemic opinions are context-specific (Moss 2018). Questions followed that probed the degree to which participants attributed various cognitive traits to biocomputers after reading the vignette on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1-‘Strongly Disagree’ to 5-‘Strongly Agree’, followed by questions that assess moral judgements toward biocomputers: these included questions designed to evaluate the perceived benefits to ethical concerns, support for biocomputing research, support for commercialization, and whether they would donate tissue for use in biocomputer research. This study focuses on the predictive power of cluster groups identified prior to participants encountering the informational vignettes.

Software and statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis was performed in RStudio with base R version 4.4.2. Correlational matrices were computed using rank-ordered Spearman correlations in the boot package. Hierarchical clustering analysis (package hclust) was performed on Euclidean distances (package stats) and calculated from Spearman correlations. Hierarchical clustering on principal components (PC) was conducted using the FactorMineR package. Other analyses, including the estimation of factor loadings, the optimal number of clusters, and the effects of each factor on cluster centroid positions were calculated using the parameters package. Prediction of dependent variables (e.g. attributions of consciousness, ethical decisions, cognitive features) were analyzed using ordinal logistic regression models to predict response variables based on cluster assignment and performed in the base stats package with cluster identity as main dependent factor. Pairwise statistical differences between groups were analyzed using the package emmeans with Tukey-adjustment for multiple test corrections. Data visualizations were primarily generated using the ggplot2 package. Package PCAtest was used to generate null distributions of empirical data to assess significance of the variance explained by each principal component using Psi and Phi statistics.

Results

Attributions of consciousness to diverse entities

To better understand how beliefs about the prevalence of consciousness may influence ethical decisions about biocomputers, we first investigated the degree to which individuals attribute consciousness to other entities, including human and nonhuman animals. Approximately 94% of respondents identified adult humans as either ‘extremely conscious’ or ‘moderately conscious’, while only about 13% attributed similar levels of consciousness to human brain models that are frequently used in the laboratory, including planar ‘human neurons’ in dish and more complex, 3D ‘human brain organoids’ maintained in culture (Fig. 1A). By comparison, fewer respondents attributed high levels of consciousness to nonhuman animals (81–90%), newborn humans (75%), and current or future AI systems (13–37%). Respondents were marginally more likely to attribute minimal levels of consciousness to brain organoids compared to planar cultures of human neurons (Wilcox two-sided signed rank test: V = 1 528, = .0643). Future versions of AI were seen as significantly more likely to be minimally conscious compared to current AI (V = 1 182, = 2.69x10−12) and brain organoids (V = 6 775, = 1.06x10−6). These results indicate that most individuals are willing to attribute non-negligible levels of consciousness to entities beyond paradigmatic humans, which is consistent with prior academic surveys that investigated the attribution of consciousness (Francken et al. 2022).

Next, we investigated the overall correlational structure of consciousness assignment across entities (Fig. 1B). As expected, there were strong correlations among nonhuman animals, such as monkeys and cats ( = 0.72, < .001). Correlations between monkeys and human newborns were higher (ρ = 0.42, < .001) than those with human adults (ρ = 0.35, P < .001). Human newborns displayed a similar yet weak positive correlation with human brain organoids (ρ = 0.16, = .041) and human neurons (ρ = 0.17, = .050). Human brain organoids demonstrated a strong positive relationship with planar human neurons (ρ = 0.55, < .001). Moderately positive relationships were also found between human brain organoids and current AI ( = .36, P < .001) and future AI systems (ρ = 0.37, < .001). Human neurons also showed moderate relationships with current AI systems (ρ = 0.32, < .001) and future AI systems (ρ = 0.34, < .001). Plants had a significant and positive relationship with human neurons (ρ = 0.30, < .001) and brain organoids (ρ = 0.20, = .001). These findings indicate that individuals willing to attribute consciousness to plants are also more likely to assign higher levels of consciousness to other nonconventional entities. Finally, future AI systems exhibited a strong positive relationship with current AI systems (ρ = 0.68, < .001). The negative correlation between adult humans and plants (ρ = −0.18, = .006), as well as current AI systems (ρ = −0.17, = .017), suggests that individuals with strong views about human consciousness are likely to attribute lower levels of consciousness to entities without conventional nervous systems.

Given these pairwise correlations, we analyzed the global hierarchical structure of consciousness assignment by visualizing the Euclidean distances between cluster groups estimated from the correlational matrix. We identified two primary branches that separated into those composed of conventional nervous systems (adult and newborn humans, cats, and monkeys) and unconventional or non-neuronal entities (AI systems, human brain models, and plants) (Fig. 1C). Within the conventional (lower) branch, views toward adult humans were more distal to other animals, including monkeys, cats, and newborn humans. For unconventional entities, views toward plants branched more distantly from biological and silicon-based entities, which grouped together, respectively. Given the overall branching structure, we next explored whether individual views toward consciousness could be represented in a lower dimensional space suggestive of an underlying structure in consciousness attribution.

The latent structure underlying consciousness attribution

To further explore public attributions of consciousness, we sought to identify the principal components (PC) that captured the most variation in individual responses. The first two PCs accounted for ~53% of total variance among respondents. To assess the statistical significance of the variance captured by the first two PCs, we compared our empirical data to a null distribution randomly re-sampled from participant responses. A significantly higher proportion of variance was explained in the first two PCs by the observed empirical data, compared to the null distribution (Fig. 2A). Additional PCs accounted for similar levels of variance between the empirical and null data. Thus, the variance explained by the first two PCs is notably higher than expected by chance. Investigation of the factors that contributed to the first two PCs revealed that views toward unconventional, non-organismal (e.g. human brain models) and non-biological (e.g. AI systems) entities dominated PC1, which represented 28.5% of the variance in the overall dataset (Fig. 2B). PC2 captured views toward conventional human and nonhuman animals (e.g. cats, monkeys) and accounted for 24% of the variance. We then visualized the PC scores for the first two dimensions and found the data suitable for clustering based on Hopkin’s clusterability statistic, H = 0.36 (Fig. 2C). Using a consensus approach, we determined the optimal number of clusters based on the results of 32 different cluster optimization algorithms (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). The highest proportion of algorithms (45%) converged on grouping survey respondents into three clusters. Cluster 1 included respondents (n = 38, 20%) who attributed high levels of consciousness to a wide variety of entities, irrespective of the entity’s underlying substrate being biological or non-biological, including human brain models and AI systems, respectively, relative to other clusters (Fig. 3A–I). Cluster 2 respondents (n = 60, 32.4%) were distinguished by an incrementally reduced tendency to assign high levels of consciousness beyond paradigmatic adult humans, including nonhuman primates and newborn humans. The largest portion of respondents were grouped into cluster 3 (n = 87, 47%), who attributed comparatively high levels of consciousness to human (e.g. newborn human) and nonhuman entities (e.g. cats and monkeys), while being less likely to assign high levels of consciousness to unconventional entities like AI systems and in vitro human brain models. The tendency to attribute consciousness across all entities exhibited a unique distribution for each cluster group characterized as unimodal for Cluster 1, scalar for Cluster 2, and bimodal for Cluster 3 (Fig. 2D). To control for potential sample bias in our data, we next investigated whether the probability of individuals being classified into one of the three cluster groups was influenced by the potential over-representation of respondents with pro-science stances (Table 1).

Pro-science stance do not strongly influence cluster group identity

The discovery of cluster groups that reflect an underlying structure of consciousness attribution could be influenced by the over-representation of individuals in our sample who are particularly interested in biocomputers, or especially pro-science, in general. Thus, we investigated the impact of pro-science stances on cluster group identity. Participants were asked questions that assessed pro-science stance on a five-point Likert-scale from +2–‘Strongly agree’ to −2–‘Strongly disagree’. These questions included whether they trust science, trust scientists, trust scientists to communicate accurately, trust scientists to create useful knowledge for society, and whether they believe that scientists have intentions to do work that benefits society. We found that 79% of respondents ‘Strongly agree’ or ‘Agree’ that scientists do their work with the intention to benefit society. By comparison, in 2022, 77% of U.S. adults expressed confidence in scientists to act in the best interests of the public, according to the National Science Foundation’s (NSF) Science and Engineering Indicators report (National Science Foundation 2024). Thus, we interpret our convenience sample as, on average, closely aligned with the NSF’s indicators of U.S. trust in science. Next, we performed an ANOVA to test whether pro-science views could predict cluster group identity. We found no significant effect of pro-science stances on cluster group identity (Table 1).

Our survey instrument did not explicitly measure respondent interest in biocomputer research. Moreover, we cannot exclude the possibility that inclusion of additional individuals with less confidence/trust in science, relative to the national average, could influence the identification of additional clusters. However, based on the results available, we concluded that cluster group identity is not strongly influenced by pro-science views and our sample is broadly aligned with national indicators of public trust in science among U.S. adults.

Cluster assignments predict attribution of cognitive capacities

We next assessed public views on the kinds of cognitive-like traits that could be attributed to biocomputers. Respondents were asked on a five-point Likert-scale to indicate whether they agree/disagree that biocomputers possess unified conceptions of cognition, including consciousness, similarity to people, possession of a spirit, as well as dimensions of cognition described by Birch and colleagues (Birch et al. 2020), such as perceptiveness, evaluative-ness (e.g. capacity for having preferences), or self-awareness (Supplementary Table 1). Overall, responses were highly correlated across traits (Fig. 4A). The degree of consciousness attributed to biocomputers was most strongly correlated with ascriptions of self-awareness and degree of similarity to people. The weakest correlations with consciousness were with attributions of having preferences and the possession of a spirit.

To what extent do experimental manipulations that vary the description of cognitive abilities and applications of biocomputers influence the public's epistemic opinions of other cognitive traits? Ordinal regression models were used to predict public attributions of each cognitive trait to biocomputers based on ordinal predictors including cluster assignment, scenario, attribute, and application (see Methods and Materials for description of factors embedded within the vignettes). Overall, we found that the informational vignettes did not have a significant influence on public views of biocomputer cognitive traits, except for a marginally significant effect of varying the attribute condition on perceptions of biocomputers having preferences (Table 2). Only individuals who encountered biocomputers being described with evaluative abilities were more likely to view them as having preferences. These non-significant and significant main effects were replicated in a separate study using a nationally-representative sample (N = 1 238) of the US population (Boyd et al. 2026).

However, cluster assignment, which indicates a latent difference among individuals to attribute consciousness to other entities, had a significant impact on responses to all questions assessing cognitive traits. Specifically, respondents grouped into Cluster 1 were significantly more likely to view biocomputers as conscious ( < .0001, z = 4.94), similar to people ( < .0001, z = 4.17), in possession of a spirit ( = .020, z = 2.68), perceptive ( = .0214, z = 2.66), having preferences ( = .0266, z = 2.58), and self-aware ( < .0001, z = 4.07), compared to Cluster 3 respondents (Fig. 4B–G). Cluster 2 respondents exhibited views that were intermediate when compared to other clusters, including the view that biocomputers are less conscious ( = .0042, z = 3.18) and self-aware ( = .0388, z = 2.44) than Cluster 1 respondents. Given the cluster-dependent differences in responses, we might also expect that views toward the moral status of biocomputers could also vary in significant ways if ethical considerations are moderated by the attribution of consciousness.

Consciousness attribution shapes ethical and moral judgements

To assess public views about the moral status of biocomputers and their potential relationship to beliefs about the possession of consciousness, we asked participants to answer questions regarding the well-being, moral worth, utility, treatment, and basic legal rights, such as the ‘right-to-life’, of biocomputers (full list of questions, Supplementary Table 2). The correlational structure of participant responses to questions of moral status and utility were hierarchically organized into two main groups; the first group of responses shared beliefs about the welfare of biocomputers (e.g. ‘People have a responsibility to ensure the well-being of the biocomputers they use’) and the second group focused more on the morality of societal benefits (e.g. ‘It is ethical to buy and sell biocomputers like other products’) (Fig. 5A). We found that the informational vignettes had little, if any, impact on public views about the moral status of biocomputers (Table 3). The application of biocomputers toward medical treatments/information technology had modest effects on whether individuals thought they should have basic legal protections and if the benefits outweighed the ethical risks. In one sense, these results are not surprising. If moral status depends on consciousness, and the extent to which an entity is conscious does not depend on its social utility, attributions of moral status should not vary across these vignettes. In another sense, however, the results are surprising. For example, one might expect that the prospects of significant benefits from biocomputers would create psychological pressure to attribute lower moral status to them, so as to remove perceived ethical barriers to research on biocomputers. No such effect of moral encroachment was detected. However, our total sample size (N = 190) and number of conditions may be underpowered to detect significant yet smaller effects. These results do inform hypotheses that can be tested with larger studies of public views. Yet in our sample, we found no significant differences among cluster groups for questions that asked about the utility of biocomputers for human usage, and thus focused subsequent analysis on participant responses to whether biocomputers should have right-to-life protections, human responsibility for wellbeing, and moral worth on par with humans, which were used to assess participants’ judgements about the moral status of biocomputers.

Overall, cluster assignment had a significant impact on ethical and moral judgements (Table 3). We found Cluster 1 respondents were significantly more likely to believe that biocomputers merit basic legal rights ( = .0016, z = 3.45) and tended to affirm human responsibility for the wellbeing of biocomputers ( = .0778, z = 2.16), compared to Cluster 3 (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, respondents in Cluster 1 were significantly less likely to agree that biocomputers are ‘always less important than people, morally speaking’, compared to Cluster 2 ( = .0165, z = −2.75) and Cluster 3 ( = .0028, z = −3.30) (Fig. 5D). Participants were generally opposed to prohibitions on biocomputer research, yet agreed that there should be laws regulating the treatment of biocomputers (Supplementary Table 2) with no cluster-specific effects detected (Fig. 5E,F).

On average, participants were neutral about the risk-adjusted benefits of biocomputers research with no cluster-specific effects (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 5G). Previous work argued that certain dimensions of consciousness are relevant to considerations of moral status (Boyd and Lipshitz 2024). Thus, we investigated whether the perceived benefits of using biocomputers, relative to ethical concerns, were related to biocomputers being attributed with perceptiveness, having preferences, or being self-aware. Interestingly, the relationship between respondents attributing these dimensions of consciousness to biocomputers and the perceived relative benefits of using them was cluster-dependent (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). For example, only Cluster 3 respondents showed a significant positive correlation between perceptiveness and risk-adjusted benefits. By contrast, attributing self-awareness to biocomputer was only strongly correlated with benefit only within Cluster 1. All clusters exhibited a strong positive correction between risk-adjusted benefits and the view that biocomputers share similarity with human beings (Supplementary Fig. 2D). While cluster group identity appears to influence the perceived risk-adjusted benefits of engineering biocomputers with specific cognitive traits, the overall similarity of biocomputers to humans appears to either raise the perception of benefits or lower risk.

Overall, our results report the identification of distinct patterns in the attribution of consciousness, which act to guide moral assessment of biocomputers. We also found high levels of overall support (mean = 4.08, SD ± 0.93) for biocomputer research (Supplementary Table 2). In our sample, 79.3% of respondents ‘Strongly agreed or “Agreed’ with the statement ‘I support biocomputer research’, compared to 69.7% of individuals from a nationally representative study in the U.S. who support brain organoid research (Evans 2023). While we found higher levels of support for biocomputer research compared to brain organoids, the level of support in our sample was similarly high, on average, across cluster groups (Fig. 6A), while the relationship between support and the level of consciousness attribution were highly cluster-specific (Fig. 6B). We cannot differentiate whether the higher levels of support given to biocomputers, compared to brain organoids, reflects the composition of our convenience sample or the public’s relatively higher level of endorsement for this derivative use of brain organoids. Yet, we found cluster-specific effects between support and attributions of consciousness to biocomputers. Respondents in Clusters 1 and 2, who regarded biocomputers as more conscious compared to Cluster 3, were significantly more likely to support research as the perceived level of consciousness increased (Fig. 6B). These results further confirm that between-cluster variation, which reflect structured differences in the likelihood of individuals to attribute consciousness, shape bioethical decisions about biocomputer research.

Discussion

Ethicists broadly agree that moral status depends, in part, on the extent to which a being possess certain cognitive capacities, such as the level of consciousness attributed to paradigmatic humans. While the mechanisms that give rise to consciousness remains an open scientific and philosophical question, there is growing appreciation that understanding public intuitions about the potential for consciousness can inform how to move forward responsibly with research and technological development. We conducted an empirical bioethics study to examine the public’s willingness to attribute consciousness to various entities and the implications these attributions might have on ethical decisions about engineered neural systems, such as embodied brain organoids. Entities with moral status possess certain moral rights, such as a right not to be damaged or destroyed without justification. Relatedly, moral agents—such as researchers—are said to have certain obligations to entities that have moral status, such as the obligation to ensure their wellbeing or avoid unjust mistreatment. Our study provides several principal findings on public judgements toward biocomputers that help resolve conflicting results reported in prior studies.

First, we report the identification of a latent three-cluster structure underlying the attribution of consciousness among members of the public. Second, we find that differences in the patterned attribution of consciousness of familiar entities influences the willingness to attribute a variety of other cognitive traits to biocomputers. Third, beliefs about the distribution of consciousness across diverse entities, measured prior to the informational vignettes, predict subsequent ethical judgements about biocomputers. Fourth, support for biocomputer research was relatively high across cluster groups, and most respondents do not endorse broad prohibitions on research. Finally, despite the observed cluster-specific differences in moral decisions, we found a general and notable trend: the more biocomputers were perceived to exhibit a variety of cognitive traits, or harbor similarity to human beings, the more individuals were willing to support biocomputer research and view the benefits as outweighing ethical concerns. Overall, the public’s ethical decisions about biocomputers appear to be guided by patterned beliefs about the possession of consciousness. Our data is consistent with other reports that recognizing such capacities in laboratory models are likely to help advance science, technology, or medicine (Haselager et al. 2020; Boyd et al. 2026).

Previous work has suggested that survey results obtained from online respondents, especially convenience samples, can be sensitive to the effects of participant attrition (Zhou and Fishbach 2016). We recognize these sources of bias and highlight several aspects of our study relevant to concerns about attribution. First, we observed that the vast majority of respondents (92%) removed during the data cleaning procedure (see Methods section, for details) occurred among participants who completed less than 5% of the survey, which indicates that these respondents clicked on the survey link embedded within online ads and bounced shortly thereafter with limited interaction with the content of the survey. Second, the experimental vignettes used in the study had very similar language and were of similar length whereby minimizing any differences in cognitive effort that would lead to participant attrition. Third, the composition of our sample is similar to but not identical to recent American Community Survey estimates. Thus, attrition biases induced from highly un-representative samples appears limited. Moreover, results from the experimental conditions in this study were also replicated in a separate nationally representative sample in the United States (Boyd et al. 2026). Fourth, we implemented approaches mentioned in the literature to ‘warm-up’ participants before assigning them to experimental conditions (Horton et al. 2011). Lastly, our analyses primarily focused on the impact of cluster assignments inferred as latent patterns in the data collected that measure differences between individuals. Attrition that may have reduced heterogeneity in our sample would be expected to also reduce our ability to detect these clusters in the first place. Thus, any homogenizing effects of attrition would indicate the potential for more significant cluster groups reflecting latent patterns among more diverse publics. Conversely, attrition effects that increased heterogeneity in our sample, presumably through exclusion of individuals with intermediary views, would likely increase our ability to detect differences between non-excluded individuals. We speculate that potential bias in the sample introduced through non-random attrition would most likely impact the relative proportion of individuals in each cluster group, which broadly represent unimodal, gradual, and bi-modal distributions in public views on the scope of consciousness across entities. Given these considerations, our study focuses on the impact cluster identity has on ethical judgements, rather than reporting representative statistics for a specific population.

Next, we discuss connections between the public’s views on the distribution of consciousness and relevant philosophical interpretations. These philosophical views are helpful when trying to describe and make sense of the internal logic of the responses found in each cluster.

A philosophical view of consciousness attribution

Participants grouped into Cluster 1 present a unimodal view toward the possession of consciousness whereby most entities are considered conscious. These views are consistent with a view known as substrate-independence, according to which consciousness can be realized in nonorganic matter, such as in digital algorithms or computers (Bostrom 2003). The belief in the possibility of ‘strong’ artificial intelligence, i.e. the belief that artificial intelligence can be not only computationally sophisticated but also conscious, is one form of substrate-independence (Searle et al. 1998). It should be noted, however, that not all substrate-independent views are expansive in their attributions of consciousness. For example, one who subscribes to substrate-independence may consistently believe that sophisticated nonorganic matter can be conscious yet deny that nonhuman animals are conscious. Cluster-1 participants, however, tend to take an expansive substrate-independent approach to consciousness.

The views of Cluster 2 represent a more scalar or gradient view of consciousness. Respondents in this group assign the highest levels of consciousness to paradigmatic adult humans with successively lower levels to monkeys, cats, and newborn humans (Fig. 3A–C,F). This result can be given a quantitative or a qualitative interpretation. According to the quantitative interpretation, respondents regard monkeys as less conscious than humans in the way we regard a person who is falling asleep (but is not yet in deep sleep) as less conscious than a person who is fully awake. It is a difference in the extent to which one is conscious. According to the qualitative interpretation, monkeys are less conscious than humans because the contents of their consciousness are less ‘thick’ or ‘rich.’ Philosophers sometimes distinguish between ‘sparse’ and ‘thick’ levels of consciousness. At the ‘sparse’ level, consciousness includes only such ‘raw feels’ as seeing red and feeling pain. At the ‘thick’ level, consciousness includes ‘complex representations of time, space, cause, body, self, world and the organized structure of lived reality in all its conceptual and nonconceptual forms’(Van Gulick 2022). In other words, conscious experiences can vary in their richness and complexity in ways that align with multi-dimensional view in philosophy of mind, including Birch et al. (2020). The idea that newborns and nonhuman animals lack richer or more complex kinds of consciousness is not inconsistent with these interpretations. It is possible, e.g. that consciousness with a more complex phenomenological structure requires experience in the world and cognitive sophistication that newborns and nonhuman animals lack.

Cluster 3 captures a bimodal distribution in the assignment of consciousness to other entities. Respondents in this cluster tend to attribute consciousness only to mammals with a central nervous system. However, our list of surveyed entities does not enable us to determine what aspect of mammalian nervous systems are most salient to Cluster 3, or whether attributions of consciousness within this cluster extend to non-mammalian species (e.g. avian, reptilian, or insect species). In the case of brain organoids, it has been argued that both biological correlates of consciousness and behavioral evidence are needed for attributions of consciousness to be justified, and that the needed behavioral evidence is lacking in the case of brain organoids (Boyd 2024; Boyd and Lipshitz 2024). Based on this rationale, a similar position can help us interpret the responses that are typical of this cluster. Mammals with a central nervous system and brain organoids may both have the basic biological ‘hardware’ thought to be necessary for consciousness, but only the former behave in ways that indicate consciousness. Thus, Cluster 3 respondents may discount the potential for consciousness in non-organismal systems, a hypothesis that calls for evaluating attributions in a more phylogenetically diverse panel of entities.

Thus, we can make sense of the internal logic of the responses typical of each cluster. We do not want to claim that respondents subscribe to any of the views just mentioned or to some other view in their vicinity. Future research will be needed to establish these connections. However, since all the views used here to rationalize responses have been defended by philosophers, we should regard all clusters as representing consistent and legitimate possible views in public discourse.

Impact of consciousness attribution on moral judgements

We observed strong correlations between the attribution of consciousness and moral decisions about biocomputers. In the ethics literature, consciousness is often regarded as a necessary and sufficient condition for having moral status (Farahany et al. 2018; Lavazza and Massimini 2018; Bayne et al. 2020; Peña-Guzmán 2022). If public views correspond to this standard view, we expect members of Cluster 1–who view consciousness as widely distributed–to show greater ethical concern for biocomputers than members of clusters 2 and 3. The data confirms this hypothesis. However, we found no significant differences across the clusters regarding the question of whether the benefits of biocomputer research outweigh the ethical risks, and we found high levels of support for biocomputer research in all groups. Moreover, participants within Cluster 1, who attributed the highest levels of consciousness to biocomputers, were also the most supportive and likely to view the benefits as outweighing the ethical risks. This is surprising, because we would expect greater ethical concern for biocomputers to be correlated with lower support for research or great endorsement of prohibitions on research, unless the rate of benefit gain outpaces the rise in ethical concerns. One possible interpretation of this data is that people tend to ignore moral status considerations when evaluating the desirability of research.

However, there are other possible interpretations of the data. Even those who hold that consciousness grounds moral status and believe that biocomputers are conscious may nonetheless believe that biocomputers have a lower moral status than paradigmatic humans. There is no contradiction in this combination of views because one may believe that there are other characteristics aside from consciousness that ground a high moral status in paradigmatic humans (Gosepath 2014; Kagan 2019). One may also believe that consciousness alone grounds moral status yet hold that the consciousness found in biocomputers is more sparse (i.e. less rich and less complex) than that found in paradigmatic humans, accounting for the lower moral status of biocomputers. Notably, respondents in clusters 2 and 3 regard biocomputers as always morally less important than humans with cluster 1 leaning toward a less hierarchical view. Thus, even respondents who believe that biocomputers are conscious and have some moral status are likely to support scientific research on biocomputers and oppose an outright prohibition on such research. Such responses align with the moral principle that if one entity has a lower moral status than another, the former’s welfare and interests matter less than the latter’s (Kagan 2019).

Furthermore, even if one accepts the view that consciousness is necessary and sufficient for moral status, attributions of consciousness have conflicting implications for the desirability of research on biocomputers. On the one hand, if consciousness indicates moral status, it indicates ethical risks in research. On the other hand, if consciousness implies a resemblance to human brains, it indicates greater benefits to humans in research using biocomputers. This is an ethical dilemma that has been previously noted in the literature (Greely 2021). The dilemma may also explain why members of Cluster 1, who tend to be more willing to attribute consciousness and human similarity to biocomputers than members of other clusters, do not express lower support for research on biocomputers.

The data supports the claim that respondents who are more likely to agree that the benefits outweigh the ethical risks are also more likely to view biocomputers as similar to human beings–a trend consistent across clusters. This result contrasts with a prominent view in the bioethical literature on the topic, one that treats the mentioned dilemma as a genuine dilemma, i.e. a conflict between values that have roughly the same ethical weight (Greely 2021). The respondents in this study appear to choose a ‘horn’ in this dilemma: they tend to side with research benefits as the decisive ethical consideration.

Impact of perceived similarity to humans

Some authors hold that all members of Homo sapiens have a higher moral status than other entities (Setiya 2018). Other authors lament what they perceive to be a view based on folk conceptions of species membership and its moral significance (Singer 1986; Niemelä 2011). Respondents in all clusters tend to agree that humans have a higher moral status than biocomputers, but that does not indicate where they stand on this broader philosophical debate. There is, however, a different issue that this study can shed light on. Some authors argue that foundational distinctions between human and nonhuman animals are central to moral reasoning (Setiya 2018). According to this proposal, we care about the human/nonhuman boundary, and we do not wish to see nonhumans cross it (Singer 1986; Niemelä 2011). This study failed to identify a cluster in which all humans and only humans were said to be conscious. What we found is that those who deny consciousness to nonhuman entities also deny it to some humans (so they deny that all humans are conscious), and those who affirm that all humans are conscious also affirm that certain other entities are (so they deny that only humans are conscious). At least regarding consciousness attribution, respondents do not appear to protect a strict boundary between humans and other entities.

Some authors have proposed that it is not consciousness per se but particular dimensions of consciousness that matter for moral status, such as self-awareness, having pleasant or unpleasant conscious states, or having conscious preferences (Singer 1986; Shepherd 2018; Koplin et al. 2021; Boyd and Lipshitz 2024). Whether respondents believe something similar is hard to tell, because in this study, people who tend to attribute some dimension of consciousness to an entity also tend to attribute other dimensions to it. For example, responses in Cluster 1 are more likely to attribute perception to biocomputers than responses in Clusters 2 and 3, but they are also more likely to attribute preferences and self-awareness to biocomputers than responses in Clusters 2 and 3. We thus do not have a clear case where we can test respondents’ views toward an entity that has only some dimension of consciousness.

That said, when considering the different clusters, certain dimensions of consciousness may be relevant for respondents’ ethical evaluation of the desirability of research on biocomputers. For example, in Cluster 1, perceived benefits over risks were positively correlated with attributions of self-awareness. In Cluster 2, this correlation was significant only for preferences. And in Cluster 3, a positive correlation was found with attributions of perception. There is no straightforward explanation of this trend, but it gestures at the possibility that different dimensions of consciousness may be more salient to different respondents, depending on the cluster to which they belong.

Future research will be needed to answer several questions not answered by the current data. First, it will be useful to examine the extent to which respondents subscribe to the views that have been mentioned here to describe and rationalize their responses. Second, more studies are needed to determine whether respondents who deny that certain entities are conscious have ‘sparse’ or ‘thick’ consciousness in mind. Third, more studies are needed to determine whether people tend to subscribe to the view that consciousness is necessary and sufficient to moral status and whether people’s willingness to attribute consciousness to various entities is correlated with their tendency to subscribe to this view. Finally, more research is needed to explain why, in different clusters, different dimensions of consciousness are correlated with the tendency to judge that the benefits of research on biocomputers outweigh the ethical risks.

Several additional limitations are worth consideration. First, empirical bioethics studies to date have taken different approaches to describing or implying consciousness in survey instruments. These word-choice differences could signal meaningful differences to respondents and account for some inconsistencies in responses across studies. However, our study suggests that board views about the scope of consciousness, unrelated to those specifically about biocomputers, play a more dominant role. Second, we gave respondents the opportunity to assess the level of consciousness for a limited set of entities. Future work could explore more diverse and phylogenetically distributed representatives of species/entities that may result in different number of clusters, however, we focus here on the conceptual role these cluster groups have on moral reasoning rather than attempting to make claims that are representative of particular populations regarding the absolute number of cluster groups that may exist, which are likely to vary across cultures. Finally, we utilized a variety of questions that serve as proxies for measuring moral status without the benefit of a validated scale for moral status, which future work could benefit from developing.

Our study provides initial insights that describe the public’s attribution of consciousness, and how antecedent beliefs can drive moral and epistemic opinions about emerging neurotechnology. While we identify the presence of underlying factors that shape public attributions of consciousness, this study is unable to determine the relative distribution of these views among a representative sample of the public. A larger, more demographically representative sample would provide insights into the public’s generalized ethical attitudes and provide more statistical power to detect subtle differences between the informational vignettes. Moreover, we expect that the proportion individuals within cluster groups are likely to vary across populations with varying cultural or epistemic beliefs about the consciousness of other beings, which based on our study, are likely to inform ethical opinions toward biocomputing technology. We also do not exclude the possibility that additional cluster groups might be identified if the public were asked about the consciousness of additional beings. Yet our general conclusion holds that beliefs about the possession of consciousness act to guide epistemic and moral decisions in novel contexts. We propose that future public engagement, communication, education, and policymaking initiatives should recognize and take into consideration the diverse views potentially held by the public.
