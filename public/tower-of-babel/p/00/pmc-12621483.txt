Patient-reported Outcome Measures in Pediatric Orthopaedics
Authors: Matthew Mohr, Philip K McClure, Niamh C Nowlan, Connor J Green
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImplementing patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) related to health-related quality of life (HRQoL) enhances how patients perceive their quality of care, boosts patient involvement and autonomy, and helps clinicians identify aspects of the patient’s condition that might otherwise be missed. However, PROMs are underutilized in pediatric orthopaedic clinical practice, with many conditions lacking validated PROM tools. In this review, we summarize the use of PROMs in pediatric orthopaedics. We provide an overview of the definitions related to different aspects of PROMs, what makes a high-quality PROM, and how to select one or more PROMs for general or specific pediatric orthopaedic patients. The goal of this primer is to serve as a resource for clinicians interested in implementing appropriate PROMs in their pediatric orthopaedic practice.Key Concepts(1)Health-related quality of life is the primary outcome that patient-reported outcome measures aim to quantify.(2)Validity is a critical concept in assessing the quality of PROMs, particularly content, construct, and criterion validity.(3)Generic PROMs can be applied to anyone, while condition-specific PROMs target the details most important to a specific population.(4)PROMs are significantly underutilized in pediatric orthopaedics, with a paucity of high-quality, condition-specific PROMs in the specialty.(5)PROM development, selection, and assessment should be aided by using available tools and protocols.Keywords: Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), Health-related quality of life (HRQoL), Pediatric orthopaedics

Introduction

Measuring health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a crucial metric in surgical care. It can assess the effectiveness of an intervention, identify and prioritize issues, improve communication between patients and the clinical team, and encourage patients to be more involved in their care [1,2]. HRQoL can also be linked to financial metrics and help support business cases for new and existing clinical practices. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are tools—usually surveys or questionnaires—that provide a measurement of HRQoL. PROMs assess a concept that cannot be directly observed: the patient’s subjective experience [3,4]. PROMs are underused in pediatric orthopaedic clinical practice [5,6]. Some conditions within pediatric orthopaedics lack validated, specific PROMs, while many of the condition-specific PROMs in use do not meet current consensus-based standards [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Most studies in pediatric orthopaedics do not routinely report the use of PROMs, with indications that over half of those reporting PROMs used non-validated ones [6]. Of the 73 PROMs identified by one study in 2018 for pediatric orthopaedics, most did not measure HRQoL, and most were generic rather than condition-specific [6]. In this review, we provide an overview of what constitutes a validated PROM and offer recommendations on how to select one or more PROMs for general or disease-specific pediatric orthopaedic patients.

What is a PROM?

HRQoL is a subjective, latent concept that cannot be measured directly. Therefore, PROMs assess HRQoL by asking questions about constructs. Constructs are clearly defined, with measurable concepts chosen as analysis units, such as walking ability. Each construct exists within a domain, which is a collection of related constructs (like mobility) that make up part of health-related quality of life. Responses can either be converted into an “index score,” which is a single number, or a “descriptive score,” composed of numbers from 1 to 10 linked together (eg, 35,132), with each number reflecting a specific area score.

PROMs can measure functional health status, HRQoL, or a combination of both. PROMs measuring HRQoL face some fundamental issues compared to those assessing functional health status. HRQoL is defined in multiple ways in the literature, often overlapping with perceived health status and quality of life, leading to confusion and a lack of comparability between some results of various HRQoL PROMs [[12], [13], [14]]. There is a lack of theoretical frameworks for HRQoL-related PROMs. For example, the World Health Organization (WHO)’s theoretical framework, the international classification of functioning, disability, and health (WHO-ICF), does not include quality of life [10].

Quality of PROMs

The quality of a PROM is defined by its measurement properties [15]. Measurement properties fall into three categories, namely validity, reliability, and responsiveness [15]. Mokkink et al. [16] state that the most important measurement property of a PROM is validity, which is composed of content validity, construct validity, and criterion validity, with content validity being the most important of those [16]. This assesses if the items are relevant, comprehensible, and comprehensive in measuring the construct. Reliability refers to how free of error the PROM score is, consisting of internal consistency, measurement error, and reliability as a counterpart to measurement error [16]. Internal consistency is how related the scores are for each individual item; if they are consistent or if they contradict each other [16]. Measurement error is the proportion of variance in scores due to the PROM’s systematic, random error, while reliability is the proportion of variance due to true differences [16]. The final measurement property category, responsiveness, refers to how sensitive the PROM is to detecting change [16]. This may be change over time in the same patient or differentiating between different patients/cohorts.

The majority of PROMs used are not fully validated for the target populations of their studies [6,17,18]. Reviews of studies in pediatric oncology and other pediatric specialties have found that studies evaluating PROM validity often focus only on content validity or internal consistency, neglecting other measurement properties [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Uimonen et al. [23] note that many pediatric surgical studies evaluating PROMs lack evidence for the tool’s validity. This may be because many PROMs predate the checklists and guidelines defined by COSMIN [19].

Types of PROMs

PROMs can be divided into three categories: generic, condition-specific, or individualized [24]. Generic PROMs assess overall QoL/HRQoL across a wide range of areas considered important to the average person’s HRQoL [25], while condition-specific PROMs focus on concepts especially relevant to a particular condition. Generic PROMs are useful because they can be applied universally and allow for comparisons with healthy populations [25]. However, they may overlook health aspects that most directly influence a patient’s HRQoL. To address this, condition-specific PROMs often show higher responsiveness, reliability, validity, and credibility than generic PROMs [6,26,27]. While this can be advantageous when assessing a specific patient group, many of these tools lack sufficient validation studies. Consequently, they may not be more beneficial than generic PROMs [24]. Individualized PROMs are still rarely used in the literature [24]. They do not ask standardized, predetermined questions. Instead, they let patients create their own constructs of their HRQoL, choosing measurement units and rating themselves on relevant scales. This approach prioritizes the areas most important to the individual patient within their condition, rather than what health care providers typically consider relevant [24]. Some authors argue that relying solely on one type of PROM, whether validated or not, is limiting. They suggest that combining a generic and a condition-specific PROM, or using a generic PROM with condition-specific modules, provides the most accurate reflection of a young person [24].

For pediatric conditions lacking a validated, disease-specific PROM, using a generic PROM is recommended over ad hoc surveys or adult, condition-specific measures [9,24,28]. An initiative that supports the use of a generic PROM is the Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS), developed by the U.S. National Institutes of Health. PROMIS is a large item bank designed to capture patient-reported outcomes across a broad range of conditions [29,30], covering physical, mental, and social well-being constructs [30]. It has been shown that PROMIS is compatible with the WHO-ICF, demonstrating the tool’s versatility [31]. However, PROMIS measures are less responsive than condition-specific PROMs in detecting changes in children with cerebral palsy undergoing surgery [32]. Kratz et al. also discovered that PROMIS was unable to differentiate among groups of children with cerebral palsy with known differences in mobility [33]. Therefore, although PROMIS is a positive development, it is not yet an ideal solution, at least not for all conditions. In condition-specific PROMs, the items are tailored to patient groups and capture their experiences more effectively than generic PROMs [34]. Therefore, a high-quality condition-specific instrument will have better measurement properties than a high-quality generic PROM [24]. The areas of pediatric orthopaedics where condition-specific PROMs are most commonly used include scoliosis and cerebral palsy [6,35,36]. The Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) PROM was reported as the most frequently used PROM in pediatric orthopaedics in 2016 [6] but the use of the updated version (SRS-22r) has been reported as superior to the original SRS [35]. However, the early onset scoliosis 24-item questionnaire has been reported to have the strongest evidence for validity, reliability, and responsiveness [[37], [38], [39]] including for translated versions [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]].

Cerebral palsy (CP) is another condition for which PROMs have frequently been used to evaluate outcomes [46]. One study identified four specific PROMs measuring HRQoL in patients with CP [47]. Eighty-nine percent of the constructs the items measured were mappable onto WHO-ICF categories, meaning that generic and CP-specific PROMs could be compared. Validation studies have recommended the use of generic PROMs, such as Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0, Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America Measure (PODCI), and Pediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory-Computer Adaptive Test [[48], [49], [50]], combined with CP-specific PROMs [[51], [52], [53], [54]]. Many of the CP-specific PROMs focus on certain aspects of the condition. For example, there is support for the use of ACTIVLIM-CP for upper and lower limb use in daily function, Gait Outcomes Assessment List for mobility, and the Questionnaire on Pain Caused by Spasticity (QPS) [[55], [56], [57], [58]].

A small number of studies have evaluated HRQoL for specific symptoms linked with spina bifida [59,60], while a few condition-specific validated PROMs measuring overall HRQoL for patients with spina bifida have also been proposed [[61], [62], [63]]. There is a lack of validated, condition-specific PROMs with strong evidence for use in areas of pediatric orthopaedics other than adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, CP, and spina bifida, including upper limb conditions [8], sport-related wrist or hand injuries [7], and childhood fractures [64]. For example, we did not find any PROMs specific to patients with Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease (LCPD, or Perthes’ disease), but did find validation studies supporting the use of PROMIS for LCPD [51,52].

Individualized PROMs are used less often than generic or condition-specific PROMs [24]. They have undefined domains and constructs, allowing patients to determine which parts of their QoL are most affected by their condition [24]. This can promote more patient-centered care and emphasize the significance of specific life areas impacted by the condition [24,53]. Individualized PROMs may also be better suited for use in multilingual or multicultural settings [53]. The Patient-Generated Index (PGI) [54] is among the most studied individualized PROMs. PGI reveals a wide range of what patients consider most important aspects of HRQoL, suggesting that a “one-size-fits-all” approach to measuring HRQoL may be inadequate [53]. While one review found that PGI has good validity and responsiveness [65], several studies warn that response shift can cause measurement error and confound property evaluations [65,66]. A response shift occurs when a patient, often facing a terminal or life-changing illness, changes their understanding of QoL without any real change in their condition. Response shift can happen with all types of PROMs [67] but has an especially strong impact on individualized PROMs [68]. The use of PGI to evaluate the occurrence and severity of response shift highlights another potential application of individualized PROMs [69].

Scoliosis was the only condition in pediatric orthopaedics for which we found the use of an individualized PROM (the PGI) to assess QoL [70]. Lonner et al. [70] used PGI, including an item measuring “decision regret” in patients undergoing surgery for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. The study identified 14 different domains of particular relevance to the patient group, including sports, general function, and overall fitness [70]. Interestingly, the concerns reported by patients were not consistent with those reported by their parents [70].

Using PROMs in pediatric patients

Integrating PROMs into pediatric care presents a unique challenge because children are often unable or unwilling to complete PROMs themselves [71]. Proxy-reported outcome measures occur when surveys or questionnaires are filled out by an informant. In pediatrics, the informant is usually a parent or guardian who is believed to have in-depth knowledge of their child’s well-being [72]. However, the literature criticizes proxy-reported outcomes for often measuring HRQoL differently than the child would. There is conflicting evidence on whether parents tend to rate their children’s HRQoL higher or lower than the child’s self-assessment [[71], [72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78], [79]]. Some studies suggest that agreement between parent and child increases with the child’s age [80,81] while others argue it decreases [71], and some state there is no correlation with age at all [78,82]. Further research is necessary to fully understand the discrepancies between child and proxy reports in various situations. Current evidence suggests that PROMs should be administered directly to the child whenever possible as the primary outcome [74,75], with proxy reports acting as a secondary, complementary measure [81].

Development and selection of PROMs

Given the scarcity of pediatric orthopaedic condition-specific PROMs, there have been calls for the development and validation of more condition-specific PROMs for the specialty [1,[9], [10], [11],28]. However, there are few guidelines or protocols on how to develop a PROM. The two phases of creating a new PROM are development and validation [83]. Development involves consulting experts or conducting literature reviews to identify key areas of concern for a given condition, while validation involves ensuring adequate face and content validity. This is done through interviews or focus groups with the target patient populations [83]. DeWalt et al. [84] describe a methodology to develop an item pool from the PROMIS item bank, which involves identifying, classifying, and reviewing items, then using focus groups to ensure full coverage of the target domain. The next step is to conduct interviews to obtain feedback on individual items, and then finally, refine readability and categorize the items [84].

Consulting patients when designing a PROM is essential [8,20,76,85,86]. Consultation is typically conducted through interviews, focus groups, or both [85]. The method of engaging with patients and caregivers for developing and validating new PROMs for pediatric orthopaedic patients has been most thoroughly described for two PROMs: LIMB-Q KIDS for lower limb deformities and QPS for patients with cerebral palsy [55,[87], [88], [89]]. When starting to develop QPS, Geister et al. [55] began with a literature review and then conducted concept elicitation interviews with children with CP and their parents or caregivers [55]. They used open-ended questions followed by probing questions to identify the most relevant areas related to the children’s HRQoL. In developing LIMB-Q KIDS, Chhina et al. [89] conducted semi-structured, face-to-face interviews with children with lower limb deformities and their parents across five international sites, representing low-, middle-, and high-income countries, following their pre-published protocol [88]. After creating an initial set of items, both Geister et al. [55] and Chhina et al. [87] presented children and their guardians with the preliminary PROM in multiple interview waves [55,87]. They used a “think aloud” protocol, encouraging children to vocalize any thoughts or confusion. The interviewers noted when children hesitated over certain words and invited them to suggest rephrasing. The children’s understanding of each question was also assessed. At the end of the interviews, Chhina et al. [87] asked if there was anything important about the child’s leg that had not been addressed to ensure completeness and conducted final reviews with parents and clinical experts to evaluate readability and overall comprehensiveness.

When insufficient patient input is included in PROM development, the risk of low content validity and measurement error increases [85]. Many validated PROMs used in pediatric orthopaedics require a reading level higher than that of the average individual in the target population, which may lead to a large proportion of respondents (both children and proxies) not fully understanding the questions they are being asked [90]. In 2016, Taylor et al. [90] found that 29 of 35 validated PROMs in pediatric orthopaedics had a reading level above the average respondent, based on the Flesch Reading Ease Score, which assesses text difficulty based on average syllables per word and sentence length and correlates with age and level of education [91]. Such PROMs included earlier versions of SRS-22r (SRS-22 and SRS-30), the PODCI adolescent and parent reports, the pediatric International Knee Documentation Committee score, and EuroQol-5D. Badarudeen and Sabharwal wrote about this readability instrument and others commonly used in health care settings [92].

With the multitude of PROMs available and the varying evidence supporting or discouraging their use, it can be overwhelming to choose which PROM to use in clinical practice or research. Van der Wees addressed this issue by creating the “PROM cycle,” a freely accessible framework that guides users on how to select and implement PROMs [3]. To help evaluate the quality of a PROM, Terwee et al. developed a literature-based methodology to examine the measurement properties of a PROM [93]. For researchers working on PROM validation, the COSMIN initiative has published guidelines for assessing PROM validation studies to determine their risk of bias [94]. Additionally, COSMIN has released reporting guidelines for studies on PROM measurement properties [95].

Conclusion

As the number of available PROMs grows and their use in clinical and research settings becomes more routine, it is important to remember the ongoing need for high-quality PROMs suitable for pediatric populations. The field of pediatric orthopaedics arguably has access to too few high-quality, condition-specific PROMs, with some continuing to rely on inappropriate ones. A lack of clear foundational definitions, understanding of the usefulness of different types of PROMs, the potential confounding effects of proxy-reported measures, and the absence of widely accepted guidelines all contribute to the complexity of integrating PROMs into research and care. We hope this primer offers some clarity on the field of patient-reported outcomes and how it is currently applied in pediatric orthopaedics. We have highlighted useful tools for research and clinical practice involving PROMs that we hope will advance the quality of care in pediatric orthopaedics.
