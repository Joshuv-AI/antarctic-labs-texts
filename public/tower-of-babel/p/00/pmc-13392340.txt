Role of the gut microbiota in hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis: potential mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Zi-Yi Zhang, Zhao-Dong Li, Chang-Wei Peng, Hong Peng, Xin-Sen Zou, Yuan-Xia Zhu, Wen-Li Liao, Tian-Ke Li, Li Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) is an acute, noninfectious inflammatory disease caused by excessively high levels of serum triglycerides, leading to an overproduction of free fatty acids. This damages the pancreas and disrupts pancreatic microcirculation, leading ultimately to HTG-AP. Clinically, HTG-AP is associated with increasing incidence, multiple complications, and a higher risk of severe disease or adverse outcomes. There is a lack of unified standards, both domestically and internationally, for the clinical management of HTG-AP. This presents challenges for HTG-AP treatment while also offering opportunities to explore more effective interventional approaches. Preliminary research suggests the importance of the gut microbiota in the development and progression of HTG-AP, potentially mediated by influencing lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses. However, little is known of the potential function of the gut microbiota in the etiology of HTG-AP. This review summarizes current evidence on gut microbiota alterations in HTG-AP, with emphasis on lipid metabolism, intestinal barrier injury, and immune-inflammatory pathways. We also discuss microbiota-targeted interventions as investigational strategies that require HTG-AP-specific clinical validation.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is one of the most common acute abdominal conditions seen in clinical practice. AP is linked to the activation of pancreatic enzymes by various pathogenic factors, resulting in localized pancreatic inflammation, with or without local or systemic complications. The condition is typically associated with a sudden onset and rapid progression. 1 Common causes of AP include biliary tract diseases, alcohol abuse, and hyperlipidemia. With changes in lifestyle, hyperlipidemia has surpassed alcohol consumption to become the second leading cause of acute pancreatitis in China. 2 Hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) represents a distinct subtype characterized by serum triglyceride (TG) levels exceeding 11.3 mmol/L or serum TG levels between 5.65 and 11.3 mmol/L with chylous serum. In contrast to patients with AP from other causes, those with HTG-AP typically experience more severe inflammation, higher rates of organ failure, and a higher risk of mortality.3–5 The role of the gut microbiota in AP has been recognized in recent years. The gut microbiota is a core component of the intestinal ecosystem and is closely linked to metabolic processes, maintenance of the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and regulation of host immune responses. 6 Furthermore, recent metagenomic and metabolomic studies have further demonstrated the involvement of the gut microbiota in AP severity and metabolic alterations, indicating the therapeutic potential of microbiological interventions.7–9 Recent subtype-specific microbiome studies suggest that hyperlipidemic acute pancreatitis may exhibit distinct microbial signatures and impaired short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) biosynthetic capacity compared with other AP subtypes. 10 To provide a clearer understanding of disease pathogenesis, this review adopts an integrated mechanistic framework linking hypertriglyceridemia, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and pancreatic injury. Elevated triglycerides and free fatty acids (FFAs) initiate systemic metabolic disturbances that reshape gut microbiota composition. This dysbiosis subsequently impairs intestinal barrier integrity, alters microbial metabolite production, and promotes immune dysregulation, including activation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). These interconnected processes contribute to both the initiation and progression of HTG-AP, rather than acting as isolated events.

Review methodology

This is a narrative review. The literature base was updated through searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus up to March 2026 using combinations of the terms “hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis,” “gut microbiota,” “microbiome,” “short-chain fatty acids,” “Paneth cells,” “intestinal barrier,” “neutrophil extracellular traps,” “lipopolysaccharide,” “bile acids,” “probiotics,” “prebiotics,” and “fecal microbiota transplantation.” Priority was given to mechanistic studies directly related to HTG-AP or hyperlipidemia-aggravated AP, comparative studies versus non-HTG AP, and recent translational or clinical reports. Where direct HTG-AP evidence was limited, closely related AP or metabolic-microbiome studies were used only to support biologically plausible links.

Pathophysiological basis of HTG-AP: Direct lipotoxicity of FFAs

The currently accepted HTG-AP pathogenesis is centered primarily on the FFA hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that during HTG-AP development, dramatically elevated FFA levels in the pancreatic parenchyma and its microcirculation lead to a direct assault on the pancreas. Within the pancreas, extensive hydrolysis of triglycerides within chylomicrons (CM) and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) within the pancreatic capillary bed by pancreatic lipase leads to local concentrations of FFAs that can induce significant cytotoxicity. 11 This damage is direct and independent of the gut microbiota, and represents the first phase of HTG-AP that distinguishes it from other types of AP. The primary mechanism underlying the direct lipotoxicity of FFAs is described below.

Microcirculatory disorders

FFAs can impair the pancreatic microcirculation via multiple pathways, thereby triggering ischemia and secondary damage. HTG is frequently associated with a hypercoagulable state. Substantial accumulation of FFAs and CM in the capillary bed of the pancreas increases the viscosity of the plasma, leading to obstruction of capillaries and subsequent impairment of the pancreatic microcirculation. Additionally, platelet aggregation disrupts the balance between the vasoconstrictors thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and prostacyclin (PGI2), causing constriction of the pancreatic microvasculature and exacerbating microcirculatory impairment.12,13 Moreover, excessively high levels of FFAs that exceed the binding capacity of albumin may directly damage vascular endothelial cells, leading to endothelial dysfunction, vascular leakage, and activation of the coagulation system.14,15 Zhang et al. 16 demonstrated that saturated FFAs, such as palmitic acid, can inhibit the activity of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), leading to downregulation of its downstream target Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2). KLF2 is a core transcription factor associated with the maintenance of endothelial homeostasis; its reduction decreases the activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and the production of nitric oxide (NO). NO is a potent vasodilator and anti-inflammatory factor. Its deficiency induces microvascular constriction, ischemia, and cellular damage, potentially leading to tissue ischemia, hypoxia, and barrier dysfunction, thereby significantly exacerbating pancreatic injury.

Oxidative stress and apoptosis

Insulin resistance is associated with markedly increased FFA production. This can ultimately induce mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to substantial accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells and thereby triggering severe oxidative stress. 17 ROS not only attack cell membranes, proteins, and DNA directly, causing cellular dysfunction, but also activate multiple signaling pathways that trigger programmed cell death in endothelial cells. 18 Su et al. 19 established a mouse model of severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) and discovered that a high-fat diet exacerbates SAP-induced damage to the intestinal barrier through inflammatory responses, necrosis, and oxidative stress. This exacerbation is mediated primarily by the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-receptor-interacting protein 3 (RIP3) pathway. Furthermore, Zhang et al. 20 showed that ROS generated by the RIP3 pathway trigger a positive feedback loop in tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-induced necrotic apoptosis, thereby elucidating the mechanism underlying signal amplification in this form of cell death. In addition, lipid overload disrupts the normal functioning of the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to the unfolded protein response (UPR). Sustained activation of the RIP3 axis stimulates a series of pro-apoptotic pathways, including those involving inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), all of which upregulate c/EBP and induce apoptosis, thereby upregulating C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) and inducing cell death. Furthermore, FFAs can promote ER stress-associated apoptosis by activating the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway. 21

Inflammatory signal transduction

FFAs can themselves act as signaling molecules to drive macrophage-associated inflammatory responses, resulting in a vicious cycle of inflammation. 22 Fatty acid transport protein 1 (FATP1) is the most abundantly expressed fatty acid transporter subtype in macrophages. It enhances the inflammatory response in macrophages by increasing fatty acid uptake, thereby elevating intracellular ceramide levels and activating JNK signaling pathways. 23 Ye et al. 24 demonstrated through non-targeted metabolomics and other methods that activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) and PPARγ can reduce the accumulation of total FFAs and triglycerides in macrophages by inhibiting the expression of FATP1. Furthermore, FFAs can interfere with the physiological functions of adiponectin and its associated signaling pathways. Adiponectin is an adipokine with anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects. Inhibition of its signaling pathway by FFAs reverses its suppression of key pro-inflammatory transcription factors, such as nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), thereby amplifying local inflammatory responses in the pancreas.25,26 Xia et al. 27 described the “adiponectin paradox” in patients with type 2 diabetes. These authors showed that FFAs can stimulate adiponectin secretion by direct activation of the PPARγ pathway, while adiponectin can exacerbate the inflammatory responses through activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways.

The role of the gut microbiota in HTG-AP

The gut microbiota and lipid metabolism

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of host lipid metabolism. Clinical microbiome analyses have shown that gut microbial composition is associated with plasma triglyceride levels and apolipoprotein profiles. In particular, individuals with elevated triglycerides may exhibit reduced microbial diversity and decreased functional capacity for SCFA production. These findings suggest that gut microbial alterations are closely linked to systemic lipid metabolic status.28,29 Microbial metabolites provide a key mechanistic bridge between the gut microbiota and host lipid regulation. SCFAs, mainly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are generated by bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber and participate in intestinal barrier maintenance, mucosal immune regulation, energy metabolism, and lipid homeostasis. 30 Butyrate supports epithelial barrier integrity and mucin production, partly through G protein-coupled receptor signaling and epigenetic regulation.31,32 Propionate has been reported to inhibit hepatic lipid and cholesterol synthesis, whereas SCFAs more broadly influence lipolysis and lipogenesis in adipose tissue.33,34 Thus, reduced SCFA production may contribute to both metabolic disturbance and impaired intestinal homeostasis. In addition to SCFAs, the gut microbiota regulates lipid metabolism through bile acid transformation and host receptor signaling. Experimental evidence indicates that gut microbiota–bile acid crosstalk can regulate host lipid metabolism through the intestinal farnesoid X receptor (FXR)–fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) axis. 35 Gut microbiota has also been shown to reprogram intestinal lipid absorption and metabolism through the long non-coding RNA Snhg9 pathway. 36 Collectively, these findings indicate that microbial metabolites, bile acid signaling, and host lipid-regulatory pathways form an integrated gut microbiota–lipid metabolism network. Therefore, dysbiosis should not be viewed only as a consequence of pancreatic inflammation, but also as a potential contributor to lipid metabolic imbalance, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and immune-inflammatory amplification in HTG-AP.

Mechanistic links between gut microbiota and HTG-AP

How FFAs alter gut microbiota

Excessive FFAs, generated from triglyceride hydrolysis under hypertriglyceridemic conditions, can reshape gut microbiota composition through both direct ecological effects and indirect host-mediated mechanisms. From an ecological perspective, increased lipid availability changes the intestinal nutrient environment and favors microbial communities that are better adapted to lipid-rich conditions. Circulating and luminal FFA profiles have been shown to correlate with distinct gut microbiota signatures and altered SCFA patterns, suggesting that fatty acid overload is closely linked to changes in microbial structure and function.29,37 Another important mechanism involves bile acid remodeling. High-fat and hyperlipidemic states increase bile acid secretion and alter the composition of the intestinal bile acid pool. 38 Because bile acids possess detergent-like and antimicrobial properties, changes in bile acid composition can selectively suppress bile-sensitive commensals while promoting bile-tolerant and inflammation-associated taxa. In particular, secondary bile acids generated through microbial metabolism may further reshape microbial ecology and influence epithelial stress responses. These bile acid–microbiota interactions provide a mechanistic bridge between lipid overload and dysbiosis in HTG-AP.39,40 In addition, FFA overload can induce oxidative stress, low-grade mucosal inflammation, and epithelial metabolic stress, all of which disturb the anaerobic intestinal niche required for the maintenance of beneficial commensals. 41 These changes may reduce microbial diversity and decrease the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria, such as Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidaceae, while promoting opportunistic or pro-inflammatory microbial communities.42,43 These findings place FFA-driven dysbiosis at an early position in the HTG-AP cascade. How dysbiosis aggravates lipid metabolism

Gut microbiota dysbiosis can further aggravate hypertriglyceridemia by disrupting microbial metabolite production, bile acid signaling, and inflammatory-metabolic homeostasis. 44 SCFAs, especially acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are important microbial metabolites involved in intestinal barrier maintenance, energy metabolism, lipid oxidation, and hepatic lipid synthesis.30–32 A reduction in SCFA-producing bacteria may weaken these regulatory effects, thereby promoting lipogenesis, impairing lipid oxidation, and reducing the metabolic resilience of the host under hyperlipidemic conditions.33,34 Dysbiosis also affects lipid metabolism through bile acid signaling. Gut microbiota regulates the conversion of primary to secondary bile acids, while bile acids in turn signal through host receptors such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5). 45 Disturbance of this gut microbiota–bile acid axis may impair FXR–FGF19-mediated feedback regulation, thereby influencing hepatic triglyceride synthesis, very-low-density lipoprotein secretion, intestinal lipid absorption, and peripheral lipid clearance. Experimental work using humanized dyslipidemia models has shown that gut microbiota–bile acid crosstalk can regulate host lipid metabolism through the intestinal FXR–FGF19 axis, supporting the causal contribution of microbiota to dyslipidemia. 35 Furthermore, dysbiosis-related barrier dysfunction increases exposure to microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which can promote chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and hepatic lipid synthesis. This inflammatory-metabolic state may further aggravate triglyceride accumulation and increase the FFA burden. 46 Recent evidence also indicates that HTG-modulated gut microbiota can promote lysophosphatidylcholine generation in a TLR4-dependent manner, thereby aggravating pancreatic injury and linking microbial dysbiosis with lipid-derived toxic metabolites. 47 This bidirectional relationship suggests that dysbiosis can help sustain the metabolic environment that originally promoted it. How dysbiosis injures Paneth cells and disrupts the intestinal barrier

Paneth cells are central regulators of intestinal host–microbe homeostasis. Located at the base of small intestinal crypts, they secrete antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), including α-defensins and lysozyme, and thereby maintain microbial spatial organization, restrict opportunistic expansion, and support the intestinal stem cell niche. In HTG-AP, dysbiosis, lipid overload, bile acid disturbance, and mucosal inflammatory stress may converge to impair Paneth cell function.48–50 Experimental evidence indicates that hypertriglyceridemia-related acute necrotizing pancreatitis is associated with intestinal microbiota dysbiosis and decreased Paneth cell AMP levels. Reduced AMP secretion weakens colonization resistance, allowing further expansion of pathobionts and aggravation of dysbiosis. 51 In parallel, high-fat feeding has been shown to alter gut microbiota and Paneth cell antimicrobial peptide expression even before marked systemic inflammatory changes occur, suggesting that Paneth cell dysfunction may represent an early intestinal event in lipid-overload states. 43 Mechanistically, lipid overload can directly affect Paneth cells. High-fat diet-induced FABP4 expression in Paneth cells promotes PPARγ degradation through K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby suppressing defensin production and reprogramming the gut microbiota. 52 In addition, high-fat feeding increases bile acid exposure and activates TGR5 signaling in Paneth cells; bile acid toxicity can induce endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy, leading to reduced AMP secretion and dysbiosis. 39 Western diet-induced Paneth cell defects may also involve microbiome alterations, FXR activation, and type I interferon signaling in intestinal epithelial cells. 40 Paneth cell dysfunction further cooperates with epithelial barrier injury. Loss of AMPs, reduced SCFAs, mucus disruption, and inflammatory signaling contribute to downregulation or redistribution of tight junction proteins, including occludin, claudins, and ZO-1, thereby increasing intestinal permeability. Rather than describing this process simply as “bacterial translocation,” it may be more accurate to define it as barrier failure with leakage of microbial products, including LPS and other luminal antigens, with possible bacterial dissemination only under severe barrier disruption. These leaked microbial products can activate TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling, further impair tight junction integrity and amplify systemic inflammation.53–55 In this setting, Paneth cell dysfunction is better viewed as part of an intestinal amplification process rather than as an isolated epithelial defect. Loss of antimicrobial peptide-mediated microbial control can further destabilize the microbiota, while the damaged barrier permits microbial products to enter host tissues and intensify inflammatory signaling (Figure 3).

How dysbiosis activates NETs and drives immune-inflammatory imbalance

NETs represent an important effector mechanism linking gut dysbiosis to pancreatic and systemic inflammation. NETs are extracellular web-like structures composed of decondensed chromatin, histones, myeloperoxidase, neutrophil elastase, and other granule proteins. Although NETs contribute to antimicrobial defense, excessive NET formation under sterile inflammatory conditions can promote tissue injury, microvascular dysfunction, and sustained inflammatory amplification.56,57 In HTG-AP, dysbiosis may facilitate NET formation through both immune and metabolic pathways. First, intestinal barrier failure increases systemic exposure to microbial products such as LPS and other pathogen-associated molecular patterns. These signals can prime neutrophils through pattern-recognition receptors, particularly TLR4, and enhance ROS production, inflammasome activation, and cytokine release.53–55 This inflammatory milieu lowers the threshold for NETosis and promotes excessive neutrophil activation. Second, microbiota-derived metabolites provide a more HTG-AP-specific link between dysbiosis and NET formation. A recent study demonstrated that gut microbiota from patients with HTG-AP promoted neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, thereby aggravating pancreatic injury. Mechanistically, HTG-AP-associated dysbiosis was characterized by reduced abundance of Bacteroides uniformis, resulting in decreased taurine production. Reduced colonic taurine promoted IL-17 release, which in turn enhanced neutrophil recruitment and NET formation. Supplementation with Bacteroides uniformis or exogenous taurine attenuated HTG-AP by inhibiting NET release, suggesting that the Bacteroides uniformis–taurine–IL-17–NETs axis may represent a disease-relevant mechanism. 58 Excessive NETs can aggravate HTG-AP through multiple downstream effects. NET components can directly injure pancreatic acinar cells, activate trypsin-related pathways, promote endothelial dysfunction and microcirculatory impairment, and amplify cytokine release.56,57,59 NETs may also interact with inflammasome activation and pyroptosis-related pathways, thereby linking neutrophil activation with macrophage and epithelial inflammatory responses.60–62 Together, these processes shift the host response from controlled local inflammation toward immune-inflammatory imbalance, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and organ dysfunction. These observations suggest that NET formation is one of the immune effector pathways through which intestinal dysbiosis may be translated into pancreatic injury. Integrated gut microbiota–host interactions in HTG-AP

The gut microbiota interacts with the host through microbial metabolites, barrier regulation, immune signaling, and lipid-derived inflammatory mediators. 29 In HTG-AP, dysbiosis is associated with reduced SCFA-producing bacteria, impaired Paneth cell antimicrobial function, increased intestinal permeability, and enhanced exposure to microbial products.42,46,51 HTG-modulated gut microbiota may further aggravate pancreatic injury by promoting lysophosphatidylcholine generation and inflammatory activation. 47 In parallel, dysbiosis-associated alterations in taurine metabolism promote IL-17-mediated neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, thereby linking intestinal microbial imbalance to pancreatic and systemic injury. 58 Together, these findings indicate that the gut microbiota participates in HTG-AP through coordinated metabolic, barrier-related, and immune-inflammatory pathways rather than through a single isolated mechanism.42,47,58

Role of gut microbiota across disease stages of HTG-AP

The role of the gut microbiota in HTG-AP should be understood from a disease-evolution perspective rather than as a single isolated event. In the initiation phase, severe hypertriglyceridemia and rapid hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins generate excessive FFAs, which directly induce pancreatic acinar injury, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and microcirculatory impairment.11,16 At the same time, lipid overload reshapes the intestinal microenvironment by altering lipid–microbiota interactions and microbial nutrient selection, thereby promoting dysbiosis. 29 These early microbial changes are reflected by reduced SCFA-producing commensals, altered microbial composition, impaired SCFA biosynthetic capacity and weakened Paneth cell-related barrier defense in HTG-AP.42,51,64 During the progression phase, persistent dysbiosis amplifies pancreatic and systemic inflammation by worsening lipid metabolic imbalance, reducing SCFA-related protective effects, increasing microbial product leakage, and promoting inflammatory activation.42,47,65 In the severe or systemic phase, dysbiosis-associated taurine depletion promotes IL-17-mediated neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, thereby aggravating pancreatic injury. 58 Together with lipid-derived toxic metabolites such as lysophosphatidylcholine, excessive NETs contribute to pancreatic necroinflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, and organ failure.47,58 In the later recovery or recurrence-prone stage, restoration of microbial homeostasis may influence epithelial repair, immune resolution, and metabolic rebalancing, but direct clinical evidence demonstrating long-term prognostic benefit in HTG-AP remains limited. 66

Therefore, microbiota alterations should be viewed as dynamic participants in HTG-AP pathogenesis and as potential stage-specific therapeutic targets, provided that future prospective studies validate their clinical relevance. This stage-specific framework provides a mechanistic rationale for microbiota-targeted interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and emerging metabolite-oriented strategies, while also emphasizing the need for rigorous clinical validation in HTG-AP. Microbiota-targeted therapeutic perspectives in HTG-AP

Given the roles of dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and immune activation in HTG-AP, microbiota-targeted interventions have emerged as potential therapeutic approaches. However, it is important to note that most current evidence is derived from preclinical studies, and robust clinical validation remains limited.

Probiotics and prebiotics

Probiotics and prebiotics may attenuate HTG-AP-related injury by restoring microbial balance, increasing short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, strengthening intestinal barrier integrity, and suppressing excessive inflammatory signaling. These mechanisms are directly related to the pathogenic pathways discussed above, particularly dysbiosis, barrier failure, and immune-inflammatory amplification. General microbiome studies and metabolic-disease reviews support the potential roles of probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, and postbiotics in regulating gut microbiota and host metabolism.67,68

Experimental studies provide mechanistic evidence for these effects. Probiotic-fermented wheat bran improved gut microbiota composition, increased fecal SCFA levels, and ameliorated lipid metabolic disturbance in high-fat diet-fed mice. 69 In acute pancreatitis models, Bifidobacterium spp. and their metabolite lactate reduced pancreatic and systemic inflammatory responses, whereas Clostridium butyricum modulated gut microbiota and metabolomic profiles through AMPK/NF-κB-related pathways.70,71 Prebiotic agents such as inulin, lactulose, and chitosan oligosaccharides have also been reported to promote SCFA production, improve intestinal homeostasis, and reduce pancreatic or intestinal injury in AP-related or inflammation-related settings.72–76 Clinical evidence remains inconsistent. A randomized controlled trial suggested that probiotics shortened hospitalization in mild acute pancreatitis, whereas the PROPATRIA trial reported increased mortality and bowel ischemia in predicted severe acute pancreatitis.77,78 A meta-analysis also found no significant benefit of probiotics in reducing pancreatic infection, surgical intervention, hospital stay, or mortality. 79 Therefore, probiotics and prebiotics should currently be regarded as investigational adjuncts rather than established treatments for HTG-AP.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) involves the transfer of gut microbiota from healthy donors to restore microbial balance in patients. 80 It has demonstrated high efficacy in recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, with cure rates exceeding 80%, and is recommended in clinical guidelines.81–83 Its therapeutic potential has also been explored in inflammatory bowel disease and metabolic disorders.84–86

In the context of acute pancreatitis, emerging evidence suggests potential benefits. Retrospective clinical studies in severe acute pancreatitis have reported improvements in systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction following FMT, associated with increased abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium. 87 In preclinical studies, FMT has been shown to remodel gut microbiota and increase levels of microbial metabolites such as nicotinamide mononucleotide, which alleviated pancreatic injury through activation of the SIRT3–PRDX5 pathway. 88 However, evidence supporting the use of FMT in HTG-AP remains limited. Key challenges include lack of standardized protocols, variability in donor selection, and insufficient data on long-term safety. Therefore, FMT should currently be considered a promising but experimental strategy rather than a routine therapeutic option for HTG-AP. Emerging microbiome- and metabolite-targeted strategies

Several emerging microbiome-targeted agents and natural compounds have shown protective effects in experimental pancreatitis by modulating microbial composition, restoring microbial metabolites, repairing the intestinal barrier, and suppressing inflammatory responses. GV-971 prevented severe acute pancreatitis by remodeling the microbiota–metabolic–immune axis, while Chaihuang Qingyi Huoxue granule ameliorated severe acute pancreatitis through gut microbiota modulation and intestinal mucosal barrier repair.89,90 Microbial metabolites may also act as therapeutic mediators; for example, Parabacteroides-derived acetate reduced neutrophil infiltration and alleviated experimental pancreatic injury. 91

Natural compounds, including Ganoderic acid A, Astragalus polysaccharides, and resveratrol, have also demonstrated microbiota-dependent protective effects in experimental models by improving gut microbial composition, increasing SCFA-related metabolites, inhibiting inflammatory pathways, and alleviating pancreatic injury.92–94 In addition, downstream inflammatory or metabolic targets, such as GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis/NET formation and triglyceride metabolism through the lipoprotein lipase pathway, may provide complementary therapeutic directions.95–97 However, these strategies are supported mainly by preclinical evidence, and their efficacy, safety, optimal dosing, and reproducibility in human HTG-AP remain unclear. Current limitations and future directions

Overall, microbiota-targeted interventions are mechanistically relevant to HTG-AP because they may modulate lipid metabolism, intestinal barrier integrity, microbial metabolite production, and immune-inflammatory activation. However, current evidence is insufficient to support routine clinical application. Most studies are animal experiments, mechanistic studies, retrospective analyses, or small clinical trials, and few are specifically designed for HTG-AP. Future studies should prioritize prospective HTG-AP-specific cohorts and randomized controlled trials, distinguish preclinical from clinical evidence, standardize intervention protocols, and integrate microbiome and metabolomic endpoints with clinically meaningful outcomes such as disease severity, organ failure, infected necrosis, recurrence, and long-term prognosis.

Conclusion

HTG-AP is a metabolically driven subtype of acute pancreatitis in which excessive triglyceride hydrolysis and FFA accumulation initiate pancreatic lipotoxicity, microcirculatory impairment, oxidative stress, and inflammatory injury. Accumulating evidence suggests that the gut microbiota is closely involved in this process by linking systemic metabolic disturbance to intestinal barrier dysfunction, altered microbial metabolite production, and immune-inflammatory activation.

From a disease-evolution perspective, gut microbiota alterations may contribute to different stages of HTG-AP. In the initiation phase, FFA overload and lipid-rich conditions may reshape the intestinal microbial ecosystem and promote dysbiosis. During disease progression, dysbiosis may further aggravate lipid metabolic imbalance, impair Paneth cell-related barrier defense, increase microbial product leakage, and amplify inflammatory signaling. In the severe or systemic phase, microbiota-associated metabolic changes, such as reduced taurine availability, may promote IL-17-mediated neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, thereby aggravating pancreatic injury and systemic inflammation. These interconnected processes support a microbiota–metabolism–barrier–immune framework for understanding HTG-AP pathogenesis.

Microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies, including probiotics, prebiotics, FMT, and emerging microbiome- or metabolite-oriented interventions, have shown mechanistic potential in modulating gut dysbiosis, SCFA production, intestinal barrier integrity, lipid metabolism, and immune-inflammatory responses. However, most supporting evidence is derived from animal experiments, mechanistic studies, retrospective analyses, or small clinical studies. Therefore, these interventions should currently be regarded as investigational or adjunctive strategies rather than established clinical treatments for HTG-AP.

Future research should prioritize HTG-AP-specific prospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials, with careful distinction between preclinical and clinical evidence. Integration of metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and single-cell sequencing may help clarify host–microbiota interactions, identify microbiota-based biomarkers for risk stratification, and guide the development of defined probiotic formulations, SCFA-based interventions, metabolite-targeted therapies, and standardized FMT protocols. Such studies will be essential for determining whether microbiota-targeted approaches can improve clinically meaningful outcomes in HTG-AP.
