Targeting the akt/mtor signaling pathway by maprotiline leads to tumor suppression in T-cell lymphoma
Authors: Xiaodong Li, Jie Chen, Riyi Zhang, Songyan Zou, Dongdong Yang, Wei Tu, Fuyi Xie, Yinyu Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractT-cell lymphoma (TCL) is a prevalent malignancy characterized by the aberrant proliferation of T cells. The molecular mechanism underlying TCL remains poorly understood, and effective therapeutic strategies are still limited. Maprotiline, a highly selective norepinephrine reuptake blocker, is primarily used in the treatment of various types of depression. Intriguingly, its potential therapeutic utility and underlying mechanisms in TCL have not been previously explored. In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that maprotiline significantly inhibits proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis in TCL cells. Furthermore, in vivo experiments using TCL xenograft mouse models revealed that maprotiline treatment effectively suppresses tumor progression while maintaining a favorable safety profile with minimal toxicity. Mechanistically, our findings reveal that maprotiline exerts its anti-tumor effect by regulating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in TCL. Notably, we discovered that maprotiline substantially enhances the sensitivity of TCL cells to histone deacetylase inhibitor, thereby unveiling a promising combination therapeutic strategy for TCL treatment. These findings not only expand our understanding of maprotiline’s pharmacological potential beyond its conventional antidepressant use, but also provide a novel therapeutic avenue for addressing the clinical challenges in TCL management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06571-z.Keywords: Maprotiline, Histone deacetylase inhibitor, Apoptosis, AKT/mTOR, T-cell lymphoma

Introduction

T-cell lymphoma (TCL) is a malignant T-cell-derived tumor with a high degree of malignancy and heterogeneity [1, 2]. It commonly occurs in the skin and mucous membranes, which can spread to other tissues and organs in the body as the disease worsens [3]. The diagnostic challenges associated with invasive pathological sampling often result in delayed detection, leading to underdiagnosis and missed opportunities for timely intervention in a substantial proportion of TCL patients. The current therapeutic landscape remains suboptimal, primarily due to the incomplete understanding of TCL pathogenesis, which has hindered the establishment of standardized treatment strategies. Despite recent advancements in targeted therapeutic approaches, the clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory, evidenced by a modest 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of merely 50% [4]. At present, the combination of multiple drugs with different mechanisms of action is commonly used in TCL treatment [5–8]. It was shown that the azacitidine-CHOP combination therapy offers favorable outcomes for TCL treatment, characterized by its high safety and excellent tolerability [9]. However, the clinical efficacy of these approaches is frequently compromised by the emergence of drug resistance and metastatic progression, ultimately culminating in therapeutic failure [10, 11]. Furthermore, the substantial adverse effect profile associated with conventional chemotherapeutic agents imposes significant limitations on their clinical applicability. Consequently, it is urgent to elucidate the pathogenesis and develop more safe and effective therapeutic drugs to improve the treatment of TCL.

Drug repurposing, an innovative strategy for discovering novel therapeutic applications of approved drugs, has emerged as a promising approach in modern pharmacotherapy [12–14]. Compared to new drug development, drug reuse can greatly reduce development cost, highlighting its pivotal role in clinical treatment [15]. The safety profile of repurposed drugs is well-established, as they have already undergone rigorous preclinical and clinical evaluations. Maprotiline, a highly selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, is clinically recognized for its potent antidepressant and neuropathic pain-relieving properties, making it a mainstay in the treatment of various depressive disorders [16–18]. Apart from the pharmacological roles of anti-depression, studies have shown that maprotiline and other antidepressants may have antitumor effects by inhibiting cell growth and triggering apoptosis [19]. Notably, Zheng et al. reported that maprotiline plays an antitumor role in liver cancer by inhibiting cholesterol biosynthesis [20]. A study by Suzanne M et al. demonstrated that maprotiline induces autophagic programmed cell death in Burkitt’s lymphoma (BL) and highlighted its potential therapeutic value in managing drug-resistant BL [21]. Despite these promising findings, the potential therapeutic role of maprotiline in TCL remains unexplored, further research is urgently needed to investigate the biological role of maprotiline in TCL and reveal the possible molecular mechanism.

The AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in TCL and various tumorigenic processes, where its aberrant activation facilitates uncontrolled cell proliferation and suppresses apoptosis, thereby contributing to tumor development [22–25]. Given its pivotal role in oncogenesis, targeting the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for TCL treatment. In this study, we aim to reveal the role of maprotiline on the proliferation, apoptosis, and drug sensitivity of TCL cells, and further elucidate the molecular mechanism both in vivo and in vitro. Utilizing TCL cell lines and xenografted tumor mouse models, we demonstrated for the first time that maprotiline exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting TCL cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis through modulation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. These findings highlight the potential of maprotiline as a novel therapeutic agent for TCL and provide a foundation for future clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The human TCL cell lines (Jurkat, H9, Hut-78, and HH) were ordered from the American Type Culture Collection. All cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a cell incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2.

Reagents and materials

Maprotiline, Chidamide, Belinostat, Z-VAD-FMK, and SC79 were purchased from Selleck (Shanghai, China). Protease and phosphatase inhibitors (78442) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The primary antibodies including AKT (4691), phospho-AKT (p-AKT) (4060), mTOR (2983), phospho-mTOR (p-mTOR) (5536), caspase-3 (9662), cleaved caspase-3 (9664), cleaved PARP (5625), phospho-S6K1 (9234), S6K1 (9202), phospho-4EBP1 (2855), 4EBP1 (9644) and GAPDH (5174) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). The secondary antibodies were purchased from Huabio (Hangzhou, China).

Cell viability assay

8 × 103 TCL cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subsequently exposed to varying concentrations of maprotiline or other agents. Following the designated incubation periods, cell viability was quantitatively evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments Winooski, VT, USA).

Cell migration assay

Transwell migration assay was performed to evaluate the migration ability of TCL cells by Transwell chambers (Corning, NY, USA). 2 × 105 cells (Jurkat or H9) resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium were seeded into the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was filled with RPMI 1640 medium containing either maprotiline (0, 10, and 20 µM) or SC79 (8 µM). The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation period, the upper chamber was carefully removed, and the plate was gently agitated to ensure uniform distribution. The number of cells that had migrated into the lower chamber was subsequently quantified.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (Liankebio, Hangzhou, China). Briefly, Jurkat or H9 cells were harvested after 48 h of treatment and resuspended in 500 µL of binding buffer. The cell suspension was then stained with 5 µL Annexin V-APC and 10 µL 7-AAD in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Apoptotic cell populations were quantified by flow cytometry using a FACSCalibur system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co, Ltd (Guangzhou, China), with transcripts showing a fold change of ≥ 1.5 and a P value < 0.05 being identified as differentially expressed genes. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying maprotiline-induced inhibition of TCL, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Western blot analysis

TCL cells were harvested and lysed using M-PER buffer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (100×) (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by incubation on ice for 10 min. The protein extracts were obtained through high-speed centrifugation and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). Protein samples were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (1×TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with 1× TBST, the membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following additional washes with 1× TBST, protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence Western blot kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, OH, USA).

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Institution’s Ethics Committee. 5-weeks-old female NSG mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a light/dark cycle-and temperature-controlled environment, with ad libitum access to food and water. 4 × 106 cells (Jurkat) suspended in 150 µL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of mice. Upon reaching a tumor volume of approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly allocated into two groups: the maprotiline-treated group (30 mg/kg) and the vehicle control group. The tumor and body weight ware measured with a vernier caliper, and the tumor volume (mm [3]) was calculated as length × width2/2 every two days. At the end of the experiments, peripheral blood samples were collected from mice, and blood cell counts were analyzed using a Sysmex XN-1000 hematology analyzer, serum was subsequently isolated, and biochemical parameters were assessed with a HITACHI automated biochemistry analyzer; all mice were euthanized and tumor tissue was collected for further analysis. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, tumor tissues were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, then stained with Ki67 and p-AKT antibodies following the manufacturer’s instructions (Haokebio, Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were independently repeated in triplicate. The data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA, USA) For comparisons between groups, a two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was employed, with a p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) considered statistically significant.

Results

Maprotiline inhibits cell proliferation, migration and induces apoptosis in TCL

The chemical structure of maprotiline is depicted in Fig. 1A. In this study, we explored the potential anti-TCL effects of maprotiline, an established antidepressant. To investigate the impact of maprotiline on the growth of TCL cells in vitro, TCL cells (Jurkat, H9, Hut-78, and HH) were treated with different concentrations of maprotiline for up to 5 days and cell viability was detected using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 1B, maprotiline significantly decreases the viability of TCL cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner. In the Transwell assay, maprotiline treatment resulted in a dose-dependent inhibition of TCL cell migration to the lower chamber compared to the control group. These findings demonstrate that maprotiline effectively attenuates the migratory potential of TCL cells. (Fig. 1C). Morphologically, it was observed that maprotiline markedly reduces the density of TCL cells and induces cellular fragmentation (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that maprotiline exerts an antitumor effect in TCL.

C The migration abilities of Jurkat and H9 cells treated with maprotiline (0, 10, or 20 µM) for 24 h were determined by chamber migration assays. Apoptosis also plays a key role in tumorigenesis, and the impairment of apoptotic activity contributes to tumor progression and malignancy [26]. To evaluate the impact of maprotiline on cell apoptosis, Jurkat and H9 cells were exposed to varying concentrations of maprotiline for 48 h, and the results indicate that maprotiline triggers apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A). Caspase activation is an important marker of apoptosis [27, 28]. To further elucidate the mechanism underlying maprotiline-induced apoptosis in TCL cells, we analyzed the expression of caspase-related proteins using Western blot. The findings revealed that maprotiline significantly enhances the cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP (Fig. 2B). As a cell-permeable pan-caspase inhibitor, Z-VAD-FMK potently inhibits caspase-mediated apoptosis [29–31]. As shown in Fig. 2C, Z-VAD-FMK markedly attenuates the inhibitory effect of maprotiline on TCL cell viability. Furthermore, Z-VAD-FMK significantly diminishes the apoptotic effects induced by maprotiline in TCL cells (Fig. 2D, E). Taken together, these findings indicate that maprotiline suppresses the growth of TCL cells by promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis.

Maprotiline induces apoptosis in TCL cells. A The apoptosis in Jurkat and H9 cells treated with different concentrations of maprotiline for 48 h was detected by flow cytometry. B Western blot was performed to detect cleaved caspase-3, caspase-3, and cleaved PARP levels in cells treated with maprotiline for 48 h. C, D Jurkat and H9 cells were pre-incubated with or without Z-VAD-FMK (50 µM) for 1 h, then treated with maprotiline (20 µM), cell viability was detected by the CCK-8 assay (72 h) and apoptosis-related proteins were detected by Western blot assay (48 h). E Flow cytometry results illustrating that Z-VAD-FMK (50 µM) effectively reverses maprotiline (10 µM)-induced apoptosis in Jurkat and H9 cells. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Maprotiline suppresses the progression of TCL by inhibiting the akt/mtor signaling pathway

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the antitumor properties of maprotiline, we conducted RNA sequencing to analyze the gene expression profiles of TCL cells treated with and without maprotiline. Applying a cutoff of |fold change| >1.5 and P values < 0.05, we identified 252 upregulated and 92 downregulated genes in maprotiline-treated TCL cells. The downregulated genes include many tumor progression related genes, such as SCL11A1, ERBB3, and FGF8 (Fig. 3A). Then, the KEGG pathway analysis indicates that the mTOR signaling pathway, which is closely associated with tumorigenesis, might play a pivotal role in mediating the inhibitory effects of maprotiline on TCL (Fig. 3B). Considering that AKT, a pro-survival signaling molecule, directly regulates mTOR upstream [32]we conducted a Western blot assay and observed that maprotiline significantly reduces the expression levels p-AKT, p-mTOR, p-S6K1, and p-4EBP1 downstream of mTOR in TCL cells (Fig. 3C). To further validate the critical role of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in the antitumor effects of maprotiline, we treated TCL cells with both maprotiline and SC79, a specific AKT activator that binds to the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of AKT [33, 34]. Our results showed that SC79 significantly reverses the inhibitory effects of maprotiline on AKT/mTOR axis, cell viability, and cell migration in TCL (Fig. 3D-F). These findings collectively demonstrate that the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway mediates the antitumor activity of maprotiline in TCL.

Synergistic effects of maprotiline and histone deacetylase inhibitors in TCL cells

Target al.teration represents a primary mechanism underlying drug resistance, which ultimately contributes to the treatment failure in TCL. Consequently, rational drug combinations hold significant promise for the treatment of various tumors. Currently, histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) related to epigenetic regulation have been used in the clinical treatment of TCL, demonstrating promising therapeutic effects. Among them, chidamide and belinostat have already been approved for market use [35, 36]. To explore potential synergistic effects between maprotiline and HDACi, we systematically evaluated the sensitivity of TCL cells to chidamide or belinostat in the presence or absence of maprotiline using CCK-8 assays, flow cytometry, and Western blot analysis. Our findings, as illustrated in Fig. 4A-C, demonstrate that the combination of maprotiline and chidamide or belinostat significantly enhances therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by reduced cell viability and increased apoptotic rates compared to monotherapy with either agent alone. Furthermore, molecular analysis revealed substantial upregulation of cleaved caspase-3 and PARP levels in TCL cells treated with two agent combination, indicating enhanced apoptotic signaling (Fig. 4D). Notably, the combined treatment exhibits synergistic inhibition of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in TCL cells (Fig. 4E). These findings provide evidence that maprotiline potentiates the sensitivity of TCL cells to HDACi, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for TCL management.

Maprotiline inhibits the malignant growth of TCL-transplanted tumors in NSG mice

To investigate the anti-tumor effects of maprotiline on TCL in vivo, we established a xenograft mouse model by subcutaneously injecting 4 × 106 cells (Jurkat) into NSG mice. When tumor volume reached about 100 mm [3]mice were randomly divided into two groups treated with maprotiline (30 mg/kg) or vehicle (DMSO + PBS) every two days (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, the tumor volume in the maprotiline-treated group was smaller than that in the control group. Similarly, the tumor size in the maprotiline-treated group was also notably smaller than that in the control group (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the tumor weight in the maprotiline-treated group was significantly lower than that in the control group, while there were no significant differences in body weight between the two groups (Fig. 5D, E). To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we performed Western blot analysis, which revealed that maprotiline significantly inhibits the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in tumor samples (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining demonstrated that the expression levels of Ki67 and p-AKT were markedly lower in the maprotiline-treated group compared to the control group (Fig. 5G). These findings collectively indicate that maprotiline effectively suppresses the malignant growth of TCL in vivo by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

In addition to its anti-tumor efficacy, the safety profile of maprotiline in mice needs thorough investigation. Morphological analysis reveals that maprotiline treatment did not induce significant alterations in liver and kidney tissues (Fig. 6A). Biochemically, maprotiline administration showed no substantial impact on renal function markers, as evidenced by unchanged levels of uric acid (UA) and creatinine (CREA) (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, serum analysis demonstrated that maprotiline treatment did not significantly affect hepatic parameters, including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), total protein (TP), and albumin (ALB) levels, suggesting that maprotiline does not damage the liver’s anabolic function (Fig. 6C). Notably, hematological analysis revealed that maprotiline did not significantly alter the levels of white blood cells (WBCs), red blood cells (RBCs), platelets (PLTs), or hemoglobin (HGB) in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6D). In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that maprotiline exhibits no apparent toxicity in murine models, supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic candidate for TCL treatment.

Discussion

T-cell lymphoma (TCL) represents a highly aggressive form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by rapid disease progression and poor clinical outcomes [37]. Despite significant advances in hematologic malignancies, the absence of specific diagnostic markers for TCL continues to pose substantial challenges in early detection and effective disease management. Patients with advanced or relapsed disease face particularly grim prospects, as the current therapeutic arsenal remains limited, often resulting in rapid disease progression and mortality. Compounding this issue is the steadily rising incidence of newly diagnosed cases, which not only underscores the growing clinical significance of this malignancy but also imposes an increasing socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems and affected families. Thus, it is particularly urgent to clarify the pathogenesis and find new therapeutic agents to improve the therapeutic outcomes of TCL.

Maprotiline, a novel tetracyclic antidepressant, primarily alleviates depressive symptoms by inhibiting the reuptake of norepinephrine in the brain. Beyond its established antidepressant properties, recent research efforts have been directed towards exploring its potential novel therapeutic applications [38]. In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that maprotiline exerts significant anti-TCL activity in both vivo and in vitro. Firstly, we conducted experiments and proved that maprotiline markedly inhibits TCL cell proliferation and migration. Given the critical role of apoptosis in tumor progression, we hypothesized that maprotiline might modulate TCL cell apoptosis. Subsequent data confirmed this hypothesis, showing that maprotiline significantly enhances apoptosis in TCL cells, as evidenced by increased levels of apoptotic cells and upregulation of apoptosis-related proteins. To further elucidate the mechanism underlying maprotiline’s apoptotic effects on TCL cells, we employed Z-VAD-FMK, a pan-caspase inhibitor. The results demonstrated that Z-VAD-FMK substantially reversed maprotiline-induced TCL cell apoptosis, confirming the caspase-dependent nature of this process. These findings suggest that maprotiline possesses significant anti-TCL properties in addition to its antidepressant effects, the precise molecular mechanisms warrant further investigation.

The AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating various biological processes, including cell growth, apoptosis, and metabolism. Its aberrant activation has been closely associated with the development and progression of multiple malignant tumors [39–41]. Through comprehensive multi-omics integration analysis, we have mechanistically demonstrated that maprotiline inhibits TCL cell proliferation by negatively modulating the AKT/mTOR pathway. The inhibitory effects of maprotiline on both cell proliferation and the AKT/mTOR pathway in TCL were attenuated by SC79, a specific AKT activator. These findings underscore the critical involvement of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in TCL pathogenesis and highlight its components as promising targets for novel pharmacological interventions, representing a significant avenue for further investigation. Due to limitations in pathological sampling accessibility, we were unable to establish a correlation between AKT/mTOR signaling pathway activation and patient prognosis. This aspect warrants further exploration to enhance our understanding of TCL biology and inform clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies. Additionally, our in vivo studies using a TCL xenograft mouse model revealed that maprotiline significantly inhibits tumor growth through the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway without inducing weight loss, metabolic disturbances, or other toxic effects. These findings not only demonstrate the compound’s favorable safety profile but also provide robust preclinical evidence to support its potential clinical application. In addition, we plan to investigate the pharmacokinetic properties of maprotiline, expecting to generate valuable data to facilitate the clinical translation of maprotiline for TCL treatment, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes and quality of life.

Due to the lack of effective therapeutic agents, the clinical management of TCL primarily relies on multi-drug combination chemotherapy. However, the long-term administration of chemotherapeutic agents often leads to the development of drug resistance, resulting in only a limited subset of patients deriving significant clinical benefits. The molecular mechanisms underlying drug resistance are complex and multifaceted, as chemotherapeutic agents exert their effects through diverse pathways. This complexity underscores the imperative for the continuous exploration of novel therapeutic strategies. As histone deacetylase inhibitors, chidamide and belinostat have been clinically used for the treatment of TCL, known for its anti-tumor efficacy through mechanisms such as DNA repair inhibition and apoptosis induction [42–44]. Our findings demonstrate that maprotiline significantly enhances the sensitivity of TCL cells to chidamide or belinostat, thereby expanding the known pharmacological repertoire of maprotiline. Furthermore, additional studies are needed to elucidate the synergistic inhibitory effects and underlying mechanisms of these two agents in animal models, which will provide valuable insights for optimizing their clinical application.

In conclusion, our study provides the first evidence that maprotiline exhibits significant anti-tumor activity against TCL both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, maprotiline exerts its therapeutic effects by suppressing tumor cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis through modulation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Furthermore, our findings reveal that maprotiline enhances the chemosensitivity of TCL cells to chidamide, indicating that the combination therapy of maprotiline with chidamide represents a promising therapeutic strategy for TCL treatment.
