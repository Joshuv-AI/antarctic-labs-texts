Intravenous haloperidol or chlorpromazine—which is more effective to improve agitation and communication in agitated delirium at the end of life?
Authors: Megumi Uchida, Tatsuya Morita, Tatsuo Akechi, Takuya Kawahara, Asao Ogawa, Kazuhiro Yoshiuchi, Naosuke Yokomichi, Akihiro Sakashita, Yoshihisa Matsumoto, Keiichi Uemura, Rika Nakahara, Satoru Iwase, Megumi Uchida, Tatsuya Morita, Tatsuo Akechi, Takuya Kawahara, Asao Ogawa, Kazuhiro Yoshiuchi, Naosuke Yokomichi, Akihiro Sakashita, Yoshihisa Matsumoto, Keiichi Uemura, Rika Nakahara, Satoru Iwase
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium frequently occurs in terminally ill patients with cancer. The purpose of this study was to determine whether intravenous haloperidol or chlorpromazine is more effective in improving agitation and communication in agitated delirium at the end of life.Patients and MethodsAll terminally ill patients with delirium treated in 39 specialized palliative care services were enrolled via the web. Delirium diagnosis and severity (Delirium Rating Scale-revised-98<DRS-R98>) were evaluated at the start of treatment (T0) and 72 h later (T1), and the psychotropic drugs used concomitantly at T1 were investigated. We evaluated whether there was an improvement in DRS-R98 agitation-related item scores and DRS-R98 communication-related item scores at T1 in patients with agitated cancer who received intravenous haloperidol or chlorpromazine from T0, and whether a significant difference existed between the two groups.ResultsOf 818 enrolled participants, 424 experienced agitation at T0, comprising 183 in the haloperidol group and 68 in the chlorpromazine group. The concomitant benzodiazepine rates were 52% and 54% in the haloperidol and chlorpromazine groups, respectively. In both groups, improvement in DRS-R98 agitation item scores was observed at T1, but there was no significant difference in improvement between the two groups. Worsening in DRS-R98 communication item scores was observed in both groups at T1, but there was no significant difference in worsening scores between the two groups.ConclusionIntravenous haloperidol and chlorpromazine are not statistically different for terminal agitation. These results should be validated in clinical trials.Keywords: cancer, end of life, delirium, palliative care, agitation, communication

The aim of this study was to compare the abilities of intravenous haloperidol and chlorpromazine to improve agitation and communication in patients of cancer with agitated delirium at the end of life. We investigated whether there was an improvement in DRS-R98 agitation related item scores and DRS-R98 communication related item scores at T1 and whether there was a significant difference between the two groups. The results revealed intravenous haloperidol and chlorpromazine in their minimum required intravenous dose were not statistically different for terminal agitation, although both made communication difficult at the end of life.

Introduction

Delirium occurs frequently in terminally ill patients with cancer, and previous studies have shown that delirium occurs in approximately 80%-90% of such cases.1–5 To treat terminal delirium, it is necessary to alleviate the symptoms and distress caused by the delirium itself, such as agitation; however, it is also important to maintain communication.6 Medical professionals and families become exhausted following the appearance of psychiatric symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, and agitation.7–11 When a patient loses continuity of self, it is impossible for the family to spend their final moments together without regret.6 Terminal delirium also impedes communication between medical professionals and patients, as well as between patients and their families, making it impossible to discuss how to end life.12,13 Additionally, family members are forced to act as legal guardians for decision-making, placing a heavy burden on families.

Several previous studies have focused on pharmacotherapy for terminal delirium,14–16 but there are limited data on this topic, and the quality of evidence is low.17 Intravenously administered haloperidol (HPD) and chlorpromazine (CP) are used in daily clinical practice to treat delirium at the end of life, but few studies have compared them.15 Recently, Hui et al. compared the dose escalation of intravenous HPD, neuroleptic rotation with CP, and combination therapy of HPD and CP, and showed that they all improved agitation.18

Additionally, in many previous studies, the outcomes included agitation, adverse events, and the use of additional drugs. Moreover, almost no studies have evaluated communication, which is particularly important in terminal delirium. Therefore, we designed this study with agitation and communication as the primary outcomes.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the improvement in agitation and communication resulting from intravenous HPD and CP in patients with cancer and agitated terminal delirium 72 h after administration (T1). It has been shown that CP has a stronger sedative effect than HPD,19 and we hypothesized that the use of HPD would be less effective at suppressing agitation but would maintain communication, whereas the use of CP would suppress agitation but cause drowsiness and make communication difficult.

Materials and methods

This is a secondary analysis of a multicenter prospective observational study aimed at examining the efficacy and safety of antipsychotics in patients with advanced cancer and delirium.20 This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Osaka University (approval number: 13 295) and the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions approved the study protocol. The requirement for informed consent was waived because all data were obtained through routine clinical practice, and there was no additional burden on the participants. Patients and their families could opt out of the study if they did not wish to participate. Data supporting the results of this study are available upon request to the corresponding author. Data are not publicly available because of privacy or ethical restrictions.

Treatment setting and participants

Participants were recruited from 39 specialized palliative care services (15 palliative care units, 15 hospital palliative care teams, and nine psychiatric consultation services) between September 2015 and May 2016. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 20 years or older with advanced cancer referred to a specialized palliative care service; (2) patients with delirium as diagnosed by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition21; (3) patients whose prognosis predicted by the attending physician was within weeks; (4) patients with agitation that satisfies any of the following at the start of delirium treatment (T0): (a) Delirium Rating Scale-Revised 98: Motor agitation item score ≥ 1, (b) Agitation Distress Scale: Motor anxiety item score ≥ 1, and (c) Nursing delirium Screening Scale: inappropriate behavior item score ≥ 1; and (5) patients who received intravenous HPD or CP. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with postoperative delirium and (2) patients with withdrawal from alcohol or other drugs.

Procedure

Patients were consecutively sampled at each study site. If eligible patients received medication as part of clinical practice, their medical information was recorded by palliative care specialists and psychiatrists via an electronic data collection system. These medical professionals’ evaluated patients at T0 (when patients started antipsychotics) and T1 (72 h later) using a delirium assessment tool (Table S1). The primary outcomes were changes in scores related to agitation (DRS-R98 agitation-related item total score) and communication-related scores (DRS-R98 cognition-related item total score), whereas the secondary outcomes were changes in another score related to agitation (RASS, DRS-R98 motor agitation single item score, ADS the extent of motor anxiety score, NuDESC inappropriate behavior score) and another score related to communication (DRS-R98 language single item score, CCS voluntary communication score, and NuDESC inappropriate communication).

Measurement tools

Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale–palliative care version

The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale for palliative care inpatients (RASS-PAL) is a one-item observational instrument that assesses sedation and agitation levels without the requirement of a patient response.22 Scores range from +4 (an overtly combative patient) to −5 (a patient who cannot be aroused either verbally or by physical stimulation). This scale was used for evaluation at T0 and T1.

Delirium rating Scale-revised-98 (DRS-R-98)

The DRS-R-98 is a 16-item clinician-rated scale that measures delirium symptom profiles and can be used for both diagnostic and severity assessment.23 The scale has 13 severity items rated from 0 to 3 points (maximum: 39 points), and another three diagnostic items (rated from 0 to either 2 or 3 points), which capture the temporal course and attribution to an underlying etiology. The validity and reliability were confirmed using the Japanese version of the DRS-R-98.24 We used the worst score 24 h before T0 or T1. In this study, the sum of five agitation items (sleep-wake cycle disturbance, perceptual disturbance, delusions, lability of affect, and motor agitation) was used to produce a DRS-R-98 agitation-related item total score, ranging from 0 to 15 points, where higher scores indicate greater severity of agitation by delirium. The sum of seven cognition items (short-term memory, long-term memory, visuospatial ability, language, orientation, attention, and thought process) was used to produce a DRS-R-98 cognitive-related item total score, ranging from 0 to 21 points, where higher scores indicate greater severity of cognitive disturbance by delirium. We also used motor agitation and language as single items.

Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC)

The Nu-DESC is a 5-item nurse-rated scale, where the responses for each item range from 0 to 2; the total possible score ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater delirium severity.25 We used items 2 (inappropriate behavior) and 3 (inappropriate communication) for this study. We used the worst score based on nursing records 8-12 h before day 0 or 3.

Communication Capacity and Agitation Distress Scale

The Communication Capacity Scale (CCS) is a 5-item clinician rating scale used to quantify patient communication capacity.26 Each item was scored from 0 to 3 or from 0 to 5, and the total score ranged from 0 to 17, where higher scores indicate lower communication capacity. We used item 4 (voluntary communication) in this study.

The Agitation Distress Scale (ADS) is a 6-item scale used to quantify the degree of agitation in delirious patients. Items were scored on a 4-point (0 to 3) observer rating scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 18, where higher scores indicate greater agitation levels. We used item 2 (motor anxiety) in this study.

Sociodemographic and medical factors

We collected sociodemographic and medical factors, including age, sex, primary tumor site, treatment setting, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status,27 prognostic prediction (days, weeks, or months) by palliative care specialists or psychiatrists, and the Palliative Performance Scale.28 We also evaluated the type and amount of antipsychotics used at T0 and T1 and whether there was a change in psychotropic medication at T1. We evaluated the following adverse events (malignant syndrome, urinary retention, aspiration pneumonitis, falls, oversedation, cardiovascular events, hyperglycemia, and sudden death) using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) and assessed their causal relationships.

Statistical analysis

Patients with agitation were enrolled in this secondary analysis. We calculated descriptive statistics for the patient characteristics. After calculating the mean and median doses of HPD and CP after 48 h, descriptive statistics for psychotropic medications after 72 h were calculated to examine the differences between the two groups. We calculated the mean and each score for the RASS-PAL score, DRS-R-98 agitation item total score, DRS-R-98 cognition item total score, DRS-R-98 motor agitation single item score, DRS-R-98 language single item score, ADS motor anxiety item score, CCS voluntary communication item score, Nu-DESC inappropriate behavior item score, and Nu-DESC inappropriate communication item score for each group at T0 and T1. We analyzed the differences between T0 and T1 in each group (HPD and CP groups) using a paired t-test and calculated the effect size. A two-sample t-test was performed to compare the HPD and CP groups (T1-T0). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. We adjusted for age, sex, primary tumor site (lung cancer, gastrointestinal, other), ECOG performance status, and presence or absence of prior delirium treatment by linear regression, as these have been shown to be potential confounders in previous studies.14 All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Side effects of the medication

We defined side effects as those that were CTCAE-G2 or higher (moderate, requiring treatment) and were considered to be causally related to the study medication. The following side effects were not observed at T1: malignant syndrome, falls, cardiovascular events, hyperglycemia, or sudden death. Oversedation (HPD: 8/168, CP: 4/60), urinary retention (HPD: 2/174, CP: 0/64), and aspiration pneumonitis (HPD4/172 CP: 1/63) were observed side effects. However, there was no significant difference between the two groups (Table S2).

Discussion

Main findings

Four hundred and twenty-four delirious patients with agitation were included in this study, including 183 in the HPD group and 68 in the CP group. The concomitant benzodiazepine rates were 52% and 54% in the HPD and CP groups, respectively.

As balancing symptom relief and communication is important for treating terminal delirium, we evaluated both agitation and communication scores. Improvements in the DRS-R-98 agitation-related item total score were observed in both groups at T1, but there were no significant differences in improvement between the two groups. Worsening in the DRS-R-98 communication-related item total score was observed in both groups at T1, but there was no significant difference in worsening between the two groups.

We hypothesized that HPD would be less effective in reducing agitation than CP, but that communication would be maintained in the HPD group. However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in reducing agitation or maintaining communication. As for agitation, one reason for this is that medications were administered by a palliative care specialist in this study, and it is assumed that minimal required doses of CP and HPD were administered to prevent side effects and excessive sedation. We think that because the doses of both HPD and CP were at the minimal required levels, it was difficult to find significant differences. The other reason for this is the difference in the backgrounds of the two groups. The use of opioids and other medications at T0 was significantly higher in the CP group than in the HPD group. As a result, the CP group should have been more effective at reducing agitation than the HPD group, but the patients in the HPD group received lower doses of opioids and other medications, which would have resulted in a similar apparent level of sedation. As for communication, if the sedation level is similar in both groups, the time spent dozing off may be similar and communication may decline in the same way. Also, in this study, communication and sedation were evaluated at their worst, but it may be better to evaluate communication at its best. If we evaluate communication at its best, we may be able to see the changes in communication that we are hoping for. In addition, the proportion of opioids and other medications may have influenced the results, resulting in no significant difference between two groups.

The RASS scores significantly improved at T1 in both the HPD and CP groups, with no significant difference between the two groups. The DRS-R98 motor agitation single item score, ADS the extent of motor anxiety score, and NuDESC inappropriate behavior score all improved significantly in both the HPD and CP groups, but there were no significant differences between the two groups except for the NuDESC inappropriate behavior score. This is likely explained by the fact that, in NuDESC evaluations, medical professionals give high scores if a subject experiences severe symptoms even once during the shift, so significant differences are likely to emerge. The DRS-R98 cognition-related item total score, DRS-R language single item score, and CCS voluntary communication score significantly worsened in both the HPD and CP groups, but there were no significant differences between the two groups. Inappropriate communication in NuDESC was not worsened in either the HPD or CP group, possibly because all other scales evaluate conditions within 24 h, but NuDESC evaluates the worst symptoms within one shift, where the short intervals between evaluations make it difficult to detect changes. A combination of nonpharmacological and pharmacological strategies may be a better approach to managing communication between patients with hyperactive delirium at the end of life.29

Many of the drugs used to treat delirium in the terminal stage are off-label drugs30–33 and require sufficient explanation to the family and patients, and approval within the facility. The usefulness of i.v. HPD has been examined in many previous studies, but little data exist on CP. Although this is not an RCT, our findings highlight the usefulness of CP.

Medication for terminal delirium

A Cochrane review of pharmacotherapy for terminal delirium, mostly in patients with mild to moderate delirium, concluded that there is currently no high-quality evidence to support or refute pharmacotherapy for terminal delirium.17 Low-quality evidence shows that in patients with mild to moderate terminal delirium, HPD and risperidone worsen delirium and increase extrapyramidal symptoms. However, the small number of included studies and the small number of patients suggest that further research is needed.14–16,34

Breitbart conducted an RCT of HPD and CP in patients with AIDS with a serious physical condition, and revealed that both were effective against hypoactive and hyperactive delirium.15 However, in this study, the drug was administered orally or intramuscularly, not intravenously. Although our study is not an RCT, it is a direct comparison of intravenous HOD and CP and showed that both are effective against agitation in hyperactive delirium, which is consistent with previous studies.

Hui et al.16 and Elsayem et al.35 attempted to intervene in patients with terminal delirium that could not be adequately controlled with HPD. Hui et al. showed that increasing the HPD dose, switching to CP, and combining HPD and CP were effective for treating refractory delirium in terminally ill newly diagnosed cancer patients.18 However, there was no comparison with the placebo group, and it is unclear whether the decrease in RASS scores was due to pharmacological effects, variability in delirium symptoms, or nonpharmacological factors. In this study, a decrease in blood pressure was observed as an adverse event, but no antipsychotic-related deaths were recorded, consistent with the results of the present study.

In general, CP is said to be more sedative and to have stronger anticholinergic effects than HPD, making it suitable for patients who are more irritated and require blood pressure monitoring.36 HPD is the gold standard treatment for delirium, but high doses produce extrapyramidal symptoms and it is necessary to confirm the QT interval with electrocardiogram.19

In this study, no major side effect appeared in either group. Observed side effects were oversedation, urinary retention, and aspiration pneumonitis but there was no significant difference between the two groups. In this study, medications were administered by a palliative care specialist, and it is assumed that minimal doses of CP and HPD were administered. However, the on/off label for parenteral administration of CP differs by hospital and country, which may also affect drug selection.

Study strengths

First, the medications used in this study were intravenous HPD and CP, which are good options for patients with poor physical conditions who cannot take oral medication. Second, we included a larger number of patients than previous studies. Third, validated assessment tools were used, but some were evaluated using single items, and their reliability and validity were not evaluated. Fourth, the prescribers and evaluators were experts in palliative care and were accustomed to administering the lowest necessary doses of antipsychotics and assessing delirium.

Study limitations

First, this was an observational study with no placebo group. Therefore, it is possible that the observed improvements were due to the natural course of recovery from delirium. Additionally, there is a possibility of rater bias. Second, the delirium was only followed up for 72 h, so the long-term progress is unknown. However, as this study included patients with a limited prognosis, this period may be appropriate. Third, delirium was evaluated only by medical staff members, not by family members or patients. However, it may be difficult to evaluate patients themselves because some are seriously physically ill. Fourth, only intravenous HPD and CP were evaluated. Therefore, intramuscular and subcutaneous injections were not included in this study. Fifth, as intravenous CP is off-label in Japan, its availability varies by facility. Sixth, communication was also evaluated at its worst point, which may have made it difficult to detect any changes. Seventh, the rates of concomitant medication use differed between the two groups.

Conclusion

Intravenous HPD and CP are not statistically different for terminal agitation. However, neither treatment improved communication in patients with terminal delirium. The results of this study should be validated in clinical trials that reexamine the methods of evaluating communication and adjust the proportion of concomitant medications in both groups.
