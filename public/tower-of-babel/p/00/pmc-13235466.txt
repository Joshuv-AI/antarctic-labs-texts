Effectiveness of health care transition interventions for sickle cell disease∗
Authors: Melissa Azul, Tarun Aurora, Jerlym Porter, Sheila Anderson, Marquita Nelson, Ugochi O. Ogu, Parul Rai, Yogindra Persaud, Jennifer Longoria, Andrew Heitzer, Martha Barton, Yunusa Olufadi, Ana A. Baumann, Guolian Kang, Jane S. Hankins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Pediatric SCD health care transition interventions improve adult care engagement and HRQOL.•SCD health care transition programs have long-term impacts on young adult health outcomes.

Introduction

Over 95% of children with sickle cell disease (SCD) reach adulthood in high-resourced settings today.1 However, SCD mortality sharply rises after the age of 18 years, and acute care use peaks between the ages of 18 and 35 years,1,2 coinciding with the transition period from pediatric to adult care.

Effective health care transition (HCT) programs are essential to support adolescents and young adults (AYA) with SCD navigate this shift,3, 4, 5 yet few high-quality studies assess their impact.6 Many lack data on transfer completion, patient characteristics, attrition rates, and long-term outcomes. The St. Jude SCD Transition Program, serving over 500 AYA with SCD since 2007,7 offers a decade-long data set to evaluate the HCT program's effectiveness.

In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of HCT interventions on care engagement, health care use, and quality of life in young adulthood. We hypothesize that greater exposure to transition program interventions improves transition and health outcomes. By evaluating HCT intervention effectiveness we aim to provide evidence to improve outcomes for individuals with SCD during the transition period.

Methods

Participant selection

All patients with SCD treated at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, regardless of sickle genotype or disease severity, born between 1 January 1989 and 31 December 2002, were identified using institutional health records. This strategy identified patients aged ≤18 years in 2007, when the St. Jude SCD Transition Program began, and aged ≥19 years by 31 December 2021, ensuring all individuals included were exposed to the transition interventions and could have had at least 1 year of adult care follow-up. Patients who died or transferred care before the age of 18 years were excluded. Those with a last pediatric visit at age <12 years were also excluded, as they would not have been exposed to transition interventions. Data included follow-up from birth to the last available record or the cutoff of data analysis.

Transition program interventions

The St. Jude SCD Transition Program follows the Got Transition Six Core Elements of HCT framework.8 The multidisciplinary program interventions have been previously described.7 Briefly, interventions included in-person and online disease education,9,10 academic planning sessions to assess needs and monitor high school graduation progress, personal health record (PHR) communication training,11 transition skills workshops (appointment scheduling, medication refill, insurance familiarization),12 and early adult care introduction (tour of the adult care facility at the age of 17 years).13 In-person SCD education sessions were held twice yearly for patients aged 12 to 25 years, with additional 6 online modules available. Participants could access up to 3 PHR training sessions and academic planning sessions. Neurocognitive, disease literacy, and transition readiness assessments were attempted at least every 4 years14,15 to customize transition planning. All interventions were integrated into routine SCD care. Transition barriers were addressed through transdisciplinary meetings, and individualized plans were created.16 Adult care introductions began for patients who were aged 17 years, through adult facility tours.13 The first SCD adult care appointment was scheduled before the last pediatric appointment. Patients were transferred to adult care at the age of 18 years or when deemed transition-ready with transitional care, until the age of 25 at adult-partner centers.17

The St. Jude SCD Transition Program consists of a multidisciplinary team that includes hematologists, a hematology nurse case manager (providing in-clinic SCD education, PHR training, transition skills workshops, and transition readiness assessments), neuropsychologists (neurocognitive testing), social workers (self-advocacy and insurance review), and a licensed educator (academic planning). At the age of 17 years, care coordination changes to a dedicated transition nurse case manager who coordinates transfer to the adult facility at the age of 18 years and supports care through the age of 25 years. The transition nurse manager attends adult visits up to the age of 25, if patients choose a partner adult program. The transition program collaborates with 2 local large adult dedicated-SCD centers offering SCD-specific hematologists, social work, and psychology services, equipped with day-hospital and infusion centers.

Outcomes

Demographics and outcome data were extracted from the Sickle Cell Clinical Research and Intervention Program database, a longitudinal, multicenter, lifespan cohort, and supplemented with medical chart abstraction.18 Outcomes were grouped as transition and population outcomes (supplemental Table 1). Transition outcomes included (1) attendance at the first adult outpatient visit, (2) successful transfer to adult care defined as attendance at 2 adult visits within the first year of adult care,19 and (3) frequency of adult ambulatory care use. Since data on scheduled adult visits were unavailable, adult care integration (≥50% completion of scheduled visits annually for 5 years after transfer)19 could not be assessed. Instead, we report the proportion of individuals who attended at least 1 adult visit per year during the first 5 years after transfer, representing 50% of the recommended minimum of 2 visits per year for SCD maintenance.20 Population outcomes included acute care use 1, 2, and 3 years after the first adult visit, high school completion, health-related quality of life (HRQOL), and mortality. Acute care use included emergency room visits and hospitalizations for any SCD-related complication. PedsQL SCD Module scores measured HRQOL21 completed between the ages of 18 and 24 years, with the latest available score used if >1 was obtained. High school attainment was classified as obtaining a high school degree or passing a general educational developmental test. Transition readiness was assessed at the ages of 15 and 17 years, using the Self-Management Skills Checklist.14,22

Analysis

Predictors of transition outcomes included participation in transition interventions. Covariates included demographics, exposure duration to disease-modifying therapies (disease-modifying therapy [DMT], hydroxyurea, chronic transfusions, l-glutamine), frequency of pediatric outpatient visits between the ages of 12 and 18 years, and transition readiness scores. Fisher exact or χ2 test assessed associations between transition outcomes and genotype, sex, participation in skills training, transition tour participation, and PHR completion. Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests assessed associations between transition outcomes and DMT duration, number of academic planning sessions, outpatient pediatric visits, and disease educational sessions. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to tests for data normality. Spearman correlation (coefficient, ρ) was used for adult care visits and acute care use. Covariates were evaluated for multicollinearity (variance inflation factor <2). Stepwise multivariable generalized linear or ordered logistic regression selected significant covariates at P values < .05. Area under the receiver operator characteristics curves assessed predictive accuracy of attendance of first adult visit and adult care transfer completion, and compared curves using DeLong’s method with binomial smoothing (method = binomial in plot receiver operating curves). Model fits were assessed using likelihood ratio tests and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values. For all analyses, complete-case analysis without imputation was performed. Because of missing data, descriptive and univariate correlation analyses were conducted for transition readiness scores. Analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons to control false discovery rate, and q-value was calculated using Benjamin and Hochberg method. q values and P values < .05 were considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.3 (R Core Team, 2023).

The St. Jude institutional review board approved this study with a waiver of consent.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 721 patients were born between 1 January 1989 and 31 December 2002. Of those, 4 patients died before reaching the age of 18 years, 38 transferred to another facility during pediatric care, and 41 had their last pediatric visit before the age of 12 years (Figure 1A). The remaining 638 patients were included in the analysis. A total of 30 deaths occurred after the age of 18 years, with a median age of 22.9 years (IQR [interquartile range], 20.7-27.5). In pediatric care, 197 individuals (30.9%) were prescribed hydroxyurea, 90 (14.1%) were on chronic red blood cell transfusions, and 2 (3.4%) were prescribed l-glutamine. Although all individuals had access to interventions, some missed because of limited clinic time or patient/parent refusal. Of these, 538 individuals (84.3%) received at least 1 transition-building intervention, and 319 individuals had accessible data beyond attendance of the first adult visit and contributed to the analysis of adult care use.

Transition outcomes

Attendance of the first adult visit

A total of 475 individuals (74.5%) attended the first adult visit. Among those who did not, 58 individuals (9%) were lost to follow-up between the ages of 12 and 18 years (Figure 1A). The median age at the last pediatric visit was 18.2 years (IQR, 18.0-18.5 years). Of the 475 who attended the first adult visit, 394 (83%) did so within 6 months of the last pediatric visit. The median gap between the last pediatric and the first adult visit was 1.3 months (IQR, 1-3). Males were less likely to attend their first visit compared with females (51.6% vs 64.8%, q = 0.03). Sickle genotype was not associated with first adult visit attendance (q = 0.22). Longer DMT exposure (3.2 vs 0.60 years, q < 0.01) and more pediatric outpatient visits (mean 21.3 vs 5.3, q < 0.01) were associated with greater attendance of first adult visit. Participation in all transition program interventions was associated with attendance of the first adult visit (Table 1), except for online SCD education (11.58% vs 4.76%, q = 0.06). Higher transition readiness scores (72.3 vs 65.0, q = 0.012) were associated with attendance of first adult visit. In multivariable logistic regression, in-person SCD education sessions completed between the ages of 12 and 18 years (odds ratio [OR], 1.56; P = .0004) and early adult care introduction (participation in an adult care facility tour when aged 17 years; OR, 2.18; P = .01) were associated with increased attendance of first adult visit odds. When controlled for other variables, males were 40% less likely to attend first adult visit compared with females (OR, 0.61; P = .038), and each additional year of DMT exposure was associated with increased first visit attendance likelihood by 28% (OR, 1.28; P < .001) (supplemental Table 2). Successful adult care transfer

Of the 475 patients who attended the first adult visit following the last pediatric visit, 13 died aged >19 years, 14 had <1 year of adult care follow-up at the time of the analysis cut-off, and in 143 patients health records were inaccessible. A total of 305 patients had at least 1 year follow-up time in adult care with accessible records. Of these, 187 patients attended 2 adult visits within the first year following the first adult visit, yielding a successful adult care transfer rate of 61.3% (n = 187/305; 95% CI, 55.6-66.8). Among all living patients, regardless of attendance of first adult visit, who had at least 1 year of follow-up time since transfer eligibility, and with accessible records, the estimated rate of successful transfer to adult care was 40.0% (n = 187/[305 + 163]; 95% CI, 35.5-44.6) (Figure 1A). Participation in all transition program interventions was associated with successful adult care transfer (Table 2). Successful transfer was associated with a shorter median gap time between the last pediatric and the first adult visit (0.16 vs 1.5 years, q < 0.00001), homozygous hemoglobin SS disease (HbSS)/Sβ0-thalassemia genotype (67.9% vs 49.8%), longer DMT exposure (5.85 vs 1.05 years, q < 0.00001), more pediatric outpatient visits (45.0 vs 4.9, q < 0.00001), increased attendance of in-person SCD education sessions (2.0 vs 1.3, q < 0.0001), increased completion of online SCD education modules (19.8% vs 5.8%, q < 0.0001), higher number of academic planning sessions (3.7 vs 1.8, q < 0.00001), increased completion of PHR training (89.3% vs 51.6%, q < 0.00001), increased attendance of a transition tour (48.7% vs 24.7%, q < 0.00001), and completion of skills lab (25.7% vs 7.6%, q < 0.00001). Transition readiness score was not associated with successful transfer to adult care (q ≥ 0.17). In multivariable logistic regression, in-person SCD education sessions at the ages of 12 to 18 years (OR, 1.95; P = .00012) and PHR training (OR, 3.01; P = .0027) were independently associated with increased odds of successful adult care transfer after adjusting for DMT exposure and pediatric outpatient visits (supplemental Table 3). The proportion of individuals who successfully completed adult care transfer steadily increased from 2007, reaching a peak of 77% in 2015, and then plateaued at 63% in 2019. We built 3 models to predict successful adult care transfer, including (1) a clinical model (DMT duration and total pediatric outpatient visits), (2) an HCT intervention model (PHR training completion and pediatric in-clinic SCD education), and (3) a combined model (Figure 3B). The combined model showed a sensitivity of 0.87, specificity of 0.81, and an area under the curve of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.89-0.94). Although adding HCT interventions did not significantly improve discriminative ability (DeLong’s test, P = .51), likelihood ratio tests (P ≤ .007) and AIC reductions indicated improved overall model fit.

Ambulatory adult care use

In total, 319 patients had accessible data after the first adult visit in adult care. Of these, 305, 264, 234, 198, and 159 patients had 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years of follow-up time after initial adult visit, respectively. A total of 252 (83%), 208 (79%), 168 (72%), 128 (65%), and 103 (65%) patients had first-to-last adult visit intervals at least 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years. 187 (61%; 187/305), 151 (57%; 151/264), 121 (52%; 121/234), 90 (45%, 90/198), and 70 (44%, 70/159) patients attended at least one outpatient adult visit per year, from 1 to 5 years after transfer (Figure 1B). As adult ambulatory care utilization plateaued 3 years after transfer, analyses of associations with transition program interventions were limited to the initial 3-year period (Table 3). Every year of additional DMT exposure was associated with 1.1 more adult visits during the first, second, and third year following attendance of first adult visit (P < .0001 for all, supplemental Table 4). PHR completion was associated with 1.6, 2.0, and 2.0 more adult outpatient visits in 1 year (estimate [est] 0.44, P = .0098), 2 (est 0.68, P = .0003), and 3 years (est 0.69, P = .0007) after attendance of first adult visit compared to those who did not complete training. Each additional outpatient pediatric visit was associated with 1 additional adult visit during the first, second, and third year after transfer (P < .0001 for all). Skills lab completion and in-person SCD education in pediatric care were associated with adult outpatient visits in the first year after transfer but not later. Sex and transition readiness assessment scores was not associated with adult outpatient visit frequency (Table 3).

Population outcomes

Acute care use during early adult care

Among patients with 1, 2, and 3 years follow-up time, 252 (83%), 208 (79%), and 168 (72%) patients completed outpatient adult visits through the 3 years after transfer (supplemental Table 5). Patients with HbSS/Sβ0 thalassemia had higher median acute care use at 2 (1, range 0-58 vs 0, range 0-11) and 3 (4, range 0-77 vs 0, range 0-17) years after transfer (q = 0.013, q = 0.002) compared with other genotypes. Longer DMT exposure correlated with increased acute care use after transfer at 1, 2, and 3 years follow-up (ρ = 0.4, 0.5, 0.55; q < 0.001). Total outpatient pediatric visits correlated with acute care use at all time points (ρ = 0.39, 0.47, 0.52; q < 0.01) but lost significance in the multivariable analysis. Sex was not associated with acute care use. Patients who completed pediatric PHR training had more median acute care visits in years 1 (0, IQR, 0-23 vs 0-12; q = 0.034), 2 (1, IQR 0-58 vs 0, IQR 0-9; q = 0.0006), and 3 (3, IQR 0-77 vs 0, IQR 0-12; q = 0.001) after transfer. Academic planning sessions were associated with higher acute care use 1 (ρ = 0.15, q = 0.034), 2 (ρ = 0.19 q = 0.013), and 3 (ρ = 0.25 q = 0.003) years after transfer. In multivariable analyses, each additional year of DMT exposure was linked to 1.2 more acute care visits in years 1, 2, and 3 after transfer (est 0.13, 0.14, 0.12; P < .001; supplemental Table 6). Patients who completed PHR training had 3.7 (est 1.3, P = .0056) and 2.7 (est 0.99, P = .019) more acute care visits in 2 and 3 years after transfer than those who did not.

HRQOL

Among 275 young adults with HRQOL data (supplemental Table 7), women had lower HRQOL than men (median 62 vs 70, q = 0.002). Higher HRQOL in adulthood was associated with more pediatric in-person education (ρ = 0.15, q = 0.048) and PHR training sessions (ρ = 0.21, q = 0.006). In multivariable analyses, males had HRQOL scores an average of 6.6 points higher than women (est 6.6, P = .0037). Each additional PHR training session completed was linked to a 4.6-point increase (est = 4.6, P = .017), and each additional in-person SCD educational session completed with a 2.5-point increase in HRQOL (est 2.5, P = .08). HRQOL was not associated with SCD genotypes, DMT duration, or outpatient pediatric visit frequency.

Academic attainment

Educational attainment data were available for 275 young adults. More outpatient pediatric visits correlated with attaining a high school diploma or higher (P = .004, q = 0.039; supplemental Table 8). Those with less than a high school degree had a mean of 27.4 pediatric visits, whereas those with some college training had 42.25 pediatric visits. In multivariable ordered logistic regression analyses, skills lab attendance (OR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.31-0.84; P = .0083), male gender (OR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.39-0.94; P = .026), and total pediatric outpatient visits (OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.003-1.015; P = .0042) were independently associated with academic attainment. No other variables were associated with education attainment.

Deaths after the age of 18 years

Among the 30 deaths that occurred in patients after the age of 18 years (median age, 22.91 years; IQR, 20.72-27.53), 28 occurred in those who transferred care (Figure 1). Death was not associated with total outpatient pediatric visits (P ≥ .090), outpatient adult visits (P ≥ .238), or acute care use after transfer (P ≥ .58).

Discussion

AYAs with SCD need structured transition services for consistent care engagement and continued quality of care. In our multidisciplinary transition program for AYAs with SCD, 74.5% attended a first adult visit after completing pediatric care, and 40% successfully transferred to adult care. Adult care outpatient use declined in the first 5 years after transfer but plateaued after 3 years. PHR training and SCD education sessions in pediatric care were associated with successful transfer to adult care and adult ambulatory use. Our results suggest that additional transition interventions are necessary to both increase short and long-term engagement in adult care.

The St. Jude SCD Transition Program effectively delivered patients into adult care with 74.5% attending their first adult visit. This reinforces the strength of a structured transition framework and program and is consistent with our previous findings.7,23,24 Although we found that initiation of adult care was high, successful completion of adult care transfer decreased to 40% and ongoing adult care ambulatory use 5 years after transfer was 44%, highlighting that short and long-term engagement is more challenging to achieve. Engagement in adult care is influenced by many factors such as insurance coverage, availability of adult SCD providers or programs, increasing prevalence of disease complications, insufficient preparedness for self-management, and shifts in personal and professional priorities such as family planning, secondary education, or career changes often requiring relocation.25 Notably, successful transfer rates sharply decreased to 26% in 2020 from 63% in 2019, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, potentially serving as an additional barrier to sustained engagement. These factors highlight that initial transfer, although important, does not guarantee sustained participation in adult care. Our data show that adult care ambulatory use sustains between 40% and 50% in the 3 to 5 years after transfer, suggesting that patients who remain engaged at 3 years after transfer are likely to continue longer-term engagement. Providing targeted HCT interventions within the first 3 years of adult care is a critical window to support and engage recently transferred patients.

Interventions delivered during pediatric care, such as PHR training, were independently associated with early adulthood outcomes, including adult ambulatory use 3 years after transfer and quality of life. These findings reinforce the importance of pediatric HCT interventions and continuous engagement during pediatric care in preparing patients for transition. Prior studies have shown that those who are lost to follow-up in pediatric care are at high risk of unsuccessful transfer, highlighting the need for close monitoring and early support for those at risk of disengagement while in pediatric care.26,27 In our cohort, however, 9% of patients (58/163) who did not attend their first adult visit were lost to follow-up after the age of 12, suggesting that even among those who remain engaged in pediatric care, additional challenges during early adulthood may also hinder successful transfer. Together, these findings indicate that while pediatric HCT interventions and engagement contribute to transition readiness, additional strategies are still needed to target young adults in pediatric care for transition success.

Our findings demonstrate that different SCD HCT interventions were associated with distinct outcomes. For example, disease education was associated with higher HRQOL in early adulthood. Prior research in SCD and other chronic diseases suggests disease education improves self-care and coping, in addition to increased perception of disease control.28 Transition readiness scores were associated with attendance of first adult visit but not with early adulthood outcomes such as retention, suggesting scores reflect short-term preparedness rather than sustained engagement in adult care.29,30 No transition intervention in our study was associated with reductions in acute care use, aligning with prior research in SCD HCT6 and enforcing that HCT programs are designed to improve self-management and care continuity. Increased acute care use during the transition period reflects complex biopsychosocial interactions unique to this period, including social determinants of health, systems of power,25 mental health, and potential disease progression. Future research is needed to understand targeted drivers of increased acute care use during this period. Together, these findings emphasize the importance of aligning intervention strategies and outcome measures with specific goals of HCT.

There has been a longstanding lack of a universally accepted definition of a successful HCT in SCD. In a recent systematic review endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the authors aimed to identify quality indicators for HCT in childhood chronic diseases. They found that 51% of HCT quality indicators were patient-centered (ie, measures that improve patient-level care, such as patient education).31 Few indicators were informed by patients, incorporated caregiver input, or addressed equity. The National Association of Sickle Cell Centers (NASCC) developed standardized definitions by expert consensus panel for successful transfer (completion of 2 visits with a comprehensive adult sickle cell program within the first year) and integration into adult care (attendance of 50% of scheduled outpatient visits annually in 5 years after transfer of care).19 Currently, no proposed successful HCT definition incorporates the perspectives of both patients and caregivers or their patient-centered outcomes (eg, HRQOL). Further focused development of HCT metrics in SCD is needed to understand patient perspectives in defining a successful HCT in SCD.

Limitations

This study had limitations. The results from our single-center may not be generalizable, particularly in settings with fewer resources. Our study uses a large sample size to mitigate this limitation and improve the reliability of our findings. As a retrospective analysis, the limited availability and quality of data, particularly within adult care settings, represent a major limitation. The fragmented nature of adult health records and a lack of integrated systems, particularly during the transition age when relocation often occurs, remains a major gap and a critical area for future research. Rigorous prospective studies and dedicated resources are needed for improved infrastructure for longitudinal data collection across pediatric and adult care. The analysis of deaths after the age of 18 should be interpreted with caution, as adult mortality data were limited to a single adult site. Data was also limited in adult HRQOL analysis. As a result, health outcome data may be skewed toward health care practices from fewer institutions. In this study, we used health care measures closely aligned with HCT definitions used in prior literature and provided evidence-based outcomes to support and complement the NASCC standard definitions. However, because of the retrospective nature of this study, the number of scheduled visits per patient could not be obtained, and NASCC-defined integration into adult care could not be calculated. Finally, societal barriers, such as poverty and structural racism, that have been shown to affect follow-up care in adults with SCD and were not included in the analysis may skew results. Thus, further work is needed to understand the impact of these barriers on HCT interventions.

Conclusions

In-person SCD education, PHR communication training, and early introduction to adult care may facilitate attendance at the first adult visit, successful transfer, and ambulatory care utilization. Future efforts should prioritize targeted interventions in early adult care and the development of robust data systems to monitor posttransfer outcomes. Validating transfer and integration metrics against clinically meaningful outcomes, such as survival, functional status, and quality of life, will be essential to advancing effective transition care in SCD.
