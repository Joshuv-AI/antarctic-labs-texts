Hidden Agents, Explicit Obligations: A Linguistic Analysis of AI Ethics Guidelines
Authors: Tricia A. Griffin, Roos Goorman, Brian P. Green, Jos V. M. Welie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since 2013, many organizations, governments, and coalitions have issued ethics guidelines aimed at achieving ethically sound artificial intelligence (AI). The literature evaluating these guidelines has so far focused more on what is in them (e.g., principles) than on who is expected to enact them (e.g., developers). We argue that ethical agency in AI Ethics guidelines has been under-scrutinized in the literature, and we seek here to fill that gap. This study relies on transitivity analysis to evaluate 87 operational ethics guidelines for the representation of moral agents and their agency. We identified normative key words, their linguistic function, and agency attribution in 6,935 statements. Our findings reveal 11 distinct agents, with deployers, developers, and AI systems being the most frequently invoked. However, the ethical agency attributed to developers and deployers was overwhelmingly implied, while the tasks assigned to them were more often normative than descriptive. That the agency of the two most powerful agents in AI development is so often hidden in ethics guidelines reveals that the challenges associated with implementing AI ethics guidelines does not stem merely from the “principles to practice” problem, but from a more deeply rooted issue regarding how guideline authors conceive of ethical agency. We evaluate the findings through the dual lenses of agent backgrounding and agent suppression and conclude with advice for authors of ethics guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11948-025-00559-8.

Since 2013, many dozens of organizations, governments, and coalitions have issued ethics guidelines1 aimed at achieving ethically sound artificial intelligence (AI). Arguably no other professional endeavor has elicited so much ethics attention in so short a period. These documents have been widely analyzed for their normative content, including the values and principles they have in common (Floridi et al., 2018; Hagendorff, 2020; Jobin et al., 2019; Ryan & Stahl, 2021) and how they reveal and entrench issues of representation and power (Daly et al., 2019; Floridi et al., 2018; Greene et al., 2019).

Scholars have also questioned whether these guidelines can be at all effective at operationalizing ethically sound AI (Hagendorff, 2022; Mittelstadt, 2019; Morley et al., 2020; Munn, 2022). Recent empirical research has also shown that when AI developers face ethics questions in their work, they do not look to AI ethics guidelines for help (Griffin et al., 2024). It perhaps goes without saying that if practitioners do not consult an ethics guideline that was formulated specifically to influence their work, the guideline is ineffective. But why is it that AI developers do not turn to ethics guidelines, of which many now exist, when facing ethics questions? The hypothesis underlying this study is that the effectiveness of ethics guidelines in AI, or rather their ineffectiveness, may be partially related to the way in which guideline authors conceive of and assign ethical agency.

Ethics guidelines are traditionally aimed at influencing the behavior of particular kinds of experts, such as physicians or engineers. These documents serve as an account of the behavior that is morally obligatory for a specific group of people, precisely because they are members of that group (Laas et al., 2022). As we illustrate in this study, AI ethics guidelines are a notable departure from the traditional usage of professional ethics guidelines in three ways. First, they aim to achieve ethical algorithms, not necessarily ethical professionals. Second, many of these guidelines are not limited to a single group of moral agents. They often include a diverse network of agents who are each responsible for particular outcomes in AI development and deployment. And third, these agents include not only persons, be they individual or institutional, but AI systems themselves.

More pragmatically, ethics guidelines are attempts by their issuing institution(s) to assign responsibility to one or more agents for achieving ethically sound outcomes from professional practice. This process can be lengthy and often involves several rounds of negotiations between multiple stakeholders (Kaptein & Wempe, 1998; Messikomer & Cirka, 2010). Yet, despite their ostensibly good faith efforts, AI organizations have been criticized for issuing these guidelines as a form of “ethics washing,” or making an appearance of virtuous behavior without changing any of their underlying structures, beliefs, or practices (Cath & Keyes, 2021; Wagner, 2018). This criticism is based in part on the unenforceability, or “toothlessness,” of the guidelines, but also on the perceived difficulty of putting high-level principles into practice (Mittelstadt, 2019; Morley et al., 2020; Munn, 2022). These challenges, we argue, will be exacerbated when guidelines fail to clearly identify and contend with ethical agency.

We have found no research to date that explores how agents or their agency are determined in AI ethics guidelines. We argue that the ways in which guideline authors or contributors are conceiving of (and communicating about) ethical agency has been under-scrutinized in the literature, and we seek here to fill that gap. Accordingly, where previous studies have focused on what is in the codes, this study focuses on who. We conduct a quantitative linguistic analysis of operational ethics guidelines in AI to bring empirical evidence into the discourse about agency in these guidelines. The article proceeds in five parts. We first describe our methods for determining the distribution of ethical agency in AI ethics guidelines. In the second section we present our results, including the distribution of agency among 11 identified agents in 87 guidelines. Third, we discuss the semantic categories of agent backgrounding and agent suppression, paying particular attention to how these arise via passive voice, nominalization, and instrumentalization. After reviewing the limitations of this research, we conclude with recommendations for the authors of ethics guidance documents.

Methods

Theoretical Framework

This study relies on transitivity analysis as described in Halliday’s (2005) systemic functional grammar framework. Transitivity analysis focuses on how linguistic choices render an author or speaker’s point of view ideologically significant. Consider, for example, the statement “John made a mistake.” Here, the point of view of the author is clear. There is an agent (John), an action (made), and an object (a mistake). But if the author were to instead say, “Mistakes were made,” we lose the agent. The author hides John from view, thus shielding him from accountability. Transitivity analysis is thus useful for understanding how authors or speakers position people and assign agency.

In this study, our focus is on a subset of transitivity that is particularly relevant in the ethical domain: modality. Modality analysis reveals an author’s judgements about certainty (via modal verbs like will and can), obligation (e.g., must, should), and probability (e.g., could, might). We apply this framework to interrogate the choices guideline authors make when communicating who is responsible for ethically sound AI. While the systemic functional grammar framework identifies 13 elements that can be quantified by transitivity (Halliday & Matthiessen, 2013), given the narrow scope of our investigation, we limit our work to two: the material process (the modal verb) and the actor assigned to that verb. These will be discussed in more detail in the sections that follow.

Document Selection

We derived the initial corpus of documents for this study from two previously published reviews of AI ethics guidelines, which we then refined and augmented. We started with Jobin et al. (2019)’s scoping review of 84 documents representing “soft law or non-legal norms” with regard to ethical AI. We then included the nine documents that Ryan and Stahl (2021) added to the Jobin corpus for their own normative analysis. We applied the following three criteria to this corpus:

First, our aim was to evaluate ethical agency in active (and thus ostensibly binding) ethics guidelines. As such, we excluded discussion documents and policy recommendations due to the provisional nature of these documents. We also excluded documents that focused exclusively on data management, because AI systems, while dependent on data, also involve issues related to model selection, deployment geographies, public safety, and power dynamics. Therefore, we included only those documents that issued comprehensive guidance about the development, deployment, and maintenance of automated systems.

Second, we included only documents issued in English, either natively or as an official translation. As this study relies on the usage and meaning of English modal verbs, we reasoned that unofficial or automated translations might not correctly translate normative language or how it would be contextually understood in the original language. We recognize the limitations involved in excluding guidance documents written in other languages and consider this an area for further research and collaboration with experts in those languages.

Third, we conducted an online search to determine whether any of the remaining documents had been updated or revised. We only included the most recent versions of the guidelines under the assumption that those were “active” and therefore directly binding to the agents named in them. This also meant removing iterative documents issued by the same organization over multiple years.

As the two baseline studies only include documents issued up to 2019, we also conducted a review of Google, Web of Science, and Scopus for documents issued between January 2020 and January 2024 that referred in their titles to “ethical AI” and “our approach” or “principles” or “guidelines” and that met the aforementioned inclusion criteria.

As a result of our literature review, 54 documents were removed from the initial corpus of documents used in prior studies and 48 were newly added; five documents were updated with final or adopted versions. This gave us a total of 87 documents for analysis (see Fig. 1). The full list of guidelines included in this study is provided in Supplementary Material, Appendix 1. As shown in this appendix, ten documents were issued by professional associations, nine by coalitions, 35 by for-profit organizations, 20 by governments, and 13 by non-profits. Only 29 documents credited the authors/contributors.

Qualitative Labeling

We pursued a three-step annotation process to identify and analyze statements (sentences) in the corpus of documents to determine the distribution of ethical agents. First, we searched the documents for normative key words, then documented the key words’ linguistic function and, finally, labeled the agent. Each of these are described below:

Normative Key Words The primary means by which normative obligations are expressed in English is via modal auxiliary verbs like must or shall, as well as via semi-modal verbs like ought to or have to. We began our annotation process by searching for the following 15 modal auxiliary and semi-modal verbs: can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, have/has to, ought to, obliged to, need/necessary to, required to, and duty to.

Modality

The second step was to identify what function the selected verbs served. That is, whether the verb was describing a potential future state (descriptive) or an obligation (normative). These functions are referred to by linguists as modality, and for this study we relied on the three modalities identified by Palmer (2003).

Epistemic Modality (Descriptive) is an expression of possibility, likelihood, or certainty. As an example, in the statement, “Delegating decisions to AI can go wrong,” the modal auxiliary verb ‘can’ indicates a possible future state. Other modal auxiliary verbs that often function epistemically include could, may, might, and would.Deontic Modality (Normative) is an expression of obligation, permission, or advice that is imposed externally onto the agent. Consider the sentence, “If the customer consents, you can legally use that customer’s data in your model.” This sentence also contains the modal auxiliary verb ‘can,’ but it is here indicating permission, and that permission is granted by an entity external to the subject (a law/regulation). More often, however, expressions of deontic modality involve modal auxiliary verbs like must, shall, and should, or semi-modal verbs like need to and have to.Dynamic Modality (Normative) is an expression of volition or ability that is within the agent’s control. For example, in the sentence, “We can program our robots to obey human laws” the verb ‘can’ signals an ability the issuer believes is within their control, and they are binding themselves to that action. For this project, dynamic modality was narrowly applied only to statements of volition or ability expressed by a document’s issuer in which they were binding themselves to an action or principle.

Reasonable people can (and do) disagree with whether a modal auxiliary or semi-modal verb is functioning in an epistemic, deontic, or dynamic way. For example, in the sentence, “AI systems can access personal data,” the verb ‘can’ could be functioning in any one of the three modalities depending on the nature of the document and the context in which the sentence is embedded. Our labeling process thus involved paying close attention to the sentences before and after the identified statement, the section headers, and the professionals (if any) the authors claimed the document was meant to guide.

Agents

For the purposes of this study, we defined an agent simply as an entity perceived to be capable of acting. We did not impose an a priori scheme for coding agents. Instead, we took the guidelines at face value since this is how people encountering them in practice would engage with them. Importantly, the authors of the guidelines also did not impose a priori definitions for agents2; they did not define nor distinguish between terms like “deployer” and “provider” or “developer” and “designer.” We therefore relied on grounded theory and allowed the agent identifications to emerge as they appeared in the statements labeled through the modal verb search (Glaser, 1992).

The primary question we asked ourselves during this stage was, “Who or what will/should/must/etc.?” We then grouped the agents that shared similar characteristics into our final set of codes. For example, our code for Developer includes instances of developer, designer, builder, engineer, technologist, scientist, and/or researcher. As the labelling exercise commenced, it became clear that the authors of the documents would sometimes explicitly name the responsible agent (e.g., “Developers of AI must…”) and at other times would imply an agent (e.g., “The development of AI must…”). We therefore labeled agents in the identified statements as either Explicit Agent or Implied Agent.

Special Cases

Some statements invoked multiple agents, modals, and/or modalities, or did not invoke an agent at all. These statements required special handling. First were statements with multiple agents that had unique modals assigned to them. For example, in the statement, “AI systems may be able to perform a given task better than humans could,” it is the AI systems that may but humans who could. Statements of this nature were given two separate label groups to avoid false associations in the data set. In contrast, statements in which multiple agents shared a common modal (e.g., “Organizations and governments should protect privacy”) were coded with a single label group to avoid double counting.

There were also cases in which statements included both a modal auxiliary verb and a semi-modal verb in tandem. For example, “Organizations may need to adjust their strategies as laws change.” We treated these phrases as a single modal unit with one modality. In the example provided, we would label the statement as epistemic because the likelihood “may” is literally and temporally happening before the deontic obligation “need to.”

Finally, many statements expressing ethical guidance did not invoke an agent at all. If it could not be inferred from the text which agent(s) the authors plausibly had in mind, we labeled the agent as Invisible. In other instances, we could logically deduce the appropriate agent(s). Statements such as “AI systems should be auditable” were labeled as both Implied Agent: Deployer and Implied Agent: Developer because no other agent(s) could plausibly make a system auditable or give an auditor access to the system.

Exclusion Criteria

Any statement that did not offer descriptive or normative counsel about artificial intelligence systems was excluded from the labeling process. We thus excluded modal statements found in tables of contents, references, legal disclaimers, definitions, interrogatives, acknowledgements, and statements describing the document itself. Also excluded were rhetorical devices such as “it should be noted that” or “as we can now see” as this usage is meant to bring attention to a fact or observation rather than to offer ethics counsel. Lastly, if the document included an executive summary in which principles and normative obligations discussed in the main body of the document were repeated verbatim, those sentences were only coded once to avoid double counting. However, if one document included a direct quote from a different document, that repeated text was coded again, since it ostensibly was deemed important by the authors quoting it.

Results

Across the 87 documents, a total of 6,935 statements met our inclusion criteria for annotation. Of these, 3,223 were epistemic, 3,434 were deontic, and 278 were dynamic. We identified 11 agents, which we define and provide grounded examples for in Table 1. The identified agents were invoked 8,066 times across the corpus (1,131 statements invoked two or more agents). Across all agents (n = 8,066), the most often invoked were deployers (33%), developers (22%), AI systems (13%), governments (7%), and the public (5%). Furthermore, agents overall were invoked explicitly 48% of the time (versus 52% implied) and given normative obligations 56% of the time (versus 44% descriptive).

Discussion

The aim of this research was to understand how authors of AI ethics guidelines treat ethical agency. Our hypothesis was that the perceived ineffectiveness of the guidelines may not be reducible merely to a principles-to-practice problem but may be more deeply rooted in the ways in which authors conceive of and assign ethical agency. Using transitivity analysis to quantify the use of modal verbs, their modality, and the agents to whom they are assigned, our results affirm that the authors of AI ethics guidelines do envision a robust network of responsible agents, with developers and deployers as the primary hubs. Furthermore, in aggregate, ethics guidance authors bring a relatively balanced approach to what these agents could, might, would, etc. do (imagining morally significant situations) and what they therefore must, should, shall, etc. do (resulting obligations).

We note, however, that the agency of developers and deployers is overwhelmingly implied. This is consistent throughout issuer types and thus cannot be attributed to any particular type of issuer or organization. Deployers and developers are arguably the two most powerful agents in the field, and it bears further inquiry into how this treatment of their agency might be affecting the usefulness of AI ethics guidelines.

While we will not speculate about the authors’ intentions or how consciously they made their word choices, what we can do is explore the effects of those choices. For this exploration, we will rely on Theo van Leeuwen’s (2008) concepts of agent suppression and agent backgrounding, including how they manifest through passive voice, nominalization, and instrumentalization.

Agent Suppression and Agent Backgrounding

van Leeuwen (2008) identifies two semantic practices that exclude or diminish agents in texts: agent suppression and agent backgrounding. Agent suppression refers to the practice of excluding from a text agents who nonetheless exist and are relevant to the situation being presented. Agent backgrounding, by contrast, occurs when authors do mention the responsible agents but de-emphasize them by pushing them into a subordinate position or clause. We note three ways in which ethics guideline authors suppress or background ethical agents: passive voice, nominalization, and instrumentalization.

Passive Voice

When authors use passive voice, they either push an agent into the background (e.g., “A mistake was made by John”) or suppress the agent entirely (e.g., “Mistakes were made”). The easiest way to identify passive voice constructions, however, is to look for the verb form “to be” followed by a past tense form of another verb (usually in an “-ed” form) (Leech, 2006). We here offer a few examples of this from our dataset:

“A culture of cooperation, trust, and transparency should be fostered among researchers and developers of AI” (Future of Life Institute, 2017).“There should be an appointed representative at each level of the organization, including the board of directors, who are ethics leaders or ethics champions, and who should meet regularly to discuss ethical issues and best practice within the organization” (Brey et al., 2019, 68).“A human rights-oriented development of technology might increase costs and force developers and business to slow their current time-to-market, as the impact of products and services on individual rights and society have to be assessed in advance” (Council of Europe, 2019, 20).

These are all examples of agent backgrounding. It would have been clearer to write, “Researchers and developers of AI should foster a culture of cooperation, trust, and transparency.” Nevertheless, it is still apparent that the authors of this statement are pointing to these agents. Of course, there may be other agents who the authors expect to help foster this culture, but at minimum we can assign implied ethical agency to researchers and developers. The same is true for the other two examples. Someone should appoint ethics champions at each level of a deployer organization, and developers and businesses might indeed slow their release schedules to comply with human rights requirements. There is still a trace of the ethical agents in these sentences, even if their agency is pushed to the background. The effect of this sentence structure on readers, however, is that their attention is drawn first to the culture, the appointing of a representative, and human rights, rather than to the agents who are ethically responsible for making these states of affairs a reality.

In contrast to this backgrounding device, we found more than 300 instances of agent suppression via passive voice, wherein we could not deduce an agent at all. We labeled the agent for these statements as “Invisible.” For example:

“The economic prosperity created by AI should be shared broadly, to the benefit of all humanity” (Future of Life Institute, 2017).“Equal respect for the moral worth and dignity of all human beings must be ensured” (HLEG, 2019, p. 11).“Technology should not be allowed to become too clever to be accountable” (Sage, 2017, 1).

“Technology should not be allowed to become too clever to be accountable” (Sage, 2017, 1).

The ethical agents in these statements are excluded entirely. In the first instance, the agent is the entity that is expected to share its economic prosperity, in the second, it is whoever is responsible for ensuring equal respect, and in the final statement the agent is the entity doing the allowing. In each case, we could hypothesize several possible agents who could share, ensure, or (not) allow, but it was unclear from the context in which we found the statements whom the document’s authors meant.

The effect of these choices is that authors focus readers’ attention on what should happen rather than who should do it. Agents are hidden from view, agents who presumably exist and who are being given important normative responsibilities. As a result, a person reading the guidelines does not have to consider whether the guideline is addressing them. Readers may reasonably assume they are not the ones responsible for sharing, ensuring, or allowing. The assertions thus serve an aspirational function rather than a normative one. Authors promote an ideal future state but hang the responsibility for it on no one in particular.

Nominalization

Nominalization describes the practice of turning a verb or adjective into a noun (Leech, 2006). For example, making “develop” into “development” or “to ask” into “the ask.” In doing so, guideline authors again give priority to actions rather than the people responsible for them. Consider the following statements:

“People should be told when significant decisions about them are being made by AI” (Dubai, 2019).“The wave of automation caused by the A/IS revolution will displace a very large share of jobs across domains and value chains” (IEEE, 2019).“Individuals should be able to raise concerns with the ‘ethics leader’ within their department […]” (Brey et al., 2019).

“People should be told when significant decisions about them are being made by AI” (Dubai, 2019).

These sentences all convey inarguably important normative assertions. In each instance, however, the grammatical subject of the sentence is not the entity with ethical agency. It is left to the reader to intuit that the agent responsible in each case is the deployer of the technology. To say that “people should be told” implies that the deployer should do the telling. Likewise, it is not the A/IS revolution that will displace jobs, but business managers who will. And the only way for individuals to “be able to” raise ethics concerns at work is if their employer cultivates an environment that incentivizes it.

Had the authors of these statements explicitly named the agents (e.g., “Management teams will displace a very large share of jobs…”), they would make visible not only the responsible ethical agents, but also the political dimensions of human decision-making. The revised sentence does not simply make the ethical agents visible, it invites scrutiny. The effect of nominalization is that it creates enough distance between an agent and their actions that the agents themselves cannot be called to account.

Instrumentalization

Instrumentalization occurs when authors refer to actions or to instruments rather than to agents (van Leeuwen, 2008). In the case of assigning agency to actions, we most often saw this with respect to statements emphasizing “the development of AI” rather than “developers of AI.” Consider, for example, the following:

“The development and use of AI systems must permit the growth of the well-being of all sentient beings” (Abrassart et al., 2018).“Development and validation of AI must be based on data that accurately represent the diversity of people in the target group” (van Houten, 2020).“To create autonomous and intelligent technical systems that enhance and extend human well-being and freedom, values-based design methods must put human advancement at the core of development of technical systems” (IEEE, 2019).

The authors here imply the existence of developers, users, validators, and designers, but their agency is backgrounded by virtue of pointing to the actions they take rather than to the agents themselves. There may be valid reasons to do this kind of backgrounding, including the mandate guideline authors may (rightly) have to motivate many minds to think about what they are doing (Lichtenberg, 1996). To point too resolutely at one person or job function may result in others determining that it is “not my job” and turning away from the guidance altogether. Using implied agency by emphasizing the process (the development of AI) rather than a particular role (a developer, who may or may not also be the designer) may appeal to authors’ sensitivity towards including as many minds as possible in their ethics guidance.

But we cannot ignore another effect of this strategy. Assigning strong normative obligations to an implied agent can also result in practitioners who, because it is the work that matters and not themselves, distance themselves from engaging with their own ethical obligations or participating in moral deliberations about their work. If the statement by Abrassart et al. (2018), for example, were written with the ethical agents in full view, it would read: “Developers and users of AI systems must permit the growth of the well-being of all sentient beings.” This certainly lands differently. We will set aside whether the authors are asking too much of these agents and note only that this revised sentence places ethical agency squarely with people rather than processes, and it therefore does a better job of challenging those agents to think about what they are doing.

The second way guideline authors instrumentalized agency is by assigning it to AI systems. In our analysis, AI systems were assigned agency 1,063 times, representing roughly 13% of all labeled statements. The majority of these statements (70%) were epistemic in nature, denoting what AI systems can/might/will do. The remaining (30%) were deontic, denoting what AI systems must/should do. For example:

“Systems that make decisions and process data can also undermine economic, social, and cultural rights; for example, they can impact the provision of vital services, such as healthcare and education, and limit access to opportunities like employment” (Bacciarelli et al., 2018, p. 2).“AI systems should respect human rights, diversity, and the autonomy of individuals” (Australia Department of Industry, 2024).“AI’s predictive power and relentless nudging, even if unintentional, should be at the service of human self-determination and foster societal cohesion, not undermining of human dignity or human flourishing” (Floridi et al., 2018).

There is robust debate about whether human communities will (or should) afford moral agency to a sufficiently advanced AI (see for example, Floridi & Sanders, 2004; Himma, 2008; Johnson & Miller, 2008; Coeckelbergh, 2009; Sullins, 2009; Cervantes et al., 2020). We will not attempt to unpack the philosophical arguments for and against machine agency in this article. However, we do take the position that moral agency is, at present, exclusively a human attribute. Moral agents are humans. Algorithms are neither. Ethics guidelines are written for and distributed to human moral agents, not machines.

We, therefore, submit that when authors of ethics guidelines attribute agency to AI systems, they are, in fact, talking to developers, deployers, users, etc. They are telling human agents how to behave through the systems they build/deploy/use. Even in cases where the agency would seem to, in fact, belong to an automated system - as in the case of self-driving cars, which “must follow the rules of the road” - the moral obligation still does not belong with the vehicle. It belongs with the designers, developers, and engineers who must program, build, and maintain the cars such that they (collectively) obey traffic laws. The effect of instrumentalization is that authors draw their readers’ attention to the behavior of the AI system rather than to the humans who programmed and built them. By assigning agency to the AI system, the authors effectively hide the true ethical agents from view.

Limitations

Three important limitations must be noted in this study. First, as we wrote in our methods section, this study relies on the usage and meaning of English modal verbs, and therefore only documents issued in English natively or as an official translation were analyzed. There may be value in similar studies on documents issued in other languages, but that analysis should be conducted by experts in those languages.

Second, the modal auxiliary and semi-modal verbs selected for this study are not the only ways in which authors communicate normativity. Our goal was not to code every possible formulation of normative or descriptive statements, but to arrive at a coherent and consistent set of labels that would yield sufficient data on ethical agency. As a result, we do not claim this to be an exhaustive annotation of normative expressions in these documents, but we do think the verbs we selected for inclusion allow the study to be representative of how agency is conceived.

Third, because this article addresses the linguistic and semantic choices that authors/contributors of AI ethics guidelines make, we attempted initially to understand these authors’ backgrounds, including their credentials and experience in developing normative guidelines for AI. As we engaged in this process, however, it became clear that (1) many documents do not credit authors/contributors, and (2) a cursory examination of academic credentials, job titles, or even LinkedIn profiles would not yield sufficient information about a named author's or contributor’s qualifications or experience in crafting ethics guidelines. A comprehensive understanding of the competencies of those crafting these documents is beyond the scope of this article, and we consider it a subject for future research.

Conclusion

The ethics guidelines we evaluated in this study represent the shared practices that will, ideally, bring about “ethical AI,” or so their authors presumably hope. Guideline authors do, therefore, need to make choices about whom to include or exclude from the practices they propose. It is also true, however, that ethics guidelines are the product of a political negotiation within their issuing organizations. There may be short-term political reasons for authors to imply the agency of human agents, either through backgrounding or suppression, but the long-term risk is that those most responsible for these technologies are not required to take themselves seriously as ethical agents. Linguistic choices have normative consequences. There is no reason to expect developers or deployers to engage meaningfully with the guidance presented if the guidance does not engage meaningfully with them.

We offer three recommendations for the future of AI ethics guidance (if it is to survive at all as something more than window dressing). First, it is vital that guideline authors explicitly name the agents whom they expect to act ethically. This is not to say that authors cannot engage in what an ethical process might look like, only that their guidance should recognize that it is people who are engaging in that process, and it is those people (not the process or even the automated system) who carry the ethical responsibility. Guideline authors should consider, for example, making explicit the variety of roles covered by an umbrella term like “developer” to help distinguish the morally relevant aspects of different work streams. Likewise, they should be vigilant about their semantic choices with respect to the use of passive voice, nominalization, and instrumentalization. These practices hide moral agents from view, which undermines both the effectiveness and legitimacy of ethics guidance.

Second is to maintain a clear awareness of how descriptive (epistemic) and normative (deontic and dynamic) statements mutually enrich each other. We consider descriptive statements to be evidence of the moral imagination at work, which expresses what might, could, or would, etc. happen. Deontic and dynamic statements then establish relevant normative guardrails, or what the named agent(s) should or must, etc. do. There should be a healthy dialogue between these two kinds of statements in ethics guidance. Two documents in the corpus used this dialogical format to good effect (explicit agency + mutually enriching modalities) and can be looked to as examples (GIZ & Data, 2023; Microsoft, 2018). These documents are different in scope and address different audiences, but we were nonetheless impressed with the outputs.

Finally, we recommend that guideline authors meaningfully involve the people who they expect to follow their guidance in the creation of the guidelines. The crafting of ethics guidelines should always be the result of a collaborative effort that includes multiple stakeholders, including members of the communities who will be subjected to the deployments. But when ethics guidance is issued without the meaningful input of those whose behavior they are meant to influence, they risk becoming disconnected from practical reality and the moral complexities that practitioners face. We found little evidence that many of the agents (particularly developers) were included in their creation. For future iterations of ethics guidelines, authors from government, industry, academia, and professional practice should treat the people to whom they assign ethical responsibilities as full ethical agents capable of moral reasoning and deliberation. This means including them not just as consultants but as active participants in writing future iterations of ethics guidelines.

This study contributes to the richness of research on ethics and ethics guidelines, and aids authors of such guidance in considering the ethical agency of the people they seek to influence. AI ethics guidelines can be a meaningful vehicle for moral deliberation among people who are making decisions together. This matters in AI development because the decisions that these agents are making will often impact large populations, many of whom are vulnerable groups. Only by making decision-makers visible can we expect those decision-makers to take their own ethical agency seriously.
