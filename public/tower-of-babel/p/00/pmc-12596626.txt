Paradoxical ileocecal perforation during early anti-tuberculosis therapy: A rare case report
Authors: Yoseph Mulatu Habte, Binyam Mulatu Habte, Yabetse Alemayehu Kifle, Esimael Musema Abdu, Makida Mulatu Habte, Shimelis Ayalew Yimer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceTuberculosis remains a global health challenge, with extrapulmonary forms accounting for a significant proportion of cases. Intestinal tuberculosis, though uncommon, can present with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms and lead to severe complications, including intestinal perforation.Presentation of caseWe report a 24-year-old male with a two-year history of right lower quadrant abdominal pain, weight loss, and anorexia. Imaging and colonoscopy revealed ileocecal thickening with ulceration and stenosis; biopsies confirmed tuberculous enteritis. Six days after starting anti-tubercular therapy, he developed generalized peritonitis due to an ileocecal perforation. Right hemicolectomy with ileotransverse anastomosis was performed. He received postoperative antibiotics, analgesics, and continued anti-tubercular therapy, with good recovery on follow-up.DiscussionParadoxical intestinal perforation during anti-tubercular therapy is a rare but serious complication, likely due to a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to mycobacterial antigens, particularly in the ileocecal region. It may mimic treatment failure, delaying intervention. Early surgical intervention, guided by a high index of suspicion, is crucial to reducing morbidity and improving outcomes in intestinal tuberculosis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis, caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, is a contagious disease that primarily targets the lungs but can also affect other parts of the body, resulting in what is known as extrapulmonary tuberculosis [1]. Intestinal tuberculosis (ITB) can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, but the ileocecal region is the most commonly involved. Its symptoms are often nonspecific and may include abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and fever [[1], [2], [3]].

Intestinal tuberculosis can lead to several complications, including abscess formation, fistulas, strictures, perforation, and bowel obstruction. Additionally, 6 % to 30 % of patients undergoing anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) may develop a paradoxical reaction, defined as clinical or radiological worsening of pre-existing lesions or emergence of new lesions despite appropriate anti-tubercular therapy, which is thought to result from a delayed hypersensitivity response to mycobacterial antigens [[4], [5], [6]].

We present a case of a 24-year-old male presenting with generalized peritonitis secondary to intestinal perforation six days after initiation of anti-tubercular therapy for the diagnosis of Intestinal tuberculosis. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [7].

Case presentation

A 24-year-old male initially presented with a two-year history of intermittent, dull aching right lower quadrant abdominal pain, accompanied by anorexia, indigestion, and progressive weight loss. Despite the chronicity, he denied fever, diarrhea, or gastrointestinal bleeding, and had no known chronic illnesses. Physical examination revealed localized tenderness without signs of peritonitis, and initial laboratory tests were unremarkable. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated asymmetric thickening of the ileocecal valve with enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes, raising suspicion for a granulomatous disease. Colonoscopy revealed a severely stenosed, deformed ileocecal valve with nodular and ulcerated mucosa, blocking access to the terminal ileum. Biopsy specimens tested positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis by GeneXpert MTB/RIF, while histopathology showed well-formed epithelioid granulomas indicative of tuberculous enteritis (Fig. 1). The patient was promptly diagnosed with gastrointestinal tuberculosis and started on standard anti-tubercular therapy (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol), with plans for close follow-up.Fig. 1Granulomatous inflammation with well-formed epithelioid cell granulomas suggestive of tuberculous enteritis.Fig. 1

Six days after initiating anti-tubercular therapy, the patient presented with a 12-hour history of acute abdominal pain. The pain was dull, and localized to the lower abdomen. It was accompanied by nausea, multiple episodes of bilious vomiting, abdominal bloating, and general malaise.

On examination, he appeared acutely ill, in pain, tachycardic, and tachypneic. Oral mucosa was dry. Abdominal examination revealed reduced movement with respiration, generalized tenderness with guarding and rigidity. Bowel sounds were diminished. Clinical impression was generalized peritonitis secondary to hollow viscus perforation in the setting of gastrointestinal tuberculosis (GI TB).

A plain abdominal X-ray was obtained, which showed centrally located dilated bowel loops, few air fluid levels, and free subdiaphragmatic air beneath both hemidiaphragms, indicating pneumoperitoneum (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Plain abdominal X-ray (A) centrally located dilated bowel loops with few air fluid levels (B) free subdiaphragmatic air beneath both hemidiaphragms suggestive of bowel perforation.Fig. 2

Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis (13,000/mm3) with a left shift (92.2 %) and elevated ESR (88 mm/h), while renal and liver function tests remained within normal limits. The patient was promptly resuscitated and started on intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole before being taken for emergency exploratory laparotomy.

Under aseptic conditions, a midline laparotomy was performed. Approximately 1 L of pus mixed with gastrointestinal contents was found in the peritoneal cavity and subdiaphragmatic space. The terminal ileum was thickened and dilated, with narrowing at the ileocecal junction. A 0.5 cm perforation was identified on the anti-mesenteric border of the ileal side of the ileocecal junction. The perforation was oval-shaped with inflamed but well-demarcated edges. The surrounding ileal wall was thickened, edematous, and inflamed. Multiple enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes were also observed. The large bowel was collapsed, and the remaining small bowel appeared inflamed.

A right hemicolectomy including 20 cm of distal ileum was performed. Primary end-to-end ileo-transverse anastomosis was done. The mesenteric defect was closed, hemostasis secured, and a right paracolic drain was left in place. The abdomen was closed in layers with the skin left open. The resected specimen was sent for histopathologic examination.

Gross examination of the surgical specimen revealed multiple ulcers with thinned, necrotic walls involving the ileum and cecum, and a 2 cm ulcerated polypoid cecal lesion. A conglomerate of mesenteric lymph nodes was also noted. Microscopically, the ileal segments showed ulcerated mucosa infiltrated by mixed inflammatory cells, with numerous confluent epithelioid granulomas containing Langerhans giant cells and central necrosis, consistent with necrotizing granulomatous inflammation due to tuberculous enteritis (Fig. 3). Sections from mesenteric lymph nodes showed caseous necrosis, epithelioid cell granulomas, and Langerhans giant cells, consistent with tuberculous lymphadenitis (Fig. 4).Fig. 3Microscopic appearance of the ileal segments (A and B) showing numerous confluent epithelioid granulomas containing Langhans giant cells and central caseous necrosis consistent with intestinal tuberculosis.Fig. 3Fig. 4Sections from mesenteric lymph nodes (A & B) showing epithelioid cell granulomas and Langhans giant cells.Fig. 4

Postoperatively, the patient was managed with intravenous antibiotics, analgesics, and supportive care. After a one week hospital stay, he was discharged with oral metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, and continuation of his anti-tuberculosis regimen. He was followed up weekly for the first month, then monthly thereafter. During follow-up, the patient demonstrated steady clinical improvement, with regain of appetite and weight gain of 6 kg over 3 months, and normalization of ESR. Case discussion

Intestinal tuberculosis (ITB) represents the sixth most common form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, predominantly affecting younger individuals in endemic regions. The ileocecal region is the most frequently involved site in ITB, attributed to several anatomical and physiological factors, including the presence of abundant lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches), relatively slow transit time leading to physiologic stasis, and a high absorptive capacity in this segment of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract [5,8,9]. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) can infect the GI tract via several routes: hematogenous spread from a primary pulmonary focus, ingestion of infected sputum in active pulmonary tuberculosis, direct extension from adjacent infected lymph nodes, or through ingestion of contaminated dairy products containing Mycobacterium bovis [5,8].

Clinically, intestinal tuberculosis (ITB) presents a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific and often insidious symptoms. Patients commonly report vague abdominal pain, anorexia, weight loss, diarrhea, fever, and general malaise. However, no single clinical feature is pathognomonic, and the disease frequently mimics other chronic inflammatory conditions such as Crohn's disease, gastrointestinal malignancy, or other granulomatous diseases [1,3,10]. Distinguishing ITB from Crohn's disease can be particularly difficult, as both may present with segmental involvement of the ileocecal region, transmural inflammation, strictures, and granulomas on histopathology. Similarly, malignancies such as adenocarcinoma or lymphoma may share overlapping features, including mass-like lesions, bowel obstruction, and systemic symptoms. This nonspecific clinical presentation, compounded by the rarity of ITB in non-endemic regions, often leads to delayed or missed diagnoses, with consequent progression to advanced disease or complications [5].

Complications of ITB are diverse, including the formation of abscesses, fistulas, strictures leading to bowel obstruction, and intestinal perforation. Perforation is a less common but particularly grave complication, reported in 4 % to 7.6 % of cases, with mortality rates reported up to 30 % in some series. Perforation may precede anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) or occur during treatment. The latter scenario, often described as a paradoxical reaction, is thought to result from an exaggerated immune response, specifically, a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to mycobacterial antigens released during bacterial killing by effective chemotherapy. Such paradoxical responses manifest as clinical deterioration or new lesion development despite microbiological control [[4], [5], [6],11]. Paradoxical reactions in tuberculosis typically presents in the first 3 months after initiation of anti-tubercular therapy, with most reported cases of intestinal perforation occurring after the first month [11]. In our patient, however, the reaction occurred as early as day 6. This unusually early onset significantly deviates from the expected timeline and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion even during the first week of treatment.

While intestinal perforation in tuberculosis can result from diastatic causes secondary to distal obstruction and increased intraluminal pressure from ileocecal stenosis, this mechanism was considered less likely in our case. The perforation was relatively small and located on the anti-mesenteric border of the ileum rather than at the site of maximal stenosis. Additionally, the temporal proximity of perforation to initiation of anti-tubercular therapy and the absence of significant upstream bowel distension favor a paradoxical immune-mediated reaction as the primary etiology. Poor outcomes are associated with older age, comorbid conditions, multiple perforations, and delayed diagnosis or surgical intervention [[12], [13], [14]].

Laboratory findings in Intestinal tuberculosis are often nonspecific, typically showing elevated ESR and CRP, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia [9]. Imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT, may reveal asymmetric thickening of the ileocecal area, necrotic lymph nodes, bowel strictures, ascites, or peritoneal enhancement [15]. In our case, while CT was not performed, abdominal ultrasound revealed asymmetric thickening of the ileocecal valve and enlarged hypoechoic mesenteric lymph nodes without central echogenic hilum, features suggestive of granulomatous inflammation.

Colonoscopy is an essential diagnostic tool in suspected Intestinal tuberculosis, often identifying ulcers, mucosal nodularity, or a distorted ileocecal valve [5]. Histopathology usually demonstrates granulomatous inflammation with caseation and Langerhans giant cells. Although acid-fast bacilli are rarely detected, culture of intestinal biopsy samples remains the definitive diagnostic method despite its low sensitivity and slow processing time [5,8,11].

In suspected cases of perforation, CT scans are useful in identifying free air, fluid accumulation, or bowel wall defects, facilitating early detection. Therefore, diagnosing Intestinal tuberculosis, especially when complicated by perforation, requires integration of clinical, radiological, histopathological, and microbiological evidence [9,11,15].

The development of intestinal perforation shortly after initiation of ATT, as observed in this patient, highlights a rare but recognized paradoxical reaction phenomenon. Although the patient had undergone colonoscopy prior to initiating anti-tubercular therapy, the six-day interval between the procedure and symptom onset, combined with the location of the perforation at the ileocecal junction and histopathologic evidence of active tuberculous inflammation, makes a traumatic or iatrogenic perforation highly unlikely. Clinicians must be aware of this potential complication and maintain a high index of suspicion when patients deteriorate clinically during treatment. Management of perforated ITB requires urgent surgical intervention. Current surgical consensus favors resection of the diseased bowel segment with primary anastomosis over simple suture repair, given the high rates of anastomotic leakage and fistula formation associated with the latter [13,14].

Anti-tubercular therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment. For immunocompetent individuals, a standard 6 to 9 month regimen including isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide is effective. Treatment duration may be extended in cases of drug resistance, intolerance, or extrapulmonary involvement requiring prolonged therapy [16].

This case highlights a rare but important paradoxical reaction, intestinal perforation occurring shortly after initiating anti-tubercular therapy. Strengths include thorough documentation, microbiological and histopathological confirmation, and clear intraoperative correlation. These elements enhance clinical relevance, particularly in endemic regions where ITB is common but such complications may be overlooked. Although a preoperative CT was not performed, urgent surgery was justified by pneumoperitoneum on X-ray and clinical signs of peritonitis. The lack of CT limits radiologic-pathologic correlation but not clinical decision-making. Further research is needed to better understand the pathophysiology, risk factors, and optimal management of paradoxical perforation, as existing evidence remains limited to case reports and small series.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognizing paradoxical reactions, such as intestinal perforation, as potential complications during the early phase of anti-tubercular therapy. Maintaining early vigilance and a high index of suspicion is essential for timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention. Clinicians should consider paradoxical perforation in patients who deteriorate shortly after initiating anti-TB treatment, even when adherence is confirmed. Early surgical intervention, guided by a high index of suspicion, is crucial to reducing morbidity and improving outcomes in intestinal tuberculosis.
