Finite element analysis of biomechanical investigation on diverse internal fixation techniques in oblique lumbar interbody fusion
Authors: Qi Yu, Zhuo Lin Zou, Hui Gen Lu, Xue Kang Pan, Xu Qi Hu, Zhong Hai Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo establish a three-dimensional finite element model of the lumbar spine and investigate the impact of different fixation techniques on the biomechanical characteristics of oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF).MethodsThe study aimed to establish and validate a comprehensive three-dimensional model of the lower lumbar spine (L3-S1) using the finite element method. L4-L5 was selected as the surgical segment, and four distinct OLIF surgical models were constructed: Stand-alone (SA), unilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (UCBT), bilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (BCBT), and bilateral pedicle screw (BPS). The models were underwent a pure moment of 10N·m to simulate lumbar extension, flexion, left bending, right bending, left and right rotation movements. Subsequently, the range of motion (ROM), cage stress, and fixation stress were calculated.ResultsIn the L3-L5 segment, the BCBT group showed the most limited range of motion (ROM) under exercise load, indicating superior stability within this group. The ROM and cage stress values were found to be highest in the SA group. In contrast, the cage and internal fixation stress in the BPS group were observed to be lowest (9.91 ~ 53.83MPa, 44.93 ~ 84.85 MPa). With the exception of right bending and right rotation, the UCBT group demonstrated higher levels of internal fixation stress (102.20 ~ 164.62 MPa).ConclusionsThe study found that OLIF-assisted internal fixation improved segmental stability and reduced cage stress. The BPS group had advantages over the CBT group in preventing endplate damage and reducing the risk of cage subsidence. However, BCBT group has distinct merits in maintaining surgical segment stability, distributing stress load on the spinal motor unit, and reducing the likelihood of adjacent segment degeneration (ASD). Keywords: Biomechanical study, Lumbar spine, Oblique lumbar interbody fusion, Adjacent segment degeneration, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Lumbar interbody fusion (LIF) is a classical and effective approach for treating degenerative lumbar pathologies [1]. Advancements in surgical instrumentation and techniques have given rise to the prominence of minimally invasive interventions as a prevailing trend in the field of surgery. Oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF), a minimally invasive technique for lumbar fusion, has gained wide acceptance due to its unique advantages.

The OLIF surgery uses a lateral approach to access the spinal structure through the retroperitoneal space and the anterior border of the psoas. This approach allows direct access to the affected intervertebral disc while avoiding disruption to the spinal canal and reducing potential damage to posterior muscles, ligaments, and bone structures [2, 3]. Additionally, the cage used in the OLIF technique can span across both bilateral epiphyseal rings and extend to the dense bony carinae surrounding the vertebral body on either side, increasing the support strength of the cage [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that OLIF has produced positive results in managing degenerative lumbar diseases, leading to restoration of intervertebral space height and improvement of sagittal correction [6–8].

OLIF surgery can be performed in stand-alone (SA), or with bilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (BCBT), unilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (UCBT), or bilateral pedicle screw (BPS) [9]. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive biomechanical research to determine the most suitable internal fixation method for OLIF surgery. Finite element analysis offers effective solutions to various problems and has been successfully used in spine surgery for decades. Consequently, further biomechanical analysis is needed to better understand the impact of various internal fixation methods on OLIF surgery outcomes [10].

In this study, four models incorporating different additional internal fixation methods (SA, UCBT, BCBT, BPS) were developed based on a finite element model of the normal lumbar spine (L3-S1). The biomechanical stability of OLIF was analyzed and compared under different internal fixation methods. Furthermore, it also serves as a reference for the clinical treatment of degenerative lumbar diseases.

Materials and methods

Construction of intact lumbar finite element model

A 25-year-old healthy male volunteer (height 175 cm and weight 68 kg, without lumbar disease) was selected for the study. A total of 210 computed tomography (CT) images of the lumbar were acquired and stored in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format (DICOM). Images from L3-S1 were segmented and 3D reconstructed using Mimics 21.0 (Materialize, Belgium). The optimization and surface fitting process utilized Geomagic Wrap 2017 (Geomagic Company, USA), followed by the establishment of a solid model in SolidWorks 2021. Subsequently, biomechanical analysis of the L3-S1 FE model was performed with ANSYS (ANSYS Company, USA). This model included vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and ligament system, as shown in Fig. 1. The vertebral body comprises cortical bone, cancellous bone, and superior and inferior endplate. The cortical bone had a thickness of 1 mm, while the endplate was 0.5 mm thick [11, 12]. The disc consists of 44% nucleus pulposus and 56% annulus fibrosus. In this study, the attachment points of ligaments were defined based on anatomical relationships, while springs were employed to simulate the intervertebral ligaments [13, 14]. Construction of surgical finite element model

The internal fixation device was modeled in 3D based on the actual parameters. The cage was modeled based on Oracle cage (DePuy Synthes), measures 40 mm length, 22 mm width, 11 mm anterior height, 8 mm posterior height, and 8° lordosis. The pedicle and cortical bone screws are 5.5 mm in diameter and 45 mm in length, while the rod measures 5.5 mm in diameter and 53 mm in length. The material properties are in Table 1. The targeted surgical site was the L4-L5 intervertebral space for excision of the left annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus, and cartilage endplate. The lamina, articular process, and ligamentum flavum maintained structural integrity, while the cage remained consistently immobilized in all models. Four models were created: (1) stand-alone (SA) model, (2) unilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (UCBT) model, (3) bilateral cortical bone trajectory screw (BCBT) model, and (4) bilateral pedicle screw (BPS) model. Boundary and loading conditions

The cage and vertebral body contact surfaces were assumed to have no sliding in all models. The boundary and loading conditions were as follows: The S1 lower vertebral body surface was fixed and constrained. A 500N vertical load was applied to the L3 upper surface to simulate axial loading (upright posture). A 10 N-m pure moment was applied to the L3 upper surface to simulate six physiological movements including flexion, extension, left bending, right bending, left rotation, and right rotation. The range of motion (ROM), the peak von Mises of cage, and the peak von Mises of instruments were analyzed and compared to investigate the biomechanical stability of OLIF with various instruments.

Results

Validation of the model

After applying a comparable load, we compared the ROM results with the cadaver experimental data from Yamamoto et al. [17]. This comparison revealed consistent experimental results, confirming the reliability of the finite element model (Fig. 3).

Range of motion of the surgical segment [L4–L5]

The ROM of the L4-L5 segments in each model under combined loadings of 500N and 10N-m is illustrated in Fig. 4. Compared with the complete finite element model, ROM of the four OLIF surgical models decreased to varying degrees at the L4-L5 level, suggesting that these surgical models were effective in maintaining stability at the surgical segment. Notably, the SA model exhibited superior stability to the UCBT model specifically in flexion and left rotation. However, in other motion states, the SA model demonstrated the least stability among the four models. Conversely, the UCBT model showed the smallest increase in stability during left rotation compared to the full model. The stability of UCBT model was different in other motion postures. The BPS model is more stable than the SA and UCBT models. Notably, among the L4-L5 surgical models, the BCBT model presented the smallest ROM across all six motion scenarios, indicating that the BCBT model had the best stability.

Range of motion of the adjacent segment [L3–L4]

Compared to the intact model, the SA model exhibited a slight inclination towards increased ROM during right bending of the L3-L4 segment. Furthermore, ROM at L3-L4 was observed to be reduced to varying degrees in other surgical models (Fig. 5). In comparison with the intact lumbar spine model at L3-L4, the BCBT model demonstrated decreases in ROM for extension (56.06%), flexion (59.11%),left bending (69.98%), right bending (71.71%), left rotation (60.79%) and right rotation (74.73%). The ROM of the BCBT model exhibited smaller values compared to the other OLIF surgical models in all positions. In comparison to the UCBT model, the BPS model demonstrated a lesser reduction in ROM for left and right rotation (BPS: 11.77%, 12.65%; UCBT: 21.69%, 29.39%). Amongst the BCBT, UCBT, and BPS models, the BPS model displayed minimal limitations in left and right rotation.

Stress of the cage

The cage stress of the four surgical models in different motion states is depicted in Figs. 6 and 7. The SA model consistently exhibited higher cage stress compared to the others. The BPS model exhibited the lowest cage stress across all motion states in this study (9.91–53.83 MPa). Conversely, the BCBT model showed higher cage stress in right bending and right rotation states, but lower in other motions compared to the UCBT model.

Stress on the internal fixation

The internal fixation stress values of the three surgical models in different positions are presented in Fig. 8. In all motion states, the BPS group exhibited the lowest internal fixation stress values: flexion (83.12 MPa), extension (54.66 MPa), left bending (84.85 MPa), right bending (56.44 MPa), left rotation (44.93 MPa), and right rotation (46.24 MPa). The UCBT model demonstrated the highest stress levels, ranging from 102.20 to 164.62 MPa, across all motion states. However, this was not the case for right bending and right rotation positions. In comparison to UCBT, the internal fixation stress of the BCBT model was slightly higher, measuring 95.87Mpa during right bending and 103.69Mpa during right rotation. Regarding stress distribution, Fig. 9 illustrates that the maximum forward bending force on BPS primarily affects the titanium rod. Conversely, in both UCBT and BCBT models, the highest stress is observed at the junction between the screw and vertebra.

Discussion

The present study aimed to construct a three-dimensional finite element model of the lumbar spine in order to investigate the impact of various fixation methods on biomechanical properties during OLIF surgery. The study investigated the ROM in the surgical segment, adjacent segment ROM, stress on the cage, and stress on internal fixation. The findings revealed that: the BCBT model consistently exhibited the least ROM and the highest stability at both the operated and adjacent segments across different motion conditions. In contrast, the ROM for the BPS model was intermediate, falling between the UBCT and BCBT models. Notably, the BPS model showcased the lowest levels of internal fixation and cage stress. Thus, the biomechanical properties of the BPS model surpassed those of both the BCBT and UCBT models. Previous studies have demonstrated that OLIF technology offers numerous advantages in treating lumbar degenerative diseases, including reduced operation time, decreased blood loss, alleviated postoperative pain, shortened hospitalization duration, and accelerated postoperative recovery [18–20]. However, the risk of cage subsidence, postoperative segmental instability, and fracture remains in cases requiring supplemental posterior fixation [21, 22]. Therefore, some scholars argue that different types of internal fixation play a crucial role in maintaining surgical segment stability due to their potential impact on clinical and imaging outcomes. Currently, there is no consensus regarding the optimal internal fixation method.

In lumbar fusion surgery, the fusion rate is influenced by multiple factors such as the patient’s age, bone quality, the method of intervertebral space management, and the stability of the postoperative spinal segments. This study establishes a finite element model to eliminate other interfering factors, thereby assessing the practical effect of OLIF combined with different internal fixation techniques on the stability of the surgical segments. ROM serves as an indicator for assessing spinal stability; larger ROM values suggest inadequate stability in both the fused and adjacent segments following OLIF. In this study, the L4-L5 surgical segment was examined, revealing that all four surgical models effectively limited the ROM within the fusion segment, thereby ensuring robust fixation. This finding is consistent with previous research and further confirms the reliability of these surgical models in maintaining spinal stability [23–25]. The SA model exhibited the highest ROM among all surgical models, while demonstrating the lowest capacity to maintain lumbar spine stability. Compared to the UCBT model, the BCBT model exhibited superior stability across all motion states. This difference is largely due to the enhanced stiffness of the operated segment in the BCBT model, which effectively reduces coupled motion effects. Furthermore, the BCBT model displayed superior stability at the L4-L5 surgical level across all motion states compared to the BPS model. Previous clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that the BCBT screw technique offers enhanced biomechanical stability, which aligns with our findings [26, 27]. Interestingly, a finite element study conducted by Zhang et al. [14] revealed that BPS exhibited superior postoperative segment stability in OLIF procedures. Fan et al. [28] reported that BPS exhibited superior stability in rotation and bending compared to CBT, while demonstrating poorer stability in flexion and extension. Hence, we postulate that variations in individual lumbar spine characteristics and diverse loading conditions may be the underlying factors responsible for the different results of previous finite element studies [29, 30]. In addition, it is crucial to emphasize that a moderate range of motion is essential for achieving optimal interbody fusion.

Furthermore, despite exhibiting lower surgical segment stability compared to the BCBT and BPS models, the UCBT model still demonstrated satisfactory stability. Additionally, UCBT offers potential advantages as an alternative option due to its ability to minimize damage to paraspinal muscles, reduce blood loss, and lower device costs. Insufficient stability may result in cage displacement, leading to the reoccurrence of vertebral body instability and an increased risk of pedicle screw fracture. Conversely, excessive stability can impose excessive stress on the pedicle screw rod, thereby increasing the likelihood of screw rod fracture.

The endplate serves as the primary structural component of the vertebral body and acts as the load-bearing interface. The integrity of the endplate not only plays a crucial role in the loading capacity and mechanical conduction of the vertebral body but also ensures stability of the cage. Elevated cage pressure can result in endplate damage, rupture, and even accelerate degeneration while increasing the risk of cage subsidence. The SA model exhibited the highest cage stress in all directions compared to other internal fixation models in our study, potentially increasing the risk of cage subsidence. Cheng et al. [31] reported on 48 patients who underwent OLIF-SA surgery, and found that the postoperative cage subsidence rate was significantly higher compared to the internal fixation group, thus further confirming the significant correlation between OLIF-SA and cage subsidence. Compared to the UCBT model, the BCBT model exhibited higher cage stresses in right bending and right rotation, while demonstrating lower stresses in other motion states. Further investigation is warranted to explore whether this discrepancy arises from an imbalance in internal fixation of the surgical segment due to unilateral cortical screw fixation, leading to stress redistribution. It is noteworthy that in our study, the BPS model exhibited the lowest cage stress across all motion states. This finding aligns with previous research [14]. The authors postulated two potential reasons for this observation: (1) BPS fixation employs a three-column fixation technique, which effectively distributes the majority of load from the cranial direction, ensuring spinal stability and providing sufficient rigidity to minimize pressure on the cage in all directions of motion. (2) CBT screws exhibit enhanced load-bearing capacity, thereby generating stronger mechanical reactions against surrounding vertebral bone when subjected to higher pressures. Furthermore, the delineation of cage stress division areas should be established in future studies, with the utilization of percentage forms serving as a more intuitive representation. This presents a promising avenue for subsequent research endeavors [32].

In this study, the BCBT model showed higher internal fixation stress than the UCBT model during both right bending and right rotation states. This difference may be due to stress redistribution and the uneven distribution resulting from UCBT fixation. Additionally, the internal fixation stress of the CBT model was higher than that of the BPS model in all motion states. Moreover, the stress distribution in the CBT screw is primarily concentrated at the nail tail, while for the BPS screw, it is mainly concentrated at the nail rod. The author believed that such a situation can be attributed to two different ways of nailing resulted in stress concentration degree of difference. In contrast to the pedicle screw technique, the cortical bone trajectory screw technique requires the screw to be inserted caudally to the cephalad in the sagittal plane and medially to laterally in the coronal plane, thus increasing maximal tricortical fixation. This leads to a more concentrated stress distribution on the overall motor unit in CBT. This unique characteristic of CBT has been further validated through the high biomechanical strength demonstrated in cadaveric biological experiments. According to the principle of energy conservation, energy can be transferred but will not disappear. When BCBT screws are subjected to increased stress, they have the potential to alleviate the stress burden on adjacent spinal tissues and contribute to the maintenance of spinal stability. Additionally, regardless of the type of internal fixation used, the applied pressure remains within the yield strength range (897–1034 MPa) of the titanium alloy. In conclusion, this study posit that the BCBT screw represents a promising alternative to traditional BPS.

In contrast to previous studies, this investigation employed finite element analysis to assess the impact of various internal fixation methods on the stability of surgical adjacent segments (L3-L4). All models, except for the SA model, which exhibited slightly higher ROM during right bending, demonstrated enhanced stability compared to the complete L3-L4 model. From a biomechanical standpoint, after the fusion of surgical segments, compensatory overloaded activity of adjacent segments is often necessary to facilitate motion in the lumbar spine. This can potentially lead to an increase in ROM within these neighboring segments. However, our study did not observe any increase in ROM within adjacent segments. This finding can be attributed to the fact that the surgical model utilized in this study was constructed based on an intact model and did not involve disruption of surrounding soft tissues. Moreover, the finite element analysis primarily simulates the immediate postoperative state, and the gradual nature of changes in adjacent segment ROM may not be immediately discernible during the early postoperative stages. Previous studies have demonstrated favorable therapeutic outcomes following lumbar interbody fusion with both CBT and BPS screws. Particularly, the CBT fixation group demonstrated superior results in terms of the incidence of adjacent segmental degeneration (ASD) compared to traditional pedicle screw fixation [33, 34]. Additionally, the finite element analysis results further substantiate the superior adjacent segment stability of the BCBT model in terms of flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. These findings are consistent with the outcomes reported in the aforementioned clinical trials. The utilization of CBT screws contributes to reducing interference with the articular process during surgical procedures, thereby decreasing stress on both the facet joint and intervertebral disc. This protective effect contributes to maintaining lumbar spine stability and lowers the risk of ASD development. However, the correlation between ASD and biomechanical alterations remains a subject of ongoing debate. Wai et al. [35] conducted an MRI evaluation of the lumbar spine in patients more than 20 years post-surgery. They proposed that degeneration of adjacent segments may be attributed to the natural history of intervertebral discs, rather than solely to biomechanical changes. Lawrence et al. [36] postulated that adjacent segment degeneration is a multifactorial process, with age playing a pivotal role. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct large-scale prospective experiments to further substantiate the validity of the CBT model with regards to adjacent segment stability.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the 3D finite element model in this paper is reconstructed based on CT scan data obtained from a representative human body. Due to inter-individual variations, it is challenging to fully replicate the in vivo conditions even with meticulous handling during the modeling process. While the study successfully met the validity requirements, it did not validate the application of the axial load of 500 N in its model. Furthermore, the model was not validated specifically for the L5/S1 segment. Additionally, the simulation of surgical methods employed a more idealized approach, neglecting to consider potential soft tissue injuries that may arise from each surgical mode.

Conclusion

The study revealed that OLIF-assisted internal fixation enhanced segmental stability and mitigated cage stress. Moreover, the BPS group exhibited superior efficacy in preventing endplate damage and reducing the risk of cage subsidence compared to the CBT group. However, the BCBT group demonstrated distinct advantages in maintaining surgical segment stability, distributing stress load on the spinal motor unit, and minimizing the likelihood of ASD.
