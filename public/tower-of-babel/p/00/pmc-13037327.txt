Factors associated with uptake of second year of life vaccines among children 24–35 months in Western Region One, The Gambia
Authors: Bakalilu Kijera, Sarja Jarjusey, Mary Bobb, Lamin F Manjang, Alieu Sowe, Seth Kwaku Afagbedzi, Francis Anto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA booster dose of Diphtheria-Pertussis-Tetanus (DPT4) and the second dose of Measles Containing Vaccine (MCV2) are recommended in all countries, and these vaccines are scheduled during the Second Year of Life (2YL). There are gaps in coverage between the vaccines given in infancy and the 2YL. This study assesses the caregivers’ factors associated with the uptake of 2YL vaccines and health workers’ knowledge and perception of 2YL vaccination. MethodsA cross-sectional study design was used. The study participants include 355 caregivers with children aged 24-35 months and 24 health workers who offer immunization services. A multi-stage cluster sampling strategy, based on the WHO 30-cluster sampling method, was adapted to select households. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. ResultsThe DPT and MCV2 vaccines recorded 59.5% and 54.4% coverage, respectively. About half (49.9%) of the study participants received the OPV Booster. Children of fathers who did not participate in routine immunization services were nearly twice as likely to have incomplete 2YL vaccination compared with those whose fathers participated (aOR = 1.96; 95% CI: 1.00–2.64). The analysis revealed that caregivers who lacked knowledge of the recommended MCV vaccination schedules had higher odds of incomplete 2YL vaccination (AOR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.01–4.72). Mothers whose education ended at the primary level [aOR = 0.41; CI = 0.19–0.87] and secondary level [aOR = 0.35; CI = 0.18–0.67] had lower odds of incomplete vaccination compared to those with no formal education. Among the 24 health workers interviewed, only 6 (25.0%) correctly identified the denominator used to calculate MCV2 coverage.ConclusionThe 2YL vaccine coverage is relatively low in Western Region One. Mothers' educational level, ethnicity, fathers’ participation, and caregivers' awareness of MCV schedules were significantly associated with the uptake of 2YL vaccines among children 24-35 months in WR1. The Ministry of Health should regularly support the continuous professional development of immunization staff.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26729-2.Keywords: Second Year of Life, Vaccination coverage, Uptake

Introduction

In 1974, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) to fight against six vaccine-preventable diseases: Tuberculosis, Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Yellow fever, and Measles [1]. Initially, children could complete their routine vaccination schedules within the first year of life because all the recommended antigens were typically delivered within one year unless there were delays. Increasing the number of recommended vaccines doses in national schedules such as the second dose of Measles Containing Vaccines (MCV2) administered 15–18 months, a booster Diphtheria Pertussis & Tetanus (DPT) dose given at one year after the third dose of a DPT containing vaccine, Oral polio Virus booster at 18 months, etc., necessitated the Second Year of Life (2YL) platform. The WHO encourages national immunization programs to expand immunization schedules beyond the first year of life to include additional vaccine doses aside from those in the first year of life [2].

Vaccination in the 2YL increases the immunity levels, serves as a catch-up for children who missed their vaccines in the first year of life, and serves as a platform to integrate with other health interventions [2]. Vaccination can serve as a foundation for delivering other crucial health services, such as growth monitoring and promotion, management of common illnesses, proper sanitation, and, in some places, deworming, vitamin A supplementation, micronutrient supplementation, malaria prevention and care, and HIV/AIDS care. Early interventions to promote growth and development have a greater impact than those made later in life. It also provides an opportunity to counsel parents on diet, hygiene, home care techniques, family planning, and prompt care-seeking during a scheduled visit [3].

Significant coverage gaps exist between vaccines administered in the first year of life and those administered in the second year of life; a pattern reflected in the disparity between first-dose (MCV1) and second-dose (MCV2) measles-containing vaccine coverage globally. At least 95% coverage with both doses is recommended by WHO to achieve and sustain measles elimination, yet global estimates show MCV1 (> 80%) remains higher than MCV2 coverage (~ 76%) [4]. About 15 million children received MCV1 but did not receive MCV2 in 2021, with the WHO AFRO Region accounting for the most [5]. Insufficient communication and social mobilization, occupation, household income level, parity, vaccine supply, and human resources have been reported as factors influencing the uptake of 2YL vaccines [6].

The Gambia started routine immunization in May 1979 to contribute to the morbidity and mortality reduction of vaccine-preventable diseases with 6 antigens. Currently, there are 14 antigens in The Gambia EPI vaccination schedules, as shown in Table 1 [7]. Diphtheria, Pertussis & Tetanus (DPT booster), MCV2, and OPV booster are administered in the 2YL. Following the global pattern, coverage is higher in doses administered in the first year of life than in the second year in The Gambia. This is reflected in the last demographic and health survey of the Gambia, which reported a coverage of 85% for the first dose of MCV1 and 62% for the second dose of MCV2 [8]. Thus, understanding factors associated with low uptake of vaccines administered in the second year of life (2YL) is critical for strengthening the routine immunization program.

Methods

Study design

A quantitative cross-sectional design was used. The study was conducted in WR1 between March and July 2023. We developed an interview questionnaire to collect data. The participants in the study include caregivers of children aged 24–35 months and health workers providing immunization service delivery. The WHO 30 cluster sampling method was adapted to select households.

Study area

The study was carried out in WR1 of The Gambia (Fig. 1), which in 2022 had a total population of 1,021,836 and a surviving infant population of 31,432 [9]. WR1 is the most populous of the seven regions of The Gambia, accounting for 40.4% of the national population. The region comprises three districts—Banjul, Kanifing Municipality, and Kombo North. As of 2022, regional health records indicate that routine immunization services were provided through 29 health facilities, comprising 13 public and 16 private facilities. These facilities conducted 212 static and 19 outreach vaccination sessions per month across the region. Nearly half (44%) of the targeted population in the region missed their MCV2 vaccines in 2020 [8]. It is an urban area that has an increase in rural-urban migration in recent years.

Map of the study area

Sample size determination

The MCV2 immunization coverage for The Gambia was 71% in the 2019–2020 Demographic Health Survey [8]. In this study, we used MCV2 coverage proportion because MCV2 coverage is widely used as the tracer indicator for monitoring second-year-of-life immunization platform performance [2]. With a 95% confidence interval and a margin of error of 5%, the sample size was calculated using the formula below. A non-response rate was incorporated into the sample size estimation for potential losses due to caregiver refusal to participate, or incomplete interviews, which are common in community-based cross-sectional surveys.

n= minimum required sample size

= standard normal deviation 1.96 (CI 95% or 0.95)

Thus:

n = 317

The minimum sample size for the study was 349 participants. A 10% non-response rate is added to the minimum sample size in case of missing data. Thus, 0.10*349 = 31.7 app. 32. Thus, the total sample size for the caregivers is 317 + 32=349.

Sampling method

Caregiver study

A multi-stage cluster sampling strategy was used. In stage 1, The Gambia Bureau of Statistics randomly generates 30 clusters (Enumeration Areas) from the three districts in WR1. Fifteen (15) clusters were randomly selected from Kombo North, 13 from Kanifing, and 2 from Banjul. The number of clusters per district was based on the probability proportionate to size. In stage 2, Systematic random sampling was done to select 12 households from each enumeration area. All households in the EA were listed from the maps and numbered sequentially. A sampling interval was calculated by dividing the total number of households in each EA by 12. A random starting household was selected using a random number between 1 and the interval, and subsequent households were selected by repeatedly adding the interval until 12 households were selected. Finally (stage 3), caregivers were recruited from households. If one caregiver is found in a household, the caregiver is recruited. However, if the house had more than 1 caregiver, then 1 of them was selected using simple random sampling.

Health facility study

There are 13 public and 16 private health facilities in WR1. We divided health facilities into strata (private and public), wherein, in each stratum, 50% of health facilities were randomly selected. We selected 8 health facilities from each of the two strata, and 24 health workers were interviewed. Two immunization officers (the unit head and one randomly selected) from government health facilities and unit heads from the selected private health facilities were interviewed. We assessed health workers’ knowledge and perception of the 2YL vaccines given. We obtained health facility-level information from only the heads of the public health units and observed vaccine monitoring and data management.

Inclusion criteria

The study participants include caregivers of children aged 24–35 months who were residing in the region during the study period, and health workers who provide immunization services in WR1.

Exclusion criteria

Caregivers who lived outside the region in the past year, and health workers with less than six (6) months of working experience were excluded from this study.

Dependent variable

Receive all three 2YL vaccines (OPV, DPT Booster, and MCV2).

Independent variable

The caregivers’ factors include the following variables:

Nominal (sex, marital status, occupation, religion, ethnicity, staff appraisal, MCV schedules, knowledge, and perception of immunization), Ordinal (educational level, household income, waiting time, scale time), Discrete (age, mother’s age), and Binary (SMS reminder, Infant Welfare Card, father’s participation, availability of IWC, experience abscess).

Data collection

Research assistants were students recruited from the Department of Public Health, University of The Gambia, to administer face-to-face questionnaires in communities and health facilities. Each research assistant was provided with printed maps illustrating enumeration areas, household numbers, and landmark areas, among other prominent features.

An interview questionnaire and an observational checklist were used. The interviewees’ consent was obtained, and immunization records of the vaccines received by children aged 24–35 months were taken upon completion of the interview. Caregivers whose children had not received all recommended 2YL vaccines through vaccination records or recall were asked additional questions to explore reasons for missed vaccinations.

The immunization monitoring charts and coverage analysis were observed in all the sampled health facilities. Data entry was conducted using Android-based mobile devices with questionnaires designed in Kobo Collect, and completed forms were synchronized daily to a secure server.

Data processing and analysis

Data was thoroughly checked for missing values and outliers, and participants were contacted to provide any missing information. Variables were coded and organized in Microsoft Excel, and the cleaned dataset was then exported to STATA version 17 for statistical analysis. We performed descriptive statistics of socio-demographic characteristics of caregivers with children 24–35 months (such as religion, ethnicity, occupation, age, marital status, etc.) and health workers who provide immunization services. We developed a stacked graph to present vaccine coverage. We also calculated BCG – MCV1 and MCV1 – MCV2 dropout rates and presented them in a graph.

We summarized continuous variables and categorical variables using proportions. We conducted bivariate and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent factors associated with 2YL vaccine uptake. The dependent variable was the receipt of the 2YL uptake of children aged 23–35 months. Complete 2YL vaccination is achieved when a child receives the second dose of measles and rubella vaccine, a booster dose of Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus (DPT), and a booster dose of OPV by 35 months and is classified as “0” in the regression analysis. Those who failed to take the recommended doses of 2YL vaccines were classified as “1”. Crude and Adjusted Odds Ratios (COR and AOR) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI), and p-values were reported. The variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Variables with p-values < 0.05 in the multivariable model were reported to be statistically significantly associated with the uptake of 2YL vaccines. We analyzed health workers’ awareness of 2YL vaccines and observed the data management and analysis as part of health services delivery factors in the government and private health facilities.

Quality control

The research assistants and the team leader were trained in interview questionnaires, observational checklists, and map readings. The questionnaires were pre-tested in Western Region Two and North Bank East Region to assess the quality of the questions before the training. The questionnaires were evaluated, and amendments were made to ensure an easy flow of questions. All participants were assigned a unique identifier. We employed validation rules to minimize entry errors, used geo-points to ensure the selected clusters were visited, and monitored the data collectors.

Results

We interviewed 355 caregivers who took part in the study. Overall, vaccine coverage among children varied across sociodemographic groups. Coverage was highest among Mandinka children (59.3%) and children of mothers with secondary (56.2%) or tertiary education (54.8%), while it was lowest among Fula children (32.1%) and children of mothers with no formal education (32.0%). Children of mothers engaged in petty trading (56.1%) or civil service (56.8%) had slightly higher coverage than those of unemployed mothers (45.3%). Coverage was relatively consistent across maternal age groups and marital status, and slightly higher among Muslim families (47.4%) than Christian families (38.5%), as shown in Table 2.

Caregivers cited various reasons for not receiving all the 2YL vaccines. The most common reason was social engagements or being too busy, accounting for 26.6%. Travel, both within and outside the country, and fears of adverse events following immunization (AEFI) were the next most frequent reasons, at 12.8% and 12.3%, respectively. Vaccine shortages in routine immunization accounted for 4.4%, while 3.0% of caregivers had concerns about vaccine safety. Other reasons mentioned included an industrial strike by Public Health Officers, the loss of vaccination cards (IWC), the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, pregnancy, and religious beliefs, as shown in Fig. 4.

Children of Fula ethnicity had significantly higher odds of incomplete 2YL vaccination compared with Mandinka children (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.35; 95% CI: 1.19–4.62). Lack of father’s participation in immunization service delivery was also associated with increased odds of non-uptake (AOR = 1.96; 95% CI: 1.00–2.64). Mothers with primary-level education and secondary education had lower odds of incomplete 2YL vaccination compared to those with no formal education (AOR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.18–0.88) and (AOR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.17–0.65), respectively. In addition, caregivers who lacked knowledge of the recommended MCV vaccination schedules had higher odds of incomplete 2YL vaccination (AOR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.01–4.72). Other factors, including religion, occupation, household income, receipt of SMS reminders, reported adverse events, waiting time, maternal age, and marital status, were not significantly associated with 2YL vaccine uptake after adjustment (See Table 3).

Discussion

We assessed the caregiver factors associated with the uptake of 2YL vaccines in Western Health Region One. This study’s findings showed low coverage of 2YL vaccines in the region. Educational status, ethnicity, father’s participation, and awareness of MCV schedules were significantly associated with the uptake of 2YL vaccines among children 24–35 months in WR1. We also assessed health workers’ characteristics, knowledge, and perception of 2YL vaccination. Notable inconsistencies were found in knowledge of MCV2 scheduling, particularly for children who received MCV1 late, with a considerable proportion of respondents providing incorrect or delayed schedules. The immunization coverage has been relatively low over the years, especially for 2YL vaccines [8]. This study shows that vaccine coverage decreases over time toward the last routine immunization doses. Caregivers’ recall is acceptable globally in estimating immunization coverage. Albeit it is subject to recall bias. In this study, MCV2 coverage was 54.4%, similar to what is found in Trans Nzoia County, Kenya [10]. and relatively lower than what was found in Malawi [11]. The coverage is slightly higher than the 52% administrative MCV2 coverage reported in WR1 in 2021. The region conducted numerous Periodic Intensification of Routine Immunization Activities (PIRI) in 2021 and 2022. The EPI, in collaboration with regional health directorates, has also implemented community engagement activities to improve 2YL vaccination coverage. Albeit the Gambia DHS in 2020 [8] reported higher MCV2 coverage (71%) across all regions in The Gambia.

The MCV1-MCV2 dropout rate was far higher than the WHO-recommended target of 10%. This conforms with the administrative coverage of WR1, and a study conducted in Kenya in 2022 also reported a 41.9% dropout rate [10]. The high dropout rates may reflect limited caregiver awareness of the extended immunization schedule, particularly the misconception that routine vaccination ends at the first birth day when the child appears healthy [12]. In addition, inadequate communication by health workers regarding follow-up doses and return schedules may further contribute to incomplete 2YL vaccination [13].

Social engagement/busyness, travel, fear of AEFI, laziness, forgetfulness, lack of awareness, and long waiting hours are often cited as reasons for incomplete vaccination [14–17]. The findings of this study similarly reported these reasons. Mothers take care of almost all household chores, and the health and welfare of children. Thus, it becomes difficult to maintain the status quo for regularly taking their child for routine immunization services, especially if the child is healthy. Mobility and migration among caregivers, including inter-regional and cross-border movement, may interrupt access to routine immunization services and contribute to incomplete documentation of children’s vaccination status. Several studies have shown that children of recent migrants tend to have lower immunization coverage and face challenges with continuity of care, including the cost of health record cards and loss of vaccination cards [18, 19].

In this study, 46.8% of caregivers said their child had at one point experienced an abscess, and almost three-quarters of these abscesses occurred in the left thigh (vastus lateralis). In The Gambia, the Pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Human Influenza Type B, and Hepatitis B) is given on the left vastus lateralis at 2, 3, 4 months, and 1 year after dose three. Of the 12.3% who mentioned fear of AEFI, their child could have been affected. This corroborates with Akwataghibe et al. (2019), who stated that the experience that caregivers/mothers experience excessive crying and fever due to AEFI promotes fear among caregivers. Vaccine safety is a critical factor [20] for incomplete vaccination. However, in this study, few caregivers mentioned vaccine safety; thus, the low uptake of 2YL might be due to caregivers’ attitudes and other related factors.

Ethnicity was significantly associated with low 2YL vaccine uptake.WR1 is a highly urbanized area with a considerable migrant and socio-demographically diverse population. Evidence from The Gambia suggests that urban mothers — particularly recent migrants and socially vulnerable groups — have higher patterns of immunization schedule non-completion and default, likely due to social exclusion and barriers to health service access [21]. Akwataghibe et al., (2019) reported that culture and ethnicity are associated with vaccine completion, as some women could not complete their child’s visits due to some traditional rituals or ideology [22]. Engaging community structures in targeted communities can improve acceptance and continuity of services among underserved ethnic populations.

Children whose fathers do not actively participate in immunization decisions or support vaccination services have shown lower odds of completing 2YL vaccination schedules. Studies in Sub-Saharan African settings report that fathers’ involvement in decision-making and fathers’ education are associated with higher immunization coverage and completion rates, whereas lack of fathers’ support is linked with lower uptake [23]. Fathers’ support to mothers could be financial or moral, or fathers take the child to immunization centers. In The Gambia, fathers who take their children to the clinic are prioritized to encourage them to take responsibility for their children’s immunization services. Policy strategies may include integrating fathers into antenatal services, men as immunization champions to promote vaccination, and integrating immunization messages into workplaces and religious gatherings, which have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing fathers’ support for child health services.

This study found that caregivers had limited knowledge of vaccination. A study conducted in Ethiopia, Legesse & Dechasa, (2015) reported low awareness among caregivers [15]. The limited awareness could be due to inadequate or inappropriate interpersonal communication between health workers and their clients during immunization sessions [13].

The findings in this study also show that mothers with primary, secondary, or tertiary education had reduced odds of not receiving all the 2YL vaccines compared to those with “no formal education”. Although the tertiary level was not significant after adjusting for other variables. This finding is in line with Budu et al. (2020) [24]. This could be explained by mothers knowing the immunization schedules [25]. Educated mothers can understand the immunization schedules once the due date of the next vaccination is indicated on the Infant Welfare Card. It is important to note that educated mothers have more information on social media about the importance of immunization and the risks of lower immunity.

Most government health facilities regularly analyze their routine immunization data. However, this is not a replica of private health facilities in the region. Most health officers who conduct immunization services in private health facilities are not trained as Public Health Officers, who are the custodians of immunization services in the country. The health workers’ knowledge of the 2YL vaccination coverage analysis was low. Only one-quarter correctly stated the denominator for MCV2 coverage calculation. High staff attrition and the hiring of new staff at private health facilities could be reasons for this gap.

The Missed Opportunities for Vaccination (MOV) could also contribute to incomplete 2YL vaccination. In this study, nearly half (45.83%) of the health workers wrongly screened MCV2 for a child who received MCV1 at 18 months. Similarly, 37.50% wrongly screened MCV2 for a child who did not receive MCV1 until 12 months. Ogbuanu et al., (2019) and Hanson et al., (2018) Stated that the booster doses and older children recorded a higher number of MOV [26, 27]. The late start of clinic sessions leads to overcrowding, which could cause MOV once pressure is mounted on health workers [28].

The findings show that supervision is relatively concentrated on government health facilities. This finding corroborates the study by Kwedi Nolna et al. (2018), who found that private health facilities are often concentrated in cities and the EPI program and the District Health Management Teams do not always support or supervise private health facilities [29]. This could be a result of private health facilities not under the purview of Result-Based Financing, where regional staff are obliged to conduct routine supportive supervision.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The results can be used to guide subsequent work around immunization coverage. It is among the first studies on 2YL vaccination in The Gambia. Vaccination coverage estimates in this study were derived from Infant Welfare cards and caregiver recall. The high dropout rates observed between MCV1 and MCV2 (37.9%), although exceeding national and WHO targets of 10%, may be partly overestimated due to incomplete documentation, reliance on recall, and population mobility, where children may have received subsequent doses in different health facilities without presenting infant welfare cards. In addition, due to resource constraints, the sample size for health facilities and health workers in this study was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of facility-level findings.

Conclusions

The 2YL vaccine coverage is relatively low in WR1. Mothers’ educational level, ethnicity, fathers’ participation, and caregivers’ awareness of MCV schedules are critical to 2YL vaccine uptake. In addition, health workers’ knowledge and analytical skills were low. The Ministry of Health, through the Expanded Program on Immunization, should conduct continuous professional development of immunization for health workers and increase Advocacy, Communication, and Social Mobilization activities, especially in communities with high default rates of 2YL vaccines. Interventions such as involving men in antenatal services and men as immunization champions to promote vaccination in communities are critical to 2YL vaccination.
