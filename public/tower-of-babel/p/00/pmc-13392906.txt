Coal-Derived Oil in the Upper Paleozoic Coals of the Ordos Basin: Occurrence, Origin, and Exploration Implications
Authors: Tianqi Zhou, Guoxin Li, Dan Liu, Junfeng Zhang, Qun Zhao, Ze Deng, Zhensheng Shi, Litao Ma, Fanhui Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the recent breakthroughs in deep coalbed methane exploration in the Ordos Basin, Upper Paleozoic coals are widely recognized as strongly gas-prone, whereas their oil-generating capacity has long been underestimated. However, abundant light oil has been repeatedly observed in flowback fluids from Benxi Formation coalbed methane wells in the Linxing and adjacent areas, indicating these coals can act as both effective source rocks and oil reservoirs. Integrating coal petrography, organic geochemistry, and fluid inclusion microthermometry, this study re-evaluates the condensate potential of these marine-continental transitional coals. Crucially, we demonstrate that the Benxi coals host a high-maturity, strongly self-sourced primary condensate–wet-gas system, fundamentally challenging the traditional view that they contain only severely cracked residual oil. Liquid hydrocarbons occur primarily in two modes: free oil within structural microfractures (providing the dominant migration pathways) and adsorbed oil immobilized by capillary blocking within kaolinite aggregates. More importantly, we reveal a distinctive oil-generation mechanism unique to marine-continental transitional facies. Unlike strictly continental coals dominated by higher plants, the principal oil-prone parent materials here are significantly enhanced by marine transgressions. The incorporation of transgression-derived aquatic algal-planktonic organic matter, alongside terrestrial hydrogen-rich liptinite, provides highly efficient, aliphatic-rich, and high-H/C precursors. This synergistic dual-source mechanism significantly boosts condensate generation, offering a new perspective on the hydrocarbon systems of transitional coals and broadening the exploration targets for coal-derived liquids.

Introduction

“Coal-derived oil” refers to the associated oil formed during the bituminization process of coal-forming materials dominated by humic organic matter in coal-bearing strata. − It is mainly transformed from hydrogen-rich components (liptinite and fluorescent vitrinite) of higher plant lipids during coalification and oil-gas formation. Over the past three decades, the formation mechanisms of coal-derived oil have attracted sustained attention, as they link coalification, bituminization, and hydrocarbon generation in coal-bearing strata. The theory of “coal-derived oil” originally originated from the 18th-century hypothesis that “oil may be derived from the distillation of coal”. − In the mid-19th century, the existence of liquid hydrocarbons in coal was first confirmed. In the 1970s, breakthrough progress was made in coal-derived oil, with large-scale discoveries successively made in the Mesozoic-Cenozoic coal-bearing strata of the Gippsland Basin in Australia, − the Kut Basin in Indonesia, the Scotia Basin in Canada, as well as the Turpan-Hami, , Junggar, Qaidam, and Tarim Basins in China. − These discoveries have confirmed that this type of oil and gas has great exploration potential and is of great significance for enriching the global fossil energy structure system.

In general, coal-derived oil has characteristics in five aspects:(1) Many documented cases occur in Late Jurassic–Paleogene coal measures, when coal-forming systems favored enhanced inputs of hydrogen-rich higher-plant constituents (e.g., resins and waxes), thereby increasing HI and oil potential. − (2) Oil-prone coals typically develop in eutrophic coastal to lacustrine settings (e.g., swamps, deltas), where elevated aquatic productivity promotes additional algal/sporinite input and enriches lipid- and wax-like components in peat. , (3) Coal-forming environments are often characterized by weak hydrodynamic conditions and strong reducing environments, providing excellent preservation conditions for organic matter. − (4) The parent material type of oil-generating coal is dominated by humus, , but it is mixed with hydrogen-rich subcomponents such as lipids and waxes, such as liptinite (sporinite, alginite, resinite, and wax) or hydrogen-rich vitrinite (e.g., collinite, desmocollinite). , (5) The thermal evolution is in the effective oil-generating window period. It is concentrated in the range of 0.8%Ro–1.2%Ro, with the peak oil expulsion period at Ro ≈ 0.8–1.0%. When Ro > 1.2–1.3%, it enters the stage where both condensate oil and gas coexist.

The Linxing area and its adjacent areas on the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin are rich in coal-measure oil and gas resources. − A large-scale breakthrough has been made in coalbed methane, with proven reserves exceeding 1010 × 108m3, but no commercial discovery of coal-derived oil has been made yet. , In recent exploration practices, oil seepage was discovered in the Benxi Formation coal-bearing strata of Well M172, and a thick oil film could be seen on the surface of the flowback fluid of this well. , At the same time, many scholars have found that crude oil of the Benxi Formation is associated with natural gas reservoir in the Hengshanbao area on the western margin of the Ordos Basin, the Yulin and Suide areas in the eastern part, the Yimeng Uplift Zone, and the Sulige area inside the basin. , These direct observations confirm that coal-derived oil reservoirs may developed in the Linxing area and its adjacent areas. The Ro value is concentrated in the range of 0.8–1.5%, the liptinite content in some areas can reach 10%, and the HI value is concentrated in the range of 100–300 mg/g TOC, which is close to the HI value range of Jurassic oil-generating coal in the Santanghu Basin and Yanqi Basin. ,,, Therefore, they have similar coal-accumulating environments and oil-generating backgrounds. Then, can oil-generating coal be developed in the marine-continental transitional environment? What is the reservoir performance of coal reservoirs? Can large-scale accumulation form reservoir? Solving these problems will break new fields for the exploration and development of coal-derived oil.

This study takes the Benxi Formation coal reservoirs in the Linxing area and its adjacent areas on the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin as the research object. By using technologies such as optical thin section-fluorescence observation-scanning electron microscopy (SEM) combination, gas component analysis, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis (GC-MS), and fluid inclusion analysis, it conducts the characterization of liquid hydrocarbon occurrence space, crude oil composition analysis, oil-source correlation, oil and gas charging time restoration, and coal oil-bearing property evaluation. Through identifying the type of oil and gas, determining the genesis and source of coal-derived oil, and predicting the exploration potential of coal-derived oil reservoir in the study area, this study aims to provide theoretical guidance for the exploration and development of coal-derived oil reservoir.

Geological Settings

The Ordos Basin, situated in the western part of North China, is a large cratonic basin bounded by the Yin Mountains to the north, the Lvliang Mountains to the east, the Qinling Mountains to the south, and the Liupan and Helan Mountains to the west. − The basin primarily contains Paleozoic marine-continental transitional and Mesozoic continental sedimentary sequences, hosting multiple petroleum systems. ,

The Linxing area, located on the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin, covers approximately 2600 km2, spanning the Yishan Slope and the Jinxi Fold Belt (Figure a). A Cretaceous Zijinshan alkaline complex intrudes the southeastern part of the area, accompanied by fault development. The northern part of Linxing is adjacent to the Shenmu and Fugu areas. During the Late Carboniferous to Early Permian, the Linxing area and its northern vicinity were predominantly situated in a marine-continental transitional environment, with paleo-water depth generally increasing from north to south (Figure b). , The sedimentary environment during this period evolved through three main stages: marine transgression and mixed-platform construction (corresponding to the Benxi and Taiyuan Formations), marine regression and coal-forming swamp development (corresponding to the Shanxi Formation), and inland lacustrine basin expansion with large-scale delta progradation (corresponding to the Shihezi Formation). The Benxi Formation is characterized by a barrier coast–tidal flat–lagoon depositional system, marking the onset of large-scale marine transgression in the Late Paleozoic. , The lithological assemblage of this formation exhibits significant “mixed” characteristics, dominated by medium- to coarse-grained sandstones, calcareous claystones, and sandy mudstones, interbedded with thin limestone layers. Coal seams, gray mudstones, and limestone interbeds developed in the middle-upper sections and the top, reflecting a reducing depositional environment induced by marine transgression. , The Taiyuan Formation inherited the marine-continental transitional setting of the underlying Benxi Formation and formed an extensive shallow marine depositional environment. , This stage represents the peak of marine transgression in the study area, after which a gradual regression commenced. The depositional environment of the Taiyuan Formation was more complex, reflecting frequent interactions between terrigenous input and marine conditions. Major sedimentary associations include carbonate platforms/tidal flats, barrier islands, lagoons, shallow-water deltas, and braided rivers.

By the time of Shanxi Formation deposition, large-scale marine regression caused the coastline to shift eastward and southward, drastically reducing marine influence and shifting the dominant depositional environment from marine-continental transitional to predominantly continental. − The Shanxi Formation is characterized by fluvial-deltaic systems, including meandering river delta plains, delta fronts, and lacustrine facies. , During the deposition of the Lower Shihezi Formation, the sea had completely retreated from the Ordos Basin, and the eastern margin evolved into a large inland lacustrine basin. , The depositional system was entirely controlled by continental fluvial-deltaic-lacustrine systems. The coal seams, carbonaceous mudstones, and dark mudstones developed within the Benxi, Taiyuan, and Shanxi Formations constitute the most important hydrocarbon source rock intervals in the Ordos Basin. ,

Samples and Methods

Samples

A total of 215 core samples, including mudstones, siltstones, and coals from the Benxi Formation, were collected from the Linxing area and its adjacent regions for this study. To ensure consistent lithologic classification within the coal measures, we used ash yield (Ash, wt%) and total organic carbon (TOC, wt%) as primary screening criteria. Samples with Ash > 40 wt% or TOC < 40 wt% were classified as carbonaceous mudstone, whereas samples with Ash ≤ 40 wt% and TOC ≥ 40 wt% were classified as coal. Proximate analysis (moisture, ash yield, volatile matter, and fixed carbon), together with TOC and related basic coal-quality parameters, is reported in Table S1. The sample set comprises 33 mudstone samples, 11 siltstone samples, and 170 coal samples. These samples were obtained from eight drill holes: ZJS20–1C, ZJS22–1C, HT1H, MT5, MZ13, S124, H20, and H21 (Figure ), with sampling depths concentrated between 2233 and 2799 m. Furthermore, gas composition data, along with carbon and hydrogen isotopic compositions of methane, were obtained for 91 coal samples directly at the wellsite. All siltstone, coal, and mudstone core samples were immediately sealed and protected from light to prevent the loss of light hydrocarbons.

Methods

Microscopic Characteristics of Bitumen

A subset of 33 mudstone and 170 coal samples were selected for microscopic analysis. The samples were cut perpendicular to the bedding plane, vacuum-impregnated with epoxy resin (40 °C for 24 h), and progressively polished to a high-quality mirror finish (0.05 μm), followed by argon ion beam milling for final surface cleaning. , Maceral composition was determined using a JVC microscope equipped with a 546 nm interference filter and a 50× oil-immersion objective, under plane-polarized light, cross-polarized light, and reflected light modes (Table S2). , Quantitative statistics on the percentage content of each maceral were performed to classify the maceral types. The occurrence of bitumen was further observed in transmitted light, combined with its fluorescence characteristics.

For fluorescence observation, polished sections of the aforementioned samples were prepared using 502 adhesive instead of epoxy resin. These sections were analyzed under blue-light (λ = 436 nm) using the same JVC microscope fitted with a mercury lamp source (HBO 100, operating at 50% power) and a filter set No. 09 (wavelength 450–490 nm, emission wavelength ≥ 515 nm). − Observations were conducted with a 40× oil-immersion objective to identify the types of bitumen based on their fluorescence response.

Furthermore, 68 selected samples from the set were prepared with mechanically polished and argon-ion polished surfaces. The polished surfaces were subsequently coated with a thin gold film. The types of reservoir spaces hosting bitumen were examined using an FEI Quanta field emission scanning electron microscope. −

Vitrinite Reflectance (Ro) Measurement

Twenty-eight coal samples were prepared as polished blocks, from which telocollinite particles were isolated, ensuring avoidance of fractures and mineral inclusions (Table S1). Vitrinite reflectance (Ro) measurements were performed using a MPV-SP microphotometer coupled with a universal microscope under oil immersion at 500× magnification. The system was calibrated with monochromatic light (546 nm) and a sapphire standard (Ro = 0.589%). , For each sample, a minimum of 100 vitrinite particles were measured. The resulting reflectance data were compiled into histogram, and outliers lying beyond ±2σ were excluded. The random reflectance (Rran) values were subsequently converted to maximum reflectance (Rmax) using a linear regression method, achieving an analytical precision of ±0.02%. ,,

Stable Isotope Measurements of Organic Fractions and Gases

Methane isotopes for 28 coal samples were determined in accordance with SY/T 5238–2019 (carbon) and SY/T 7313–2016 (hydrogen) on a continuous-flow Trace GC Ultra–GC Combustion III–MAT 253 (Plus) IRMS system (Thermo Fisher). Gas aliquots, stored in steel cylinders to prevent fractionation, were injected onto a PoraPLOT Q column (25 m × 0.32 mm × 10 μm; He 1 mL min–1) − with an oven ramp at 33 °C for 1 min, increased to 80 °C at 8 °C·min–1, then raised to 320 °C at 5 °C·min–1.

For δ13C­(CH4), the CH4 peak was oxidized to CO2 at 980 °C, purified in a 640 °C Cu-reduction furnace, and analyzed at 2.6 × 10–6 mbar source vacuum and 9.5 kV accelerating voltage; precision is ± 0.3 ‰, referenced to USGS-40/USGS-44 (c3). For δD(CH4), the effluent was pyrolyzed at 1420 °C to H2 + CO and measured on a parallel MAT 253 Plus, calibrated against NIST RM 8559 (δ13C = −40.3‰, δD = −178‰, Table S3).

Soluble organic matters of 11 oil-bearing siltstone samples, 23 coal samples and 5 mudstone samples were measured as chloroform-bitumen “A” by Soxhlet extraction following SY/T 5118–2005 and GB/T 19144–2017. Pulverised samples (<0.18 mm, oven-dried 40 °C, 24 h) were packed in pre-extracted chromatographic paper thimbles. 50 g of sample was Soxhlet-extracted with 300 mL anhydrous, redistilled CHCl3 at 78 ± 2 °C. Extraction was continued until the reflux liquor gave ≤grade-3 fluorescence under 365 nm UV, typically 72 h for shales and 96 h for coals. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation at 40 °C, and the residue weighed to obtain the chloroform-bitumen “A” yield. Analytical precision is better than ± 0.2 mg.

Nine coal samples and 23 mudstone samples were selected for kerogen isolation. Carbonate and silicate minerals were removed by successive treatments with hydrochloric and hydrofluoric acids, resulting in the concentration of insoluble organic matter (kerogen). The carbon isotopic composition (δ13C) of the purified kerogen from these mudstone samples, along with that from 9 coal samples, was determined using an elemental analyzer (Thermo Flash EA 1112) coupled online with an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Thermo Delta V Advantage). In addition, the carbon isotopic compositions (δ13C) of chloroform bitumen “A” extracts from 10 coal-bearing siltstone samples were also analyzed to facilitate oil–source correlation (Table S4).

Rock-Eval Pyrolysis Measurements

Rock-Eval pyrolysis was conducted on 20 coal and 24 mudstone samples using a Rock-Eval 6 instrument (Table S5). Powdered samples (−100 mesh) were heated from 300 °C (held for 3 min) to 650 °C at 25 °C min–1 under N2 to record S1 (free hydrocarbons) and S2 (kerogen pyrolysis hydrocarbon peak), with Tmax recorded during S2 generation. The hydrogen index (HI = S2/TOC × 100) and production index (PI = S1/(S1 + S2)) were derived from these data. Subsequently, the samples were oxidized in air at 650 °C to determine S3­(CO2 released during pyrolysis), enabling calculation of the oxygen index (OI = S3/TOC × 100). Residual carbon (RC) was measured by heating to 850 °C, and total organic carbon (TOC) was calculated as S1 + S2 + RC. Analytical accuracy was verified using certified reference material IFP 160000, maintaining S1 error within ±0.1 mg HC g–1. ,

Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS)

Twenty-one samples (including 11 oil-bearing siltstone samples, 3 mudstone samples, and 7 coal samples) were analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The saturated, aromatic, and diamondoid fractions were analyzed using a Thermo Trace 1310/ISQ 7000 equipped with an HP-5MS column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) and helium as the carrier gas at 1.0 mL·min–1. The injector and transfer line temperatures were set at 300 °C and 300–310 °C, respectively. For quantification and instrumental performance control, internal standards were added to each fraction prior to GC–MS analysis: squalane (for the saturated fraction), hexamethylbenzene (for the aromatic fraction), and adamantane-d16 (for the diamondoid fraction), each at 20 mg·L–1 in the final solution. Squalane is widely used as an internal standard for quantitative monitoring of n-alkanes in petroleum samples, and adamantane-d16 is commonly adopted for diamondoid quantification; hexamethylbenzene is a frequently used aromatic internal standard (Horstad et al.,1990). For saturated hydrocarbons (GB/T 18606–2017), the oven temperature was held at 60 °C for 1 min, increased to 260 °C at 6 °C·min–1, then to 320 °C at 1.5 °C·min–1 with a 20-min hold. Data acquisition included full-scan (m/z 50–550) and SIM (m/z 191, 217, 218). Aromatic compounds were analyzed using the same temperature program with full-scan and SIM (m/z 178, 192, 198, 202, 208). Adamantanes and diamondoids (SY/T 7470–2020) employed a program starting at 50 °C for 5 min, ramping to 80 °C at 15 °C·min–1, then to 230 °C at 2 °C·min–1, and finally to 320 °C at 4 °C·min–1 with a 20-min hold, using SIM for detection. Peak identification combined retention times, mass spectra, and authentic standards. Data processing and quantification with internal-standard normalization were performed using Xcalibur 4.1.

Fluid Inclusion Petrography

Fluid inclusion microthermometry was performed on 12 siltstone samples following the industry standards SY/T 6010–2011, using a Zeiss Axioskop 40A universal microscope coupled with an INSTEC Mk1000 liquid-nitrogen cooling/heating stage. − Measurements focused on phase behavior, composition of vapor bubbles, and homogenization temperatures of inclusions at room temperature. The fluid inclusions were classified into three types: gas-aqueous two-phase inclusions, pure hydrocarbon liquid inclusions, and vapor-phase inclusions. Hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions were clearly correlated with their coeval aqueous inclusions for subsequent interpretation.

Results

Petrographic and Fluorescence Evidence for Liquid Hydrocarbons

Based on integrated observations using optical microscopy, blue-light fluorescence, and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) on 206 coal samples from the Benxi Formation, four main occurrence modes of bitumen were identified: oily bitumen, asphaltene bitumen, colloidal bitumen, and carbonaceous bitumen. Among these, oily bitumen is the most abundant and constitutes the focus of this study. Under blue-light excitation, it exhibits bright sky-blue fluorescence, indicating dominance of soluble light hydrocarbon components. Oily bitumen occurs in four principal microscale settings. (1) Intercrystalline pores within kaolinite aggregates. Oily bitumen is unevenly distributed in patchy or dispersed form within disordered kaolinite aggregates (Figure a-c). It preferentially accumulates along aggregate margins and relatively wider intercrystalline gaps, whereas denser interiors show lower filling, forming a characteristic “edge-inward” distribution pattern. FE-SEM observations reveal that kaolinite occurs as granular or vermicular aggregates (1–2 μm crystal size) with abundant strip-like to honeycomb intercrystalline pores ranging mainly from 100–400 nm, locally reaching 1–2 μm (Figure c).

Intergranular pores among aggregated clay particles are partially or completely filled with oily bitumen. (a) Plane-polarized light (PPL) image; (b) blue-light fluorescence image showing bright yellow–orange fluorescence of oily bitumen; (c) SEM–EDS spectrum indicating that the clay aggregates are dominated by kaolinite. (d–f) Sample from the ZJS22 well (2303.90 m). Oily bitumen enclosing aggregated clay minerals within plant tissue-related primary pores. (d) PPL image; (e) blue-light fluorescence image; (f) SEM–EDS spectrum confirming kaolinite as the dominant clay mineral filling the plant tissue pores. (g–i) Sample from the ZJS20 well (2305.63 m). Oily bitumen developed within intracrystalline pores of ankerite and filling internal fractures of ankerite crystals. (g) PPL image; (h) blue-light fluorescence image; (i) SEM–EDS spectrum identifying ankerite. (j–l) Sample from the ZJS22 well (2303.03 m). Oily bitumen filling microfractures within kerogen with fractures penetrating both detrital mineral layers and organic matter laminae. (j) PPL image; (k) blue-light fluorescence image; (l) SEM–EDS spectrum indicating that the fracture-filling material is oily bitumen rather than mineral phases.

(2) Clay-filled plant tissue pores. In plant tissue–related primary pores, clay minerals (dominantly kaolinite, locally associated with calcite or dolomite) infill the original voids, and oily bitumen occurs as encapsulated interparticle fillings between clay crystals (Figure d-f). This mode reflects secondary mineral infilling followed by hydrocarbon charging. (3) Fractures within ferrocarbonate minerals. Ferrocalcite and ferrodolomite veins commonly develop zigzag microfractures along cleavage planes (Figure g-i, 2–20 μm wide). Oily bitumen preferentially fills these internal fractures, displaying bright blue fluorescence, homogeneous texture, and blurred boundaries, suggesting high mobility and early migration of light components. Statistical analysis indicates an average filling rate of 67.2% by oily bitumen, with an additional 15.8% filled by calcite. The dense fracture-filling texture, with only minor 2–5 μm honeycomb pores and rare nanopores, suggests limited postentrapment degassing and supports the interpretation that ferrocarbonate-related fractures acted as key migration conduits and trapping sites. (4) Microfractures in the coal matrix. This is the most significant occurrence space for oily bitumen. Bitumen-bearing fractures exhibit curved or irregular boundaries, variable thickness, and frequent transgranular cross-cutting relationships with mineral grains, indicating late-stage brittle–shear fracture development rather than early diagenetic shrinkage cracks (Figure j-l). Coordinated optical and fluorescence observations show that these fractures represent the primary migration pathways and accumulation sites of light hydrocarbons.

Geochemical Characteristics of Coal Kerogen

Vitrinite Reflectance and Maceral Composition

Vitrinite reflectance (Ro) measurements of the Benxi Formation coal samples in the study area range from 1.14% to 1.79%, with a mean value of 1.47% (Table S1). Most of the samples display Ro values between 1.3% and 1.7%, corresponding to a coal rank dominated by coking coal with minor lean coal. These samples are mainly from wells S124, MT5, ZJS22–1C, and ZJS20–1C in the Linxing area. About 17.6% of the samples show relatively low thermal maturity (Ro < 1.3%), yet remain in the medium-metamorphic stage and are classified as bituminous coal, primarily distributed in well HT1 in the northern part of the Linxing area. The remaining samples are characterized by higher maturation levels (Ro > 1.7%), reflecting an advanced metamorphic stage transitional to semianthracite, and are mainly found in well MZ13 in the Yulin area of southern Linxing.

Maceral composition analysis of 39 coal samples reveals a predominance of vitrinite, followed by inertinite, with liptinite being the least abundant component. Vitrinite content ranges from 61.4% to 90.1%, inertinite from 5.4% to 41.0%, and liptinite from 0.6% to 3.8%. In terms of vitrinite submacerals, collotelinite is the most abundant (46.6–81.6%). Structural vitrinite is generally scarce, indicating poor preservation of original plant tissue morphology within the vitrinite group. The overall low content of liptinite (including sporinite, cutinite, and resinite) suggests limited input of hydrogen-rich terrestrial waxes and resins. Inertinite content varies notably among wells, with localized samples from H21, HT1H, and MT5 showing concentrations of 30%-41%, primarily composed of semifusinite and fusinite. This implies localized oxidation, wildfire events, or enhanced postdepositional alteration during peat accumulation, leading to significant enrichment of inert carbonaceous material.

Rock-Eval Pyrolysis Results and Kerogen Type

Pyrolysis results indicate that the coal samples exhibit high TOC contents ranging from 45.65% to 79.24%, with S2 values of 43.92–154.64 mg/g and generative petroleum potential (S1 + S2) of 45.83–164.33 mg/g (Table S5). The Hydrogen Index (HI) varies from 80.96 to 238.68 mg HC/g TOC, while the Oxygen Index (OI) is low, ranging between 0.34 and 2.16 mg CO2/g TOC (Figure ). The Production Index (PI) falls within 0.04–0.08, and Tmax values are distributed between 453 °C and 495 °C. Notably, wells HT1H and H20 display hydrogen-rich characteristics, with high HI and elevated S2 values. Overall, the Benxi Formation coals are characterized by high TOC, moderate to high S2, low OI, and low PI, suggesting a predominance of Type III kerogen (Figure ). Notably, a subset of the Benxi Formation coal samples from this study stands out. These samples display relatively high HI values, ranging from 200 to 238.68 mg HC/g TOC, coupled with Tmax values between 440 and 470 °C. This places them in the early- to midmature oil window (Ro ≈ 0.8–1.0%) and within the upper boundary of the Type II2 kerogen field. This characteristic suggests that, despite the overall gas-prone nature of the regional coals, these specific intervals within the Benxi Formation possess a significant potential for generating condensate and associated gas, making them promising targets for exploration (Figure ).

For the mudstone samples, TOC contents range from 0.98% to 59.7% (mean: 21.4%), S2 values vary between 0.34 and 60.69 mg/g (mean: 22.89 mg/g), and S1+S2 mainly ranges from 0.43 to 64.88 mg/g. Similar to the coals, the Benxi Formation mudstones are dominated by Type III kerogen, with localized development of Type II2 kerogen (Figure ).

Carbon Isotopic Composition of Kerogen

Kerogen carbon-isotope data from the C2b mudstone and coal samples show coherent δ13C characteristics. The mudstone kerogen is consistently 13C-enriched, with values ranging from −23.63‰ to −23.07‰ and an average of −23.57‰. The coal kerogen exhibits a wider spread, ranging from −23.98‰ to −23.24‰, with an average of −23.67‰ (Figure ). Compared with published kerogen δ13C fields from the overlying Taiyuan and Shanxi formations, which are generally more 13C-depleted, the Benxi Formation remains isotopically heavier overall − (Figure ). The Benxi Oil data plot within the Benxi kerogen envelope, spanning −24.64‰ to −23.57‰ with an average of −24.10‰, and they show close isotopic affinity to Benxi coal. In contrast, Shihezi Oil and the Shanxi+Taiyuan Oil are substantially 13C-depleted, with average δ13C values of −25.52‰ and −25.92‰, respectively − (Figure ). This isotopic correspondence provides strong evidence that the analyzed oils were sourced predominantly from the Benxi Formation itself, rather than from younger coal-bearing units (Figure ).

Geochemical Characteristics of Coal-Derived Gas

Gas Composition

The composition of coal-derived gas in the study area is dominated by hydrocarbon gases, which range from 70.87% to 98.61% (mean: 91.22%). Methane (CH4) constitutes the majority of hydrocarbons with an average content of 87.71%, while heavier homologues such as ethane (C2H6) and propane (C3H8) are present in low concentrations, averaging 2.82% and 0.14%, respectively (Table S3). The dryness coefficient (C1/ΣC1–C4) averages 0.95, indicating a methane-rich gas composition; however, the presence of C2–C3 components and the isotopic evidence suggest a condensate-associated wet-gas system rather than a purely dry coalbed gas. Nonhydrocarbon components include minor amounts of CO2 (0.39–6.60%) and N2, the latter averaging 5.38%.

Carbon and Hydrogen Isotopic Compositions of Gas

The methane in the study area exhibits δ13C1 values ranging from −48.5‰ to −29.8‰ (mean: −41.1‰). The corresponding δD1 values range from −246‰ to −164‰ (mean: – 222.7‰ (Figure , Figure ). Here we project the methane carbon- and hydrogen-isotope data onto two classic genetic templates(i) the δ13C1 vs C1/(C2+C3) template after Bernard (1977) (Figure a) and (ii) the δ13C1 vs C1/(C2+C3) diagram of Dai et al. (1992, Figure b). The δ13C1–C1/(C2+C3) discrimination plot (Figure a) shows that all samples fall within the thermogenic gas field and cluster predominantly along the Type II–III kerogen domain, indicating that the source rocks in the Linxing area are characterized by mixed Type II–III kerogen. The relatively moderate C1/(C2+C3) ratios suggest limited dry coal-type gas generation and a minor contribution from pure coal-derived pyrolysis gas. Classification and Occurrence of Hydrocarbon-Bearing Fluid Inclusions

Fluid inclusions are widely developed within siltstone interbeds of the Benxi Formation coal measures, primarily hosted in quartz dissolution pores and microfractures (Figure ). Based on phase state, fluorescence characteristics, and Raman spectroscopy, four types of hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions are distinguished. Type I inclusions are low-maturity gas–liquid two-phase inclusions showing yellow to green fluorescence, indicative of relatively immature oil. They occur as isolated inclusions with irregular to elliptical shapes, diameters of 5.6–12.4 μm, and gas-to-liquid ratios of 0.12–0.20. Raman spectra of the gas bubbles exhibit a distinct peak at ∼1285 cm–1, indicating CO2-dominated compositions (Figure a,b). Type II inclusions are gas–liquid two-phase inclusions displaying light brown transmitted-light colors and bluish-white fluorescence, consistent with higher-maturity condensate oils. These inclusions commonly occur along healed quartz fractures in linear or clustered arrangements, suggesting significant condensate migration and charging. They are circular to elliptical, 5.2–16.3 μm in diameter, with gas-to-liquid ratios of 0.26–0.42. Raman spectra show characteristic CH4 peaks at 2909–2919 cm–1, indicating methane-dominated bubble compositions (Figure b, c). Type III inclusions are gas-phase hydrocarbon inclusions coeval with Type II inclusions. They appear dark gray in transmitted light with thick boundaries and predominantly circular morphologies. Raman analyses identify methane as the dominant component, accompanied by light hydrocarbons such as C4H10 (2959–2960 cm–1) and C7H16 (2873 cm–1), indicating comigration of condensate-range hydrocarbons with methane (Figure b, d). Type IV inclusions are bitumen-bearing inclusions, occurring sparsely as patchy bodies. They are brown in transmitted light and nonfluorescent. Raman spectra display distinct D and G bands at 1339 cm–1 and 1614 cm–1, respectively, confirming the presence of solid bitumen formed by postoil cracking of earlier petroleum (Figure b, e).

Discussion

Occurrence of Oily Bitumen

Integrated petrographic and EDS analyses reveal that liquid hydrocarbons in the Benxi Formation coals occur primarily as free and adsorbed oil (Figure ). Free oil preferentially fills late-stage structural microfractures within the coal matrix and Fe-rich carbonate minerals (Figure g–l). These microfractures crosscut host grains and serve as the dominant migration conduits during hydrocarbon-generation overpressure, exhibiting bright, uniformly distributed fluorescence indicative of high oil saturation and mobility. − Conversely, adsorbed oil is predominantly trapped within early diagenetic clustered kaolinite aggregates. These aggregates exhibit a “rim-enriched, center-depleted” oil distribution, indicating that their formation preceded hydrocarbon charging (Figure a–c). Authigenic kaolinite significantly refines pore throats and enhances water-wetness, leading to robust hydrocarbon retention via two synergistic mechanisms: physical entrapment (capillary snap-off in narrow throats) and chemical adsorption (selective binding of polar resins and asphaltenes onto Al–OH and Si–OH surface sites). ,

Notably, while structural microfractures display stable sky-blue fluorescence, the bulk coal matrix appears largely nonfluorescent. This apparent “visual absence” of oil does not equate to an oil-free matrix; rather, it stems from instrumental and physical constraints. First, optical resolution is restricted by the Abbe diffraction limit (∼200 nm), rendering the dominant nanometer-scale matrix micropores (<50 nm, comprising >80% of the total pore volume) effectively invisible under conventional fluorescence microscopy. − Second, at the current high-maturity stage (Ro 1.3–1.7%), the highly aromatized and polycondensed macromolecular framework of the coal exerts strong fluorescence quenching. − Consequently, the weak fluorescence emitted by trace hydrocarbons within nanometer-scale pores is severely masked by the electron-rich coal matrix, resulting in a signal-to-noise ratio too low for effective detection.

If the limitations of fluorescence observations are primarily controlled by optical resolution, then capillary dynamics provide a fundamentally physical explanation for the selective occurrence of oily bitumen in different pore systems. The resistance encountered by liquid hydrocarbons when entering different pore media is governed mainly by the pore-throat radius and the contact (wetting) angle (eq ): , 1 Pc=2σcos⁡θr where σ is the oil–water interfacial tension, θ is the contact angle, and r is the pore radius. θ is typically taken as 140° (cosθ = −0.766), and σ is commonly 0.485 N/m.

Calculations indicate that the lower limits of capillary pressure for liquid hydrocarbon entry into calcite internal fractures and structural fractures in the coal matrix are 0.045 and 0.066 MPa, with upper limits of 0.527 and 1.501 MPa, respectively. In comparison, the lower limits for entry into kaolinite interparticle pores and kaolinite intercrystalline pores are 0.178 and 0.649 MPa, with upper limits of 5.945 and 49.54 MPa, respectively. This demonstrates that the capillary pressure required for liquid hydrocarbons to enter microfractures is at least 1 order of magnitude lower than that for entering kaolinite pores. Under identical charging pressures, liquid hydrocarbons preferentially overcome the capillary threshold of fractures, forming high-saturation oil phases, which explains the significantly higher degree of liquid hydrocarbon filling observed in microfractures compared to kaolinite aggregates related pores.

However, the most stringent constraint arises from the coal matrix itself. The average pore diameter of the coal matrix is 0.51 nm, with these pores accounting for 93% of the total pore volume and 99.5% of the specific surface area, dominating the pore system yet not serving as effective storage space for liquid hydrocarbons. , Substituting the average pore radius (0.255 nm) into the Kelvin equation (eq ): 2 ln(PP0)=−2γVmrRT where P is the minimum pressure required for hydrocarbons to enter the pore throat, P 0 is the saturated vapor pressure of the hydrocarbons, γ is the hydrocarbon–water interfacial tension, V m is the molar volume of the hydrocarbons, r is the pore throat radius, R is the ideal gas constant, and T is the absolute formation temperature.

The calculated lower limit of capillary pressure required for liquid hydrocarbons to enter such matrix micropores is approximately 1.5 GPa. This value far exceeds the migration pressures attainable under realistic geological conditions, , indicating that matrix micropores in the coal exert a strong expulsive effect on liquid hydrocarbons. Therefore, matrix micropores in the coal essentially act as an expulsion zone rather than a storage zone for liquid hydrocarbons. During hydrocarbon generation, the large capillary-pressure contrast provides a strong thermodynamic drive that forces newly generated liquid hydrocarbons to overcome resistance, be expelled from the matrix, and migrate toward lower-potential structural microfractures and inorganic pores.

Genetic Types of Coal-Derived Oil

Based on gas composition, methane carbon–hydrogen isotopes, the composition of hydrocarbon-bearing fluid inclusion bubbles, saturated hydrocarbon GC–MS data, and diamondoids, it is concluded that the oil in the Benxi Formation coals of the Linxing area and adjacent regions is predominantly condensate oil, whereas the associated coalbed gas is mainly wet gas. The first line of evidence comes from natural gas composition and methane carbon–hydrogen isotopes. In the study area, the gas samples display a distinctive feature of markedly negative δ13C1 and δD1 values. Specifically, the C2b coalbed methane shows δ13C1 values ranging from −48.5‰ to −29.8‰ and δD1 values from −246‰ to −164‰ (Figure ). Cross-plots of δ13C1 versus C1/(C2 + C3) indicate that most coalbed methane in the Linxing area and adjacent regions is oil-associated gas or condensate-associated gas (Figure b), − with only a few data points plotting in the mixed field of coal-derived gas and oil-cracked gas (Figure b). These relationships imply that a certain amount of oil was generated and preserved contemporaneously with methane, which is consistent with the conclusion in Section that the liquid hydrocarbons stored in the coal reservoir are mainly condensate oil. Compared with the southern segments of the eastern Ordos margin, such as the Daning–Jixian, Suide–Mizhi, and Hengshan–Yulin areas, the coals in the Linxing area have Ro values mainly between 1.0% and 1.5%, indicating relatively lower maturity and positioning them within the condensate oil–gas window. ,− The correspondingly lighter methane carbon isotope signatures further suggest that reaching the condensate oil–gas window is a necessary condition for the development of oil-bearing coal in the Upper Paleozoic coal measures along the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin.

The second line of evidence comes from hydrocarbon-bearing fluid inclusions. Their vapor phases are rich in light hydrocarbons, and the inclusions commonly exhibit sky-blue fluorescence, further indicating that the liquid hydrocarbons in the coal are predominantly condensate oil (Figure c). In detrital quartz from siltstones adjacent to the Benxi Formation coal seams in the Linxing area and its surroundings, gas–liquid two-phase hydrocarbon inclusions and vapor-phase hydrocarbon inclusions are widely developed and occur in close paragenesis. This intimate coexistence indicates that methane and condensate oil belong to a single, cocharged system rather than two separate hydrocarbon charging events. In the vapor bubbles of the gas–liquid two-phase inclusions and their coexisting vapor-phase hydrocarbon inclusions, in addition to CH4, substantial amounts of light hydrocarbons such as C4H10 and C7H16 are detected (Figure b).

Molecular geochemical evidence also confirms that the retained hydrocarbons in the Benxi Formation coal reservoir are mainly condensate oil and have not undergone severe secondary cracking. The n-alkane distributions of Benxi coal samples show unimodal to bimodal saturated hydrocarbon chromatograms, with carbon-number distributions dominated by the early peak. The main peak is concentrated between nC13 and nC20, typically centered at nC14 or nC15, whereas high-carbon-number n-alkanes above nC30 are only weakly developed (Figure ). This unimodal pattern, together with CPI values close to unity, indicates that the coal-derived oil is within the main oil window (Ro = 0.7–1.0%), consistent with the regional maturity range. With increasing maturity, long-chain n-alkanes crack into shorter-chain hydrocarbons, shifting the main peak toward C14–C15 and giving rise to condensate oil that coexists with primary kerogen-derived thermal gas. −

Oil–Source Correlation

By integrating the evolution of depositional redox conditions, comparisons of carbon isotopes between oils and source-rock kerogen, and the relative contributions of aquatic organic matter versus terrigenous higher-plant organic matter, it is demonstrated that the Benxi Formation crude oil is derived mainly from its own coal-measure source rocks, rather than from the overlying Shanxi and Taiyuan Formation sources.

The genetic affinity between hydrocarbons hosted in Benxi siltstones and those generated from the Benxi coal-bearing source interval is further supported by the m/z 85 mass chromatograms of saturated hydrocarbons (Figure ). The siltstone extract adjacent to the coal seam exhibits an n-alkane distribution that closely mirrors that of the Benxi coal sample, spanning nC13 to nC30 and showing a pronounced dominance of low–medium carbon-number alkanes. In both chromatograms, the main peaks concentrate in the nC13–nC18 range, whereas high-carbon-number n-alkanes are comparatively subdued, indicating a consistent compositional bias toward lighter hydrocarbons. Moreover, the relative prominence and positioning of pristane and phytane are comparable between the coal and siltstone extracts, suggesting broadly similar redox-related and depositional imprints on the precursor organic matter. Collectively, these similarities in peak pattern, dominant carbon-number range. Given the closer match to the coal signature and the strong coal control on light n-alkane enrichment, the siltstone-hosted oils are most plausibly dominated by contributions from Benxi coals, with mudstones acting as a subordinate or complementary source component.

The three parameters Pr/Ph, Pr/nC17, and Ph/nC18 are commonly used to characterize oil sources and the redox conditions of the depositional environment. ,− Compared with oils from the Shanxi and Taiyuan formations, Benxi Formation oils are characterized by lower Pr/Ph and higher Pr/nC18 values, indicating that they were generated in a relatively less oxic, weakly oxic to weakly reducing transitional environment rather than in a strongly oxic setting (Figure a,b). This differs markedly from the typical lacustrine–terrestrial coal-measure source rocks of the Junggar, Turpan–Hami, and Tarim basins. − From the perspective of carbon isotopes, the δ13C values of the Shanxi and Taiyuan Formation coal-measure source rocks and their associated oils are overall about 1–3‰ lighter than those of the Benxi Formation oils, effectively ruling out the Shanxi and Taiyuan source rocks as the principal sources of the C2b oils (Figure ). Within the Benxi Formation, the δ13C ranges of coal and mudstone kerogen closely match those of the Benxi oils and are overall slightly heavier, consistent with the common fractionation pattern in which oils are slightly depleted in 13C relative to their parent kerogen (Figure ). ,,,

Sterane distributions provide an additional, independent line of evidence for correlating the Benxi oils with the coal-bearing source interval. The relative abundances of C27–C29 regular steranes are widely used to discriminate organic-matter inputs and to support oil–source correlations, particularly when evaluated together with redox-sensitive parameters such as Pr/Ph. On the cross-plot of C27/C29 sterane versus Pr/Ph (Figure b), the Benxi oil samples cluster closer to the Benxi coal data than to the mudstone field, and they occupy a domain indicative of comparatively more reducing conditions and mixed higher-plant and algal/phytoplankton contributions. This inference is further corroborated by the classical C27–C28–C29 sterane ternary diagram (Figure c), where the oil data points plot near the Benxi coal cluster within the mixed-source region, rather than shifting toward an end-member dominated exclusively by terrestrial plants or aquatic algae. Taken together, the concordant sterane signatures across both the bivariate and ternary frameworks indicate that the oils in the Benxi siltstones are genetically linked to the Benxi coal-bearing strata and were generated from kerogen with mixed terrestrial–aquatic organic-matter inputs under relatively reducing depositional conditions. Given the systematically closer proximity of the oil samples to the coal field in both plots, the sterane evidence supports a dominant contribution from Benxi coals, with associated mudstones likely providing a subordinate or complementary input.

The last line of evidence comes from the ternary composition of fluorene (F), dibenzofuran (DBF), and dibenzothiophene (DBT), which has been widely used for oil–source correlation and for diagnosing depositional redox conditions and water salinity because DBT is generally favored under sulfur-rich and more reducing settings, whereas DBF and F are more characteristic of relatively freshwater and less reducing conditions. In the F–DBF–DBT ternary diagram (Figure d), the C2b oils plot tightly within the same fluorene-rich domain as the C2b coals, with both data sets clustering toward the F apex and remaining far from the DBT-enriched field associated with strongly reducing, euxinic–saline environments. This close overlap indicates that the aromatic compositions of the oils are highly consistent with those of the Benxi coals and reflects broadly similar depositional and early diagenetic conditions, most plausibly a freshwater to weakly oxidizing–weakly reducing setting. By contrast, the C2b mudstones show a larger scatter, including at least one sample shifted toward higher DBF and/or DBT, implying more variable facies conditions and a less consistent match to the oils. Overall, the F–DBF–DBT evidence reinforces the conclusion that the oils accumulated in the Benxi siltstones were generated predominantly from the Benxi coal-bearing strata and represent a near-source petroleum system characterized by self-sourcing and short-distance migration into adjacent sand bodies (Figure d).

Oil-Prone Parent Materials

Analysis of condensate oil occurrence in the coal reservoir, combined with oil–source correlations, reveals that the Benxi Formation coals possess an unusually high oil-generation potential, challenging the conventional view that coal seams generally have weak oil-generating capacity. This capacity can be attributed to the unique composition of the oil-prone parent organic matter. In this study, two major types of oil-prone substrates are identified in the coals: algal-related aquatic organic matter introduced during marine transgression, and apparently low-abundance liptinite-group macerals.

The Benxi coals contain not only organic matter derived from higher terrestrial plants, but also a significant admixture of marine algae and plankton, which provides a critical hydrogen source for oil generation from coal. Two lines of evidence for the involvement of algal input are identified in this study. (1) The Pr/nC17–Ph/nC18 cross-plot indicates that the Benxi coals, mudstones, and oils were derived from organic matter contributed by both higher terrestrial plants and aquatic algae/phytoplankton. Most data plot in the mixed Type II/III field and partly trend toward the algal Type II domain, rather than clustering solely in the terrigenous Type III end-member. This pattern suggests that the source precursors were dominated by higher-plant Type III kerogen with a measurable Type II kerogen contribution from aquatic biomass­(Figure a). (2) The C27–C28–C29 regular steranes display a “V-shaped” distribution, with C27 regular sterane contents comparable to those of C29. This pattern contrasts with the “L-shaped” sterane distribution typical of algal-rich oil-prone shales (Banuwati Formation) and the inverted “L-shaped” pattern characteristic of higher-plant-derived oils from shaly coal in the Makats Formation. , Instead, it indicates a mixed contribution from terrigenous higher plants and marine aquatic organisms such as algae and plankton (Figure ). Consistent with our mixed higher-plant plus aquatic, algal source interpretation, similar terrestrial–aquatic organic-matter mixing has been documented in other marine–continental transitional Late Paleozoic coal successions, including Permian humic coals of the Sydney Coal Basin and Pennsylvanian coal–shale cyclothems of the Eastern Interior (Illinois) Basin, USA. , Together, these indicators demonstrate that, during Benxi Formation deposition, a stratified, weakly reducing, and mildly saline, high-productivity water column was developed, which was highly favorable for the proliferation and preservation of algae and bacteria. These findings collectively suggest that, under the influence of global sea-level rise triggered by the melting of the Gondwana ice sheet during the Carboniferous–Permian, − the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin experienced three major marine transgressions from east to west. These transgressions generated brackish to saline, poorly oxygenated to anoxic, reducing water columns (Figure d, Figure ). On the one hand, they enhanced the supply of marine nutrients and promoted algal blooms; on the other hand, the increased salinity and chemical stratification of the water column were favorable for algal survival and preservation. ,

The input of such aquatic algal organic matter fundamentally enhanced the oil-generating capacity of the Benxi coals. Elementally, the kerogen derived from this aquatic organic matter is characterized by relatively high H/C and low O/C ratios, with HI values commonly exceeding 200–300 mg HC/g TOC. These HI values are significantly higher than those of Type III kerogen dominated by lignin–humic material, indicating a much stronger potential for liquid hydrocarbon generation. , Structurally, algal biomass is highly enriched in neutral lipids, wax esters, and triacylglycerolshigh-energy lipid compounds that largely occur as long-chain fatty acids, isoprenoids, sterols, and terpenoids. During coal diagenesis and hydrocarbon generation, these components readily crack to form n-alkanes, isoprenoid alkanes, steranes, terpanes, and other light hydrocarbons, making them among the most efficient molecular precursors of crude oil. ,

Although liptinite-group macerals in the Benxi coals have an average content of only 3.6%, they still represent an important oil-prone parent material. Maceral observations indicate that liptinite in these coals are dominated by sporinite and cutinite (Table S1). − Fluorescence petrography provides direct microscopic evidence for the presence of hydrogen-rich, oil-prone liptinite in the Benxi coal measures. In reflected light, sporinite and resinite are readily identified within a collotelinite/collodetrinite-dominated matrix, whereas under fluorescence illumination these liptinite macerals exhibit intense yellow to orange fluorescence with clear morphologies and boundaries. The occurrence of strongly fluorescent resinite and sporinite confirms that hydrogen-rich macerals capable of generating liquid hydrocarbons are present in the Upper Paleozoic coals. In the Linxing area and adjacent regions, where thermal maturity slightly exceeds the conventional oil window, part of the original liptinite may have undergone postgenerative structural alteration and aromatization, which can weaken fluorescence and lead to an underestimation of liptinite abundance due to fluorescence quenching. − As coal rank increases, progressive polymerization and aromatization enhance π-electron delocalization and promote nonradiative relaxation pathways (i.e., fluorescence quenching), resulting in a systematic decrease in fluorescence intensity. Nevertheless, the observed bright fluorescence of resinite and sporinite demonstrates that oil-prone liptinite was indeed developed in the coal measures and likely contributed to the generation of coal-derived liquids. From the perspective of chemical composition and molecular structure, sporinite and cutinite have exceptionally high oil-generating potential. Both possess macromolecular skeletons that are strongly aliphatic-rich, hydrogen-rich, oxygen-poor, and highly resistant to degradation, all of which favor oil generation. During thermal evolution, long-chain aliphatic C–C bonds in these macerals preferentially break to form liquid hydrocarbons, while the flexible aliphatic network suppresses extensive condensation of aromatic nuclei into inert char. As a result, at comparable levels of thermal maturity, sporinite and cutinite exhibit higher oil-generation efficiency and greater liquid hydrocarbon yields than vitrinite and inertinite. ,−

Conclusions

(1)Benxi Formation coals show two oil occurrence modes: free oil mainly resides in tectonic microfractures (and fractured ferroan carbonates), whereas adsorbed oil is confined to intergranular pores of authigenic kaolinite aggregates. Capillary controls dominatemicrofractures have low entry pressures (0.045–0.066 MPa) and thus provide the main storage/migration space, while the nanoporous coal matrix (expulsion pressure ∼ 1.5 GPa) acts primarily as a generation–expulsion domain.(2)The Linxing–adjacent Benxi coal measures host a primary condensate-oil + wet-gas system, rather than severely cracked residual oil. This is supported by maturity-consistent CH4 C–H isotopes, condensate-type fluid inclusions (sky-blue fluorescence with CH4 + C4–C7), and GC–MS signatures of high-maturity condensate with low diamondoid abundances.(3)The condensate system is largely self-sourced and self-contained, sourced mainly from Benxi coals with a secondary contribution from coal-measure mudstones. Oil-prone precursors include transgression-related aquatic OM (algae/plankton) and hydrogen-rich terrestrial liptinites (sporinite/cutinite), consistent with liptinite depletion and fluorescence attenuation across Ro ≈ 0.5–1.3%.
