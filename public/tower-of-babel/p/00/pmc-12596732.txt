Recent advances in understanding and management of intravascular large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Kazuyuki Shimada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is a rare type of extranodal large B-cell lymphoma characterized by the proliferation of tumor cells within the lumina of small vessels in systemic organs. Diagnosis remains challenging due to the frequent presentation of non-specific symptoms such as fever and general malaise. Since its listing as a distinct disease entity in the 4th edition of the World Health Organization classification, awareness of IVLBCL has increased, leading to improvements in diagnostic accuracy. Nevertheless, patients with delayed or difficult diagnosis continue to be reported. Recent genetic studies have identified recurrent genetic abnormalities associated with IVLBCL, and the potential utility of liquid biopsy for diagnosis and treatment monitoring has been suggested. However, despite these advances, a gap remains between translational research and clinical application, particularly regarding how liquid biopsy can be incorporated into clinical practice. Regarding treatment, although standard chemotherapy with central-nervous-system-directed therapy has demonstrated favorable outcomes in initial management, the efficacy and safety of emerging immune cell therapies in IVLBCL remain largely unknown. This article aims to review recent advances in the understanding and management of IVLBCL, with the ultimate goal of improving clinical outcomes and advancing therapeutic management of IVLBCL.Keywords: intravascular large B-cell lymphoma, genetic alterations, central nervous system involvement, R-CHOP therapy

Introduction

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is a rare type of extranodal B-cell lymphoma characterized by the selective growth of tumor cells within the small vessels of various organs throughout the body (Fig. 1) [1, 2]. According to the 5th edition of the WHO classification [3, 4], IVLBCL is defined as ‘an aggressive extranodal B-cell lymphoma characterized by the proliferation of large neoplastic B cells virtually exclusively within the lumina of blood vessels’. This disease type was first reported in 1959 by Pfleger and Tappeiner [5]. At the time of its discovery, monoclonal antibodies were not yet available, and thus its origin from B cells was not recognized. Instead, it was thought to be a tumor originating from vascular endothelial cells. Following the development of monoclonal antibodies in the 1980s, the cell of origin of various cancers could be elucidated and IVLBCL was confirmed to be of B-cell origin [6–8]. In the 3rd edition of the WHO classification published in 2001 [9], IVLBCL was described as a subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), and since the 4th edition of WHO classification published in 2008, it has been listed as a distinct disease entity [10].

Unlike typical malignant lymphomas, IVLBCL normally lacks lymphadenopathy—a hallmark feature of malignant lymphoma—making diagnosis particularly challenging. Prior to its inclusion in the WHO classification, awareness of the disease was limited, with reports mainly from Japan, U.S., and some European countries [11–14]. However, its formal inclusion in the WHO classification has led to increased awareness, and patients have been reported worldwide. Accordingly, it has become clear that although it is a rare disease entity, IVLBCL has a certain prevalence.

Epidemiology of IVLBCL

According to cancer statistics, the estimated annual incidence of malignant lymphoma in Japan is ~37 000 per year (http://gdb.ganjoho.jp/), ranking 9th among cancers in males and 7th in females. Although there are no statistics specifically for IVLBCL, the Blood Disease Registry managed by the Japanese Society of Hematology indicates that the number of patients diagnosed with IVLBCL had been gradually increasing in the past, but that recently, the number of registered patients has remained at ~200–250 annually. The incidence ratio of IVLBCL to DLBCL has been gradually increasing year by year, which implies that the number of patients accurately diagnosed with IVLBCL has increased (Fig. 2). Considering that ~21 000 patients with malignant lymphoma are registered in the Blood Disease Registry each year, it is estimated that around 400–500 patients develop IVLBCL annually in Japan, constituting ~1% of all malignant lymphomas. Accurate epidemiological data are largely unavailable for other countries. An evaluation using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results registry data published in 2017 indicated that age-adjusted incidence of IVLBCL in the U.S. is rare, with an occurrence rate of 0.095 cases per 1 million. However, the incidence of IVLBCL has increased remarkably compared with nodal DLBCL. The annual percentage change between 2000 and 2013 of 9.84% for IVLBCL versus 0.39% for DLBCL is probably due to improved awareness of the disease based on the listing in the WHO classification [15]. Despite its rarity, the reported incidence of IVLBCL has been increasing, likely due to advances in diagnostic procedures and improved recognition of the disease [16].

Clinical manifestations of IVLBCL

Common clinical manifestations at the onset of IVLBCL include fever, malaise, neurological symptoms, and skin rashes [16–21]. These features and their incidences are summarized in Table 1. Fever is the most frequently observed symptom, followed by various nonspecific symptoms such as malaise. These symptoms can occur in nonmalignant diseases such as collagen disease and infectious disease, leading to difficulty in diagnosis. In addition, there is a geographical difference in observed symptoms between Asian countries and European countries [11, 17, 22]. The current WHO classification describes three subtypes of IVLBCL: classic subtype, cutaneous subtype, and hemophagocytic subtype [3, 4]. Neurological symptoms and restriction of the disease to the skin, (i.e. cutaneous subtype) are more frequently present in patients in European countries than in Japan. In contrast, hemophagocytic subtype (which presents with symptoms related to hemophagocytic syndrome, hepatosplenomegaly, and thrombocytopenia with rapid onset and clinical course) is considered more prevalent in Asian countries; however, a recent report from Europe described a substantial proportion of patients with macrophage activation syndrome, suggesting that this subtype is not restricted to a specific geographic region [16]. Due to the absence of lymphadenopathy, which is a common diagnostic clue in other types of malignant lymphoma, the presence of IVLBCL is often not suspected until the appearance of nonspecific symptoms such as fever, body weight loss, and night sweats, collectively termed ‘B symptoms’. Notably, IVLBCL is a unique subtype of malignant lymphoma in which the disease is typically suspected only after it has reached an advanced stage. In typical DLBCL, B symptoms are present at diagnosis in ~20%–35% of patients, as reported in clinical trials [23, 24]. In many of these patients, the disease is initially suspected due to the presence of lymphadenopathy and/or mass lesions, allowing for diagnosis prior to the onset of B symptoms, particularly when the tumor burden is still relatively low. In contrast, most patients with IVLBCL are diagnosed only after substantial disease progression and often present with high tumor burden at the time of diagnosis. Although large B-cell lymphomas typically exhibit progression over several months, a marked elevation in serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) indicates impending clinical deterioration and a limited window before the onset of life-threatening conditions. IVLBCL is frequently accompanied by significant elevation of LDH at the time of initial presentation, and a short interval between diagnosis and the commencement of initial treatment is often inevitable. In my clinical experience, the interval between initial presentation and the initiation of treatment in IVLBCL is typically limited to ~2 weeks to 2 months.

Pathogenesis of IVLBCL

A hallmark of IVLBCL is the selective growth of tumor cells within the small vessels; however, the underlying mechanisms regarding intravascular proliferation of tumor cells remain unclear. In the 2000s, studies investigating cell adhesion molecules revealed deficiency in certain integrins such as CD29 (beta-1 integrin), CD54 (intercellular adhesion molecule [ICAM]-1), and CD11a (lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 [LFA-1]) [25, 26], but subsequent validation has been insufficient. Generally, examination of tumor samples via biopsy specimens is employed to elucidate disease mechanisms. In IVLBCL, however, the intravascular localization of tumor cells in the small vessels means that there is a low density of tumor cells in biopsy specimens, which makes it difficult to obtain sufficient tumor cells. This is a major obstacle for elucidating the biological nature of the disease. The development of severely immune-deficient mice such as NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Sug/ShiJic (NOG) mice and NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice has enabled successful engraftment of various type of tumors, leading to the development of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [27–29]. We found that bone marrow samples from IVLBCL patients could engraft into NOG mice even when the proportion of tumor cells in the graft was low compared with that in DLBCL samples [30, 31]. As shown in Fig. 3, despite the low tumor cell fraction in the graft, successful engraftment of tumor cells was achieved in approximately half of the samples, allowing for in vivo expansion of tumor cells in the PDX models [32]. Immunoglobulin heavy chain clonality analysis confirmed that an identical clone of tumor cells in bone marrow samples from IVLBCL patients was detected in successfully engrafted NOG mice. Subsequent gene expression and genomic copy number analyses of PDX tumor cells revealed that most IVLBCL tumors exhibited genomic copy number alterations similar to those reported in activated B-cell (ABC) type DLBCL and classified as ABC type by gene expression profiling, consistent with prior immunohistochemistry (IHC) findings of non-germinal center B-cell type origin [31, 33]. Furthermore, a substantial concentration of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was detected in plasma at diagnosis, significantly elevated compared to healthy individuals and even higher than that in DLBCL [34]. CfDNA in patients with IVLBCL allowed us to perform comprehensive mutation analyses such as targeted and whole exome sequencing (WES). Our WES analysis using cfDNA revealed high frequencies of MYD88 (57%) and CD79B (67%) mutations, with 17 of 21 (81%) patients classified as the MCD type. Notably, nearly all patients exhibited CDKN2A deletion (~86%), and frequent mutations were observed in genes associated with somatic hypermutation such as PIM1 (95%) and IGLL5 (90%). Also detected were other driver mutations, IRF4 (38%), ITPKB (14%), NFKBIE (14%), and TNFAIP3 (24%); genes associated with B-cell receptor/NF-κB signaling, PRDM1 (43%) and TOX (33%); genes related to B-cell development, SETD1B (57%), KMT2D (24%), and EP300 (14%); and genes related to histone/chromatin modification factors [34]. This mutation profile closely resembles those of primary central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma and primary testicular lymphoma, which arise in immune-privileged sites and are characterized by a high frequency of MCD-type genetic alterations. Other groups have also reported genetic analysis of IVLBCL using circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples [35–37]. A study from China using targeted sequencing of 34 patients with IVLBCL revealed high frequencies in MYD88, CD79B, CDKN2A, and CDKN2B; and found that mutations in BCL6 and CD58 were associated with secondary CNS involvement [38].

The shared genetic features between IVLBCL and lymphomas arising in immune-privileged sites (CNS and testis), along with the intravascular proliferation of tumor cells in IVLBCL, prompts us to consider that tumor cells may escape immune surveillance. Frequent 9p24 amplification and PD-L1 expression have been reported in Hodgkin lymphoma and primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma [39]. Similarly, in adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, the 3’ UTR disruptions of CD274 lead to PD-L1 overexpression contributing to tumorigenesis [40]. These findings suggest that immune evasion plays a role in lymphomagenesis. In terms of IVLBCL, high frequency of PD-L1 expression has been demonstrated by IHC, and our WES using cfDNA revealed the structural variant of CD274 and PDCD1LG2 in eight out of 21 patients, indicating that genomic alterations of these genes contributed to this finding [34, 41–43]. Although there is no known association between PD-L1 expression of IVLBCL tumor cells and intravascular proliferation, the potential involvement of PD-L1 in the association between immune evasion and the biological nature of IVLBCL is intriguing. Moreover, this finding raises the possibility of PD-L1 as a potential therapeutic target for IVLBCL.

Given the gene expression profile of ABC type and the high frequency of MYD88 mutations in IVLBCL, it is hypothesized that BTK inhibitors may be effective. We thus conducted preclinical studies to evaluate the efficacy of BTK inhibitor using IVLBCL PDX mouse models. The ex vivo culturing of tumor cells from IVLBCL patients has been difficult, which has hindered ex vivo investigation of IVLBCL. We first developed an ex vivo culture system based on spheroid culture techniques, using culture medium supplemented with human serum, cytokines, reductant, and oligonucleotides, which enabled us to culture IVLBCL PDX tumor cells ex vivo for over one week. Drug screening of 49 compounds for susceptibility in five IVLBCL PDX tumor cells revealed that ibrutinib, a BTK inhibitor, showed activity. Further evaluation demonstrated that ibrutinib exhibited comparable efficacy to CHOP-like chemotherapy in in vivo experiments, supporting its therapeutic potential for IVLBCL [44]. We are also investigating the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors using PDX models. Although the underlying mechanism of intravascular proliferation of tumor cells remains unresolved, emerging spatial transcriptomics approaches may deepen our understanding of IVLBCL in the near future.

Diagnosis of IVLBCL

A definitive diagnosis of IVLBCL is established by identification of tumor cells within small vessels in biopsy specimens. As large atypical cells within vessels are a highly specific finding for IVLBCL, when IVLBCL is clinically suspected, the diagnosis is not difficult if CD20-positive large atypical cells are lodged in the lumina of vessels. Recently, it has been clarified that positive findings can be obtained from either random skin biopsy and/or bone marrow aspiration/biopsy [45–47]. If an appropriate biopsy is performed and tumor cells are abundantly observed in the vessels, accurate diagnosis is not challenging. Random skin biopsy has been widely performed in clinical practice since reports of its utility in the 2000s [48, 49]. As tumor cells are frequently observed within subcutaneous adipose tissue, Matsue et al. recommend excisional biopsies from multiple sites such as trunk and inner thigh for better diagnostic yield, despite the invasiveness of this approach [46]. However, diagnosis can remain challenging in certain patients even when biopsy is performed, and often arises when punch biopsy is employed due to concerns regarding complications and/or invasiveness due to low platelet count or blood abnormal coagulation. In such cases, insufficient sampling of subcutaneous adipose tissue—where tumor cells are more likely to be present—may lead to false-negative results. Similarly, diagnostic sensitivity may be reduced when tumor burden in the bone marrow is low. In bone marrow specimens, tumor cells are typically seen in clusters along the sinusoids. However, such characteristic findings are not observed consistently; in some cases, only a small number of large CD20-positive cells may be observed, rendering the diagnosis of IVLBCL difficult based on morphological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Estimation of pre-test probability prior to random skin biopsy or bone marrow aspiration/biopsy is thus crucial. Matsue et al. investigated the final diagnosis of patients with suspected IVLBCL who underwent random skin biopsy at their institution. Among 111 patients who underwent random skin biopsies, 33 patients were finally diagnosed with IVLBCL [50]. Based on these data, the pre-test probability of IVLBCL at the time of random skin biopsy is estimated to be 30%. Although this represents a relatively high proportion, it also underscores the diagnostic difficulty of IVLBCL, particularly for patients in whom only a small number of CD20-positive cells are detected.

Efforts have been made to leverage the abundant cfDNA in patients with IVLBCL at diagnosis. Okamoto et al. analyzed MYD88 and CD79B mutations in the plasma of patients with fever of unknown origin in whom IVLBCL was included in the differential diagnosis. As expected, they reported that no patients harboring MYD88 and/or CD79B mutations were diagnosed with diseases other than IVLBCL (https://doi.org/10.1182/blood-2022-157438). Chen et al. analyzed 34 IVLBCL patients and found that the concentration of ctDNA was significantly higher than that observed in DLBCL, and was positively correlated with LDH and ferritin levels [38]. Furthermore, a recent report has demonstrated that droplet digital PCR analysis of cerebrospinal fluid can contribute to the diagnosis of IVLBCL limited to the CNS [51]. These findings suggest that detection of cfDNA in peripheral blood and other body fluids may serve as a useful clue for diagnosis of IVLBCL. Although a definitive diagnosis of IVLBCL cannot currently be made based on genetic abnormalities, such findings can contribute to increasing the pre-test probability and thereby support an accurate and timely diagnosis.

In addition, previous reports have indicated that biopsy of sites that demonstrate fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on FDG-PET/CT can be useful for diagnosis [52–54]. A recent report from China displayed that 42 of 50 patients with IVLBCL underwent FDG-PET/CT, of whom 31 exhibited positive FDG uptake. The most frequently involved sites were lung (11 patients), lymph node (10 patients), spleen (nine patients), and adrenal gland (eight patients) [20]. Other studies have reported that patients who had pulmonary FDG uptake underwent transbronchial lung biopsy, further supporting the diagnostic utility of FDG-guided biopsies reported in previous studies [55, 56].

Treatment of IVLBCL

As previously described, IVLBCL is often not diagnosed until the disease has progressed significantly, typically presenting with B symptoms. Consequently, delays in initiating therapy directly contribute to further disease progression, increased difficulty in treatment, and poor clinical outcomes. Historically, treatment outcomes of IVLBCL have been unsatisfactory, largely due to delayed and inaccurate diagnoses [33, 57]. Since its inclusion in the WHO classification, awareness of the disease has improved, and the number of reported cases has increased worldwide. Nevertheless, clinical outcomes of IVLBCL remain poor, suggesting that many patients have advanced disease with impaired performance status (PS) at diagnosis [21]. In our retrospective analysis, e.g. 59% of patients had a PS greater than 2 [18]. Given that deteriorating PS is likely attributable to disease progression, it is presumed that delayed diagnosis leading to poor PS contributes significantly to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Indeed, recent data regarding the outcomes of patients with a PS greater than 1 remain limited [58].

Retrospective analyses have demonstrated that the application of rituximab-containing chemotherapies has improved clinical outcomes [18, 59, 60], but secondary CNS involvement leads to poor clinical outcomes [61]. Therefore, in the light of the high risk of secondary CNS involvement, a phase 2 study (PRIMEUR-IVL) incorporating CNS-directed therapy with standard chemoimmunotherapy was conducted [19]. This study enrolled IVLBCL patients without apparent CNS involvement at diagnosis, thus excluding one of the major causes of poor PS. As selection bias is inherent in clinical trial settings, and patients with PS 4 were excluded, the proportion of patients with PS 3 in the study was lower than that observed in the retrospective cohort [62]. The results of the study were good, with 2-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 76%, 2-year OS of 92%, and a 2-year cumulative incidence of secondary CNS involvement of 3% [19]. Notably, the incidence of secondary CNS involvement in the study was lower than that observed in the retrospective analysis at the primary analysis. The result of the final analysis revealed 5-year PFS of 68% and 5-year OS of 78%, with no relapse events occurring after the primary analysis (Fig. 4A, 4B, and 4C) [63]. These findings suggest that late relapses beyond 2 years are rare. Importantly, no secondary CNS involvement events were observed after the primary analysis, resulting in a 5-year cumulative incidence of CNS involvement of 3%. Taken together, these results support the incorporation of CNS-directed therapy into standard chemoimmunotherapy as an active treatment for IVLBCL patients without apparent CNS involvement at diagnosis.

In contrast, there is no established standard of care for patients with apparent CNS involvement at diagnosis. High-dose methotrexate-based regimens are often employed; however, available data are still limited to individual case reports, and treatment outcomes have not been comprehensively evaluated [64, 65]. Robust clinical data are required to study optimal therapeutic strategies for these patients. Recent advances have demonstrated the efficacy of BTK inhibitors in primary CNS lymphomas [66–68]. As shown in our preclinical study, considering the biological background, BTK inhibitors are expected to be effective in IVLBCL. While a limited number of case reports have described the use of a BTK inhibitor combined with chemoimmunotherapy (https://doi.org/10.1182/blood-2021-152417), data should be evaluated in the clinical trial setting.

Regarding high-dose therapy with autologous stem cell transplantation (HDT with ASCT) in first remission, a randomized trial evaluating the role of upfront HDT with ASCT in previously untreated aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma—predominantly DLBCL—with high or high-intermediate IPI demonstrated a significant improvement in PFS in the HDT with ASCT group, although OS did not differ between the groups. However, in a subgroup analysis of patients with high-risk IPI, both PFS and OS were significantly superior in those who received HDT with ASCT, suggesting that upfront HDT with ASCT is beneficial for patients with high-risk IPI [69]. Given that most patients with IVLBCL are classified as high-risk IPI [18], upfront HDT with ASCT may also be advantageous in this population. Indeed, registry data from Europe and Japan have shown favorable outcomes [70, 71]. Younger patients and those with central nervous system (CNS) involvement at diagnosis may be appropriate candidates for upfront HDT with ASCT, although the majority of patients in Japan are elderly and therefore not suitable for this approach. In addition, polatuzumab vedotin—expected to be effective in DLBCL of ABC type origin [72–74], to which most IVLBCL cases belong—has been introduced into clinical practice for IVLBCL. It would be thus challenging to evaluate the utility of HDT with ASCT in IVLBCL patients within clinical trial settings.

For relapsed or refractory DLBCL, immune cell therapy including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy and bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) therapy have significantly improved outcomes and changed treatment paradigms [75–78]. However, data on the use of these immune cell therapies in IVLBCL are extremely limited, and their safety and efficacy are largely unknown. In particular, given the intravascular localization of tumor cells in IVLBCL, the risks of cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome in relation to CAR-T cells and BsAbs remain unknown. Further investigation is needed to determine the feasibility and safety of these modalities in this peculiar disease setting. Although we have experience administering CAR-T therapy in relapsed IVLBCL patients, we have encountered no patients with active intravascular tumor burden at the time of infusion. Accumulation and sharing of experiences are essential to establish the role of immune cell therapy in IVLBCL.

Unresolved issues

Although recent advances such as comprehensive genomic analyses from liquid biopsy have provided biological insights into the cell of origin and genetic alterations in IVLBCL, the fundamental mechanism underlying the selective growth of tumor cells in the lumina of vessels remains unknown. Whether these unresolved issues can be elucidated through emerging technologies such as spatial transcriptomics is quite intriguing. From the clinical aspect, there remains unmet medical need for development of optimal treatment strategies for IVLBCL with CNS involvement and for the establishment of effective therapies for relapsed disease.

Conclusion

In this review, I have summarized recent advances in the understanding and management of IVLBCL. Although an increasing number of studies have been published, many reports still indicate poor clinical outcomes, underscoring the critical importance of accurate and timely diagnosis. Enhancing disease awareness and accumulating diagnostic experience are essential. It is imperative for clinicians to recognize the existence of rare disease entities such as IVLBCL and to maintain vigilance in daily practice to ensure appropriate management of IVLBCL.
