Aptamer-functionalized exosomes combined with doxorubicin suppress GBM progression and enhance chemoradiosensitivity by promoting pyroptosis
Authors: Jing Wang, Li Peng, Xinxin Zhong, Siqi Zheng, Xiuhua Liang, Menghan Du, Xiangning Xia, Ting Guo, Lidan Zheng, Yingying Fang, Yinghong Tian, Yusheng Shi, Xingmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is the highly lethal intracranial tumor characterized by low survival rates and high recurrence, partly attributable to the challenges posed by the blood-brain barrier (BBB). To enhance therapeutic efficacy, the Exo-U2-Dox complex was engineered by functionalizing mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes with the GBM-targeting aptamer U2 and integrating them with doxorubicin (DOX). This complex is designed to augment the sensitivity of GBM to chemo-radiotherapy. Here, it is found that Exo-U2 effectively accumulates in GBM-bearing mice, thereby inhibiting tumor progression. When administered in conjunction with DOX and radiation, Exo-U2-Dox increases DNA damage in GBM cells, and diminishes invasiveness. Mechanistically, Exo-U2 targets and inhibits the autophosphorylation of Epidermal growth factor receptor variant Ⅲ (EGFRvⅢ) in GBM cells, thereby activating the NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis pathway, which leads to increased expression of Gasdermin D (GSDMD) and Cysteine-aspartic acid protease-1 (caspase-1), ultimately suppressing GBM cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Furthermore, the combination of Exo-U2 with X-ray treatment inhibits the expression of p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1), reduces phosphorylation of the Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated/Checkpoint kinase 2 (ATM/Chk2) pathway, resulting in the accumulation of DNA damage. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of aptamer-functionalized exosomes in conjunction with DOX as a promising strategy for GBM treatment. This approach not only broadens the therapeutic applications of DOX but also provides a novel direction for targeted GBM therapies.Keywords: Aptamer, Exosomes, Pyroptosis, Glioblastoma, Doxorubincin, Chemo-radiosensitivity

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most prevalent and aggressive primary brain tumor in adults and is characterized by high mortality and low survival rates [1]. Despite standard treatments such as surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy (e.g., temozolomide, TMZ), patient survival rarely exceeds 15 months [2,3]. The complexity and heterogeneity of GBM limit treatment efficacy, particularly after recurrence, with existing therapies often proving inadequate. Clinically, GBM presents challenges such as radiation resistance, poor drug targeting, and difficulty in crossing the BBB [4]. Tumor stem cell-like populations within GBM contribute to treatment evasion, promoting recurrence and resistance [5,6].

In the context of first-line clinical treatment, radiation therapy is a crucial standard intervention [7,8]. However, the adaptive response of tumor cells to radiation often results in recurrence in many patients post-treatment. In the case of GBM, effective drug therapy necessitates penetration of the BBB, which poses a significant challenge [9]. The BBB restricts the penetration of active drug compounds, thereby hindering the effective delivery of many therapeutic agents to the lesion site. Consequently, the efficacy of existing chemotherapeutic agents, such as TMZ, is compromised because the BBB impedes drug delivery, thereby limiting their therapeutic impact [10]. Enhancing the treatment sensitivity of GBM in clinical settings remains an urgent issue requiring resolution. In response to these clinical challenges, researchers are actively investigating novel strategies to overcome both the radiation resistance and drug delivery barriers associated with GBM. For example, innovative drug delivery systems developed through nanotechnology, such as liposomes and functionalized nanoparticles, have demonstrated an enhanced capacity to penetrate the BBB and improve the targeting of tumor cells [11,12]. Furthermore, the methodologies for drug delivery are continually being refined to increase the efficiency of BBB penetration. By employing novel nanocarrier technologies, the ability of drugs to traverse the BBB can be augmented, thereby achieving more effective targeted therapies. Among these, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have garnered significant attention as emerging nanocarriers because of their superior biocompatibility and ability to cross the BBB [13,14]. Research indicates that loading anticancer drugs into exosomes can markedly increase drug concentrations at tumor sites while minimizing adverse effects on healthy tissues [15]. Additionally, aptamers, often referred to as "chemical antibodies," have emerged as promising bio-molecular tools with significant potential to address the challenge of inadequate drug targeting [16]. For example, research has demonstrated that the conjugation of aptamers with anticancer drugs facilitates more efficient targeted delivery, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing toxic side effects on healthy tissues [17]. Moreover, aptamers have potential applications in imaging and diagnostics, supporting the advancement of personalized medicine [18].

Our research group previously identified and validated that aptamer U2 could inhibit the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GBM cells, as well as promote apoptosis [19]; and effectively deliver therapeutic agents in conjunction with gold nanoparticles, thereby increasing the sensitivity of GBM cells to radiation therapy [20]. To further increase the targeting precision and biocompatibility of drug delivery systems, our group covalently linked EVs with the aptamer U2 and investigated the effects and molecular mechanisms of this extracellular vesicle‒aptamer complex on GBM. By integrating DOX and radiation therapy, we explored the therapeutic efficacy of the extracellular vesicle-aptamer-Dox complex on GBM, offering a novel treatment strategy aimed at improving the survival rates of patients with GBM.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animals

Human U87EGFRvⅢ cell lines were generously supplied by Dr. Webster K. Cavenee (Ludwig Cancer Institute, San Diego, CA) and have been previously described [21]. The culture medium required for U87EGFRvⅢ cells is DMEM (Corning, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Waltham, USA) and 200 mg/mL G418 (Sigma, MO, USA). Primary compact bone-derived mouse MSCs were obtained from C57BL/6J neonatal mice. (5-6 days old). The MSCs were maintained in MEM (Corning, NY, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco, Australia, MA) supplementation. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a mixture of 95% air and 5% CO2.

C57BL/6J neonatal mice (5-6 days old) were purchased from the “Southern Medical University Laboratory Animal Center”. Male BALB/c nude mice (4-6 weeks old) were purchased from the “Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center” to construct GBM models.

Bone marrow MSC isolation and cultivation

Bone marrow MSCs were obtained using a method have been previously described [22]. Total MSCs were harvested from the femur and tibia in a sterile condition. Next, the isolated cells were plated in 25 cm2 Corning culture flasks and maintained in complete culture media containing 10% FBS (Gibco), penicillin (100 IU/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (Gibco). After 72 h, the media were replaced to remove non-adherent cells. Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, the adherent cells were subcultured for further expansion. Second-passage cells were harvested and assessed by flow cytometry to determine the MSC immunophenotype [14].

MSC-derived exosomes collection and characterization

Exosomes were isolated from the conditioned media of stem cells using the exoEasy Maxi Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). When the third-passage cells reached approximately 70% confluency, the media were removed, and followed by three washes of the cells with PBS. Subsequently, the media were replaced with exosome-depleted serum-containing complete media. Following 48 h, the media were harvested and spun at 300 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was moved to a fresh tube and spun at 2000 g for 20 min at the same temperature, followed by a further spin at 10,000 g half an hour at the same temperature. A 0.22-μm membrane filter (Millipore, USA) was used to filter the final supernatant, which was subsequently transferred into a 10-kDa molecular weight cut-off ultrafiltration device. The device was spun at 3200 g for 20 min at 15 °C. Finally, exosomes were extracted using the QIAGEN exoEasy Maxi Kit according to the operating instruction, then harvested and preserved at −80 °C.

A portion of exosomes in PBS was used for bovine serum albumin (BSA) quantification, western blotting, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Size distribution was determined with a ZetaVIEW instrument (Particle Metrix, Germany). For analysis, 2 μL of sample was diluted in 500 μL PBS, vortexed, and briefly sonicated to ensure resuspension before loading into the ZetaVIEW chamber. Exosome presence was confirmed by western blotting for CD63, CD81, and TSG101, with calreticulin serving as a negative control protein [14].

Preparation of the aptamer-exosome complex (Exo-U2)

Firstly, the 5′ end of aptamer U2 (Sangong Biotech, China) was modified with cholesterol and then resuspended in double-distilled water (ddH2O) to achieve a concentration of 100 μM. Subsequently, 50 μg of exosomes or 2 nmol of Chol-U2 was introduced into 100 μL of PBS. The mixed solution was agitated at an ambient temperature for 5 min. Unbound Chol-U2 was removed and concentrated using a 500-μL 100-kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore). The solution concentration was then measured using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

The conjugation efficiency of aptamers to exosomes was determined by subtracting the quantity of free aptamers (measured in the supernatant using Nanodrop) from the total aptamers added initially. The result was divided by the total input and expressed as a percentage: Conjugation Efficiency (%) = [(Total Input − Free in Supernatant)/Total Input] × 100.

Preparation of the Dox-exosome complex

DOX and exosomes were mixed in PBS such that both reached a final concentration of 400 μg/mL. The mixture underwent ultrasonication using a Hielscher ultrasonic homogenizer (Germany). The sonication parameters were configured as follows: 20% amplitude, with a cycle of 4 s pulse-on, followed by 2 s pulse-off, repeated for a total of six cycles. The samples were cooled on ice for 2 min after each cycle. The mixture underwent incubation at 37 °C for 1 h to facilitate the reassembly of the exosomal membrane. Subsequently, the drug-loaded exosomes were isolated by centrifugation at 120,000 g for 70 min at 4 °C. Finally, the pellet was washed with pre-chilled PBS to eliminate unencapsulated DOX [23].

Determination of Encapsulation Efficiency (EE%): The supernatant collected after centrifugation was used to quantify the amount of free doxorubicin (DOX) by measuring its absorbance at 480 nm using a UV-Vis spectrometer, with reference to a standard calibration curve of DOX. The encapsulation efficiency was calculated indirectly using the following equation: EE (%) = [1 - (Amount of free DOX in the supernatant/Total amount of DOX initially added)] × 100%.

Determination of Drug Loading: To measure the amount of DOX encapsulated within the exosomes, 100 μL of the DOX-loaded exosome (Exo-Dox) preparation, quantified using a BCA assay kit, was mixed with 1900 μL of acetonitrile to extract the drug and precipitate the exosomal proteins. After 2 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 25 min to pellet the precipitated proteins. The supernatant was then collected, and the concentration of DOX was estimated by measuring its intrinsic fluorescence (excitation at 480 nm) using a UV-Vis spectrofluorometer, based on a standard curve prepared from serial dilutions of DOX.

Drug Release Kinetics Study: The in vitro release profile of Doxorubicin (DOX) was analyzed using a dialysis method under three different pH conditions (7.4, 6.5, and 5.3) to simulate physiological, tumor microenvironment, and lysosomal conditions, respectively. One milliliter of the prepared Exo-Dox complex was transferred into a cellulose dialysis bag (molecular weight cut-off: 3.5 kDa) and immersed in 100 mL of the corresponding phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4, 6.5, or 5.3. To maintain homogeneity, the medium was continuously agitated at 100 rpm/min and kept at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h), a 2 mL aliquot of the PBS release medium was withdrawn. After measuring the DOX concentration, the aliquot was returned to the original vessel. The amount of released DOX was quantified fluorometrically using a UV-Vis spectrofluorometer (excitation: 480 nm) against a standard curve of DOX prepared in PBS. The relevant results and corresponding figure are presented in Fig. S6.

Assessment of the cellular uptake of Exo-U2 complexes in U87EGFRvⅢ glioma cells via confocal laser scanning microscopy

The aptamer U2 was conjugated at the 3′ end with the Cy5 fluorescent dye, while exosomes were labeled with PKH67 (Sigma, USA). The U87EGFRvⅢ cells were incubated with PKH67-Exo, U2-Cy5, or PKH67-Exo-U2-Cy5 complexes for 3 h. Following fixation, nuclear staining was performed and followed by three washes of the cells with PBS. Images were obtained using a CLSM system (A1/C2-DUVB, Nikon, Japan) and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation assay

U87EGFRvⅢ cells were distributed into cell culture plates with six replicate wells for each experimental condition. Following 24 h of culture, the cells were treated with the Exo-U2 complex or exosomes alone at final concentrations of 0.1 and 0.2 μg/μL. Following an additional culture period of 24 or 48 h post-transfection, cell proliferation was colorimetrically evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (Dojindo, USA). The relative cell survival rate was calculated, with each experiment repeated three times and three wells included per replicate. Furthermore, following the same cell treatment procedures as described above, cell proliferation was subsequently assessed using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo488 In Vitro Kit (RiboBio).

Cell migration and invasion

This experiment was conducted using a Transwell migration assay, following previously established protocols. Briefly, 1 × 105 U87EGFRvⅢ cells were subjected to serum starvation for 6 h before treatment with 0.1 μg/μL Exo-U2 complex or Exos in Transwell chamber (Corning, NY, USA). For invasion assays, Transwell inserts were prepared by coating with Matrigel (BD, USA) diluted 1:5 in serum-containing media, and placed at 37 °C until the gel was formed. Subsequent steps mirrored those of the migration assay. Post treatment, the cells were incubated with crystal violet for staining (Sigma, USA). After PBS washing and air drying, cells were imaged using a microscope and five randomly selected fields per chamber were imaged at 20 × magnification for a quantitative analysis.

Colony formation

U87EGFRvⅢ cells were plated in plates and treated with either 0.1 μg/μL Exo-U2 complex or exosomes, followed by exposure to X-ray irradiation (2 Gy). All irradiations were conducted employing a linear accelerator operating at 6 MV X-ray energy, with a dose rate of 500 cGy/min. After incubating for 15 days, the cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation and then stained with crystal violet (Sigma, USA). Imaging the plates to assess colony formation, and a quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Generation of orthotopic glioma models in mice

All the animal experiments received approval from the “Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University” and were conducted in compliance with the “Administrative Measures for Experimental Animals of Southern Medical University” and “Guidelines for Ethical Review of Animal Experiments of Southern Medical University”. Experimental model nude mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions. A U87EGFRvⅢ-luc cell line stably expressing firefly luciferase was developed by transfection with the luciferase reporter gene construct. Following anesthesia, the mice were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. A minor craniotomy was conducted in the striatal region via a dental micro-drill (−2.0 mm, 1.25 mm, −3.0 mm) relative to bregma. Subsequently, 3 μL of a cellular suspension containing 5-20 × 104 U87EGFRvⅢ-luc cells was meticulously infused into the intracranial space. The needle was maintained in position for 2 min before gradual withdrawal. The incisions on the scalps were sutured, and the mice were placed on a incubator to recover their body temperatures, after which they were returned to their original housing environment.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

Tumor formation within the striatal region was confirmed using an IVIS imaging system (Bruker FX Pro) 10 days after tumor cell inoculation. The U2-Cy7, Exo-U2-Cy7, Cy7-Exo-Dox, and Cy7-Exo-U2-Dox complexes were administered via the caudal vein at a single dose of 100 μL. At specified intervals following injection (0.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), the mice were anesthetized and imaged using the IVIS system to monitor Cy7 fluorescence signals and luciferase activity. At 24 and 96 h, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain of the mice from the experimental groups were collected after euthanasia for ex-vivo Cy7 fluorescence detection using the IVIS system.

Therapeutic effects of Exo-U2 and Exo-U2-Dox on GBM mouse models

Three days after U87EGFRvⅢ cell inoculation, baseline tumor bioluminescence was quantified and documented using the IVIS imaging system. Intracranial glioma-bearing nude mice were assigned to six groups using a randomization process (n = 6 per group) based on tumor fluorescence intensity in an unbiased manner. The groups received intravenous injections of the following treatments via the caudal vein: PBS, Exo (at an administered volume equivalent to that of Exo-U2), free DOX, Exo-U2, Exo-Dox, and Exo-U2-Dox (each group received Exo-U2 equivalent to 2.5 mg/kg). These treatments were administered every 3 days until mortality was ensured. Tumor fluorescence intensity was monitored on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 after the beginning of the treatment using the IVIS bioluminescence imaging system. All the mice were monitored bi-daily, and their survival times were recorded for a Kaplan–Meier analysis.

Immunofluorescence

U87EGFRvⅢ cells were cultured on glass coverslips and, following 24 h of drug treatment, cells were fixed in 4% PFA at RT for half an hour. Coverslips were then washed with PBS, blocked and permeabilized for non-specific binding in PBS-T-BSA buffer (PBS containing 0.5% Triton and 5% BSA) for 2 h. After rinsing with PBS, samples were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-pH2A.X (CST, 9718, 1:1000) and GSDMD (abcam, AB210070, 1:1000) antibody at 4 °C for 24 h under gentle agitation. The following day, after washing to remove unbound primary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit, 1:500 dilution) was applied and incubated for 1.5 h. Cells were then exposed to a brief staining with DAPI solution (1:1000) for 10 min for nuclear staining. Coverslips were inverted onto glass slides, and fluorescence images were acquired with a laser scanning confocal microscope. After drug administration, brain tissues were collected for cryosection following euthanasia of the mice. All the additional procedures adhered to the standard protocols for cellular immunofluorescence. Image processing and analysis were conducted using Adobe Photoshop and ImageJ software.

Immunoblot analysis

The cells were subjected to lysis in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. To normalize the loading control, protein concentrations were measured with a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, 20 μg of protein was used for the experiment, separated by SDS-PAGE, and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore). The following primary antibodies were used under conditions specified by the manufacturers’ protocols: anti-GAPDH (Sigma, AF0911, 1:2000), anti-β-actin (Boster, BM0627, 1:2000), anti-53BP1 (CST, 4937, 1:500), anti-pATM (Ser1981) (CST, 5883, 1:500), anti-ATM (CST, 2873, 1:1000), anti-EGFR (CST, 4267, 1:1000), anti-pEGFR (tyr1038) (CST, 3777, 1:1000), anti-pChk2 (T68) (CST, 2661, 1:500), anti-Chk2 (CST, 2662, 1:500), anti-pH2A.X (S139) (CST, 9718, 1:1000), anti-cleaved-caspase-1 (tyr1038) (GeneTex, GTX133447, 1:500), anti-GSDMD (Abcam, AB210070, 1:1000), anti-N-GSDMD (ABclonal, A22523, 1:500), anti-caspase-1 (Abcam, AB207802, 1:1000), anti-NLRP3 (ABclonal, A5652, 1:1000), anti-caspase-3 (abcam, ab32351, 1:2000), anti-GSDME (abcam, ab215191, 1:2000), anti-CD63 (Abcam, AB217345, 1:1000), anti-TSG101 (Abcam, AB125001, 1:5000), anti-calnexin (Abcam, ab22595, 1:1000), and anti-CD81 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, BS70675, 1:500). Immunodetection was performed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein bands were visualized with an ECL Western Blotting Substrate Kit (Bio-Rad). Then the ImageJ software was used to analyse the results.

Therapeutic effects of five Exo-U2 or U2-Exo-Dox treatments on GBM mouse models

Ten days after U87EGFRvⅢ cells inoculation, baseline tumor bioluminescence was quantified and documented using the IVIS imaging system. Intracranial glioma-bearing nude mice were assigned to six groups using a randomization process (n = 6 per group) based on tumor fluorescence intensity in an unbiased manner. The mice received the following treatments via the caudal vein: PBS, Exo (at a dose equivalent to that of Exo-U2), free DOX, Exo-U2, Exo-Dox, or Exo-U2-Dox (each group received an Exo-U2 equivalent dose of 2.5 mg/kg). Five different doses were administered. Tumor fluorescence intensity was measured using IVIS bioluminescence imaging on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 after the beginning of the treatment. In addition, all the mice were monitored bi-daily, and their survival times were recorded for a Kaplan–Meier analysis.

Therapeutic effects of Exo-U2 and Exo-U2-Dox combined with X-ray irradiation on GBM mouse models

The irradiated groups received 2 Gy per fraction for five fractions following injections of PBS, Exos, free DOX, Exo-U2, Exo-Dox, or Exo-U2-Dox. On the day following drug injection, the mice were anesthetized and placed in a prone position. The irradiation field of the linear accelerator was adjusted to confine solely to the tumor site in the mouse. The radiation field of the linear accelerator was collimated to include only the head. Immediately after the procedure, the mice were placed on an electric heating pad for rewarming. All radiotherapy was delivered using a linear accelerator with 6 MV X-rays at a dose rate of 500 cGy/min. The remaining experimental procedures were conducted as described in Section 2.15.

Hematoxylin & eosin and immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses. The antibodies used were anti-Ki-67 (Servare, GB111499, 1:500), anti-phosphorylated histone H3(p-H3) (Proteintech, 66863-1-Ig, 1:4000) and anti-CD34 (Abcam, ab81289, 1:2500). Whole-slide images of all samples were acquired using an Olympus VS200 digital slide scanner (Tokyo, Japan) and subsequently analyzed quantitatively with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. To evaluate the statistical significance of intergroup differences, Student's t-tests or One/Two-way ANOVA with Dunnett's post-hoc test were employed. The survival analysis was determined using the Kaplan-Meier estimate (log-rank test). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Exosome engineering and characterization

We successfully extracted typical fibroblast-like MSCs from neonatal mice via primary culture (Fig. 1A and B). The flow cytometry results revealed that the morphological properties of the cell population were uniform and positive for high expression of the mesenchymal cell surface antigens CD29 (100%), CD44 (99.5%) and CD105 (60.6%) (Fig. 1C); positive for high expression of the stem cell marker Sca-1 (98.6%) (Fig. S1); low expression of the cellular markers of the hematopoietic system, CD11b (0.82%), CD34 (5.12%), and CD45 (5.25%) (Fig. 1C); and low expression of the endothelial cell marker CD31 (0.15%) and the costimulatory molecules CD86 (7.28%) and Ia (0.056%) (Fig. S1), which is consistent with the immunophenotypic characteristics of MSCs described in the literature [24]. In addition, more than 95% of the MSCs (Fig. 1C) were positive for these immuno-phenotypic characteristics. We isolated exosomes from the supernatant of MSC cultures via differential centrifugation combined with the membrane affinity method (Fig. 1A). The results of NTA and WB subsequently revealed that the exosomes presented a typical cup vesicle-like structure with a complete membrane structure; a particle size of approximately 120 nm; positive expression of the exosome protein markers CD63, CD81 and TSG101; and negative expression of the cellular endoplasmic reticulum protein marker calnexin (Fig. 1D–F). Cholesterol has a high affinity for the cell membrane and can bind to exosomes with a membrane structure. We added cholesterol modification at the 5′ end of aptamer U2 and incubated the two with normal temperature oscillation. LDS results revealed that the aptamer was successfully connected to the surface of the exosome (Fig. 1G). In addition, we modified the 3′ end band of U2 with the Cy5 fluorophore, and the resulting exosomes were labeled with PKH67, a celluar membrane dye. The PKH67-labeled Exos or Exo-U2 complexes were coincubated with the GBM cell line U87EGFRvⅢ for 3 h, after which the nuclei were stained with DAPI and finally observed by using laser confocal microscopy. We found that in the Exo group, the cytoplasm of U87EGFRvⅢ cells was filled with exosomes with PKH67 green fluorescence. In the Exo-U2 group, the cytosol of U87EGFRvⅢ cells was not only filled with exosomes with green fluorescence but a large amount of aptamer U2 with Cy5 red fluorescence was also observed. In the U2 group, the aptamer U2, which emits red fluorescence, was concentrated mainly at the cell membrane of U87EGFRvⅢ cells (Fig. 1H).

Effects of the Exo-U2 complex on the U87EGFRvⅢ cell line in vitro

The effect of the Exo-U2 complex on the proliferation of U87EGFRvⅢ cells was investigated using the EdU assay. Compared with that of the control group, the celluar proliferation of the two Exo-U2 concentration groups tended to decrease, and the effect became more obvious with increasing concentration. However, neither Exo concentration had a significant effect on cell proliferation (Fig. 2A). To further assess the inhibition of U87EGFRvⅢ cell proliferation by the Exo-U2 complex, we performed a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay. Under treatment with the Exo-U2 complex at 0.1 and 0.2 μg/μL, the relative survival rate of U87EGFRvⅢ cells decreased significantly, and the longer the duration of action was, the lower the cell survival rate. Conversely, treatment of U87EGFRvⅢ cells with exosomes for 24 h or 48 h did not significantly inhibit proliferation (Fig. 2B). A Transwell assay was used to determine whether Exo-U2 complex inhibited the migration and invasion of U87EGFRvⅢ cells(Fig. 2C–E). The migration of U87EGFRvⅢ cells treated with Exo-U2 complex was significantly lower (∗∗∗p < 0.001), suggesting that Exo-U2 complex could inhibit the migration of U87EGFRvⅢ cells. Moreover, the Transwell invasion assay revealed that the number of invasive U87EGFRvⅢ cells decreased after Exo-U2 treatment for 24 h (∗∗∗p < 0.001). Additionally, we used WB analysis to detect the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, AKT and these total protein expression to explore the possible molecular mechanism of Exo-U2-mediated inhibition of U87EGFRvⅢ cell proliferation. After 24 h of treatment with Exo-U2 complex, the expression levels of pEGFR and pAKT decreased, whereas the total protein expression level of EGFR and AKT remained unchanged (Fig. 2F and G). These results suggest that the Exo-U2 complex may exert its effects by inhibiting EGFR and AKT autophosphorylation and subsequently inhibiting cell proliferation.

Exo-U2 complex specifically targets glioma and inhibits glioma growth in vivo

We know that exosomes have the ability to penetrate the BBB [25] and that the aptamers can bind specifically to the target [26]; thus, we wanted to determine whether the Exo-U2 complex could successfully penetrate the BBB to the tumor site and play a tumor-suppressive role. The distribution and metabolism of the Exo-U2 complex in the mice at 0.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 96 h were determined, and the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain organs were dissected at 24/96 h. These parameters were recorded via the IVIS system (Fig. 3A). The results revealed that aptamer U2 alone also crosses the BBB to reach the GBM tumor site, and we believe that the reason for this result is the impaired BBB at the tumor site. However, owing to its relatively short half-life, the amount of U2 reaching the brain is very limited, and U2 is only present in vivo for a short amount of time. The Exo-U2 complex successfully penetrated the BBB into the tumor site in the mouse brain, where it strongly aggregated in the brain. In addition, the fluorescence intensity of the Exo-U2-Cy7 complex in mice at 96 h was much greater than that for the U2-Cy7 group (Fig. 3B and C), which indicated that exosomes can prolong the half-life of U2 in vivo, providing a temporal basis for U2-targeted therapy for GBM. Compared with U2, the Exo-U2 complex also shows a longer half-life in C57BL/6J mice (Fig. S2). Additionally, we observed that Exo-Cy7 lacks targeting ability in bearing intracranial U87EGFRvⅢ-luc glioma mice (Fig. S3) and is only detectable in metabolic organs at 96 h post-injection. We subsequently examined the antiglioma effect of the Exo-U2 complex in vivo. Compared with the PBS group, the Exo-U2 group presented significant tumor suppression after 12 days of treatment; however, no significant tumor suppression effect was observed in the exosome group. On day 8, the tumor growth rate of the Exo-U2 group was much lower than those of the PBS and Exo groups (∗∗∗p < 0.001) (Fig. 3D and E), and the survival time of the nude mice in the orthotopic glioma model was prolonged (∗∗∗p < 0.001) (Fig. 3F and G). Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues from glioma-bearing mice revealed that, compared to the Control and Exo groups, the Exo-U2 group exhibited a significant reduction in the percentage of Ki67-and p-H3-positive cells, indicating markedly suppressed tumor proliferation (Fig. 3H). These results show that the Exo-U2 complex has favorable anti-GBM efficacy in vivo.

Pyroptosis induction and radiosensitization of the Exo-U2 complex in vitro

When pyroptosis occurs, the cells swell and exhibit many bubble protrusions, which are important markers for distinguishing pyroptosis from other types of programmed cell death [27]. By exploring the tumor suppressive effect of the Exo-U2 complex on U87EGFRvⅢ cells in vitro, we found that after treatment with the complex, bubble-like protrusions on the plasma membrane of U87EGFRvⅢ cells could be clearly identified (Fig. 4A). GSDMD immunofluorescence results revealed that the protein expression of GSDMD in U87EGFRvⅢ cells treated with Exo-U2 was significantly greater than that in the control/Exo group (Fig. 4B and D). Notably, this increase could be attenuated by the caspase-1-specific inhibitor VX-765, suggesting the occurrence of caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis in U87EGFRvⅢ cells after Exo-U2 treatment. Meanwhile, GSDMD immunofluorescence of mice results revealed that the protein expression of GSDMD in the tumor tissues of Exo-U2-treated mice was also significantly greater than that in the control group (Fig. 4C and E), suggesting the occurrence of pyroptosis in tumor tissues after Exo-U2 treatment. Then, we used WB analysis to measure the levels of activated caspase-1 and GSDMD and total protein expression to explore the possible molecular mechanism of Exo-U2-induced pyroptosis in U87EGFRvⅢ cells. After 24 h of Exo-U2 treatment, the expression levels of caspase-1 and GSDMD, as well as their activated forms of cleaved-cas-1 and N-GSDMD, increased. The expression level of NLRP3 also increased (Fig. 4F) (Fig. S4A). Compared with the control group, the expression levels of the NLRP3, CASP1, and GSDMD mRNAs were increased in U87EGFRvⅢ cells treated with the Exo-U2 complex (Fig. S5). Similarly, the results of Western blot experiments in nude mice revealed that the protein expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD, cleaved-cas-1, and N-GSDMD was increased (Fig. 4G) (Fig. S4B) compared with that in the control group.

It is reported that cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) damage can be recognized by cytoplasmic inflammasomes, leading to the activation of Caspase-1. The activated Caspase-1 subsequently performs a dual function: on one hand, it processes and activates the cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, thereby initiating an inflammatory response; on the other hand, it cleaves and activates the GSDMD protein. The N-terminal fragment of GSDMD (GSDMD-N) then forms pores in the cell membrane, ultimately triggering the characteristic features of pyroptosis, including cellular swelling, organelle damage, and eventual cell lysis [28]. Immunofluorescence experiments with pH2A.X revealed a significant reduction in the number of pH2A.X foci in treated cells compared with those in control cells (∗∗∗p < 0.001) (Fig. 4H) (Fig. S4C). In addition, we examined protein expression in U87EGFRvⅢ cells after Exo-U2 treatment and irradiation through WB analysis to further explore the mechanism by which the Exo-U2 complex promotes the radiosensitivity of U87EGFRvⅢ cells. The Exo-U2 complex inhibited EGFR phosphorylation in irradiated U87EGFRvⅢ cells. Moreover, the complex downregulated the protein expression of 53BP1 and the phosphorylation of Chk2, ATM, and H2A.X (Fig. 4I) (Fig. S4D). DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are major and severe types of damage to genomic DNA induced by ionizing radiation. When DSBs occur, the histone protein H2AX is phosphorylated and recruited at DSB sites to form phosphorylated H2AX foci as a DSB biomarker. Whether pyroptosis leads to increased effects of radiotherapy should be further investigated.

Glioma-suppressive effects of the Exo-U2-Dox complex in vivo

Cy7-labeled Exo-Dox and Exo-U2-Dox complexes were injected through the tail vein, and then, their distribution and metabolism were determined at 0.5, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h. The heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain organs were dissected at 24 h (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that the Exo-U2-Dox complex reached the middle of the head and was close to the GBM tumor location at 24 h after injection, but the Exo-Dox complex remained in the mouse brain fontanelle. The Exo-U2-Dox complex can penetrate the BBB to reach brain tumors resulting in strong fluorescence aggregation (Fig. 5B and C). These findings indicate that U2 has a strong tumor targeting ability and is a notable GBM tumor-targeting indicator that can carry Dox to the tumor site to play a therapeutic role.

After 12 days of treatment, no significant tumor suppression effect was observed in the Exo group compared with the PBS group. Dox slightly inhibited tumor proliferation (Mechanistically, this effect may be mediated through the induction of caspase-3-dependent pyroptosis (Fig. S7)), while the Exo-U2 (p < 0.001) and Exo-Dox (p < 0.001) groups presented better tumor suppression than the Dox group did. In addition, no significant difference was found between the Exo-U2 and Exo-Dox groups. Compared with the Exo-U2 and Exo-Dox groups, the Exo-U2-Dox group presented more obvious tumor suppression (Fig. 5D and E). Combining Exo-U2-Dox treatment with X-rays showed synergistic antiglioma efficacy in vivo

The above results showed that the Exo-U2-Dox complex had excellent antiglioma efficacy, but whether quantitative drug therapy had the same effect was not known. Therefore, we conducted the following animal experiments. With the first drug injection as the starting point (day 0), the drug was injected through the tail vein every 3 days for a total of five times. The tumor fluorescence signal of the mice was recorded every 4 days from the first injection of drugs. No further treatment was performed after five injections, and then, the survival of the mice was recorded(Fig. 6A). After 15 days of treatment, the Exo-U2-Dox group presented the most obvious tumor growth rate inhibition; the Exo-Dox and Exo-U2 groups had relatively significant therapeutic effects; the DOX group also exhibited a slight inhibitory effect on tumor proliferation while the PBS group and the Exo group exhibited no tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 6B and C). The survival analysis (Fig. 6D) results indicated that the nude mice bearing orthotopic glioma xenografts injected with PBS had the shortest survival, with a median survival of 25.5 days. However, the Exo-U2-Dox group had the longest survival period, with a median survival of 32.5 days, which was significantly longer than those of the PBS and DOX groups and greater than those of the Exo-U2 and Exo-Dox groups (Fig. 6E). The above experimental results show that the quantitative drug therapy had the same effect as the drug intervention treatment until the mice died.

The results showed that treatment with the Exo-U2 complex combined with chemotherapy drugs shows the remarkable antiglioma efficacy. Therefore, whether this treatment combined with X-ray radiation therapy could have better antiglioma efficacy was explored in animal experiments. With the first drug injection as the starting point (day 0), the drug was injected through the tail vein every 3 days five times, with each injection followed by 2 Gy X-ray irradiation the next day for a total dose of 10 Gy. After 15 days of treatment, in the PBS group, DOX inhibited tumor proliferation slightly. Compared with the Dox treatment group, the Exo-U2 (∗∗∗p < 0.001) and Exo-Dox (∗∗∗p < 0.001) groups presented better tumor suppression effects, with no significant difference between the two groups. Compared with the Exo-U2 and Exo-Dox groups, Exo-U2-Dox had a more pronounced tumor-suppressive effect (Fig. 6F and G), which demonstrated the potential of the Exo-U2-Dox complex as a radiosensitizer. We also recorded the survival of nude mice bearing orthotopic glioma xenografts in each treatment group to evaluate the effectiveness of treatment in each group. The survival curve (Fig. 6H) revealed that tumor-bearing nude mice in the PBS group had the shortest survival period, with a median survival of 30.5 days. However, tumor-bearing nude mice in the Exo-U2-Dox group had the longest survival (median: 43 days), significantly longer than the PBS and DOX groups and longer than the Exo-U2 and Exo-Dox groups. The survival of the nude mice in all the treatment groups ranged from long to short in the following order: Exo-U2-Dox (43 days) > Exo-U2 (39 days) > Exo-Dox (36.5 days) > DOX (34 days) > PBS (30.5 days) = Exo (30.5 days) (Fig. 6I). This result is consistent with the antiglioma effect in vivo in each treatment group. After treatment, the tumors of the mice were dissected (Fig. 6K). The results revealed that the tumor volume of the nude mice decreased in the following order: PBS, Exo > DOX > Exo-U2, Exo-Dox > Exo-U2-Dox, which was consistent with the antiglioma effect in each treatment group.

We performed colony formation assays to further test whether the Exo-U2 complex has potential effects on radiosensitization in vitro. We found that U87EGFRvⅢ cells formed fewer colonies than did the other two groups after exposure to irradiation and Exo-U2 treatment (∗∗p < 0.01) (Fig. 6J). Next, we examined the malignancy of in situ glioma tissue in tumor-bearing nude mice and the expression of Ki67 and CD34 by H&E and IHC experiments. The expression level of Ki67 in the GBM tissues of the tumor-bearing nude mice was the highest in the PBS group but the lowest in the Exo-U2-Dox treatment group. No significant differences in Ki67 expression levels were observed among the PBS, Exo, and DOX treatment groups in the glioma tissues of the tumor-bearing nude mice. Compared with that in the PBS group, a slight decrease in Ki67 expression levels was observed in the glioma tissues of tumor-bearing nude mice in the Exo-Dox and Exo-U2 treatment groups, whereas significant downregulation of Ki67 expression was detected in the Exo-U2-Dox treatment group (Fig. 6L) (Fig. S8). The expression levels of Ki67 in the GBM tissues of the tumor-bearing nude mice across the treatment groups were ranked in descending order as follows: PBS, Exo > DOX > Exo-U2, Exo-Dox > Exo-U2-Dox. Similarly, the expression of CD34, a marker of angiogenesis, in glioma tissues decreased in the following pattern: PBS, Exo, DOX > Exo-U2, Exo-Dox > Exo-U2-Dox (Fig. 6L) (Fig. S8). In addition, no obvious tissue damage in major organs was observed in the treatment groups, suggesting that the treatments were safe nanomedicines for glioma therapy (Fig. S9). These results demonstrate that the Exo-U2-Dox complex effectively suppresses glioma cell proliferation and neovascularization, thereby significantly inhibiting GBM growth.

Discussion and conclusions

GBM is a type of grade IV glioma; the high invasiveness and heterogeneity of GBM, along with the existence of the BBB, have led to consistently poor clinical treatment outcomes. On this basis, researchers have focused on methods for administering intracranial therapeutic drugs, such as intranasal, cannula or intraventricular approaches, in an attempt to bypass the BBB and achieve direct administration [29]. However, such drug administration methods carry the risk of infection. In addition, the hypoxic tumor microenvironment within GBM increases resistance to TMZ. Therefore, an urgent need exists for multidimensional treatment of GBM with molecular targeted drugs combined with conventional antitumor drugs. On the basis of previous work, our research group has developed a composite biological agent based on aptamers combined with exosomes–Dox, to assist in the radiotherapy of GBM and increase the sensitivity of GBM to treatment. In this experimental study, exosomes secreted from mesenchymal stem cells have good biocompatibility with GBM cells and their ability to penetrate the BBB is significant. By increasing pyroptosis in GBM cells, radiosensitization can be achieved, providing a reference treatment path for improving the survival of patients with GBM.

EVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells, which are characterized by their high drug loading capacity, minimal immunogenicity, and superior biocompatibility, demonstrate exceptional efficacy as drug delivery systems [15]. Compared with other nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems, exosomes, which are naturally produced by cells, possess enhanced safety profiles, including biodegradability, broad inclusivity, and an increased specific surface area conducive to binding therapeutic molecular targets [30,31]. Currently, exosomes have been employed as delivery vehicles for small-molecule drugs, such as aptamers, paclitaxel, and rapamycin, in the treatment of various diseases [32]. In alignment with our research, other studies have documented the use of aptamer-modified exosomes in conjunction with materials such as gold nanoparticles for photothermal therapy aimed at eradicating tumor cells [32]. Specifically, AS1411 aptamers and CD63 were conjugated with exosomes, leveraging the photothermal properties of gold nanorods to ablate cancer cells under near-infrared radiation, thereby inhibiting cancer cell viability and promoting apoptosis [33]. The presence of inorganic nanomaterials warrants attentionbecause of their potential toxicity [34], which arises from the absence of biodegradation pathways. In the domain of tumor research, exosomes play dual roles as drug delivery vehicles and therapeutic agents [35]. Consistent with our findings, exosomes modified with aptamers and conjugated with DOX effectively inhibited the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells [36] and triple-negative breast cancer cells [37]. In neurological research, extracellular vesicles have exhibited favorable pharmacokinetics for crossing BBB [32], making them promising tools for enhancing the survival of patients with GBM. Researchers have developed a biodegradable nanoplatform, CSI@Ex-Apt, utilizing silica nanoparticles to encapsulate catalase and the acoustic sensitizer indocyanine green (ICG), which are then coated with AS1411 aptamer-modified macrophage exosomes. This configuration facilitates efficient BBB penetration and targeted GBM therapy. Elevated glutathione levels in GBM cells induce degradation of the nanoplatform, thereby releasing catalase to catalyze oxygen production and mitigate the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, ultimately achieving targeted GBM therapy [38]. Similar to our experimental findings, recent studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes, when used to deliver rapamycin, can effectively traverse the BBB, target the tumor microenvironment, enhance sustained release efficiency, modulate the VEGF axis, and inhibit the progression of GBM [15]. These findings suggest a promising new strategy for GBM treatment. These findings underscore the potential of aptamer-modified exosomes loaded with specific therapeutic agents to achieve targeted intracranial therapy. Although existing nanomedicine delivery systems have shown initial advantages in GBM, no reports have been published on the comprehensive efficacy of the aptamer Exo-U2-Dox complex in GBM combined with radiation.

The aggressive nature and high proliferative capacity of GBM contribute to the limited overall survival of patients, which averages 14.6 months. Our research group employed cell-selex technology to screen for aptamer U2 in preliminary studies and validated its efficacy both in vitro and in vivo. The aptamer U2 inhibits the downstream signaling pathway mediated by the EGFRvⅢ membrane protein in GBM cells by binding to it, thereby reducing GBM invasion and migration while promoting apoptosis [19]. When combined with radiation exposure, aptamer U2 functions as a radiosensitizer, enhancing the sensitivity of GBM to X-rays by inhibiting the 53BP1/ATM/Chk2 signaling pathway [20]. DOX, an FDA-approved broad-spectrum antitumor agent, primarily impedes tumor growth by suppressing DNA replication in tumor cells, exacerbating DNA damage following reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and activating apoptotic pathways [39]. In the context of GBM, the presence of the BBB reduces the distribution of DOX within the cranial cavity, thereby diminishing its therapeutic efficacy [40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that DOX has a more pronounced inhibitory effect on GBM than the first-line drug TMZ does in vitro [41]. Consequently, our research group has opted to encapsulate DOX within exosomes secreted by MSCs to investigate the therapeutic potential of this complex.

Pyroptosis represents a distinct form of programmed cell death characterized by its inflammatory nature, primarily mediated by gasdermin D (GSDMD)-induced membrane pore formation, cellular swelling, and rapid lysis, which subsequently leads to the release of substantial quantities of proinflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) [42]. The initiation of pyroptosis is intricately linked to the activation of various inflammasomes, with the NLRP3 inflammasome being the most extensively investigated. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome facilitates the activation of caspase-1, which subsequently triggers GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and enhances the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 [43]. As a significant form of programmed cell death, pyroptosis has garnered considerable attention because of its involvement in immune responses and pathological conditions. A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms and regulatory pathways governing pyroptosis is essential for the development of novel therapeutic strategies, particularly in the management of inflammatory diseases and cancer [44].

The literature indicates that EVs secreted by neutrophils can induce pyroptosis in macrophages during acute lung injury [45], whereas EVs derived from stem cells have been shown to inhibit pyroptosis in muscle cells and facilitate the repair of skeletal muscle damage through other extracellular mechanisms [46]. In the context of the nervous system, research has demonstrated that EVs secreted by mMSCs can suppress the apoptosis of BV2 cells via the miR-146a-5p/TRAF6 axis. These findings suggest that the impact of EVs on cellular apoptosis is contingent upon their molecular contents [47]. In our investigation, we observed morphological changes in GBM cells treated with Exo-U2, which were confirmed to be pyroptotic bodies. This phenomenon was attributed to the activation of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway, a classical mediator of pyroptosis, whereas treatment with exosomes alone did not induce GBM cell pyroptosis. These findings indicate that the pyroptosis triggered by the Exo-U2 complex is mediated by the aptamer U2, a novel finding that has not yet been reported.

Research indicates that the inhibition of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated cell death signaling pathway in tumor cells may contribute to the development of resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [48]. In addition, targeting EGFR with microRNA-123a has been shown to decrease EGFR expression, which subsequently increases the mRNA and protein levels of NLRP3, leading to the upregulation of caspase-1, N-GSDMD, ASC, IL-1β, and IL-18 proteins, thereby promoting apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells [49]. This evidence suggests that the Exo-U2 complex may induce apoptosis in GBM cells by downregulating the intracellular signaling pathways mediated by EGFRvⅢ, ultimately resulting in the inhibition of GBM proliferation and progression both in vitro and in vivo.

GBM is located deep in the intracranial space; therefore, adjuvant radiotherapy remains necessary following in situ resection. However, the presence of therapeutic resistance to radiotherapy in GBM often leads to suboptimal treatment outcomes. Therefore, strategies aimed at enhancing radiation accumulation within the tumor and improving the sensitivity of the tumor to radiotherapy represent critical avenues for improving therapeutic efficacy. In our study, the application of the Exo-U2-Dox complex in conjunction with radiation therapy in mice bearing GBM significantly extended survival and delayed GBM progression. These findings are consistent with those of Jiang et al., who reported that the Exo-miR-197-3p complex acts as a radiosensitizer, enhancing the radioresistance of nasopharyngeal carcinoma through modulation of the AKT/mTOR axis and HSPA5-mediated autophagy [50]. Additionally, the literature indicates that the intraperitoneal administration of circulating exosomes can induce apoptosis in small intestinal epithelial cells and exacerbate DNA damage in mice [51], which aligns with the conclusions of our study. Recent investigations have also shown that engineered exosomes derived from autologous cells can facilitate targeted radioimmunotherapy for melanoma by modulating the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby effectively treating invasive melanoma [52], which will be the future direction of our in-depth research.

Our experiments demonstrated that the Exo-U2-Dox complex effectively inhibits GBM growth and enhances radiosensitivity. Nevertheless, several critical questions remain unanswered. How does the Exo-U2-Dox complex traverse the BBB to reach the GBM within the tumor? By what mechanisms does the Exo-U2-Dox complex deliver signals intracellularly and to the nucleus in GBM cells, and through which organelles is the pyroptosis signal transduced? Addressing these questions is essential for advancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of the Exo-U2-Dox complex as a preclinical therapeutic agent. Furthermore, optimizing the quantitative production of exosomes is crucial for advancing functional research in this area.

This investigation reveals the characterization of exosomal constructs combined with the aptamer U2, demonstrating their efficacy in attenuating GBM proliferation and invasion both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, the exosome-aptamer U2 complex promotes pyroptosis in GBM cells, enhancing radiosensitization effects. Moreover, the combined treatment of DOX with the exosome-aptamer U2 significantly curtails tumor advancement, suggesting a synergistic enhancement of radiosensitivity and overall therapeutic potential against GBM progression. These findings substantiate the promising role of exosomal delivery systems in improving treatment outcomes for aggressive tumors. In conclusion, our findings provide novel evidence supporting the potential of exosome-delivered drugs for GBM treatment, thereby offering promising directions for future clinical applications.
