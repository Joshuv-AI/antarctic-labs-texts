All-Suture Repair of a Posterior Sternoclavicular Fracture Dislocation in an Adolescent Athlete

Video Transcript

In this video, we present a surgical technique for the open reduction and internal fixation of a posterior sternoclavicular (SC) physeal separation in an adolescent athlete using an all-suture repair. While various fixation methods exist for this rare and potentially dangerous injury, we present an all-suture technique that provides robust, physeal-respecting stability, thereby avoiding the complications associated with bulky hardware use in a skeletally immature patient. Additionally, we provide surgical technique pearls and pitfalls for this approach, focusing on safe transosseous tunnel creation and suture passage to protect vital mediastinal structures.

Background

Traumatic injuries to the SC joint are uncommon, accounting for approximately 3% of all shoulder girdle injuries and <1% of all joint dislocations. 6 The inherent stability of the SC joint is derived primarily from a robust ligamentous complex, including the stout posterior capsular ligament, rather than from its bony architecture. 2 Traumatic dislocations are typically classified as anterior or posterior, with posterior dislocations being far less frequent but significantly more dangerous. 6

The medial clavicular physis is the last ossification center in the body to fuse, 8 representing a point of relative biomechanical weakness compared with the strong ligamentous restraints of the SC joint. Therefore, forces applied to the shoulder girdle that would cause a true SC joint dislocation in an adult are much more likely to result in a Salter-Harris type I or II physeal fracture in a patient with an open physis. 14

Posterior displacement of the medial clavicle, whether from a true dislocation or a physeal fracture, constitutes an orthopaedic emergency. 2 The proximity of vital mediastinal structures—including the innominate vessels, internal jugular vein, aortic arch, vagus and phrenic nerves, trachea, and esophagus—creates a high risk for life-threatening compressive complications. 11 The SC injury typically results from 1 of 2 mechanisms: a high-energy, indirect force—such as a posterolateral compressive load applied to the shoulder—or, less commonly, a direct force to the medial clavicle. 10 The diagnostic paradigm for a suspected posterior SC injury in an adolescent begins with either chest radiography or a serendipity view of the clavicle, but these are often inconclusive. Therefore, diagnosis often relies on noncontrast computed tomography (CT) to definitively characterize the injury and assess for mediastinal impingement.1,4 Furthermore, the potential for significant hemorrhage during reduction necessitates a multidisciplinary approach, with cardiothoracic surgery consultation and availability considered the standard of care. 13

Indications

Case Presentation

A 14-year-old, right-hand dominant female presented to the emergency department with acute, severe right anterior chest pain after a grappling incident during her jiu-jitsu competition. She reported that her opponent forcefully pulled her right arm, causing immediate, sharp pain localized to her right SC joint. She was unable to continue competing at that time.

Upon initial evaluation, the patient was not in distress and had no dyspnea, dysphagia, or hoarseness of voice. A subtle but distinct depression was palpable at the site of the right medial clavicle when compared with the contralateral side, and she was tender about this area. Her distal neurovascular examination was intact, with symmetric pulses and normal sensation in both upper extremities.

Initial radiographs of the chest, clavicle, and shoulder showed asymmetry of the right clavicle at the manubrium, raising suspicion for an SC dislocation or physeal separation. To confirm the diagnosis and evaluate the adjacent vital structures, a noncontrast CT scan of the chest was performed. The CT scan confirmed the diagnosis of a posteriorly displaced fracture of the medial clavicle. The displaced metaphyseal fragment was noted to abut the junction of the right brachiocephalic vein and superior vena cava.

Operative Indication

Given the acute, unstable, and posteriorly displaced nature of the injury, open reduction and internal fixation were recommended. Operative management was indicated to reduce the risk of neurovascular compromise and prevent future instability.

Technique Description

Preoperative Planning

The patient remained hemodynamically stable, allowing for the procedure to be scheduled electively 2 days after injury. Given the proximity to critical vascular structures, the cardiothoracic surgery team was consulted preoperatively to ensure their availability. Of note, the draping preferences of the cardiothoracic team should be confirmed, as some may request full exposure inferiorly to access the groin.

Surgical Technique

After obtaining informed consent, the patient was taken to the operating room. The cardiothoracic surgery team was notified and remained on standby in the event of an iatrogenic vascular injury.

The patient was positioned supine in a modified beach-chair position on a radiolucent operating table. A sterile bolster was placed longitudinally between the scapulae to retract the shoulders and improve exposure of the SC joint and fracture. The contralateral chest was also prepped and draped to allow for a potential open vascular approach if needed. In this case, a wider draping area could have been used to provide greater exposure of the contralateral chest. The ipsilateral hand, covered in Coban, was clamped to the drapes to aid in positioning.

A 6-cm curvilinear incision was made, centered over the right SC joint and extending over the medial clavicle and manubrium. We carried the dissection down through the subcutaneous tissue and platysma to the clavipectoral fascia. Mobile windows were raised to assist in visualization. The clavipectoral fascia was incised using electrocautery, with repeated palpation of the clavicle to ensure the incision remained centered over bone. The clavicular head of the pectoralis major muscle was carefully elevated from its origin using subperiosteal dissection to expose the clavicle and fracture site. We meticulously preserved the periosteal sleeve to maintain blood supply and enhance the healing potential. This exposure allowed for direct visualization of the clavicular metaphysis, cartilaginous medial epiphysis, and the posterior displacement of the metaphysis.

We then passed three 1.3-mm suture tapes from medial to lateral, through the anterior surface of the epiphysis and across the fracture site. This was done before reduction to take advantage of the exposed fracture surface and to avoid interference from the lateral fragment. Not utilized here, but if passing the needle through the epiphysis is proving challenging, heating the needle with the Bovie enables easier passage. The sutures were then clamped away from the surgical field, and we proceeded with the reduction.

For the reduction, 2 Bennet retractors were placed deep to the metaphyseal fragment to provide leverage and exposure. We first attempted reduction with a towel clamp, applying an anteriorly directed force on the metaphyseal fragment. This was unsuccessful. We then removed the Bennet retractors and used a Freer elevator in a shoehorn fashion to lever the metaphyseal fragment anteriorly, resulting in a successful reduction.

To prepare for fixation, three 2-mm transosseous tunnels were drilled from the anterior surface of the metaphysis into the fracture surface. Before drilling, 2 Bennet retractors were used as both a lever and a backstop to prevent blindly drilling deep to the clavicle. Three shuttling sutures were then passed from lateral to medial through the transosseous tunnels.

This is a preliminary view of our fixation construct. The suture tapes were then sequentially shuttled from the medial to the lateral through the metaphyseal fragment. The suture tapes are now shuttled through both fracture fragments. Once all sutures were passed, we tied them down sequentially with a needle driver to ensure robust knot fixation. The suture tails were cut, creating an extremely low-profile internal fixation construct.

The stability of the fixation was rigorously tested by passively ranging the shoulder through full flexion, abduction, and rotation. The reduction remained stable with no evidence of displacement. The surgical site was then copiously irrigated with sterile saline. The capsule is first repaired to improve stability. The previously elevated periosteum and pectoralis major fascia were repaired over the suture knots using absorbable suture to provide a soft-tissue envelope and obliterate dead space. Finally, the platysma and subcutaneous tissues were closed in layers, and the skin was approximated with a running subcuticular suture.

Results

For the first 3 weeks, the patient was nonweightbearing with her right arm in a shoulder sling at all times. Shoulder strengthening was initiated after 6 weeks, or once she achieved a pain-free range of motion and radiographic signs of fracture union. She was cleared to return to sports 3 months postoperatively. Recommended postoperative imaging occurs at 2 and 6 weeks postoperatively, and includes radiographs of the clavicle and a chest radiograph with 40° cephalic tilt.

Discussion

Posterior SC joint injuries are a rare but potentially life-threatening orthopedic injury.3,7,12,15 To reduce immediate and late complications, the standard of care includes open reduction and internal fixation after appropriate workup and multidisciplinary coordination. Multiple studies have reported excellent outcomes with an open reduction and all-suture internal fixation construct.5,13,14,16 Despite the small cohort size of these studies, nearly all patients returned to preinjury function with full participation in sports.

Several pearls and pitfalls for this procedure should be considered. First, we agree with other authors that coordination with the cardiothoracic team preoperatively is mandatory.9,13 Second, passing sutures into the epiphysis before fracture reduction enables greater ease with suture placement. Additionally, if passage of the suture needle through the chondroepiphysis is proving challenging, using the bovie to heat the tip of the needle allows passage of the needle more easily. Third, fracture reduction is often challenging, but the “shoehorn” technique with a Freer elevator is often successful. Furthermore, a gentle posteriorly directed force on the glenohumeral joint, with lateral traction, can assist in reduction. Fourth, be mindful of retractor placement during the drilling of transosseous tunnels into the metaphyseal fragment, as the risk for iatrogenic vascular injuries is greatest during this step.

Understanding the true risk of complications after operative management of this injury is challenging, given the paucity of data. The systematic review in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery published by Kendal et al 6 in 2018 examined 32 studies with complication data and found a cumulative complication rate of 16% (n = 14/90). The most common complications included recurrent instability and symptomatic hardware. 6

Conclusion

Three months after the procedure, the incision was well-healed and nontender. The patient demonstrated a full, painless range of motion. Radiographic imaging confirmed a union in anatomic position, and the patient was subsequently released to full, unrestricted activity.
