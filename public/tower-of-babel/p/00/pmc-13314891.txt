DMRT2 participates in chicken gonadal development by regulating CHUK and SOX9-mediated cell proliferation and apoptosis
Authors: Xiao Lin, Zidi Jin, Aneeqa Imtiaz, Jing Ge, Haizhou Gong, Yihui Zhang, Yun Zheng, Minmeng Zhao, Daoqing Gong, Long Liu, Tuoyu Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding sex determination mechanism is crucial for elucidating sexual evolution and sex control in animal husbandry. In this study, data showed that doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 2 (DMRT2) expression in gonads was developmentally regulated in a sex- and lateralization-specific manner. The nuclear localization signal of DMRT2 was identified, showing high expression in the female gonadal cortex. Transcriptome and chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) analyses screened and validated target genes (component of inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B kinase complex (CHUK) and SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9)) and downstream signaling pathways (e.g., sex determination, cell apoptosis, and inflammation related). It was also validated that DMRT2 could promote cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis. The effect of DMRT2 on apoptosis and inflammation was mediated by CHUK. These factors may potentially regulate apoptosis pathway and Wnt signaling pathway. In conclusion, DMRT2 participates in sex differentiation and development of chicken gonads by regulating proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammatory pathways through target genes such as CHUK and SOX9. The novel mechanism of DMRT2 regulating avian gonadal differentiation and development may deepen understanding of vertebrate sexual evolution and facilitate sex control in poultry.Keywords: Chicken, Gonad, Sexual differentiation, DMRT2, CHUK

Introduction

As a common avian model organism, chickens play an indispensable role in elucidating avian evolutionary patterns and vertebrate developmental biology. They are also vital economic animals, contributing to global food security by providing affordable and high-quality meat and egg products. The evolution of sex is a significant field within animal evolutionary research, and sex is closely linked to the production performance of economic animals. For instance, egg production requires only hens, not roosters. If sex control could prevent rooster production, it would enhance the economic efficiency of egg farming and improve animal welfare. Unraveling the mechanisms of sex determination in animals not only deepens our understanding of the evolutionary process of animal sex but also offers new insights for the production of single-sex animals. However, unlike mammals, whose sex is determined by the XY sex chromosome system, avian sex determination relies on the ZW system, representing an independent and highly divergent evolutionary trajectory. Currently, beyond the discovery that the doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 1 (DMRT1) gene (Smith et al., 2009) and microRNA 2954 (miR-2954) gene on the Z chromosome (Fallahshahroudi et al., 2025) are key factors in male sex determination in chickens, no other sex-determining genes have been identified. Overall, understanding of the mechanisms underlying sex determination and differentiation in birds remains limited, lagging significantly behind that of mammals.

Both known members of the DMRT family contain a conserved motif with DNA-binding capability, the DM domain, and are involved in Drosophila sex determination. DMRT1, doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 2 (DMRT2), and doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 3 (DMRT3) are located on chromosome 9 in the human genome and on the Z chromosome in the chicken genome. Their gene loci are adjacent, and their arrangement order is conserved between humans and chickens. In humans, DMRT family members not only participate in sex determination and differentiation but also contribute to the formation of various tissues and organs and the maintenance of their functions (Bellefroid et al., 2013). In avian species, studies indicate that the DMRT1 gene promotes the differentiation of male gonads into testes by activating the expression of downstream genes such as SRY-box transcription factor 9(SOX9), anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), and hemogen (HEMGN) (Lambeth et al., 2014).

DMRT2 is a protein-coding gene involved in embryonic somite formation, the development and differentiation of somites into vertebral and cutaneous myotomes, testis development, embryonic sex reversal, neurogenesis, and the activation of the myogenesis-determining gene myogenic factor 5 (MYF5) (Sato et al., 2010). In humans, DMRT2 is located within a critical region on chromosome 9 associated with gonadal dysgenesis and XY sex reversal, making it a candidate gene for sex determination (Raymond et al., 1999). Studies have shown that DMRT2 deficiency causes defects in mouse embryonic somite formation (Seo et al., 2006). In zebrafish, its paralogs, doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 2A (DMRT2A) and doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 2B (DMRT2B), regulate left-right patterning in the lateral mesoderm: DMRT2A is essential for symmetrical segment formation, while DMRT2B controls body segment differentiation and influences muscle development (Lourenço et al., 2010). Regarding gonadal development, DMRT2 exhibits sex-specific expression patterns across species. For example, in mandarin fish, DMRT2 expression is significantly higher in female gonads than in males between 36 and 42 days post-fertilization (Rong et al., 2025). In flathead flounder, DMRT2 shows higher expression in male gonads, particularly in germ cells, with low promoter methylation correlating with elevated mRNA levels (Zhu et al., 2019). These findings suggest the role of DMRT2 in testis differentiation, development, and germ cell maturation. Although DMRT2 plays a broad and important role in various biological processes, its function in chicken sex determination and differentiation remains unclear.

Given the key role of DMRT1 in male sex determination in chickens and the involvement of DMRT2 in gonadal development in other species, this study proposes the following scientific hypothesis: DMRT2 participates in the sex determination and differentiation process in chickens. The research focuses on the expression pattern of the DMRT2 gene during chicken gonadal growth and differentiation, as well as its functional mechanisms within gonadal cells. This includes examining changes in DMRT2 expression levels in the gonads during the embryonic stage, sex-specific differences, and asymmetries between the left and right gonads in females. Additionally, the study investigates the cellular localization of DMRT2, differential gene expression, and signaling pathway alterations induced by DMRT2 overexpression. It also involves screening and validating DMRT2 target genes via chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis, identifying and confirming DMRT2 transcriptional regulators using DNA pull-down technology, validating the functions of DMRT2 by knocking down its target genes, and performing in vivo validation of some cellular findings. In summary, this study aims to elucidate the role and mechanisms of DMRT2 in sex determination and differentiation during chicken embryonic gonadal development. The findings from this study may not only bridge the critical knowledge gap regarding the DMRT gene cluster in avian organogenesis but also provide an evolutionary insight into vertebrate sex differentiation and a potential strategy for sex control in the poultry industry.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (SYXK(Su)2021–0020).

Animal and sample collection

All eggs were sourced from Hy-Line Grey laying hens at Kangwei Farm (Yangzhou, China) and incubated at the Yangzhou University laboratory. They were incubated at 37.5°C and 60% humidity, with the blunt end facing upward, and rotated every 30 min until the desired embryonic stage was reached. At stages E6.5 (day 6.5 of incubation, before sex determination), E8.5, E12 (after sex determination), E15, E18, and E20 (complete gonadal morphogenesis), eggs were removed from the incubator, and embryos were dissected. Briefly, crack open the blunt end of the egg to expose the embryo, which was then removed and placed in a dish containing phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Subsequently, embryonic membranes were peeled away, abdominal skin was torn with forceps, and structures including gonads, liver, brain, leg muscles, and heart were collected. Concurrently, leg muscle tissue, wings, or toes from E6.5 and E8.5 chicken embryos were collected for subsequent RNA extraction with TRIzol (Cat. No. DP424; Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), protein analysis with RIPA Lysis buffer (Cat. No. C1053; Applygen Technologies Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), and genetic sex determination, respectively. Additionally, the gonadal-mesonephric complex was collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and reserved for subsequent paraffin sectioning and immunostaining.

Amino acid sequence alignment

Using DNAMAN software, we performed a sequence alignment analysis of the chicken DMRT2 amino acid sequence with those of DMRT2 from various species in the NCBI database. The species included: chicken (XP_046792506.1), human (NP_001374487), mouse (NP_665830), Canis lupus familiaris (XP_038448292.1), wild boar (XP_003480574), green sea turtle (XP_043403171), African lungfish (XP_043916762.1), warty-billed Swan (XP_040395865.1), barn Swallow (XP_039946349.1), mallard (XP_027302732.2).

Fadadrozozole (FAD) treatment of chicken embryos

FAD is an aromatase inhibitor that causes gonadal sex reversal in female embryos (Rahaie et al., 2018). Eggs were removed from the incubator at stage E2.5. The blunt end was wiped with a 75% alcohol swab. A small hole was made at the blunt end using forceps. Then, 0.2 mg of FAD (Cat. No. HY-14247A; MedChemexpress Biotechnology, Inc., New Jersey, USA) dissolved in 100 μL of PBS (containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin) was injected into the air sac. The egg was sealed with medical tape and returned to the incubator until stage E12. Gonadal samples were subsequently collected as described above for RNA (n = 6) or protein (n = 4) extraction, and nephridioplexus complexes were harvested from the gonads for immunostaining.

Estradiol (E2) treatment of chicken embryos

To establish a male-to-female sex reversal model, 17β-estradiol (Cat. No. HY-B0141; MedChemexpress Biotechnology, Inc., New Jersey, USA) was injected into the blunt end of E2.5-stage chicken embryos. The procedure was as follows: 17β-estradiol was first dissolved in 100% ethanol at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, then diluted with sesame oil to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Embryos in the experimental group received 100 µL of the 1 mg/mL solution (containing 0.1 mg of 17β-estradiol) injected into the blunt end, while the control group received an equal volume of sesame oil containing 10% ethanol. Before injection, a small hole was made in the blunt end of the egg to allow injection into the air chamber. The eggs were then resealed and re-incubated until stage E12. Subsequently, gonadal samples were collected for RNA extraction (n = 6) or protein extraction (n = 4), and the gonadal nephridioplexus was harvested for immunostaining.

Determination of the genetic sex of chicken embryos

Genomic DNA was extracted from leg muscle tissue, wings, or toes to serve as a template. TaKaRa Mighty Amp DNA Polymerase (Cat. No. DR070; Baorizhi Medical Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to amplify the chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein (CHD) gene sequence located on the sex chromosomes. Embryo sex was determined based on the distinct band sizes observed on the Z and W chromosomes (Z chromosome: 506 bp; W chromosome: 351 bp). The CHD forward and reverse primer sequences were as follows: CHD-F: AGTGCATTGCAGAAGCAATATT; CHD-R: GCCTCCTGTTTATTATAGAATTCAT. Results showed that female (ZW) individuals exhibited two bands at 506 and 351 bp, while male (ZZ) individuals displayed a single band at 506 bp.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR

Following the kit instructions, total RNA was extracted from gonads and other tissues using TRIzol Universal Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Cat. No. DP424; Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). For E6.5 embryos, total RNA was extracted from six sets of gonads per sex (5 pairs of gonads from the same sex pooled into one set; n = 6). For E12 embryos, total RNA was extracted from six pairs of gonads per sex (n = 6). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR reverse transcription kit (Cat. No. R123–01; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed using the Vazyme AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix kit (Cat. No. Q111–02/03; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The chicken hydroxymethylbilane synthase gene (HMBS) (Liu et al., 2019) served as the internal control for the spatiotemporal expression of DMRT2 in chicken embryos. Beta-actin (β-Actin) or glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) genes were used as internal controls for the remaining experiments. Primer sequences for qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Following the protocol, nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractions were extracted from E15 chicken gonads (n = 3) using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent kit (Cat. No. 78833; Thermo Scientific Co., Ltd., Boston, MA, USA). Total gonadal proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer (Cat. No. C1053; Applygen Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and protein concentrations were determined using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat. No. P0010; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Immunoblotting was performed as previously described (Geng et al., 2015). Briefly, 10 μg of protein samples were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature (15–25°C) for 1 h, then imaged. Scanned images were analyzed using Image Studio Lite v.5.2. Primary antibodies used in this study included DMRT2 antibody (Cat. No. orb183127, Wuhan Boote Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), Lamin B (Cat. No. ab133741, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), β-Actin antibody (Cat. No. bsm-33036M, Boosun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and GAPDH antibody (Cat. No. bs-0755R, Boosun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used as a control.

Paraffin sectioning and immunostaining

For histological analysis, the complex renal tissues were placed in 4% paraformal-dehyde for 24 h. Next, the tissue shape was adjusted under a stereomicroscope. Subsequently, the tissue and corresponding labels were placed in a dehydration cassette, de-hydrated with low to high ethanol concentrations, treated with xylene, and cleared for paraffin embedding. The embedded tissues were serially sliced to a thickness of 3 μm. Next, the slices were floated on a spreader of 40°C warm water to spread the tissues. After, the tissues were fished up with slides, baked in an oven at 60°C, dried, and removed for storage at room temperature. Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described (Zhao et al., 2010). Briefly, the slides were washed in PBS at 37°C for 30 min and closed in PBS containing 10% donkey serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.3% Triton X-100 at 24°C for 2 h. The slides were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C, washed in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 before incubation, and then incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Lastly, the slides were washed in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, and sections were treated with Hoechst solution (10 mg/mL) for 5 min to stain cell nuclei. The working concentrations of the antibodies used were as follows: DMRT2 antibody, 5 mg/ml; and Hoechst 33342 (Cat. No. C1025; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), 1:100.

Cells were seeded into culture plates with coverslips and allowed to adhere and form cell monolayers. The vector was then transfected into the cells. After 24 h, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Cat. No. P0099; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 15 min, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Cat. No. ST1723; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 20 min and washed three times with PBS. The cells were blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. Following blocking, cells were immunolabeled with DMRT2 antibody diluted 1:200 or other specific antibodies, which were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by three washes with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit antibody labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) diluted 1:200 (Cat. No. A0423; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor® 568) antibody (Cat. No. ab175471; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at room temperature for 1 h. For nuclear staining, incubate immunolabeled cells with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Cat. No. C1025; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), washed three times with PBS, and finally mounted with an anti-fluorescence quencher (Cat. No. P0126; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Observe cells using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Construction of overexpression vectors

Based on the coding region of chicken DMRT2 (NCBI gene ID: 100858556, accession number: XM_003642987.6), Shanghai Jima Co., Ltd. designed and constructed DMRT2 overexpression plasmids and their deletion/truncation mutant plasmids (DMRT2-504, DMRT2-537, and DMRT2-△NLS). Additionally, based on the coding regions of chicken component of inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B kinase complex (CHUK) (NCBI gene ID: 423669, accession number: XM_015278675.4), shRNA-expressing lentiviral vectors were designed and constructed by China Nanjing CoreBio Technology Co., Ltd. Based on the coding region of chicken nuclear factor I C (NFIC) (NCBI Gene ID: 396208, accession number NM_205271.2), the NFIC overexpression plasmid was designed by Nanjing Core-S Bioscience Co., Ltd., China.

Isolation and processing of chicken embryo gonadal cells

On E12 (right after sex determination, early stage of gonadal development, n = 6), cells from the female and male gonads were isolated from chicken embryos using colla-genase (Cat. No. 17104019; Gibco, New York, USA). Briefly, E12 chicken embryo gonads were collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes with PBS. After aspirating and discarding PBS, 500 μL of prewarmed collagenase was added, and the tissues were pipetted and heated at 37°C for 30 s. This step was repeated until the gonads became invisible. Subsequently, 800 μL of 10% bovine serum (FBS)-Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) was added to the digestion solution, mixed by pipetting, and centrifuged at 700 × g for 5 min at room temperature. After aspirating and discarding the supernatant, we added 1 mL of PBS to resuspend and wash the cells, which were centrifuged at 700 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Next, the supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of complete medium (DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 IU/mL), and 10 μL epidermal growth factor (EGF) (20 ng/mL) was added, and the mixture was filtered into a 50 mL conical tube using 70 μm nylon mesh. After counting the cells, a complete medium was added to plate and culture the cells at 1 × 106 cells/well. The cells were then transfected with DMRT2 overexpression plasmid and empty vector plasmid at 70% cell fusion using Lipofectamine 2000(Cat. No. 11668–027, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Briefly, 4 μL of Lipofectamine 2000, DMRT2 overexpression plasmid and empty vector plasmid were diluted with 96 μL Opti-MEM. Next, the diluted Lipofectamine 2000 was mixed with diluted DMRT2 overexpression plasmid and empty vector and left to stand at room temperature for 23 min. The mixture was then added to the cultured cells. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were washed twice with PBS and then collected with 1 mL of TRIzol reagent or 100 μL of RIPA lysate and subjected to phenotypic assays.

Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from cells transfected with DMRT2 overexpression vectors and empty vectors, then sent to Novogene for transcriptome sequencing analysis. The quantity and quality of total RNA samples were assessed using Qubit 2.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), respectively. Qualified total RNA samples were subsequently used to construct cDNA libraries via reverse transcription. Briefly, eukaryotic mRNA samples rich in dT-binding oligonucleotides were loaded onto magnetic beads, followed by random fragmentation of mRNA in fragment buffer. The first cDNA strand was synthesized using a six-base random primer (random hexamer). Subsequently, the second cDNA strand was synthesized by adding reaction buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA polymerase I to the previous solution. The cDNA samples were purified using Ampur XP beads. cDNA ends were repaired, sequencing adapters were ligated, and appropriate fragment sizes were selected using Ampur XP beads to construct a cDNA library enriched by PCR. The cDNA library quality was then assessed and sequenced using synthetic technology on the Illumina HiSeq X-ten high-throughput sequencing platform. Clean data with Phred scores of Q30 or higher were selected for further analysis based on computational evaluation of the large volume of raw data. Annotation of clean data was performed using the HISAT2 system by aligning against the chicken reference genome sequence. Individual genes (or single genes) were assembled by comparing read results with the StringTie program. Expression of individual genes was calculated by normalizing reads to the Fragments Per Kilobase Transcript/Million Fragments (FPKM) plot. Differential expression analysis between the two comparison groups was performed using DESeq software (v.1.20.0). DESeq was employed to screen differentially expressed genes based on the following criteria: |log2FoldChange| > 1 and a significance threshold of P-value < 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using topGO, calculating P-values via the hypergeometric distribution method (significance threshold: P-value < 0.05). These identified GO terms are significantly enriched by differentially expressed genes, thereby determining their primary biological functions. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes enrichment analysis (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler (v.3.4.4), focusing on pathways with P-values < 0.05.

Cell phenotype

The proliferative ability of chicken embryonic gonadal cells was assessed using CCK-8 (Cat. No. A311-02; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells transfected with DMRT2 overexpression vector or negative control vector were inoculated in 96-well plates (100 μL/well) at 2 × 103/well, and five replicate wells were set up for each group. The cells were incubated for 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h with 10 μL CCK-8 in each well, and the absorbance (A450) value of each well was measured at 450 nm by enzyme marker after incubation for 2 h at 37°C. The background A450 value was removed by zeroing the blank control group, and the cell growth curve was statistically analyzed and plotted. Flow cytometry was used to detect apoptosis in chicken embryonic gonadal cells, and DMRT2 overexpression vector or negative control vector was transfected according to the instructions of the cell Apoptosis Kit (Cat. No. A211; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Untreated cells were used as a negative control. Additionally, for flow cytometry detection, annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) monochromes were required to regulate compensation. After 48 h, cells in each group were digested with trypsin without ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and collected. Next, the cells were washed twice with precooled PBS, and then 100 µL of 1 × binding buffer was added and gently blown into a single-cell suspension. In addition, annexin V-FITC and PI staining were performed on the samples for flow cytometry detection to regulate compensation. After transfection with the DMRT2 overexpression vector for 48 h, the cells in each group were digested with trypsin without EDTA and collected. The cells were washed twice with precooled PBS and then gently blown into a single-cell suspension with 100 µL of 1 × binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI staining solution were added at room temperature (20–25°C) for 10 min, followed by 400 μL of 1 × binding buffer, and the mixture was blended gently. The samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h of staining, and the apoptosis distribution map was statistically analyzed and drawn. According to the manufacturer’s instructions for the kit (Cat. No. C1052; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), the cell cycle of chicken embryonic gonad cells was detected by flow cytometry. Briefly, the cells in each group were collected by trypsin digestion after transfection with the DMRT2 overexpression and negative control vectors for 48 h. After 1 mL of precooled PBS was added to resuspended cells, 1 mL of 70% ethanol was added to fix the cells at 4°C for 12 h and then precooled 1 mL PBS was added to resuspended cells. Lastly, 0.5 mL of PI staining solution was added the pre-prepared. After the resuspended cells were precipitated and incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 min, the cell cycle distribution was detected by flow cytometry.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation combined with high-throughput sequencing technology

Grind chicken embryonic gonadal tissue into a powder. Cross-link and fix the tissue with 37% formaldehyde solution, then terminate the reaction with glycine. Thoroughly lyse the cells using a mixture of cell lysis buffer and ChIP buffer. Sonicate the lysate on ice until DNA fragments reach approximately 200 – 500 base pairs. Centrifuge the lysate at 4°C and 10,000 g for 10 min; collect the supernatant. Measure the concentration of a portion of the supernatant by performing decrosslinking and DNA recovery. Dilute the remaining supernatant with ChIP buffer to adjust the concentration to 40 μg/μL. Transfer 490 μL to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, labeling them as the IgG group and the IP group. Separately, dilute 10 μL supernatant in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube with 140 μL ultrapure water as the Input control group. Aliquot the remaining supernatant and store it at −80°C. Add 4 μL of DMRT2 antibody to the immunoprecipitation (IP) group and 4 μL of IgG antibody to the IgG group. Mix thoroughly and incubate gently at 4°C overnight. Add pre-treated magnetic beads and incubate at 4°C for 2 h. Wash the beads with wash buffer, then add Elution Buffer and incubate at room temperature with gentle rotation for 15 min. Collect the supernatant and add NaCl to each tube to release decovalent binding over 3 h. Purify and recover DNA using a DNA purification and recovery kit (Cat. No. DC301-01; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Finally, send the purified DNA samples to Novogene for high-throughput sequencing analysis.

Raw fluorescence image files obtained from the Illumina platform were converted into short reads (raw data) through base calling. These short reads were recorded in FASTQ format (Cock et al., 2010), which contains sequence information along with corresponding sequencing quality metrics. Sequence artifacts—including reads with adapter contamination, low-quality reads, and unrecognized nucleotides (N) undoubtedly hinder reliable downstream bioinformatics analysis. Therefore, quality control is an essential step to ensure meaningful subsequent analysis. We employed fastp (v.0.23.1) for basic statistical quality assessment of raw reads (Chen et al., 2018). The data processing steps were as follows: discard read pairs exhibiting adapter contamination; discard read pairs where either read contains more than 10% unresolved bases; and discard read pairs if either read contains more than 50% low-quality bases (Phred quality score < 5). Reference genomes and gene model annotation files were downloaded directly from genome databases. The reference genome index was constructed using BWA (v.0.7.12), and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA (Li et al., 2009). At specific binding sites, significant enrichment of reads occurs. For paired-end sequencing, the insertion fragment size (estimated fragment length) is predicted and used for subsequent peak identification.

After aligning the sequencing reads to the reference genome, we used MACS2 (v.2.2.7.1) for peak identification to detect IP-enriched regions relative to the background (Zhang et al., 2008). All datasets employed a q-value threshold of 0.05. Following peak identification, we visualized distributions of chromosomal coverage, peak width, enrichment fold, significance level, and peak-to-peak distance for each peak. Interactions between transcription factors or histone modifications and DNA exhibit sequence-specific preferences rather than random distribution. We utilized Homer to detect de novo sequence motifs and to match known motifs. Peak vertex positions near gene transcription start sites can predict protein-gene interaction sites. ChIPseeker was used to identify the nearest genes to peaks and to annotate the genomic regions of the peaks (Yu et al., 2015). ChIPseeker also enabled the identification of genes associated with peaks, followed by GO enrichment analysis to identify functionally enriched terms. GO enrichment analysis was performed using the GOseq R package, which corrects for gene length bias. GO terms with corrected P-values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched among peak-associated genes. KEGG is a database resource for understanding higher-level functions and utilities of biological systems (e.g., cells, organisms, ecosystems) from molecular-level information, particularly large-scale molecular datasets generated through genome sequencing and other high-throughput experimental techniques. We employed KOBAS software to examine the statistical enrichment of genes associated with peaks in KEGG pathways. Different peak analyses were based on the enrichment fold of peaks across various experiments. Using the same methodology, genes associated with different peaks were identified and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. For data with biological replicates, DiffBind software was used for differential peak analysis; for data without biological replicates, edgeR software was employed for differential peak analysis.

Prediction and conservative analysis of DMRT2 nuclear localization signal

To preliminarily determine the location of the nuclear localization signal (NLS) in DMRT2, the full amino acid sequence of DMRT2 was analyzed using an online prediction website. The prediction results indicated the presence of an NLS sequence. Sequence alignment was performed for DMRT2 from various species, including Homo sapiens (XP_016869703.1), chicken (XP_003643035.3), goose (XP_047907662.1), duck (XP_0273302732.2), mouse (NP_665830.1), Bos taurus (XP_005210039), Sus scrofa (XP_003480574), Canis lupus familiaris (XP_032489806.1), and rabbit (XP_002708232.1).

ChIP-qPCR assays

To validate the enrichment of target proteins at specific genomic sites, this study performed chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) analysis. Specifically, ChIP experiments were conducted using both the target protein-specific antibody and isotype control IgG. Subsequently, using the obtained Input, IP, and IgG samples as templates, qPCR amplification was performed under identical reaction conditions with primers designed for specific enrichment regions (primer sequences detailed in Table S2). Data calculation employed the classical ΔΔCt method for relative quantification. The enrichment fold-change was calculated as follows: Enrichment factor=2^(-ΔΔCt), where ΔΔCt=ΔCt (ChIP-target)–ΔCt (ChIP-IgG). This formula calculates the fold difference between the target ChIP product and the negative control (IgG), representing how many times the DNA pulled down by the target protein antibody exceeds the background value pulled down by IgG.

DNA pull-down assay and mass spectrometry

Experiments were conducted using the DNA pull-down kit (animal) (Cat. No. JKR23006A; genecreate.cn, Wuhan, China) following these steps: E18-stage chicken embryonic gonadal tissue was ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, transferred to a pre-chilled homogenizer, and lysed on ice for 10 min with PMSF-containing lysis buffer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4°C and 1500 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Nuclear extraction buffer was added to the pellet, which was then resuspended and centrifuged at 4°C and 16,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected as the nucleoprotein extract. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. Eighty microliters of streptavidin magnetic beads were washed with 1 × TES buffer to remove impurities. Add 4 μg biotin-labeled DNA probe to the washed beads and gently incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow DNA binding to the beads. After incubation, magnetic separation was performed, and the supernatant was discarded. Seventy to one hundred micrograms of nucleoprotein extract and binding buffer were added, and the mixture was gently inverted at 4°C for 16 h to form the DNA-protein complex. Following incubation, magnetic separation was performed, and the supernatant containing unbound proteins was discarded. The beads were then washed five times with pre-chilled protein dilution buffer to remove non-specifically bound proteins. To ensure experimental specificity, a control group was established to exclude false positives. After the binding reaction and washing, 20 μL of 6 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added to the magnetic bead complex, which was heated at 99°C for 10 min. Following magnetic separation, the supernatant was collected for silver staining and protein identification via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry.

For mass spectrometry analysis, the supernatant samples were first separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue dye. The gel was then cut, destained and dehydrated with triethylammonium bicarbonate (Cat. No. T7408-500ML, Sigma, MI, USA) and acetonitrile (Cat. No. A955-4, Thermo Fisher Chemical, MA, USA). Subsequently, DL-dithiothreitol (Cat. No. D9163-25G, Sigma, MI, USA) was added to the gel for reduction, and iodoacetamide (Cat. No. I6125-25G, Sigma, MI, USA). Subsequently, triethylammonium bicarbonate, trypsin (Cat. No. V5280, Promega, Madison, USA), CaCl2, acetonitrile, and formic acid (Cat. No. A117-50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were applied to the gel for in-gel digestion. The digested protein samples were collected, desalted, and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with a C18 column. After peptides were separated using Orbitrap Exploris 480 equipped with high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (Thermo Fisher) and Nanospray Flex™ ion source. The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed at Hangzhou Guangke Ande Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Based on protein database, all the resulting spectra were identified using Proteome Discoverer 2.2 (PD 2.2, Thermo Fisher) search engine. To improve the accuracy, the search results were further filtered, and only the proteins with credible and matched peptide spectra with FDR no more than 1.0% were retained. Finally, the identified proteins were subjected to functional annotations, including GO, KEGG pathway, and other database annotations (Ashburner et al., 2000, Kanehisa et al., 2004). The protein database used was 1301693led.fasta, containing 47,254 sequences.

Protein silver staining analysis

The experiment was conducted using the Silver Stain Kit (Cat. No. JKR23005; genecreate.cn, Wuhan, China) with the following this procedure: After separating protein samples via SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, place the gel in a 12 cm diameter glass dish and gently wash it with deionized water at room temperature for 5 min. Discard the water, then add the fixing solution (composition: 17.5 mL anhydrous ethanol, 3.5 mL acetic acid, 14 mL deionized water). Gently agitate at room temperature for 30 min, then discard the fixing solution. Gently wash with deionized water at room temperature for 20 min, replacing the water every 10 min. Extend the duration or increase the number of washes as needed to reduce background staining. Add freshly prepared sensitizing solution (composition: 31.5 mL deionized water, 3.5 mL sensitizing solution I, 70 μL sensitizing solution II, 100 μL sensitizing solution III). Shake gently in deionized water at room temperature for 30 min. Discard the sensitizing solution, then wash gently twice with deionized water at room temperature, each wash lasting 2 min. Add freshly prepared staining solution (35 mL deionized water, 0.35 mL silver solution, 30 μL developing solution II) and shake gently at room temperature for 20 min. Finally, discard the staining solution and wash twice with deionized water at room temperature, each for 1 min. Re-add freshly prepared developing solution (31.5 mL deionized water, 3.5 mL developing solution I, 15 μL developing solution II). Shake gently at room temperature for approximately 2 min. Replace the developing solution with fresh solution once it becomes cloudy. Continued development until the target protein bands are clearly visible. Immediately terminate the reaction by thoroughly rinsing the gel with deionized water. Proceed with image acquisition.

Preparation of biotin-labeled probe

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of the chicken DMRT2 gene, we first designed and synthesized primers targeting the 1000 bp promoter region upstream of its transcription start site. Using chicken genomic DNA as a template, we amplified the target promoter fragment via PCR with a high-fidelity DNA polymerase. This fragment was ligated into the pMD19-T vector, and the ligation product was transformed into DH5α competent cells. The transformed bacterial culture was spread onto LB plates containing ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37°C, followed by picking of a single colony. Positive clones were preliminarily screened via colony PCR. Plasmid DNA was extracted and Sanger sequenced. After sequence and orientation verification, vector construction was successfully completed. Subsequently, probe labeling was performed using the Biotin Random Primer DNA Labeling Kit (Cat. No. D3118; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). First, the DNA template was mixed with ultrapure water to a total volume of 34 µL. Then, 10 µL of Random Primer in buffer was added, mixed thoroughly, and heated at 100°C for 5 min before cooling in an ice-water bath. Next, 5 µL of Biotin-Labeling Mix and 1 µL of Klenow enzyme were added to the reaction mixture, which was incubated at 37°C for 18 h. After the reaction, 3 µL of Termination Buffer was added, and the labeled product was stored at −20°C for later use. Following labeling, appropriate gradient dilutions of the probe were prepared and detected using the Chemiluminescent Biotin-Labeled Nucleic Acid Detection Kit. The procedure was as follows: dissolved the blocking solution and wash buffer. Block the membrane with 15 mL of blocking solution on a shaking platform for 15 min. Subsequently, add blocking solution containing diluted Streptavidin-HRP and continue incubation for another 15 min. Then, wash the membrane four times with diluted wash solution. After equilibrating the nylon membrane in detection equilibration buffer for 5 min, add freshly prepared BeyoECL Moon working solution and incubate for 3 min. Finally, image the membrane using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as the means ± the standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test for two-group comparison. For comparisons across three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc test was performed. The probability (P) < 0.05 was chosen as a priori indicative of statistical significance.

Similarity between chicken DMRT2 and DMRT1 amino acid sequences

The similarity between the amino acid sequences of chicken DMRT1 and DMRT2 was analyzed using DNAMAN (v.9.0) software. As shown in Fig. 1, the similarity is low (17.01%). This low similarity suggests that the chicken DMRT2 protein may have functions that differ significantly from those of the chicken DMRT1 protein.

Conservation analysis of DMRT1 amino acid sequences across vertebrates

We conducted a homology comparison analysis of amino acid sequences for the DMRT2 protein across 10 vertebrate species using DNAMAN (v.9.0) software. The species analyzed included chicken (Gallus gallus), human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus), pig (Sus scrofa), domestic dog (Canis lupus familiaris), green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas), African lungfish (Protopterus annectens), barn swallow (Hirundo rustica), mallard (Anas platyrhynchos), and mute swan (Cygnus olor). As shown in Fig. 2, the chicken DMRT2 amino acid sequence exhibits the highest homology with the mallard at 87.9% and also shows high similarity with the mute swan and barn swallow (85.4% and 81.5%, respectively). The lowest homology was observed with human DMRT2, at only 65.3%. Notably, regions of high sequence conservation were located within the DM domain. These findings indicate that, although DMRT2 protein sequences vary significantly across species due to evolutionary divergence, the conserved DM domain suggests that DMRT2 may have similar functions and mechanisms of action across different species.

Changes in mRNA and protein expression levels of DMRT2 in chicken gonads with the time of embryonic development

Quantitative PCR was used to detect the mRNA expression levels of DMRT2 in the left gonads of male and female chicken embryos at stages E6, E8.5, E12, E15, and E20. These results indicate that both mRNA and protein expression levels of DMRT2 increase with gonadal development in chicken embryos, peaking in late development. This suggests that DMRT2 plays a more critical role in the late stages of gonadal development.

The difference in expression level of DMRT2 in embryonic gonads between sexes of chicken

By comparing the mRNA expression levels of DMRT2 in the left gonads of chicken embryos at different embryonic stages (E6.5, E8.5, E12, E15, and E20) between sexes, it was found that the mRNA expression levels of DMRT2 in male embryos at E6.5 and E8.5 were lower than those in females, with the difference at E6.5 reaching statistical significance. In contrast, at E12, E15, and E20, male expression was significantly higher than female expression (Fig. 5A). Analysis of DMRT2 protein expression levels in the left gonads at E6.5, E12, and E15 revealed that DMRT2 protein expression was significantly higher in male gonads than in females at E6.5 (Fig. 5B). At E12, this trend reversed, with significantly higher protein expression in female gonads compared to males (Fig. 5C). However, no significant difference was observed at E15 (Fig. 5D). These results indicate that DMRT2 participates in gonadal sex differentiation in chicken embryos, and its early protein levels are regulated by post-transcriptional mechanisms.

Effects of FAD and E2 treatments on DMRT2 expressions in chicken embryonic gonads

Given the sex-specific expression pattern of DMRT2 in chicken embryonic gonads, this study investigated the role of DMRT2 in gonadal sex differentiation by inducing sex reversal in embryos. The expression distribution of DMRT2 protein across chicken embryonic gonads

Sections of male and female chicken embryonic gonadal tissues at different stages (E6.5, E12, and E18) were stained using DMRT2 protein antibodies and Hoechst dye. The results showed that DMRT2 protein was uniformly expressed in both female and male chicken embryonic gonads at E6.5. At E12, DMRT2 expression differed between male and female gonads as gonadal tissue developed and differentiated. Specifically, while DMRT2 was expressed in both the medulla and cortex of female gonads, it was predominantly localized in the cortex. In males, DMRT2 was almost uniformly distributed throughout the entire gonadal tissue. The pattern observed at E18 was similar to that at E12 (Fig. 8A). To further investigate the association between DMRT2 protein distribution and sex, this study examined changes in DMRT2 expression patterns within the gonads of FAD-treated female embryos and E2-treated male embryos. Similar to normal male embryonic gonads, DMRT2 protein was distributed almost uniformly throughout the gonadal tissue in FAD-treated female chicken embryos (Fig. 8B). Conversely, similar to normal female embryonic gonads, DMRT2 protein was expressed in both the medulla and cortex of E2-treated male chicken embryonic gonads but was predominantly distributed in the cortex (Fig. 8C). Notably, when examining E18 female and male chicken embryonic gonads, DMRT2 protein was found to exist in both the nuclei and cytoplasm of gonadal cells in the female cortical region. In contrast, DMRT2 protein predominantly existed in the nuclei in the female medullary region. However, no such distribution pattern was observed in male gonads (Fig. 8D). This indicates that DMRT2 exhibits distinct functional mechanisms in cortical and medullary cells.

Subcellular localization of DMRT2 protein in chicken gonadal cells

To investigate the subcellular distribution of DMRT2, immunoblotting was performed to detect DMRT2 protein levels in different fractions after nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation of E18 male and female chicken gonads. The results showed that cytoplasmic DMRT2 protein levels were significantly higher than nuclear levels in both male and female gonads, and cytoplasmic DMRT2 level in female gonad was significantly higher than that in male gonad (Fig. 9A). To identify NLS of DMRT2, bioinformatics analysis of the full-length amino acid sequence of chicken DMRT2 revealed a potential NLS located at amino acid residues 169–180 (Fig. 9B). Sequence alignment of DMRT2 from different species demonstrated high conservation of the predicted NLS sequence (Fig. 9C). To validate the role of this NLS in DMRT2 nuclear localization, two DMRT2 mutant overexpression vectors were constructed and transfected into primary chicken gonadal cells: one lacking the NLS and its downstream sequence (DMRT2-504), and another retaining the NLS but lacking the downstream sequence (DMRT2-537) (Fig. 9D). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, immunofluorescence analysis assessed the impact of NLS deletion on DMRT2 nuclear localization. The results showed that cells overexpressing the DMRT2-504 mutant exhibited a significant absence of nuclear fluorescence signals, whereas cells overexpressing the DMRT2-537 mutant retained distinct nuclear fluorescence signals (Fig. 9E). These findings indicate that the NLS functions as the signal sequence mediating DMRT2 protein nuclear localization, suggesting that DMRT2 may act as a transcription factor within the nucleus.

Identification of genes and pathways affected by DMRT2 overexpression in gonadal cells

To investigate the downstream regulatory genes and pathways of the DMRT2 gene, DMRT2 gene was overexpressed in primary gonadal cells derived from E12 female chicken embryos and analyzed DMRT2 expression levels using qPCR and Western blot. The results showed that, compared to the empty vector control group, both mRNA and protein expression levels of DMRT2 were significantly increased in gonadal cells transfected with the DMRT2 overexpression vector, confirming the successful construction of the overexpression vector (Fig. 10A, B). Subsequently, transcriptome sequencing analysis of left gonadal cells from female chicken embryos transfected with these vectors identified 3439 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Among these, 1580 DEGs were significantly upregulated, and 1859 DEGs were significantly downregulated relative to the control group (Fig. S4). To validate the reliability of the transcriptome data, 12 DEGs were randomly selected for qPCR validation. These included 6 upregulated genes: prokineticin 2 (PROK2), pleckstrin homology domain containing, family N member 1 (PLEKHN1), fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10), follistatin-like 3 (FSTL3), uromodulin-like 1 (UMODL1), and LIM homeobox 6 (LHX6), and 6 downregulated genes: SET and MYND domain-containing 1 (SMYD1), proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 4 (PCSK4), immunoglobulin superfamily member 10 (IGSF10), embigin (EMB), tubulin alpha-like 3 (TUBAL3), and apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1). qPCR results confirmed that the expression trends of 11 genes were consistent with the transcriptome analysis, indicating that the transcriptome analysis was generally reliable (Fig. 10C). Additionally, qPCR detection of mRNA expression levels for the sex-determining genes DMRT1, SOX9, forkhead box L2 (FOXL2), and cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily a member 1 (CYP19A1) aligned with transcriptomic analysis results (Figs. 10D and S5), suggesting that DMRT2 may participate in gonadal sex determination. GO enrichment analysis of DEGs identified by transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment in biological processes including ribosomal structural components, ribosomal subunits, regulation of molecular function, phosphorus metabolism, and regulation of protein metabolism (Fig. S6). KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs showed enrichment in apoptosis, NOD-like receptor signaling, p53 signaling, and necroptosis pathways (Fig. 10E). To validate whether DMRT2 regulates the expression of apoptosis and inflammation/immunity-related genes, this study randomly selected 13 DEGs for qPCR validation. These included phorbol-12-Myristate-13-Acetate-Induced Protein 1 (PMAIP1), BCL2-Like 10 (BCL2L10), MCL1 Apoptosis Regulator, BCL2 Family Member (MCL1), NF-κB Inhibitor Alpha (NFKBIA), nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1 (NFKB1), interleukin 1 beta (IL1B), interleukin 6 (IL6), TNF Receptor Associated Factor 2 (TRAF2), receptor Interacting Serine/threonine kinase 2 (RIPK2), jun Proto-Oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (JUN). qPCR results showed that the expression trends of 12 genes were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results (Fig. 10F). This suggests that DMRT2, as a transcription regulator, may participate in gonadal development and sex determination by modulating pathways such as apoptosis.

Effects of DMRT2 overexpression on cell proliferation and apoptosis in gonadal cells

To further validate the effects of DMRT2 overexpression on apoptosis and proliferation, this study assessed cell proliferation capacity and apoptosis levels in female gonadal cells following DMRT2 overexpression. The results demonstrated a significant increase in cell proliferation at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection (Fig. 10G), accompanied by changes in cell cycle distribution: an increased proportion of cells in G2 phase and decreased proportions in G1 and S phases (Fig. 10H). Additionally, apoptosis levels were markedly reduced (Fig. 10I). These findings further confirm the regulatory role of DMRT2 in gonadal cell proliferation and apoptosis, specifically promoting proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis.

Identification of DMRT2 target genes by ChIP

Using chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) technology and DMRT2-specific antibodies, this study systematically screened the target genes of DMRT2 in embryonic gonadal tissue at the E18 stage, which is followed by pathway enrichment analysis. The results revealed significant enrichment of candidate target genes in the apoptosis pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, oocyte meiosis pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 11A), suggesting that DMRT2, as a transcription factor, may participate in chicken gonadal development and differentiation through these target genes. Based on this, a joint analysis of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data was subsequently performed. The results indicated that the core regulatory network of DMRT2 involves the MAPK signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, and cellular senescence pathway. Given the significance of these pathways, CHUK, insulin like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R), SET domain containing 7, histone lysine methyltransferase (SETD7), and homeodomain interacting protein kinase 2 (HIPK2) were selected for further validation and functional studies. qPCR analysis following DMRT2 overexpression confirmed that CHUK, SETD7, and HIPK2 are target genes of DMRT2 (Fig. 11B). Furthermore, considering SOX9 as a key gene involved in male gonadal sex determination and its identification in both RNA-seq and ChIP-seq analyses, qPCR after DMRT2 overexpression also confirmed that SOX9 is a target gene of DMRT2 (Fig. 10D). This suggests that DMRT2 participates in chicken gonadal sex determination by directly regulating SOX9 expression.

To validate whether DMRT2 specifically binds to the promoter regions of the SOX9 and CHUK genes, this study confirmed through ChIP-qPCR analysis that DMRT2 indeed binds to the promoter regions of these two genes (Fig. 11C, D). To determine whether CHUK and SOX9 expression is influenced by DMRT2 nuclear localization, we successfully constructed overexpression vectors for wild-type DMRT2 (DMRT2-WT) and a mutant lacking its nuclear localization signal (NLS) (DMRT2-MU) (Fig. 11E). The DMRT2-MU and DMRT2-WT overexpression vectors, along with an empty vector, were separately transfected into gonadal cells. Western blot analysis after overexpression revealed significantly higher protein expression levels of DMRT2 in the DMRT2-WT group compared to the DMRT2-MU group, consistent with increased FLAG-tagged protein in the DMRT2-WT group, confirming successful overexpression (Fig. 11F–H). Under these conditions, DMRT2-WT overexpression resulted in significantly increased mRNA expression levels of SOX9 and CHUK compared to the DMRT2-MU group (Fig. 11I, J). These findings further confirm that DMRT2, upon nuclear localization via its nuclear localization signal, binds to the promoter sequences of its target genes SOX9 and CHUK to regulate their transcription.

The target gene, CHUK, mediates the effects of DMRT2

To elucidate the biological functions mediated by DMRT2 through its target gene CHUK, this study selected three significantly enriched signaling pathways including apoptosis, Toll-like receptor signaling, and NOD-like receptor signaling, based on transcriptome analysis of cells transfected with DMRT2 overexpression vectors. DMRT2 overexpression significantly upregulated mRNA expression levels of differentially expressed genes associated with these pathways, including apoptosis-related genes PMAIP1 and BCL2L10, and inflammation-related genes IL1B and NFKBIA (Fig. 10F). Following 52% knockdown of CHUK expression using shCHUK-1, mRNA levels of PMAIP1, BCL2L10, IL1B, IL6, NFKB1, and NFKBIA significantly increased (Fig. 11K, L). These results suggest that DMRT2 influences apoptosis and inflammation-related signaling pathways by directly activating its target gene CHUK while negatively regulating the expression of downstream genes. Notably, DMRT2 overexpression induced the expression of these genes regardless of CHUK knockdown status.

Screening and verification of transcription factors of DMRT2 by DNA pulldown assay

To elucidate the upstream transcriptional regulatory mechanism of DMRT2, this study employed DNA pulldown-mass spectrometry (DNA pulldown-MS) to screen for potential transcription factors binding to the DMRT2 promoter region. Proteins bound to biotin-labeled DNA probes were isolated via SDS-PAGE and silver staining. The results revealed distinct protein band distributions in samples pulled down with DMRT2-specific probes compared to negative controls (unlabeled DNA probes) (Fig. S7). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the candidate transcription factors identified significant enrichment in the apoptosis pathway, suggesting these factors may participate in apoptosis by regulating DMRT2 transcription (Fig. 12A). To validate the transcriptional regulatory role of candidate transcription factors on DMRT2, NFIC was selected, and an overexpression vector was constructed to investigate its effect on DMRT2 transcription. The NFIC overexpression vector and empty vector were separately transfected into E15 chicken embryonic primary gonadal cells. The mRNA expression levels of NFIC, DMRT2, and DMRT2 downstream genes associated with apoptosis and inflammatory responses were measured by qPCR. Results showed that, compared to the empty vector control group, transfection with the NFIC overexpression vector significantly increased NFIC mRNA expression in gonadal cells, confirming successful vector construction (Fig. 12B). Concurrently, NFIC overexpression significantly elevated the mRNA expression of DMRT2 (Fig. 12C) and its downstream genes PMAIP1 and BCL2L10, as well as inflammatory factors IL6, NFKB1, and NFKBIA (Fig. 12D). These results indicate that NFIC acts as a transcriptional activator of DMRT2 and regulates the expression of apoptosis- and inflammation-related genes through DMRT2.

Discussion

Unlike mammals, the genes responsible for sex determination in birds remain incompletely understood. To date, the DMRT1 gene (Fallahshahroudi et al., 2025), located on the Z chromosome, have been identified as key factors in avian sex determination. As a member of the same gene family as DMRT1, although DMRT2 participates in gonadal sex differentiation in species such as fish, its role in avian sex determination and differentiation remains unclear.

This study used chickens as model animals and revealed the role and related mechanisms of DMRT2 gene in sex differentiation and gonadal growth and development through a series of experiments and analyses. By comparing the known amino acid sequences, it was found that the sequence consistency between DMRT2 and DMRT1 is low, indicating that DMRT2 has significant functional differences from DMRT1. However, DMRT2 exhibits a high degree of conservation among different vertebrate species, indicating that the function of the chicken DMRT2 gene may be similar to that of other vertebrates such as fish, participating in sex differentiation of the gonads. By measuring the expression level of the DMRT2 gene in the gonads of chicken embryos at different stages, it was found that the mRNA and protein expression levels of DMRT2 gradually increased with embryonic age and reached their peak in the later stages. This result suggests that DMRT2 is involved in the growth and development of chicken gonads. By comparing the expression levels of DMRT2 in the left and right gonads of female chicken embryos, it was found that the expression level of DMRT2 was lower in the gradually degenerated gonad on the right side than on the left side. This indicates that DMRT2 is involved in the degeneration of the gonad on the right side of chicken embryos, further confirming its involvement in the growth and development of chicken gonads. By comparing DMRT2 expression levels between male and female gonads in chicken embryos, it was found that the expression level of DMRT2 in the gonads exhibited a pronounced sexually dimorphic expression pattern, suggesting DMRT2 involvement in gonadal sex differentiation. Using FAD and E2-induced sex reversal models in chicken embryos, it was further confirmed that DMRT2 is involved in the gonadal sex differentiation of chicken embryos. By predicting the NLS and constructing DMRT2 lacking the NLS, it was found that the nuclear localization of the DMRT2 protein was significantly impaired, confirming that DMRT2 can function as a transcription factor in the nucleus by influencing the expression of its downstream genes. Through transcriptome sequencing analysis and cell proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis assays after overexpression of DMRT2, it was found that DMRT2 may participate in the growth and development of gonads and sex differentiation by regulating the expression of genes related to inflammatory response and sex differentiation, promoting cell proliferation, and inhibiting cell apoptosis. Further validation of DMRT2 as a transcription factor for SOX9 and CHUK was achieved through ChIP-seq, ChIP-qPCR, and wild-type and NLS deficient DMRT2 overexpression assays. Through qPCR analysis of inflammation and apoptosis related genes after knocking down CHUK expression, it was found that knocking down CHUK expression can increase the expression of inflammation and apoptosis related genes, indicating that DMRT2 can inhibit cell apoptosis and inflammation through CHUK. NFIC was screened as a candidate transcription factor for DMRT2 through DNA pull-down combined with mass spectrometry analysis, and NFIC overexpression assay confirmed that the expression of DMRT2 and apoptosis and inflammation related genes are regulated by NFIC. This suggests that NFIC may activate DMRT2 through transcription to regulate apoptosis and inflammation during gonadal development. In summary, DMRT2 mediates the regulation of upstream signals such as NFIC, and participates in the sex differentiation and growth and development of chicken gonads through target genes such as SOX9 and CHUK.

Although DMRT2 and the chicken sex determining gene DMRT1 are members of the same family, this study suggests that DMRT2 is different from DMRT1 and is not a chicken gonadal sex determining gene. DMRT2 may possess biological functions distinct from those of DMRT1 as the amino acid sequence similarity between chicken DMRT2 and DMRT1 is very low (17.01%). However, similar to reports in other species (Rong et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2019), chicken DMRT2 is also involved in gonadal sex differentiation. This participation is mainly reflected in the relatively conserved amino acid sequence of DMRT2 among different vertebrates, and the expression pattern of DMRT2 in chicken gonads is sex-specific. In the sex reversal model, the expression level of DMRT2 also changes with sex. Another interesting finding is that the expression of DMRT2 is higher in the cortical region of female gonads than in the medullary region, and the content in the cytoplasm is higher than in the nucleus. This phenomenon is different from that of males, that is, the expression distribution of DMRT2 in the gonads has sex dimorphism. This sex dimorphism may be due to the regulation of DMRT2 expression by other genes involved in sex differentiation. This study found that the expression of DMRT2 is regulated by the transcription factor NFIC gene, but whether NFIC is involved in the sex differentiation of chicken gonads still requires further research.

The involvement of DMRT2 in the sex differentiation of chicken gonads may partially depend on its ability to regulate cell proliferation and apoptosis. This study reveals the regulatory ability of DMRT2 in cell proliferation and apoptosis from multiple perspectives. Firstly, as the chicken embryo gonads grow and develop, the expression level of DMRT2 gradually increases and reaches its peak in the later stage. Secondly, the expression level of DMRT2 in the degenerated gonads on the right side of female chicken embryos is significantly lower than that in the normally developing left gonads. Thirdly, transcriptome sequencing analysis and cell proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis assays after overexpression of DMRT2 revealed that DMRT2 can affect pathways such as inflammatory response, cell proliferation, and apoptosis. Fourthly, the target gene CHUK of DMRT2 can mediate its regulatory effect on the expression of inflammatory response and apoptosis related genes. Finally, overexpression of NFIC, an upstream transcription factor of DMRT2, can affect the expression of apoptosis and inflammation related genes. It is worth mentioning that many genes involved in the inflammatory response are also involved in cell apoptosis and proliferation. Therefore, the regulation of inflammation by DMRT2 at least partially reflects its regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis.

This study observed that in the E6.5 gonads, the mRNA expression level of DMRT2 in female chicken embryos was higher than that in male chicken embryos, while the protein expression level was the opposite. This suggests that the protein expression level of DMRT2 may still be regulated by post-translational factors, such as ubiquitination, during early development. Similarly, in E6.5 female gonads, the lateral asymmetry of DMRT2 mRNA levels did not correspond with that of protein levels, suggesting that DMRT2 protein may also be subject to post-translational regulatory mechanisms. The lateral asymmetry in DMRT2 mRNA levels shifts with embryonic development, potentially reflecting transcriptional regulation of left-side DMRT2 by early developmental signals.

Beyond demonstrating nuclear localization of DMRT2 via immunoblotting analysis after nuclear-cytoplasmic separation, this study further confirmed the presence of a nuclear localization signal in DMRT2 through deletion mutation. This finding is consistent with immunofluorescence analysis showing nuclear localization of DMRT2 protein in both male and female gonads. Subsequent ChIP experiments demonstrated that DMRT2 functions as a transcription factor by binding to upstream regulatory sequences of its target genes, such as CHUK and SOX9. Interestingly, in the gonadal cortex of E18 female chicken embryos, DMRT2 protein is not only present in the nucleus but also in the cytoplasm. This suggests that DMRT2 performs functions in the cytoplasm distinct from its role as a transcription factor in the nucleus, such as interacting with other cytoplasmic proteins.

This study found that overexpression of DMRT2 in primary chicken embryonic gonadal cells could induce the expression of the male gonadal marker gene SOX9 and suppresses the expression of the female gonadal marker genes FOXL2 and CYP19A1. This finding suggests that DMRT2 promotes male-directed gonadal differentiation. The role of DMRT2 in gonadal differentiation may be crucial for the maturation or complete sexual reversal of male gonads.

Due to technical limitations, this study cannot yet validate the role of DMRT2 in chicken gonadal sex determination, sex differentiation, and gonadal growth and development using DMRT2 knockout or transgenic chickens. With the advancement of poultry genome editing technology, we hope to address these limitations in future studies using DMRT2 knockout or transgenic chickens.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the role and mechanisms of DMRT2 in the growth and development of chicken embryonic gonads. Specifically, DMRT2 is transcriptionally regulated by NFIC and participates in gonadal growth, development, and sexual differentiation by regulating apoptosis, proliferation, and inflammatory responses in gonadal cells through its target genes (e.g., CHUK and SOX9). Importantly, although DMRT2 is not a sex-determining gene in chicken gonads, it influences gonadal sex differentiation by regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis.
