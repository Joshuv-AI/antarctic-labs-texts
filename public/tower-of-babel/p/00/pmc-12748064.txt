Microbiota γ‐Butyrobetaine Is Associated With Increased Risk of Major Adverse Limb Events in People With Lower Extremity Arterial Disease Undergoing Endovascular Therapy
Authors: Zheng‐Wei Chen, Wei‐Kai Wu, Jiun‐Yang Chiang, Nai‐Chen Cheng, Jen‐Kuang Lee, Ming‐Shiang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripheral artery disease (PAD), a significant contributor to both acute and chronic illnesses, indicates a grave prognosis, but it is often unrecognized and receives inadequate treatment. γ‐Butyrobetaine, formed during gut microbial metabolism of L‐carnitine, acts as a proatherogenic intermediate in the production of trimethylamine N‐oxide (TMAO). While TMAO has been linked to a heightened risk of cardiovascular mortality of individuals with PAD, the impact of γ‐butyrobetaine remains unclear. The objective of this study was to examine the prognostic value of serum γ‐butyrobetaine for patients with PAD.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 395 patients with symptomatic PAD. Comprehensive medical histories, encompassing demographic and medication data, were collected, and serum biochemistry data, including TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine, were obtained. These patients, with a mean age of 72.2 years (61% men), were followed for an average of 1.5 years. They were categorized into 2 groups: 165 patients with intermittent claudication and 230 patients with critical limb‐threatening ischemia. The primary outcome studied was major adverse limb events (MALE), which included lower‐limb revascularization and amputation. MALE developed in 89 (22.5%) patients. Following adjustment for confounding factors in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, γ‐butyrobetaine was significantly associated with MALE (hazard ratio, 1.93 [95% CI, 1.35–2.76]). By contrast, TMAO did not show a significant association with the risk of MALE.ConclusionsWhile both TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine were linked to increased major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with PAD, only γ‐butyrobetaine was associated with an elevated risk of MALE.Keywords: major adverse cardiovascular events, major adverse limb events, peripheral artery disease, trimethylamine N‐oxide, γ‐ButyrobetaineSubject Categories: Peripheral Vascular Disease

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is a common condition characterized by reduced blood flow to the extremities due to atherosclerosis. It is associated with heightened cardiovascular mortality 1 , 2 and affects a significant number of people, estimated to exceed 230 million individuals worldwide. 3 While PAD is less common among younger individuals, its prevalence rises with age 3 and it affects a substantial proportion (29%) of high‐risk individuals, including those >70 years of age without additional risk factors or individuals aged 50 to 69 years with a history of cigarette smoking or diabetes. 4 Despite its high prevalence and poor prognosis, PAD often receives inadequate attention in the scientific literature compared with coronary artery disease and other atherosclerotic conditions. 5 As a result, predictors of mortality and the underlying pathophysiology of PAD remain poorly defined, contributing to the persistently high risk of mortality and ischemic amputations in patients with PAD. 6 There is an urgent need to identify new prognostic markers that can shed light on the underlying pathophysiology of PAD, improve long‐term clinical risk prediction, and suggest potential therapeutic targets, especially considering the increasing prevalence of PAD associated with changing demographics and lifestyle factors.

Emerging research has highlighted the significant role of the gut microbiota in maintaining the body's metabolic balance and organ function. 7 In particular, there is growing evidence suggesting that the liver's conversion of trimethylamine produced by gut microbes into trimethylamine N‐oxide (TMAO) could contribute to increased risk of cardiovascular problems by promoting the development of atherosclerotic lesions. 8 It appears that the primary compounds used for trimethylamine production by the gut microbiota are phosphatidylcholine/choline and L‐carnitine, including red meat, eggs, and processed meat products. 9 γ‐Butyrobetaine, an intermediary proatherogenic metabolite, is abundantly produced by gut microbes in close proximity to the site of formation of trimethylamine and at a rate ≈1000 times greater than the rate of production of trimethylamine. 10 While TMAO has been shown to be a predictor of cardiovascular 11 and all‐cause mortality 12 of patients with PAD, its relationship with major adverse limb events (MALE) remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to examine and compare the clinical prognostic significance of plasma TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine levels in patients with angiography‐established PAD.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Participants and Study Design

Between May 2015 and May 2021, 395 patients with lower‐extremity PAD treated at the National Taiwan University Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan were prospectively enrolled in this study. These patients visited the cardiovascular outpatient clinic at Taiwan University Hospital due to intermittent claudication or chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI). Invasive angiography was performed for patients with an abnormal ankle–brachial index, the presence of >50% stenosis in at least 1 lower extremity determined by Doppler ultrasound or computed tomography angiography, and/or a history of prior angioplasty or stenting. 13 , 14 Subjects with lower extremity PAD confirmed by angiography participated in the study after providing informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taiwan University Hospital (approval number: 202012164RIND).

Determination of TMAO and γ‐Butyrobetaine

Ten microliters of plasma was extracted using methanol containing an internal standard. Following vortexing and centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and then further diluted using the same extraction solvent. The levels of TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine in plasma were analyzed using liquid chromatography (LC‐20ADxr, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) coupled with mass spectrometry (QTrap 5500, SCIEX Corp., MA). The concentrations of TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine in the samples were determined using calibration curves, which included blanks and replicates. The analysis was based on the peak area ratio of each analyte to its corresponding isotopic internal standards, d9‐TMAO and d9‐γ‐butyrobetaine.

To prepare the calibration curves, a mixed standard solution containing TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine was serially diluted. The TMAO calibration curve ranged from 2 to 50 ng/mL in 50% methanol (Figure S1 and Table S1), while the γ‐butyrobetaine calibration curve ranged from 2 to 250 ng/mL in 50% methanol (Figure S2 and Table S2). Both followed the same sample preparation protocol as previously described.

Covariates and Outcomes

Detailed medical histories, including demographic and medication data, were collected and serum biochemistry data were obtained at the initial assessment. The primary outcome in the study was MALE, which included severe limb ischemia necessitating lower‐limb revascularization (ie, endovascular therapy and bypass, and amputation). The secondary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which included ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death. Hospitalization for heart failure (HHF) was also evaluated. The main cause of death, available from the National Taiwan University Hospital‐Integrated Medical Database, and the date of death, recorded in the Taiwan Death Registry, were linked. To analyze each time‐to‐event outcome, patients were followed from the day of discharge to the day of outcome occurrence, death, or the end of follow‐up (June 30, 2021), whichever came first.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients who subsequently developed MALE and those who did not were analyzed using either the independent‐samples t test (for continuous variables, eg, age) or the χ2 test (for categorical variables, eg, sex). Since the data on TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine were apparently skewed with a few outliers, we compared these 2 biomarkers between the MALE and non‐MALE groups using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. The associations between metabolites (TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine) and the risk of clinical outcomes were investigated using the Cox proportional hazards model. Since the sample size and number of events (eg, 89 MALE events) were limited in this study, a rigorous covariate adjustment was not feasible. Therefore, we adjusted only the following crucial covariates: age, sex, body mass index, glycated hemoglobin level, and low‐density lipoprotein level. Because the data on the metabolites were not normally distributed, they were natural‐log transformed. We noted that the correlation between renal function (defined by the estimated glomerular filtration rate) and these 2 metabolites was high (r = −0.64 and −0.68 for TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine, respectively). A multivariable model would be problematic due to this collinearity. Therefore, the data on renal function were not adjusted in the multivariable model and renal function was treated as a subgroup instead.

Two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, since the shortest follow‐up period in the primary analysis was 30 days (from May 31, 2021 to June 30, 2021), and patients within this timeframe were still in the acute phase, those with follow‐up periods of <90 days were excluded. The previously described Cox models were then reanalyzed. Second, the associations between the 2 metabolites and clinical event incidence were assessed using the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model, accounting for all‐cause death as a competing risk. The same covariates were adjusted for in both sensitivity analyses.

To determine the optimal cutoff points for TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine that corresponded to the most significant relationship with the primary outcome (MALE), maximally selected rank statistics were adopted. After dividing the sample according to the cutoff point, the cumulative event rates of MALE were compared between the high and low groups using the log‐rank test. The potential linear and nonlinear relationships between the metabolites and the risk of MALE were explored by treating the metabolites as restricted cubic splines in the Cox model. The locations of knots in the restricted cubic splines modeling were set as the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. Finally, the analysis of the associations between the metabolites and the risk of selected outcomes (ie, MALE, MACE, and HHF) was stratified by chronic kidney disease (CKD) (estimated glomerular filtration rate <60) and chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI). A 2‐sided P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R version 4.2.2 (R Development Core Team).

During the follow‐up period, 89 (22.5%) patients experienced MALE. Compared with patients without MALE, a higher proportion of patients with MALE had CLTI (84.3%), were older, and had higher systolic blood pressure. Laboratory data showed higher levels of creatinine and lower levels of albumin and high‐density lipoprotein in patients with MALE. While the level of γ‐butyrobetaine was statistically significantly higher in patients with MALE, the TMAO level only showed a tendency to be higher compared with that in patients without MALE. In the case of comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension was lower and the prevalence of CKD was higher in patients with MALE. The incidence of other comorbidities, including diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, chronic heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, was similar between the 2 groups. The rates of previous coronary intervention, amputation, and endovascular therapy were also comparable. In the case of medication use, a higher percentage of patients without MALE used cilostazol, while there were no statistically significant differences in the use of other medications, such as hypoglycemic agents, insulin, lipid‐lowering agents, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and antihypertensive agents, between the 2 groups (Table 1).

During follow‐up, MALE occurred in 89 (22.5%) patients and included 48 cases of lower‐limb revascularization and 63 cases of major amputation. MACE developed in 52 (13.2%) patients and included 20 myocardial infarction events, 30 ischemic stroke events, and 10 cardiovascular deaths. Forty‐five (11.4%) patients underwent HHF during follow‐up (data not shown).

TMAO and γ‐Butyrobetaine and Clinical Outcomes

Univariate analysis found a significant association between log‐transformed TMAO and myocardial infarction, MACE, and HHF. In multivariable analysis adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, glycated hemoglobin, and low‐density lipoprotein, log‐transformed TMAO continued to be associated with myocardial infarction (hazard ratio [HR], 1.73 [95% CI, 1.21–2.48]), MACE (HR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.10–1.78]), and HHF (HR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.15–1.92]) with statistical significance. However, neither the univariate analysis nor the multivariable analysis found an association between TMAO and any lower‐limb outcomes (Table 2).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

After excluding 64 patients with follow‐up periods of <90 days, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Tables S3 and S4). Similarly, the results accounting for competing death were consistent with the primary analysis (Tables S5 and S6). In order to assess the association of TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine with outcomes of interest in specific patient groups, the selected outcomes (ie, MALE, MACE, and HHF) were stratified based on the presence of CKD and CLTI. Interestingly, TMAO showed a stronger association with the risk of HHF of patients with CLTI compared with those without CLTI (HR=1.81 versus 0.99; P=0.042 for the interaction) (Figure 3A). However, the observed association between TMAO and the risks of MALE, MACE, and HHF was consistent across patients with and without CKD (P for interaction >0.05). On the other hand, γ‐butyrobetaine showed a stronger association with MALE in patients without CKD compared with those with CKD (HR=2.21 versus 0.84; P=0.045 for the interaction) (Figure 3B). Nevertheless, the observed association between γ‐butyrobetaine and the risks of MACE and HHF was consistent across patients with and without CKD (P for interaction >0.05).

DISCUSSION

Our study highlights the value of γ‐butyrobetaine as a predictive marker of MALE in patients with PAD. While TMAO has been associated with cardiovascular mortality and all‐cause mortality in patients with PAD, our findings demonstrate for the first time that γ‐butyrobetaine may be a more relevant biomarker for assessing the risk of MALE. These findings suggest that γ‐butyrobetaine could serve as a prognostic tool for identifying individuals at higher risk of MALE, further emphasizing the significance of gut microbial metabolism for cardiovascular outcomes.

The impact of the gut microbiota on the host metabolism and their involvement in various pathological conditions, including obesity, 16 insulin resistance, 17 diabetes, 18 autoimmune disease, 19 CKD, 20 atherosclerosis, 21 heart failure, 22 and other cardiovascular disease, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 are gaining recognition. Trimethylamine‐containing nutrients, such as phosphatidylcholine, choline, and L‐carnitine, are metabolized by the gut microbiota to produce trimethylamine, which is subsequently converted to TMAO by the liver enzyme flavin‐containing monooxygenase 3. 27 TMAO is present in the bloodstream and is absorbed by peripheral tissue and primarily excreted in urine at a rate exceeding 90% in healthy individuals. 28 Dietary sources of TMAO precursors are diverse and include eggs yolk, liver, milk, meat (red meat and poultry), fish, shellfish, green vegetables, whole grains, spinach, and beets. 29 Furthermore, previous research has shown that increased plasma levels of TMAO are associated with an elevated risk of experiencing significant adverse cardiovascular events and are directly involved in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. 30 Our current prospective study expands on these findings by specifically focusing on patients with a confirmed diagnosis of PAD in the lower extremities. Carmen et al. demonstrated a correlation between TMAO levels and disease activity in patients with lower‐extremity PAD, and further showed that elevated TMAO levels predicted cardiovascular mortality. 11 Senthong et al. found that TMAO was a significant predictor of 5‐year all‐cause mortality of patients with PAD. 12 Subgroup analysis revealed that the prognostic value of TMAO was similar for individuals with carotid artery, non–carotid artery, or lower extremity PAD.

Contrary to our expectations, our study did not find that TMAO was of significant value for predicting cardiovascular death of patients with PAD. However, γ‐butyrobetaine emerged as a significant predictor of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular death, MACE, and HHF. Notably, we also investigated the prognostic value of these microbiome metabolites for MALE; while TMAO had no predictive value for lower‐limb outcomes, γ‐butyrobetaine was strongly correlated with the risks of endovascular therapy, amputation, and MALE. The higher rate of γ‐butyrobetaine production (1000 times) compared with trimethylamine may explain its superiority over TMAO, highlighting its potential significance for cardiovascular health. 10 Another possible explanation may be attributed to the distinct underlying pathology of PAD compared with coronary artery disease. In coronary artery disease, acute coronary syndrome, a component of MACE, typically arises from the rupture or erosion of atherosclerotic plaques, which usually contain large necrotic cores and thin, inflamed fibrous caps. 31 In contrast, PAD involves thrombosis that can develop regardless of the level of atherosclerosis present. Approximately 75% of peripheral arteries with significant stenosis have thrombi, with about two thirds occurring in areas of nonsignificant atherosclerosis. 32 PAD is also characterized by obliterative thrombi and significant calcification of the medial vascular layer, especially in cases of critical limb ischemia. 33 These features indicate a more localized thrombogenic or embolic mechanism, distinctly differing from CAD, and highlight the potential benefits of antithrombotic agents in prevention. 34 Based on our analysis, γ‐butyrobetaine may be more effective than TMAO in detecting the thrombosis component of MALE.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that TMAO had a stronger association with the risk of HHF of patients with CLTI compared with those without CLTI. TMAO has been implicated in the promotion of development of heart failure through various mechanisms, including myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, inflammatory response, impaired vascular endothelial function, and altered mitochondrial metabolism. 22 This association may be influenced by the higher prevalence or severity of heart failure in patients with CLTI, as well as the elevated levels of TMAO observed in CLTI compared with intermittent claudication at baseline. Conversely, γ‐butyrobetaine demonstrated a stronger association with MALE in patients without CKD compared with those with CKD. Previous pharmacokinetic studies indicated that TMAO is mainly excreted in urine, while γ‐butyrobetaine is primarily eliminated in feces. 35 , 36 This suggests that γ‐butyrobetaine may be less influenced by renal function compared with TMAO. In our analysis, the presence of CKD did not significantly affect the association between TMAO level and clinical outcomes. This is consistent with previous studies showing that TMAO increases the risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events in patients with CKD. 37 Accumulating evidence also suggests a direct causal relationship and a modifiable link between kidney function and circulating TMAO levels. 38 , 39 However, since patients with CKD are associated with higher rates of MALE than those without, this could potentially diminish the association between γ‐butyrobetaine and MALE, as well as the similar but statistically nonsignificant trend observed between γ‐butyrobetaine and MACE. These findings underscore the importance of considering individual patient characteristics in assessing the prognostic value of gut microbial metabolites and highlight the differences in the associations of TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine with specific outcomes in different patient subgroups.

Our findings demonstrate the potential of γ‐butyrobetaine as a prognostic tool for identifying individuals at a higher risk of experiencing MALE. This novel association adds to the growing body of evidence highlighting the importance of gut microbial metabolism and its impact on cardiovascular outcomes. The identification of γ‐butyrobetaine as a predictor of MALE offers new insights into the pathophysiology of PAD and may aid in developing targeted interventions to mitigate adverse limb events. Further research is warranted to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms through which γ‐butyrobetaine influences the progression of PAD and its associated complications.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, a potential limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size and low statistical power, which may have resulted in too few events for reliable stratified analyses. Secondly, it was a single‐center study conducted in a tertiary care setting; therefore, its findings may not be generalizable to other populations. External validation with different cohorts is needed to confirm our results. Nevertheless, it is important to note that for all the patients included in this study, PAD in the lower extremities was confirmed through peripheral angiography. Thirdly, potential factors, such as antibiotic use, dietary assessment, dietary changes, flavin‐containing monooxygenase 3 activity, environmental factors, and socioeconomic status 40 that could influence the production of microbiome metabolites were not assessed nor considered as confounding variables in our analysis. Future studies should include these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the associations. Nonetheless, our study is the first to investigate and compare the prognostic value of TMAO and γ‐butyrobetaine for MACE and MALE in patients with PAD.

CONCLUSIONS

In patients with PAD, γ‐butyrobetaine showed a stronger association with MALE compared with TMAO, highlighting its potential as a more reliable predictor of MALE in this population.
