Mobile health management needs among infertile patients: a cross-sectional study based on the PRECEDE-PROCEED model
Authors: Anjiang Lei, Xu Liao, Li Liu, Huaxuan You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfertility imposes significant physical and psychological burdens on affected individuals. As mobile health (mHealth) tools become increasingly widespread, understanding the specific health management needs of infertile patients is essential for designing effective, patient-centered digital interventions. This study aimed to assess these needs and provide a foundation for future mHealth system development.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2023 and January 2024 at a women’s and children’s medical center in southwestern China. Infertile patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were recruited. A self-designed questionnaire, developed on the basis of the PRECEDE–PROCEED model, was used to assess patients’ mobile health management needs. Independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVAs and multiple linear regression models were conducted to examine associations between sociodemographic and clinical variables and each of the 10 dimensions of mobile health management needs.ResultsA total of 319 infertile patients participated in the study. The most prominent mobile health management needs identified were medication guidance, followed by postoperative rehabilitation and disease-related knowledge. These needs varied according to sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Among various sociodemographic and clinical factors, education level emerged as the only consistent predictor of these needs across different dimensions. Patients with higher education level reported broader demands, likely due to greater health awareness and digital literacy. Background

Infertility, defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after at least 12 months of regular unprotected sexual intercourse, affects both men and women and has emerged as a growing global concern [1]. The prevalence of infertility varies across countries and regions. A study in Ukraine reported that the prevalence of primary infertility was 5.8%, and the prevalence of secondary infertility was 19.6% [2]. In Britain, the reported prevalence of infertility was 12.5% among women and 10.1% among men [3]. Among couples of reproductive age in China, the prevalence of infertility was 25% [4]. Globally, the age-standardized prevalence rate of infertility increased by 0.370% per year for females and 0.291% per year for males from 1990 to 2017 [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has predicted that infertility will become the third most prevalent disease of the 21st century, following cardiovascular diseases and cancer [6]. Owing to its multifactorial nature and psychosocial implications, infertility can significantly impact family relationships and social stability [7].

Infertility is not only a medical issue but also a psychological and social challenge, affecting quality of life, family stability, and social dynamics [8, 9]. With the introduction of the three-child policy, fertility and reproductive health have become particularly important public health concerns in China. The 14th Five-Year Plan for Health and Wellness, issued by China’s State Council, emphasizes the need to strengthen reproductive technology services and enhance overall reproductive health. Consequently, understanding and addressing the ongoing health management needs of infertile patients is crucial for enhancing reproductive outcomes and well-being.

Studies have shown that continuous health management after hospital discharge can improve patients’ physical and mental health, increase fertility rates, and increase quality of life [7]. However, notable discrepancies exist between the focus of medical staff and patients regarding disease and treatment processes, and the health information support provided by medical professionals often does not fully align with patients’ actual needs [10]. With the rapid advancement and widespread adoption of mobile electronic devices in recent years, mobile health (mHealth) has become an increasingly accessible and scalable tool for supporting patients outside traditional clinical settings. Among existing studies, few have explored the mobile health management needs of infertile patients, and there is limited research on how mHealth platforms can support these patients in the posttreatment phase. Therefore, providing specific and personalized health information support on the basis of the actual needs of patients is both necessary and urgent.

To effectively address these unmet needs, a structured approach is required. While previous studies have explored infertility, digital health, and patient needs separately, few have integrated these perspectives within a structured behavioral framework. To systematically assess and address these individualized needs, this study employed the PRECEDE–PROCEED model as a guiding theoretical framework. Developed by health educators Green and Kreuter [11], the PRECEDE–PROCEED model offers a structured and comprehensive approach for planning health promotion programs. According to the model, effective behavioral change results from the combined influence of predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors. A preliminary assessment of these factors helps to identify the target population’s primary health issues and the determinants influencing their behavior. On the basis of this framework, tailored interventions can be designed, implemented, and evaluated for their effectiveness [12]. Accordingly, this study developed a postoperative mobile health management needs questionnaire for infertile patients, guided by the PRECEDE–PROCEED model. The objective was to systematically assess their health management needs, identify priority areas, and offer evidence to support the design of targeted, patient-centered mHealth interventions.

This study applies the PRECEDE–PROCEED model to systematically assess the mobile health management needs of infertile patients. By identifying needs across multiple dimensions and examining their associations with sociodemographic and clinical factors, the study provides structured, theory-informed evidence that can inform the development of tailored mHealth interventions and support more patient-centered infertility care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2023 and January 2024 at a women’s and children’s medical center in southwestern China.

Participants

According to Kendall’s experience and methods for sample size estimation [7], the sample size can generally be considered to be 5–10 times the number of independent variables. The questionnaire in this study had 35 independent variables. Taking into account a dropout rate of 10%, the study’s sample size was estimated to be between 175 and 350.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years (2), voluntary participation with signed informed consent (3), diagnosis of infertility and having undergone relevant surgery, and (4) ability to use a smartphone.

The exclusion criteria included (1) severe postoperative complications (2), a diagnosis of mental or cognitive disorders that impeded cooperation, and (3) the presence of serious comorbid conditions affecting other organ systems.

Measurements

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic information, such as the patient’s age, race, education level, place of residence, family income, number of reproductive surgeries, duration of infertility, and type of infertility.

The questionnaire used to assess the mobile health management needs of infertile patients was developed through a comprehensive process. This included a review of the relevant literature, expert consultations with healthcare professionals, and direct interviews with patients. The “Questionnaire of Mobile Health Management Needs of Postoperative Infertile Patients” was initially formulated using the PRECEDE–PROCEED model. The 10 dimensions were hypothesized a priori, grounded in the PRECEDE–PROCEED model’s conceptualization of predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors. Each dimension was designed to reflect a specific type of need within this structure. The draft was reviewed and modified by five experts from the fields of reproductive endocrinology, nursing, health education, and statistics. To ensure content validity, although quantitative indices were not calculated, due to the exploratory nature of this initial instrument development, content validity was established through qualitative expert judgment. The experts assessed each item for relevance, clarity, and coverage of key concepts, based on their clinical and academic experience. Items were revised or retained based on consensus among the experts. Each expert initially rated the items independently before discussing and reaching a consensus through a group review process. Following these revisions, a pilot survey was conducted among 30 infertile patients to assess item clarity, feasibility, and initial reliability. Based on their feedback, minor adjustments were made to the wording of certain items. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with the final questionnaire achieving a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.994. While this high value suggests strong reliability, item selection was carefully reviewed during both expert consultation and pilot testing to minimize redundancy while preserving the conceptual richness and comprehensiveness of each dimension.

The finalized questionnaire consists of 10 dimensions: medication guidance (2 items), postoperative rehabilitation (7 items), disease knowledge (1 item), prepregnancy preparation (6 items), online consultation and appointment (1 item), skills to conceive (1 item), early pregnancy management (4 items), guidance on ovulation monitoring (2 items), online follow-up (1 item), and fertility problem stress counseling (2 items). The English version of the questionnaire is provided in Supplement 1.

Data collection

Two professionally trained investigators were responsible for distributing and collecting the questionnaires. The investigators explained the study’s purpose, content, and the principle of confidentiality to the participants. After providing signed informed consent, the participants completed the questionnaire independently. Completion of the questionnaire required approximately 15–20 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means (M) and standard deviations (SD). The statistical analyses, including t-tests and one-way ANOVAs, were conducted in an exploratory manner to examine potential differences in mobile health management needs across various sociodemographic and clinical subgroups. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare mobile health management needs scores between two groups (i.e., race, place of residence, and type of infertility). One-way ANOVA was conducted for variables with more than two categories, including age, education level, family per capita monthly income, and number of reproductive surgeries. Multiple linear regression models were conducted to examine associations between sociodemographic and clinical variables and each of the 10 dimensions of mobile health management needs. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analyses were included in the multivariable models. All independent variables were simultaneously entered into each model. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Mobile health management needs among infertile patients

This study identified the primary mobile health management needs among infertile patients, with “medication guidance” and “postoperative rehabilitation” ranking the highest. These needs reflect patients’ focus on information and care closely tied to treatment outcomes, consistent with previous studies [13]. Mobile health interventions should therefore focus on these core dimensions to maximize patient engagement and therapeutic efficacy.

Factors influencing mobile health management needs among infertile patients

This study revealed that among various sociodemographic and clinical variables, education level was the only consistent predictor of mobile health management needs across different dimensions in multiple linear regression models. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that individuals with higher level of education are more likely to demonstrate greater health awareness, digital literacy, and engagement in health-seeking behaviors, particularly in managing chronic conditions such as infertility [14, 15]. Patients with higher education level may also be more adept at recognizing and expressing their informational and emotional needs, which could explain their broader expressed demands in mobile health management.

Although age, income level, and reproductive surgical history were significantly associated with certain need dimensions in univariate analyses, these associations did not remain significant after controlling for other variables. This may indicate that education level mediates or moderates the influence of other variables, reinforcing its central role in shaping patients’ capacity to navigate digital health resources. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies that highlight education as a key determinant of digital health engagement and self-management behavior [16, 17].

These findings raise important equity considerations. The relatively lower reported needs among patients with lower education level do not necessarily reflect a true absence of need but may instead result from limited health literacy or digital competency [18, 19]. These patients may be less able to recognize, prioritize, or express their health concerns—especially within digital health contexts [20]. If mobile health services are predominantly shaped by the preferences and capabilities of more educated users, there is a risk that marginalized groups may be underserved or excluded from meaningful engagement [21]. Therefore, mHealth interventions should incorporate inclusive design principles, such as simplified interfaces, visual and audio supports, and culturally tailored educational content, to ensure accessibility, usability, and responsiveness across diverse literacy levels [22]. For patients with lower education level, it is essential to provide plain-language, audiovisual, and culturally tailored resources, possibly integrating family support or offline reinforcement. Future research should further investigate barriers faced by lower-educated patients in accessing and benefiting from digital health interventions [23]. Mixed-methods studies may help uncover unmet needs that are not easily captured through structured questionnaires. Additionally, evaluating the effectiveness of tailored mHealth interventions in improving reproductive outcomes across diverse subgroups could provide more actionable guidance for policy and program design. Health professionals should be trained to identify latent needs and tailor interventions to enhance digital health equity across different educational backgrounds [22].

Potential implementation challenges

Although this study provides valuable insights into the mobile health management needs of infertile patients, several challenges may arise in practical implementation. Disparities in internet access and digital literacy between urban and rural areas may hinder the accessibility and effective utilization of mHealth tools, particularly for patients in remote or underserved regions. Additionally, although the proportion of ethnic minority participants in this study was relatively low, it remains essential to consider linguistic and cultural diversity in future intervention designs. The incorporation of multilingual support and culturally sensitive content can enhance the inclusiveness and effectiveness of mobile health programs. Therefore, future mHealth interventions should emphasize user-friendly designs, including multilingual functionalities, and address digital barriers to ensure equitable access and usability across diverse patient populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it focuses on the relatively underexplored topic of mobile health management needs among infertile patients, particularly from the patient perspective. While mobile health interventions have received growing attention in broader healthcare contexts, their specific application in infertility care remains limited. Second, the study employed the PRECEDE–PROCEED model, a comprehensive theoretical framework that effectively guided the identification of needs and the planning of interventions. Third, the relatively adequate sample size allowed for subgroup analysis based on key sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, providing nuanced insights into diverse patient needs.

This study has several limitations. First, this single-center cross-sectional survey limits causal inference, and the use of convenience sampling may have biased the findings toward more engaged patients. Second, the use of self-reported electronic questionnaires may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Third, although content validity was qualitatively assessed through expert review and consensus, no quantitative measures were calculated. This limits the ability to statistically quantify the validity of individual items. Finally, the high Cronbach’s alpha (0.994), while suggesting excellent internal consistency, may also indicate potential item redundancy.

Conclusions

This study highlights the key mobile health management needs among infertile patients, with medication guidance, postoperative rehabilitation, and disease-related knowledge being the most prominent. The findings emphasize the critical role of education level as a consistent predictor of mobile health management needs, with patients of higher education level expressing broader demands for health management. These results suggest that future mHealth interventions should be tailored to address the specific needs of different sociodemographic groups, particularly by providing more accessible and simplified resources for patients with lower education level. Furthermore, while the study provides valuable insights into the health management needs of infertile patients, it also underscores the importance of considering health equity in mobile health design. Future research should explore barriers faced by lower-educated patients in accessing and benefiting from digital health tools and assess the effectiveness of targeted mHealth interventions across diverse populations. These efforts will help ensure that mobile health resources are inclusive, accessible, and impactful, thereby improving reproductive health outcomes for all patients.
