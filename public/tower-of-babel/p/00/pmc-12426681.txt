Cardioneuroablation for Refractory Vasovagal Storm Secondary to a Right Hilar Tumor

History of Presentation

A 67-year-old woman with normal resting electrocardiogram experienced multiple episodes of syncope at the end of July 2024. She was admitted to the emergency department at a peripheral hospital, where computed tomography (CT) angiography ruled out a pulmonary embolism, given a recent diagnosis of a right hilar tumor. While under continuous cardiac monitoring, the patient had 2 additional syncopal episodes, both associated with prolonged sinus pauses of up to 30 seconds (Figure 1).

Prolonged Sinus Pause

An 18-second telemetry recording demonstrates the first 12 seconds of a prolonged 30-second sinus pause, during which no escape QRS complex is observed, resulting in syncope.

Past Medical History

The patient had sustained blunt chest trauma 1 month before admission. Given her persistent chest pain, her primary care physician had ordered a thoracic CT scan. The imaging revealed a rib fracture and an incidental right hilar mass with malignant features, measuring 30 × 21 mm in the transverse plane and 44 mm in height. No additional lesions suggestive of secondary localization were observed.

Differential Diagnosis

In the setting of a normal resting electrocardiogram, syncope presenting as sinus arrest may result from a vasovagal cardioinhibitory mechanism or an intrinsic sinus node dysfunction.

Investigations

The patient was subsequently admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit at our center, where temporary pacing via femoral venous access was initiated. At the time of admission, the patient was no longer experiencing chest pain and was not receiving any analgesic treatment. She was not taking any medications known to cause sinus node dysfunction, and there were no other health-related conditions (eg, flu, gastrointestinal symptoms) that could have triggered a vagal response. Notably, the malignant nature of her tumor had been confirmed 2 weeks earlier, and no specific treatment had been initiated.

Over the next few days, the patient experienced daily recurrent episodes of sinus arrest accompanied by flushing and global discomfort, which were symptoms consistent with peripheral vasovagal activation. Although right ventricular backup pacing (VVI 45 beats/min) effectively prevented progression to syncope, it did not address the underlying vagal activation, which accounted for the persistent symptoms. Detailed analysis of the telemetry recordings showed a cardioinhibitory pattern suggestive of a vasovagal mechanism, characterized by gradual slowing of the sinus rate preceding sinus arrest (Figure 2). Transthoracic echocardiography and resting electrocardiogram were both within normal limits. No electrolyte abnormalities were detected.

Typical Pattern of Sinus Arrest Due to Vasovagal Mechanism

Telemetry recording demonstrated a gradual slowing of the sinus rate (black star). The final sinus beat preceding sinus arrest (red star) also exhibited evidence of atrioventricular conduction delay. During the period of sinus arrest, temporary pacing at VVI 40 beats/min effectively prevented syncope. Sinus node activity resumed after more than 30 seconds of sinus arrest (green star).

A mechanical compression of the right vagus nerve or cardiac parasympathetic ganglionated plexi (GP) by the hilar tumor was suspected. Given these findings and the cardioinhibitory response suggestive of a vasovagal mechanism, we elected to perform a cardioneuroablation (CNA) procedure. CNA was initially preferred over permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation owing to the high risk of complications (eg, infection) in the context of neoplasia.

Management

The radiofrequency ablation procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Electroanatomical mapping of the left and right atria was carried out using the CARTO mapping system (Biosense Webster). The procedure followed an anatomically guided approach based on the preprocedural CT scan, in which the fat pad was carefully segmented using the CARTOSEG CT module (Biosense Webster) and was fused with the electroanatomical mapping (Figure 3).

(Lower left panel) The ablation sites targeting the right posterior GP are shown; ablations performed from the right side are not depicted. (Right panel) A posterior view of the left atrium illustrates the right superior GP along with its corresponding ablation site. The close anatomical proximity between these GP and the right hilar tumor can also be appreciated. GP = ganglionated plexi.

Ablation was directed at the right superior GP (interaortocaval region) via the left atrium and the right posterior GP via both the right and left atria, according to the nomenclature described by Armour et al.1 Radiofrequency energy was delivered at 35 W, with an ablation index target of 450; the total duration was 14 minutes. During energy delivery, the patient's heart rate increased from 56 to 80 beats/min, indicating a positive response. This effect was sustained after the procedure (Figure 4). No ablation was performed on the remaining GP, as the patient exhibited mainly sinus pause–related symptoms, consistent with activation of the right vagus nerve only.

Discussion

Vasovagal syncope with a predominant cardioinhibitory component can have various etiologies and may also occur in older adults. Recognition of the characteristic cardioinhibitory response pattern on telemetry monitoring is essential, as it enables differentiation from organic sinus node dysfunction. This distinction is clinically important, given that vagally mediated mechanisms may be suitable for treatment with CNA rather than requiring PPM implantation.

CNA is an emerging and promising therapy for patients with debilitating symptoms due to vagally mediated bradycardia, atrioventricular block, or sinus arrest with a predominant cardioinhibitory response, as observed in our patient.2 Current evidence regarding its efficacy primarily involves younger patients with minimal or no comorbidities, and limited data are available on its effectiveness in older populations.3 Furthermore, the full spectrum of patient profiles eligible for this intervention remains to be clearly defined.

Although PPM remains the standard therapy for neurocardiogenic syncope, the decision to favor CNA over PPM is primarily driven by patient age, as patients older than 60 years have been under-represented in the existing literature. In younger populations, CNA has been shown to be noninferior to PPM in preventing syncope.4 The rate of major adverse events associated with CNA is expected to be comparable to that of conventional right and left atrial ablation procedures and lower than that of atrial fibrillation ablation, owing to shorter radiofrequency application times. In a recent study by Tung et al5 of a multicenter CNA registry in the U.S., the major complication rate was 1.5%, although long-term data were limited. In contrast, dual-chamber pacemaker therapy had a complication rate of 4.8% within 30 days in the MOST trial, and up to 8.2% within 90 days in real-world data.6 Given these considerations, the decision to perform CNA in patients older than 60 years should be individualized, based on institutional expertise and shared decision-making with the patient.

In this report, we present to the best of our knowledge the first documented case of successful CNA performed for a vasovagal storm characterized by symptomatic sinus pauses, presumably triggered by mechanical stimulation of the right vagus nerve or cardiac GP.7 However, we cannot definitively exclude the possibility that the patient's symptoms were unrelated to tumor compression and may have resolved spontaneously, independent of the intervention. CNA may serve as a valuable alternative to PPM implantation in cases where a vagally mediated cardioinhibitory mechanism is strongly suspected, regardless of the patient's age.8 Further studies are needed to better delineate the indications, limitations, and long-term outcomes of this evolving therapy.

Follow-Up

The patient remained free from syncope and sinus pauses immediately after the procedure and throughout follow-up (>6 months). However, she continued to experience minor peripheral symptoms consistent with ongoing vasovagal activation. Three months later, after confirmation of malignancy, the patient underwent successful surgical resection of the tumor with a right lobectomy. After the surgery, these residual symptoms resolved completely, supporting the hypothesis of vagal activation related to the tumor.

Conclusions

Vagal cardioinhibitory response can be triggered by mechanical compression of the vagus nerve or cardiac GP, and CNA is a safe and efficient alternative to pacemaker implantation.
