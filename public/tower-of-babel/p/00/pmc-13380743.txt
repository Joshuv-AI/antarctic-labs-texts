Efficacy and safety of a mobile app–based, flexible-schedule digital therapeutic intervention versus psychoeducation for panic disorder: An open-label randomized controlled trial
Authors: Deung Hyun Kang, Hyunchan Hwang, Da Seul Kim, Hyeri Kim, Sujin Kwak, Changyu Kim, Soo-Hee Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital therapeutics (DTx) have the potential to improve the accessibility and personalization of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for panic disorder. However, trials comparing DTx with structured active comparators remain limited.ObjectiveWe evaluated the efficacy and safety of PanicDoc, a mobile app-based DTx for panic disorder, versus structured psychoeducation.MethodsIn this open-label randomized trial, 52 patients with panic disorder were randomly assigned to PanicDoc or psychoeducation, both delivered with therapist guidance (n = 26 per group). PanicDoc provided CBT-based modules with algorithm-based personalization over an 8- to 12-week treatment period, whereas the control group received biweekly structured psychoeducation over 12 weeks. The primary outcome was change from baseline in clinician-rated panic severity, assessed using the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS).ResultsThe PanicDoc group showed significantly greater improvement in PDSS in the end-of-intervention mixed-effects model for repeated measures (least squares mean difference = −3.71, P = .02). Post-treatment Clinical Global Impression–Improvement also favored PanicDoc over controls (t(39)= − 2.78, P = .008). Among participants allocated to PanicDoc, 21 of 26 (80.8%) completed the program; mean app usage was 47.9 (±39.8) minutes/week. No significant between-group differences were observed on self-reported outcomes, although both groups showed within-group improvements on several secondary measures over time. No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionsGuided DTx for panic disorder was associated with greater improvement in clinician-rated panic severity than an active psychoeducation control. However, superiority was not observed on self-reported outcomes, and the open-label design with unblinded clinician ratings warrants cautious interpretation.Trial Registration: KCT0009912 (https://cris.nih.go.kr/).

Introduction

Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent unexpected panic attacks, in which intense fear becomes linked to vague bodily sensations, resulting in anticipatory anxiety and maladaptive behaviors (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). According to the World Mental Health Survey conducted across 25 countries, the lifetime prevalence of panic disorder was 1.7% and 13.2% of individuals reported lifetime panic attacks, of whom 66.5% experienced recurrent episodes (De Jonge et al., 2016). A lifetime comorbid mental disorder was observed in 80.4% of individuals with lifetime panic disorder, including depression, other anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders, which collectively increase clinical complexity and patient burden (De Jonge et al., 2016; Roy-Byrne et al., 2006). Higher levels of panic symptoms have also been associated with greater functional impairment, including an increased number of underproductive days (Kessler et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2021). Despite its significant burden, many individuals with panic disorder remain untreated or inadequately treated (Alonso et al., 2018).

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is recommended as a first-line treatment for panic disorder in major clinical guidelines and is supported by substantial evidence for reducing panic symptoms and preventing relapse (Stein and McIntyre, 2010; Baldwin et al., 2014; Excellence NIfHaC, 2011; Andrews et al., 2018). Core CBT components include psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, interoceptive and in vivo exposures, and relaxation techniques such as breathing retraining (Craske and Barlow, 2022; Arch and Craske, 2009). Despite its established efficacy, engagement with CBT remains challenging; avoidance itself is a core feature of panic disorder (Arch and Craske, 2009), and the structured format of conventional CBT—requiring attendance at fixed times and locations—further limits accessibility, particularly among working individuals (Haby et al., 2006; Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2017).

Internet-delivered CBT (iCBT) was developed in part to overcome these barriers (Käll et al., 2024). Panic disorder was among the earliest clinical targets of iCBT. Early trials suggested that iCBT, including self-help programs with minimal therapist contact, could yield improvements broadly comparable to face-to-face or group CBT (Carlbring et al., 2005; Bergström et al., 2010). More recently, digital interventions have extended to smartphone applications (Spies et al., 2025; Ebenfeld et al., 2021), giving rise to digital therapeutics (DTx)—software-based medical devices designed to prevent, manage, or treat medical conditions (Alliance, 2018). Unlike many conventional CBT programs that follow fixed treatment schedules and standardized module sequences, DTx can offer more flexible and personalized delivery through algorithm-guided monitoring and content adaptation based on user engagement and clinical progress (Wang et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2025).

Across digital mental health interventions, therapist support has been associated with improved treatment engagement and may enhance clinical outcomes compared with fully self-guided approaches (Käll et al., 2024). A recent meta-analysis found that the efficacy of digital interventions for panic disorder is influenced by both clinician guidance and comparator type, with larger effects observed in clinician-guided interventions and attenuated effects observed when active comparators are used (Cho et al., 2025). However, many prior trials have relied on passive or waitlist control conditions. As a result, relatively few studies have examined whether clinician-guided digital CBT provides benefits beyond those of a structured active control.

The present study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of PanicDoc, a digital CBT mobile app for panic disorder, in a randomized controlled trial conducted at two clinical sites. PanicDoc incorporates a flexible treatment schedule and adaptive features that tailor content delivery based on individual treatment needs. In this trial, therapist guidance was provided throughout the intervention period to support treatment engagement and implementation. The control group received structured, therapist-guided psychoeducation over a fixed period. We hypothesized that PanicDoc would result in greater improvements in clinician-rated panic severity than the active control condition.

Method

Study design

This study was designed as a randomized controlled trial with the purpose of verifying the efficacy of PanicDoc, a digital therapeutic device developed to treat panic disorder. The study was conducted at two medical centers in Seoul, South Korea: Chung-Ang University Hospital and Seoul National University Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Chung-Ang University Hospital (IRB No. 2309-019-571) and Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2311-013-1482). Clinical trial registration was completed before the participant enrollment began at the Clinical Research Information Service as Prospective Randomized Controlled Confirmatory Clinical Trial of a Digital Therapeutic Device for Panic Disorder (KCT0009912), which is registered at the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. The digital therapeutic device was approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety of Korea for clinical trial prior to the start of the trial.

Participants

Participants were recruited from outpatient clinics and through online advertisements between March and June 2024. The standardized recruitment advertisement and telephone screening script described the study as a clinical trial of a CBT-based mobile app for panic disorder and explained that participants would be randomly assigned to either the app-based intervention or structured psychoeducation. Participants who voluntarily signed the informed consent form were evaluated for eligibility. Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the PanicDoc or the control group. To ensure comparable levels of study contact between groups, all participants attended five hospital visits and completed two telephone-based checkups throughout the intervention period, from baseline through post-treatment assessment, according to the study schedule shown in Table 1. Scheduled contacts primarily involved participant assessment, safety monitoring, concomitant medication review, and adherence monitoring. During these contacts, the PanicDoc group received guidance and checkups regarding the use of the app and its CBT program, whereas the control group received guided psychoeducation according to the study protocol. The screening visit took approximately 30 min, baseline and post-treatment assessments each took approximately 1 h, and interim hospital visits or telephone checkups took approximately 20 min. These contacts were not designed to provide separate clinical care outside the study procedures or additional CBT sessions. Board-certified psychiatrists provided study explanations, obtained informed consent, reviewed medical history and medication use at baseline, and performed clinician-rated assessments. Trained research staff coordinated the remaining procedures, including delivery of the assigned intervention (PanicDoc guidance or guided psychoeducation). All research staff involved in participant interactions had a background in counseling psychology or relevant clinical experience. The intervention group received the digital therapeutic device PanicDoc and was instructed to use it over an 8- to 12-week treatment period. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Adults aged between 19 and 65 years; (2) Individuals diagnosed with panic disorder according to the DSM-5 criteria, as assessed through clinical interviews conducted by board-certified psychiatrists with more than 10 years of clinical experience; (3) A score of 8 or higher on the self-report version of the Panic Disorder Severity Scale; (4) No difficulty in reading and writing Korean; (5) No difficulty in using mobile apps on smartphones or tablet PCs; (6) Individuals who provided written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Presence of any of the following psychiatric symptoms at baseline assessment: schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, manic episodes, major depressive episodes, or substance use disorder; (2) Lifetime diagnosis of schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, or substance use disorders; (3) Uncontrolled or hospitalization-requiring illness at baseline assessment; (4) Suicide attempt within the past month or deemed to be a high risk of suicide by a psychiatrist; (5) Received CBT within the past month; (6) Visual or auditory impairments, organic brain disorders, or neurocognitive disorders; (7) Deemed unsuitable for participation by the investigator because of concerns regarding participant safety, compliance with study procedures, or the reliability of outcome assessment; (8) Change in psychiatric medication within the past month, excluding pro re nata (PRN; as-needed) benzodiazepine prescriptions.

Participants were permitted to continue psychiatric medications that had remained stable in type and dosage for at least one month before study enrollment. PRN benzodiazepine prescriptions were exempt from this criterion because they were regarded as as-needed rescue medication rather than changes in maintenance pharmacotherapy.

In addition, concomitant non-psychiatric medications and treatments initiated before study enrollment were allowed if they were used for stable pre-existing medical conditions and were judged by the investigator not to confound the interpretation of the study results. Examples included medications for dyslipidemia, atopic dermatitis, and gastritis. However, CBT during the study was prohibited because it could confound the evaluation of the device's efficacy.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention group used PanicDoc, a mobile app designed to improve symptoms of panic disorder, over an 8-week program that could be extended to 12 weeks based on individual progress. Participants accessed the app via their personal smartphones or tablet PCs. This app provides cognitive-behavioral therapy-based contents, and the overall structure and progression of the PanicDoc program are shown in Fig. 1. It consists of a weekly structured session: 1) psychoeducation, 2) self-monitoring, 3) relaxation training, 4) identification of maladaptive belief, 5) cognitive restructuring, 6) interoceptive exposure, 7) in vivo exposure, and 8) relapse prevention; daily training: 1) emotional diaries, 2) panic diaries, 3) relaxation training, and 4) exposure training; and personalized feedback provided once a week on the participants' training activities. The program adaptively provided review modules based on engagement (e.g., fewer than five emotional diaries) and anticipatory anxiety evaluations at weeks 3, 5, and 7, allowing a personalized and flexible progression through the 8-week core contents. Exposure tasks were personalized based on each participant's identified panic triggers, and users selected from system-guided tasks across stage 1–3 tailored to their symptom profiles. Participants were recommended to access PanicDoc at least three times per week for a minimum of 10 min per session, corresponding to at least 30 min per week. Participants were provided with a printed app user guide and received detailed instructions from trained research staff on how to use PanicDoc, including progression through the sequential weekly modules, recording of emotional and panic diaries, use of app reminders, and monitoring of weekly app usage time.Fig. 1Structure and progression of the PanicDoc program.The program consisted of eight sequential modules and supplementary modules provided according to participant progress. Structure and progression of the PanicDoc program.

The program consisted of eight sequential modules and supplementary modules provided according to participant progress. Emotional diaries and panic records were completed daily, while relaxation, cognitive, and exposure components were introduced within the corresponding modules. Participants in the control group received psychoeducation on panic disorder. Educational materials were developed by a psychiatrist based on the Mastery of Your Anxiety and Panic, 5th edition (MAP-5) (Craske and Barlow, 2022; Barlow and Craske, 2022), a widely used CBT treatment program for panic disorder. The psychoeducational materials were provided before each of the seven scheduled sessions. Topics included introduction to panic disorder, symptoms, causes, treatment, and prognosis. This control condition therefore consisted of structured psychoeducation with scheduled study contact but did not include systematic cognitive restructuring or interoceptive or in vivo exposure practice.

Measurements

Assessments were conducted twice: at baseline and post-intervention. In the PanicDoc group, post-assessments were performed at 8, 10, or 12 weeks depending on each participant's completion of the app. In the control group, the post-assessment was conducted at 12 weeks (Table 1).

Primary outcome

Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS)

The primary outcome was the PDSS. The PDSS is a clinician-administered tool that consists of seven items: frequency of panic attacks, distress during panic attacks, anticipatory anxiety, agoraphobic fear and avoidance, interoceptive fear and avoidance, impairment of or interference with work functioning, and impairment of or interference with social functioning (Shear et al., 1997). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4). Higher scores indicated greater symptom severity. In this study, Cronbach's alpha for the PDSS at baseline was 0.78.

Secondary outcome

Self-report version of the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS-SR)

The PDSS-SR consists of seven items (Houck et al., 2002). Unlike the original scale, the assessment period was modified from four weeks to one week to enhance the reliability of participants' recall. In this study, Cronbach's alpha for the PDSS-SR at baseline was 0.83.

Albany Panic and Phobia Questionnaire (APPQ)

The APPQ, developed by Rapee, measures fear related to activities that provoke unpleasant internal sensations, agoraphobia, and social phobia (Rapee et al., 1994). It consists of 27 items rated on a 9-point scale ranging from 0 (“not at all fearful”) to 8 (“extremely fearful”) and includes three subscales: interoceptive fear, agoraphobia, and social phobia. In this study, the APPQ-Agoraphobia subscale was used as a scale-derived measure of baseline agoraphobic fear burden in post hoc sensitivity analyses. Cronbach's alpha for the APPQ at baseline was 0.96.

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI)

The BDI is a self-report tool designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. It consists of 21 items rated on a 0–4 scale (B et al., 1996). In this study, Cronbach's alpha for the BDI at baseline was 0.93.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

The BAI is a self-report tool used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms (Beck et al., 1988). It also comprises 21 items, with participants rating the extent to which they experienced anxiety symptoms over the past week on a 4-point scale (0–3). In this study, Cronbach's alpha for the BAI at baseline was 0.93.

Clinical Global Impression (CGI)

The CGI-Severity (CGI-S) assesses the overall severity of a participant's psychiatric symptoms, and the CGI-Improvement (CGI-I) evaluates the degree of improvement in the participant's overall psychiatric condition (Busner and Targum, 2007). Both the CGI-S and CGI-I are single-item scales rated on a 7-point scale, with higher scores indicating a greater severity of psychiatric impairment. In addition, the frequency of panic attacks during the past week was assessed.

Satisfaction survey

In the post-assessment, a satisfaction survey developed for this study was administered exclusively to participants in the PanicDoc group. The survey consisted of 23 items that evaluated the app's interface, usability and overall user experience. 22 items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and one item was open-ended, allowing participants to provide their opinions. The total satisfaction score was calculated by summing the 22 items, with a maximum possible score of 88; higher scores indicated greater satisfaction.

Adherence and adverse events

Adherence was assessed weekly by evaluating each participant's access time to the program and the completion rate of the emotional diary. A total of 30 min of app use per week was considered 100% adherence. This threshold was established during program development as the minimum recommended weekly use, considering the expected time required to complete weekly app activities, the feasibility of app use in daily life, and user burden. In this trial, participants progressed sequentially through the assigned weekly modules under therapist guidance. Accordingly, adherence was assessed using overall app use time, which was intended to reflect engagement with the intervention as a whole rather than with individual modules. Emotional diary completion was assessed separately as the percentage of days per week on which participants completed the emotional diary. It was also used as an indicator of participant engagement to determine whether adaptive review modules should be provided. For the control group, adherence was monitored based on completion of the scheduled psychoeducation sessions.

Adverse events were assessed at each visit to evaluate the safety of the medical device. Data on adverse events were collected from participant reports, physical examinations, and other clinical assessments.

Sample size

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether the PanicDoc group was superior to the control group in reducing PDSS scores. Sample size estimation was based on between-group effect size estimates reported in two previously published waitlist-controlled web-based CBT trials for panic disorder (Allen et al., 2016; Oromendia et al., 2016). The reported effect sizes were 1.17 and 1.09, and the smaller estimate (1.09) was selected for a conservative sample size calculation. Assuming a two-tailed test with α = 0.05 and power = 0.90, the required sample size was 17.7 per group, which was rounded up to 18 participants per group (1:1 allocation). A previous study reported that dropout rates in CBT for panic disorder ranged from 9.8% to 42.1% (Apolinário-Hagen, 2018). Considering an expected dropout rate of 30%, 52 participants (26 per group) were enrolled.

Randomization and blinding

Participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were randomly assigned to either the PanicDoc or control group in a 1:1 ratio, according to their order of enrollment. Randomization was conducted at the Medical Research Collaborating Center (MRCC) of Seoul National University Hospital. An independent researcher at the MRCC generated the randomization schedule using SAS software and implemented the allocation using REDCap, an electronic data capture system, with stratification by sex. Participants self-reported sex (male/female) at enrollment. Gender identity was not assessed. This was an open-label trial; blinding was not applied to participants, intervention providers, or clinician-rated outcome assessors.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary efficacy analysis was based on the full analysis set (FAS), following the intention-to-treat principle. The per-protocol set (PPS), which included participants who completed the study according to the protocol without major protocol deviations, was analyzed as a sensitivity analysis.

Baseline characteristics were compared between the PanicDoc and control groups using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. The primary efficacy analysis was performed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) without imputation. The model included group, time, and the group-by-time interaction as fixed effects, with repeated measurements specified within participants. Least-squares means (LSMeans) and standard errors (SEs) were estimated at baseline and end of intervention (EOI), and between-group LSMean differences at EOI with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. In the PanicDoc group, EOI was defined as each participant's post-treatment assessment conducted at app completion (weeks 8–12), whereas in the control group, EOI was defined as the fixed week-12 assessment.

To address the difference in post-assessment timing between groups, a sensitivity MMRM analysis based on common assessment points was conducted using only participants with week-12 outcome assessments in both groups. Other sensitivity analyses included a PPS-based dropout-excluded MMRM analysis, MMRM analyses adjusted for age, marital status and baseline APPQ-Agoraphobia score, and LOCF-based ANCOVA analyses. The LOCF-based ANCOVA models were adjusted for the baseline value of each outcome, age, marital status, and baseline APPQ-Agoraphobia score.

Effect sizes were calculated using Hedges' g. Within-group Hedges' g was calculated by dividing the baseline-to-EOI change by the corresponding baseline standard deviation and applying the small-sample correction factor. For symptom severity measures, positive within-group values indicated improvement. Between-group Hedges' g was calculated by dividing the LSMean difference between the PanicDoc and control groups at EOI by the pooled baseline standard deviation and applying the small-sample correction factor. The 95% CIs for between-group Hedges' g were derived by standardizing the 95% CIs of the LSMean differences using the pooled baseline standard deviation. All tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant's allocation and demographic characteristics

A total of 62 participants underwent eligibility screening, of which 10 were excluded (Fig. 2). Therefore, 52 participants were enrolled in this study and randomized. One participant in the intervention group discontinued the study after randomization without using the PanicDoc app, resulting in 51 participants being included in the FAS. Among the FAS, four participants in the PanicDoc group and six participants in the control group withdrew from the study due to personal reasons. Among participants in the FAS, EOI outcome data were available for 21 of 25 participants in the PanicDoc group (84.0%) and 20 of 26 participants in the control group (76.9%). In addition, 3 participants in the PanicDoc group and 1 participant in the control group were excluded from the PPS because of the use of non-permitted medications. Consequently, a total of 37 participants were included in the PPS. Baseline comparison of clinical measures

No significant differences were found between the PanicDoc and control groups at baseline across all clinical measures, including PDSS, PDSS-SR, APPQ, BDI, BAI, CGI-S, and frequency of panic attacks in the past week (all, P > .26; Supplementary Table 1).

Comparison of changes in clinical measures between groups

As shown in Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1, the MMRM showed a significant between-group difference in PDSS scores at EOI, with the PanicDoc group showing greater improvement than the control group. At the EOI assessment, the PanicDoc group showed significantly greater improvement in CGI-I scores than the control group. However, no significant differences were found between the groups for changes in PDSS-SR, APPQ, BDI, BAI, CGI-S, or the frequency of panic attacks in the past week. Adherence, adverse events and satisfaction

Among the 21 participants who completed the PanicDoc, mean app usage time was 47.9±39.8 min per week, which corresponded to 92.8% adherence. The week with the longest app usage was week 3 at 96.0±105.5 min per week, and the week with the shortest app usage was week 10 at 16.0±1.4 min per week. All participants who remained in the control group completed the scheduled psychoeducation sessions.

Adverse events are summarized in Supplementary Table 9. A total of 11 adverse events were reported during the study, including three in the PanicDoc group and eight in the control group. In the PanicDoc group, the reported adverse events were common cold and flu-like symptoms. In the control group, reported adverse events included common cold, thyroiditis, diabetes, gastritis, gastrointestinal cramp, and self-harm. All adverse events were mild in severity and were judged to be unrelated to the study intervention. The assigned intervention was continued in all cases, and no serious adverse events were reported. There was no significant between-group difference in the incidence of adverse events.

Twenty-one participants completed the satisfaction questionnaire. The mean satisfaction score was 75.9±9.2 out of a maximum possible score of 88, corresponding to 86.3% of the maximum possible score.

Discussion

In this trial, PanicDoc showed significantly greater improvements in PDSS and CGI-I compared with a structured psychoeducation control condition. In contrast, no significant between-group differences were observed on secondary self-reported outcomes, including PDSS-SR, APPQ, BDI, BAI, and weekly panic attack frequency. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of greater improvement on clinician-rated outcomes rather than broad superiority across all symptom domains. Across study visits, however, both groups demonstrated significant symptom improvement on most clinical measures, such as the PDSS-SR, APPQ, BAI, and CGI-S. These within-group improvements suggest that structured psychoeducation, when delivered with regular study contact, may offer meaningful therapeutic benefits.

The discrepancy between clinician-rated and self-reported outcomes is central to the interpretation of the present findings. Because clinician-rated assessments were conducted in an open-label setting without blinded evaluators, observer bias, expectancy effects, and allegiance effects cannot be excluded. However, the observed pattern was not uniform across all clinician-rated outcomes. Although PDSS and CGI-I favored PanicDoc, no significant between-group difference was observed on CGI-S, suggesting that the findings cannot be fully explained by a generalized tendency for clinicians to evaluate participants in the intervention group more favorably. Moreover, CGI-I directly evaluates improvement relative to baseline and has been reported to demonstrate greater sensitivity and reliability than CGI-S, making it plausible that treatment-related differences would emerge on CGI-I but not necessarily on CGI-S (Forkmann et al., 2011).

Furthermore, psychometric studies indicate that the clinician-administered PDSS and PDSS-SR are related but not fully interchangeable measures. Wuyek et al. (Wuyek et al., 2011) reported significant correlations between the two versions at the total-score level but weaker-than-expected agreement at the item level, with more robust validity evidence for the clinician-administered version. In addition, the two instruments differed in their assessment windows in the present study: PDSS evaluated symptoms over the preceding month, whereas PDSS-SR assessed symptoms during the preceding week. Therefore, the two measures captured not only different reporting perspectives but also somewhat different temporal aspects of symptom severity.

Interestingly, clinician-rated and self-reported panic severity were broadly comparable at baseline, whereas divergence emerged only after treatment. This pattern may suggest that PanicDoc preferentially influenced aspects of improvement that were more readily captured through clinician-integrated assessments, such as avoidance behavior, functional impairment, or overall clinical status, than through patients' appraisals of their recent symptoms. At the same time, because PDSS-SR assessed symptoms during the preceding week, the lack of a corresponding self-reported advantage is not readily explained by differences in recall period alone. One possible interpretation is that clinician-observed improvement and subjective symptom experience changed at different rates during treatment. This possibility is broadly consistent with theoretical models proposing partial dissociation between clinically observable threat responses and consciously experienced anxiety or fear (LeDoux and Pine, 2016). Notably, similar discrepancies between clinician-rated and self-reported outcomes have been reported in other areas of psychotherapy research. A recent meta-analysis of psychotherapy trials in depression reported that clinician-rated outcomes frequently yield larger treatment effect estimates than self-reported outcomes, even in studies employing blinded assessment procedures (Miguel et al., 2025). Together, these findings suggest that differences between clinician- and patient-reported outcomes may reflect distinct perspectives on treatment response rather than assessment bias alone. However, because the present study did not directly assess the mechanisms underlying this discrepancy and clinician ratings were obtained in an open-label setting, these interpretations remain speculative and the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

The choice of comparator is also important when interpreting the present findings. Unlike many previous digital CBT trials for panic disorder that used waitlist or passive control conditions (e.g. (Ebenfeld et al., 2021; Lalk et al., 2025)), the current study employed a structured psychoeducation comparator delivered with comparable study contact and based on established treatment principles of panic disorder (Craske and Barlow, 2022; Barlow and Craske, 2022). Both groups received five in-person sessions and two telephone contacts, thereby reducing potential confounding related to therapeutic attention and support. Consistent with prior evidence that effect sizes are generally attenuated when digital interventions are compared with active rather than passive controls (Jung et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2025; Pompoli et al., 2018; Laws et al., 2022; Polak et al., 2021; Efron and Wootton, 2021), both groups demonstrated meaningful improvement across several outcomes. Psychoeducation itself may confer therapeutic benefit in panic disorder (Dannon et al., 2002), and the observed within-group improvement in the control arm supports this possibility. Although PanicDoc incorporated a broader range of CBT-based components than the structured psychoeducation comparator, the present study was not a classic add-on design because therapist-guided structured psychoeducation was not provided as a common background intervention to both groups. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of the comparative efficacy of PanicDoc relative to an active psychoeducational intervention, rather than as the isolated effect of adding specific CBT components.

The observed clinician-rated benefit of PanicDoc may partly reflect its adaptive and flexible treatment design. Previous iCBT and digital interventions for panic disorder have generally demonstrated efficacy, but many previously evaluated programs deliver a fixed sequence of modules with limited accommodation for individual differences in treatment progress or symptom fluctuation (Jung et al., 2025; Allen et al., 2016; Pompoli et al., 2018; Farvolden et al., 2005; Rubel et al., 2024). In contrast, PanicDoc allowed participants to progress through core CBT components at their own pace within a predefined treatment window while incorporating adaptive review modules when engagement was low or anticipatory anxiety remained elevated before exposure exercises. These features may have facilitated sustained engagement while allowing greater flexibility in treatment progression. This interpretation is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that personalized intervention elements may enhance treatment efficacy in digital CBT for panic disorder and agoraphobia (Jung et al., 2025). However, because component-level engagement was not analyzed, the present findings should be interpreted as the effect of a multicomponent digital intervention rather than attributed to any specific CBT component or personalization feature.

PanicDoc incorporated core cognitive-behavioral components—specifically, cognitive restructuring, relaxation techniques, interoceptive exposure, and in vivo exposure. These components have been identified as key therapeutic elements for panic disorder in prior meta-analyses (Pompoli et al., 2016). However, component-level engagement was not examined in relation to clinical outcomes. Therefore, the observed improvement in clinician-rated outcomes should be interpreted as the effect of a multicomponent digital intervention rather than attributed conclusively to CBT content alone. Repeated self-monitoring, app-based feedback and prompting may have contributed to improvement as digital intervention features (Heron and Smyth, 2010). Changes in panic-related beliefs such as anxiety sensitivity, and increased self-efficacy or perceived control in managing panic symptoms may also have contributed to clinical improvement (Gallagher et al., 2013). Finally, therapist guidance provided as part of the PanicDoc intervention may also have contributed to the observed treatment effects, although its independent contribution cannot be completely disentangled from the effects of the digital intervention itself. Future studies examining engagement patterns across individual intervention modules, rather than overall app use alone, may help clarify individual differences in engagement with specific treatment components and how these engagement patterns relate to clinical outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted in our study. First, although both groups received comparable levels of study contact, the study design was open-label. Clinician-rated outcomes were assessed by board-certified psychiatrists with clinical and research experience in panic disorder and digital therapeutics. The assessors were independent of the personnel who provided therapist guidance or psychoeducation. However, they were not blinded to treatment allocation. Given that between-group differences were confined to clinician-rated outcomes and were not observed on self-reported outcomes, observer bias, allegiance effects, expectancy effects, or Hawthorne effects cannot be excluded (Adair, 1984). Accordingly, the clinician-rated findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Second, although the sample size was determined a priori for the primary outcome using the approved protocol, the assumed effect size was derived from prior waitlist-controlled trials rather than from studies using structured active comparators. As active comparators typically yield smaller between-group differences, the study may have been underpowered to detect modest treatment effects, particularly on secondary self-reported outcomes.

Third, although the structured psychoeducation comparator was therapeutically intended and included scheduled therapist contact — reflecting a clinically feasible low-intensity add-on to routine psychiatric care — it did not constitute a full evidence-based treatment for panic disorder. Therefore, the findings primarily inform the comparative efficacy of PanicDoc relative to structured psychoeducation, and should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority over frontline evidence-based psychotherapies.

Fourth, differences in post-treatment assessment timing and the absence of follow-up beyond the treatment period limit interpretation. This may have made it difficult to fully distinguish treatment effect from effect related to elapsed time or intervention duration, although sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings. Furthermore, given the recurrent course of panic disorder, the durability of the observed between-group effects remains unknown (Roy-Byrne et al., 2006). Future studies should use harmonized assessment schedules and include longer-term follow-up assessments, such as 3- or 6-month post-treatment assessments.

Fifth, the intervention group's improvement may have been influenced by the natural course of panic disorder or concurrent pharmacotherapy (Adair, 1984; Bruce et al., 2003). However, this potential confounding was partly mitigated by employing a structured psychoeducation comparator with equal study contact, thereby reducing the influence of nonspecific treatment effects. Medication changes were monitored at each visit and were not permitted during the trial, and baseline concomitant psychiatric medication use was comparable between groups. Nevertheless, residual confounding by stable background treatment cannot be fully excluded.

Sixth, formal diagnostic characterization of comorbid conditions, especially agoraphobia, was not systematically performed. We therefore could not reliably report the frequency of comorbid psychiatric conditions. Although we conducted post hoc sensitivity analyses using the baseline APPQ-Agoraphobia score as a scale-derived measure of agoraphobic fear burden, these analyses should not be interpreted as adjustment for a formal diagnosis of agoraphobia.

Finally, the study sample consisted of individuals who were digitally literate, relatively well-educated, and motivated enough to meet stringent inclusion criteria. Although the standardized recruitment advertisement and call script did not explicitly describe exposure exercises, the possibility that additional explanations were provided during outpatient-based recruitment or the informed consent process cannot be ruled out. Therapist guidance during regular study contact, smartphone-use requirements, eligibility screening, and investigator judgment of suitability may have contributed to the relatively low dropout rate. Therefore, the low dropout rate should not be attributed solely to PanicDoc's personalization features, and the retention findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to routine clinical populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, PanicDoc was associated with greater improvement in clinician-rated panic severity than structured psychoeducation, without serious adverse events. However, superiority was not observed on self-reported outcomes, and the open-label design with unblinded clinician ratings warrants cautious interpretation. Further trials with larger samples, blinded outcome assessment, harmonized assessment timing, longer follow-up, detailed component-level adherence metrics, and comparisons against established evidence-based CBT are needed to determine its incremental clinical value beyond structured psychoeducation. Future work may help clarify and strengthen PanicDoc's clinical value through component-level analyses (Jung et al., 2025), exposure optimization based on inhibitory retrieval approach (Craske et al., 2022), and potential integration with wearable devices (Tsai et al., 2022). Conducted in a culturally relevant context, this study also contributes to the growing cross-cultural evidence base for dCBT.
