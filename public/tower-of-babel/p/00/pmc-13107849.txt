Clinical impact of a multidisciplinary remote-based hybrid antibiotic stewardship program in critically ill patients during COVID-19 pandemic in Korea: a prospective pilot implementation study
Authors: Suhyun Lee, Soyun Park, Min Gu Shin, Hyeong Geun Jo, Kyeoul Jeong, Ji-Eun Yoon, Kyong Nam Ye, Yeo Jin Choi, Minseo Choe, Seungwon Yang, Pureunnarae Kang, Jung-Tae Kim, Sung Wook Kang, Eun Kyoung Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) are recommended to optimize antibiotic use in intensive care units (ICUs); however, many institutions lack infectious disease specialists and pharmacy expertise for full implementation. The COVID-19 pandemic further disrupted conventional stewardship. While tele-stewardship models have shown promise, evidence for hybrid programs operating without infectious disease specialist leadership remains limited, particularly outside high-income countries. We developed a hybrid ASP (hASP) collaboratively led by an on-site intensivist and an off-site faculty clinical pharmacist to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use and improve outcomes in a resource-constrained ICU.MethodsThis prospective, single-center, pre-post study was conducted in the medical ICU of a 733-bed university teaching hospital in Seoul, South Korea. The pre-intervention period (August to October 2017) with no ASP was compared with the post-intervention period (August to October 2020) following hASP implementation. Stewardship activities were primarily performed off-site by trained clinical pharmacists via secured messaging, telephone, and video conferencing, with on-site rounds every other weekday. Key outcomes were inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions per 100 patient-days, 30-day all-cause mortality, ICU length of stay, preventable adverse drug events, and days of therapy, assessed using Delphi-derived appropriateness criteria.ResultsThirty-three and 37 admissions were analyzed (364 and 251 patient-days). On-site activities were limited to three hours daily or less. Inappropriate prescriptions declined from 83.8 to 20.7 per 100 patient-days (p < 0.001), ICU length of stay from 14 to 6 days (p < 0.05), and preventable adverse drug events from 4.4 to 2.8 per 100 patient-days (p < 0.05). Thirty-day all-cause mortality was comparable (24.2% vs. 24.3%). The most frequent pharmacist interventions were dose optimization (47.8%) and antimicrobial discontinuation (40.6%), with an overall acceptance rate of 62.3%. Days of therapy for broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting multidrug-resistant organisms declined while those for narrower-spectrum agents increased, suggesting a shift toward targeted therapy.ConclusionThe hASP, collaboratively led by an intensivist and a faculty clinical pharmacist with off-site pharmacist-led interventions via virtual communication, significantly reduced inappropriate prescribing, ICU length of stay, and preventable adverse drug events. Hybrid stewardship models may serve as a feasible alternative to on-site programs in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01740-9.

Background

Antibiotic resistance is one of the most pressing threats to global public health, attributable to approximately 4.95 million deaths in 2019 and projected to surpass cancer-related mortality by 2050 if left unaddressed [1–5]. Critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) are disproportionately affected: frequent empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic prescribing, approximately 40% of which is considered inappropriate accelerates the emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) and contributes to preventable adverse drug events (pADEs), including life-threatening and fatal cases [6–10]. Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs), particularly those employing prospective audit with feedback and preauthorization, are widely recommended to optimize antibiotic selection, dosing, route, and duration, ultimately improving patient safety and treatment outcomes [11–15].

Effective implementation of full-scale ASPs requires dedicated multidisciplinary personnel, typically including infectious disease physician specialists and on-site clinical pharmacists, along with substantial institutional resources [11–15]. However, many healthcare settings face significant barriers: critical global shortages of infectious disease specialists, limited pharmacy expertise dedicated to stewardship, and insufficient financial resources to hire full-time ASP personnel [15–17]. A nationwide survey in Korea found that pharmacy expertise was underutilized in ASPs, with most teams lacking a dedicated clinical pharmacist [17]. These manpower and expertise constraints have driven growing interest in alternative approaches, including tele-stewardship and hybrid ASP (hASP) models that leverage off-site clinicians and virtual communication technologies to supplement or replace conventional on-site stewardship activities [15, 18].

Several studies have evaluated remote and hybrid stewardship models across a range of settings. In a cluster-randomized trial across 15 small community hospitals, Stenehjem et al. demonstrated that the most intensive ASP intervention, combining on-site pharmacist review with centralized infectious disease (ID) support, achieved an 11% reduction in total antibiotic use and a 24% reduction in broad-spectrum antibiotic use [19]. In the Veterans Affairs healthcare system, a tele-antimicrobial stewardship model connecting rural medical centers with remote ID specialists through weekly videoconferences demonstrated the feasibility of extending stewardship support to facilities lacking local ID expertise [20]. Shively et al. reported that a telehealth-based ASP implemented across a community hospital health system improved antibiotic use, with sustained reductions in broad-spectrum prescribing [21]. These studies collectively indicate that remote or hybrid stewardship delivery can meaningfully improve antibiotic use across diverse healthcare settings [22, 23]. Nonetheless, published evidence for hybrid models that specifically combine limited on-site activity with off-site pharmacist-led virtual stewardship in ICU settings, particularly in Asian healthcare contexts, remains sparse. Moreover, most existing tele-stewardship programs have relied on infectious disease physician leadership, whereas models led by non-infectious disease clinicians such as intensivists or hospitalists have been proposed but infrequently studied [15, 19, 20].

The challenges of sustaining full-scale on-site ASPs were further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which strained global healthcare systems, enforced quarantine and physical distancing, reallocated ASP personnel to pandemic countermeasures, and simultaneously increased inappropriate empiric antibiotic prescribing in the absence of confirmed bacterial co-infection [24–28]. In response to these constraints, our institution, a 733-bed university-affiliated tertiary teaching hospital in Seoul, South Korea, developed and implemented a remote-based hASP in the medical ICU (MICU). The hASP was led by an on-site intensivist with an off-site faculty clinical pharmacist as co-leader, with the majority of stewardship activities performed by trained clinical pharmacists operating primarily outside the hospital through virtual communications, including secured messaging, telephone, and video conferencing. This model was designed to maintain rigorous stewardship activities while minimizing additional workload for on-site clinical personnel during the pandemic.

Although the tele-stewardship literature has demonstrated the promise of remote ASP models, existing evidence has primarily originated from settings led by ID specialists in high-income countries [22, 23]. Data on hASP models led by non-ID physicians such as intensivists remain scarce, and evidence from middle-income or pandemic-affected settings is particularly limited. Moreover, considering the variability in demographic, clinical, and sociocultural factors among different countries, the outcomes of such programs should be investigated in specific national contexts [29]. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of a multidisciplinary remote-based hASP on inappropriate antibiotic use and treatment outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic in the MICU of a university teaching hospital in South Korea.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, single-center, pre-post intervention study was conducted in the MICU of Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong (Seoul, Republic of Korea), a 733-bed university-affiliated tertiary hospital. Our MICU has 29 dedicated beds and is staffed by board-certified intensivists and critical care nurses. The pre-intervention period was August to October 2017 when no ASP was established at our institution. During the pre-intervention period (August–October 2017), no ASP was established. Antibiotic prescribing in the MICU was at the discretion of attending physicians and residents without systematic pharmacist review, prospective audit, or feedback. After regular working hours, clinicians on duty could contact pharmacists on duty for routine prescription review, but no formal ASP-related after-hours consultation pathway existed.

A conventional on-site ASP was initiated in January 2018; however, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in late 2019 substantially disrupted the on-site ASP operations due to enforced quarantine, personnel reallocation to COVID-19 countermeasures, and physical distancing requirements. In response, a multidisciplinary remote-based hASP was designed and implemented in February 2020. Our institution’s infection control policies comprise six major points: (1) design and implementation of infection countermeasures; (2) sterilization of hospital environment and device; (3) hand hygiene; (4) education; (5) infection surveillance system such as isolation, annual antibiogram, and reporting MDR or high-alert cases to the national agency, i.e., Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA as the counterpart of the CDC of the US); and (6) restriction of visitors to patients in the ICUs. During the COVID-19 pandemic, these policies were substantially reinforced: all visitors were required to wear face masks, only one caregiver per patient was permitted in patient rooms, and only essential clinical personnel were allowed on medical wards and in ICUs. As a consequence of these restrictions, bedside patient rounds were scaled down from daily to every other day, which directly informed the hASP schedule of on-site activities every other weekday.

After a 6-month run-in period of the multidisciplinary remote-based hASP (February – July 2020), the post-intervention period was from August to October 2020. Data from the conventional ASP period (January 2018 – January 2020) were not prospectively collected using the same standardized appropriateness criteria employed in this study and are therefore not available for direct comparison.

Participants

Adult patients at the age of 18 years or older, hospitalized in the MICU, were eligible to be included in this study. Only patients who received systemic antibacterial therapy via oral or intravenous routes were eligible for inclusion. Patients receiving antibacterial agents exclusively via local routes of administration (e.g., inhalation or topical therapy) without concomitant systemic antibiotic therapy were excluded. We excluded individuals treated with long-term antibiotic therapy over ≥ 4 weeks such as those with tuberculosis and those receiving an extremely short course of antibiotics for < 48 h. Patients with COVID-19 co-infection were excluded, as they were admitted to a separate, isolated COVID-19 ICU. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong (IRB No. 2020-05-038) with informed consent waived due to the observational nature of the research.

Implementation of the remote-based hybrid antimicrobial stewardship program

A full-scale, on-site ASP was initiated for patients in the MICU in January 2018. With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in late 2019, on-site ASP activities were substantially curtailed due to reallocation of ICU-based pharmacy personnel and enforcement of quarantine and physical distancing measures. Accordingly, the hASP was developed and implemented with a reduced on-site presence, comprising the following core members:

(1) An on-site intensivist physician specialized in critical care as the ASP team leader,

(2) A faculty clinical pharmacist with extensive clinical experience in critical care and infectious diseases, only available off-site,

(3) On-site resident or fellow physicians assigned to the ICU, and.

(4) Clinical pharmacists who completed ≥ 1 year of post-graduate clinical training in critical care and infectious disease pharmacotherapy, primarily available outside the MICU.

The full hASP team included the intensivist (on-site, team leader), the faculty clinical pharmacist (off-site, co-leader), resident and fellow physicians (on-site), and staff pharmacists (Table S1) [12, 13]. Other healthcare providers such as nurses and physicians of other specialties remotely participated in the hASP depending on cases as needed.

The faculty clinical pharmacist served as off-site co-leader of the hASP, supervising all intervention recommendations proposed by frontline clinical pharmacists and participating in virtual rounds for complex consulted cases (Table S1). On average, approximately one case per week was reviewed with the faculty pharmacist through virtual rounds, accounting for roughly one-third of all cases discussed during off-site virtual rounds. In addition, the faculty clinical pharmacist provided weekly educational sessions to clinical pharmacists covering ASP principles, infectious disease management, and critical care pharmacotherapy.

According to the practice guidelines for ASPs [11–15], we employed prospective audit with intervention and feedback as a core strategy of ASPs. Prospective audit with intervention and feedback was performed by clinical pharmacists reviewing electronic medical records (EMRs) of the enrolled MICU patients to identify any potentially inappropriate antibiotic therapy prior to participating in the on-site MICU rounds in the morning (approximately at 8:30) every other day during weekdays. Reviewed data in the EMRs included laboratory tests results, ongoing or newly prescribed antibiotic orders, and any other relevant clinical data.

Off-site pharmacists (including faculty pharmacists) lacked direct access to patient EMRs; similarly, this restriction applied to all clinical pharmacists on their off-site days. Instead, pertinent clinical information for consulted cases was transferred by on-site clinicians, including physicians and/or staff pharmacists, to off-site pharmacists via secured communication, and pharmacists formulated recommendations based on this information. Virtual communication was conducted using three modalities: (1) telephone calls; (2) the hospital’s secured in-house messaging system, which delivered SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation)-formatted messages to the cell phones of each hASP member; and (3) Zoom video conferencing for virtual rounds, secured by unique meeting IDs, required passcodes, ‘Waiting Room’ attendee screening functionality, and meeting lock upon initiation. On the subsequent on-site day, all patient cases were comprehensively re-evaluated, including those that had been reviewed remotely on the preceding off-site day. Prospective feedback for antibiotic prescriptions was documented in the EMR system only on the days of on-site rounds.

After regular working hours (i.e., typically, after 17:30), physicians on duty addressed any urgent issues with the hASP team using the secured SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation) communication system sending urgent messages based on the SBAR template to cell phones of each team member [30, 31].

Data collection and outcomes

Relevant clinical data were extracted by reviewing EMRs for included patients. The following data were collected from EMRs of each patient: demographics; comorbidities; laboratory test results such as renal function, nutritional status, microbiological culture and susceptibility tests; procedures performed such as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO); and medication administration records, focusing on antibiotics, including drug name, dose, duration of therapy, and route of administration [32]. Other relevant information potentially associated clinical responses (e.g., pain) and adverse events (e.g., rash) of antimicrobial therapy was collected from documented EMRs based on the previous antibiotic pharmacovigilance study [32].

Primary outcome of this study was the incidence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions, estimated by the number of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions divided by100 patient-days [33]. Appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions was assessed using the previously published criteria for antimicrobial prescribing in critical care [34]. Briefly, the previous criteria established by a Delphi process defined the antimicrobial therapy to be appropriate if the patient had a documented or suspected infection and all of the following statements were satisfied [34]: (1) the spectrum of antimicrobial therapy was neither too broad nor too narrow, (2) the patient had no documented history of allergic reaction to the antimicrobial therapy, (3) the patient had no contraindication to the antimicrobial therapy, (4) the antimicrobial therapy was administered via an appropriate route for the infection site and clinical condition of the patient, (5) the patient received the antimicrobial therapy at sufficient dose and dosing frequency for treatment of the infectious disease, (6) the antimicrobial therapy was anticipated to reach the target site(s) at sufficient concentrations, and (7) duration of the antimicrobial therapy for the patient was not greater than evidence-based lengths of treatment. Each component of antibiotic therapy appropriateness was assessed based on the most current clinical practice guidelines at the time of the study, published by globally recognized professional societies (predominantly the Infectious Diseases Society of America [11, 12]) and nationally recognized organizations including the Korean Society for Antimicrobial Therapy and the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases [13, 14]. For antibiotic doses not specifically recommended in available clinical practice guidelines, appropriateness was evaluated using labeled doses approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety in Korea.

An inappropriate prescription was defined at the level of the individual antibiotic order, irrespective of the number of doses administered or the total duration of therapy. Appropriateness was assessed daily for each antibiotic order based on the pre-specified criteria. Because off-site pharmacists did not have remote EMR access, antibiotic prescriptions from off-site days were re-evaluated on the closest on-site service day or during weekly table rounds for collected data review. For patients receiving combination antibiotic therapy, each individual antibacterial agent in the regimen was counted and classified separately; however, the appropriateness of the spectrum of antimicrobial coverage was evaluated based on the combination regimen as a whole. Appropriateness of the spectrum of antimicrobial therapy was assessed based on either empiric therapy or targeted therapy only when culture and susceptibility test results were available. Specific pathogens with susceptibility results were identified in fewer than two thirds of patients, and raw culture/susceptibility data were not separately recorded. EMRs for study patients are no longer accessible as the maximum electronic storage period (five years) has elapsed.

Antimicrobial therapy was considered inappropriate if any of the aforementioned statements was not met. Appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions was independently assessed by two clinical pharmacists; discrepancies between evaluators were resolved by ICU ASP team meetings in the presence of the intensivist and the faculty clinical pharmacist. For inappropriate antimicrobial therapy, the clinical pharmacist on duty provided recommendations to physicians during on-site rounds or virtually, primarily through secured messaging systems; pharmacist-led interventions were documented in the EMRs.

Secondary outcomes included the incidence of all-cause mortality in 30 days, the length of ICU stay (LOIS), the incidence of pADEs per 100 patient-days, and the total days of antimicrobial therapy (DOT) primarily for antibiotic agents typically considered to have a broad spectrum of antibacterial activity including those active against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Preventable adverse drug events were defined as ADEs resulting from inappropriate antibiotic use, which could have been avoided with appropriate antibiotic therapy, based on the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Center (WHO-UMC) criteria [35].

The antibiotics included in this study for the calculation of DOT and the evaluation of appropriateness are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for baseline characteristics, expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or median with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparison (i.e., pre- vs. post-intervention) was performed using the student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test for normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Categorical variables were compared between the two groups employing the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 33 ICU admissions (31 patients, 364 patient-days) in the pre-intervention group and 37 ICU admissions (33 patients, 251 patient-days) in the post-intervention group met the inclusion and exclusion criteria; two courses of hospitalization in the MICU were included for three patients in the pre-intervention group and four patients in the post-intervention group over the study period. None of the included patients in either study period had confirmed COVID-19 co-infection. One patient was transferred from the COVID-19 ICU to the MICU after completion of COVID-19 treatment with confirmed non-infectivity and was included in the analysis.

Overall, baseline characteristics on the day of ICU admission, including age, sex, height, weight, and comorbidity indices, were comparable (Table 1, Table S3). No other significant differences in severity of illness or underlying conditions at baseline were noted (Table 1, Table S3).

Appropriateness of antibiotic therapy with pharmacy-led interventions

Overall, 709 and 556 antibiotic orders were prescribed to our study patients during the pre- and the post-intervention period, respectively. Among the prescribed antibiotic orders during the study period, 305 (43.0%) and 52 (9.4%) antibiotic prescriptions in the pre- and post-intervention group, respectively, did not meet the pre-defined criteria for appropriate antibiotic therapy (Table 2). The estimated incidence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions was 83.8/100 patient-days during the pre-intervention period; this incidence was significantly decreased after the implementation of the hASP by 75.3% (i.e., 20.7/100 patient-days, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

For the 52 potentially inappropriate prescriptions identified during the post-intervention period, clinical pharmacists prospectively addressed 69 prescribing issues through verbal or written communications regarding the followings: increasing or decreasing antibiotic dosages, discontinuation of antibiotics for excessive DOT, expanding spectrum of antibiotic therapy (e.g., polymicrobial infections, infections caused by suspected or confirmed resistant organisms), streamlining antibiotic therapy based on the clinical microbiology test results (i.e., culture and susceptibility), substituting an antibiotic agent due to potential or confirmed ADEs, suggesting additional monitoring, and changing the route of administration. The most frequently recommended intervention was dose optimization (n = 33, 47.8%), followed by antimicrobial discontinuation (n = 28, 40.6%) (Fig. 1).

Type and acceptance of pharmacy-led interventions in the remote-based hybrid antimicrobial stewardship program (hASP)

Among the 69 pharmacy-led interventions, 43 (62.3%) were accepted by physicians with the highest acceptance rate for substitution of an antibiotic agent due to ADEs (3/3, 100%) followed by dose optimization (24/33, 72.7%) and then, antimicrobial discontinuation (16/28, 57.1%) (Fig. 1). None of the interventions for the followings were accepted by physicians: change from intravenous to oral antibiotic therapy (n = 1), expanding the spectrum of antibiotic therapy (n = 3), and antibiotic streamlining (n = 1).

Secondary outcomes

Despite significantly improved appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions, 30-day all-cause mortality was comparable during the pre- and post-intervention period (24.2% vs. 24.3%, p = 0.742) (Table 2). However, the LOIS was significantly shortened from 14 days before implementing the hASP to 6 days after the implementation (p < 0.05), resulting in a relative reduction in the LOIS by 57.1%.

The overall DOT was not significantly changed in the post-intervention period compared to that in the pre-intervention period (177.5/100 patient-days vs. 204.4/100 patient-days, p = 0.475). However, the DOT was substantially shortened after implementing the hASP for antibiotic agents frequently used for treatment of infections caused by MDROs (Fig. 2), including fluoroquinolones (by 12.7 days), antimicrobials active against MRSA (i.e., anti-MRSA agents, by 10.9 days), and colistin (7 days). For broad-spectrum antibiotics typically used as empiric therapy, a smaller change in the DOT during the post-intervention period compared to the pre-intervention period was noted with a decrease by 0.9 days for meropenem and 4.5 days for cefepime as well as an increase by 3.2 days for piperacillin/tazobactam (Fig. 2). After implementing the hASP, a substantial increase in the DOT was noted for antibiotic agents with a more targeted spectrum of activity such as third generation cephalosporins (by 5.1 days), clindamycin (7.7 days), and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (11.2 days) (Fig. 2).

After the implementation of the hASP, the incidence of pADEs was significantly reduced from 4.4 (during the pre-hASP period) to 2.8 cases/100 patient-days, resulting in a relative reduction by 36.4% (p < 0.05) (Table 2). The majority of pADEs during the pre-intervention period were associated with piperacillin/tazobactam (9 of 16 total events, 56.3%; Table 3), most commonly resulting in myelosuppression followed by hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and myopathy. The most frequent pADE during the pre-intervention period was myelosuppression (n = 5) (Fig. 3), followed by nephrotoxicity (n = 4), hepatotoxicity (n = 3), skin reaction (n = 2), QT prolongation (n = 1), and myopathy (n = 1). The incidence of each pADE reported in the pre-intervention period was substantially reduced in the post-intervention group (Fig. 3), except levofloxacin-induced QT prolongation whose incidence increased during the post-intervention period compared to the pre-intervention period (n = 3 vs. n = 1; Table 3; Fig. 3). Nephrotoxicity associated with supratherapeutic doses of vancomycin and myelosuppression associated with prolonged use of sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim occurred in one patient each during the post-intervention period, which were not reported in the pre-intervention period (Table 3). Otherwise, no newly reported pADEs were noted in the post-hASP group compared to the pre-hASP group.

Discussion

Implementation of the remote-based hASP was associated with a 75.3% reduction in inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions (from 83.8 to 20.7 per 100 patient-days, p < 0.001), a 57.1% reduction in the median length of ICU stay (from 14 to 6 days, p < 0.05), and a 36.4% decrease in the incidence of preventable adverse drug events (from 4.4 to 2.8 per 100 patient-days, p < 0.05). Thirty-day all-cause mortality did not differ between the two periods (24.2% vs. 24.3%, p = 0.742). These findings suggest that a hybrid ASP model utilizing off-site pharmacist-led interventions through virtual communication can meaningfully improve antibiotic prescribing practices and safety outcomes in a resource-limited ICU setting.

In this present study, we developed a remote-based multidisciplinary hASP model and evaluated its clinical impact on treatment outcomes of antibiotic therapy as well as appropriate use of antibiotics. The relative paucity of a feasible and effective ASP model in Korea might account for the higher rates of antibiotic consumption than the average rates in countries in the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) [36], potentially contributing to a relatively high prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens [6, 9, 37–40]. To overcome the barriers in securing substantial institutional resources for implementing sustainable on-site ASP models, particularly in the resource-limited settings such as the COVID-19 pandemic or the recent South Korean healthcare system crisis [41], our ASP model was developed as a hybrid form primarily operationalized off-site through secured messaging systems, phone calls, or video conferences with remote-based pharmacists including a faculty-level clinical pharmacist specialized in critical care and infectious disease pharmacotherapy.

It should be noted that our study compared the hASP period to a baseline before any ASP was established (2017), rather than to the conventional on-site ASP that operated between January 2018 and early 2020. Data from the conventional ASP period are not available, as systematic prospective data collection under the same Delphi-derived appropriateness criteria was not conducted during that period. The observed improvements should therefore be interpreted as gains relative to a no-stewardship baseline, and we cannot conclude from our data whether the hASP was superior, equivalent, or inferior to the conventional on-site program. Given the difficulty of implementing a full ASP model in many healthcare settings, our findings suggest that hASP with technology supplementation can serve as an alternative approach to maintaining stewardship activities.

Our present study demonstrated the feasibility of developing and implementing an effective hASP collaboratively led by an on-site intensivist and an off-site faculty clinical pharmacy specialist with the majority of ASP activities performed by off-site clinical pharmacists. Although standard ASPs have heavily relied on infectious disease physicians commonly leading the program [11–15], serious shortages of infectious disease specialists have been noted globally, especially in resource-limited settings such as middle-income regions or during COVID-19 pandemics when most of the infectious disease physicians were diverted to COVID-19-related cases and measures [24, 25]. To address these challenges, previous studies suggested clinicians other than infectious disease specialists such as hospitalists to be a potentially effective leader considering their substantial contribution to patient care, frequent prescribing of antibiotics, and sufficient leadership experiences within a healthcare system [15, 19, 20]. In our hASP, the intensivist served as the overall team leader providing medical oversight, while daily stewardship activities were operationally driven by clinical pharmacists under the supervision of a faculty clinical pharmacist. This dual-leadership model differs from conventional ASPs typically led by infectious disease physicians.

In addition, as suggested by previous studies [42–47], roles of adequately trained off-site clinical pharmacists have substantially expanded in our hASPs with minimizing additional workload for on-site clinicians during the COVID-19 pandemic through efficient virtual team communications for disseminating feedback and recommendations almost in real time. Significant reduction in the incidence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions after implementing pharmacist-led interventions in our hASP by 63.1/100 patient-days (Table 2) further supported the feasibility and effectiveness of utilizing pharmacists as essential personnel of ASP [44–47]. However, caution needs to be exercised that all of the pharmacists in our hASP collaborated with physicians and other clinicians as well as completed at least one year of didactic and experiential training in infectious diseases, antibiotic therapy, and critical care provided by a pharmacy specialist and physicians. Overall, our hASP confirms well-prepared pharmacists as an effective driver to achieve stewardship objectives even without constant input from an on-site infectious disease specialist physician if reliable communication systems and protocols as well as interprofessional collaboration are in place [17].

Consistent with previous studies [17, 48], dose optimization was considered the primary ASP intervention (Fig. 2) to adjust antibiotic doses based on altered pharmacokinetics in critically ill patients due to physiologic changes associated with critical illness [49–51]. In addition, our present study showed similar findings as previous studies suggesting antibiotic discontinuation due to prolonged duration of therapy as another frequent intervention. Overall, the acceptance rate of pharmacist-led interventions was relatively high (62.3%) with the highest acceptance rate of 100% for antibiotic substitution due to adverse events (3 out of 3 interventions) followed by 72.7% for interventions to optimize the antibiotic dosages (24 out of 33 interventions) (Table 2). Our findings further support previous study findings; pharmacists are often perceived as the clinical expert regarding adverse drug events and medication dosing in practice [52–54]. However, previous studies reported relatively higher rates of acceptance than those in our present study, ranging from 55.6% to 96.3% [48, 55, 56]. This might result from the design of our hASP model, reducing in-person communications while primarily operationalized through off-site virtual communications. In fact, a previous study implied a higher rate of accepting pharmacist-led interventions with on-site communications in the presence of the stewardship pharmacist accompanying patient evaluation performed by physicians [56]. Furthermore, several barriers to interprofessional communication in our hASP might exist, including, but not limited to, strict hierarchy in healthcare systems, cultural environments, physician autonomy [52, 57, 58], and limited human resources. Although not directly assessed, these barriers might collectively contribute to the relative lack of diversity in the type of pharmacist-led interventions as well as a slightly lower acceptance rate of pharmacist interventions in our present study. As our hASP was at an early operational stage, continued interprofessional training may further improve intervention diversity and acceptance over time [55, 57].

In contrast to previous studies suggesting 5 to 10% of reduction in inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions with ASPs [59, 60], the hASP in our study was associated with a 75.3% reduction (Table 2), although this large magnitude should be interpreted in the context of comparison against a no-ASP baseline rather than an existing program. As reported in a previous nationwide study conducted in Korea [61], this might be associated with a higher prevalence of antibiotic prescriptions including inappropriately prescribed antibiotics, particularly in critically ill patients in Korea compared to other countries. The substantial reduction in inappropriate antimicrobial prescribing in our present study was especially notable considering the hASP was implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic when broad-spectrum empiric antibiotic use was reported to increase inappropriately for the fear of secondary bacterial infections without confirmed diagnosis [62–64]. Pharmacist interventions to discontinue antibiotics following inappropriately excessive duration of use in our hASP resulted in substantially shortened DOTs (Table 2), particularly for broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting MDROs such as fluoroquinolones, anti-MRSA agents, and colistin (Fig. 2). However, DOTs were substantially lengthened for more targeted antibiotic therapy including sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, clindamycin, and third generation cephalosporins (Fig. 2). This indicates an appropriate shift in antibiotic prescribing pattern from broad-spectrum to targeted narrow-spectrum antimicrobial agents. No substantial change in DOTs was observed for cefepime, meropenem, and piperacillin/tazobactam (Fig. 2). Considering they are commonly used as an empiric agent [65–71], empiric antibiotic therapy at our institution might be appropriate throughout the study period regardless of the implementation of the hASP. This might suggest the effectiveness of restrictive measures sustained throughout the pre- and post-intervention period, preventing prolonged use of the broad-spectrum antibiotics typically used as empiric therapy. Overall, the significant reduction in inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions as well as decreased DOTs for broad-spectrum antibiotics might contribute to significant decrease in post-hASP LOIS (Table 2), potentially indicating an improvement in patient outcomes. However, caution should be exercised when interpreting our study findings; significantly shorter LOIS and DOTs for broad-spectrum antibiotics with our hASP might be associated with limited utilization of healthcare resources, including reductions in hospital stays, during the COVID-19 pandemic (i.e., our post-intervention period) [72]. Furthermore, it should be noted our current study failed to show a significant improvement in mortality with the implementation of hASP (Table 2), consistent with a recent study [60]. Considering mortality is a multifactorial and complex outcome influenced by various factors, multifaceted interventions might need to be implemented in a larger number of patients over a prolonged period time to reduce mortality [61, 73]. Overall, our study findings highlight the potential effectiveness of our hASP approach for critically ill patients in resource-limited settings, especially under pandemic-related workforce constraints.

While our hASP was developed in response to pandemic-related constraints, the operational model has broader applicability beyond crisis settings. Full ASP model implementation is difficult for many healthcare facilities, and hASP utilizing technology to supplement various aspects of stewardship can serve as an alternative. Hospitals in geographically remote or underserved areas, smaller community institutions that lack dedicated on-site infectious disease expertise, and facilities experiencing non-pandemic-related workforce shortages could adopt similar hybrid frameworks. The demonstrated feasibility of off-site pharmacist-led review using virtual communication platforms suggests that the core elements of our model are transferable to diverse healthcare environments. A recent systematic review confirmed that telehealth-based ASP was associated with decreased antimicrobial consumption and improved guideline adherence across a range of clinical settings [23].

In addition to effectiveness, safety of antibiotic therapy was substantially improved after implementing pharmacist-led interventions through our hASP with the incidence of pADEs significantly reduced by 36.4% (Table 2). In critically ill patients, ADEs commonly occur owing to frequent polypharmacy, underling comorbidities, and substantially complex pathophysiologic processes associated with critical illness [9, 74–76]. They are particularly concerning in critically ill patients due to their potential contribution to increasing mortality and morbidity in this patient population [77–80]. According to our previous studies, antibiotics such as piperacillin/tazobactam and ceftriaxone were most frequently implicated in clinically significant ADEs including life-threatening to fatal cases [9, 10]. The increased risk for clinically significant ADEs associated with antibiotics might result from a high prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions in critically ill patients as shown in our present study as well as previous studies [9, 61, 77–83]. As shown in Table 2, our hASP significantly decreased the incidence of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, and thus, substantially reduced the risk of pADEs associated with antibiotics. Indeed, most of the antibiotic-associated pADEs whose incidence was remarkably reduced in the post-intervention period were dose- or duration-dependent toxicities such as myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and myopathy [84–88]. Our findings support the effectiveness of proactive stewardship activities, particularly dose optimization and antibiotic discontinuation due to prolonged use in our hASP, for mitigating the risk of antibiotic-associated pADEs, ultimately leading to safe use of antimicrobial agents in critically ill patients. Despite improved safety of antibiotics with the implementation of our hASP, the risk for QT prolongation induced by levofloxacin was increased in the post-intervention period compared to that in the pre-intervention period (Table 3; Fig. 3). As suggested in previous studies, more complicated models such as those developed by neural networks using comprehensive data including demographic, clinical, and genetic assessment results might be warranted to quantitatively predict the risk for drug-induced QT prolongation with sufficient accuracy and precision [89–95]. Therefore, stewardship-focused interventions to prevent excessive doses or DOTs should be utilized in combination with a more comprehensive surveillance strategies to minimize the risk of antibiotic-associated pADEs in critically ill patients.

There are study limitations to be noted. First, our present study was conducted in a single MICU of a university-affiliated hospital in Korea with a small sample size over three months each for pre- and post-intervention period, respectively, potentially limiting generalizability of our study findings. In addition, patient comorbidities were evaluated using the Charlson comorbidity index; however, the impact of each specific comorbid condition on the appropriateness of antibiotic use was not assessed. Our patients had zero to more than five comorbid conditions documented in the electronic medical records, yet the potential influence of individual comorbidities, including immunocompromised status, on antibiotic prescribing outcomes was beyond the scope of this study. Future studies are warranted to investigate potential impacts of specific comorbid conditions on the appropriateness and outcomes of antibiotic therapy through ASP interventions. Second, due to the relatively short study period, sustainability of our hASP was not assessed in terms of cost avoidance as well as safety and effectiveness outcomes including long-term resistance patterns. Third, a temporal gap of approximately three years exists between the pre-intervention period (August – October 2017) and the post-intervention period (August – October 2020). A conventional on-site ASP was in operation at our institution from January 2018 until early 2020 and was subsequently restructured into the hASP following disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Because prospective outcome data were not collected during the 2018–2019 on-site ASP period using the same appropriateness criteria, a direct comparison between the hASP and the conventional ASP was not possible. The present study therefore compares the hASP with a no-ASP baseline, and changes in prescribing practices, resistance epidemiology, and institutional antibiotic policies that may have occurred between 2017 and 2020 independently of the hASP cannot be accounted for. Nonetheless, the operational framework described herein may hold practical relevance beyond the specific study period, given that full ASP implementation remains difficult in many healthcare settings; hASP utilizing technology to supplement stewardship activities can offer a workable alternative where conventional on-site programs are not feasible. Although the two study periods were matched by calendar months (i.e., August to October in 2017 and 2020, respectively), potential seasonal effects on antibiotic prescribing patterns at other times of the year could not be evaluated. Fourth, although the COVID-19 pandemic allowed us to implement the hASP model in our institution, surges in patients with COVID-19 and shortages of resources including administrative support might introduce uncontrolled and unmeasured confounders related to heterogeneity in the patient population and the overall quality of patient care, potentially affecting the overall outcome of our hASP. Lastly, The COVID-19 pandemic context may have introduced confounding factors beyond the hASP itself. Pandemic-related changes in hospital operations, including bed management pressures to optimize ICU capacity, heightened infection prevention protocols, shifts in the patient case-mix admitted to the MICU, and altered empiric prescribing behavior in response to concerns about secondary bacterial superinfection, may have influenced the observed outcomes independently of the hASP [24, 27, 42, 62, 64]. Although none of the patients in our post-intervention cohort had confirmed COVID-19 infection, the broader institutional changes during the pandemic cannot be fully disentangled from the effects of the stewardship intervention. Future multi-institutional research over an extended period of time should be warranted to longitudinally assess the program sustainability as well as clinical and economic outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a feasible, effective hASP model as a potential alternative to a fully on-site ASP to be applicable in resource-limited settings such as the COVID-19 pandemic period. The primary personnel in our hASP were clinical pharmacists adequately trained and led by a faculty clinical pharmacist as well as an intensivist, minimizing additional workload of on-site clinicians. Virtual communications through secure messaging, video conferencing, and notes on the EMRs allowed our hASPs to be operationalized primarily off-site with efficient and timely feedback on antibiotic therapy for individual patients. Our hASP significantly reduced inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions, contributing to substantial improvement in the safety and effectiveness of antibiotic therapy in critically ill patients. Considering the feasibility and effectiveness of our hASP, similar flexible approaches as our ASP should be considered in other resource-limited settings such as rural areas and healthcare system crises, contributing to the continuous battle against the ongoing threat of antimicrobial resistance.
