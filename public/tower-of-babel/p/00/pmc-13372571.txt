Laryngotracheobronchitis due to Candida albicans in a Patient Admitted to the Intensive Care Unit: A Case Report
Authors: Maura Silva, Cristina Mendes, Kátia Diogo, João Silva, Margarete Arrais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeveral studies have identified Candida albicans as the predominant fungal pathogen involved in respiratory infections. Candida laryngotracheobronchitis is a rare but serious condition, typically observed in immunocompromised patients with predisposing conditions, and its diagnosis poses a challenge due to its nonspecific presentation. We report the case of a 39‐year‐old woman who was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit for diabetic ketoacidosis associated with severe pneumonia, requiring mechanical ventilation and broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy. She initially responded favourably to treatment; however, 12 h after extubation, she presented with a dry cough, dysphonia and signs of persistent bronchospasm. The findings from bronchofibroscopy and the culture of tracheal and bronchial secretions and washings were consistent with laryngotracheobronchial candidiasis. She was treated with antifungal, resulting in significant clinical improvement and resolution of the endoscopic lesions. Currently, the patient remains stable and is under outpatient follow‐up by Pulmonology, Otolaryngology and Endocrinology.

Introduction

Candida is a fungus that is part of the microbiota of healthy individuals and rarely becomes pathogenic, however, numerous conditions predispose these species to become pathogenic, including age, endocrine disorders, malignancies, immunodeficiencies and prolonged treatment with antibiotics and immunosuppressants [1, 2, 3]. Several studies have identified Candida albicans as the predominant fungal pathogen involved in respiratory infections [1, 4]. Bronchopulmonary candidiasis is clinically classified into three types: tracheobronchitis (bronchitis type), pneumonia (pneumonia type) and allergic bronchopulmonary (hypersensitivity type) [4]. The bronchitis type is defined by Candida infection confined to the bronchial mucosa, without extension to the lung parenchyma. This form represents the mildest variant with the best prognosis among the three classifications and is frequently observed in patients with mildly compromised immune function or in those who have recently undergone broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy [4]. We describe the case of a woman who was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) at the Military Hospital in Luanda, Angola, for diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and severe pneumonia, underwent mechanical ventilation and treatment with broad‐spectrum antibiotics and subsequently developed laryngotracheobronchitis caused by Candida albicans .

Case Report

A 39‐year‐old woman, a nonsmoker with no known medical history, and no personal or family history of diabetes, was admitted to the ICU for severe DKA (blood glucose: 576 mg/dL; pH: 7.24; pCO2: 32 mmHg; pO2: 75 mmHg; HCO3: 15 mmol/L; BE: −16.7 mmol/L; Lactate: −3 mmol/L; SaO2: 93%; Na+: 134 mmol/L; K+: 3 mmol/L), associated with severe community‐acquired pneumonia. During hospitalization, she experienced cardiopulmonary arrest and was placed on invasive mechanical ventilation for 9 days and received broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy, resulting in progressive resolution of DKA and pneumonia and clinical stabilization. Twelve hours after extubation, she presented with a dry cough, dysphonia and signs of persistent bronchospasm, for which she was evaluated by otolaryngology and pulmonology. On physical examination, the patient was alert, oriented to time, place and person, in reasonably good general condition and nutritional status, with normally coloured mucous membranes, non‐icteric sclera and no fever. Oropharyngoscopy without alterations. Vital signs within normal limits (HR: 86/min; BP: 112/80 mmHg; RR: 18/min; SpO2:98% on room air). Normal chest configuration, vesicular breath sounds present, with some scattered rhonchi and wheezes. Cardiac sounds rhythmic and regular, without murmurs or extra sounds. Flat abdomen, mobile with respiratory movements, painless and without visceromegaly. Symmetrical limbs without edema. The patient underwent bronchofibroscopy, which revealed the following findings (Figure 1): The larynx (A), and the subglottic region (B) showed whitish plaques adherent to the mucosa and inflammatory signs; Tracheal mucosa covered by whitish plaques, firmly adherent, associated with inflammatory signs that caused a reduction in its lumen throughout its entire length (C); In the right main bronchus, the same changes to a lesser extent and without alteration of the bronchial structure (D). Culture of tracheal secretions and bronchial lavage did not reveal bacterial growth, the GeneXpert MTB (RT‐PCR) test was negative, and direct examination (Gram staining) and quantitative culture (Sabouraud Dextrose agar SDA) for fungi revealed the presence of Candida spp. and Candida albicans respectively. Cytological examination (Grocott's staining) of tracheal secretions and bronchial lavage revealed the presence of Candida albicans hyphae. No fungal elements, granulomatous lesions or neoplastic cells were observed in biopsy fragments of tracheal or bronchial mucosa obtained by bronchofibroscopy. Based on these findings, the patient began antifungal treatment with 400 mg/day of fluconazole and was discharged for outpatient follow‐up. By the 6th week of antifungal treatment, the patient was asymptomatic and showed no abnormalities on physical examination. A chest X‐ray revealed no pleuropulmonary abnormalities, and spirometry results showed that ventilatory parameters were within normal limits. The patient underwent a follow‐up bronchofibroscopy during the 8th week of antifungal treatment, during which we observed a slightly hyperemic tracheal mucosa that appeared normal (Figure 2A) and faint whitish plaques in the right main bronchus (Figure 2B). The patient continued treatment with fluconazole for 3 months, remaining asymptomatic and without complications. Currently, the patient remains stable and is under outpatient follow‐up by Pulmonology, Otolaryngology and Endocrinology.

Discussion

Fungal laryngotracheobronchitis is a rare clinical condition, and its diagnosis poses a challenge due to its nonspecific presentation, requiring differential diagnosis with granulomatous or malignant diseases [2]. We report the case of a 39‐year‐old woman who was admitted to the ICU for DKA associated with severe pneumonia, requiring mechanical ventilation and broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy. She initially responded favourably to treatment; however, 12 h after extubation, she developed laryngotracheobronchial candidiasis, which was treated with antifungal therapy, resulting in significant clinical improvement and resolution of the endoscopic lesions. According to Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines, the isolation of Candida spp. in respiratory specimens rarely requires antifungal therapy since their growth often indicates colonization [5]. The main distinction between colonization and infection lies in confirming whether the microorganisms invade the host tissues and trigger pathological reactions, with histopathological evidence being the gold standard for diagnosis [5]. In our case, the histopathological examination did not reveal the presence of Candida albicans; however, considering that this is a diabetic patient, the absence of other pathological conditions (such as oropharyngeal colonization), with the endoscopic findings described and the quantitative culture showing growth of Candida albicans in tracheobronchial secretions and washings, combined with significant improvement following targeted antifungal therapy, allows us to conclude that Candida albicans may have played a pathogenic role in this specific case, rather than being a colonizing organism. The discrepancy between positive quantitative culture and cytology for Candida albicans in secretions and lavages and negative histopathology in biopsy specimens from the tracheal mucosa and the right main bronchus, obtained via bronchofibroscopy, in our case can be explained by sampling factors, since tracheobronchial candidiasis has a multifocal distribution and predominantly superficial invasion, limited to the respiratory epithelium; therefore, small samples obtained by biopsy may fail to capture areas with hyphae/pseudohyphae, reducing histological sensitivity [5].

According to the IDSA, triazoles such as fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole and posaconazole have similar activity against most Candida species [5]. We opted for antifungal treatment with 400 mg/day of fluconazole due to its documented efficacy, good absorption and bioavailability, as well as its lower incidence of side effects [5]. Our patient was treated with fluconazole and was asymptomatic by the 6th week of treatment; however, although she remained symptom‐free by the 8th week of treatment, she underwent a follow‐up bronchofibroscopy, and because some fungal lesions persisted in the right main bronchus, we decided to continue treatment until the third month. It is also crucial to treat and control predisposing factors, as in our patient's case, controlling diabetes and resolving the pneumonia were key factors in the favourable outcome. Similar cases have been described in a retrospective study of seven patients with fungal laryngitis, all of whom were immunocompromised (some due to diabetes mellitus) and underwent antifungal treatment, resulting in clinical resolution and healing of the lesions [2]. A consecutive series of 11 cases of chronic Candida bronchitis showed a higher frequency of Candida spp. in cultures of the patients' bronchial secretions, all of whom had imaging abnormalities on chest CT scans [1]. The authors observed that the lack of antifungal treatment or early discontinuation of treatment was associated with symptom recurrence and death in one patient, concluding that chronic Candida bronchitis is associated with significant morbidity but responds well to antifungal treatment [1]. We conclude that Candida laryngotracheobronchitis is a rare but serious condition, typically observed in immunocompromised patients with predisposing conditions. Clinical suspicion and endoscopic examination of the airways were crucial for the rapid diagnosis and timely treatment, contributing to the patient's favourable outcome. The management of these cases must be tailored to the individual and requires a multidisciplinary approach.
