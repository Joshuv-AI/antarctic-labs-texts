Safety and efficacy of lorundrostat, an aldosterone synthase inhibitor, in patients with uncontrolled hypertension: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: A.B.M. Kamrul-Hasan, Subhankar Chatterjee, Lakshmi Nagendra, Sunil Nair, Deep Dutta, Joseph M. Pappachan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Limited data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) indicate that lorundrostat, an aldosterone synthase inhibitor, may be effective in managing uncontrolled hypertension (HTN). Currently, no systematic review or meta-analysis (SR/MA) has assessed the safety and efficacy of the drug in such clinical conditions; this SR/MA aims to fill that knowledge gap.Methods:Electronic databases and registers were searched from inception to June 30, 2025, for RCTs involving lorundrostat in the intervention arm and placebo in the control arm in individuals with uncontrolled HTN. The primary outcome was lorundrostat safety; additional outcome was its efficacy in lowering blood pressure (BP). Meta-analyses were conducted using the RevMan web with random-effects models, presenting results as risk ratios (RR) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).Results:Three low-bias RCTs with 1568 subjects were included. Over 6–12 weeks, lorundrostat (50 mg: RR 1.4 [1.23, 1.63]; 100 mg: RR 1.49 [1.29, 1.72]) increased risks of any adverse events (AEs), but not serious AEs, compared to placebo. Lorundrostat at both doses increased the risk of symptomatic hypotension. The lorundrostat group experienced a larger decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate and an increase in serum potassium levels. Lorundrostat 50 mg lowered office systolic (MD −11.11 mm Hg) and diastolic BP (MD −3.87 mm Hg) more than placebo, but not 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP. Lorundrostat 100 mg lowered office systolic (MD −8.09 mm Hg) and 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP (MD −7.18 mm Hg) more than placebo, but did not affect office diastolic BP.Conclusion:Lorundrostat effectively manages uncontrolled HTN but increases the risk of some AEs. More RCTs involving diverse populations are needed to improve clinical decision-making.

1. Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is the most common chronic disease and the leading risk factor for disability and premature deaths in the world, accounting for more than 9 million deaths annually.[1] Resistant hypertension (RH) is defined as blood pressure (BP) remaining above target despite treatment with 3 antihypertensive medications, including a diuretic at optimal or maximally tolerated doses, or by the need to use 4 or more agents to reach the desired BP control.[2] Clinically, distinguishing true RH, White coat syndrome, and nonadherence can be challenging. A meta-analysis of 91 studies from 1991–2017 with over 3 million treated hypertensive patients found true-RH prevalence to be 10.3%.[3] The pathophysiology of RH is multifactorial and not fully understood. Key mechanisms include excessive sodium retention, obesity, an overactive sympathetic nervous system, drug use for other conditions, and inappropriate activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosteronesystem (RAAS).[4] RH is strongly linked to adverse health outcomes, especially cardiovascular events and chronic kidney disease. A prospective cohort study involving 470,386 individuals with HTN enrolled in the Kaiser Permanente Southern California health-care program found significant associations between RH and all-cause mortality, ischemic heart events, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular accidents, and end-stage renal disease.[5] Treatment of RH involves removing contributing factors, managing secondary causes, and using effective multidrug regimens. Nonpharmacologic therapies like weight loss, exercise, salt reduction, and moderating alcohol consumption should be encouraged for all patients. Recent advances include therapies such as renal denervation, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), dual endothelin antagonists, atrial natriuretic peptide analogues, RNA-based therapies, sodium-hydrogen exchanger-3 inhibitors, compound 17b, and aldosterone synthetase inhibitors (ASIs).[6]

Excess aldosterone promotes inflammation, vascular changes, and tissue fibrosis linked to cardiometabolic diseases.[7] Due to its high prevalence as a cause of poor BP control, current RH guidelines recommend MRAs after 3 antihypertensives, though side effects, especially antiandrogenic ones, often limit use.[2] The emerging role of aldosterone-targeted therapies, including ASIs, is promising. Current ASI options include osilodrostat, baxdrostat, dexfadrostat, vicadrostat, and lorundrostat.[8] Aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) shares 93% homology with 11β-hydroxylase (CYP11B1), involved in cortisol production. Lorundrostat, a highly selective inhibitor of CYP11B2, is a potentially safe and effective treatment for aldosterone-dependent, uncontrolled HTN, including treatment-resistant HTN. Lorundrostat demonstrated highly selective inhibition of CYP11B2 in vitro, with a 374-fold selectivity for CYP11B2 over CYP11B1.[9] Lorundrostat, as a selective inhibitor of aldosterone synthase, spares cortisol synthesis. A recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examined the therapeutic role of 4 different ASIs in managing patients with HTN.[10] However, there is no comprehensive systematic review or meta-analysis (SR/MA) evaluating the therapeutic efficacy and safety of lorundrostat individually using data from the RCTs of the drug. This SR/MA aims to bridge this knowledge gap, supporting clinicians in making informed decisions for managing patients with uncontrolled HTN, including RH.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethical compliance

This SR/MA adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and the procedures outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.[11,12] The SR/MA has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251087974), and a protocol summary is available online.

2.2. Search strategy

Several databases and registries were systematically searched, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search covered these sources from their inception to July 8, 2025. Using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR,” the following terms were searched: “lorundrostat,” “MLS-101,” “hypertension,” “uncontrolled hypertension,” and “treatment-resistant hypertension.” All published and unpublished clinical studies were thoroughly searched using search terms applied across all fields without language restrictions. This investigation entailed examining relevant publications and citations within the clinical studies incorporated in this study. The complete search strategies are included in a supplementary file (Appendix 1).

2.3. Study selection

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study (PICOS) design was employed as a methodological framework to establish the eligibility criteria for the clinical trials included in this SR/MA. The patient population (P) comprised adults with uncontrolled HTN who were receiving stable doses of 2 or more antihypertensive medications. The intervention (I) involved daily oral administrations of lorundrostat tablets in addition to other antihypertensive drugs. The comparison or control (C) group consisted of individuals who received a placebo in addition to other antihypertensive medications. The assessed outcomes (O) included adverse events, changes from baseline (CFB) in automated office systolic and diastolic BP, and average 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP until the study end. The randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with a duration of at least 4 weeks were considered the study type (S) for inclusion. For inclusion, the trials must have at least 2 treatment arms/groups: one receiving lorundrostat and the other receiving a placebo. Studies of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of lorundrostat, as well as nonrandomized trials, pooled analyses of clinical trials, retrospective studies, case reports, letters to the editor, commentaries, conference abstracts, and articles lacking data on relevant outcomes, were excluded from consideration. Clinical trials involving animals or healthy humans, as well as RCTs lasting <4 weeks, were also excluded. The study selection process involved 3 independent authors who, after removing duplicates, assessed titles and abstracts according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This screening eliminated clearly irrelevant studies, allowing potentially eligible ones to move forward. After the authors reached a consensus, they retrieved and independently reviewed the full texts of the remaining articles in detail to determine eligibility. Exclusion reasons were recorded for each article, as shown in the PRISMA flow diagram. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus.

2.4. Outcomes analyzed

The primary outcome of interest was the safety outcomes in the lorundrostat vs placebo arms. Such safety outcomes included any adverse events (AEs), serious AEs, symptomatic hypotension, severely elevated BP, CFB in serum potassium levels and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), as well as hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and reduction of eGFR. The efficacy outcomes were CFB in automated office systolic and diastolic BP, average 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP, and achievement of target systolic BP at the study end.

2.5. Data extraction and handling of missing data

The extracted data included study characteristics (e.g., authors, publication year, journal, study design, clinical trial ID number, and funding sources), participant characteristics (e.g., sample size, age, sex distribution, baseline systolic and diastolic BP, eGFR, and antihypertensive medications at randomization), intervention and control group details, and trial duration. Additionally, as previously mentioned, data on primary and secondary outcomes were extracted. All disagreements were resolved through consensus. The details of the data extraction process and handling missing data are discussed in a previously published article by the authors.[13] Missing standard deviations were estimated from confidence intervals when possible, or study authors were contacted.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Review Manager (RevMan) computer program, version 7.2.0.[14] The results of the outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MDs) for continuous variables and risk ratios (RRs) for dichotomous variables with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The RevMan web-generated forest plots illustrated the MD or RR for the observed outcomes: the left side of the forest plot favored the lorundrostat group, whereas the right side favored the placebo group. Random-effects analysis models were selected to account for the expected variability resulting from differences in population characteristics and variations in trial duration. The inverse variance statistical method was applied in all cases. CI was calculated using the Wald-type method, while Tau2 was computed using the DerSimonian and Laird method. Meta-analyses were conducted in cases where at least 2 trials reported a variable. A significance level of P < .05 was established.

2.7. Risk of bias assessment

Two authors independently assessed the risk of bias (RoB) in the included RCTs using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials, version 2 (RoB2). RoB2 assesses bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.[15] The details of the RoB2 assessment have been described elsewhere.[13] The Risk-of-Bias VISualization (robvis) web app was utilized to create RoB plots.[16]

2.8. Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity assessment began by analyzing forest plots. Following this, Chi-squared tests were employed with N-1 degrees of freedom and a significance level of 0.05 to evaluate statistical significance. Additionally, the I2 test was applied in the subsequent analysis. The thresholds for I2 values were set at 25% for low heterogeneity, 50% for moderate heterogeneity, and 75% for high heterogeneity.[17] The details have been discussed elsewhere.[13]

3. Results

3.1. Search results

Figure 1 illustrates the steps involved in selecting studies. The initial search identified 145 articles, which were narrowed to 4 after screening titles, abstracts, and subsequent full-text reviews. A detailed evaluation led to the inclusion of 3 RCTs involving 1568 subjects that met all the inclusion criteria.[18–20] One Phase I pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics study was excluded.[9]

Flowchart on study retrieval and inclusion in the meta-analysis.

3.2. Characteristics of included studies

All 3 RCTs in this SR/MA were multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trials. The Advance-HTN[18] and Target-HTN[19] were Phase II trials conducted at multiple centers within the United States, whereas the Launch-HTN[20] was a Phase III trial conducted across 13 countries. The included participants in all studies were adults having uncontrolled HTN while taking 2 to 5 antihypertensive medications. The durations of the Advance-HTN, Launch-HTN, and Target-HTN were 12 weeks, 6 weeks (with outcome data reported up to 12 weeks), and 8 weeks, respectively. The baseline characteristics of the included RCTs were consistent across the lorundrostat and control groups. In the Advance-HTN trial, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to either receive a daily placebo (the placebo group), 50 mg of lorundrostat daily for 12 weeks (the stable-dose group), or 50 mg of lorundrostat daily for at least 4 weeks with an option to increase to 100 mg daily for the remaining 8 weeks if their office systolic blood pressure was 130 mm Hg or higher at week 4 and they met other biomarker criteria (the dose-adjustment group).[18] In the Launch-HTN trial, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:2:1 ratio to receive 50 mg of lorundrostat for 6 weeks, then possibly 100 mg for another 6 weeks (n = 270) if they met the prespecified criteria at week 6, 50 mg of lorundrostat once daily for 12 weeks, or a placebo once daily.[19] We considered the lorundrostat 50 mg throughout the study as the lorundrostat 50 mg group, and the dose increase to 100 mg as the lorundrostat 100 mg group for these 2 trials. The Target-HTN trial consisted of 2 cohorts. In the first cohort, participants were randomly assigned to receive either a placebo or one of 5 lorundrostat dosages: 12.5 mg, 50 mg, or 100 mg once daily, or 12.5 mg or 25 mg twice daily. In the second cohort, participants were randomized in a 1:6 ratio to receive either a placebo or 100 mg of lorundrostat once daily.[20] In this SR/MA, the trial arms for Target-HTN using once-daily doses of 50 mg and 100 mg of lorundrostat are referred to as the lorundrostat 50 mg and 100 mg groups, respectively. 3.3. Risk of bias of the included studies

Figure S1 illustrates the specific and overall RoB in the included studies. All 3 studies had a low overall RoB. Publication bias was not evaluated due to the insufficient number of RCTs (at least 10) in the forest plots.[21]

3.4. Safety outcomes

Compared to the placebo, a greater proportion of the study participants receiving lorundrostat experienced any AEs (for lorundrostat 50 mg: RR 1.41, 95% CI [1.23, 1.63], P < .00001, I2 = 0%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: RR 1.49, 95% CI [1.29, 1.72], P < .00001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2A). However, the risks of serious AEs were comparable in the lorundrostat and placebo groups (for lorundrostat 50 mg: RR 1.29, 95% CI [0.34, 4.87], P = .71, I2 = 56%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: RR 0.92, 95% CI [0.14, 6.01], P = .93, I2 = 65%) (Fig. 2B).

Forest plot highlighting the proportion of study subjects in the lorundrostat vs placebo groups that experienced. (A) Any adverse events, (B) Serious adverse events, (C) Symptomatic hypotension, (D) Severely elevated blood pressure.

Lorundrostat, at both doses, imparted higher risks of symptomatic hypotension (for lorundrostat 50 mg: RR 3.11, 95% CI [1.11, 9.89], P = .03, I2 = 0%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: RR 2.90, 95% CI [1.08, 7.77], P = .03, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2C). Compared to the placebo, a lower proportion of subjects had severely elevated BP with lorundrostat 50 mg (RR 0.44, 95% CI [0.20, 0.97], P = .04, I2 = 0%); however, the proportions were identical for the lorundrostat 100 mg and placebo groups (RR 0.49, 95% CI [0.07, 3.49], P = .48, I2 = 71%) (Fig. 2D).

Greater declines in eGFR were observed in the lorundrostat group than in the placebo group (for lorundrostat 50 mg: MD −7.16 mL/min/1.73m2, 95% CI [−11.26, −3.06], P = .0006, I2 = 18%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: MD −8.03 mL/min/1.73m2, 95% CI [−12.88, −3.18], P = .001, I2 = 62%) (Fig. 3A). Compared to the placebo, a higher proportion of subjects had reduction in eGFR with lorundrostat 100 mg (RR 3.09, 95% CI [1.14, 8.40], P = .03, I2 = 0%); however, the proportions were identical for the lorundrostat 50 mg and placebo groups (RR 2.08, 95% CI [0.54, 8.03], P = .29, I2 = 37%) (Fig. 3B).

Forest plot highlighting. (A) Mean difference in the changes from the baseline in estimated glomerular filtration rate, (B) Proportion of the study subjects that had a reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate, (C) Mean difference in the changes from the baseline in serum potassium levels, (D) Proportion of the study subjects that had hyperkalemia in the lorundrostat vs placebo groups.

The lorundrostat group experienced larger increases in serum potassium levels compared to the placebo group (for lorundrostat 50 mg: MD 0.40 mmol/L, 95% CI [0.07, 0.73], P = .02, I2 = 89%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: MD 0.39 mmol/L, 95% CI [0.17, 0.60], P = .0004, I2 = 65%) (Fig. 3C). Compared to the placebo, a higher proportion of subjects had hyperkalemia with lorundrostat 50 mg (RR 6.55, 95% CI [1.53, 28.08], P = .01, I2 = 0%) and lorundrostat 100 mg (RR 7.00, 95% CI [1.93, 25.42], P = .003, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3D). Lorundrostat 100 mg increased the risk of hyponatremia (RR 2.48, 95% CI [1.38, 4.47], P = .002, I2 = 1%), but lorundrostat 50 mg did not (RR 1.79, 95% CI [1.00, 3.22], P = .05, I2 = 0%) (Fig. S2).

3.5. Change in blood pressure

At the end of the trials, the reductions in the automated office systolic BP were greater in the lorundrostat groups than in the placebo group (for lorundrostat 50 mg: MD −11.11 mm Hg, 95% CI [−15.01, −7.20], P < .00001, I2 = 0%; and for lorundrostat 100 mg: MD −8.09 mm Hg, 95% CI [−12.16, −4.01], P = .0001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4A). A greater reduction in automated office diastolic BP was achieved with lorundrostat 50 mg (MD −3.87 mm Hg, 95% CI [−6.11, −1.63], P = .0007, I2 = 0%), but not with lorundrostat 100 mg (MD −4.02 mm Hg, 95% CI [−8.21, 0.16], P = .06, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4B). Lorundrostat 100 mg outperformed placebo in reducing the average 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP (MD −7.18 mm Hg, 95% CI [−11.24, −3.11], P = .0005, I2 = 0%); however, such an effect was not observed with lorundrostat 50 mg (MD −4.93 mm Hg, 95% CI [−11.74, 1.87], P = .16, I2 = 58%) (Fig. 4C). More subjects reached the target systolic BP with lorundrostat 50 mg than with placebo (RR 1.90, 95% CI [1.56, 2.31], P < .00001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. S3).

4. Discussion

4.1. Main findings of the review

This SR/MA is the first to exclusively evaluate the safety and efficacy of lorundrostat in patients with uncontrolled HTN based on evidence from RCTs. The results indicate that lorundrostat, a selective ASI, is effective in reducing BP, particularly office systolic BP, in patients inadequately controlled on standard antihypertensive therapy. However, its use is associated with increased risks of certain adverse events, including symptomatic hypotension, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and decline in eGFR.

Lorundrostat demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in office systolic BP at both 50 mg and 100 mg doses, with a more pronounced effect at 50 mg. Interestingly, while lorundrostat 100 mg significantly reduced 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP, such a benefit was not evident at the 50 mg dose, suggesting a possible dose-response relationship in ambulatory BP reduction. Such conflicting findings regarding the optimal dose of lorundrostat might be due to physiological variations in BP or simply the result of statistical variability given the relatively short trial durations. Longer duration trials are necessary to determine the most effective dose of lorundrostat for reducing systolic BP. However, the 100 mg dose did not significantly reduce diastolic BP in office measurements, which may be attributed to lower baseline diastolic BP values or sample variability.[18–20] These findings are consistent with the pharmacodynamic profile of lorundrostat, which targets aldosterone-mediated sodium retention and volume overload, mechanisms more strongly correlated with systolic rather than diastolic HTN, especially in older or comorbid populations.[9]

The efficacy outcomes observed are in line with those reported in the broader class of MRAs, such as spironolactone and eplerenone, which are recommended in resistant HTN due to their aldosterone-blocking properties. However, MRAs are limited by adverse effects such as gynecomastia and menstrual irregularities due to their antiandrogenic properties.[10] Lorundrostat, by directly inhibiting aldosterone synthesis and sparing cortisol production, offers a theoretically safer and more targeted approach.[9] Our findings, therefore, underscore lorundrostat potential as a novel alternative or adjunct to MRAs, especially in patients intolerant to hormonal side effects.

The safety profile, while generally acceptable, warrants attention. Compared to placebo, lorundrostat significantly increased the incidence of nonserious adverse events and symptomatic hypotension, reflecting the drug potent natriuretic and vasodilatory effects.[9] Notably, serious adverse events did not differ significantly between groups, which is reassuring and suggests a tolerable risk-benefit ratio under monitored conditions.

However, the consistent and significant rise in serum potassium and reduction in eGFR, especially at the 100 mg dose, raise concerns about potential renal adverse effects and hyperkalemia, a known complication of therapies that inhibit the RAAS.[22] These findings mirror safety concerns previously noted with dual RAAS blockade strategies and MRAs, reinforcing the need for regular monitoring of renal function and electrolytes during lorundrostat therapy. The increased risk of hyponatremia, especially at higher doses, although less expected with aldosterone inhibition, may be due to the widespread use of thiazide or thiazide-like diuretics among nearly all study participants (Table 1).[23] Reduced sodium and water reabsorption by the kidneys due to inhibition of aldosterone synthesis by lorundrostat can lead to volume contraction, which may also explain this effect.[24]

4.2. Clinical implications

Lorundrostat emerges as a promising agent for patients with uncontrolled or treatment-resistant HTN, especially those with aldosterone-driven pathophysiology. Its ability to reduce systolic BP effectively without affecting cortisol levels offers a mechanistically appealing alternative to existing RAAS inhibitors. However, routine monitoring of serum potassium and renal function is essential, particularly when used in combination with other potassium-sparing agents. Until further data become available, clinicians should weigh the benefits of BP reduction against the risks of metabolic disturbances, particularly in patients with chronic kidney disease or diabetes.

4.3. Strengths and limitations

The strength of this analysis is the low RoB in the included studies and the consistency of findings across trials, as reflected by low statistical heterogeneity for most outcomes. The use of automated and ambulatory BP readings enhances the validity of results, reducing measurement error and White coat effects. Nonetheless, the analysis is limited by the small number of RCTs and modest sample sizes, which preclude robust subgroup analyses (e.g., by age, sex, race, or comorbidities). Furthermore, the follow-up periods (6–12 weeks) may not fully capture long-term efficacy and adverse event trends. Importantly, all included RCTs were industry-sponsored, and the lack of publicly available individual patient data restricted deeper investigation into patient-level modifiers of response. Future independent, large-scale, and longer-duration studies across diverse populations are necessary to confirm these findings and evaluate long-term renal and cardiovascular outcomes.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, lorundrostat demonstrates significant antihypertensive efficacy with a favorable short-term safety profile. While it holds promise as a novel therapeutic option in the management of uncontrolled HTN, its use necessitates careful patient selection and monitoring. Further studies with longer follow-up and broader representation are needed to establish its place in treatment algorithms.
