Sex differences in nerve injury-induced neuropathic pain via REST in primary sensory neurons☆
Authors: Aadhya Tiwari, Ashok Subedi, Asieh Etemad, Yuying Huang, Biji Chatterjee, Samantha M. McLeod, Yungang Lu, Eun-Myung Mary Song, DiAngelo Gonzalez, Sanjay K. Singh, J. Jack Lee, Hui-Lin Pan, Sadhan Majumder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The RE1 silencing transcription factor (REST) is a key epigenetic and chromatin regulator that binds to the consensus RE1 sequence within its target genes via its DNA-binding domain (Ballas and Mandel, 2005, Gopalakrishnan, 2009, Kagalwala et al., 2008). REST contains two repressor domains that recruit multiple corepressors, including CoREST, HDAC1, HDAC2, Sin3a, G9a, MeCP2, and DNMT1, which collectively remodel the chromatin landscape to repress transcription. Initially thought to regulate only a small set of neuronal differentiation genes, REST is now recognized as a broad transcriptional regulator influencing both normal development and disease states (Ballas and Mandel, 2005, Gopalakrishnan, 2009, Kagalwala et al., 2008). Our previous studies demonstrated that aberrant maintenance of stemness through REST constitutes a critical oncogenic mechanism in a subset of medulloblastomas (Fuller et al., 2005, Lawinger et al., 2000, Su et al., 2006). We and others later identified a similar REST-dependent mechanism in glioblastoma (Bai et al., 2014, Conti et al., 2012, Kamal et al., 2012, Liang et al., 2016, Marisetty et al., 2017, Marisetty et al., 2019). Further, we and others discovered that REST also determines neural cell identity (Chong et al., 1995, Gopalakrishnan et al., 2010, Immaneni et al., 2000, Schoenherr and Anderson, 1995, Su et al., 2004, Watanabe et al., 2004). A recently developed full-length Rest knockout (KO) mouse model confirmed REST’s role as a repressor of neurogenesis during embryonic development (Nechiporuk et al., 2016). Beyond tumorigenesis, REST has been implicated in the regulation of embryonic stem cells, ischemic injury, epilepsy, chronic pain, and neurodegenerative disorders such as Huntington’s and Parkinson’s diseases (Ferrante et al., 2025, Hwang et al., 2013, Mall et al., 2017, Viskadourou et al., 2026, Willis et al., 2016, Yoo et al., 2009, Zhang et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2022).

Chronic neuropathic pain (NP) remains difficult to manage and represents a major ongoing clinical problem, profoundly diminishing the quality of life for millions of people in the United States. (Finnerup et al., 2016, Fullerton et al., 2018, Ghosh and Pan, 2022, Thouaye and Yalcin, 2023). Sustained increases in pro-nociceptive gene expression together with suppression of anti-nociceptive genes drive long-term abnormal excitability of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, resulting in enhanced nociceptive signaling to the spinal cord (Garriga et al., 2018, Laumet et al., 2015, Wang et al., 2002). For example, nerve injury triggers increased expression of α2δ-1 in the DRG, leading to enhanced NMDA receptor–mediated synaptic activity and greater glutamatergic input to neurons in the spinal dorsal horn (Chen et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2022). Moreover, nerve injury suppresses the expression of most voltage-gated K+ channels, thereby promoting increased excitability of DRG neurons (Chen et al., 2009, Laumet et al., 2015, Rasband et al., 2001). Epigenetic control mediated by the histone methyltransferase G9a in DRG neurons is likewise essential for the shift from acute pain to a chronic pain state after nerve injury (Laumet et al., 2015). Moreover, insights from pivotal studies across multiple systems have shaped our understanding of NP by defining key mechanisms and appropriate experimental approaches, thereby substantially advancing the prospects for developing effective therapeutic strategies (Bella et al., 2022, Burke et al., 2017, Crawford et al., 2024, He et al., 2022, He et al., 2023, Iyer et al., 2024, Laumet et al., 2015, Lauzadis et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2023, Puopolo, 2019, Rahn and Hohmann, 2009, Su et al., 2022, Tang et al., 2024, Wirt et al., 2025).

Recent studies have shown that nerve injury-induced NP and its alleviation through therapeutic approaches produce similar molecular and pain outcomes in male and female mice (Fiore et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2025). However, other research focusing on specific molecules, pathways, or therapeutic interventions has revealed distinct gender-dependent outcomes (Fiore et al., 2024, Grundtner et al., 2025, Sarka et al., 2024, Tam et al., 2024, Zaheer et al., 2025). As a result, it remains unclear whether sex specifically impacts nerve injury-induced NP.

Recent work has further shown that spared nerve injury (SNI) induces elevated expression of REST in the DRG of male mice (Mucha et al., 2010, Subedi et al., 2024, Willis et al., 2016, Zhang et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2018), which represses the transcription of pain-related genes. However, it is not well understood whether and how REST in the DRG of female mice regulates SNI-induced NP.

Further, earlier investigations of REST-driven chronic pain relied on a Rest conditional knockout (cKO) mouse model that was later shown to achieve only incomplete deletion of Rest (Nechiporuk et al., 2016, Zhang et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2018). This incomplete deletion has contributed to inconsistencies in studies of primary sensory neurons. To address this limitation, the Mandel laboratory subsequently generated a robust full-length Rest cKO mouse line (Nechiporuk et al., 2016), which was used in our prior work and will also be utilized in the current study.

Results

Peripheral nerve injury elicits sustained pain hypersensitivity in female wild-type mice

To determine whether neuropathic pain (NP) develops in a sex-dependent manner, we subjected female wild-type C57BL/6 mice to spared nerve injury (SNI) or sham surgery. Ipsilateral hindpaw withdrawal thresholds were then measured in response to noxious pressure (mechanical hyperalgesia), noxious radiant heat (thermal hyperalgesia), and von Frey filament stimulation (tactile allodynia) over a 4-week period (Fig. 1A). In female mice, SNI significantly reduced withdrawal thresholds in the injured hindpaw, mirroring the responses previously observed in male wild-type mice (Subedi et al., 2024). Because pain hypersensitivity was assessed using reflex-based measures, we also conducted rotarod testing to determine whether SNI affected motor performance. As shown in Fig. 1B, female mice subjected to SNI did not differ significantly from sham-operated controls in rotarod performance, indicating intact motor function in both groups. These findings are consistent with results previously reported in male wild-type mice (Subedi et al., 2024).

Spared nerve injury elevates Rest mRNA and suppresses Oprd1 and Cnr1 gene expression in the DRG of female wild-type mice

To directly compare DRG molecular signatures between female and male mice, we performed RT–qPCR to quantify mRNA levels of Rest, Oprd1, and Cnr1 in L3/L4 DRG tissues from female mice 28 days after SNI. As shown in Fig. 1C, nerve injury led to a marked upregulation of Rest accompanied by reduced expression of Oprd1 and Cnr1, closely paralleling the changes previously observed in male wild-type mice (Subedi et al., 2024). This pattern of transcriptional modulation paralleled the protein expression states determined by Western blot analyses (Fig. S1A and B).

The rescue effect of Rest cKO on pain hypersensitivity was significantly delayed in females compared to males

We used full-length Rest cKO mice to determine whether the increased expression of Rest in DRG neurons has a causal role in the development of chronic neuropathic pain. To generate Cre-induced Rest gene deletion in DRG neurons, we crossed the Rest cKO (−Cre) mice with Advillin-Cre mice. We subjected female Rest cKO mice to SNI or sham surgery, generating three experimental groups: Rest cKO (−Cre) + sham, Rest cKO (−Cre) + SNI, and Rest cKO (+Cre) + SNI (Fig. 2A). Ipsilateral hindpaw withdrawal thresholds to noxious pressure, radiant heat, and tactile stimulation were monitored over a 4-week period, as in the wild-type cohorts. Following SNI, female control (−Cre) mice exhibited robust reductions in mechanical, thermal, and tactile withdrawal thresholds. During the initial days after injury, corresponding to the acute pain phase, withdrawal thresholds did not differ significantly between female Rest cKO (+Cre) mice and their (−Cre) littermate controls (Subedi et al., 2024). In contrast, from days 10 to 26 post-SNI, hypersensitivities were substantially attenuated in Rest cKO (+Cre) mice. To determine the specific time points at which male and female responses differed, we further examined the sex × day interaction. For tactile sensitivity, a significant sex difference was observed on Day 8. For pressure sensitivity, significant differences were detected on Days 6 and 8. For thermal sensitivity, significant differences were observed on Days 3, 6, 8, and 10.

These results suggest that in males the rescue of hypersensitivity occurred earliest for thermal sensitivity, followed by pressure, and then tactile responses. In contrast, females showed the slowest recovery in thermal sensitivity, followed by pressure and tactile responses. Together, these findings suggest that the rescue of hypersensitivity occurs significantly later in females and that the temporal dynamics of recovery depend on the specific sensory modality.

DRG neuron–specific Rest cKO restores Oprd1 and Cnr1 expression in the DRG of female mice following SNI

We next assessed the contribution of Rest in DRG neurons to SNI-induced suppression of Oprd1 and Cnr1 expression in female Rest cKO mice. RT–qPCR was performed on L3/L4 DRG tissues collected 28 days after SNI or sham surgery from Rest cKO (−Cre) + sham, Rest cKO (−Cre) + SNI, and Rest cKO (+Cre) + SNI groups. As shown in Fig. 2C, SNI in female control Rest cKO (−Cre) mice resulted in increased Rest expression and reduced Oprd1 and Cnr1 expression, mirroring the patterns observed in male Rest cKO mice (Subedi et al., 2024).

Moreover, in female Rest cKO (+Cre) mice subjected to SNI, the injury-induced suppression of Oprd1 and Cnr1 expression was significantly reversed compared with control Rest cKO (−Cre) + SNI mice. These findings demonstrate that nerve injury potently represses Oprd1 and Cnr1 through REST in primary sensory neurons of female mice, consistent with observations in males (Subedi et al., 2024).

Discussion

Together, these findings indicate that REST contributes to neuropathic pain by suppressing Oprd1 and Cnr1 mRNA expression in the DRG of nerve-injured female mice, paralleling our observations in males (Subedi et al., 2024). As previously shown in male mice, Cnr1 expression in DRG neurons is a key determinant of pain sensitivity: DRG-specific deletion of Cnr1 results in heightened pain hypersensitivity (Liu et al., 2021). In complementary studies, we demonstrated that nerve injury decreases Cnr1 expression in the DRG via the histone methyltransferase G9a (Luo et al., 2020), a known REST cofactor. Moreover, reduced Cnr1 expression is associated with REST occupancy at the Cnr1 chromatin in injured DRG, supporting a model in which REST–G9a cooperatively regulate Cnr1 transcription following nerve injury.

In parallel, others have reported that conditional deletion of Oprd1 in DRG neurons enhances pain hypersensitivity after nerve injury or inflammatory insult, indicating that Oprd1 expression in primary sensory neurons normally dampens pain responses (Gaveriaux-Ruff et al., 2011, Jin et al., 2022). A recent multidisciplinary study showed that IL-10 signaling during pain remission enhances δ-opioid receptor expression and antinociceptive function in DRG neurons (Inyang et al., 2025). Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of this pathway precipitates pain relapse, highlighting the importance of δ-opioid receptor pathways in maintaining homeostasis in this process. Notably, because a canonical REST binding site is absent from the Oprd1 locus (Subedi et al., 2024), REST is likely to influence Oprd1 expression indirectly, potentially through modulation of intermediary transcription factors or microRNA-dependent regulatory pathways.

NP profoundly affects quality of life, often leading to substantial physical, emotional, psychological, and social burdens. Its development and persistence are shaped by numerous interacting factors, including stress, inflammation, diet, neuroimmune alterations, the endocannabinoid system, genetic background, and even early-life experiences (Bella et al., 2022, Burke et al., 2017, Colloca et al., 2017, Crawford et al., 2021, Crawford et al., 2024, Faust et al., 2024, Iyer et al., 2024, Kandah et al., 2023, Kringel and Lotsch, 2025, Rahn and Hohmann, 2009, Su et al., 2022, Tang et al., 2024, Wirt et al., 2025). Despite extensive investigation, studies examining sex differences in NP have produced inconsistent conclusions.

Our data suggest that although SNI-induced NP in both sexes involves REST upregulation in DRG neurons and repression of the same downstream target genes, REST plays a more prominent role in driving the transition to chronic neuropathic pain in males than in females. This sex-dependent difference suggests that REST is a more critical determinant of chronic pain development in males.

Sex hormones exert a major influence on pain sensitivity by differentially modulating neural pain pathways and circuits (Maurer et al., 2016, McEwen and Milner, 2017). In addition, sex-dependent variations in neuroendocrine–immune interactions—such as inflammatory signaling and microglial activation (Acosta-Martinez, 2020, Alexander et al., 2023, Alexander et al., 2024, Habib and Beyer, 2015)—as well as differences in pain susceptibility, including pain thresholds, endogenous pain inhibition, and stress responsiveness, further contribute to divergent pain experiences between sexes (Edwards et al., 2005, Staud, 2012). Thus, sex differences in nerve injury–induced chronic pain in mice reflect a multifaceted interaction among hormonal, genetic, immune, and neurobiological factors that collectively shape pain sensitivity and injury responses.

Further, the influence of the estrous cycle on neuropathic pain in females remains controversial, with inconsistent effects reported across injury models and pain modalities, generating significant interest in this area of research (Athnaiel et al., 2023, Boullon et al., 2025, Mogil, 2012, Vacca et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2023). We acknowledge the lack of estrous-stage stratification as a potential limitation of the current study, as cycle phase may contribute to variability in both behavioral magnitude and DRG transcriptional outcomes (Fig. 1A and C). Future studies will address this directly by prospectively monitoring estrous stage and stratifying analyses across defined cycle phases in neuropathic pain paradigms.

Although Oprd1 and Cnr1 are predominantly expressed in DRG neurons—and REST deletion in our model was also targeted to DRG neurons—future single-nucleus RNA-seq studies in both male and female mice will be necessary to achieve higher cell-type resolution and to determine potential sex-specific global transcriptional differences.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Research Animal Support Facility at MD Anderson Cancer Center. Additional wild-type C57BL/6 mice were sourced from the in-house MD Anderson Experimental Radiation Oncology facility and subsequently bred in our animal colony.

Full-length Rest conditional knockout (cKO) mice were generously provided by Dr. Gail Mandel (Nechiporuk et al., 2016). These mice were characterized by assessing Rest transcript and protein levels in littermate control (−Cre) and Rest cKO (+Cre) mice. DRG neuron–specific deletion of Rest was achieved by crossing female Rest-loxP+/+ mice with male Advillin-Cre mice (strain 032536, The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME). First-generation Advillin-Cre+/−: Rest-loxP+/− offspring were backcrossed with female Rest-loxP+/+ mice to generate Advillin-Cre+/−: Rest-loxP+/+ mice (Rest cKO). Littermates lacking Cre expression (Advillin-Cre−/−: Rest-loxP+/+) served as controls.

Mice were ear-tagged at weaning (3 weeks of age), and tail biopsies were collected for PCR-based genotyping. Genotyping of Advillin-Cre was performed using the following primers: Avil/003 (CCCTGTTCACTGTGAGTAGG), Avil/002 (AGTATCTGGTAGGTGCTTCCAG), and Cre/01 (GCGATCCCTGAACATGTCCATC), yielding a 500-bp wild-type band and a 180-bp mutant band. For Rest-GTi genotyping, primers GTA5 (TGGATGTTGAGGTCCGTTGTG), GTB5 (GCTACGGATCCCTTCTTCCC), and GTB1 (AACGGCCCCCGACGTCCCTGG) were used to generate a 480-bp wild-type product and a 600-bp mutant product. Western blot analyses confirmed a substantial reduction of REST protein in the DRG of female Rest cKO (+Cre) mice compared with their (−Cre) littermates (Fig. S1), consistent with findings in male mice (Subedi et al., 2024).

Adult female mice (8–10 weeks old; N = 5–6 per group) were used for all final experiments. To enhance breeding efficiency of Rest cKO mice, DietGel Prenatal Supplement (69-503-02; ClearH2O, Inc., Westbrook, ME) was provided to breeding pairs as a dietary supplement (10 g per mouse, twice weekly).

Spared nerve injury

Spared nerve injury (SNI) surgery was performed as previously described (Laedermann et al., 2014, Laumet et al., 2015). Mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane, and the sciatic nerve and its three terminal branches (sural, common peroneal, and tibial nerves) were exposed in the left hindlimb under a surgical microscope. The tibial and common peroneal nerves were tightly ligated with 6–0 silk sutures and transected distal to the ligation, while the sural nerve was left intact. Postoperative monitoring was performed daily for 3 days, and IACUC-approved postoperative records were completed. No opioid analgesics were administered post-surgery to avoid confounding behavioral outcomes.

Nociceptive behavioral testing

Pain-related behaviors were assessed by measuring hindpaw withdrawal thresholds in response to noxious pressure (mechanical hyperalgesia), radiant heat (thermal hyperalgesia), and von Frey filament stimulation (tactile allodynia), as previously described (Jin et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2018). To acclimate mice to the testing environment, animals were placed on the wire mesh grid and thermal testing apparatus for 30 min daily for 3 consecutive days prior to baseline testing.

Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated using a Rodent Pincher Analgesia Meter (IITC Life Science). The hindpaw was positioned between the device’s arms, and pressure was gradually increased until a nocifensive response (paw withdrawal and/or vocalization) occurred. The applied force (g) was recorded. Each mouse underwent three trials with 2-minute intervals, and the mean value was calculated.

Thermal sensitivity was assessed using the plantar test (Hargreaves method) on a heated glass surface maintained at 30 °C with radiant heat intensity set to 30% (IITC Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA). After 30 min of acclimation in the testing chamber, radiant heat was applied to the plantar surface, and paw withdrawal latency was automatically recorded. Three trials were performed per mouse, and the average latency was used for analysis.

Tactile allodynia was measured using von Frey filaments (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL). Mice were habituated on a wire mesh platform for 30 min before testing. Filaments were applied to the plantar surface for 3–5 s, and brisk withdrawal or paw licking was scored as a positive response. The 50% withdrawal threshold was determined using the up–down method (Chaplan et al., 1994).

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using a rotarod apparatus (Panlab Harvard Apparatus, Cornella, Spain) set to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm over 300 s. Mice were trained three times daily for 5 min each over three consecutive days. On the test day, mice were placed on the rotating rod starting at 4 rpm, and latency to fall was recorded as speed increased. Each mouse completed three trials, and the mean latency was used for analysis.

Tissue collection

Following behavioral testing, mice were deeply anesthetized with 3–4% isoflurane and euthanized. L3 and L4 dorsal root ganglia (DRGs) were rapidly harvested using the hydraulic extrusion method as previously described (Richner et al., 2017, Subedi et al., 2024).

Quantitative RT–PCR

Total RNA (1 µg) was treated with RNase-free DNase and reverse-transcribed using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (11766050; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (2 µL), diluted fivefold, was added to a 20-µL PCR reaction containing SYBR Green Master Mix (A25778; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) using the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 45 s. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Western blot analyses were performed as previously described (Subedi et al., 2024). Protein lysates from L3/L4 DRGs were prepared using RIPA buffer (89900; Thermo Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (78440; Thermo Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (23228; Thermo Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (NP0335BOX; Thermo Scientific) under reducing conditions and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (1620112; Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye 800CW (926–32213) and IRDye 680RD (926–68072) secondary antibodies (1:15,000; LI-COR) for 1 h. Blots were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies included rabbit anti-REST (07579; 1:250; Millipore) and mouse anti-GAPDH (MAB374; 1:300; Millipore).

Statistical analysis for Table 1

These analyses were performed using the Prism 8.0 statistical program. The data were presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analysis for two groups was conducted using Student’s t-test. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Turkey’s post hoc test. A difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. To determine the statistical analyses to assess response differences between male and female Rest cKO mice, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied to analyze the raw data. There were three response variable: Tactile, Pressure, and Thermal. The model has one between factor (sex: male vs female), one within factor (measurement in days), and their interactions. The sex by day interaction was further analyzed to identify the specific days in which the measurements between male and female might be different.
