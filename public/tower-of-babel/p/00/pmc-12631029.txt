Early-life exposure to secondhand smoke and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons: associations with epigenetic aging among children
Authors: Sneha S Patil, Anne K Bozack, Ajay Pillarisetti, Smita S Patil, Kyle Steenland, Kalpana Balakrishnan, Lance A Waller, Shirin Jabbarzadeh, Jennifer Peel, Thomas F Clasen, Dana Boyd Barr, Naveen Puttaswamy, Andres Cardenas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpigenetic clocks are valuable tools for assessing biological age. Exposure to secondhand smoke (SHS) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) has been linked with epigenetic age deviation (EAD) in adults. However, associations in children remain largely unexplored. We investigated relationships between exposure—spanning prenatal, early postnatal, current (36–60 months, time of sample collection) periods—and EAD in preschool-aged children. DNA methylation was measured in buccal cells from 43 children (mean age: 4.1 years) in the CAries Risk from exposure to Environmental tobacco Smoke—Household Air Pollution Intervention Network cohort. SHS exposure was assessed using urinary cotinine and 3-hydroxycotinine (3-HC), while PAHs exposure was assessed via urinary 2-naphthol (2-NAP) and 1-hydroxypyrene (1-PYR). Nicotine equivalents were calculated as molar sum of cotinine + 3-HC. EAD was estimated using Horvath, Skin&Blood, Pediatric Buccal Epigenetic (PedBE), and DNA methylation-based telomere length (DNAmTL) clocks. Associations between exposures and EAD were evaluated using linear regression, adjusting for study site, mother’s education, child’s body mass index z-score, age, and sex. Chronological age significantly correlated with all epigenetic clocks (r = 0.37–0.78) and with DNAmTL estimator (r = −0.38). Current SHS exposure, as measured by urinary cotinine, was associated with Horvath EAD (B = 0.23 years, P = .05). Similarly, current 3-HC and nicotine equivalents were positively associated with PedBE EAD (B = 0.08 years, P = .04; B = 0.11 years, P = .05, respectively). Among PAH metabolites, current 2-NAP levels were associated with principal component-based Skin&Blood EAD (B = 0.21 years, P = .02) and PedBE EAD (B = 0.13 years, P = .05). Findings suggest early-life SHS and PAHs exposure may contribute to accelerated epigenetic aging in children.Keywords: epigenetics, children, epigenetic aging, secondhand smoke, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons

Introduction

Environmental exposures account for 70%–90% of the global disease burden and nearly a quarter of all deaths, disproportionately affecting children under five and those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. These exposures are diverse and pervasive, with many pollutants capable of crossing the placenta and disrupting fetal cellular programming. The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease hypothesis posits that early-life environmental insults can trigger long-term physiological alterations, heightening disease susceptibility later in life [2].

Among modifiable environmental exposures, secondhand smoke (SHS) is a ubiquitous public health issue [3]. Despite a decline in smoking rates over the past half-century, 37% of the world’s population is still exposed to SHS, with a higher prevalence in children, and health risks exacerbated by racial and economic disparities [3, 4]. SHS contains over 7000 chemicals, including 69 known carcinogens [5, 6]. Of particular concern are polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), a class of chemicals generated from the incomplete combustion of tobacco, charred food, coal, and wood [7]. Epidemiological and clinical studies have corroborated associations between SHS [5, 6] and PAHs [8, 9] exposure and inflammation, oxidative stress, epigenetic modifications, and downstream adverse health outcomes, underscoring the need for reliable biomarkers to predict disease risk and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, represent a promising and persistent biomarker of exposure that may reflect a mechanistic link between SHS, PAHs, and lagged health effects. DNA methylation, primarily occurring at cytosine residues upstream of guanine (CpG sites), is influenced by environmental factors from the intrauterine period onward [10], with prenatal smoking having a strong signature in umbilical cord blood DNA methylation [11]. Growing evidence highlights the roles of direct DNA damage and epigenetic mechanisms in SHS and PAHs-related diseases [12, 13].

In recent years, epigenetic clocks derived from DNA methylation patterns have emerged as powerful machine-learning tools for estimating biological or chronological age and predicting age-related disease and overall health. The difference between epigenetic age (estimated via DNA methylation) and chronological age, known as epigenetic age deviation (EAD), offers a novel lens to evaluate health status and the impact of early-life exposures on aging trajectories [14]. In children, greater EAD has been linked with multiple detrimental health outcomes, such as asthma, obesity, cognitive impairment, cancer, and other morbidities—conditions robustly associated with SHS and PAHs exposure [5, 6, 15–18].

Although EAD has been linked to environmental exposures such as air pollution, adult tobacco smoke, and cadmium, studies have focused on the prenatal period or adult populations, with sparse and inconsistent evidence among exposed children [15–21]. Furthermore, prior work has largely examined single exposures and relied on blood-derived DNA methylation, leaving accessible, non-invasive tissues like buccal cells underexplored. To address these gaps, our study investigates epigenetic age biomarkers in buccal cells among children exposed to SHS and PAHs across prenatal, early-life, and current periods. We hypothesize that, similar to adult smoking, early-childhood exposure to SHS and PAHs would accelerate epigenetic biomarkers of aging.

SHS exposure and EAD

In adjusted models (Table 2), a two-fold increase in average prenatal cotinine concentration was inversely associated with Horvath EAD [B (95% CI) = –0.22 years (–0.44, –0.01), P = .04]. Conversely, a doubling of average postnatal and current cotinine concentrations was positively associated with Horvath EAD, with effect estimates of 0.23 years (95% CI = –0.00, 0.44, P = .05) and 0.18 years (95% CI = –0.00, 0.35, P = .05), respectively. Although associations with other clocks were not statistically significant, effect estimates for Skin&Blood and PedBE EAD were consistently positive but small. Average prenatal and average postnatal 3-hydroxycotinine (3-HC) concentrations showed no meaningful relationships. However, a two-fold rise in current 3-HC concentration was significantly associated with both longer DNAmTL (B (95% CI) = 0.02 kbs (0.00, 0.04), P = .02], and increased PedBE EAD (B (95% CI) = 0.08 years (0.00, 0.16), P = .04).

Discussion

Our study is among the first to apply multiple DNA methylation-based aging biomarkers, including the Horvath, Skin&Blood, PedBE, and DNAmTL clocks, to buccal samples from children in LMIC settings born to non-smoking mothers. Notably, epigenetic age estimates were significantly correlated with chronological age, reinforcing the applicability of the clocks to buccal-derived DNA in the pediatric South Asian population. Our results, particularly for current exposures, support the hypothesis that tobacco-related toxicants contribute to epigenetic aging signatures in early childhood. Average postnatal levels and current cotinine concentrations were significantly associated with Horvath EAD, while current 3-HC and nicotine equivalents correlated with PedBE EAD (B (95% CI) = 0.11 (0.00, 0.21), P = .05) and longer DNAmTL estimates (B (95% CI) = 0.03 (0.00, 0.05), P = .03). Current 2-NAP levels were linked to increased PC Skin&Blood EAD. These novel findings contribute to the evidence on how early-life environmental exposures shape epigenetic aging trajectories in children.

While previous studies have shown that prenatal maternal smoking is associated with accelerated epigenetic aging in cord blood, at birth [15–17], we observed a deceleration in buccal epigenetic aging with average prenatal SHS and PAHs exposure. This divergence may reflect tissue-specific responses, the timing of sample collection, or in-utero adaptations that mitigate toxicant effects or influence developmental maturity throughout childhood, though the long-term implications remain to be completely elucidated. Discrepancies in exposure source and intensity (active smoking vs. secondhand exposure) may also underlie these inconsistencies. By focusing on SHS and PAHs exposure during the early childhood period, our study expands the literature, emphasizing the role of pediatric epigenetic clocks in environmental insults beyond the prenatal window [16, 17].

Elevated average postnatal levels and current cotinine concentrations were positively associated with Horvath EAD in concordance with Simpkin et al. and de Prado-Bert et al. [15, 17]. Additionally, current levels of 3-HC and nicotine equivalents were related to PedBE acceleration and longer DNAmTL, suggesting that SHS exposure may influence multiple molecular markers of aging. Although an increase in DNAmTL may seem counterintuitive, telomere dynamics in early childhood are complex. Telomeres shorten most rapidly from birth to around age three, followed by relative stabilization; however, lengthening has also been documented in some children, potentially reflecting telomerase activity in hematopoietic stem cells or shifts in leukocyte cell composition during growth [22]. Thus, increases in DNAmTL in our study may reflect altered cellular turnover or compensatory proliferative responses in buccal epithelial tissue rather than simple telomere attrition. Prior research suggests that DNAmTL captures broader aspects of cellular aging independent of telomere attrition and telomerase activity [23]. Increased DNAmTL has also been reported in certain cancer contexts, further underscoring the complexity of interpreting these epigenetic markers in relation to environmental exposures. Parallel associations were discerned between PAH metabolites: 2-NAP and postnatal 1-PYR and acceleration in Skin&Blood and PedBE clocks, implicating the putative role of PAHs as epigenetic modulators during critical developmental periods.

The application of epigenetic clocks in children remains fairly novel. While first-generation clocks primarily predict chronological age, newer clocks trained on phenotypic aging or mortality aim to capture broader biological aging processes but are mostly trained in adults [24]. Among those tailored for pediatric populations, the PedBE clock, trained on 1032 individuals aged 2–20 years, demonstrated the strongest correlation with age in our data as expected. The Horvath and Skin&Blood clocks also yielded significant, albeit weaker correlations with chronological age (P < .001), likely due to their multi-tissue training sets, which included buccal cells and the broader age range represented in their training data. We excluded second-generation clocks developed from adult phenotypes, as these may be less relevant to early-life biology. Despite promising patterns, it remains unclear how variation in pediatric epigenetic clocks translates to long-term health outcomes. The limited CpG overlap among clocks implies that each may index distinct biological domains, such as transcriptional regulation, telomere biology, cellular activation, and senescence [25]. While significant associations were observed with the traditional Horvath clock, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The PC Horvath clock, which mitigates missing CpGs and technical variability, did not demonstrate comparable significance, suggesting that the Horvath clock associations may in part reflect measurement noise in our relatively small sample. Replication in larger cohorts is imperative to substantiate these findings. Persistent associations observed across multiple clocks in our study suggest that early-life SHS and PAHs exposure may broadly perturb interconnected aging processes, potentially via oxidative stress and immune-inflammatory dysregulation, although the precise molecular intermediaries remain elusive.

Congruous with our a priori hypothesis, adjustment for buccal cell-type composition modestly attenuated associations with Horvath EAD, suggesting partial confounding via immune or inflammatory-mediated pathways. Conversely, associations with PedBE and Skin&Blood clocks persisted, indicating either reduced sensitivity to tissue heterogeneity or enhanced specificity to exposure-related molecular alterations. Together, these results accentuate the complexity of environmentally driven epigenetic aging during early childhood. While buccal DNA methylation profiling offers a non-invasive and scalable alternative for pediatric research, the lack of clocks calibrated exclusively for buccal tissue and interindividual cellular heterogeneity may introduce measurement imprecision for certain EAD metrics. Nonetheless, our work demonstrates the feasibility of conducting high-dimensional epigenetic analyses using buccal-derived DNA from young children in LMIC settings. This represents a distinctive strength given the logistical, infrastructural, and ethical challenges of pediatric biospecimen collection in such contexts.

Additional strengths include the detailed assessment of environmental exposures from the prenatal to early-life period, key windows of rapid biological plasticity, and use of sensitivity analyses that collectively augmented the robustness of our findings. However, some limitations merit consideration. The relatively small sample size for DNA methylation analyses may have limited our ability to detect small to modest associations. Despite covariate adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured variables remains possible. Moreover, though we assessed exposures across early-life, the full complexity of the exposome, or all potential contributors to EAD was not captured. Our analyses focused exclusively on established epigenetic clocks, which are calculated using DNA methylation levels at CpG sites representing a small subset of the epigenome. Consequently, other exposure-related methylation changes may not have been detected. While this approach was appropriate for our hypothesis-driven focus on age- and development-related pathways, it does not encompass the full epigenome. Future epigenome-wide analyses with larger sample sizes and appropriate replication may provide broader insights. Finally, although the LMIC context amplifies the public health relevance of these findings, it may limit generalizability to other populations with different environmental or genetic backgrounds. Validation in other cohorts is warranted.

In conclusion, our study identified early-life exposure to SHS and PAHs as modifiable determinants of EAD in children, adding to growing evidence that epigenetic clocks serve as sensitive biomarkers of environmental exposures in early-life spanning from the prenatal period to childhood. While some associations were modest and did not attain significance, the consistency in directional patterns, particularly for current exposures, implicates these environmental pollutants as potential modulators of biological aging processes during crucial developmental windows. Future prospective investigations are needed to determine whether such early-life exposures develop epigenetic alterations over time and, in turn, whether these changes confer increased disease risk in later life.

Methods

Study population

The CAries Risk from exposure to Environmental tobacco Smoke (CARES) study is nested within the Indian cohort of the multi-country Household Air Pollution Intervention Network (HAPIN) trial. Details of the study design and participants are provided elsewhere [26–28]. Between April and September 2023, 712 children aged 3–5 years were followed up in Tamil Nadu. Of these, 48 children were selected for DNA methylation analyses using a stratified multistage cluster sampling design. Stratification was based on district (Villupuram and Nagapattinam) and caregiver-reported environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) exposure. Within each stratum, clusters—defined as villages or administrative blocks—were randomly drawn to ensure representation across the ETS exposure spectrum.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at Stanford School of Medicine and Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (IEC-NI22/JUL/83/82). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants. Trained interviewers administered structured questionnaires to collect data on sociodemographic factors, maternal education, dietary diversity, and child characteristics. The child’s body mass was measured using a foot-to-foot bioimpedance scale, and height was measured in triplicate using a stadiometer and averaged. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. ETS exposure was assessed via caregiver-reported household smoking behaviours. Ambiguous or missing responses were resolved through follow-up calls.

Exposure assessment

Urine samples were collected from pregnant women in each trimester and from children at postnatal visits (3, 6, 12, and 36–60 months). Biomarker concentrations were normalized to urinary creatinine to account for urine dilution [29], which was measured by photometric detection using the Jaffé method [30].

Urinary SHS biomarkers

Urinary concentrations of cotinine and 3-HC were measured using liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) [29, 31]. A 200 µL urine sample was spiked with an isotopically labelled internal standard, mixed with cold acetone, refrigerated, and centrifuged to precipitate proteins. The supernatant was processed, and 10 µL was injected for analysis. All analyses were performed without glucuronidase deconjugation in order to quantify free (unconjugated) analytes, which represent the biologically active fraction most likely to mediate epigenetic effects. Quality control samples, including blanks, were analyzed concurrently. The limit of detection (LOD) for both cotinine and 3-HC was 0.05 ng/mL, with mean accuracy ranging from 81.7% to 88.7% (Supplementary Table S4). Nicotine equivalents were calculated as the sum of cotinine + 3-HC.

Urinary PAH metabolites

Urinary levels of monohydroxy PAH metabolites (2-NAP and 1-PYR) were measured using a validated method [32]. A 2 mL urine aliquot was incubated with β-glucuronidase (from Helix pomatia, type HP-2) and sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5). Analyses were conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography (Shimadzu LC-2010A HPLC system, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with RF-10AXL fluorescence detector. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Gemini C6-Phenyl RP column. The mobile phase consisted of phosphate buffer and acetonitrile. The LODs were 0.02 ng/mL for 2-NAP and 0.06 ng/mL for 1-PYR, with mean accuracies of 102.8% and 104.6%, respectively. Quality control samples were included for each batch (Supplementary Table S4).

Microarray quantification of DNA methylation

Buccal cell samples were collected using Oragene kits (OC-175, DNA Genotek), and genomic DNA was extracted using QIAGEN kits per manufacturer’s protocols. DNA yield was assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Bisulfite modification of genomic DNA was performed using the Zymo DNA Methylation kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), and samples were hybridized to Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip arrays (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Samples were randomized across chips prior to measurement. DNA methylation data were imported into R (version 4.4.1) and subsequently preprocessed using the SeSAMe package [33]. Non-linear dye-bias correction and background subtraction were performed using the normal-exponential out-of-band (noob) method [34]. Sample-level QC included dropping samples based on low intensity (<10.5) (n = 0), inadequate bisulfite conversion (n = 0), or sex mismatches (n = 5). ComBat from the sva package was used to remove residual batch effects from the sample row in the BeadChip [35]. Density distributions were visualized at each processing step, and principal component analysis was conducted to evaluate associations of technical, biological, and measured traits with global DNA methylation variation. The DNA methylation status for each CpG locus was calculated as a Beta value, ranging from zero to one representing % methylation. Buccal cell proportions—epithelial, fibroblast, and immune cells, were estimated using EpiDISH [36].

Calculation of epigenetic aging biomarkers

Epigenetic clocks were calculated using the methscore [37] function in the ENmix package [38], including Horvath, Skin&Blood, PedBE, and DNAmTL due to their performance in children [23, 39–41]. Due to the missingness of clock CpGs on the EPIC v2.0 BeadChip, we also calculated PC-based versions of the Horvath clock, Skin&Blood, and DNAmTL [42]. This approach is less influenced by the missingness of individual CpGs and array-type differences. We estimated the outcome of interest, EAD, as the residuals from regressing each clock on the participants’ chronological age. A positive EAD indicates advanced biological aging relative to chronological age, and a negative age-adjusted DNAmTL reflects shorter telomere length. Supplementary Table S5 summarizes these biomarkers, their health outcomes predicted, and the tissues used in training.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated as mean and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. To meet assumptions of normality and heteroscedasticity, we used log₂-transformed exposure concentrations in the analyses, where a one-unit increase reflects a doubling of the original levels. Average (i.e. cumulative mean) prenatal and postnatal levels were calculated as the mean of concentrations measured across the three trimesters and at 3, 6, and 12 months, respectively. Current exposure (36–60 months) reflects levels at sample collection. To evaluate the performance of the epigenetic clocks, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients and the MAE between estimated epigenetic age and chronological age. Potential confounders were identified a priori and included study site (Villupuram or Nagapattinam), maternal education (no formal education, primary school completed, or secondary school/college completed), child’s age- and sex-specific BMI z-score (derived from WHO growth standards), age (continuous), and biological sex. Associations between exposure and EAD were evaluated using linear regression models, adjusting for these covariates. Sensitivity analyses included additional adjustments for estimated cell-type proportions (epithelial, fibroblast, and immune cells). Results are reported as point estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P-values from covariate-adjusted models.
