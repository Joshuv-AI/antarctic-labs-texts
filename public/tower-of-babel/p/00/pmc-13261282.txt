Trends in cardiovascular mortality among disaggregated Asian American subgroups: 2018-2022
Authors: David H. Yang, So Hee Naomi Ahn, Thirumagal Gowrikanthan, Franco Miguel Valencia, Karin Kai Wing Wang, Jasmin Choi, Lester Andrew Uy, Armaan Jamal, Nilay S. Shah, Nitya Rajeshuni, Gloria Kim, Malathi Srinivasan, Latha Palaniappan, Adrian Matias Bacong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsian Americans, one of the fastest-growing racial groups, experienced an increase in cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic. The objective of our study was to examine specific trends in CVD mortality rates in Asian American subgroups from 2018 through 2022 to better contextualize trends before and after the pandemic.MethodsWe analyzed US death certificate data for ischemic heart disease (IHD) and heart failure (HF) in non-Hispanic Asian American subgroups (Chinese, Filipino, Asian Indian, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese) and Non-Hispanic White individuals before and during the pandemic. We calculated age-standardized mortality rates, proportional mortality ratios, and annual percent change of age-standardized mortality rates for IHD and HF.ResultsDuring the study period, there was no significant change in IHD mortality rate for all Asian American subgroups and for Non-Hispanic Whites during the study period. HF mortality rate significantly increased in Vietnamese American females (11.6% per year [95% CI, 2.2–23.0]). The highest mortality rates were found in Asian Indian and Filipino American males and Asian Indian American females.ConclusionsThere was heterogeneity in cardiovascular mortality rates among Asian American subgroups, with IHD mortality rate stagnant in all subgroups between 2018 to 2022 and HF mortality rate increasing among Vietnamese American females during the study period. Greater surveillance of the burden of and mortality from CVD is needed for all Asian American subgroups.

Introduction

While cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality rates in the United States have declined since 1978, these trends have plateaued since 2011 [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Recently, the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated this trend, with pandemic-related risk factors such as limited healthcare access and worsening of racial disparities likely aggravating cardiovascular conditions [[5], [6], [7]]. Asian Americans, one of the fastest-growing racial groups, experienced an approximately 20 % increase in CVD-related mortality during the pandemic.

However, this aggregation of data into a combined Asian category likely masks heterogeneity in this group, as Asian Americans encompass diverse groups such as Chinese, Asian Indians, Vietnamese, Koreans, Japanese, and Filipinos [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Reporting of CVD-related mortality statistics in a disaggregated fashion is essential in understanding heterogeneity in outcomes and allows for tailoring of disease prevention and management of disproportionately impacted Asian American subgroups [9].

For example, while Asian Americans in aggregate have a lower ischemic heart disease (IHD) mortality rate compared to Non-Hispanic White (NHW) people, further examination by subgroups shows that Asian Indian and Filipino people have a higher proportionate mortality burden from IHD compared to other [2,[14], [15], [16]]. Additionally, age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) from IHD decreased among Chinese, Filipino, and Japanese men between 2003 and 2017, but remained stagnant in Asian Indian and Vietnamese men, indicating persistent disparities within Asian American subgroups [2]. Although Asian Americans in aggregate experienced a disproportionate rise in cardiovascular disease deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic [6], the patterns of mortality trends among disaggregated Asian Americans is unknown. Thus, the objective of this study was to examine specific trends and disparities in CVD-related mortality rates in Asian American subgroups from 2018 through 2022.

Methods

We used the National Center for Health Statistics’ US Death certificate data, known as the National Vital Statistics System Mortality (NVSS-M) database, to calculate mortality statistics from 2018 to 2022. All states adopted the 2003 revision of the US Standard Certificate of Death before 2018, which allowed for the disaggregation of decedents from one of the six largest Asian American subgroups (Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, Asian Indian, Korean, and Vietnamese American). Within the Asian American population, we excluded those who were categorized as Hispanic or as multiple Asian ethnicities due to small event counts. Non-Hispanic White (NHW) decedents were included as the comparator group given that they are the largest racial population in the U.S. The study was determined exempt by the institutional review board at Stanford University School of Medicine, given the use of de-identified vital statistics data.

We used the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes to identify the following CVD-related underlying causes of death: Ischemic Heart Disease (IHD, ICD-10 code I20-I25) or Heart Failure (HF, ICD-10 code I50) (Table S1 and Table S2). First, we calculated mortality rates by dividing the mortality count from an underlying cause in each disaggregated Asian American subgroup with the population size for each of these subgroups. We estimated population sizes for each disaggregated Asian American subgroup using data from the 2018–2022 American Community Survey (ACS), encompassing population counts for all people residing in the United States, including both US-born and foreign-born individuals regardless of citizenship or document status (Table S3). The ACS is an annual demographic survey program conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau to gather information about the U.S. population. It employs a complex sampling design to select representative sample households from every geographic region in the U.S. each year. Specific sampling and survey methodology for ACS has been described elsewhere [17].

Proportional mortality rates (PMR) and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) for cardiovascular deaths were calculated using direct standardization by sex, disaggregated Asian American subgroups, and underlying cause of death for each year in the study period. We used the 2010 US standard population for age adjustment. Proportional mortality for IHD and HF were calculated by dividing the number of deaths from each underlying cause in each race and Asian American subgroup by the number of deaths from all causes in that subgroup. The PMR was estimated by dividing the proportional mortality from an underlying cause in each disaggregated Asian American subgroup with the proportional mortality from the same underlying cause in the US general population similar to previous methods [2,18]. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.0 (R Core Team, 2021).

Additionally, joinpoint regression models were used to estimate trends in ASMR over the study period from 2018 through 2022 [19]. Trends in mortality rates were calculated and analyzed using joinpoint software from the National Cancer Institute (2024). ASMR from heart failure

The trends in ASMR from HF for males and females are illustrated in Table 1 and Fig. 2. Among Asian American male subgroups, the ASMR was greatest in Asian Indian Americans in 2018 (12.3), 2021 (11.6), and 2022 (13.9) and Filipino Americans in 2019 (13.0) and 2020 (16.4). The ASMR was lowest in Korean Americans in 2018 (7.1), 2019 (5.7), and 2021 (6.3), Vietnamese Americans in 2020 (7.3), and Chinese Americans in 2022 (7.2). Among Asian American female subgroups, the ASMR was greatest in Asian Indian Americans in all five years(11.8 – 14.2). The ASMR was lowest in Chinese Americans in 2018 (5.6), 2019 (4.8), and 2022 (5.8) and Korean Americans in 2020 (5.3) and 2021 (5.6). Table 2 displays joinpoint regression analysis of ASMR for HF by sex. From 2018 to 2022, ASMR significantly increased in Vietnamese American females (11.6 % per year [95 % CI, 2.2–23.0]), and NHW males (0.8 % per year [95 % CI, 0.04–1.6]). The relative burden of IHD and HF in Asian American subgroups compared to the general US population is presented in Fig. 3, Fig. 4 as well Table S4 and S5. Among Asian American male and female subgroups, the proportional mortality ratios (PMR) for IHD was greatest among Asian Indian Americans in all 5 years (1.3–1.4). The PMR for IHD was also greater than 1 for All Asians (male and female; 1.0–1.1), Filipino Americans (male and female; 1.0–1.1), Japanese Americans (male; 1.0–1.1), Chinese Americans (female; 1.0–1.2), and Korean Americans (female; 1.0–1.1). The PMR for IHD was lowest in Asian American males for Korean Americans in 2018 (0.9), 2021 (0.8), and 2022 (0.9) and Vietnamese Americans in 2019 (0.9) and 2020 (0.8). Discussion

CVD-related mortality rates over the past decade experienced a general decline, with a recent reversal of this progress since the COVID-19 pandemic [7]. While Asian Americans experienced a similar reversal in mortality rates, this is the first study, to our knowledge, that has found that the heterogeneity of CVD-related mortality rates among disaggregated Asian American subgroups persisted. The IHD mortality rate between 2018 to 2022 was stable for all Asian American subgroups, with the highest IHD mortality rates found in Asian Indian American males and females. Asian Indian American, Chinese American, Filipino American, Japanese American, and Korean Americans all had proportional mortality for IHD greater than the US population. Although Asian Americans generally had lower proportional mortality from HF compared to the US population, the HF mortality rate from 2018 to 2022 notably increased in Vietnamese American females.

The mortality patterns we observe over the past decade among Asian American subgroups contribute to ongoing trends seen in other studies [6,14,16]. Previous mortality surveillance from Shah et al. using data between 2003 and 2017 showed a decline in mortality rates for IHD and an increase in mortality rates for HF for most Asian American subgroups. We observed that mortality rates in IHD for all Asian American subgroups are no longer in decline since 2017. For example, the mortality rate for Filipino American males with IHD remained stagnant over the last 5 years despite previously being in decline [2]. Meanwhile, the mortality rate for HF has increased for Vietnamese American females while the mortality rate for other Asian American subgroups is unchanged over the last 5 years. This increase in mortality rate for HF among Vietnamese American females is worrisome and may be related to a number of explanations. First, while there have been no large-scale studies focusing on Vietnamese Americans, a number of small studies point to a greater prevalence of hypertension and diabetes among Vietnamese Americans compared to other Asian American subgroups [20,21]. Second, Vietnamese Americans have the lowest English fluency and access to a physician among Asian Americans [22,23]. These barriers may prevent treatment of cardiovascular risk factors like hypertension and diabetes, resulting in adverse cardiovascular health among Vietnamese American females.

The mostly stagnant mortality rates for IHD among the Asian American subgroups are consistent with other recent work describing a reversal of sustained progress in reducing CVD-related mortality rates in the United States and have several possible explanations [4,7]. First, the increased CVD-related mortality is at least partially explained by the COVID-19 pandemic and its disruption to health care access and possible cardiac involvement [24]. Prior studies have described elevated CVD-related mortality between 2020 and 2022 [6,[25], [26], [27]]. This is likely related to the substantial burden of mortality from cardiovascular causes among Asian American subgroups [24,28,29]. Data preceding the pandemic found heterogeneity in CVD-related risk factors among Asian American populations [30,31]. For example, smoking prevalence ranges from 43 % in Vietnamese American males to 13 % among Chinese Americans [30]. Additionally, disruptions to health care access and patient hesitancy to seek care in the months following the pandemic may have led to delayed detection and treatment of CVD-related and related risk factors [[32], [33], [34]].

Our analysis furthers the discussion on presenting disaggregated Asian American data and the invisibility caused by data aggregation. Previous research described the low mortality of Asian Americans relative to other racial groups during the COVID-19 pandemic [14,16]. We continued to observe a proportional mortality ratio greater than 1 for IHD among several Asian American subgroups compared to the general U.S. population, highest particularly in IHD PMRs among Asian Indian American males and females. Additionally, the fact that most Asian American subgroups experienced stagnant CVD-related mortality rates over the past five years is concerning. Previous research has documented a widening of racial and ethnic disparities in CVD-related mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic, but described how Asian Americans continued to have the lowest mortality [6,[35], [36], [37]]. Our findings reinforce that disaggregation of Asian American subgroups unveils considerable heterogeneity in mortality rates. While the cause of these variations remain unclear, certain subgroups, like Filipino American and Vietnamese Americans face disproportionate challenges relative to other Asian American subgroups, including limited access to healthcare, lower educational attainment and lower socioeconomic status [2,[38], [39], [40]]. Additionally, variability in CVD risk factors like diabetes and hypertension contributes to this heterogeneity in mortality rates. For example, Filipino Americans experience the highest hypertension-related and diabetes-related CVD-related mortality among all Asian American subgroups [41,42]. Continued efforts should be made to collect and report disaggregated Asian American health data to improve our understanding of the health disparities that different Asian American subgroups experience [9].

The continued increase in heart failure mortality for Vietnamese American females is concerning. Previous studies analyzing heart failure mortality from 2003 – 2017 highlighted this trend, which persists in this current analysis covering 2018 to 2022 [31]. These rising mortality rates may be partially explained by cardiovascular risk factors, with more than 20 % of Vietnamese American adults with multiple these risk factors [31,43,44]. However, sex-specific factors contributing to this disparity have largely been unexplored, with Vietnamese American females having the second highest rate of gestational diabetes [45]. Understanding these factors will be essential for developed effective and tailored interventions to address the growing heart failure mortality among Vietnamese American females.

A strength of this study is its use of disaggregated national mortality statistics related to cardiovascular deaths in disaggregated Asian American subgroups between 2018 and 2022. An additional strength is that we build on previously reported research describing cardiovascular deaths in this population [2]. However, there are some notable limitations to our findings. First, ICD-10 causes of death coding errors on death certificates may lead to potential issues of misclassification for CVD-related outcomes, leading to underestimating CVD-related mortality [[46], [47], [48], [49]]. Second, our analysis only includes the six largest Asian American subgroups and fails to include other Asian American subgroups, such as Hmong American or Cambodian American. Additionally, we build on a prior study reporting ASMR for Asian American subgroups in this dataset began in 2018 and the stagnant mortality rates may reflect our limited timeframe. This dataset also did not include variables that could potentially explain the heterogeneity of CVD-related mortality rates, such as hypertension and diabetes [30,31]. Third, the relatively low number of deaths in some Asian American subgroups, leading to missing data from data suppression for public use, prevents evaluation of mortality rates at different age groups and in different regions in the US.

Conclusion

Results from this study highlight the importance of disaggregating Asian Americans in CVD-related mortality. The Asian American population is not a monolith, and our findings clearly identify certain Asian American subgroups to target to decrease the burden of CVD in the U.S. In addition, our findings show that CVD-related mortality has been stable but not declining in the past five years, speaking to the need for targeted prevention efforts for Asian American subgroups.
