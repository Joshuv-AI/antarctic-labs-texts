Unilateral flywheel resistance training versus traditional resistance training for change-of-direction performance in elite soccer players: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Ziqi Han, TianLiang Zhao, ZhiWei Wen, Tao Yang, Yue Dou, JiaHui Hu, Bo Zhang, DingMeng Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoccer requires frequent high-intensity decelerations, braking actions, and multidirectional re-accelerations. Flywheel resistance training may provide a braking-oriented inertial stimulus that is relevant to change-of-direction (COD) performance. However, randomised controlled evidence directly comparing unilateral flywheel resistance training with unilateral traditional resistance training in high-level soccer players remains limited. This study compared the effects of unilateral flywheel resistance training and unilateral traditional resistance training on COD-related performance in high-level male soccer players.MethodsTwenty-six high-level male soccer players were randomly allocated to a flywheel resistance training group (FRT, n = 13) or a traditional resistance training group (TRT, n = 13), and 22 players completed the study and were included in the final analysis (FRT, n = 11; TRT, n = 11). Both groups completed an 8-week intervention twice per week in addition to regular soccer training. The unilateral Bulgarian split squat was used in both groups, with training structure and movement pattern matched between conditions. Performance was assessed before and after the intervention using 10-m and 30-m linear sprint tests, the T-test, Pro-agility test, Arrowhead change-of-direction test, and AFL-derived change-of-direction tasks performed without and with the ball. Intervention effects were analysed using Bayesian repeated-measures analysis of variance, with evidence for the group × time interaction quantified using inclusion Bayes factors (BFincl).ResultsThe FRT group showed greater improvements than the TRT group in the T-test (BFincl = 135.96), Pro-agility test (BFincl = 107.01), Arrowhead-L (BFincl = 34.55), Arrowhead-R (BFincl = 25.63), and AFL-derived COD task without the ball (BFincl = 17.33). Evidence for an interaction was moderate for the AFL-derived COD task with the ball (BFincl = 3.24), anecdotal for the 10-m sprint (BFincl = 2.52), and favoured no interaction for the 30-m sprint (BFincl = 0.64).ConclusionsUnder matched unilateral movement conditions, flywheel resistance training was associated with greater reductions in completion time than traditional resistance training across several pre-planned COD tasks in high-level male soccer players. However, this pattern was less consistent for linear sprinting and the ball-constrained AFL-derived COD task, suggesting task-specific transfer of training adaptation.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07567677. Background

Soccer is a high-intensity intermittent team sport in which players are frequently required to perform short-duration explosive actions, including accelerations, decelerations, sprints, and rapid changes of direction (COD) [1]. As the frequency of high-intensity actions in modern soccer has increased, well-developed COD ability may help players gain a competitive advantage during brief offensive and defensive actions [1–4]. COD performance is not determined solely by lower-limb strength or power output, but represents a multifactorial ability influenced by neuromuscular qualities, technical execution, and task constraints [5–7]. Effective COD execution depends not only on concentric force production during the re-acceleration phase, but also on eccentric strength, braking impulse control, and single-leg support stability during the deceleration phase. In addition, technical factors such as foot placement, trunk control, lowering of the centre of mass, and stride adjustment can substantially influence COD efficiency [6–8]. This suggests that even athletes with high levels of strength or power may still be limited in COD performance if they lack effective technical execution [5]. Therefore, although eccentric strength and braking capacity are important determinants of COD performance, and their importance becomes greater as the cutting angle increases, strength adaptations do not necessarily translate into improved COD performance [3–6]. How to more effectively enhance eccentric braking capacity and transfer these adaptations to COD performance across different task contexts remains one of the key issues to be addressed in soccer conditioning.

Traditional resistance training (TRT) is an important training modality for developing maximal strength, power output, and lower-limb braking capacity in soccer players, and has been shown to improve COD performance to some extent [9, 10]. In the present study, TRT refers specifically to gravity-dependent free-weight resistance training, in which the same nominal external load is used during the concentric and eccentric phases. This definition excludes flywheel inertial loading, weight releasers, externally accentuated eccentric loading, and deliberately prolonged eccentric-tempo resistance training [11]. Flywheel resistance training (FRT) is a resistance training modality based on rotational inertia, and its loading is not dependent on gravity. During FRT, the energy produced by the athlete during the concentric phase is stored in the rotating flywheel and subsequently absorbed through active braking during the eccentric phase. Therefore, under appropriate technical execution, effort level, and inertial load conditions, FRT may impose relatively high eccentric braking demands [11, 12]. Previous studies have reported positive effects of FRT on lower-limb strength, power output, sprinting, and COD performance, and FRT may be more effective than TRT for improving COD performance in male athletes [9, 12–14]. However, evidence regarding the transfer effects of FRT to linear sprinting, acceleration ability, and soccer skill-constrained tasks remains inconsistent [15]. Therefore, FRT may have practical value in team sports that require rapid deceleration and re-acceleration [9, 12–14]. Compared with FRT, the external load in traditional gravity-dependent free-weight training is usually limited by the load that can be overcome during the concentric phase, and its eccentric stimulus may not consistently match the peak eccentric demands required during rapid braking actions [11, 12]. Particularly in highly trained athletes with extensive training experience, simply increasing TRT load or training volume may not result in proportional improvements in COD performance, as training transfer may be constrained by movement specificity, eccentric stimulus characteristics, and task complexity [10, 16]. In high-level soccer players, direct comparative evidence between unilateral FRT and unilateral TRT under comparable movement patterns and training intensity remains limited.

From a specificity perspective, high-speed change of direction (COD) actions in soccer predominantly occur under single-leg support conditions and are characterised by unilateral braking followed by rapid re-acceleration, representing a continuous sequence of deceleration, centre-of-mass transition, and directional redirection [6, 8]. During cutting, turning, or defensive tracking actions, the lower limbs are required to tolerate high eccentric braking forces within a very short time frame while maintaining postural stability through trunk control [6, 8, 16, 17]. Therefore, based on the principles of dynamic correspondence and training specificity, combining unilateral movement patterns with the inertial resistance characteristics of flywheel training may theoretically induce more targeted adaptations in key limiting factors of COD performance by enhancing eccentric braking capacity [12, 16, 18]. Although previous studies have shown that unilateral flywheel training can improve COD ability and related performance outcomes, the current body of evidence remains limited, with considerable heterogeneity in training content, exercise design, and performance assessment protocols [9, 14, 15, 17]. More importantly, some studies have employed traditional resistance training or non-training control groups, making it difficult to distinguish whether the observed adaptations are attributable to the flywheel inertial loading mechanism, the specificity of unilateral strength training, or the additional exposure to resistance training stimuli [14, 15, 17]. Therefore, when movement patterns, training frequency, set-repetition structure, and supervision are closely matched, direct comparisons between unilateral flywheel resistance training and unilateral traditional resistance training may help clarify whether flywheel-specific eccentric braking stimuli provide additional adaptations beyond those induced by general unilateral resistance training. This remains an important gap in the current evidence [9, 14, 15, 17].

Taken together, although previous studies have suggested that FRT may improve strength and COD performance in soccer players, direct randomised controlled evidence regarding whether unilateral FRT provides additional benefits over matched unilateral TRT remains limited in high-level soccer players [9, 14, 15, 17]. Therefore, the present study used an exploratory randomised controlled design with a hierarchical outcome structure to compare the effects of 8 weeks of unilateral flywheel resistance training and unilateral traditional resistance training on sprint performance and different levels of COD performance in high-level male soccer players. Both groups performed the same unilateral Bulgarian split squat exercise, with training frequency, sets, repetitions, rest intervals, and supervision matched between groups to focus the comparison as much as possible on the loading mode itself. A Bayesian inference framework was further applied to quantify the strength of evidence supporting between-group differences across task outcomes. Based on previous evidence, we expected unilateral FRT to be more likely to produce greater improvements in closed, pre-planned COD tasks requiring substantial braking and re-acceleration, whereas its transfer effects to linear sprinting and ball-constrained change-of-direction tasks may be more uncertain.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07567677) on 28 April 2026. Participant recruitment began in January 2026, and familiarisation with the testing procedures and training exercises, together with baseline testing, was completed in early March 2026. Randomisation was performed after completion of baseline testing and before the start of the formal intervention. The 8-week intervention was conducted from 5 March 2026 to 5 May 2026. Because trial registration occurred after participant recruitment, baseline testing, and randomisation, the study should be considered retrospectively registered. The delay in registration was mainly related to administrative review and document submission procedures during the registration process. Given this registration timeline, the exploratory nature of the trial was explicitly stated throughout the Methods, Results, and Discussion, and the outcome hierarchy was defined before statistical analysis to improve transparency and consistency of reporting. The study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement, and the completed CONSORT checklist is provided as Additional file 1.

Participants

A total of 26 high-level male soccer players were recruited through purposive sampling. Eligible participants were healthy male athletes who had competed at a high level and held National First-Class Athlete status, were currently engaged in systematic soccer-specific training, had accumulated at least 8 years of soccer training experience, maintained regular soccer training three times per week for approximately 120 min per session during the study period, and had not sustained any musculoskeletal injury affecting lower-limb performance within the 6 months preceding the study. According to the participant classification framework proposed by McKay et al., the participants in the present study were classified as Tier 3, corresponding to Highly Trained/National Level athletes [19]. All participants were instructed to maintain their usual training routines and lifestyle habits throughout the study period. Randomisation was performed by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, testing, or training. Using an online random sequence generator, a unique randomised sequence from 1 to 26 was created, and participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the flywheel resistance training group (FRT, n = 13) or the traditional resistance training group (TRT, n = 13). The allocation sequence was generated and sealed in a paper folder by the independent researcher and remained concealed from the recruitment, testing, and training staff until completion of baseline assessments. After baseline assessment, the independent researcher implemented group allocation according to the pre-generated randomisation sequence.

During the intervention period, four participants withdrew from the study; two were unable to continue the intervention because of lower-limb discomfort after regular soccer match participation, and two were unable to complete all training sessions and tests because of academic commitments. All withdrawals were documented in the participant flow diagram. Because these participants did not complete post-intervention testing, the present study was primarily based on a completer analysis. Finally, 22 participants completed the full training intervention and pre- and post-testing and were included in the statistical analysis (FRT = 11, TRT = 11; Fig. 1) [17]. Descriptive analyses indicated that the two groups were comparable at baseline in demographic characteristics and performance outcomes, with all baseline Bayes factors favouring the null hypothesis of no meaningful between-group differences (all BF10 < 1; baseline participant characteristics are presented in Table 1, and baseline performance outcomes are presented in Table 2).

Owing to the practical constraints of recruiting high-level soccer players, no formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted. The sample size was primarily determined by the number of eligible athletes who were able to complete the full training cycle. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as definitive efficacy evidence, but rather as preliminary comparative evidence based on Bayesian evidence strength and effect sizes across different COD-related tasks. The limited sample size is acknowledged as a study limitation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (Approval No. 2025611 H) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). All participants were informed of the potential benefits and risks before data collection and provided written informed consent.

Training intervention

During the 8-week intervention period, all participants completed the experimental intervention in addition to their usual soccer-specific technical and tactical training. Participants in both groups were from the same soccer-specific class, and the content and schedule of regular soccer training were identical between groups, consisting of three soccer-specific training sessions per week, each lasting approximately 120 min. The experimental intervention was conducted independently of routine soccer training. Before the formal intervention, all participants completed two familiarisation sessions involving both flywheel and traditional resistance training. During the intervention period, participants completed two experimental training sessions per week, with at least 48 h between sessions to reduce the potential influence of residual fatigue on training adaptation. The overall intervention procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Experimental intervention procedure

To evaluate the consistency of training implementation, intervention adherence, regular soccer training exposure, internal load, and movement execution were recorded throughout the intervention period. Intervention adherence was defined as the percentage of completed experimental training sessions relative to the planned sessions. Regular soccer training exposure was recorded using weekly training frequency, session duration, and attendance rate to confirm that both groups maintained a similar soccer-specific training background during the study period. RPE was recorded 15–30 min after each experimental training session, and session-RPE load was calculated as training duration (min) × RPE to describe internal load. External training execution was monitored using the number of valid repetitions completed, set-by-set MCV, and deviations from the prescribed movement standards. Repetition completion rate was defined as the percentage of valid repetitions completed relative to the planned repetitions. All deviations from the training prescription, including missed sessions, incomplete sets, load adjustments, inadequate movement quality, or device abnormalities, were recorded by the research staff. These indices of training adherence, internal load, velocity monitoring, and movement execution consistency were summarized in the Results section.

Training exercises and execution

To control training variables other than the resistance mode as much as possible, both groups performed the unilateral Bulgarian split squat as the core training exercise, with training frequency, set–repetition structure, limb allocation, inter-set rest, and supervision standardised between groups. The principal difference between groups was the mode of resistance application, namely flywheel inertial resistance versus barbell gravitational loading. Before the formal intervention, all participants completed two familiarisation sessions to practice exercise technique, unilateral support stability, squat depth control, and the prescribed movement rhythm. The intervention lasted 8 weeks, with two sessions per week separated by at least 48 h. In both groups, participants performed 4 sets of 6 repetitions per leg, with 2–3 min of rest between sets, and both lower limbs were trained unilaterally. All training sessions were supervised by the research staff throughout. The exercise setup is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Flywheel Bulgarian Split Squat Training

All training sessions were conducted under standardised movement criteria. Participants started from the unilateral Bulgarian split squat position, with the lead foot firmly supported on the ground, the rear foot placed on an elevated support, and the trunk maintained in a neutral position. The concentric phase was initiated after descending to approximately 90° of knee flexion in the lead leg. To standardise movement characteristics as much as possible, both groups were instructed to perform the concentric phase explosively and the eccentric phase under control. In the TRT group, tempo was further standardised to approximately 1 s for the concentric phase and 3 s for the eccentric phase to ensure movement quality and safety. It should be noted that, because the two training modalities differ in their source of resistance, differences in eccentric duration and mechanical work between FRT and TRT may still exist; this issue is acknowledged in the limitations. Throughout all training sessions, investigators monitored squat depth, trunk position, unilateral support stability, and overall movement quality. If insufficient depth, obvious compensatory movement, loss of tempo, or abnormal device readings occurred, the repetition was not counted as valid and was repeated immediately when necessary.

The FRT group trained using a KBox 4 Pro device (Exxentric, Sweden) with available inertial loads of 0.010, 0.050, 0.070, and 0.095 kg·m². Participants in the FRT group were instructed to produce force rapidly with maximal controllable intent during the concentric phase and to actively resist the flywheel pull during the eccentric phase. To improve safety and movement stability, participants were required to maintain controlled lowering during the early eccentric phase and actively brake before reaching the bottom position. Six valid repetitions were recorded for each set. If a repetition was performed with insufficient depth, obvious body compensation, or failure to actively brake, it was not counted as valid. The TRT group performed the same exercise using a barbell, with the initial training load set at 80% of one-repetition maximum (1RM). To standardise concentric velocity characteristics between the two training conditions, mean concentric velocity (MCV) was used as the reference criterion for load prescription [20]. It should be emphasised that MCV matching was used only to standardise concentric movement velocity between groups and does not imply that eccentric loading, total mechanical work, impulse, time under tension, or internal load were fully matched between conditions [12]. Specifically, under the TRT condition, each participant first completed one set of six high-quality repetitions at 80%1RM, during which MCV was recorded for each repetition using the GYMAWARE system, and the average value across the six valid repetitions was used as the individualised velocity reference. Participants then performed one set of six repetitions under each flywheel inertial condition, and the corresponding MCV values were recorded. The inertial load that produced the smallest absolute difference from the TRT reference MCV was selected as the individualised flywheel load for the subsequent intervention period.

During training monitoring, the variables recorded for consistency auditing included the load parameter used in each session (inertial setting for FRT and barbell load for TRT), set-by-set MCV, number of completed repetitions (per set and per leg), rating of perceived exertion (RPE, 0–10), and any deviation from the prescribed movement standard. In the FRT group, if MCV deviated from the TRT-derived individual reference by more than ± 10% in two consecutive sessions, the inertial load was adjusted to the adjacent setting, and both the reason for adjustment and the updated load were documented. In the TRT group, if the prescribed repetitions could not be completed while maintaining movement tempo and technique, the load was reduced by 2.5–5.0%; if all repetitions were completed consistently in two consecutive sessions with clear reserve, the load was increased by 2.5–5.0%. Through these procedures, both groups were matched for training frequency, movement pattern, limb allocation, set–repetition structure, inter-set rest, and supervision, and concentric velocity characteristics were standardised between conditions. However, because FRT and TRT differ inherently in resistance source and eccentric-phase mechanical characteristics, the two interventions should not be interpreted as fully equivalent in eccentric loading, total mechanical work, impulse, time under tension, or internal load [12]. Testing procedures

All tests were conducted on the same standardised outdoor artificial-turf soccer field, with testing conditions kept as consistent as possible before and after the intervention, including time of day, weather conditions, temperature, humidity, and surface status. Figure 5 shows the layout of the actual testing field, and all tests were completed on this same outdoor artificial-turf surface. All testing sessions were supervised by the same research staff. Before testing, participants completed a standardised 15-min warm-up consisting of light jogging, dynamic stretching, and lower-limb activation exercises.

Schematic diagrams of the performance tests

The testing battery included 10 m and 30 m linear sprint tests [22, 23], Pro-agility test [24], T-test [24], Arrowhead change-of-direction tests (left/right) [25], a modified AFL-derived COD task (without ball) [26], and a modified ball-constrained AFL-derived COD task [27]. The T-test was used to assess integrated deceleration–braking–reacceleration capacity [24], while other COD tests evaluated directional change performance under different movement constraints. Linear sprint tests were used to assess maximal linear running speed [28, 29].

It should be noted that the AFL-derived protocols used in this study were pre-planned change-of-direction tasks rather than reactive agility tests, as they did not involve external stimulus-driven decision-making [7]. Therefore, these tests were classified as COD performance measures rather than agility assessments.

Each test was performed twice, with the fastest valid trial retained for subsequent analysis. Trials were repeated if participants committed obvious errors (e.g., slipping, missing gates/cones, incorrect movement path, or false starts), with sufficient rest provided before re-attempt. A minimum of 3 min of passive recovery was provided between trials and 10 min between test batteries. All performance times were recorded to the nearest 0.01 s.

Timing performance was assessed using two electronic timing systems. Linear sprint tests (10 m and 30 m) and selected COD tests were measured using the Smart Speed system (Fusion Sport, Australia) [30], with dual-beam timing gates positioned at a height of 80 cm. The start line was placed 20 cm behind the first gate to avoid premature triggering. Additional soccer-specific COD tasks were assessed using a football-specific timing system, which employed either single- or dual-gate configurations depending on task requirements, with timing gates positioned at a standardised height of 30 cm.

To ensure within-test consistency, identical timing systems and identical configurations were used for each respective test during both pre- and post-intervention assessments.

All participants wore their habitual soccer training footwear, which was kept consistent across testing sessions. Standard size-5 soccer balls were used for all ball-constrained tests. Trials were considered invalid if participants slipped, missed a gate/cone, deviated from the prescribed path, initiated movement prematurely, lost ball control, or knocked over markers, and were repeated after adequate rest.

The study was designed as an exploratory randomised controlled trial. Outcome measures were pre-specified with a hierarchical structure: the T-test was defined as the primary outcome, Pro-agility, Arrowhead, and non-ball COD tasks were considered key secondary outcomes, and ball-constrained COD and linear sprint tests were classified as exploratory outcomes to examine transfer effects under skill constraints.

A familiarisation session was conducted two days prior to formal testing to minimise learning effects; however, learning effects in complex COD tasks cannot be fully excluded.

Testing was conducted across three designated zones. Station 1 was performed in Zone B, Station 2 in Zone C, and Station 3 used all zones.

Linear Sprints: Distances from A to B were set at 10 m and 30 m, respectively. Participants started from a stationary standing position on artificial turf. Photocell timing gates were positioned at the start line, 10 m, and 30 m to automatically record split times over 0–10 m and 0–30 m [22, 23].

Pro-agility Test: The distances from A to B and from A to C were both 5 m, whereas the distance from B to C was 10 m. Participants adopted a three-point start position at the centre line (point A). On the start signal, they sprinted to the right to touch point B, then changed direction and sprinted to the far-left point C, and finally changed direction again to sprint back through the finish line at A. Participants were required to touch the marker with the hand or clearly cross the designated line for the trial to be considered valid [24].

Test: The test area was arranged in a “T” configuration, with A–B set at 10 m and B–C/B–D set at 5 m. Participants sprinted forward from A to B and touched the base of the marker, then side-shuffled left to C, side-shuffled right to D, returned to B by side shuffle, and finally backpedalled to the starting line at A. Trials were considered invalid if participants crossed their feet or failed to touch the markers [24].

Arrowhead Change-of-Direction Test: The distances A–B and D–C were 10 m, and B–C and B–D were 5 m. Participants sprinted from A to the apex at B and then completed either the left or right pathway according to the test condition. They then turned around the lateral marker (C or E), changed direction toward point D, and finished with a straight sprint to the end line. Left and right trials were performed and recorded separately [25].

AFL-derived COD task [26]: An adapted AFL-derived COD task was used as one of the complex multidirectional pre-planned COD assessments. The route consisted of five markers (A–E), with distances of 5 m between A–C and C–E, a total distance of 10 m between A–E, and a lateral distance of 2.5 m between A and B. Participants followed a pre-planned route around the markers, which required multiple cutting, braking, re-acceleration, and directional reorientation actions. This task was not interpreted as a true agility test because it did not include an external stimulus, opponent movement, or real-time decision-making. The original AFL test was developed for Australian football and is typically performed using tall poles as turning targets; in the present study, markers were used and both no-ball and ball-constrained conditions were included. Therefore, this task should be considered an adapted pre-planned COD task rather than a direct replication of the original AFL agility protocol. The ball-constrained condition [27] was included only as an exploratory skill-constrained COD task to examine potential transfer under ball-control demands. If a participant clearly lost ball control, deviated from the prescribed route, or knocked over a marker, the trial was considered invalid and repeated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP (Version 0.19.3). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test [30], and homogeneity of variances for between-group comparisons of change scores was examined using the Brown–Forsythe test. Baseline comparability between groups was evaluated using Bayesian independent-samples t-tests and expressed as BF10, where BF10 > 1 indicates evidence for the alternative hypothesis and BF10 < 1 indicates evidence for the null hypothesis [29].

Intervention effects were analysed using Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA, with time (pre vs. post) as the within-subject factor and group (FRT vs. TRT) as the between-subject factor. The primary quantity of interest was the time × group interaction, reported as BFincl, where BFincl > 1 supports inclusion of the interaction term and BFincl < 1 supports exclusion [29]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying the Cauchy prior width for fixed effects (r = 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75), while all other settings were held constant.

For transparency of effect estimation, change scores were calculated as post-test minus pre-test values for each outcome, and between-group differences in change were summarised with 95% confidence intervals. For outcomes showing unequal variances, sensitivity analyses using Welch’s t-test were performed and yielded materially similar conclusions. Standardised effect sizes were expressed as Hedges’ g [31]. Evidence strength was interpreted as follows: 1–3, anecdotal; 3–10, moderate; 10–30, strong; 30–100, very strong; and > 100, extreme evidence.

Results

Training adherence and monitoring outcomes

Training adherence and monitoring outcomes are presented in Table 4. Both groups showed high training adherence, with attendance rates of 0.95 ± 0.04 in both FRT and TRT (BF10 = 0.23). Mean RPE (7.87 ± 0.34 vs. 7.76 ± 0.38, BF10 = 0.28) and session-RPE load (279.62 ± 11.38 AU vs. 276.13 ± 13.32 AU, BF10 = 0.27) also showed no clear between-group differences, suggesting similar perceived internal load between groups. Mean MCV was 0.49 ± 0.02 m·s⁻¹ in FRT and 0.48 ± 0.01 m·s⁻¹ in TRT (BF10 = 1.26), while MCV deviation was 6.00 ± 2.00% and 5.00 ± 2.00%, respectively (BF10 = 1.83), indicating broadly similar concentric velocity characteristics between groups. The MCV-referenced load prescription was 80.62 ± 1.44% in FRT and 80.51 ± 0.91% in TRT (BF10 = 0.22). For movement execution variability, values were 0.12 ± 0.00 in FRT and 0.13 ± 0.01 in TRT (BF10 = 4.32), suggesting slightly greater movement velocity variability in the TRT group. Overall, the monitoring data indicated good consistency between groups in attendance, perceived internal load, MCV-referenced prescription targets, and concentric velocity characteristics; however, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the two training modalities were fully equivalent in eccentric loading, total mechanical work, impulse, or internal load.

Within the overall exploratory framework of this trial, performance outcomes were reported according to a pre-specified outcome hierarchy, including the prioritised change-of-direction outcome, additional pre-planned change-of-direction outcomes, and lower-priority sport-specific and linear sprint outcomes. This hierarchy was used to structure the presentation and interpretation of the findings, rather than to imply confirmatory hypothesis testing.

Collectively, these pre-planned COD outcomes showed a consistent exploratory pattern, suggesting that FRT may be associated with greater reductions in completion time than TRT in COD tasks requiring rapid braking and re-acceleration.

For the lower-priority sport-specific and linear sprint outcomes, the ball-constrained AFL-derived COD task showed moderate Bayesian evidence for a time × group interaction (BFincl = 3.24). The FRT group improved from 10.54 ± 0.52 s to 10.11 ± 0.36 s (− 0.43 s, approximately − 4.1%), whereas the TRT group improved from 10.76 ± 0.67 s to 10.55 ± 0.62 s (− 0.21 s, approximately − 2.0%). The between-group difference in change was − 0.22 s, with a smaller standardised effect (g = − 0.35), suggesting that the magnitude of the between-group difference was limited in this sport-specific task. For linear sprint outcomes, the 10 m sprint showed only anecdotal evidence for an interaction (BFincl = 2.52), whereas the 30 m sprint provided anecdotal evidence against an interaction (BFincl = 0.64), with both groups showing broadly similar improvements. Therefore, these lower-priority outcomes should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating findings, and their pattern was less consistent than that observed for the pre-planned COD tasks.

Discussion

In this exploratory completer analysis, following an 8-week intervention performed twice weekly using the same unilateral Bulgarian split squat framework, unilateral flywheel resistance training (FRT) showed a relatively consistent pattern of reduced completion times across several pre-planned change-of-direction (COD) tasks, whereas this pattern was not consistently observed in linear sprinting or the AFL-derived task with the ball. These findings suggest that performance changes following FRT may be more likely to appear in pre-planned COD tasks characterised by braking, directional reorientation, and re-acceleration demands, whereas transfer to linear speed or ball-constrained sport-specific tasks may be more limited [9, 12, 15]. Given the task-specific nature of COD performance, differences in movement distance, turning angle, entry velocity, braking demands, and technical constraints may influence the transfer of training effects. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating pattern rather than confirmatory evidence [3, 31, 32]. Accordingly, the Discussion is organised around three themes: first, the potential transfer of FRT to pre-planned closed COD tasks; second, weaker or less consistent transfer to linear sprinting and ball-constrained tasks; and third, the practical interpretation and transfer boundaries of the present findings.

For the prioritised T-test outcome, both groups showed reduced completion times after the intervention, although the FRT group demonstrated a more consistent downward trend. It should be emphasised that the observed improvement refers to a reduction in the time required to complete the T-test, rather than an improvement in the test itself. This finding is broadly consistent with one previous soccer-specific study by Jarosz et al. (2023), which reported improvements in 5-0-5 COD test time following both flywheel and free-weight training in soccer players. However, differences in training design, exercise selection, sample characteristics, and COD outcome structure mean that the present results should not be interpreted as direct confirmation of prior findings [14]. In contrast to that study, the present study used the unilateral Bulgarian split squat as the core exercise in both FRT and TRT, thereby comparing flywheel inertial loading with traditional gravitational loading under a relatively matched unilateral movement pattern. Existing reviews comparing unilateral and bilateral resistance training also suggest that training adaptations are task-specific: unilateral training may be more favourable for some unilateral performance outcomes, but its superiority over bilateral training is not consistently observed across speed, strength, and COD outcomes [33, 34]. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was not to demonstrate that unilateral training is superior to bilateral training, but rather to examine the potential influence of different resistance modes on changes in T-test completion time within a matched unilateral training framework. Because the T-test includes forward running, lateral shuffling, backward running, and multidirectional transitions, performance depends not only on acceleration ability but also on braking control, centre-of-mass control, and re-acceleration capacity. Accordingly, unilateral FRT adaptations may be more likely to transfer to pre-planned, multidirectional COD tasks characterised by braking and re-acceleration demands.

For the other pre-planned COD outcomes, completion times in the Pro-agility, Arrowhead-L/R, and AFL-derived COD task without the ball also decreased, and the overall pattern of change was consistent with the T-test findings. Although these tasks differ in movement distance, directional demands, and task complexity, they are all pre-planned closed COD tasks that involve clearly defined braking, directional reorientation, and re-acceleration phases. Therefore, the observed pattern may not reflect a broad transfer to all athletic performance outcomes, but rather a task-specific transfer to structurally similar closed COD tasks. Nygaard Falch et al. (2019) emphasised that COD ability is highly task-specific and that training effects may be influenced by turning angle, movement distance, entry velocity, number of directional changes, and testing structure, which provides a theoretical basis for interpreting the relatively consistent pattern observed across different COD tasks in the present study [3]. In addition, Asencio et al. (2024) suggested that the transfer of flywheel training to soccer-specific performance may be influenced by inertial load, training-volume distribution, and seasonal fatigue [35]. Compared with these studies, a potential contribution of the present study is that both FRT and TRT were performed within a unilateral training framework, allowing the movement pattern to be relatively matched between conditions. Thus, the observed between-group pattern may be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating explanation related to resistance source and loading characteristics. However, given the exploratory design and limited sample size, this interpretation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Compared with the pre-planned closed COD tasks, the lower-priority 10 m and 30 m linear sprint tests and the AFL-derived task with the ball did not show the same consistent between-group pattern. The 10 m sprint showed only weak evidence for a group × time interaction, whereas the 30 m sprint tended to show a lack of interaction evidence. Although the direction of change in the AFL-derived task with the ball suggested some between-group difference, the standardised between-group effect was small, indicating that FRT-induced adaptations may not have transferred consistently to linear speed or to a more skill-constrained sport-specific task. Some soccer-specific flywheel training studies also suggest that improvements in strength or selected COD outcomes do not necessarily translate into improvements in sprinting or more complex sport-specific performance. For example, Pecci et al. (2023) reported that 6 weeks of in-season flywheel squat training improved knee flexor and extensor strength in professional female soccer players, but did not produce between-group improvements in CMJ, COD, or 30 m sprint performance [36]. Similarly, Asencio et al. (2024) found that different flywheel intensity programmes improved 1RM, but did not produce significant between-group differences in CMJ, COD, or 30 m sprint performance, and suggested that training load, match scheduling, and residual fatigue may influence transfer to sport-specific performance [35]. Linear sprint performance depends not only on lower-limb strength but also on horizontal force production, acceleration mechanics, step-frequency and step-length organisation, and starting strategy. Because the present intervention was centred on unilateral resistance exercise and did not directly include sprint-specific or horizontal force-oriented training, transfer to 10 m and 30 m sprint performance may have been limited. In contrast, the AFL-derived task with the ball introduced additional constraints related to ball control, touch rhythm, ball-distance regulation, and body–ball coordination, and performance may therefore have been influenced by technical proficiency and perception–action coupling. Sheppard and Young (2006) noted that agility performance involves not only change-of-direction speed but also perception, decision-making, and response to external stimuli [7]. Therefore, the AFL-derived task with the ball in the present study should be interpreted as a pre-planned ball-constrained COD task rather than a true reactive agility test. Taken together, the weaker or less consistent findings in the sprint and ball-constrained outcomes do not necessarily indicate that FRT was ineffective, but rather suggest that flywheel training adaptations may have task-specific transfer boundaries.

It should be noted that the reductions in completion time observed in the pre-planned COD tasks should not be attributed solely to physical adaptations, because COD performance may also be influenced by technical execution and familiarisation with the testing protocols. Previous research has shown that the reliability and stabilisation of 505 change-of-direction performance may depend on the number of familiarisation sessions, with performance stabilising on day 2 for the static-start condition and on day 3 for the flying-start condition [37]. Jones and Dos’Santos also emphasised that the assessment of change-of-direction and agility performance should consider testing route, movement technique, foot placement, body-position control, and previous testing experience [38]. Therefore, although both groups completed the same testing procedures and a standardised familiarisation session before formal testing, more complex tasks such as the Pro-agility, Arrowhead, and AFL-derived COD task may still have been influenced by learning effects or improvements in technical execution [37]. Accordingly, the reductions in completion time observed in the present study should be interpreted as performance changes influenced by training adaptation, technical execution, and test familiarisation, rather than as direct evidence of purely physical improvement.

In summary, within the exploratory completer-analysis framework, unilateral FRT showed a relatively consistent pattern of reduced completion times across several pre-planned closed COD tasks, but this pattern did not consistently extend to linear sprinting or ball-constrained tasks. These findings suggest that unilateral FRT may serve as a supplementary strategy for developing pre-planned COD performance involving braking, directional reorientation, and re-acceleration demands; however, its transfer to linear speed, ball-constrained tasks, and match-play reactive agility appears limited. From a practical perspective, an 8-week unilateral FRT intervention may be incorporated as a complementary component within soccer conditioning programmes to target pre-planned COD performance, but it should not replace linear sprint training, technical training, or match-context agility training involving perceptual–decision-making demands.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the intervention used the unilateral Bulgarian split squat as the primary training exercise. Although this exercise partly reflects the single-leg support demands of soccer and has a degree of movement specificity, it remains a single-exercise intervention and may not fully represent different force-production patterns or multidimensional loading stimuli. Future studies should compare different combinations of unilateral exercises or different flywheel inertia strategies while preserving a comparable unilateral movement structure to further clarify their transfer to COD performance.

Second, the sample size was relatively small, and no non-training control group was included. Therefore, it was not possible to fully distinguish intervention effects from time effects or external training-related influences. In addition, the present study was based on a completer analysis and did not include a full intention-to-treat analysis of all randomised participants. As a result, the findings may have been influenced by participant withdrawal and missing data, which may limit the stability of statistical estimates, the precision of effect sizes, and the generalisability of the results.

Third, this study did not directly assess mechanistic variables related to deceleration, braking, or re-acceleration, such as peak eccentric force, braking force, lower-limb joint kinetics, or electromyographic activity. Therefore, the potential neuromuscular or biomechanical mechanisms underlying the effects of FRT on pre-planned COD performance could not be directly verified. The mechanistic interpretations should therefore be regarded as speculative explanations based on functional performance outcomes rather than direct mechanistic evidence. Future studies should incorporate inertial sensors, three-dimensional motion analysis, force-platform assessment, or electromyography to better clarify the effects of FRT on braking control, eccentric force production, and re-acceleration capacity.

Fourth, regarding the testing procedures, although all participants completed standardised test instructions, warm-up, and familiarisation procedures before formal testing, this study did not include an independent multi-session familiarisation phase, nor was test–retest reliability assessed in the present sample. Therefore, potential learning effects on pre- to post-intervention changes in completion time cannot be fully excluded, particularly for more complex COD tasks such as the Pro-agility, Arrowhead, and AFL-derived COD tasks. Although both groups completed the same testing procedures, which may have partially controlled this influence in between-group comparisons, learning effects may still have affected the interpretation of the intervention effects. In addition, COD performance is influenced by technical factors such as foot placement, trunk control, lowering of the centre of mass, stride adjustment, and ball-control skill. Therefore, the present findings cannot fully distinguish whether performance changes were primarily attributable to physical adaptations or improved technical execution.

Fifth, regarding task design, the AFL-derived task with the ball used in this study was a pre-planned ball-constrained COD task rather than a reactive agility test involving responses to external stimuli. Therefore, findings from this outcome should not be directly generalised to reactive agility or agility performance under opponent-constrained match-play conditions.

Conclusion

The present findings showed that an 8-week unilateral flywheel resistance training intervention performed twice weekly was associated with greater reductions in completion time than traditional resistance training across several pre-planned closed COD tasks, particularly the T-test, Pro-agility, Arrowhead-L/R, and AFL-derived COD task without the ball. However, this pattern was not consistently observed in linear sprinting or the ball-constrained AFL-derived COD task. These results suggest that unilateral FRT may be considered as a complementary strategy for improving pre-planned COD performance in similar high-level soccer training contexts, whereas its transfer to linear speed and ball-constrained tasks appears limited.
