Psychosocial specificity in biological aging: selective associations of telomere length with stress, life evaluation, emotional utilization, and decision-making
Authors: Albertas Skurvydas, Ruta Dadeliene, Tomas Aukstikalnis, Natalja Istomina, Emilija Strazdaite, Agne Predkeliene, Neringa Baranauskiene, Danielius Serapinas, Dovile Valanciene, Daiva Majauskiene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTelomere length is a key biomarker of biological aging, yet it remains unclear whether psychosocial influences are broadly distributed across psychological functioning or concentrated within specific processes related to stress appraisal, emotional functioning, life evaluation, and decision-making.ObjectiveTo examine how specific psychosocial factors (e.g., mood, well-being, impulsivity, emotional intelligence, personality traits, and moral decision-making) are associated with telomere length in midlife adults.MethodsWe examined links between telomere length and psychosocial factors (mood, well-being, impulsivity, emotional intelligence, personality, and decision-making) in 84 midlife adults (50.9 ± 7.4 years). Telomere length was measured using high-throughput quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (HT Q-FISH), assessing both median telomere length and short telomere length (20th percentile).ResultsAge- and sex-adjusted regression analyses suggested selective associations between psychosocial factors and telomere length. Higher perceived stress, material satisfaction, and non-utilitarian decision-making were associated with shorter telomeres, whereas calmness and emotion utilization showed positive associations in primary models. Most cognitive, personality, and well-being measures showed small, non-significant associations.ConclusionsTelomere length was primarily associated with perceived stress, material satisfaction, emotional utilization, and decision-making tendencies, whereas personality traits, cognitive abilities, self-esteem, impulsivity, and most emotional characteristics showed little evidence of association. These findings suggest that biological aging may be more closely linked to how individuals evaluate, emotionally process, and respond to everyday life experiences than to broad psychological dispositions.

Introduction

Social and psychological factors are increasingly seen as key drivers of human health and biological aging, influencing outcomes through complex neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways, as well as through social conditions such as education and living environment [1, 2]. Notably, modern frameworks of aging now recognize psychosocial and social influences as essential components that interact with the core biological markers of aging [3]. Chronic psychological stress increases allostatic load and is consistently linked to negative health outcomes and faster biological aging [4–6]. Conversely, higher levels of emotional well-being–covering positive affect, life meaning, and life satisfaction–are associated with better physical health, less inflammation, and lower risk of death [7, 8]. Evidence also shows that lifestyle factors, especially physical activity, are strongly connected to improved emotional states, reduced stress, higher emotional intelligence, and better cognitive function [9–14]. These psychological and behavioral resources may serve as protective factors, buffering the impact of stress on biological aging [15, 16]. Importantly, not only emotional states but also cognitive and socio-moral processes–such as impulse control, reflective thinking, and decision-making–are closely linked to emotion and stress regulation [17–19]. Experimental evidence indicates that stress can influence moral decision-making by reducing reflective evaluation and increasing the impact of automatic emotional responses [20]. Collectively, these findings suggest that psychological, social, cognitive, and socio-moral factors interact as part of a complex system that contributes to individual differences in biological aging, especially in midlife populations. Telomere attrition is a key indicator of biological aging and has been directly connected to various age-related diseases and increased risk of death [3, 21–24]. Although telomere length is partly influenced by genetics and established early in life, it is increasingly seen as being dynamically affected by environmental and behavioral factors throughout life [25]. Meta-analytic and longitudinal evidence shows that oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are key biological factors driving telomere shortening [26], while lifestyle factors such as physical activity, sleep quality, and metabolic health also influence individual differences [27–29]. Importantly, an increasing body of high-impact research highlights that psychosocial factors have a significant impact on telomere dynamics. Chronic psychological stress is strongly linked to shorter telomeres and lower telomerase activity [30], while higher allostatic load and poorer psychosocial resources are associated with faster telomere loss [5]. Socioeconomic disadvantage, including lower education and poor living conditions, has also been linked to shorter leukocyte telomere length, partly through behavioral and physiological pathways [30]. Conversely, positive psychosocial environments — such as supportive relationships — have been associated with telomere preservation or even telomere lengthening in longitudinal studies [31]. These findings collectively suggest that telomere length is a sensitive biomarker of the cumulative biological impact of both adverse and protective psychosocial experiences throughout life.

By examining a broad range of psychosocial characteristics in relation to both median and short telomere length, while accounting for age and sex, the present study provides a comprehensive assessment of psychosocial correlates of telomere biology among midlife adults. Because psychosocial functioning spans multiple levels of psychological organization—from relatively stable traits and cognitive characteristics to more dynamic emotional, evaluative, and self-regulatory processes—we examined multiple psychosocial domains within a single analytical framework. This design allowed us to evaluate whether associations with telomere length were broadly distributed across psychosocial functioning or concentrated within specific processes more proximal to stress-related physiological regulation and biological embedding. Exploratory multivariable models were then used to examine whether associations reflected unique or shared contributions within broader psychosocial domains. Drawing on biological embedding and allostatic load frameworks, we hypothesized that telomere length would be associated selectively with psychosocial processes involved in stress appraisal, emotional regulation, subjective life evaluation, and behavioral self-regulation rather than with broad psychological characteristics per se. Specifically, stronger associations were expected for perceived stress, satisfaction with material living conditions, components of emotional intelligence, impulsivity, and decision-making tendencies than for higher-order constructs such as personality traits, cognitive abilities, or self-esteem.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study involved 84 midlife adults recruited from the community in Lithuania using a convenience sampling approach. Participants were selected according to the study eligibility criteria, which included being between 40 and 64 years old and having peripheral blood samples suitable for telomere length measurement. The average age of participants was 50.9 ± 7.4 years, representing a midlife group. The sample consisted of 54 men (64.3%) and 30 women (35.7%), with most participants having completed tertiary education (82.1%).

Exclusion criteria were established to ensure participants could complete the full assessment protocol and to minimize potential confounding due to severe physical limitations. Individuals were excluded if they were physically inactive because of functional limitations, unable to perform physical or functional assessments, or had a body mass index of 35 kg/m2 or higher. Participants with medical conditions that could make safe participation in study procedures impossible were also excluded.

The final sample comprised 84 midlife adults. Recruitment was limited to a relatively homogeneous population with a narrow age range and comparable distributions of body mass index and physical activity, thereby reducing background variability and increasing sensitivity to detect psychosocial correlates of telomere length. Sample size considerations are detailed in the Statistical Analysis section. Although the sample represents a relatively healthy and functionally capable midlife population, these criteria may limit how well the results apply to individuals with more severe health issues or functional impairments.

Human ethics and consent to participate

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. Participants were informed about the aims of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity of their responses, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Vilnius Regional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee, a sui generis body operating under Vilnius University (approval No. 2025/4–1660-1110).

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, and education level, were recorded during assessment. Higher education was defined as completion of a university or non-university tertiary education degree.

Blood collection and processing

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected under standardized conditions after an overnight fast. For telomere analysis, whole blood was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes, kept at 4–8 °C, and shipped within validated time frames to a certified external laboratory (Life Length Laboratories, Madrid, Spain).

For biochemical analyses, blood was collected into serum-separating tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature, centrifuged at 1,500–2,000 × g for 10 min, and serum aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. All samples were processed according to standardized and validated laboratory protocols to ensure high analytical reliability and consistency.

Telomere length assessment

Analytical platform

Leukocyte telomere length was assessed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using high-throughput quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (HT Q-FISH) at Life Length Laboratories (Madrid, Spain), a clinical laboratory accredited under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards. HT Q-FISH quantifies telomere fluorescence intensity at the individual-chromosome level across large numbers of cells, enabling assessment of the full distribution of telomere lengths within each sample, including the proportion of short telomeres. All samples were processed according to standardized laboratory procedures established by the provider.

Laboratory procedures

An 8-mL venous blood sample was collected from each participant and placed in EDTA-containing tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation and hybridized with a fluorescently labeled peptide nucleic acid probe complementary to the repeated telomeric sequence TTAGGG. Fluorescence images were acquired using automated high-content microscopy and analyzed with specialized image-analysis software. Fluorescence intensity values were converted to absolute telomere lengths (kilobases, kb) using calibration curves generated from internal reference cell lines with known telomere lengths. Thousands of telomeres were analyzed per participant, enabling construction of an individual telomere length distribution.

For the primary analyses, median telomere length and short telomere length were used as the main outcome variables, whereas telomere age acceleration was summarized descriptively.

Anthropometry

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Participants were classified according to World Health Organization criteria as normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), or obesity (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [32].

Physical activity and sedentary behavior

Physical activity and sedentary behavior were measured using the Danish Physical Activity Questionnaire (DPAQ), a validated 24-h recall tool based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire [33]. Participants reported the time spent at different activity intensities, defined by metabolic equivalents (METs), over seven consecutive days. Derived variables included sedentary time, light physical activity, moderate physical activity, vigorous physical activity, and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA).

Psychosocial measures

For multi-item psychosocial instruments, internal consistency in the present sample was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients.

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was measured using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [34], a 10-item instrument assessing the degree to which participants perceived their lives as unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded during the past month. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, and total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the PSS-10 was α = 0.80.

Personality traits

Personality traits were evaluated using the Big Five Inventory (BFI-44) [35], a 44-item instrument measuring five personality dimensions: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale and scored according to standard procedures, with higher scores indicating higher levels of each respective personality trait. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha values for the BFI-44 subscales were α = 0.72 for extraversion, α = 0.74 for agreeableness, α = 0.80 for conscientiousness, α = 0.76 for neuroticism, and α = 0.78 for openness.

Emotional intelligence

Emotional intelligence was assessed using the Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test (SSREIT) [36], a 33-item instrument assessing emotional intelligence across four domains: emotion perception, emotion utilization, management of one’s own emotions, and management of others’ emotions. Emotion perception refers to recognizing and attending to emotional signals. Emotion utilization refers to using emotional information to facilitate thinking, motivation, and goal-directed behavior. Management of one’s own emotions refers to regulating or modifying one’s own emotional responses, whereas management of others’ emotions refers to responding to and influencing the emotional responses of other people. Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater emotional intelligence. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the total SSREIT score was α = 0.90.

Impulsivity

Impulsivity was evaluated using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11) [37], a 30-item self-report instrument assessing attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsiveness. Items are scored according to standard procedures and summed to calculate a total impulsivity score, with higher scores indicating greater impulsivity. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the BIS-11 total score was α = 0.75.

Core self-evaluations

Core self-evaluations were measured using the Core Self-Evaluations Scale (CSES) [38], a 12-item self-report instrument assessing a higher-order construct that includes self-esteem, locus of control, generalized self-efficacy, and emotional stability. Items are scored according to standard procedures and summed to calculate a total score, with higher scores indicating more positive core self-evaluations. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the CSES was α = 0.85.

Moral decision-making

Moral decision-making was evaluated using a shortened version of Greene’s moral dilemma paradigm [17], which included six high-conflict personal moral dilemmas. These dilemmas required participants to decide whether it would be acceptable to personally harm one individual in order to prevent harm to a larger number of people. For example, participants were presented with scenarios structurally similar to the footbridge dilemma, in which pushing one person would stop a trolley from killing five others, and other personal-sacrifice dilemmas involving direct harm to one person to save several others. Responses endorsing personal harm to maximize overall outcomes were classified as utilitarian, whereas responses rejecting personal harm despite greater aggregate harm were classified as non-utilitarian. A total non-utilitarian decision-making score was calculated by summing the number of non-utilitarian responses across the six dilemmas, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward non-utilitarian decision-making.

Cognitive reflection

Cognitive reflection was evaluated using the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) [39], a three-item task designed to assess the tendency to override an intuitive but incorrect response and engage in more reflective reasoning. Each item presents a problem for which an immediately appealing answer is incorrect, whereas the correct answer requires deliberation. Responses were scored as correct or incorrect and summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater cognitive reflection.

Mood states

Mood states were measured using the Brunel Mood Scale Lithuanian version (BRUMS-LTU) [40], which assesses six transient mood dimensions: tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, and confusion. Participants rated how they felt “right now” on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 to 4. Subscale scores were treated as continuous variables, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of the respective current mood state. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha values for the BRUMS-LTU subscales were α = 0.80 for tension, α = 0.75 for depression, α = 0.84 for anger, α = 0.76 for vigor, α = 0.87 for fatigue, and α = 0.77 for confusion.

Weekly well-being and subjective measures

Weekly well-being was assessed using study-specific single-item self-report ratings of calmness, motivation, positive mood, and feeling rested over the past week. These items were not derived from a standardized multi-item affect scale such as the PANAS, PANAS-X, or State Affect Questionnaire. Each item was analyzed separately as a global weekly self-report indicator, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the respective well-being state. Subjective health and happiness-related indicators, including satisfaction with material living conditions and spiritual happiness, were assessed using single-item 10-point rating scales, with higher scores indicating more favorable self-ratings.

Chronic diseases

Information about chronic diseases was collected using structured self-report. Participants were asked whether they had any chronic or long-term medical condition and, if applicable, to specify the condition in an open-text response. For analysis, responses were coded as a dichotomous variable indicating the presence or absence of any self-reported chronic disease. Specific disease categories were not analyzed separately because the variable was intended to capture overall self-reported chronic disease status rather than diagnosis-specific effects.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted on 84 midlife adults. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are shown as counts and percentages. Descriptive statistics characterized the sample across demographic, lifestyle, psychosocial, physical, and telomere-related measures. The primary outcomes were median telomere length (MTL) and short telomere length, defined as the 20th percentile of the telomere length distribution, both measured in kilobases (kb). Telomere age acceleration and percentile-based assay comparisons were summarized descriptively. Associations between psychosocial factors and telomere outcomes were analyzed using linear regression. All models were a priori adjusted for age and sex, because both are commonly considered key demographic covariates in studies of telomere length and biological aging [21, 22, 25, 41]. Education was not included as a primary covariate because education level showed limited variability in the present sample, with most participants having completed tertiary education. Given the modest sample size, including this unevenly distributed variable in all primary models could have reduced model stability.

Results are reported as standardized regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and two-sided p values. Standardization allowed comparison of effect sizes across psychosocial variables assessed on different scales. General linear models (GLMs) were used for all regression analyses. Before interpreting the models, key assumptions were evaluated. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics, and residual diagnostics were examined to assess normality, homoscedasticity, and the presence of influential observations. Multicollinearity was low across all models (all VIF values < 2.0 and tolerance values > 0.50). Residual diagnostics indicated no serious departures from normality or homoscedasticity, and no influential observations were identified. The analysis involved two steps. First, each psychosocial variable was analyzed in a separate age- and sex-adjusted model to estimate its association with telomere outcomes, independent of basic demographic confounders. Second, to determine whether broader psychosocial domains had collective associations with telomere outcomes, domain-level regression models were used for personality traits, weekly mood states, mood components, and emotional intelligence factors. These models were used to evaluate the unique contributions of conceptually related variables while accounting for shared variance within each psychosocial domain. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. Assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), α = 0.05, 80% power, and three predictors in the regression model (the focal predictor, age, and sex), a minimum sample size of 77 participants was required for the primary analyses. For the exploratory domain-level models with 6–8 predictors, approximately 100 participants would have been required to detect a medium-sized effect under the same assumptions. Accordingly, the primary regression analyses were adequately powered, whereas the multivariable domain-level models were considered exploratory and intended to evaluate broader patterns of association within psychosocial domains. As a sensitivity analysis, statistically significant associations identified in the primary regression analyses were re-estimated using heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors. Robust standard errors were used to assess whether the observed associations remained significant under potential violations of the homoscedasticity assumption. Explained variance is shown as R2, along with the overall significance test of each model. Statistical significance was set at two-sided p < 0.05. Due to the multidimensional and hypothesis-driven nature of the analyses, and the high intercorrelation among psychosocial variables, formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni) was not used, as it would be overly conservative and increase the risk of Type II error. Instead, focus was placed on the consistency, direction, and effect size of associations across both telomere outcomes (median and short telomeres), as well as on convergence within psychosocial domains.

Results

Participants (n = 84) were mostly middle-aged (50.9 ± 7.4 years), with a high prevalence of overweight/obesity (72.6%) but generally healthy lifestyle characteristics, including low smoking rates (89.3% non-smokers) and moderate physical activity levels (Table 1). Psychological profiles revealed relatively low stress and negative mood, along with high emotional intelligence and well-being scores. The median telomere length was 9.6 ± 0.76 kb, while the average telomere length was 11.1 ± 0.7 kb, with moderate variability in biological aging, as shown by telomere age acceleration (4.6 ± 5.4 years) (Table 2). Regression analyses indicated a consistent pattern for both median telomere length (MTL) and short telomeres (20th percentile), with several psychosocial factors showing significant associations, all adjusted for age and sex (Table 3). Material satisfaction (β = − 0.26 to − 0.26, p = 0.01), perceived stress (β = − 0.27 to − 0.29, p = 0.01), and non-utilitarian decision-making (β = − 0.26 to − 0.25, p = 0.02) were moderately and inversely related to MTL and telomere length at the 20th percentile. In contrast, calmness (β = 0.35–0.36, p = 0.02–0.01) and the ability to utilize emotions (β = 0.27–0.27, p = 0.01 for both outcomes) showed moderate positive relationships with longer telomeres, whereas positive emotions showed an inverse association in the primary models, but this association was attenuated in HC3 robust sensitivity analyses. These associations refer to age- and sex-adjusted single-predictor models, in which each psychosocial variable was entered separately. In the domain-level emotional intelligence model, where all emotional intelligence components were entered simultaneously, emotion utilization remained independently associated with short telomere length, but not with median telomere length. Most remaining cognitive, mood, personality, and emotional intelligence measures showed small and non-significant associations. Sensitivity analyses using HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors yielded largely comparable results for the single-predictor models. The associations of material satisfaction (MTL: p = 0.03; short telomeres: p = 0.03), perceived stress (MTL: p = 0.04; short telomeres: p = 0.02), utilizing emotions (MTL: p = 0.01; short telomeres: p = 0.01), and non-utilitarian decision-making (MTL: p = 0.02; short telomeres: p = 0.02) remained statistically significant across both telomere outcomes. The association of calmness remained significant for median telomere length (p = 0.03) but not for short telomere length (p = 0.10). Discussion

Consistent with previous research, older age was associated with shorter median and short telomere lengths, whereas no significant associations were observed for sex. Beyond these established demographic correlates, several psychosocial characteristics were associated with telomere length. Robustness analyses indicated that material satisfaction, perceived stress, non-utilitarian decision-making, and the ability to use emotions showed the most consistent associations across both telomere outcomes, whereas calmness was related only to median telomere length. In contrast, no reliable associations emerged for the Big Five personality traits, individual mood components, or most dimensions of emotional intelligence. Although exploratory multivariable models suggested broader patterns of psychosocial influence, these findings require cautious interpretation and replication in larger samples. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine personality traits, emotional experiences, self-esteem, logical reasoning, decision-making style, and multidimensional emotional intelligence simultaneously in relation to both median and short telomere length, thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of psychosocial correlates of biological aging.

Our initial finding suggests a common link between psychosocial adversity and faster cellular aging, starting with perceived stress. This aligns with evidence that chronic psychological stress increases oxidative stress, decreases telomerase activity, and speeds up telomere shortening, supporting allostatic load models where repeated stress becomes biologically embedded [4–7]. We also found that more non-utilitarian decision-making was associated with shorter telomeres. Agreeableness showed an inverse association in the initial domain-level model, but this finding was not robust and should be interpreted cautiously. The link between non-utilitarian decision-making may indicate greater reliance on emotion-driven, stress-sensitive processing [7, 20]. More broadly, the association between non-utilitarian decision-making and shorter telomeres may suggest that telomere biology is linked not only to emotional states themselves but also to the cognitive–affective strategies used to evaluate and resolve emotionally salient situations. According to dual-process models of moral judgment, non-utilitarian responses rely more heavily on immediate emotional reactions, whereas utilitarian judgments involve greater engagement of cognitive control, reflective reasoning, and the capacity to override intuitive emotional responses [17–20]. From this perspective, the observed association may reflect individual differences in the balance between emotion-driven and reflective modes of information processing. Because effective cognitive regulation can attenuate physiological stress responses and facilitate adaptive coping, greater reliance on reflective processing may indirectly contribute to a lower cumulative allostatic burden, whereas predominantly emotion-driven responding may be associated with more sustained physiological activation over time [16, 20]. Although causal interpretations cannot be drawn from the present cross-sectional data, this finding raises the possibility that stable differences in cognitive–emotional decision-making styles are reflected in biological aging markers. Importantly, moral decision-making has rarely been investigated in relation to telomere biology; therefore, the present results point to a potentially novel and previously underexplored pathway linking psychosocial functioning, self-regulation, and cellular aging.

Similarly, although agreeableness is often seen as a socially desirable trait, it has been linked to increased interpersonal sensitivity, compliance, and emotion suppression, which may raise vulnerability to chronic social stress and allostatic load, thus contributing to biological aging [16, 25]. Interestingly, higher material satisfaction was also associated with shorter telomeres. One possible reason is that individuals who report greater material satisfaction may have accumulated their socioeconomic status through prolonged exposure to effort- or achievement-related stress, which is known to have lasting biological effects, including telomere shortening [1, 2, 16]. Therefore, subjective material satisfaction might sometimes reflect a history of sustained stress rather than reduced biological burden. This counterintuitive finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect unmeasured socioeconomic, occupational, or lifestyle factors rather than material satisfaction per se. Importantly, these relationships were consistent across both median telomere length and short telomeres, with similar effect patterns observed in both measures, indicating a strong and consistent pattern of associations.

Our second key finding underscores the central role of affective states, with calmness consistently showing a positive association. The beneficial link of calmness aligns with evidence that lower physiological arousal and effective stress regulation decrease chronic activation of neuroendocrine and inflammatory pathways involved in telomere attrition [25, 42]. Calm affective states may indicate more efficient recovery processes and reduced allostatic load, which in turn limit oxidative stress and cellular damage known to contribute to telomere shortening [16, 26].

Our third key finding shows that emotional intelligence was not uniformly associated with telomere biology; rather, the association was specific to emotion utilization. In the SSREIT framework, emotion utilization refers to the use of emotional information to support thinking, motivation, and goal-directed behavior, and should not be interpreted as identical to emotion regulation or emotion management [36]. This distinction is important because the SSREIT domains assessing management of one’s own emotions and management of others’ emotions were not significantly associated with telomere length in the present study. Therefore, the observed association appears to reflect the functional use of emotional information rather than a general capacity to regulate or manage emotions.

One possible interpretation is that individuals who are better able to use emotional information in a goal-directed manner may engage in more adaptive appraisal, coping, or behavioral responses to daily challenges [16, 36]. Such processes could indirectly support stress recovery and reduce cumulative physiological burden through lower allostatic load and reduced activation of stress-related neuroendocrine, inflammatory, and oxidative pathways, which are relevant to cellular aging processes [5, 16, 25, 42]. However, because emotion utilization was measured as a distinct emotional intelligence domain rather than as a direct measure of emotion regulation, this interpretation should be considered tentative. The lack of significant associations for the emotion management domains in the present study further suggests that not all aspects of emotional intelligence are equally relevant to telomere biology.

This specificity is particularly relevant because the independent association of emotion utilization was observed for short telomere length in the domain-level model. Critically short telomeres, more than average telomere length alone, are considered important drivers of genomic instability, cellular senescence, and age-related disease processes [43]. Overall, these findings suggest a more specific model in which the goal-directed use of emotional information, rather than global emotional intelligence or emotion management more broadly, may be linked to biological aging markers.

Our fourth key finding is the lack of associations across a wide range of psychosocial domains, including overall well-being (except for calmness), self-esteem, logical reasoning, most personality traits, and many components of mood and emotional intelligence. This pattern suggests that psychosocial influences on telomere biology are not broad but highly specific. One possible explanation is that many of these constructs mainly reflect cognitive appraisal or trait-level dispositions, which may be more distant from the physiological pathways directly involved in cellular aging. In contrast, telomere dynamics are more closely linked to chronic activation of stress-related biological systems–such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroendocrine dysregulation–which are not necessarily reflected in measures of overall well-being or cognitive functioning [16, 25, 42]. Additionally, previous research indicates that psychosocial resources do not uniformly translate into biological protection and may influence health through complex, context-dependent behavioral and physiological pathways [1, 2, 5, 25]. The lack of associations with self-esteem and logical reasoning further supports the idea that higher-order evaluative or cognitive processes are insufficient to impact cellular aging without effective stress regulation. Collectively, these findings support a model of specificity, in which only certain psychosocial processes–especially those related to stress regulation and recovery–are biologically embedded in telomere dynamics.

The present findings further suggest that the observed specificity may reflect differences in how fully psychosocial characteristics become biologically embedded. Although constructs such as personality traits, self-esteem, and general well-being are important determinants of psychological functioning, they may not directly capture the frequency or intensity of physiological stress responses that accumulate over time. In contrast, perceived stress, emotional utilization, calmness, and decision-making tendencies may more closely reflect recurrent patterns of appraisal, regulation, and recovery that repeatedly engage neuroendocrine, inflammatory, and oxidative pathways implicated in telomere maintenance and cellular aging [16, 25, 42]. From this perspective, telomere length may be less sensitive to broad psychological dispositions per se and more sensitive to psychosocial processes that translate into sustained physiological regulation and allostatic burden [5, 16, 25]. This interpretation offers a potential explanation for why many theoretically relevant psychosocial constructs showed little evidence of association with telomere length despite their established relevance for health and well-being.

The midlife context is particularly important for interpreting these findings. Midlife represents a transitional period during which cumulative psychosocial exposures may begin to become more clearly reflected in biological aging processes, while there may still be meaningful opportunities for prevention and intervention. In this age range, patterns of stress appraisal, emotional functioning, subjective life evaluation, and decision-making may influence long-term health trajectories by shaping repeated physiological responses to everyday demands. The present findings therefore suggest that psychosocial processes associated with telomere length in midlife may be relevant not only as correlates of current biological aging but also as potential early indicators of later-life health vulnerability. Identifying these selective psychosocial correlates during midlife may help inform future longitudinal research on modifiable pathways linking psychological functioning to healthy aging.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it combines a wide and multidimensional set of psychosocial constructs–including personality traits, detailed affective states, cognitive functioning, self-esteem, decision-making, and emotional intelligence–within a single analytical framework. This approach allows for a more comprehensive examination of psychosocial factors related to biological aging than is typical in the literature. Second, examining both median telomere length and short telomeres (20th percentile) at the same time marks an important methodological improvement, since critically short telomeres are increasingly recognized as key drivers of cellular dysfunction and aging-related processes. The consistency of results across these two indicators enhances the robustness and clarity of the findings. Third, by using standardized effect sizes and systematically comparing different psychosocial domains, the study provides support for a specificity model. It shows that only certain psychosocial processes–especially those involving stress regulation and emotional functioning–are connected to telomere biology, thus reducing the overgeneralization often seen in this field.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present study had a relatively modest sample size. The sample was sufficient to detect medium-sized effects in the primary regression analyses, which examined a focal psychosocial predictor while adjusting for age and sex. However, broader multivariable models with 6–8 predictors were used to evaluate the unique contributions of conceptually related variables within the same psychosocial domain, such as personality traits, mood components, and emotional intelligence dimensions. Although these analyses provided a more comprehensive assessment of psychosocial influences, the available sample was somewhat smaller than the size recommended for detecting medium-sized effects in models of this complexity. Consequently, these multivariable analyses should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and non-significant findings may partly reflect limited statistical power.

Second, because multiple psychosocial predictors were examined and formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied, the possibility of Type I error cannot be excluded. Although we emphasized consistency of effect direction, convergence across both telomere outcomes, and HC3 robust sensitivity analyses, these procedures do not fully eliminate the risk of chance findings. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require replication in larger, independent samples.

Third, the cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions, and it remains uncertain whether psychosocial factors influence telomere dynamics or whether underlying biological aging processes shape psychosocial functioning. Additionally, psychosocial variables were based on self-report, which could introduce bias and may not fully capture underlying physiological or behavioral processes linked to cellular aging. Although the models were adjusted for age and sex, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as lifestyle behaviors, early-life stress, or genetic influences on telomere maintenance, cannot be ruled out [25, 41].

Fourth, Cronbach’s alpha values were calculated for the multi-item psychosocial instruments used in the present sample. However, several subjective variables were assessed using single-item ratings, for which internal consistency cannot be calculated. Therefore, these measures should be interpreted as global self-report indicators rather than multi-item psychometric scales.

Fifth, the sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of racial and ethnic background, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Previous studies have shown that leukocyte telomere length may differ across racial and ethnic groups and that race/ethnicity may also shape associations between social exposures and telomere biology [45]. Therefore, the present findings should be replicated in larger and more racially and ethnically diverse samples. Finally, telomere length represents only one marker of cellular aging within the broader framework of biological aging hallmarks [44]. Future studies should examine whether the present psychosocial associations replicate when using complementary and increasingly used biomarkers of biological aging, such as DNA methylation-based epigenetic clocks, which may provide broader estimates of biological age and biological aging rates [46].

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that psychosocial influences on biological aging are characterized by specificity rather than generality. Associations with telomere length were concentrated in processes related to stress appraisal, subjective life evaluation, emotional utilization, and decision-making, whereas broad personality traits, cognitive abilities, self-esteem, impulsivity, and most emotional characteristics showed little evidence of association. These findings raise the possibility that biological embedding may depend less on broad psychological dispositions and more on how individuals experience, interpret, and regulate everyday life. By identifying a selective set of psychosocial processes associated with both median and short telomere length, this study provides preliminary support for a more targeted framework for understanding how psychological functioning may become biologically embedded across adulthood. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether these pathways contribute causally to biological aging and whether they represent modifiable targets for promoting healthy aging.
