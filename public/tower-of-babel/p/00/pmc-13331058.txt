Screening of hereditary elliptocytosis caused by SPTB mutations and identification of its association with significant jaundice among Thai neonates
Authors: Kritiya Rattanaseksan, Noppawan Tangbubpha, Praguywan Kadegasem, Tanyanee Khlangtan, Prathana Kongurai, Kanuengnit Emrat, Nongnuch Sirachainan, Pharuhad Pongmee, Duantida Songdej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary elliptocytosis (HE) caused by heterozygosity of three common SPTB mutations, namely SPTB Providence (c.6055T>C), SPTB Buffalo (c.6074T>G) and SPTB Chiang Mai (c.6224A>G), is the most prevalent form of red blood cell membranopathy in Thailand. Here, we explored the prevalence of the mutated SPTB-causing HE using molecular screening and demonstrated its impact on the development and severity of jaundice among Thai neonates.MethodsThis cross-sectional study identified the three common SPTB mutations in cord blood samples from all neonates born at Ramathibodi Hospital from March to November 2024 using a recently developed robust, rapid molecular method. Clinical data related to neonatal jaundice were obtained from medical records and were analysed according to the presence of the SPTB mutations.ResultsA total of 1505 neonates with a mean (SD) gestational age of 37.5 (2.3) weeks were enrolled. Thirteen unrelated neonates were identified as having HE caused by the screened SPTB mutations, yielding a prevalence of 0.86%. The analyses revealed the mutated SPTB-causing HE as an independent risk factor for neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy (risk ratio 5.01, 95% CI 2.56 to 9.79, p<0.001). The mean total bilirubin levels of neonates with the mutated SPTB-causing HE were significantly higher than those of neonates with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency throughout the first 3 days of life (p=0.009 to 0.049). Most neonates with HE caused by the common SPTB mutations who required phototherapy did not exhibit anaemia and were not diagnosed clinically at birth. Coinheritance of the SPTB mutations and G6PD deficiency likely aggravated anaemia and worsened the degree of jaundice.ConclusionsHE caused by the three common SPTB mutations is associated with the development of significant jaundice requiring treatment among Thai neonates. Given the region’s high prevalence, screening for the SPTB mutations is likely beneficial for comprehensive monitoring of neonatal jaundice and for disease counselling.Keywords: Epidemiology, Jaundice, Neonatology, Low and Middle Income Countries, Genetics

Introduction

Hereditary elliptocytosis (HE) is the most common red blood cell (RBC) membrane disorder in the Thai population1 and is characterised by decreased mechanical stability and increased susceptibility to RBC membrane fragmentation.2 HE is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern due to a heterozygous mutation in one of the genes encoding horizontal RBC membrane proteins.2 These included SPTA1, SPTB and EPB41, which encode α-spectrin, β-spectrin and protein 4.1R, respectively.3 Unlike HE found in the population of Western countries and West Africa, in which mutated SPTA1 is frequently identified,4,7 Thai individuals with HE were most often found to harbour SPTB mutations.1 A unique feature of mutated SPTB-causing HE in the Thai population is that the vast majority (approximately 90%) of the mutations recur within the three most common SPTB mutations in the exon 29/30 (NM_001024858.2), namely SPTB Providence (SPTB c.6055T>C, p.S2019P), SPTB Buffalo (SPTB c.6074T>G, p.L2025R) and SPTB Chiang Mai (SPTB c.6224A>G, p.E2075G).1 8 9 The inheritance of one or more of these three mutations on both alleles resulted in hereditary pyropoikilocytosis (HPP), which was recently revealed as the most common non-thalassemic severe inherited haemolytic anaemia (IHA)10 among the Thai population.8 9 This suggests that the prevalence of HE caused by these three SPTB mutations is likely high in the Southeast Asian subcontinent.

Older children and adults with mutated SPTB-causing HE typically manifest without symptoms. However, similar to patients with hereditary spherocytosis (HS), a significant proportion of these individuals reported having a history of neonatal jaundice, some of whom necessitated intensive phototherapy or required RBC transfusion due to haemolysis at birth.1 Nevertheless, studies on the actual prevalence of the three common SPTB mutations-causing HE and their magnitude of contribution to neonatal jaundice among Thai neonates remain scarce. In 2022, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) launched a revised clinical practice guideline on the management of jaundice in the newborn infant at 35 or more weeks of gestation.11 This guideline aims to minimise the occurrence of severe jaundice and kernicterus by identifying neonates at risk and initiating early phototherapy. Besides glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and ABO blood group incompatibility, it is currently unclear whether HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations should be considered a risk factor for neonatal jaundice that warrants screening in the region.

Diagnosis of HE typically relies on the presence of elliptocytes in peripheral blood smears (PBS). This is because RBC indices and the eosin-5’-maleimide-binding assay are not informative in most HE cases.10 12 Routine visualisation of RBC morphology may not be feasible in daily clinical practice, and its interpretation may be difficult for general paediatricians, particularly when the proportion of elliptocytes is low. By leveraging recurrent causative variants, a molecular method with a rapid turnaround time for diagnosing HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations was recently established.13 This makes it possible for an additional screening of neonates who may be at risk of having significant jaundice. The aim of this study is to identify the regional prevalence of neonates with HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations through molecular screening of umbilical cord blood samples and to primarily explore the impact of the disorder on the development and severity of neonatal jaundice.

Methods

Cord blood screening of the common SPTB mutations

This cross-sectional study recruited all neonates born at Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, from March to November 2024. Those with parental written informed consent obtained early after birth were enrolled. Neonates with evidence of direct hyperbilirubinemia in the first week of life, if present, would be excluded from further analysis. This was aimed to exclude neonates with certain underlying diseases causing jaundice, such as severe congenital infections, sepsis, biliary atresia, neonatal hepatitis or inherited metabolic disorders. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Thailand (COA. No. MURA 2024/72) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

At our institution, all neonates are routinely screened for G6PD deficiency using cord blood samples collected in EDTA tubes (Fluorescent spot test, FST; R&D Diagnostic) for the purpose of neonatal jaundice monitoring. The residual cord blood samples were collected and subjected to DNA extraction and identification of the three common SPTB mutations: SPTB Providence (c.6055T>C), SPTB Buffalo (c.6074T>G) and SPTB Chiang Mai (c.6224A>G). The mutation analysis was performed in batches for this study using the recently developed technique of multiplex amplification refractory mutation system-PCR combined with high-resolution melting (ARMS-PCR/HRM) curve assay.13 The results of all mutated and randomly selected wild-type samples were confirmed using Sanger sequencing. Mutation reports and genetic counselling were provided to the parents of neonates with the common SPTB mutations within 6 months of genetic testing.

Clinical data collection

All participants underwent routine neonatal care. General clinical data, including gestational age (GA), sex, birth weight, perinatal events and data related to the occurrence and management of neonatal jaundice, were obtained from medical records. Monitoring and management of neonatal jaundice were guided by the 2022 AAP clinical practice guidelines for the management and prevention of hyperbilirubinemia in newborn infants ≥35 weeks of gestation11 or the 2012 management of hyperbilirubinemia in preterm infants <35 weeks of gestation,14 as appropriate.

Total bilirubin (TB) level was assessed using total serum bilirubin (TSB) or transcutaneous bilirubin (TcB) measured using Jaundice Meter JM-105 (Drӓger Medical, Germany). Daily TcB measurement was performed for at least 2–3 days or until the day of discharge in most enrolled neonates. Once the TcB level approached or exceeded the hour-specific threshold for diagnosis of neonatal jaundice, at least one TSB measurement and a ‘jaundice work-up’ were performed to determine other possible causes besides G6PD deficiency. This additional jaundice work-up included blood tests for direct bilirubin, complete blood count, reticulocyte count, maternal and neonatal ABO and Rh blood groups and direct antiglobulin test. PBS from individual neonates was obtained on request from clinicians, typically when haemolysis was suspected. Treatment of significant neonatal jaundice with phototherapy or total exchange blood transfusion was given to affected neonates according to the AAP guidelines. Subsequent TB follow-up and monitoring were performed using TSB or TcB at the discretion of the neonatologists. The AAP guidelines allowed the use of TcB for a reasonable estimation of TSB,11 and the Jaundice Meter was routinely calibrated for clinical use.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA software, V.18.0 (StataCorp LLC). Study variables were reported using appropriate descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means (SD) or medians (IQR) for continuous variables. Comparative analysis of categorical variables was conducted using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Comparisons of two independent parametric and non-parametric data were performed using the Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test, respectively. The association between parameters of interest and the development of significant neonatal jaundice was evaluated using the binary and multiple logistic regression models. A p value of less than 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Enrolled neonates and the prevalence of HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations

There were 1672 neonates born at Ramathibodi Hospital during the study period. Of these, we excluded 167 because the parents denied consent, leaving 1505 (90%) neonates (males=815, females=690) enrolled in the study. None of these neonates were identified as having direct hyperbilirubinemia in the first week of life. The overall mean (SD) GA at birth was 37.5 (2.3) weeks, with 1260 (83.7%) neonates born term (≥37 weeks). The three common SPTB mutations were identified in 13 unrelated neonates (SPTB Providence; n=6, SPTB Buffalo; n=4, SPTB Chiang Mai; n=3), resulting in an HE prevalence of 0.86% in the studied population. Examples of ARMS-PCR/HRM curves for mutation identification are shown in online supplemental figure S1. Sanger sequencing also confirmed heterozygosity for the three common SPTB mutations in affected neonates (online supplemental figure S2). The majority of neonates with HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations (9/13, 69%) experienced significant neonatal jaundice that required phototherapy. This event occurred without any observed differences among neonates with different SPTB mutation types. No neonates required a total exchange transfusion in this study. Two of 13 neonates affected with HE were also diagnosed as having G6PD deficiency by routine FST, both of whom required phototherapy. HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations as an independent risk factor of neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy

Clinical and laboratory parameters potentially associated with neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy were primarily explored using univariate analysis. Five parameters were retained, including preterm birth (GA <37 weeks), a 1-week decrease in GA at birth (from 40 weeks), low birth weight (<2500 g), G6PD deficiency and the common SPTB mutations (table 2). Subsequent multiple logistic regression analysis identified four significant parameters: preterm birth, low birth weight, G6PD deficiency and the common SPTB mutations. Among these parameters, HE identified in those who inherited the three common SPTB mutations was an independent risk factor for neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy, with the highest risk ratio of 5.01 (95% CI 2.56 to 9.79, p<0.001) (table 2).

HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations accounted for 4% (9/228) of all neonates with jaundice requiring phototherapy in this study. Notably, only two of the nine identified neonates with HE who required phototherapy were initially referred to a haematologist and diagnosed before discharge. One of them was a term female neonate with G6PD deficiency who required double phototherapy from the 10th hour of life. Her PBS showed a few spherocytes and fragmented RBCs (figure 1A), while one of the parents’ PBS showed a few elliptocytes (figure 1B,C). The other neonate was a preterm female neonate (born at GA 36 weeks) who required single phototherapy from the 17th hour of life. A few spherocytic RBCs were also seen on visualisation of her PBS. Additionally, this neonate was readmitted for the second round of phototherapy on the 6th day of life. Identification of the common SPTB mutations showed that the two neonates were positive for SPTB Providence and SPTB Buffalo, respectively. Initial clinical diagnoses of the remaining seven neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE who required phototherapy were jaundice of prematurity (n=1), G6PD deficiency (n=1), neonatal jaundice due to suboptimal intake (n=1), haemolytic jaundice of unknown cause (n=1) and inconclusive jaundice (n=3).

Characteristics of neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE who required and did not require phototherapy

Considering all enrolled neonates with jaundice, the median (min-max) onset of jaundice in neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE (25 (10–73) hours) was significantly earlier than that of those without (44 (5–292) hours, p=0.027), although the significance was not observed when considering phototherapy and non-phototherapy groups separately. Among neonates requiring phototherapy, the mean (SD) highest TB was not significantly different between those with and without SPTB mutations (14.0 (3.6) vs 13.5 (4.7) mg/dL, p=0.75). This was given that the mean GA at birth and the proportion of those with G6PD deficiency were comparable between both groups (table 1). Additionally, the median phototherapy duration did not differ significantly between the two groups. Interestingly, neonates with HE caused by the common SPTB mutations who required phototherapy did not exhibit significantly lower initial mean Hb levels or higher mean reticulocyte counts than those without (p=0.11 and p=0.67, respectively). Of the two neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE and G6PD deficiency, one had anaemia, defined as Hb<13 g/dL, at the initial jaundice work-up, while the other developed anaemia at 1-month follow-up (Hb 9.6 g/dL). None of the remaining seven neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE who required phototherapy had anaemia at initial jaundice work-up. In this group, Hb levels were repeated within the first 2 months in five neonates, and only one additional neonate developed anaemia (Hb 10.8 g/fL) on the 9th day of life.

Among enrolled neonates who did not require phototherapy, a substantially higher proportion of neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE, compared with those without, had TcB levels that approached or exceeded the hour-specific threshold for jaundice work-up (50% vs 13%, p=0.026). This implied that the vast majority of neonates with the SPTB mutations suffered from neonatal jaundice at some level.

Severity of jaundice in neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE versus G6PD deficiency

Given that G6PD deficiency is a well-established risk of neonatal jaundice,15,17 the clinical significance and magnitude of jaundice in neonates with isolated HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations (n=11) and those with isolated G6PD deficiency (n=89) were compared. The proportions of neonates with isolated HE and isolated G6PD deficiency who required phototherapy were 64% and 46%, respectively (p=0.27). Among those requiring phototherapy, neonates with isolated HE tended to have an earlier median onset of jaundice and a higher mean highest TB level than those with isolated G6PD deficiency, although the differences were not statistically significant (p=0.21 and p=0.38, respectively; table 3). However, the median phototherapy duration and the mean Hb level were similar between the two neonate groups. The day-by-day data of all enrolled neonates, shown in figure 2, also demonstrated that the mean (SD) TB level of neonates with isolated HE caused by the common SPTB mutations was significantly higher than that of those with isolated G6PD deficiency throughout the first 3 days of life. The severity of jaundice in two neonates with coinheritance of mutated SPTB-causing HE and G6PD deficiency was even more pronounced. The TB levels of these neonates were higher than the mean (SD) TB levels of neonates with HE or G6PD deficiency alone, especially on the first day of life, leading to an early initiation of phototherapy. This suggests that HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations and G6PD deficiency are likely additive risks of severe neonatal jaundice.

Discussion

This study found that the prevalence of HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations is approximately 0.9% among neonates born in the central region of Thailand. Approximately two-thirds of the affected neonates suffered from significant neonatal jaundice requiring treatment. We also revealed that among neonates with significant jaundice requiring treatment, 4% inherited mutated SPTB-causing HE. This proportion was similar to the 4.8% contribution of this disorder to the diagnosis of neonates with jaundice reported in a recent retrospective study from the northeastern region of Thailand,18 suggesting a sizeable prevalence across different parts of the country. Additionally, we demonstrated that the three common SPTB mutations are associated with a fivefold increased risk of developing neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy, regardless of the specific mutation type. This risk ratio was higher than the pooled risk ratio of 3.01 for the need for phototherapy in neonates with G6PD deficiency compared with G6PD-normal neonates, as reported in a previous meta-analysis.19 This suggests that HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations should be a matter of concern for routine neonatal care and monitoring of neonatal jaundice development in endemic countries, such as Thailand.

The prevalence of HE caused by the common SPTB mutations identified in cord blood from consecutively born neonates in this study is slightly higher than the 0.65% reported prevalence among Thai blood donors in our previous work.13 This supports that most affected neonates with this disorder eventually become asymptomatic and can be blood donors as they get older. The prevalence of HE reported in this study is comparable to that in other malaria-endemic regions, such as Africa (0.6% to 1.6%), although the common causative mutations differ.2 4 This prevalence is higher than the HE prevalence in Europe and the USA, where approximately 0.03% to 0.05% prevalence was observed.2 4 20

This study showed that, overall, the mean TB level was highest in neonates with HE caused by the common SPTB mutations compared with those with isolated G6PD deficiency and those without both disorders during the first 3 days of life. This explained the earlier onset of jaundice requiring jaundice work-up and/or phototherapy in neonates with HE as compared with the other two neonate groups. The mean onset of jaundice requiring phototherapy of 25th hours marked the early second day of life as a critical period for jaundice monitoring in neonates with HE. However, no neonates in this study had kernicterus or required a total exchange transfusion. Additionally, the mean highest TB level and the median duration of phototherapy in neonates with HE caused by the common SPTB mutations did not differ from those of neonates without HE. The finding was dissimilar to previous studies conducted in neonates with G6PD deficiency and hyperbilirubinemia, which showed higher maximum TB levels in G6PD-deficient compared with G6PD-normal groups and a higher incidence of kernicterus in a rare case.15 16 These were likely owing to a recent implementation of the revised AAP guideline, allowing comprehensive monitoring and earlier treatment for neonates at risk of developing severe jaundice.11 The three common SPTB mutations and G6PD deficiency are likely additive risks for significant jaundice, as two neonates with the coinheritance of these conditions suffered neonatal jaundice, which required phototherapy as early as the first day of life. However, further studies with a larger number of neonates who coinherit the two disorders are required to demonstrate consistent results.

Unlike the diagnosis of HS, there are often no clues leading to the diagnosis of HE in neonates with significant jaundice, unless direct visualisation of PBS and/or molecular diagnosis are performed. The diagnosis of HS in neonates with jaundice is often brought to attention on the presence of specific RBC indices, including a high mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC, >36.5 to 37 g/dL), a low mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and an MCHC-to-MCV ratio >0.360.21 Conversely, RBC indices are not informative for diagnosing most HE cases.12 RBC morphologic diagnosis of HE caused by common SPTB mutations, although considered helpful, can be challenging. PBS of neonates harbouring one or more alleles of these SPTB mutations sometimes show spherocytes and fragmented RBCs, rather than ovalocytes/elliptocytes typically seen in affected individuals in childhood or adulthood.8 This finding was also observed in one of the neonates with HE and jaundice in this study (figure 1), which could potentially lead to a misdiagnosis of HS if the molecular diagnosis of common SPTB mutations was not performed. Additionally, neonates with HS who present with neonatal jaundice are most often affected by concomitant anaemia,22,24 whereas this study showed that neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE and jaundice did not have anaemia in the majority of cases. These challenges explain why only 2 of 9 neonates with HE who required phototherapy were diagnosed before discharge. This information also supported that molecular diagnosis plays a crucial role in differentiating HE caused by the common SPTB mutations from HS in neonates with significant jaundice, especially those suspected of having RBC membrane disorder. The importance lies in the differences in the clinical courses of HE and HS. While neonates with HE often become asymptomatic without further acute haemolytic episodes after the first few months of life, neonates with typical HS often manifest chronic haemolytic anaemia (Hb ~8–11 g/dL) and reticulocytosis throughout life.1 8 Therefore, family counselling should be provided according to the correct diagnosis. Furthermore, as biallelic inheritance of the three common SPTB mutations results in HPP, the most common non-thalassemic severe IHA in the region,8 genetic counselling on the ‘carrier-like’ status of affected neonates with HE caused by these mutations should be provided.

As mentioned above, most neonates with HE and significant jaundice in this study did not have anaemia, despite elevated reticulocyte counts. We hypothesise that this was because the degree of haemolysis in affected neonates with HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations may not be as pronounced as that usually observed in affected neonates with HS. This finding also aligns with the related literature,25 which reports that most HE cases exhibit inapparent clinical symptoms. A few cases of HE caused by autosomal dominant inheritance of an SPTA1 or SPTB mutation have been reported, presenting with severe neonatal haemolytic anaemia that gradually resolved within the first few months. This phenotype is also known as HE with infantile poikilocytosis variant.26 From this study, it was likely that the coinheritance of G6PD deficiency was an additional factor aggravating the infantile poikilocytosis phenotype, as two of the three neonates who developed anaemia within the first 2 months of life were those with mutated SPTB-causing HE and G6PD deficiency. On the other hand, the recent study from the northeastern part of Thailand reported that up to 30% of neonates with HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations had concomitant anaemia.18 This might partly be due to a selection bias towards more severe cases in investigations of this disorder. Additionally, other genetic factors, including coinheritance of thalassaemia, which was not universally screened in both studies, might also play a role in the development of anaemia in neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE. This issue requires further investigation.

The strength of this study is that we obtained prevalence estimates for HE caused by the common SPTB mutations from a large number of consecutively born neonates, rather than identifying mutations only in neonates with jaundice or those already suspected of having RBC membrane defects. This allowed us to characterise the magnitude of jaundice as a result of being neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE, and the actual contribution of the SPTB mutations in Thai neonates with significant jaundice. The 4% contribution of HE caused by the common SPTB mutations in neonates with jaundice requiring treatment is noticeable and worth screening, given that a robust technique for molecular diagnosis with a rapid turnaround time (4 hours) is available.13 For comprehensive jaundice monitoring and treatment, universal screening for the SPTB mutations in cord blood would be preferred over screening only when the jaundice work-up is required. Such a benefit would be obvious in affected neonates coinheriting G6PD deficiency, in whom severe jaundice and anaemia may occur from the first day of life. Nevertheless, screening for the SPTB mutations in Thai neonates with jaundice at any time during postnatal hospitalisation remains beneficial for the counselling of the asymptomatic nature of the affected individuals in their childhood/adulthood, while having a carrier-like status for the much more severe disease (HPP) to the offspring. The limitation of this work is the lack of a cost-effectiveness study demonstrating a sufficiently high prevalence of the three SPTB mutations to support policy-level screening, given that the cost of an individual test is approximately US$37. Therefore, further prevalence studies across different parts of the country and such cost-effectiveness evaluations are essential.

In conclusion, HE caused by the three common SPTB mutations is associated with a significant risk of developing neonatal jaundice and contributes to a noticeable proportion of Thai neonates with jaundice requiring treatment. The magnitude of jaundice was greater in the affected neonates compared with neonates with G6PD deficiency. Coinheritance of G6PD deficiency likely worsens jaundice and may aggravate anaemia in neonates with mutated SPTB-causing HE. Screening for common SPTB mutations in neonates is likely beneficial, particularly in areas with high prevalence, to support proper jaundice monitoring and genetic counselling.
