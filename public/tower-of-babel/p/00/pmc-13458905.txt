The Dawn of the Era of Clopidogrel Monotherapy Following Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Hirotoshi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: aspirin, cardiovascular event, clopidogrel, dual antiplatelet therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention

Low-dose aspirin has long been an unquestioned therapy option as the default single antiplatelet therapy for patients with coronary artery disease. This was built on decades of clinical practice, low cost, easy availability, and historical evidence showing that low-dose aspirin could reduce recurrent thrombotic events after myocardial infarction, later reinforced by collaborative meta-analyses.1,2 For patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention with coronary stents, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors was established as a cornerstone for the prevention of stent-related thrombosis. Even in such cases, aspirin was the drug continued at baseline; the duration of DAPT was determined by adding a P2Y12 inhibitor for a certain period and then discontinuing it, and it was common practice to continue lifelong aspirin monotherapy after discontinuing DAPT. However, just as the optimal duration of DAPT following the implantation of drug-eluting stents has been a major clinical question, so too has the choice of which drug to use as monotherapy after discontinuing DAPT.

Ticlopidine, an older thienopyridine, was unsuitable for lifelong therapy because of its serious side effects, particularly hematologic adverse events. In contrast, clopidogrel was better tolerated, with substantially fewer serious adverse effects, making it a more plausible candidate for long-term, potentially lifelong, antiplatelet monotherapy. The CAPRIE (Clopidogrel versus Aspirin in Patients at Risk of Ischaemic Events) trial demonstrated the superiority of clopidogrel over aspirin in patients with established atherosclerotic disease, including not only coronary artery disease but also cerebrovascular and peripheral disease.3 Although clopidogrel showed a significant reduction in the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding compared with aspirin, its effect on cardiovascular events showed a significant interaction in the atherosclerotic disease subgroup; specifically, no significant reduction in cardiovascular events was observed with clopidogrel in the coronary artery disease subgroup. Moreover, this subgroup was not confined to patients treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and therefore did not represent a dedicated post-PCI study group.

On the other hand, the recent 2 randomized trials from South Korea were conducted in patients who underwent PCI without clinical events during DAPT, and randomization was conducted at the time of DAPT discontinuation to select a single antiplatelet agent.4,5 The results demonstrated a consistent risk reduction of cardiovascular events in clopidogrel compared with aspirin. Further studies and meta-analyses also consistently demonstrated the superiority of clopidogrel.6, 7, 8 However, to date, there has been no large-scale real-world evidence to support the results of these randomized trials.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Cho et al9 published a large-scale cohort study comparing clopidogrel monotherapy with aspirin monotherapy for secondary prevention after PCI. The analytical study group was over 130,000 patients from the nationwide database in South Korea, who initiated clopidogrel or aspirin monotherapy after DAPT between 2009 and 2019 and did not have an ischemic or bleeding event during DAPT. It is noteworthy that, at this stage, clopidogrel and aspirin monotherapy are used equally among patients in South Korea, and the study has sufficient statistical power to detect differences in event rates. After adjusting for confounders using inverse probability of treatment weighting, clopidogrel monotherapy was associated with a highly significant 24% risk reduction of major adverse cardiovascular events, a 10% reduction in major bleeding, and significant reductions in each component of major adverse cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke. There was no significant interaction between the subgroups, such as age, sex, or index presentation, and the effect of antiplatelet therapy. Study limitations include inherent limitations of an observational study, such as unobserved confounding factors between the 2 groups that could not be fully adjusted, the long 11-year enrollment period during which coronary stents and other drug therapies have evolved, and disease-code-based event adjudication rather than detailed event assessments that consider clinical course or examinations. The DAPT duration in the analytical study group was limited to 3 months to 2 years, and patients with DAPT durations of 3 months or less, as in recent short DAPT trials, were excluded. Nevertheless, unlike randomized clinical trials, this study provides evidence from a study group that reflects real-world clinical practice, and the consistency of its efficacy with respect to ischemic events, in particular, complements the results of the aforementioned clinical trials.

Recent reports on the superiority of clopidogrel monotherapy over aspirin monotherapy in reducing cardiovascular events among patients with coronary artery disease have primarily come from East Asia.4, 5, 6, 7,9 Another meta-analysis suggests that these findings may differ by ethnicity, with a significant interaction between reports from East Asia and those from non–East Asia.10 Nevertheless, based on this evidence, clopidogrel is likely to become the treatment of choice for monotherapy in secondary prevention of coronary artery disease in East Asia.

However, there are still several challenges to the widespread adoption of lifelong clopidogrel monotherapy as a secondary prevention strategy. 1) The interindividual variability in clopidogrel efficacy due to CYP2C19 polymorphisms. No studies with sufficient statistical power have yet been conducted to determine whether clopidogrel efficacy differs based on the genotype. 2) The issue of cost-effectiveness. Compared with inexpensive aspirin, lifelong treatment with clopidogrel results in drug costs that are several times higher, and a health economic analysis is needed to determine whether this leads to a commensurate reduction in cardiovascular events. 3) The management of patients who require a surgical procedure, especially in emergent cases. Unlike aspirin, emergency surgery performed when the effects of P2Y12 inhibitors are still present is thought to increase the risk of bleeding. It may complicate perioperative management of hemostasis and antithrombotic agents. 4) The interactions with proton pump inhibitors, which interfere with CYP2C19. Particularly in Japan, proton pump inhibitors are widely prescribed even with antiplatelet monotherapy because of concerns about the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding. 5) The type or dose of P2Y12 inhibitors. Are potent P2Y12 inhibitors such as ticagrelor or prasugrel more effective without increased risk of bleeding, or does the reduced dose of P2Y12 inhibitors have a similar effect on cardiovascular events with a protective effect for bleeding events? These issues will likely be explored in future research.
