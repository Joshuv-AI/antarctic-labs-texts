Clinical Profile and Survival Outcomes of Children with Acute Kidney Injury Requiring Renal Replacement Therapy: A Retrospective Study from a Tertiary Pediatric Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Swarnim Swarnim, Arnab Ghorui, Sneh Kumar, Arun Prasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsAcute kidney injury (AKI) in children is a serious and potentially life-threatening condition, particularly among critically ill patients. Despite advances in renal replacement therapy (RRT), mortality remains high. This study aimed to evaluate the etiology and indications for RRT in pediatric AKI, identify predictors of mortality, and compare survival outcomes between hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD).Patients and methodsThis single-center, retrospective observational study included children aged 1 month to 18 years who received RRT for AKI at a tertiary care hospital in Eastern India between January 2023 and December 2024. Acute kidney injury was defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criteria. Demographic, clinical (including illness severity scores), and laboratory data were extracted from medical records. Predictors of mortality were evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. Kaplan–Meier estimation, the log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis were used to compare survival between HD and PD.ResultsOut of 40 patients, 28 (70%) received HD and 12 (30%) underwent PD. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 27.5% (11/40). The most common cause of AKI necessitating RRT was sepsis (39.5%), which included urosepsis and systemic sepsis. Refractory fluid overload (>10%) was the primary indication for RRT initiation in 67.5% of patients. Non-survivors had a significantly higher prevalence of sepsis, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), underlying comorbidities, vasopressor use, and greater mean percentage of fluid overload (mean: 16.8% in non-survivors vs 8.3% in survivors; p = 0.003). In univariate analysis, dialysis modality, comorbidities, fluid overload, sepsis, MODS, and vasopressor use were significantly associated with mortality. However, none of these were independently predictive in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Median time to in-hospital death or discharge was significantly longer in the HD group (88 vs 22 days, p = 0.0012). Cox regression showed an increased association with in-hospital mortality risk in the PD group [hazard ratio (HR) = 4.41, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.19–16.33, p = 0.026].ConclusionHigher mortality was observed in patients with sepsis, MODS, and significant fluid overload. The higher mortality in the PD group is likely attributable to selection bias, as PD was preferentially used in sicker, hemodynamically unstable patients.How to cite this articleSwarnim S, Ghorui A, Kumar S, Prasad A. Highlights

This study of 40 children with acute kidney injury (AKI) requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT) found sepsis as the leading cause. Mortality was 27.5%, higher with fluid overload, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and vasopressor use. Survival was significantly better with hemodialysis (HD) than the peritoneal dialysis (PD). Illness severity (sickness) and comorbidities primarily influenced poor outcomes; the better survival with HD is likely due to selection bias.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury is a critical and potentially life-threatening condition that presents significant challenges in pediatric healthcare, particularly in critically ill children. It is characterized by a rapid decline in renal function, which can result from a variety of factors, including infections, dehydration, nephrotoxic medications, and underlying systemic diseases.1 The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria for diagnosing AKI are primarily based on changes in serum creatinine (SCr) levels, urine output, or both, providing a standardized framework for diagnosing this condition in children.2 The incidence of AKI in pediatric populations varies significantly, reported between 10 and 82%, depending on the study cohort, underlying illness, and the criteria used for diagnosis.3 Despite advances in medical technology and the introduction of various RRT modalities, including intermittent HD, PD, and continuous RRT (CRRT), the mortality rates associated with severe AKI in children remain alarmingly high, often ranging from 36 to 50%.4–6 A holistic approach—combining optimized RRT, early detection, etiology-specific management, and comorbidity mitigation—is essential to improving survival and recovery rates in AKI. The need for RRT in children with AKI is typically driven by complications that do not respond to conservative management, such as fluid overload, electrolyte disturbances, and uremia. These complications can exacerbate renal failure and lead to life-threatening conditions if not promptly addressed.7 Fluid overload, in particular, has been identified as a key risk factor for poor outcomes, with studies showing that patients with fluid overload exceeding 10% of their body weight are at a significantly higher risk of mortality.8 Other factors contributing to adverse outcomes in pediatric AKI requiring RRT include MODS, sepsis, and the use of nephrotoxic drugs, which can further compromise renal function and increase the complexity of treatment.9

Despite the availability of different RRT modalities, there is a lack of comprehensive studies that examine the comparative effectiveness of these treatments in the pediatric population. Current literature suggests that the choice of RRT modality, such as intermittent HD, PD, or CRRT, may impact clinical outcomes, including survival and kidney function recovery, but the evidence remains inconclusive.10 The effectiveness of RRT in improving these outcomes is not fully understood, and it is essential to identify key factors that may predict survival and recovery in this vulnerable population.

This retrospective observational study aims to fill this gap in the literature by investigating the clinical outcomes of pediatric patients who received RRT for AKI. By analyzing both clinical and laboratory data, this study seeks to identify predictors of survival and evaluate the effectiveness of RRT modalities in this cohort.

The primary objective was to evaluate the etiology of AKI in pediatric patients undergoing RRT and to identify the underlying indications for initiating RRT in this population. Secondary objectives included identifying predictors of mortality and comparing survival outcomes between patients treated with HD and those receiving PD.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single-center, retrospective observational study conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Eastern India. The pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) has a 12-bed capacity with an annual admission of approximately 450 cases, commonly including cardiac, respiratory, renal, and neurological systems. The available RRT modalities at the institute were PD and HD (for appropriate age), as CRRT could not be used because of logistical limitations.

Patient Population and Data Collection

The study included all eligible children aged 1 month to 18 years who had AKI stage III (defined by KDIGO 2012 criteria) and received RRT between January 2023 and December 2024. All patients included in the final analysis were admitted to the PICU for dialysis. Patients with known congenital kidney abnormalities or pre-existing chronic kidney disease were excluded.

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were obtained through a review of medical records. Demographic variables include: Age, gender, and weight. Clinical parameters include diagnosis, illness severity scores [pediatric logistic organ dysfunction-2 (PELOD-2)] at admission, vasoactive inotrope score (VIS) at the initiation of RRT, fluid balance until RRT initiation, RRT modality, indication for RRT, use of vasopressors (recorded as a binary variable), and in-hospital mortality

All the outcomes were assessed during the hospitalization period. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the modified Schwartz formula.11

RRT Modality Selection and Bias

The decision regarding RRT initiation and modality was based on a combination of clinical judgment, patient condition, resource availability, and institutional protocols, as determined by the consultant nephrologist. The mode of RRT used is either PD or HD; CRRT could not be used due to logistical limitations. The choice between PD and HD was not randomized but based on clinical judgment and patient status. In our cohort, PD was often selected for patients who were hemodynamically unstable, had lower Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, or were younger/smaller, introducing confounding by indication.

Sample Size

As this was a retrospective observational study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. All eligible pediatric patients (n = 40) who met the inclusion criteria and underwent RRT for AKI between January 2023 and December 2024 were included to maximize data representativeness. However, we acknowledge that the small sample size limits statistical power and the precision of effect estimates, particularly in multivariate analyses.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (Version 2.3).

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test.

Survival analysis was performed to compare outcomes between the HD and PD groups. The analysis began at the date of RRT initiation, with the primary event defined as in-hospital death. Patients were censored at the time of hospital discharge, representing the composite outcome of time to either in-hospital death or discharge. Kaplan–Meier estimation was used to generate survival curves, and differences between the HD and PD groups were assessed using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied to estimate the hazard ratio (HR), and the proportional hazards assumption was verified before model fitting.

Results

Among 44 patients who received RRT for AKI during the study period, 4 were excluded because their records were incomplete. The final analysis, therefore, included data from 40 patients. Of these 40 patients, 28 (70%) underwent HD and 12 (30%) were treated with PD. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 27.5% (n = 11).

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified several factors associated with mortality, including mode of RRT, presence of comorbidities, percentage of fluid overload (%FO) at RRT initiation, sepsis, MODS, use of nephrotoxic drugs, and vasopressor requirement (Supplementary Table S1). However, after adjusting for potential confounders, none of these variables were found to be independent predictors of mortality in the multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Survival Analysis (HD vs PD)

A survival analysis was conducted to compare outcomes between patients receiving HD and those undergoing PD. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve visually represents the estimated probability of in-hospital survival over time, distinguishing between patients who received HD and those who received PD (Fig. 2). The plot clearly illustrates a marked divergence in survival trajectories between the two modalities. The HD curve remains consistently higher than the PD curve throughout the follow-up period, indicating a significantly better survival probability for the HD group (log rank test, p = 0.0012).

Kaplan–Meier survival curve depicting time to in-hospital death or discharge for the hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD) groups

The estimated median time to in-hospital death or discharge was 88 days for patients on HD, substantially longer than the 22 days observed for the PD group. The survival lines incorporate censored data, typically marked by vertical ticks or crosses, which signify patients who were discharged alive and thus removed from the population at risk of the event (in-hospital death). The Cox proportional hazards model showed that patients on PD had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to those on HD, with an HR of 4.41 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.19–16.33, p = 0.026]. This finding should be interpreted as an association rather than implying causation, given the high likelihood of selection bias.

Discussion

This study highlights the clinical characteristics, etiologies, and outcomes of pediatric patients undergoing RRT in a tertiary care center, with findings that both align with and diverge from several recent studies across different populations and settings.

In terms of etiology, sepsis, whether as urosepsis in patients with underlying structural kidney disease or as systemic sepsis, was the most common cause of AKI in this study, accounting for nearly half (39.5%) of all cases. These findings are consistent with those of Voraruth et al., who identified sepsis (56.7%) as the predominant cause of AKI, and Çeleğen and Çeleğen, who also reported sepsis (40.5%) as a frequent diagnosis.22,23

The overall mortality rate in this study cohort was 27.5%, which is considerably lower than that reported in larger pediatric RRT studies, such as those by Voraruth et al. (2022, Thailand), Çeleğen and Çeleğen (2023, Turkey), and Alrashdi et al. (2025, Saudi Arabia), who reported mortality rates of 55.4, 27, and 70%, respectively.22–24

In this study, refractory volume overload (>10%)—either alone or alongside other indications—was the most frequent indication for initiating RRT, accounting for 67.5% of cases. Additional indications included uremic encephalopathy (45%), dyselectrolytemia refractory to medical management (25%), anuria (15%), and severe refractory metabolic acidosis (10%). These findings are consistent with those reported by Boschee et al., in which fluid overload was also the common indication for RRT initiation in 77% of cases, followed by uremia (22%), hyperkalemia (26%), and metabolic acidosis (22%).16 A comprehensive review by Selewski and Goldstein highlighted that the percentage of fluid overload (%FO) has a direct impact on outcomes in children with AKI, emphasizing FO as a modifiable risk factor and a target for intervention.8 Similar to these findings, this study also underscores the significant association between FO and patient outcomes. In this study cohort, non-survivors had significantly higher mean FO at RRT initiation (16.8 vs 8.3%, p = 0.003). This is consistent with the findings of Voraruth et al., who reported a mean FO of 17.3% in non-survivors compared to 11.9% in survivors.22 Selewski and Goldstein emphasized that FO of 10–20% or greater serves as a threshold linked to adverse outcomes in pediatric patients on CRRT.8 Similarly, Sutherland et al. found that children with ≥20% FO at CRRT initiation had a significantly higher mortality rate (65.6%) compared to those with 10–20% (43.1%) or <10% (29.4%) FO, with the risk of mortality increasing with each additional percent of fluid overload.17 Notably, although FO was a significant factor in univariate analysis, neither this study nor the others identified it as an independent predictor of mortality in multivariate regression. This may be due to multicollinearity with other severe clinical conditions, such as MODS or vasopressor use.

The pathogenesis of sepsis associated AKI in recent studies highlights microcirculatory dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and a dysregulated inflammatory response with cytokine and nitric oxide imbalance, leading to renal tubular injury even in the presence of stable global renal blood flow.25 Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome arises from systemic inflammation, mitochondrial failure, endothelial injury, and coagulation abnormalities, exacerbating renal dysfunction as AKI, both triggers and results from organ failures.26 Fluid overload significantly worsens outcomes by causing tissue and organ edema, venous congestion, impaired oxygen delivery, and increased intra-abdominal pressure, reducing renal perfusion and increasing mortality risk. Current literature emphasizes tight hemodynamic monitoring, early fluid de-resuscitation, and organ support as critical to improving survival in pediatric sepsis-associated AKI complicated by MODS and fluid overload.27

The survival analysis showed a significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality associated with the PD group (HR = 4.41, p = 0.026), with a median survival of 22 days for PD vs 88 days for HD. This result must be interpreted as an association, not a causal link. Additionally, as noted in the methods, PD was frequently selected for patients who were hemodynamically unstable, younger, or had lower GCS scores. This strongly suggests selection bias (confounding by indication), where the modality was chosen based on the patient's underlying severity of illness. Patients requiring PD were likely sicker and therefore had poorer outcomes, regardless of the RRT modality used. This inherent bias is a major limitation of this retrospective study in drawing conclusions about the efficacy of different RRT modalities. This finding is also supported by the results of Çeleğen and Çeleğen, who observed higher mortality in patients undergoing CRRT and reported that these patients had lower blood pressure and elevated PRISM III scores, reflecting greater baseline illness severity.23

Univariate logistic regression revealed several variables associated with mortality, including RRT modality, comorbidities, fluid overload, sepsis, MODS, and vasopressor use; however, none of these remained independent predictors in the multivariate analysis. This differs from larger studies, which identified sepsis, vasopressor requirement, and severity scores as independent predictors, possibly due to their larger sample sizes and the inclusion of CRRT for the sickest patients. Voraruth et al. found that sepsis and nephrotoxic drug use were significant independent predictors of mortality.22 Similarly, Çeleğen and Çeleğen identified vasopressor requirement and higher PRISM III scores as strong independent predictors of mortality.23 Our findings nonetheless reinforce the conclusion that mortality is primarily driven by the severity of the underlying illness and comorbidities (e.g., sepsis, MODS, need for vasopressors).

Limitations

A major limitation of this study was its retrospective design, limited sample size, and the unavailability of CRRT. Additional limitations include the use of Sepsis-3 criteria, which are not formally validated for children, and the survival analysis only accounts for in-hospital survival (as patients were censored at discharge), thus preventing conclusions about long-term or overall survival. These factors may reduce the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Sepsis was the most common cause of AKI requiring RRT. This study also corroborates the established understanding that mortality in pediatric AKI requiring RRT is primarily driven by the severity of the underlying illness, particularly the presence of sepsis, MODS, and fluid overload.

The observed higher mortality associated with PD, although statistically significant, is a likely reflection of selection bias (PD being chosen for sicker, hemodynamically unstable patients) rather than an inherent inferiority of the modality. These findings reinforce the importance of early recognition of high-risk features and careful fluid and hemodynamic management to improve survival in this vulnerable population.
