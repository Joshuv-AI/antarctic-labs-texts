Validation of drug‐nondrug choice procedure to model maladaptive behavioural allocation to opioid use in rats
Authors: Setareh Azizzadeh, Milad Rahimpour, Kamran Rakhshan, Bahador Makkiabadi, Esmail Riahi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreased allocation of behaviour to substance abuse at the expense of personal and social rewards is a hallmark of addiction that is reflected in several of DSM‐5 criteria for diagnosis of substance use disorder. Previous studies focused on refining the self‐administration (SA) model to better emulate an addictive state in laboratory animals. Here, we employed concurrent SA of sucrose pellets and morphine as two competing natural and drug rewards, respectively, to validate the feasibility of capturing pathological behavioural allocation in rats. A custom‐made three‐lever operant chamber was used. With one active and one inactive lever presented, rats were trained to self‐administer morphine (0.5 mg/kg/infusion; 2 h/day) under a fixed‐ratio 1 (FR‐1) schedule until a stable response was achieved. Next, they were trained to self‐administer morphine in the presence of a third lever dispensing sucrose pellets (20 mg) under FR‐1. Concurrent morphine‐sucrose SA sessions (2 h/day) were continued until stable morphine taking behaviour was re‐established. In another experiment, rats first established stable sucrose pellet SA (2 h/day, FR‐1) and then were trained to take morphine (0.5 mg/kg/infusion; 2 h/day). Subsequently, all rats underwent extinction training, in which morphine was replaced with saline while sucrose pellets were still available upon lever pressing, followed by cue‐induced reinstatement of morphine seeking behaviour. Results showed that rats retained morphine SA when sucrose pellets were also available, but they showed binge‐like sucrose intake when morphine was removed during the extinction sessions. However, morphine SA did not develop in rats that had previously established sucrose pellet SA. In conclusion, morphine SA developed even in the presence of a potent competing nondrug reward in rats. Adding an effort‐based contingent delivery of a natural reward to the standard SA model, this protocol may provide an improved model of drug addiction in laboratory animals.Keywords: animal models, morphine dependence, rats, self‐administration, sucrose

INTRODUCTION

Drug addiction is a multifaceted and chronic behavioural disorder characterized by physical and psychological dependence and relapse to drug use. Psychological dependence, which is primarily responsible for the recurrence of drug use, manifests itself in symptoms such as euphoria, drug cravings, compulsive drug seeking and loss of control over drug use. 1 For several decades, drug self‐administration (SA) has widely been used to investigate behaviour, neurobiology and genetic factors of addiction. 2 It involves controlled administration of a drug based on animals' behavioural response and closely mimics human addiction compared to other models. 3 The SA model is considered highly reliable as it can be applied to almost all substances of abuse, including food and sugar. 4 However, there have been some concerns regarding the face, construct and predictive validity of animal models of addiction. Some authors suggested that SA and conditioned place preference should be recognized at best as models of drug reward and nonaddictive substance use, not as models of addiction. 5 , 6 , 7 However, these models may capture distinct elements underlying critical features of the disorder. 8

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) accredits 11 criteria for clinical diagnosis of substance use disorder that fall under four major groups including uncontrolled drug‐taking, physical dependence, social problems and risky use. 9 There have been enormous attempts to reverse‐translation of clinical aspects of addiction into progressively well‐defined addiction‐like behaviour in animals. For example, signs of impaired control are indicated by: 1) animals' inability to withhold drug seeking (persistent drug seeking when the drug is not available and signalled as so); 2) elevated motivation for the drug (progressive ratio schedule or behavioural economics) and 3) continued drug use despite negative consequences (punishment schedule). 10 , 11 Loss of control over drug use is measured by the escalation of drug SA using an extended‐ or intermittent‐access schedule. 12

One important aspect of addiction is the increased allocation of behaviour to substance use rather than behaviours maintained by nondrug alternatives such as social interaction, food and sex. 13 Indeed, addiction has been theorized as a behavioural allocation disorder. 14 This aspect has been reflected in several DSM‐5 criteria such as giving up desirable social, occupational and recreational activities because of drug use; spending considerable time obtaining the drug; and frequent failure to carry out major duties at work, school or home because of drug use. 9 To model this feature of addiction in preclinical studies, some pioneering works subjected laboratory animals to a natural reward‐drug reward conflict in a SA paradigm where an alternative nondrug reward was provided beside the addictive drug (reviewed in 15 , 16 ). Such conflict‐based concurrent reinforcement schedules or choice procedures have been used to examine the reinforcement value of a drug by measuring the relative strengths of reinforcers or the addictive liability of a drug. 17 , 18 , 19 However, to date the validity of these models has not been examined for morphine, an intensely rewarding and highly addictive opioid drug. In addition, studies that evaluated the addictive strength of heroin employed a discrete choice protocol where the animals were not allowed to freely receive rewards during the whole test session. 19 , 20 In other words, rats could show their reward preference but not their appetite for taking a high amount of reward. Therefore, in this study, we investigated a continuous concurrent reinforcement schedule in which rats were given a free choice between morphine and sucrose pellets and showed that this protocol may capture maladaptive behavioural allocation in addiction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

In this study, 19 male Wistar rats weighing 270–350 g were used. All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions as follows: 20–22°C room temperature, 12 h reversed light/dark cycle (lights off at 7:00 a.m. and on at 7:00 p.m.) and 30–50% humidity. All surgical and behavioural experiments were performed during the dark phase. The rats had initially free access to standard rodent chow and water but later had restricted access to food (80% of normal daily intake) over the entire course of SA experiments. For food restriction, the daily consumption of rats was measured at the outset of the study and 40% of the daily need was given at the beginning of the light phase and another 40% was given after the behavioural experiments in the dark phase. Body weight remained stable in the morphine and morphine‐sucrose SA phases and gradually increased in the extinction phase (Figure S1). All protocols were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.TUMS.AEC.1401.110).

Drugs

Morphine sulfate (Temad, Iran) was dissolved in normal saline (7.5 mg in 5 ml) and used freshly for drug reinforcement in SA experiments. Sodium pentobarbital (Sigma‐Aldrich) was dissolved in saline (9 mg/ml) and infused at 10 μl/s to confirm catheter patency once per week. Heparin sodium (5,000 IU, Darou Pakhsh, Iran) was used as an anticoagulant for perfusing intravenous catheters. Ketamine 10% and Xylazine 2% (both purchased from Alfasan, the Netherlands), Ketoprofen 10% (Razak Laboratories, Iran) and cefazolin (Exir Pharmaceutical, Iran) were used as the anaesthetics, analgesic and antibiotic, respectively.

Self‐administration device

We employed a custom‐made SA chamber (Cravy‐Check, Iran) measuring 30 × 30 × 35 cm, equipped with three metal levers (5 × 3 cm, 4 cm apart) and LED cue lights with different colours located 4 cm above each lever. The chamber was constructed using 10 mm thick black plexiglass with a transparent door, illuminated by a house light and ventilated by a noise‐less fan. The ceiling contained a 1‐cm‐wide slit leading to a central hole with a diameter of 5 cm to allow for passage of the injection tube. The floor was a removable grid made of stainless‐steel rods (1 cm thick, 1 cm apart). A tone generator was installed to emit sound cues when necessary. An infusion pump and a pellet dispenser were included for intravenous morphine infusion and oral administration of sucrose pellets, respectively. Sucrose pellets were delivered in a food cup located under the middle lever. A fluid swivel situated on a mechanical arm provided unrestricted rotation of the injection tube when the animal turned around.

Jugular vein catheterization

Anaesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). A longitudinal incision 2 cm in length was made from the point between the two scapulae downward. The skin around the incision was separated from the underlying tissues to create a space for implanting the catheter base. A second diagonal incision 1.5–2 cm in length was made on the skin above the right jugular vein, just above the clavicle. The jugular vein was exposed and freed from the surrounding tissue and sutured at the uppermost possible point using 3–0 silk to halt blood flow from the brain. Then, the vein was partially cut by means of a 23‐gauge needle and a silicone catheter 7–9 cm in length was gently inserted into the vein and secured in place with two sutures. Next, the free end of the catheter was subcutaneously conducted to the posterior incision where it was connected to the catheter base. After the surgery, the rats were administered ketoprofen (10 mg/kg, IP) for pain relief and cefazolin (300 mg/kg, IM) for infection prophylaxis and were housed individually in homecage over the recovery (7 days) and the SA period. During the recovery period, the intravenous catheter was flushed with heparinized saline (0.1 ml, 100 U/ml) every day.

Self‐administration protocol

Morphine SA

After the recovery from surgery, the rats underwent daily sessions of morphine (n = 5) or saline (n = 9) SA under a fixed‐ratio 1 (FR‐1) schedule. Briefly, the infusion tether was connected to the catheter port on the back mount and the rats were placed in the SA chamber for 2 h with the left and middle levers extended. The house light was illuminated at the beginning of each session. Pressing the left lever immediately resulted in the following effects at the same time: morphine infusion (0.5 mg/kg/infusion, in a volume of 100 μl), 21 turn‐off of the house light, illumination of the cue light above the lever and emitting a pure tone (2,500 Hz, 85 dB) all for 5 s followed by a 5 s time‐out period, during which the house light remained off and any responses on the left lever was not reinforced but recorded. After the timeout, the house light was illuminated again to signal the availability of morphine upon pressing the lever. Responding on the middle lever had no programmed effects. Morphine SA sessions were continued on a daily basis until a stable responding rate on the active lever at least twice as that on the inactive lever was achieved. Stable responding was defined as when a coefficient of variation (CV) less than 0.2 for lever pressing was present over three consecutive sessions. In the saline group, animals received an equal volume of saline instead of morphine over 5 s upon pressing the left lever. The patency of the catheters was tested by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital at the end of each week in all phases of the SA experiments.

Morphine and sucrose SA

After a stable morphine SA was established on the FR‐1 schedule, morphine SA training was continued with the same protocol while the rats were allowed to freely lever press for taking sucrose pellets (unflavored sugar spheres, 20 mg, Sehramiz Chocolate, Iran). In this phase, all levers were extended during the SA sessions (2 h/day). Responses on the left and middle levers had the same effects as in the previous phase (left: morphine infusion; middle: inactive). Pressing the right lever delivered a sucrose pellet under the FR‐1 schedule without the activation of conditioned cues. The aim of this phase was to establish a relatively stronger dependence on morphine in the animals, allowing them to receive morphine even in the presence of a powerful natural reward. The criterion for successful morphine SA (and termination of training sessions) was the same as used for the previous phase. In the saline group, animals received saline upon pressing the left lever and sucrose pellets upon pressing the right lever. No cutoff level was programmed for sucrose pellet intake.

In a different set of experiments, a separate group of rats (n = 5) underwent the same protocols described so far unless they were subjected to an additional phase of sucrose SA performed on a daily basis (2 h/day) under a FR1 schedule before the morphine SA phase.

Extinction of morphine SA

In this phase, the rats had free access to all three levers for 2 h/day. All experimental conditions were the same as in the previous phase unless saline was substituted for morphine and the audiovisual cues were not activated. Therefore, the left lever was assigned to saline injection, the middle lever remained inactive and the right lever was assigned to sucrose pellet delivery under the FR‐1 schedule. The criterion for the extinction of morphine SA was obtaining a response rate on the left lever < 20% of the average response on the same lever over the last three sessions of the previous phase.

Reinstatement of morphine SA

One day after the extinction, rats were tested for cue‐induced reinstatement of morphine seeking behaviour for 2 h. All experimental conditions were the same as described for the previous phase unless the audiovisual cues were activated upon pressing the left lever.

Statistical analysis

Data is expressed as mean ± SEM. Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to determine the normal distribution of the data. Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney test was used for comparing two means and ANOVA was used for comparing three or more means. A two‐sided p‐value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

RESULTS

Morphine reinforced lever pressing behaviour

In the drug‐taking phase, rats were given access to the left and middle levers assigned, respectively, to the intravenous infusion (active) and no programmed effect (inactive). They were allowed to self‐administer morphine or saline on a daily basis (2 h/day) until each of them reached stable responding (CV < 0.2) on the active lever for three consecutive days. The morphine‐SA rats showed an increasing trend in active lever response and in the number of infusions earned throughout the phase, indicating the successful development of morphine reinforcement. Conversely, the number of responses on the inactive lever remained around zero during the phase (Figure 1A). There was a significant difference in the mean lever responding during the final three sessions between the active and inactive levers (Mann–Whitney: p < 0.01, Figure 1B).

Mean lever response in the last three sessions of individual rats was illustrated in B and D for morphine‐SA and saline‐SA groups, respectively. Mann–Whitney test: **p < 0.01, ns = nonsignificant. N = 5 and 9 in morphine and saline groups, respectively. Mor, morphine; Sal, saline.

On the other hand, the saline‐SA rats exhibited no visible changes in the number of responses on the active and inactive levers or in the number of saline infusions earned over the daily sessions (Figure 1C). Likewise, the rats showed no significant difference in their mean response on the active and inactive levers during the last three sessions (Mann–Whitney: p = 0.33, Figure 1D). Additionally, the morphine‐SA rats had significantly greater responses on the active lever for taking morphine infusions during the last three sessions than the saline‐SA rats did for saline infusions (Mann–Whitney: p < 0.05, Not shown).

Morphine reinforcement persisted in the presence of sucrose

The rats were then trained to continue morphine SA and regain a stable morphine intake after introducing the right lever delivering sucrose pellets so that the rats were able to choose between morphine and sucrose rewards. It is worth mentioning that sucrose pellet‐only training was not performed prior to the choice stage because learning the reward contingency associated with the right lever was controlled with the saline‐SA group. In the morphine‐SA group, the frequency of pressing the left lever to receive intravenous morphine showed a consistent increase over the sessions (Figure 2A). However, except in one rat, the frequency of pressing the right lever to receive sucrose pellets remained consistently lower than that of the morphine lever, suggesting a greater preference for morphine compared to the sucrose pellets. Figure 2B displays the average number of lever presses during the final three days of this phase. An ANOVA test [F(2, 11) = 8.9, p = 0.005) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test showed that the mean response on the morphine lever was significantly higher than the responses on the inactive (p < 0.01) and sucrose pellet (p < 0.05) levers. Additionally, there was no significant difference between the mean responses on sucrose and inactive levers (p = 0.97).

Response on the right lever of one outlier rat, found by ROUT (Q = 1%) method and excluded from B, was shown separately. Mean lever response in the last three sessions of individual rats was illustrated in B for morphine‐SA and in D for saline‐SA groups. In the saline‐SA group, an enormous increase was observed in the sucrose lever press, but not in the saline lever press, indicating an immensely stronger preference for contingent sucrose intake over operant saline infusion (Figure 2C). The ANOVA [F(2, 9) = 34.0, p < 0.001] and Tukey's tests revealed that the mean lever press for sucrose pellet during the final three days was significantly higher than both the saline and inactive lever press (p < 0.001), while the latter two did not differ significantly from each other (p = 0.99, Figure 2D).

Morphine withdrawal in the extinction phase shifted the behaviour towards sucrose pellet intake

Rats were then proceeded to the extinction phase, in which they had access to all levers during the sessions (2 h/day). In both groups, pressing the left lever resulted in saline infusion without conditioned cues, while pressing the right lever resulted in sucrose pellet delivery. As shown in Figure 3A, response on the left lever remained erratic over the sessions revolving around the inactive lever response rate, whereas response on the right lever was released from morphine suppression and stayed consistently at an elevated level. The sessions were continued on a daily basis until individual rats were successfully withdrawn from morphine, reaching a response rate on the left lever lower than 20% of its final average on the previous drug‐sucrose‐taking phase over three consecutive sessions. ANOVA [F(2, 12) = 116.9, p < 0.001] and Tukey tests showed that the mean response rate on the left lever was not significantly different from the mean response on the inactive lever (p = 0.99, Figure 3B). However, the mean response on the right lever was significantly more than the response on both the left and inactive levers (p < 0.001, Figure 3B). Thus, in the absence of morphine and conditioned cues, the animals gradually ceased responding on the left lever, but maintained a high response rate on the right sucrose pellet‐delivering lever.

Mean lever response in the last three sessions of individual rats was illustrated in B for morphine‐SA group and in D for saline‐SA group. Response rate on the morphine lever was significantly lower than that on sucrose lever, but was the same as that on the inactive lever, indicating the extinction of morphine seeking behaviour. In the saline‐SA group, lever responding remained consistently higher for sucrose pellets than for saline during the phase (Figure 3C). The average lever response for sucrose pellets during the last three sessions was significantly higher than that for saline [ANOVA: F(2, 9) = 48.50, p < 0.001; Tukey: p < 0.001], but there was no significant difference between the average responding on the saline and inactive levers (Tukey: p = 0.94, Figure 3D).

Re‐exposure to conditioned cues reinstated morphine seeking behaviour in the presence of high response rate for sucrose pellets

In the final phase of the experiments, morphine‐SA rats underwent cue‐induce reinstatement of morphine seeking one day after near‐complete cessation of lever pressing for morphine in each rat. With all three levers available, pressing the left lever resulted in the presentation of conditioned audiovisual cues without morphine injection, pressing the right lever resulted in sucrose pellet delivery and pressing the middle lever had no effect. As depicted in Figure 4, there was a significant increase in the mean response on the left lever compared to the last three sessions of the extinction (Paired t‐test: p < 0.001), whereas responses on the right and middle levers did not exhibit any significant difference (Paired t‐test: p = 0.21).

Reinstatement phase. Lever response for IV infusions and sucrose pellets in morphine‐SA rats in the reinstatement phase as compared to the last three sessions of the extinction phase. Pressing the left lever resulted in the presentation of conditioned cues without morphine administration, whereas pressing the right lever led to the delivery of sucrose pellets. Response rate on the morphine lever was significantly higher in the reinstatement phase than in the extinction phase, indicating the renewal of morphine seeking behaviour. Multiple paired t‐tests: ***p < 0.001, ns = nonsignificant. N = 5. Mor, morphine; SP, sucrose pellet.

Morphine reinforcement was not developed in rats with prior sucrose intake

To test whether morphine SA could be developed if animals had established sucrose SA, an additional experiment was conducted in a different cohort of rats (n = 5). The rats were initially trained to press the right lever to obtain sucrose pellets (2 h/day, FR1), and after reaching a stable response (CV < 0.2), in the second stage, they were trained to press the left lever to receive intravenous morphine (2 h/day, FR1) while the right lever was retracted. Here, morphine SA and sucrose SA trainings were done in different and subsequent stages because there was no saline group to control for it. In the choice stage, they were allowed to self‐infuse morphine while a sucrose lever was also available (2 h/day, FR1; Figure 5A). These rats exhibited an intense sucrose intake in the first stage (Mann–Whitney: p < 0.01, Figure 5B), low response rate on the morphine lever (not more than the inactive lever) in the second stage (Mann–Whitney: p = 0.69, Figure 5C) and a vigorous preference for sucrose pellets over the morphine in the third stage [ANOVA: F(2, 6) = 20.8, p = 0.002, Figure 5D]. These results suggest that morphine reinforcement and preference only occurred in rats originally trained for morphine SA but not in rats that initially established sucrose SA.

The rats failed to show significant behavioural reinforcement on morphine lever, whereas they showed significantly more response on sucrose lever. DISCUSSION

In the present study, we incorporated the choice of taking a natural reward (sucrose pellet) beside the drug reward (morphine infusions) into the standard SA paradigm to improve the preclinical replication of drug addiction by removing the motivational shift that occurs with opioid consumption. First, we have shown that in the presence of a choice between saline and sucrose pellets, rats consistently built a strong preference for sucrose pellets and actively engaged in appetitive (lever press) and consummatory (eating) behaviours. Then, we tested whether this sucrose‐taking taking behaviour interacts with the positive reinforcement made by morphine. The main finding of our study was that in a setting where rats were provided with an unrestricted choice between intravenous morphine and palatable sucrose pellets upon pressing the previously familiarized levers, their response depended on the prior history of reward taking. In rats initially trained to take morphine in a closed reward availability context (where no alternative was available), later presentation of an additional option for taking sucrose pellets did not shift their reward preference. In other words, they continued to prioritize morphine over sucrose. Nevertheless, in rats initially trained to take sucrose pellets and were later provided with the possibility to obtain morphine (either alone or along with sucrose), morphine SA did not even emerge, yet a binge‐like sucrose intake appeared.

Several studies integrated drug and natural rewards in SA models, albeit with different fundamental premises and methodologies. For example, one study attempted to maintain a consistent nicotine SA by an adjunctive food delivery schedule and body weight reduction, thereby suggesting a paradigm for testing drug and environmental interactions. 22 Other studies pursued measuring the addiction liability of drugs versus nondrug alternatives under a discrete‐trials choice procedure. In this regard, one study demonstrated that a large majority of rats that were allowed to choose mutually exclusively between oral saccharin solution and intravenous cocaine injection preferred the sweet taste, leading to the conclusion that intense sweetness can outdo cocaine reward even in drug‐sensitized and addicted animals. 23 Other studies from the same laboratory showed that the value of cocaine is much lower than that of saccharin. It has been estimated that in the majority of rats, the value of an intravenous cocaine injection (0.25 mg) was on average equal to the value of a 20‐s access to 0.2% saccharin solution (~0.02 ml). Most rats readily abstained from cocaine use in favour of the nondrug alternative irrespective of the dose available and the severity of past cocaine use. 18 This cocaine abstinence seems not to be accounted by anxiogenic (versus rewarding) properties of the drug, since diazepam further decreased responding to cocaine without affecting responding for saccharin. 24 Collectively, these studies showed that the majority of rats were resilient to addiction to cocaine and only a minor proportion (~15%) continued to prefer cocaine over saccharin. However, under similar choice conditions, it turned out that the proportion of drug‐preferring rats was approximately three times higher with heroin (51%) than with cocaine (15%), which may explain why more drug users become addicted to heroin than to cocaine. 19

It is worth noting that we did not use a discrete‐trials choice procedure in which the subject chooses the reward in a mutually exclusive or either/or manner. This procedure has been used as a standard model for comparing the subjective value of rewards and measuring their addiction liability. Instead, we used a continuous choice procedure similar to that used by Vandaele et al where the drug and the natural reward were constantly available in a mutually‐inclusive condition. 25 This model was set in an attempt to reproduce a clinical state of drug addiction that resembles to the greatest possible extent what occurs in the real world. In other words, human addicts frequently have the possibility to choose from among several rewards including the drug to which they are addicted, but they abnormally allocate behaviour to substance use rather than natural rewards. In this regard, we have shown that rats refrained from morphine if they had prior experience of sucrose pellet intake, supporting the high addiction liability of sucrose. 26 On the other hand, most of the rats that had initially been put on morphine avidly self‐infused morphine, even in the presence of sucrose pellets and in spite of food restriction before the choice. This is in contrast to previous findings of discrete choice models that rats consistently preferred saccharine to heroin over a range of heroin doses, especially with a short access schedule. 19 , 20 Although an intoxicating effect of the drug on sweet reward seems plausible (the direct effects of self‐administered morphine on operant responding for sucrose), 27 , 28 this reward‐resistant morphine intake can also be construed as a sign of pathological behavioural allocation, that is procuring and consuming the drug at the expense of everyday natural rewards or recreational activities. 9 Of note, our results add to previous findings that when saccharin SA and heroin SA were established on alternate training days, continuous choice procedure led to a remarkable increase in saccharin intake, whereas heroin intake remained unchanged. 25

After the removal of morphine in the extinction phase, the morphine‐SA rats exhibited a dramatic rise in sucrose pellet intake compared to the phase in which morphine and sucrose pellet were both available. This heightened response for taking sucrose during morphine abstinence could be explained by consummatory cross‐sensitization in which animals sensitized to one drug may show increased intake of a different substance. In support of this notion, it has been shown that ethanol‐dependent rats consumed more sucrose than rats with no prior ethanol experience, indicating ethanol as a gateway to sugar intake. 29 In addition, locomotor cross‐sensitization was shown between morphine and sweet food in mice. 30 It has been shown that sugar consumption increases dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens (NAc) shell. 31 Therefore, the loss of the dopamine rush from morphine might cause the brain to crave a substitute such as sucrose. Furthermore, the rats exhibited a blunted morphine‐seeking behaviour, in that their response on the lever that formerly delivered morphine was similar to that on the inactive lever. An acceleration of the extinction mechanism driven by the sucrose pellets cannot be postulated here because lever responding disappeared only after two weeks of the extinction training. This is consistent with previous findings that exposure to sucrose during the withdrawal period did not alter drug seeking in cocaine SA rats. 32 Alternatively, it can be explained by a confounding effect of sucrose pellets on morphine seeking. On the other hand, sucrose pellets did not affect the renewal of extinguished morphine‐seeking behaviour. Indeed, in spite of highly elevated behaviour directed towards sucrose intake in the reinstatement phase, rats still showed a significant increase in their response to the lever previously associated with morphine.

It is important to make a distinction between the effort‐based contingent sucrose taking used here and the noncontingent taking of food and water that were available in the operant chamber in some other studies. 33 , 34 Free access to food and water during the drug SA sessions may provide an opportunity for showing a hedonic response, whereas the effort‐based food SA indicates a goal‐directed behaviour which can be affected by drug addiction. 35 For example, with protracted withdrawal following a period of intensive morphine administration, effort‐based motivated behaviour towards 20% sucrose solution decreased in male mice. However, hedonic behaviour towards sucrose was not affected, as there was no change in the amount of freely consumed 1% sucrose in the two bottles choice test. 36

An interesting finding of this study was that prior experience with sucrose SA resulted in an inability to learn lever pressing for acquiring morphine infusion when the sucrose‐paired lever was not available and, consequently, prioritizing sucrose over morphine when both rewards were available. This outcome is difficult to reconcile with many previous morphine SA studies in initially sucrose‐trained rats in which sucrose pellets were used to facilitate operant response for morphine. 37 , 38 A possible explanation is that the previous studies had set a maximum attainable number of rewards in each training session (e.g. 50 sucrose pellets per session). In contrast, in our study, rats were allowed to take sucrose unrestrictedly (sometimes more than 800 pellets per session) so that binge‐like intake ensued. Research has shown that intermittent exposure to sucrose can produce effects similar to those seen in drug abuse, including changes in brain reward circuits and addictive‐like behaviours. Sucrose activates the reward system and increases dopamine levels in the NAc. 31 In addition, both chronic morphine 39 , 40 , 41 and intermittent sugar consumption 42 decreased D2 dopamine receptor mRNA and increased D3 receptor mRNA in the NAc. There is compelling evidence that excessive sugar intake can lead to a behavioural profile that fulfils several of the DSM‐5 criteria for substance abuse, manifested in binging, withdrawal, cross‐sensitization and craving (for a review see 43 ). For example, excessive sugar intake created a state of endogenous opioid dependence in which naloxone caused behavioural and neurochemical signs of opioid withdrawal. 44 The opioid peptide enkephalin, which functions as a positive reinforcer, increases the release of dopamine in the NAc by activating both mu and delta receptors. 45 In rats, intermittent access to sucrose leads to a decrease in enkephalin mRNA production in the NAc. 42 This reduction may contribute to the repressed morphine intake after the establishment of sucrose SA. Moreover, there is evidence that sucrose intake decreases the availability of mu‐opioid and D2/D3 dopamine receptors in several brain areas associated with reward including the NAc. 46 This modification at the level of receptor availability may also contribute to the lack of lever responding for morphine in rats with a prior history of sucrose binging.

There were some limitations to our study as well as some differences with similar studies. For example, training of the rats to lever press for morphine and sucrose pellet was done in separate stages (similar to 47 ), however, in other studies, it was performed on alternate days. 17 , 48 Another limitation was that we did not counterbalance lever side for morphine and sucrose pellet across rats or a reversal of lever‐reinforcer relationship. 17 An additional limitation was that we did not include a sucrose‐only SA phase before the choice phase for rats initially trained to self‐administer morphine. However, we controlled for this by including a separate saline group. On the other hand, some advantages of our design were the lack of long (10 min) inter‐trial interval 17 , 18 , 19 , 23 , 48 and the use of a short access schedule (2 h) instead of extended access (5–9 h) 19 , 25 , 48 that make it a better model of human behaviour in a natural environment.

CONCLUSION

The choice between morphine and sucrose pellet depended on the starting point of reward taking behaviour. If training for sucrose intake preceded morphine SA, rats exhibited a stronger preference to receive sucrose pellets throughout the course of addiction, even in the presence of morphine. In contrast, when rats were trained to self‐administer morphine before the presentation of sucrose pellets, there was a greater tendency for morphine intake. In addition, when morphine was removed during the withdrawal phase, the allocation of behaviour sucrose intake increased substantially, probably to compensate for the lack of morphine reward. However, the increased preference for sucrose pellets did not prevent the reinstatement of morphine seeking behaviour, indicating a state of profound psychological morphine dependence behind the state of overt sucrose intake. Taken together, to closely mimic clinical symptoms of a substance use disorder, we suggest that an effort‐based delivery of a natural reward should be continuously available, preferably after the phase of basal training for establishing drug SA and during the phase of active drug taking. However, further studies are needed to find the best design for incorporating the natural reward.
