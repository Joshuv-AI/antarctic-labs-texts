A Rapid Pathological Diagnosis Based on Unfixed Frozen Tissue Section Leads to the Misdiagnosis of Eosinophilic Myocarditis as Lymphocytic Myocarditis
Authors: Saya Hirata, Kenichiro Suwa, Atsushi Sakamoto, Hayato Ohtani, Takashi Tsuchida, Yuichiro Maekawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myocarditis requires an urgent endomyocardial biopsy for an accurate diagnosis and appropriate therapeutic decision-making. We herein report the case of a 64-year-old man who presented with fever and progressive dyspnea and was initially diagnosed with acute myocarditis based on the clinical and imaging findings. A rapid pathological analysis of unfixed frozen tissue obtained from an endomyocardial biopsy suggested lymphocytic myocarditis; however, a subsequent examination of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue revealed extensive eosinophilic infiltration, thus confirming eosinophilic myocarditis. Clinical and functional improvements were observed after immunosuppressive therapy. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with different tissue preservation techniques, emphasizing the limitations of a frozen section analysis. Keywords: acute myocarditis, unfixed frozen tissue, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue

Introduction

Acute myocarditis exhibits a broad clinical spectrum ranging from mild symptoms to cardiogenic shock (1). Histologically, they are classified into lymphocytic, eosinophilic, and giant cell types, each requiring distinct therapeutic approaches. Although immunosuppressive therapy remains controversial in lymphocytic myocarditis, urgent initiation may contribute to a better prognosis in eosinophilic and giant cell myocarditis (1). Thus, a prompt endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) and histological diagnosis are essential, particularly in fulminant cases. A rapid diagnosis using unfixed frozen tissue is recommended; however, cautious interpretation is required because of potential discrepancies. We herein report a case of divergent diagnoses between unfixed frozen and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections.

Case Report

Clinical presentation

A 64-year-old male patient with no significant medical history visited his general practitioner because of fever lasting for one week. He was initially treated with cold medication, including acetaminophen; however, his shortness of breath gradually worsened. Chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed a conduction defect and ST-T abnormality. The serum creatinine kinase level was also elevated (258 U/L). The patient was referred to our hospital for two days with a suspected diagnosis of acute myocarditis. On admission, his physical examination revealed normal vital signs, with a blood pressure of 121/95 mmHg, pulse rate of 95 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and body temperature of 37.0°C, suggesting that his condition was relatively stable. Blood tests revealed elevated serum C-reactive protein (1.64 mg/dL), elevated serum N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (5,473 pg/mL), and mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin T (0.664 ng/dL) levels. The serum creatinine kinase level (184 U/L) did not increase compared to that in the previous evaluation. The plasma eosinophil count (530 /μL) was mildly elevated. The 12-lead ECG showed a complete right bundle branch block with a QRS duration of 136 ms, horizontal ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF, and a low QRS voltage in the limb leads (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed diffuse left ventricular (LV) hypokinesis with an ejection fraction (EF) of 27.4% and mild pericardial effusion; the wall thickness was within the normal range. Emergency coronary angiography revealed no significant stenosis of the coronary artery. Simultaneously, an EMB was performed on the right ventricular (RV) septum to obtain four myocardial specimens. One myocardial specimen was kept unfixed and frozen for a rapid pathological diagnosis, which is optional for EMB, while the rest of the specimens were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin for a definitive pathological diagnosis, which is essential for EMB.

12-lead electrocardiography at admission. A notably wide QRS with complete right bundle branch block and ST-segment elevation in II, III, and aVF leads was observed.

Pathological findings from the frozen unfixed tissue section

The protocol for the preparation of frozen specimens by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was as follows: The tissue was embedded in a cryomold using a freezing embedding medium, then it was subsequently frozen in acetone cooled to -75°C, and sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm. The sections were briefly immersed in a solution consisting of 45 mL 100% ethanol, 5 mL formaldehyde, and a drop of acetic acid. Immediately afterward, the sections were rinsed in warm water to remove any residual embedding medium. Hematoxylin staining was performed for 30 s, followed by a single dip in HCl-ethanol and rinsing in warm water to facilitate color development. Counterstaining with eosin was performed for 10 s. Dehydration was carried out through five changes in ethanol, followed by five changes in xylene for clearing. Finally, the sections were mounted with a coverslip using Marinol mounting medium. A rapid pathological diagnosis was made within an hour and revealed myocyte necrosis accompanied by extensive lymphocyte infiltration and a few eosinophils (Fig. 2A-C). Based on these pathological findings and the clinical presentation characterized by preceding common cold symptoms, including fever and dyspnea, acute lymphocytic myocarditis was diagnosed.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining of frozen, unfixed endomyocardial tissue. Low-power field images show diffuse inflammatory-cell infiltration (A). High-power field images reveal myocyte necrosis and a dense infiltration of inflammatory cells. The morphological features of these cells, small mononuclei, limited cytoplasm, and absence of eosin-stained granules, are consistent with lymphocytes (B), although a few eosinophils are also present (arrowheads in B and C). In frozen sections, H&E staining is relatively faint, making eosinophil identification somewhat challenging. The high-power image C corresponds to the white rectangular field shown in B.

Patient monitoring using cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging

The patient's hemodynamic status was stable; therefore, we decided to closely monitor the patient without administering any pharmacological treatment. CMR imaging, including late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), T2 black blood (T2BB), T1, and T2 mapping, was performed on the fourth day of hospitalization. Both marked LGE (Fig. 3A) and significantly elevated signal intensity in T2BB (Fig. 3B) images were noted in the basal inferoseptal segment, whereas increased global extracellular volume fraction (ECV, 36%, Fig. 3C) and T2 values (70 ms, Fig. 3D) were observed diffusely throughout the LV. These observations suggest dense focal myocardial edema and fibrosis in the basal inferior wall, with diffuse intercellular space expansion due to myocardial edema. The CMR findings met the revised Lake Louise criteria (2) and confirmed the diagnosis of acute myocarditis.

Cardiac magnetic resonance images. Images included late gadolinium enhancement (A), T2 black blood (B), extracellular volume fraction map (C), and T2 map (D). The yellow arrow and arrowhead indicate focal late gadolinium enhancement and T2 signal elevation in the basal inferior wall, respectively.

Pathological findings from FFPE sections

On the fourth day of hospitalization, H&E and Giemsa staining of the three FFPE specimens obtained by EMB on the initial day, in contrast to the rapid diagnosis results, revealed extensive eosinophilic infiltration (Fig. 4A, B). Atrophic changes and the dropout of myocytes adjacent to dense eosinophilic infiltrates were observed, leading to a definitive diagnosis of eosinophilic myocarditis. Notably, eosinophilic granule major basic protein 1 (MBP-1) staining, as revealed by immunohistochemistry, confirmed eosinophil degranulation (Fig. 4C). An immunofluorescence analysis using antibodies against MBP-1 and Galectin 10, a peripheral cytoplasmic eosinophil marker, confirmed the presence of eosinophils (Fig. 4D, E). Representative viral antibody titers, including those for influenza A and B, coxsackieviruses A9, A16, and B1-B6, and adenoviruses, did not increase between days 1 and 15, thus supporting the aforementioned diagnosis. Several blood tests, including antibodies for collagenous diseases, β-D-glucan, aspergillus antigen, and multiple allergen-specific immunoglobulin E tests, were negative. No findings were suggestive of secondary eosinophilia, including allergic diseases, eosinophilic granulomatous polyangiitis, or parasitic infections.

Immunosuppressive treatment

During the observation period, the serum troponin T levels and plasma eosinophil counts gradually declined, reaching 0.157 ng/dL and 211 /μL, respectively, on day 5 (Fig. 5). However, TTE revealed a persistent LV dysfunction and residual pericardial effusion. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the patient would benefit from immunosuppressive therapy. Eventually, immunosuppressive treatment was initiated on the sixth day after the administration of 30 mg of oral prednisolone. LVEF by TTE gradually improved, and serum NT-proBNP level decreased. Chest radiography revealed reduced cardiomegaly, and the patient was discharged on the 23rd day. The oral prednisolone dosage was reduced by 5 mg/week, and treatment was discontinued on the 46th day. The serum NT-proBNP level declined to 606 pg/mL. Follow-up TTE showed a further increase in LVEF (47.9%), while CMR demonstrated normalized T2 signal intensity in the basal inferoseptal segment, decreased ECV (31%), and T2 relaxation time (57 ms) compared to the initial measurements. These follow-up results indicated an improvement in myocardial edema. A pathological examination of the FFPE specimen obtained by EMB on the 49th day, revealed no inflammatory cell infiltration in the myocardium.

Time course of the laboratory parameters, left ventricular ejection fraction, and immunosuppressive therapy during hospitalization. The plasma eosinophil counts, serum troponin T and NT-proBNP levels decreased gradually, whereas reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) persisted through day 5. After the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy on day 6, eosinophil counts promptly declined and remained within the normal range. The left ventricular function subsequently improved, with LVEF reaching 44.0% at discharge.

Discussion

Eosinophilic myocarditis is a relatively rare form of myocarditis characterized by eosinophilic infiltration and is often accompanied by eosinophilia. An international multicenter cohort study analyzed 220 cases presenting with histologically diagnosed acute myocarditis with LV systolic dysfunction and found that lymphocytic myocarditis was the most common form of myocarditis (72%), followed by eosinophilic myocarditis (15%), and giant cell myocarditis (10%) (3). Acute eosinophilic myocarditis has a relatively low mortality rate (approximately 7%) (4). Mild cases may be adequately managed with symptomatic treatment alone, accompanied by appropriate monitoring. However, patients with heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or fatal arrhythmias have poor prognoses; therefore, an early and definitive diagnosis of myocarditis is important. Aggressive treatment strategies, including steroid therapy and mechanical circulatory support, should be considered to address the deterioration of the disease conditions. Specifically, the accurate pathological identification of the inflammatory cell type is crucial. Steroid therapy is known to be effective in eosinophilic and giant cell myocarditis; however, its efficacy in lymphocytic myocarditis remains uncertain and can occasionally be detrimental. A definitive diagnosis of eosinophilic myocarditis can only be made by performing EMB, followed by pathological observation of tissue sections. In this study, a definitive diagnosis based on FFPE sections confirmed myocyte necrosis accompanied by eosinophil infiltration. However, we observed a discrepancy between the pathological diagnoses of the unfixed frozen and FFPE specimens.

There are several possible reasons for this discrepancy in the results. First, frozen and FFPE tissue exhibit different physical properties. Frozen tissue sections are usually thicker and sometimes folded, as observed in this case, which may affect the accuracy of diagnosis. In addition, the need for a rapid diagnosis limits the staining quality and types of staining used for pathological evaluation. Therefore, 3.9-4.6% of pathological diagnoses based on frozen sections tend to be inconsistent with the definitive diagnosis obtained from FFPE sections (5). Indeed, in the present case, eosinophils confirmed by immunohistochemistry using an anti-MBP-1 antibody showed weaker eosin staining in H&E-stained frozen tissue than in H&E-stained FFPE tissue, resulting in less distinct differentiation from lymphocytes (Supplementary material). Clinicians should thus be aware of the limitations of unfixed frozen tissues. In cases where the infiltrated inflammatory cell type is not well characterized, further evaluation using FFPE is recommended, as it can provide a definitive diagnosis within 24 hours.

Second, sampling errors in myocardial tissue biopsies may occur because of the non-uniform distribution of inflammatory cell infiltration. In the present case, lymphocyte infiltration was predominant in the frozen specimens, whereas eosinophil infiltration was dominant in all three FFPE samples. These findings suggest that sampling errors may be an additional contributing factor to a misdiagnosis. Whether EMB accurately identifies the cause of myocarditis is closely associated with the extent and distribution pattern of the inflammatory cell infiltrates. Diffuse inflammatory infiltrates in the myocardium have a higher sensitivity to EMB than do localized or patchy infiltrates. A mottled interstitial myocardial eosinophilic and lymphocytic infiltrate is present in eosinophilic myocarditis as well as in more severe diffuse necrotizing myocarditis. Moreover, most myocarditis cases involve varying degrees of lymphocyte and eosinophil infiltration. Therefore, distinguishing between lymphocytic and eosinophilic myocarditis is challenging. Eosinophilic myocardial infiltrates ranged from mild to severe in 69 patients with hypersensitive eosinophilic myocarditis as diagnosed at autopsy. In half of these cases, infiltrates with focal lesions were missed even when EMB was performed (6).

Currently, the European Society of Cardiology Working Group statement (7) recommends performing EMB at three or more sites to increase the diagnostic accuracy of myocarditis. The drawback of EMB is the risk of serious complications including myocardial perforation or conduction defects. Therefore, we allocated three samples for a definitive diagnosis using FFPE specimens and one sample for a supplementary rapid diagnosis using frozen unfixed specimens. Moreover, the diagnostic accuracy of LV EMB is significantly higher than that of RV EMB when structural or functional abnormalities including ventricular hypertrophy, dilation, or hypokinesis are present alone or predominantly in the LV (8). Therefore, a minimum and sufficient number of biopsy sites and samples while identifying optimal biopsy sites using multimodal imaging may improve diagnostic accuracy. In this case, CMR imaging (T2BB and LGE) confirmed that the strongest inflammation was present in the basal inferoseptal segment. Thus, the EMB of the RV septum appears to be the optimal approach in the current case.

Conclusion

An early pathological diagnosis is crucial for acute myocarditis because certain types, including eosinophilic myocarditis, benefit from the prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. Although a rapid pathological evaluation using frozen tissue sections can assist in the diagnosis, its diagnostic accuracy is limited and likely inferior to that of FFPE sections. Therefore, prompt FFPE processing with multiple samples should be considered when performing a rapid EMB diagnosis.
