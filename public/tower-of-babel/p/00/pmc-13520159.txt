Genome-wide characterization of the glutathione S-transferase gene family identifies GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 as anthocyanin-associated GSTs in soybean
Authors: Taotao Han, Tianhao Lu, Xinyue Tang, Mingli Sha, Wenwei Chu, Guopeng Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnthocyanins are essential flavonoid pigments that contribute to plant coloration, stress resilience, and nutritional quality. Despite their importance, the mechanisms underlying anthocyanin transport and accumulation in soybean (Glycine max) remain poorly understood. Here, we performed a comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the glutathione S-transferase (GST) gene family in soybean and functionally investigated GmGSTF members potentially associated with anthocyanin stabilization, intracellular trafficking, and vacuolar sequestration.ResultsA total of 104 GmGST genes were identified and classified into seven subfamilies, including 14 Phi-class GmGSTF members. These genes exhibited diverse exon-intron structures, conserved motif compositions, and uneven chromosomal distributions, with segmental duplication likely contributing substantially to family expansion. Phylogenetic and comparative amino acid sequence analyses showed that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 clustered closely with previously characterized anthocyanin-associated GSTs and contained conserved GST-N-Phi and GST-C-Phi domains. Expression profiling revealed tissue-specific and stress-responsive patterns among GmGSTF genes. Notably, GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 showed coordinated expression with two key anthocyanin biosynthetic genes GmUGT78K2 and GmF3′5′H, respectively, and their expression profiles were closely associated with anthocyanin accumulation across soybean tissues. These phylogenetic and co-expression results prompted us to further investigate whether GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 have conserved anthocyanin-associated functions. Subcellular localization analysis showed that both GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 were mainly distributed in the cytoplasm. Ectopic expression of GmGSTF8 or GmGSTF11 in Arabidopsis thaliana tt19 mutants restored stem-base pigmentation and increased relative anthocyanin content in the tt19 background; seed coat pigmentation was also partially restored. These results indicate that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 are conserved anthocyanin-associated GST proteins that may function as carrier- or chaperone-like proteins involved in anthocyanin stabilization and vacuolar sequestration.ConclusionTaken together, this study provides systematic characterization and heterologous functional evidence for soybean GSTF members associated with anthocyanin accumulation and identifies GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 as candidate targets for future studies aimed at improving flavonoid accumulation in soybean. These findings provide a valuable framework for future mechanistic and metabolic engineering studies in legumes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09501-x.

Introduction

Anthocyanins are water-soluble flavonoid pigments widely distributed in plants, contributing to diverse coloration in flowers, fruits, seeds, and vegetative tissues [1]. In addition to their roles in pigmentation, anthocyanins are essential for plant fitness, functioning in the attraction of pollinators and seed dispersers, protection against ultraviolet radiation, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [2, 3]. Owing to their strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, anthocyanins also have important implications for human health. Anthocyanins are synthesized via the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway through the coordinated action of structural enzymes, including PAL, CHS, CHI, F3H, F3′H, DFR, ANS, and UFGT [4]. The expression of these genes is tightly regulated by the MYB-bHLH-WD40 (MBW) transcriptional complex, which is conserved across plant species [5, 6]. Following biosynthesis on the cytosolic side of the endoplasmic reticulum, anthocyanins must be transported into the vacuole for stable accumulation, a process essential for their biological function [7, 8].

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed for anthocyanin transport, including membrane transporter-mediated transport, vesicle trafficking, and glutathione S-transferase (GST)-mediated sequestration [4]. Membrane transport involves ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters and multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) proteins located on the tonoplast, whereas vesicle trafficking relies on the fusion of anthocyanin-containing vesicles with the vacuole [9–12]. Among these mechanisms, GST-assisted sequestration has been widely recognized as an important process in anthocyanin accumulation, with GSTs are generally proposed to function as ligandin-like carrier or chaperone proteins rather than direct membrane transporters [13]. GSTs constitute a large and diverse enzyme family involved in detoxification, secondary metabolism, and stress responses [14–17]. In plants, GST proteins are classified into several subfamilies, among which the Phi class has been most frequently associated with anthocyanin accumulation, stabilization, and vacuolar sequestration [18]. Loss-of-function mutations in GST genes often result in reduced pigmentation phenotypes, as observed in bz2 of maize [19], an9 of petunia [20], and tt19 of Arabidopsis thaliana [21]. Arabidopsis TT19 has been implicated in the intracellular trafficking and vacuolar accumulation of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins [22, 23]. Similar functions have been reported for GSTs in various plants, including cyclamen [24], sweet potato [16, 25, 26], Morella rubra [27], grape hyacinth [13], blueberry [28], apple [29], cotton [30, 31], Litchi chinensis Sonn [32]., purple tea [33], Senecio cruentus [34], Medicago truncatula [35], and peach [36].

Soybean is a globally important oilseed crop and a major source of plant protein and bioactive compounds [37]. Flavonoids, including anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins, contribute to seed coat pigmentation and are associated with stress tolerance and nutritional quality. In colored soybean varieties, these compounds predominantly accumulate in the seed coat, whereas cotyledons generally lack anthocyanin accumulation. Although anthocyanin biosynthesis has been extensively studied, the mechanisms underlying their transport and accumulation in soybean remain poorly understood. In particular, the roles of GST family members in mediating anthocyanin sequestration in soybean have not been systematically characterized.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of the soybean GST gene family and investigated the potential functions of GmGSTF members in anthocyanin stabilization, intracellular trafficking, and vacuolar sequestration. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying anthocyanin accumulation in soybean and offer valuable targets for the genetic improvement of soybean nutritional quality.

Results

Identification and chromosomal mapping of the GmGST gene family in soybean

To systematically identify GST genes in soybean, Arabidopsis GST protein sequences were employed as queries to search the soybean genome. As a result, a total of 104 GST gene family members were identified. Based on phylogenetic relationships with previously characterized Arabidopsis GST proteins and the conserved domain composition of each clade, the identified GmGST genes were classified into seven subfamilies: Tau (GSTU), Phi (GSTF), Lambda (GSTL), Zeta (GSTZ), Theta (GSTT), Dehydroascorbate Reductase (DHAR), and Tetrachlorohydroquinone Dehalogenase (TCHQD). To maintain consistency with the nomenclature reported by Hasan et al. [38], previously assigned GmGST gene identifiers were retained whenever possible. It should be noted that these identifiers are not consecutively numbered in the final dataset. For example, the retained GSTU members include identifiers ranging from GmGSTU1 to GmGSTU79; however, this range does not indicate that 79 GSTU genes were retained, because several previously annotated candidates were removed after redundancy filtering and conserved-domain validation. Therefore, the numerical suffixes represent inherited gene identifiers rather than the actual number of retained genes in each subfamily. After rechecking the gene list, a total of 104 non-redundant GmGST genes were retained in the final dataset, including 66 GSTU, 14 GSTF, 8 GSTL, 6 GSTT, 4 DHAR, 3 GSTZ, and 3 TCHQD members. The complete list of retained GmGST genes is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The 104 GmGST genes showed an uneven chromosomal distribution across the soybean genome, with considerable variation in gene number among chromosomes (Fig. 1). Chromosome Gm08 harbored the largest number of GmGST genes (13 genes, 12.5%), followed by Gm07 and Gm18, each containing 12 genes (11.5%). Chromosomes Gm02, Gm15, and Gm19 contained 10, 8, and 2 GmGST genes, respectively, whereas Gm03 contained only one GmGST gene. Six genes were identified on each of Gm01, Gm05, and Gm13; five genes were found on each of Gm04 and Gm14; four genes were located on each of Gm06, Gm10, and Gm17; and three genes were present on each of Gm11 and Gm20. No GmGST genes were detected on Gm09, Gm12, or Gm16. Consistent with previous reports [38], the majority (approximately 90%) of GmGST genes were distributed near the chromosomal ends.

Analysis of gene structure and conserved motifs of GmGST

To further investigate the structural diversity of GmGST genes, we analyzed their gene structures and conserved motifs. The analysis revealed substantial variation both between and within subfamilies (Fig. 2A). Most GmGST genes contain 1–11 exons, with GmGSTZ2 and GmGSTT5 exhibiting a relatively high number of exons (10 and 11, respectively). Exon numbers are highly conserved among genes within the same clade, particularly for those encoding proteins with similar structures. In contrast, intron numbers vary considerably across different GmGST subfamilies (Fig. 2B). Notably, GmGSTU22, GmGSTU68 and GmGSTU76 consist solely of exons, lacking introns entirely. With the exception of GmGSTU10, GmGSTU19, GmGSTU25, GmGSTU31, GmGSTU40, GmGSTU45, GmGSTU46, GmGSTU65, GmGSTU66, GmGSTU68, GmGSTU71-GmGSTU76, GmGSTU79, GmGSTL7, GmGSTL8, GmGSTT4, GmGSTT6, GmGSTF3, GmGSTF9, GmGSTF12, and GmGSTF14, the majority of GmGST genes possess untranslated regions (UTRs), with 75.96% (79/104) containing UTRs.

To further characterize the conserved domains of GmGST proteins, we analyzed their sequences using the MEME program, with the number of conserved motifs set to 10 (Fig. 2C). GmGST proteins within the same clade displayed similar motif composition and arrangement, consistent with their gene structures, which further supports the subfamily classification. Motifs 1, 4, 6, and 7 were widely present across most GmGST proteins, with some exceptions in certain GmGSTUs, GmDHARs, and GmGSTTs. In most GmGSTUs and GmGSTZs, motifs 1, 3, and 7 were arranged in a conserved order, followed by motifs 6 and 2 in a distinct pattern. While common motifs were shared by most GmGSTUs, some subfamily members contained unique motifs. For instance, motif 9 was predominantly found in GmDHARs, GmTCHQDs, and GmGSTFs, whereas motif 10 was specific to GmGSTFs. These differences in gene structure and conserved motifs among GmGST subfamilies suggest variations in their regulatory mechanisms and functional roles.

Gene duplication and evolutionary analysis of GmGST

Gene duplication is a key mechanism driving the expansion of gene families. To investigate the duplication events within the GmGST gene family, all 104 GmGST genes in soybean were analyzed. Under the criteria used in this study, duplicated GmGST gene pairs were mainly detected within segmentally duplicated regions, whereas no tandemly duplicated GmGST gene pairs were identified. These results suggest that segmental duplication may have been a major contributor to the expansion of the soybean GST gene family (Fig. 3A). To further explore the evolutionary origins of GmGST genes, we conducted a genome-wide collinearity analysis between soybean and four representative species, including two monocotyledonous species (Oryza sativa and Zea mays) and two dicotyledonous species (Arabidopsis thaliana and Medicago truncatula), and generated a collinearity map (Fig. 3B). A total of 51 and 36 syntenic GST gene pairs were identified between soybean and M. truncatula and between soybean and A. thaliana, respectively, whereas only 7 and 4 syntenic pairs were detected with maize and rice. These results indicate a higher level of conservation of GST genes among dicot species, particularly within legumes.

Phylogenetic, structural, and expression characterization of GmGSTF genes reveals their potential association with anthocyanin accumulation

To investigate the evolutionary relationships and potential functional roles of soybean GmGSTF proteins, all identified GmGST genes were analyzed. Phylogenetic analysis classified the 104 GmGST proteins into seven distinct subfamilies, reflecting both conservation and divergence (Fig. 4A). To further identify candidate genes associated with anthocyanin accumulation, a comparative phylogenetic analysis was performed by incorporating 53 GST proteins from A. thaliana and nine functionally characterized anthocyanin-related GST proteins from other plant species. As shown in the phylogenetic tree, GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 clustered closely with known anthocyanin-related GST proteins, including AtTT19 [21], PfGST1 [39], CkmGST3 [24], PhAN9 [20], VviGST4 [40], CsGSTF1 [33], LcGST4 [32], PpGST1 [36], MdGSTF6 [29], and MrGST1 [27], suggesting that they may be associated with anthocyanin stabilization, intracellular trafficking, and vacuolar sequestration.

Multiple sequence alignment of GmGSTF proteins with anthocyanin-related GSTs from other dicotyledonous plants revealed highly conserved residues, including the canonical GST-N-Phi (Thioredoxin-like superfamily) and GST-C-Phi (GSTC-family superfamily) domains, as well as several highly conserved sites (Fig. 4B). The 14 GmGSTF proteins displayed conserved physicochemical properties, with lengths of 137–221 amino acids, molecular weights of 15.97–24.88 kDa, weakly acidic pI values (5.34–6.76), and negative GRAVY values, indicating hydrophilic characteristics. Consistent with these features, all GmGSTFs adopted the typical GST fold in predicted 3D structures (Fig. 4C; Table 1). Expression profiling showed that GmGSTF genes exhibit diverse patterns across tissues and developmental stages, with certain members displaying high, tissue-specific expression. Notably, RNA-seq-based Spearman correlation analysis showed positive correlations between GmGSTF8 and GmUGT78K2 (ρ = 0.85, P ≤ 0.001) and between GmGSTF11 and GmF3′5′H (ρ = 0.91, P ≤ 0.001), suggesting a potential association with anthocyanin accumulation and tissue-specific flavonoid metabolism (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Figure S1). Collectively, these results indicate that GmGSTF genes are evolutionarily conserved, structurally similar, and functionally diversified in soybean.

Analysis of cis-elements and expression patterns of GmGSTF genes

Promoter analysis revealed that GmGSTF genes contain abundant cis-acting elements related to light responsiveness, stress responses, and phytohormone signaling, including G-box, MBS, ABRE, and CGTCA/TGACG motifs (Supplementary Figure S2). Consistent with these regulatory features, transcriptome analysis [42, 43] showed that GmGSTF genes exhibited distinct tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression patterns under heat, drought, submergence, and recovery conditions (Supplementary Figure S3, and S4). Several genes, such as GmGSTF5, GmGSTF8, GmGSTF11 and GmGSTF13, were highly expressed in reproductive tissues. In addition, genes including GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 were induced under heat and combined stress conditions. Notably, GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 exhibited expression patterns consistent with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes and were co-expressed with key structural genes, suggesting their potential involvement in anthocyanin-related processes. Collectively, these results indicate that GmGSTF genes are regulated by multiple environmental and endogenous signals and display functional divergence in soybean.

Coordinated expression of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes

Although several GmGSTF genes showed co-expression with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 were prioritized for functional validation based on multiple lines of evidence, including phylogenetic proximity to functionally characterized anthocyanin-associated GSTFs, coordinated expression with selected anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, stress-responsive expression patterns, and conserved structural features. First, soybean samples from different tissues and seed developmental stages were collected (Fig. 5A and B) and anthocyanin contents were quantified. Anthocyanin accumulation was significantly higher in flowers than in roots, leaves, and pods (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C). In contrast, no significant variation in anthocyanin content was observed during seed development (Fig. 5D). We further analyzed the expression relationships between GmGSTF8/GmGSTF11 and key anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, including GmF3′H, GmF3′5′H, GmDFR2, GmANS2, and GmUGT78K2. Expression analysis revealed gene-specific expression patterns among anthocyanin biosynthetic genes. GmF3′5′H and GmUGT78K2 were predominantly expressed in flowers and showed stage-dependent expression patterns during seed development. GmDFR2 and GmANS2 showed higher expression in flowers than in other tissues, whereas GmDFR2, GmANS2, and GmF3′H were highly expressed at the S1 and S2 stages but decreased at later seed developmental stages (Fig. 5E-I). Notably, GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 showed expression patterns similar to those of GmF3′5′H and GmUGT78K2 across tissues and developmental stages (Fig. 5J and K), in agreement with RNA-seq data (Fig. 4D). Spearman correlation analysis further showed strong positive correlations for selected gene pairs, with Spearman’s coefficients greater than 0.9 (P ≤ 0.001, Supplementary Figure S5). These results support coordinated expression between GmGSTF8/GmGSTF11 and selected anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, but do not establish direct regulatory or functional causality.

Subcellular localization of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11

Anthocyanin-associated GSTF proteins are generally proposed to function in the cytoplasm as carrier- or ligandin-like proteins. To further investigate the subcellular localization of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11, their coding sequences without stop codons were amplified and fused in-frame with GFP under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter. As shown in Fig. 6, in N. benthamiana leaf cells expressing GmGSTF8-GFP or GmGSTF11-GFP, GFP fluorescence was mainly observed in the cytoplasm, whereas the free GFP control showed diffuse fluorescence throughout the cell. This cytoplasmic distribution is consistent with their possible roles as cytosolic anthocyanin-associated carrier proteins.

Heterologous complementation of the tt19 mutant supports anthocyanin-associated functions of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11

The Arabidopsis tt19 mutant, which is defective in anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin accumulation due to the loss of a Phi-class GST, was selected for functional complementation analysis. Transgenic tt19 plants expressing GmGSTF8 or GmGSTF11 under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter were generated and at least three independent homozygous T3 lines were obtained for each construct and compared with the tt19 mutant and wild-type plants. Phenotypic analysis showed partial restoration of seed coat pigmentation in the complemented lines. In addition, stem-base pigmentation defects in the tt19 mutant were restored in the complemented lines (Fig. 7A). Expression analysis further confirmed that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 were successfully expressed in the complemented lines (Fig. 7B, C). Quantification of relative anthocyanin content revealed that the complemented lines accumulated significantly higher relative anthocyanin levels than the tt19 mutant, reaching levels comparable to those of wild-type plants in this assay (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 can complement the tt19 mutant phenotype, supporting their conserved anthocyanin-associated functions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Soybean (Glycine max cv. Williams 82) was kindly provided by the Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Soybean seeds were sown in May in fertile, well-moisturized soil with a pH of 6.5–7.5 and grown under open-field conditions. Plant organs were classified into four tissue types, namely roots, leaves, flowers, and pods, which were sampled separately. Based on seed size and fresh weight at different developmental stages, seed development was divided into six stages: S1 (20 days), S2 (30 days), S3 (40 days), S4 (50 days), S5 (60 days), and S6 (70 days). Samples were collected at each stage for gene expression pattern analysis.

Seeds of Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia (Col-0), the tt19 mutant, the corresponding transgenic lines, and Nicotiana benthamiana were grown in a controlled growth chamber at 24 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Genome-wide identification and characterization of the GmGST family in soybean

Genomic and protein sequence data of soybean (Glycine max Wm82. a2. v1) were downloaded from Phytozome v12.1 (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/pz/portal.html) to construct a local database. Protein sequences of Arabidopsis GSTs (AtGSTs) were retrieved from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org) to serve as seed sequences. A BLASTP search was performed against the soybean proteome with an E-value threshold of < 1 × 10⁻⁵ to obtain preliminary candidate GmGST proteins. To improve the accuracy and completeness of GST identification, a Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-based approach was additionally employed. The HMM profiles corresponding to the conserved GST domains, including GST_N (PF02798) and GST_C (PF00043), were downloaded from the Pfam database and used to scan the soybean protein dataset using HMMER or the “Simple HMM Search” function in TBtools, with an E-value cutoff of < 0.01. Candidate sequences identified from both BLASTP and HMM searches were merged to generate a comprehensive candidate set. Redundant sequences and alternative splice variants were removed using TBtools, retaining only the longest protein sequence for each gene locus. To ensure the reliability of GST annotation, all non-redundant candidates were further validated for the presence of conserved GST domains using the SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/smart/batch.pl) and NCBI Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi?SEQUENCE).

Chromosomal localization of GmGST genes

The physical locations of GmGST genes were obtained from the soybean genome annotation downloaded from Phytozome v12.1. Gene coordinates were extracted and mapped onto chromosomes using TBtools to visualize their distribution [44].

Intraspecies and interspecies synteny analysis

To investigate syntenic relationships among GmGST genes within soybean and between soybean and other representative plant species (O. sativa, Z. mays, A. thaliana and M. truncatula), genomic and protein sequence data were obtained from Ensembl (https://plants.ensembl.org/info/data/ftp/index.html). Intraspecific synteny within soybean and interspecific synteny between soybean and the selected species were analyzed using TBtools with the MCScanX module. Conserved gene pairs and collinear blocks were identified and visualized using TBtools. Tandemly duplicated genes were defined as homologous GST genes located on the same chromosome within a 200-kb region and separated by no more than one intervening gene. Segmentally duplicated gene pairs were identified based on their presence within collinear blocks detected by MCScanX. Syntenic maps were generated using TBtools, and duplicated or orthologous GST gene pairs were highlighted to facilitate comparative genomic analysis.

Gene structure and motif analysis

Exon-intron structures of GmGST genes were visualized using TBtools. Conserved motifs were identified with MEME Suite v5 using the zoops model, with the number of motifs set to 10 and widths ranging from 6 to 50 amino acids.

Cis-acting element analysis

The cis-acting elements were predicted in PLACE (Plant Cis-acting Regulatory DNA Elements; http://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE/signalscan.html) and PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) databases.

Phylogenetic analysis and amino acid sequence alignment

GST protein sequences from other species (Supplementary Table S3) were retrieved from GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Genbank/) for comparative analysis with soybean GmGST. Multiple amino acid sequences were aligned using the online tool Clustal Omega (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/), and the resulting alignment files were downloaded. The alignments were then imported into MEGA X, and an unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate branch support [45]. Conserved residues were identified, and multiple sequence alignment results were visualized to illustrate sequence conservation and evolutionary relationships among the GST proteins.

Physicochemical properties and structure prediction of GmGSTF proteins

The physicochemical properties of GmGSTF proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). For three-dimensional (3D) structure prediction, the amino acid sequences of GmGSTF proteins were submitted to AlphaFold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) to obtain high-confidence structural models. The predicted structures were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL software (https://pymol.org/2/). Structural comparisons among GmGSTF members were performed based on overall folding patterns and secondary structure composition.

RNA isolation and reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from different soybean tissues, including roots, leaves, flowers, pods, and seeds at developmental stages S1-S6, using the OmniPlant RNA Kit (including DNase I) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [46]. RNA concentration and quality were assessed using an ND-1000 spectrophotometer. High-quality RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Gene-specific primers were designed for RT-qPCR, and soybean ACTIN was used as the internal reference gene. Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed with three independent biological replicates, each including at least two technical replicates to ensure data reliability [47]. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method with soybean ACTIN as the internal reference gene. Primers used in this study are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Vector construction and subcellular localization analysis

For subcellular localization analysis, the coding sequences of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 without stop codons were cloned into the PYBA1132-35 S-GFP vector to generate C-terminal GFP fusion constructs. The empty PYBA1132-35 S-GFP vector was used as a free GFP control. All constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration, following the established protocol [48]. GFP fluorescence was observed three days after infiltration using a confocal laser scanning microscope with excitation at 488 nm and emission detection at 500–540 nm.

Stable transformation of Arabidopsis

The A. thaliana transparent testa19 (tt19) mutant (SALK_105779) in the Columbia (Col-0) background was obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Arabidopsis plants were transformed using the floral dip method [49]. T1 transgenic seeds were selected on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin. Homozygous T3 lines were selected and used for phenotypic and anthocyanin analyses.

Quantification of relative anthocyanin content

Relative anthocyanin content was measured using a spectrophotometric method [50]. Approximately 180 mg of fresh plant tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and extracted with 1 mL of 1% (v/v) HCl in methanol in a 2 mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at 4 °C in the dark overnight. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was diluted 1:3 (v/v) with acidified methanol. Absorbance was measured at 530 nm (A530) and 657 nm (A657) using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. Each assay included three biological replicates, with technical replicates performed for each sample, and relative anthocyanin content was calculated as A530 − 0.25 × A657.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SD from three biological replicates. Statistical significance among multiple groups was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, and differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Spearman’s correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were calculated using the OmicShare tools, a free online platform for data analysis (https://www.omicshare.com/tools), to evaluate co-expression relationships among GmGSTF8, GmGSTF11, and selected anthocyanin biosynthetic genes. Correlations with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Accession numbers

The datasets analyzed during the current study are available in public repositories. RNA-seq raw data are available under accession numbers PRJNA238493 [41] and PRJNA574626 [43]. Additional transcriptome datasets are available in GEO under accession numbers GSE218146, GSE213479, and GSE186317 [42].

Discussion

Extension of previous soybean GST studies and evolutionary conservation of GmGSTFs

Previous genome-wide studies have provided valuable resources for understanding the soybean GST gene family, particularly in terms of gene identification, classification, chromosomal distribution, and stress-responsive expression patterns [38]. Building on these resources, the present study applied a stringent domain-based filtering strategy to obtain a high-confidence GmGST dataset and further focused on Phi-class GmGSTFs potentially associated with anthocyanin accumulation, stabilization, and vacuolar sequestration. Compared with previous studies that mainly emphasized genome-wide annotation and transcript profiling, our work extends the functional framework of soybean GST research by integrating phylogenetic comparison with known anthocyanin-associated GSTs, co-expression analysis with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, subcellular distribution analysis, and heterologous complementation in the Arabidopsis tt19 mutant.

The larger number of syntenic GST gene pairs between soybean and M. truncatula or A. thaliana than between soybean and monocot species suggests a higher degree of conservation of GST loci among dicots, particularly within legumes. Phylogenetic analysis showed that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 clustered closely with functionally characterized anthocyanin-associated GSTs, including Arabidopsis TT19, maize BZ2, petunia AN9, apple MdGSTF6, peach PpGST1, and Medicago MtGSTF7 [19–21, 29, 35, 36]. This phylogenetic proximity suggests that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 may retain conserved anthocyanin-associated functions related to flavonoid stabilization, intracellular trafficking, or vacuolar sequestration. However, possible functional divergence between these two genes should also be considered. GmGSTF8 showed a stronger co-expression relationship with GmUGT78K2, whereas GmGSTF11 was more closely correlated with GmF3′5′H, suggesting that they may be preferentially associated with different branches or stages of anthocyanin-related metabolism. Nevertheless, because both proteins contain conserved GSTF domains and both were able to complement the Arabidopsis tt19 mutant in a heterologous system, partial functional redundancy between GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 cannot be excluded.

Coordinated expression and possible regulatory mechanisms

Expression profiling further supported the potential involvement of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 in anthocyanin accumulation. GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 showed coordinated expression with selected anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, particularly GmUGT78K2 and GmF3′5′H, and their expression patterns were broadly consistent with anthocyanin accumulation across the sampled tissues, especially in flowers. Similar coordination between anthocyanin biosynthetic genes and anthocyanin-associated GST genes has been reported in several plant species and is often linked to regulation by MYB-bHLH-WD40 transcriptional complexes [3, 28, 33, 34, 51, 52]. In this study, promoter analysis identified multiple light-, hormone-, and stress-responsive cis-elements in GmGSTF promoters, suggesting that these genes may be regulated by both developmental and environmental signals. However, direct regulatory relationships between soybean MBW components and GmGSTF8 or GmGSTF11 remain to be experimentally demonstrated. Future yeast one-hybrid, dual-luciferase, electrophoretic mobility shift assay, ChIP-qPCR, and network-level analyses such as WGCNA will help determine whether GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 are direct targets of anthocyanin-regulating transcription factors.

Functional validation of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 in anthocyanin accumulation

Subcellular distribution analysis showed that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 were mainly distributed in the cytoplasm, which is consistent with the proposed carrier- or ligandin-like role of anthocyanin-associated GSTs in potentially binding or stabilizing anthocyanins before vacuolar sequestration [8, 53, 54]. Heterologous complementation of the Arabidopsis tt19 mutant provided additional evidence that GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 are functionally associated with anthocyanin accumulation. Ectopic expression of either gene restored stem-base pigmentation and increased relative anthocyanin content in the tt19 background, indicating that both soybean GSTFs can partially substitute for Arabidopsis TT19 in this heterologous system. In addition, the partial recovery of seed coat pigmentation suggests a broader role in flavonoid accumulation. However, because TT19 is involved in both anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin accumulation, the restored seed coat color may also reflect partial recovery of proanthocyanidin deposition [22]. Moreover, the spectrophotometric assay used in this study estimates relative total anthocyanin content but does not distinguish individual anthocyanin derivatives, acylated forms, or proanthocyanidins. Thus, future HPLC-MS-based flavonoid profiling will be important for determining the specific flavonoid compounds associated with GmGSTF8- and GmGSTF11-mediated accumulation. Because Williams 82 is a yellow-seeded cultivar with low basal anthocyanin accumulation in seeds, the absence of a clear developmental trend in seed anthocyanin content should be interpreted within this genetic background and should not be used to exclude possible roles of GmGSTF genes in pigmented soybean accessions [55].

A working model and future perspectives

Based on these results, we propose a working model in which GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 function as anthocyanin-associated carrier- or sequestration-related proteins in soybean (Fig. 8). In this model, anthocyanins synthesized on the cytosolic surface of the endoplasmic reticulum may be bound or stabilized by cytoplasmic GmGSTF proteins and subsequently directed toward vacuolar sequestration through pathways involving ABC transporters, MATE transporters, and/or vesicle-mediated trafficking [10, 11]. This model supports the view that anthocyanin accumulation depends on the coordinated coupling of biosynthesis, cytoplasmic stabilization, and vacuolar sequestration, rather than on a single linear transport route.

Despite these findings, several limitations remain. First, the native functions of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 in soybean remain to be validated through knockout, knockdown, or overexpression analyses in soybean. Second, the substrate specificity and binding affinity of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 toward different anthocyanin or proanthocyanidin derivatives remain unknown. Different anthocyanin or proanthocyanidin derivatives may have distinct affinities for GST proteins, which should be tested using in vitro substrate-binding assays. Third, potential functional redundancy among soybean GSTF members may mask phenotypic effects in planta. Finally, recent biochemical evidence suggests that some anthocyanin-associated GSTs may have broader catalytic or regulatory potential in flavonoid metabolism; however, such roles have not been tested for GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11. Future studies combining soybean genetic transformation, CRISPR/Cas-based mutagenesis, in vitro substrate-binding assays, protein-interaction analysis, and HPLC-MS-based flavonoid profiling will be essential to define the precise molecular functions of these two GSTF proteins.

In summary, this study builds upon previous soybean GST genome-wide analyses by shifting the focus from general stress-responsive GST profiling to the functional characterization of anthocyanin-associated GSTF members. The identification and heterologous functional characterization of GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 provide new insight into the molecular basis of anthocyanin accumulation, stabilization, and vacuolar sequestration in soybean and identify candidate genes for future studies aimed at improving flavonoid accumulation and nutritional quality.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the soybean GST gene family, identifying 104 GmGST members with diverse structural and evolutionary features. Integrated phylogenetic, promoter, and expression analyses identified GmGSTF8 and GmGSTF11 as key candidates associated with anthocyanin accumulation. Their coordinated expression with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, predominant cytoplasmic distribution, and heterologous complementation of the Arabidopsis tt19 mutant collectively support their conserved anthocyanin-associated functions. These findings improve our understanding of anthocyanin accumulation, stabilization, and vacuolar sequestration in soybean and establish a foundation for future functional dissection and metabolic engineering of flavonoid pathways.
