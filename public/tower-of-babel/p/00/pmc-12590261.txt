Introduction

With the improvement of living standards and growing concerns over food safety and health, public interest in natural health supplements has increased substantially. As a result, the substitution of synthetic additives with bioactive plant-derived compounds has become a mainstream trend in the development of functional foods and pharmaceuticals. This shift not only reflects the concept of green health but also promotes the sustainable use of plant resources.

Nanhaia speciosa (Champ. ex Benth.) J. Compton & Schrire, also known as Millettia speciosa or Callerya speciosa, is a leguminous plant widely recognized for both its nutritional and medicinal properties. Its root, commonly referred to as “Niudali,” is rich in proteins, vitamins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and other phytochemicals. It has been traditionally used in medicinal cuisines, herbal wines, and decoctions in southern China [1]. Moreover, N. speciosa is a key component of numerous traditional Chinese medicines, such as Zhuangyao Jian Shen Pills, Guilong Ointment, and Huoluo Zhitong Pills, reputed for their effects in tonifying deficiency, nourishing the lungs, strengthening muscles and bones, and treating chronic conditions including lumbar strain, arthritis, tuberculosis, and bronchitis [2,3].

Hypaphorine (HYP), an indole alkaloid, is one of the principal active constituents of N. speciosa [2]. HYP has attracted considerable attention due to its diverse pharmacological properties. It exhibits acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity, suggesting potential applications in the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease [4]. Additionally, HYP has been shown to improve sleep quality by increasing non-REM sleep in animal models, highlighting its potential in managing sleep disorders [5]. Further studies have demonstrated its anti-inflammatory effects via modulation of key signaling pathways such as NF-κB, ERK, p38, and JNK [6,7]. HYP also regulates metabolic functions by suppressing adipogenesis and enhancing insulin sensitivity, which could be beneficial in managing obesity and type 2 diabetes [8]. Moreover, its inhibitory effect on osteoclastogenesis suggests potential in the prevention of osteoporosis and other inflammatory bone diseases [7]. These multifunctional bioactivities underscore HYP’s value as a promising lead compound for pharmaceutical development. Despite its therapeutic potential, current extraction methods for HYP predominantly rely on volatile organic solvents such as methanol and ethanol [9], which pose health and environmental hazards. Therefore, the development of efficient, green, and environmentally benign extraction strategies for HYP is both necessary and urgent.

In response to the principles of green chemistry, various eco-friendly solvents have emerged for the extraction of plant bioactives, including supercritical/subcritical water [10], supercritical CO2 [11], ionic liquids [12], and deep eutectic solvents (DESs) [13]. Supercritical CO2 extraction is indeed highly efficient and has been widely applied in natural product processing; however, its reliance on expensive and technically complex equipment limits its practicality for large-scale and cost-effective production of HYP [14]. Among these, DESs, first introduced by Abbott et al. in 2003, have gained significant attention due to their tunable physicochemical properties, low toxicity, biodegradability, thermal stability, and low volatility [15,16]. Composed of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) and donors (HBDs), DESs have demonstrated excellent solvation capability for alkaloids, often outperforming conventional solvents in extraction efficiency [17,18]. In particular, choline chloride-based DES systems have been widely studied and successfully applied for the extraction of alkaloids and phytochemicals [19]. However, for HYP, there is still a lack of an efficient, low-toxicity, green, and sustainable extraction system. Simultaneously, advancements in downstream separation technologies have introduced macroporous adsorption resins (MARs) as effective materials for purifying plant-derived bioactives. MARs are favored for their high stability, regeneration potential, cost-effectiveness, and selective adsorption of polar compounds [20,21]. The integration of DES extraction and MAR purification has been proven effective in enhancing both extraction yields and purity of bioactive compounds, offering a scalable and sustainable route for phytochemical production.

In recent years, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as a powerful intensification technology for green extractions [22]. By leveraging acoustic cavitation and localized energy release, UAE enhances solvent penetration, disrupts plant cell walls, and accelerates mass transfer, thereby significantly boosting extraction efficiency without the need for elevated temperatures or prolonged durations [23,24]. When combined with DESs, ultrasound further facilitates the dissolution of target alkaloids through enhanced molecular interactions, offering a synergistic effect ideal for eco-friendly extraction processes [25,26].

The present study aimed to develop a green, efficient, and scalable extraction and purification strategy for HYP from N. speciosa by integrating ultrasound-assisted DES extraction and MAR purification. Specifically, 37 different DES formulations were screened using UPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS for HYP extraction, followed by optimization of the ultrasound-assisted process via single-factor experiments and response surface methodology (RSM). The structural and mechanistic effects of ultrasound and DES on plant matrix disruption and solute–solvent interactions were evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Finally, purification studies employing seven types of MARs were conducted to identify optimal purification conditions for high-purity HYP recovery. This work provides a theoretical and practical basis for the industrial-scale green extraction of HYP and other plant alkaloids.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

The plant samples were collected from Yunfu, Guangdong, and identified by Dr. Ruifeng Ji from Guangdong Pharmaceutical University as N. speciosa. The roots of the plant were dried at 50 ℃ in an oven (WGL-230B, Tianjin Taist Instrument Co., Ltd., China) and ground using a high-speed grinder (800C, Yongkang Hongtai Machine and Electrical Co., Ltd., China). The powder was sieved through an 80-mesh sieve and stored at 4 ℃ until use.

The following chemicals were used: glucose (99.0 %), sucrose (99.0 %), tartaric acid (99.5 %) from Guangdong Guanghua Technology Co., Ltd.; choline chloride (98.0 %) from Shanghai McLin Biotech Co., Ltd.; lactic acid (85.0 %) from Shanghai Yuanye Technology Co., Ltd.; citric acid (99.5 %), anhydrous ethanol (99.7 %) from Guangzhou Paike Chemical Reagents; fructose (99.0 %) from Guangzhou Alishan Bio-tech Co., Ltd.; and macroporous resin from Nanjing Feishier Wodun Technology Co., Ltd. Other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Preparation of DES

DES was prepared using a heating and stirring method as described in previous studies [27]. The required ratios of HBA, HBD, and water were weighed and placed in a 50 mL beaker (Table 1). A magnetic stir bar was added, and the beaker was sealed. The beaker was placed on a magnetic stirrer, and the temperature was set at 80 ℃. Stirring continued until a clear and uniform liquid was formed.

Extraction procedures of HYP

A precise amount of 0.20 g of N. speciosa powder was placed in a 10 mL centrifuge tube. The corresponding DES or traditional solvents (water/ethanol) were added according to the experimental design. The mixture was briefly vortexed. Based on the single-factor experimental design, ultrasonic extraction was performed using a KQ-1000 ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China) with ultrasonic frequency and power set at 40 kHz, respectively. Extraction conditions included: DES water content (10 %, 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, 50 %), liquid-to-material ratio (10:1, 20:1, 30:1, 40:1, 50:1), ultrasonic temperature (50 ℃, 60 ℃, 70 ℃, 80 ℃, 90 ℃), ultrasonic time (20 min, 30 min, 40 min, 50 min, 60 min), and ultrasonic power (200 W, 400 W, 600 W, 800 W, 1000 W). After ultrasonic treatment, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min using a high-speed centrifuge (L550, Hunan Xiangyi Laboratory Instrument Development Co., Ltd., China). The supernatant was diluted 50 times and analyzed using UPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS. The extraction efficiency of HYP was evaluated as the indicator.

Quantitative analysis of HYP

Quantitative analysis of HYP was performed using a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system and an Orbitrap Exploris 120 quadrupole electrostatic field orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resolution was set at 15,000. Nitrogen was used as the sheath gas at a flow rate of 50 L/min, and auxiliary gas at 8 L/min, with a sweep gas flow rate of 1 L/min. The HESI ion source was used in positive ion mode, with the capillary voltage set at 3.5 kV, capillary temperature at 325 ℃, and auxiliary gas temperature at 350 ℃. Ions in the m/z range of 100.0–300.0 were scanned. A Thermo Fisher Scientific Bremen Hypersil GOLD AQ column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) was used for separation, with column temperature at 35 ℃. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (eluent A) and 0.1 % formic acid in water (eluent B). A 95 % A isocratic elution was used for 9 min, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and injection volume of 2 μL. The detection wavelength was set to 280 nm.

HYP was identified in this study using a dual-approach combining UPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS and retention time comparison with a reference standard. Different concentrations of HYP standard solutions were prepared, and a standard curve was established. HYP was identified by comparing the chromatographic profiles and retention times with the standard, and the concentration was calculated using TraceFinder 5.1 General Quan software based on the standard curve. Experimental design and statistical analysis

Experimental design was performed using Design-Expert 13.0. The extraction process was optimized using Box-Behnken design (BBD), based on the single-factor experimental results for liquid-to-material ratio, ultrasonic temperature, and ultrasonic time. All experiments were repeated at least three times. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2, and statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software, including one-way ANOVA. Statistical differences were considered significant at (P < 0.05), with different letters indicating significant differences.

HYP extraction based on microwave-assisted extraction with DES

Microwave-assisted extraction with DES (MAE–DES) was performed to compare with the UAE–DES process. The LA-Fru (1:1) DES system containing 40 % water was used as the extraction solvent. N. speciosa powder (0.20 g) was dispersed in 10 mL of DES and extracted in a microwave reactor at 400 W for 5 min, following the reported procedure [28]. After extraction, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for HYP determination according to the analytical method described in Section 2.4.

SEM

After extracting N. speciosa powder using the optimal extraction process, the sample was centrifuged and washed with water. It was then freeze-dried for 24 h using a freeze dryer (Alpha1 − 4LSC basic, Marin Christ, Germany). After gold coating, the surface morphology of the powder before and after extraction was observed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-7610FPlus, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) at magnifications of 1000x and 2000x, with an acceleration voltage of 15 kV.

FTIR

The infrared spectra of the optimal DES and its raw materials were measured using an FTIR spectrometer (IRAffinity-1S, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). The measurement range was from 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Solid samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method, and liquid samples were measured using a liquid cell method for infrared spectral scanning analysis.

Purification procedures of HYP

After optimized DES extraction, the extract was concentrated under reduced pressure to yield a viscous residue. A 9.00 g portion of the dried extract was dissolved in 40 % (w/w) aqueous ethanol to prepare the sample solution. For purification, a preconditioned macroporous resin column (bed volume: 50 mL; resin mass: 92.00 g) was used. The solution was loaded dynamically, and fractions were collected through gradient ethanol elution. The influence of ethanol elution concentrations (30 %, 40 %, 50 %, 60 %, 70 %, 80 %), elution flow rates (1.5 BV/h, 2 BV/h, 2.5 BV/h, 3 BV/h, 3.5 BV/h), column height-to-diameter ratios (21:1, 12:1, 7:1, 4:1), and elution volumes (2 BV, 3 BV, 4 BV, 5 BV, 6 BV) on the recovery rate of HYP was investigated to optimize the purification process. The recovery rate was calculated according to formula (5).

Recovery of DES and re-extraction of HYP

The optimized LA-Fru DES system (1:1 M ratio, 40 % water content) was recovered after the initial extraction following the purification procedure described in Section 2.10. The aqueous fractions containing DES were collected after resin purification, concentrated by rotary evaporation, and freeze-dried to remove water. The DES was regenerated by adding 40 % (w/w) water as described in Section 2.2. Subsequent extractions were carried out under the same optimized conditions described in Section 2.3, and the HYP content was determined using the same analytical method. The recyclability of the DES was evaluated over up to three consecutive reuse cycles, with each cycle performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Results and discussion

Development and validation of analytical method

HRMS analysis revealed a target peak in positive ion mode (Fig. S2) with an observed m/z = 247.14397 [M + H]+. Molecular formula assignment confirmed the peak corresponds to C14H19O2N2, with a calculated value of 247.14410. The deviation between the observed and theoretical values was only –0.55715 ppm, providing strong evidence for the identification of this peak as HYP. To comprehensively evaluate the extraction efficiency, UPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS was used to detect the extracted HYP. The standard HYP is shown in Fig. S1B. The results indicated that peak 1 in Fig. S1D corresponds to HYP, with its chemical structure shown in Fig. S1A. A calibration curve for HYP was established by plotting the peak area against concentration, with the regression equation presented in Fig. S1C. Methodological validation demonstrated that the developed method met the requirements for both qualitative and quantitative analysis in terms of stability, accuracy, and precision. (Table S1). An MF value closer to 1 indicates minimal matrix interference, suggesting that matrix components do not significantly affect the detection signal of the target analyte. In this study, the calculated MF value (Table S1) was 1.038, with a deviation of 3.80 %, which falls within the acceptable range. These results confirm the reliability of the developed UPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS method for the quantification of HYP, demonstrating that the method is not notably influenced by matrix effects or ion suppression.

Evaluation of DES performance in HYP extraction

Under the extraction conditions of 30 % water content in DES, ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, ultrasonic time of 60 min, ultrasonic power of 600 W, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1, the extraction efficiency of HYP by 37 different DESs is shown in Fig. 1A. Across all tested solvents, DESs using LA as the HBA demonstrated consistently higher extraction rates compared to those with ChCl and CA as HBA. This indicates that the acidic environment provided by LA favors the dissolution of alkaloids, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that lower pH facilitates alkaloid extraction [32]. Among them, the LA-Fru (1:1) system exhibited the highest extraction efficiency. Although similar DES formulations have been reported previously, our results demonstrate that LA-Fru was the most effective solvent for HYP extraction among all 37 formulations screened in this study, representing a novel and important finding. Because ChCl, as a quaternary ammonium salt, leads to relatively higher pH when used as an HBA, its extraction efficiency is inherently reduced compared with LA-based DESs. In addition, the study found that under conditions with an equal molar ratio of HBA to HBD, LA-Fru showed higher extraction efficiency than LA-Glu and LA-Suc. This can be attributed to viscosity differences: higher viscosity hinders mass transfer, thereby lowering efficiency [18]. Among the three saccharides, Fru produces the lowest viscosity, while Suc and Glu result in progressively higher viscosities [33]. Consequently, the LA-Fru DES achieves superior extraction efficiency by maintaining relatively low viscosity while preserving a favorable hydrogen-bonding environment for HYP dissolution. Among the LA-Fru DESs, the LA-Fru (1:1) system demonstrated a higher extraction efficiency compared to other molar ratios. As the Fru content increased, the viscosity of the DES increased, and the pH also rose, which led to a lower extraction efficiency for LA-Fru (1:2), LA-Fru (1:3), and LA-Fru (1:4) systems. While LA-Fru (2:1) had lower viscosity and pH compared to LA-Fru (1:1), its extraction efficiency was lower, likely due to the number of hydrogen bonds and entropy changes, as will be discussed further. Overall, these results justify the choice of LA-Fru (1:1) as the optimal solvent. Beyond its superior extraction performance, LA and Fru are natural, renewable, and low-toxicity components, aligning with the principles of green chemistry. Thus, LA-Fru not only achieved the best efficiency among 37 tested DESs but also offered practical advantages in terms of safety, sustainability, and applicability.

Optimization of extraction operating conditions

Effect of water content on HYP extraction

Under constant conditions ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, ultrasonic time of 60 min, ultrasonic power of 600 W, liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1, the influence of water content on HYP extraction efficiency was evaluated. The results, shown in Fig. 1B, revealed a significant increase in extraction efficiency as water content increased from 10 % to 40 %, with the highest extraction efficiency observed at 40 %. However, when the water content was increased to 50 %, the extraction efficiency decreased. This can be attributed to the higher viscosity of the LA-Fru DESs mixture, which makes it more difficult for the solvent to penetrate the material. A moderate amount of water reduces the viscosity, helping the DES penetrate better and interact with HYP [34]. However, excessive water content can disrupt hydrogen bonding between the components of the DES, reducing the interaction with HYP and lowering extraction efficiency [35]. Therefore, 40 % water content was selected as the optimal value for further studies.

Effect of ultrasonic temperature on HYP extraction

With 40 % water content in the DES, ultrasonic time of 60 min, ultrasonic power of 600 W, and liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1 kept constant, the effect of ultrasonic temperature on HYP extraction efficiency was studied. The results, shown in Fig. 1C, indicated that extraction efficiency increased with temperature and peaked at 80 ℃. However, when the temperature was further raised to 90 ℃, the extraction efficiency declined. This can be explained by the decrease in viscosity of the eutectic solvent with increasing temperature, which aids in the penetration of the solvent into the material. Additionally, the increased molecular thermal motion at higher temperatures further promotes the dissolution of HYP [36]. However, high temperatures may cause reactions between HYP and lactic acid or its dehydration products [37], resulting in a decline in extraction efficiency. Based on these findings, 80 ℃ was selected as the optimal extraction temperature.

Effect of ultrasonic time on HYP extraction

With 40 % water content, ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, ultrasonic power of 600 W, and liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1 maintained constant, the effect of ultrasonic time on HYP extraction efficiency was evaluated. The results, shown in Fig. 1D, indicated that the extraction efficiency gradually increased as extraction time increased, reaching its maximum at 40 min. However, extending the extraction time beyond 40 min led to a decrease in efficiency. Prolonged extraction can negatively affect the stability of bioactive compounds, potentially altering their chemical structure [38]. Therefore, a shorter extraction time, such as 40 min, is more effective for stabilizing the DES and achieving efficient extraction. Thus, 40 min was selected as the optimal extraction time.

Effect of liquid-to-solid ratio on HYP extraction

With 40 % water content, ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, ultrasonic time of 40 min, and ultrasonic power of 600 W kept constant, the effect of liquid-to-solid ratio on HYP extraction efficiency was evaluated. The results, shown in Fig. 1E, revealed that extraction efficiency increased as the liquid-to-solid ratio was raised from 10:1 to 20:1. The highest extraction efficiency was observed at a ratio of 20:1. However, further increasing the liquid-to-solid ratio led to a decrease in extraction efficiency. Literature suggests that mass transfer is positively correlated with the liquid-to-solid ratio, and increasing the ratio enhances the mass transfer between DES and HYP [39]. However, excessively high ratios may lead to the re-adsorption of extracted HYP, reducing extraction efficiency [40]. Therefore, 20:1 was selected as the optimal liquid-to-solid ratio.

Effect of ultrasonic power on HYP extraction

With 40 % water content, an ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, extraction time of 40 min, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1 kept constant, the effect of ultrasonic power on HYP extraction was examined. As shown in Fig. 1F, extraction efficiency increased markedly from 200 to 600 W, reaching a maximum at 600 W. Further increases in power produced no significant improvement. This trend aligns with the general understanding that higher ultrasonic power enhances cavitation, thereby improving solvent penetration, cell wall rupture, and mass transfer of target compounds [41]. However, excessive power may cause unstable cavitation and localized overheating, leading to compound degradation or reduced yields [42]. Thus, 600 W was selected as the optimal ultrasonic power for subsequent experiments.

Validation of optimal extraction conditions

Under the optimal conditions of ultrasonic temperature of 80 ℃, ultrasonic time of 40 min, and solid–liquid ratio of 20:1, three parallel validation experiments were conducted using 40 % water-containing LA-Fru (1:1). The results were compared to the traditional solvent extraction method for extracting HYP from N. speciosa, as shown in Fig. 3A. The experimental results indicated that under these conditions, the extraction rate of HYP was 3.15 mg/g, which closely matched the predicted extraction rate, with an error of only 0.83 %. This demonstrates that the optimal process parameters obtained are reliable. Compared to water and ethanol as extraction solvents, the extraction rate of HYP was increased by 105.19 % and 70.62 %, respectively. Therefore, the optimized extraction process demonstrates a higher efficiency compared to traditional solvent extraction methods. The optimized LA-Fru DES system (1:1 molar ratio) offers notable advantages in both operational efficiency and environmental sustainability. It functions under mild conditions without requiring high temperatures, high pressures, or complex equipment, which simplifies the extraction process. Moreover, its recyclability significantly reduces raw material consumption and waste generation. Composed of lactic acid and fructose, both naturally occurring and biocompatible substances, this DES is safe for use in food and pharmaceutical industries. These features highlight LA-Fru DES as a promising green solvent for sustainable extraction applications.

Comparative analysis of HYP extraction based on MAE–DES

To further evaluate the efficiency of the UAE–DES process, a comparison was made with MAE–DES. The results showed that MAE–DES yielded 2.07 mg/g of HYP, while UAE–DES under optimized conditions achieved a 52.17 % higher yield (Fig. 3A). Although microwave irradiation accelerates mass transfer, the cavitation and mechanical effects generated by ultrasound provided more effective cell wall disruption and release of intracellular compounds. UAE also offers simpler, more cost-effective, and more easily scalable equipment compared with microwave systems, highlighting its practical advantages in large-scale applications.

Morphological characterization of N. Speciosa residues

Fig. 3B–F show the morphological changes in N. speciosa powder before and after extraction using different solvents and techniques under SEM. Before extraction, the powder surface was smooth and even, with no apparent wrinkles or cracks. After MAE–DES extraction, the residues exhibited surface shrinkage, wrinkling, and partial cracking of the cell walls, indicating that microwave irradiation caused structural damage, though the extent of disruption was moderate compared with UAE–DES. Following UAE–DES extraction, the powder surface exhibited significant wrinkles, cracks, and severe structural damage, forming numerous irregular holes. This is likely due to the ultrasonic treatment, which disrupted the surface of the sample, promoting the leaching and release of HYP from the cells [43]. In comparison, UAE–Ethanol and UAE–H2O extractions caused only limited disruption. The residues presented localized fractures and a small number of pores, but no extensive perforation or large-scale collapse, which corresponds with their relatively low extraction efficiencies. SEM analysis therefore indicates that UAE–DES caused the most extensive cell wall rupture and pore formation, in line with its superior extraction yield.

FTIR spectroscopic analysis

FTIR spectroscopy provides information on vibrational changes in atomic groups (chemical bonds), making it an essential characteristic for analyzing DES [44]. In this study, FTIR spectra of LA, Fru, and LA-Fru (1:1) were obtained to investigate potential structural changes in chemical bonds, as shown in Fig. 4. The FTIR spectrum of LA shows a stretching vibration peak at 2980 cm−1 for C–H, at 3568 cm−1 for –OH, and at 1749 cm−1 for C=O. The FTIR spectrum of Fru shows a stretching vibration peak at 3407 cm−1 for –OH, at 1645 cm−1 for C=O, and at 2944 cm−1 for C–H. In comparison to the individual compounds, significant shifts in the FTIR absorption peaks were observed for LA-Fru (1:1). Specifically, the Fru–OH absorption peak shifted from 3407 cm−1 to 3200 cm−1, and the LA C=O and –OH absorption peaks shifted from 1749 cm−1 and 3568 cm−1 to 1731 cm−1 and 3529 cm−1, respectively. These changes indicate that no destruction of functional groups or formation of new functional groups occurred. The results suggest that LA and Fru likely formed a DES through hydrogen bonding between the –OH group on Fru and the –COOH group on LA, without altering their basic functional structures.

DFT analysis

The interaction between LA-Fru (1:1) and hydroxyproline (HYP) was simulated to explore the mechanism of HYP extraction using LA-Fru (1:1). The optimized geometric structure of LA-Fru (1:1) is shown in Fig. 5A–B. The computational results indicate that the oxygen atom of the carbonyl group (C=O) in LA interacts with the hydrogen atom in the hydroxyl group (–OH) of Fru, forming a hydrogen bond (O–H···O). Additionally, the hydrogen atom from the carboxyl group (–COOH) of LA interacts with the oxygen atom in the –OH group of Fru, forming another hydrogen bond (O–H···O). This result is consistent with the infrared (FTIR) analysis, confirming the presence of hydrogen bonding between the carboxyl group of LA and the hydroxyl group of Fru, which contributes to the formation of the DES.

The hydrogen bonds and distances formed between LA-Fru (1:1) and HYP are also shown in the figure. Three hydrogen bonds are formed between LA-Fru (1:1) and HYP: O–H···O, O–H···O, and C–H···O, with a binding energy of − 6.27 kcal/mol. For D-LA-Fru (1:1) and HYP, the hydrogen bonds formed are C–H···O, C–H···O, and O–H···O, with a binding energy of − 40.87 kcal/mol. Since DL-lactic acid was used in the experiment, its binding energy is considered the average of the two, approximately − 48.57 kcal/mol. The study suggests that the higher the absolute value of Eads, the stronger the solvent's ability to extract the target compound [45]. Compared to literature data [46], the binding energy of DL-lactic acid-Fru (1:1) for extracting HYP is relatively high, indicating that this DES has good extraction efficiency for HYP. Additionally, the binding energy of L-LA-Fru (1:1) is higher than that of D-LA-Fru (1:1), which may further enhance the extraction efficiency, though this hypothesis requires further verification.

Macroporous resin selection

The adsorption and desorption capacity of macroporous resins for the target compounds are key factors in their evaluation. In this study, macroporous resin was used to purify HYP under the optimal extraction conditions with DES. The performance of seven different macroporous resins was evaluated by comparing the adsorption amount, desorption amount, and recovery rate of HYP. Table. Under identical conditions, DM132 macroporous resin exhibited the highest adsorption, desorption, and recovery rates for HYP, while D101 and HP-20 resins showed poor performance. Generally, the adsorption and desorption capacity of macroporous resins are related to their physical and chemical properties [47]. The physical properties of the resins, including average pore diameter, pore volume, and surface area, play a significant role in their ability to adsorb similar substances [48]. Research has shown that resins with smaller pore diameters and larger surface areas are more effective in adsorbing compounds, resulting in better adsorption capacity [49]. DM132 resin demonstrated strong adsorption capacity, likely due to its smaller average pore diameter and larger specific surface area, allowing it to adsorb HYP from DES more effectively. In contrast, D101 and HP-20 exhibited lower adsorption capacities, possibly due to their larger pore diameters, which made it difficult to desorb HYP after adsorption [50]. The chemical properties of macroporous resins, especially polarity, play a key role in adsorption behavior [51]. A comparison between LK37 (weakly polar) and DM2 (non-polar) resins showed similar specific surface areas and pore sizes, yet their polarity differed. Despite this, both exhibited comparable adsorption capacities, desorption efficiencies, and recovery rates for HYP. These findings indicate that, under our experimental conditions, polarity had only a limited influence, while physical parameters such as pore size and surface area were more decisive in determining adsorption and desorption efficiency.

Among the tested resins, DM132, a cross-linked polystyrene-based resin, demonstrated superior performance compared to X-5, a non-cross-linked polystyrene copolymer. The enhanced efficiency of DM132 may be due to its smaller pore size and stable adsorption sites provided by cross-linking, which promote stronger interactions with target compounds. In contrast, the lack of cross-linking in X-5 likely results in less stable pore structures and weaker adsorption. Although functionalized resins and composite materials have attracted increasing attention for their higher selectivity in recent studies, their complex synthesis, high cost, and limited reusability restrict their broader applicability. In contrast, DM132 combines high efficiency, structural stability, and commercial availability, making it a more practical and sustainable option for large-scale purification. Therefore, the combined evaluation of pore structure, surface area, and resin stability provided the rationale for selecting DM132 as the most suitable resin for subsequent separation and purification experiments.

Purification single-factor results and discussion

Effect of elution solvent concentration

The effect of ethanol concentration on the recovery rate of HYP was investigated under constant conditions of elution flow rate (3 BV/h), column height-to-diameter ratio (21:1), and elution volume (5 BV). As shown in Fig. 6A, when the ethanol concentration was between 30 % and 50 %, the recovery rate of HYP increased significantly from 51.92 % to 82.24 %. However, when the ethanol concentration exceeded 50 %, the increase in recovery rate plateaued. This may be because 50 % ethanol effectively elutes HYP, and increasing the concentration does not significantly improve the recovery rate [52]. Additionally, higher ethanol concentrations may elute some impurities along with HYP. Therefore, 50 % ethanol was selected as the elution solvent for subsequent experiments.

Effect of elution flow rate

The effect of elution flow rate on the recovery rate of HYP was examined using 50 % ethanol as the elution solvent, with the column height-to-diameter ratio maintained at 21:1 and elution volume at 5 BV. As shown in Fig. 6B, the recovery rate of HYP increased gradually with the flow rate between 1.5 and 2.5 BV/h. The maximum recovery rate of 87.08 % was achieved at a flow rate of 2.5 BV/h. However, when the flow rate exceeded 2.5 BV/h, the recovery rate decreased. This phenomenon could be attributed to incomplete elution at lower flow rates [53], and insufficient contact time between the elution solvent and HYP on the resin at higher flow rates [54]. Therefore, an elution flow rate of 2.5 BV/h was chosen for subsequent experiments.

Effect of column height-to-diameter ratio

The influence of the column height-to-diameter ratio on the recovery rate of HYP was investigated under the conditions of 50 % ethanol as the elution solvent, 2.5 BV/h elution flow rate, and 5 BV elution volume. As shown in Fig. 6C, the recovery rate of HYP increased with the height-to-diameter ratio. The highest recovery rate of 86.35 % was obtained when the column height-to-diameter ratio was 21:1. This phenomenon can be explained by the theory of chromatography: longer columns provide a higher number of theoretical plates, which enhances separation efficiency [55]. Therefore, a column with a height-to-diameter ratio of 21:1 was selected for subsequent experiments.

Effect of elution volume

The effect of elution volume on the recovery rate of HYP was examined under constant conditions of 50 % ethanol as the elution solvent, 2.5 BV/h flow rate, and a column height-to-diameter ratio of 21:1. As shown in Fig. 6D, the recovery rate of HYP increased as the elution volume increased. The recovery rate reached its maximum when the elution volume was 4 BV. After 4 BV, the recovery rate stabilized. Once HYP was sufficiently desorbed, further increasing the elution volume did not significantly improve the recovery rate [53]. Therefore, an elution volume of 4 BV was selected as the optimal amount for subsequent purification processes.

Verification of optimal purification conditions

The optimized purification process, using DM132 resin, 50 % ethanol as elution solvent, 2.5 BV/h flow rate, 21:1 column height-to-diameter ratio, and 4 BV elution volume, was validated through three parallel experiments. The average recovery of HYP was 89.53 ± 3.23 %, and the purity was 79.57 %, demonstrating both high efficiency and reproducibility of the purification method. This comprehensive approach combines structural analysis, computational modeling, and experimental optimization to achieve efficient extraction and purification of HYP from N. speciosa, showcasing the power of DES and macroporous resin for natural product isolation.

Evaluation of HYP extraction efficiency using recycled DES

The LA-Fru DES system (1:1 M ratio, 40 % water content) was applied under optimized conditions for HYP extraction and subsequently recovered for reuse. In the first reuse, the recycled DES retained 94.21 % ± 2.74 % of the initial extraction yield. In the second reuse, the extraction efficiency decreased to 83.47 % ± 2.35 %, representing a reduction of more than 10 % relative to the first reuse. In the third reuse, the efficiency further declined to 75.33 % ± 1.82 %. These findings show that although the DES can be reused, its performance diminishes noticeably after the second cycle. The decline in efficiency is most likely due to two factors. First, polar compounds from the plant matrix accumulate in the solvent during repeated use, reducing its solvation and adsorption capacity. Second, variations in the recovery process alter the water content of the DES, which affects its viscosity, polarity, and hydrogen-bonding network. These results indicate that the LA-Fru system remains viable for a limited number of cycles while preserving acceptable efficiency. Although efficiency declined in later uses, the capacity to recycle DES still provides meaningful economic and environmental benefits, aligning with the principles of green chemistry.

Conclusion

A green, low-toxicity, and efficient strategy integrating UAE–DES extraction with MAR purification was successfully established for the extraction and enrichment of high-purity HYP from N. speciosa. Among 37 screened DESs, the LA-Fru (1:1) system exhibited superior extraction performance, significantly outperforming conventional solvents and MAE. The enhanced efficiency was attributed to the synergistic effects of ultrasound cavitation and hydrogen-bond interactions between DES and HYP. SEM analysis confirmed structural disruption of plant cells, while FTIR and DFT calculations demonstrated multiple hydrogen-bond formations, thereby elucidating the molecular-level extraction mechanism. Subsequent purification with DM132 resin achieved high selectivity and adsorption capacity, leading to improved purity and recovery. Moreover, the DES retained over 94 % efficiency after one recycling cycle, underscoring its potential for sustainable and cost-effective reuse.

This study represents the first systematic application of the UAE–DES–MAR framework to HYP, an indole alkaloid with unique polarity and structural complexity. The combined approach of comprehensive DES screening, mechanistic characterization, and rational resin selection provides a valuable reference for the green extraction and purification of alkaloids with challenging physicochemical properties. While the present work focused on process development and mechanistic elucidation, further pharmacological investigations are warranted to better explore the biological activities and therapeutic potential of HYP. Overall, this study expands the application scope of UAE–DES–MAR and offers a promising strategy for the sustainable extraction of alkaloids from traditional Chinese medicine and other natural product resources.
