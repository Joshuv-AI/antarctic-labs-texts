Natto Red Yeast Rice Supplement Regulates Lipid Profiles
Authors: Fangchao Liu, Shufeng Chen, Fengchao Liang, Jianxin Li, Keyong Huang, Yingxian Sun, Qing Wang, Shouling Wu, Mingfeng Ma, Ling Yu, Jinchun Lin, Xiangfeng Lu, Jianfeng Huang, Dongfeng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNatto Red Yeast Rice (NRYR) is a dietary supplement used to lower blood lipid. However, evidence on its effects on regulating lipid levels is limited.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to assess whether NRYR can lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and regulate other lipids, both alone and in addition to simvastatin.MethodsWe enrolled 1,110 participants who were randomized into 4 groups to receive a 3-month intervention of placebo, NRYR (1,950 mg/d), simvastatin (20 mg/d), or NRYR (1,950 mg/d) plus simvastatin (20 mg/d). The primary outcome was the change in LDL-C levels after 3 months, which were evaluated using linear mixed models. The primary analyses were conducted according to the modified intention-to-treat principle. Safety outcomes were also recorded.ResultsSignificant reductions in LDL-C levels were observed in the NRYR, simvastatin, and combination group. The differences in the absolute/percent change in LDL-C levels from baseline to 3 months were −21.02 mg/dL (95% CI: −27.98 to −14.06 mg/dL)/−13.21% (95% CI: −17.88% to −8.54%), −25.80 mg/dL (95% CI: −32.64 to −18.97 mg/dL)/−17.08% (95% CI: −21.67% to −12.50%), and −32.74 mg/dL (95% CI: −39.74 to −25.73 mg/dL)/−21.22% (95% CI: −25.92% to −16.52%), respectively, compared with placebo (all P < 0.001). Compared with simvastatin alone, adding NRYR demonstrated a significant decrease in LDL−C levels with a net absolute/percent change of −7.95 mg/dL (95% CI: −13.79 to −2.11 mg/dL; P = 0.008)/−5.05% (95% CI: −8.87% to −1.24%; P = 0.009) at 1 month, but the effect was not significant at 3 months. Adverse event rates were similar across the 4 groups.ConclusionsNRYR alone lowered LDL-C levels, and its combination with simvastatin was associated with numerically but not significantly lower LDL-C levels than using simvastatin alone. (ChiCTR2200064214, ChiCTR2200064215)Key Words: clinical trials, dyslipidemia, LDL, natto red yeast rice, statins

Dyslipidemia is a major public health issue worldwide and in China.1 According to data from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), as the main risk component of hyperlipidemia, has led to 3 million deaths worldwide in 2021, among which one-quarter were from China.2, 3, 4 As a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), the prevalence of high LDL-C in the Chinese population has increased significantly, rising from 5.6% in 2010 to 8.0% in 2018, and the control rate was as low as 4.0% in 2015.5,6 Effective measures for controlling dyslipidemia are urgently needed.

Statins are the first-line clinical medication for dyslipidemia, as recommended by guidelines, to reduce the risk of CVD.7,8 Nevertheless, elevated liver transaminase levels, muscle symptoms and other adverse events related to statin use may reduce treatment adherence and lead to higher discontinuation rates, an issue that is more prevalent in the Chinese population.9,10 The challenge lies in the contradiction where low-dose statin therapy fails to elicit the anticipated therapeutic effect, while moderate- or high-dose statin treatment is often accompanied by intolerable adverse drug reactions.9,11,12 Consequently, it is of paramount importance to identify a safe and effective pharmaceutical or dietary supplement that can be used synergistically in conjunction with statin treatment.

Red yeast rice is the product of yeast (Monascus purpureus) and contains a natural compound, Monacolin K, which shares the same chemical structure as the active ingredient in lovastatin.13,14 Natto is also a nutraceutical containing nattokinase, which has potential cardiovascular benefits such as lipid-lowering and fibrinolytic activity.15 Although previous studies have attempted to provide evidence on the lipid reduction effects of red yeast rice or nattokinase alone, limited studies have investigated their combined effects.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Furthermore, only a couple of studies have explored the lipid-lowering effect of supplement or nutraceutical containing red yeast rice combined with statins, but these studies have been limited by small sample sizes or population heterogeneity.22,23 Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether Natto Red Yeast Rice (NRYR) supplement has additional enhancing effects beyond that of statins.

Thus, we conducted a multicenter, double-blinded, randomized controlled trial of 1,110 participants, named ENTRY (Evaluation of NaTto Red Yeast Rice on Regulating Blood Lipid) Study, to investigate the effect of NRYR supplement (1,950 mg/d) both without and in combination with simvastatin (20 mg/d) on blood lipid levels, as well as the synergistic effect, among patients with LDL-C levels of 3.4 to 5.0 mmol/L, giving preference to those with a moderate (5%-10%) predicted CVD risk calculated by sex-specific risk prediction models from the Prediction for Atherosclerotic CVD Risk in China (China-PAR) project, aiming to provide evidence on its potential role in the management of dyslipidemia and the primary prevention of CVD.

Methods

Study design

This study was a multicenter, double-placebo, double-blinded, randomized controlled trial conducted in 6 centers in China. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to 1 of the following 4 intervention groups, including the placebo group (NRYR placebo + simvastatin placebo), the NRYR group (NRYR + simvastatin placebo), the simvastatin group (NRYR placebo + simvastatin), and the NRYR plus simvastatin group (NRYR + simvastatin). After baseline examination, all participants received intervention for 3 months, and the trial was overseen by a safety steering committee. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at both Fuwai Hospital (Approval No: 2020-1375) and Southern University of Science and Technology (Approval No: 20210149). The trial has also been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry database (ChiCTR2200064214, ChiCTR2200064215), and the protocol has been published elsewhere.24 All participants provided written informed consent. The data sets analyzed in this paper are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study participants

We recruited all the trial participants from 6 sites in China, including Nangong (Hebei Province), Tangshan (Hebei Province), Fenyang (Shanxi Province), Shenzhen (Guangdong Province), Fuzhou (Fujian Province), and Shenyang (Liaoning Province) by distributing promotional leaflets and posters, posting recruitment information on the internet, and conducting community screenings. Participants were eligible if they were 35 to 74 years of age and had a fasting LDL-C level ranging from 3.4 to 5.0 mmol/L without taking lipid-lowering medications. Priority was given to those with a moderate (5%-10%) 10-year predicted risk of CVD, which was calculated based on models developed by the China-PAR project.25,26 Variables used in the China-PAR assessment tool included age, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), diabetes mellitus, waist circumference, current smoker, geographic region, urbanization, and family history of CVD. Exclusion criteria included familial hypercholesterolemia or secondary hyperlipidemia (eg, hyperlipidemia resulting from hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, and systemic lupus erythematosus); liver and kidney disease or other chronic diseases; body mass index ≥30 kg/m2; current use of any type of antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulants, corticosteroids, antiretroviral, or antipsychotic medications; concurrent participant in another clinical trial; pregnancy or lactation; or being deemed unsuitable for participation by the investigators.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was conducted via an online central randomization system, and each eligible participant was given a unique random code, which was generated by an independent blinded statistician and embedded in the Interactive Web Response System. Sealed envelopes containing allocation information were provided to researchers at each center for emergency use. This study employed a double-blinded design.

Intervention procedure

The simvastatin (20 mg/tablet) used in this trial is manufactured by Merck Sharp and Dohme Ltd (United Kingdom). The NRYR (325 mg/capsule) was manufactured by BYHEALTH Co, Ltd. Placebos identical in appearance and smell were produced and uniformly packaged according to a standard process. Study drug kits were prepared by packers according to a drug code provided by the independent and blinded statistician. The simvastatin tablets were supplied by Fuwai Hospital. Simvastatin, NRYR, and their matching placebos were repackaged and shipped directly to the local sites. Following randomization, participants received either simvastatin or a matching placebo (1 tablet at bedtime daily), and either NRYR or a matching placebo (3 capsules twice/d after breakfast and dinner) for the period of 3 months under the supervision of a physician.

Blood sample collection and biochemical analysis

Blood samples fasting for at least 10 hours were drawn from participants and centrifuged within 3 hours of collection to separate plasma and serum. All samples were frozen at the field centers and then transported under frozen conditions to the central laboratory at Fuwai Hospital. Serum samples were analyzed for biochemical indicators, including lipid profiles, glucose, creatinine, liver transaminases, and creatine kinase, following standard procedures on a Beckman Coulter AU680 Chemistry Analyzer. LDL-C and HDL-C levels were measured using reagents from Minaris Medical Co, Ltd, while all other indicators were assayed using reagents manufactured by BioSino Bio-Technology and Science Inc.

Quality control

This study implemented rigorous quality control measures across all stages, including product preparation, participant enrollment, intervention, data collection, and laboratory testing. All NRYR products comply with the required standards. Quality control of the raw materials (nattokinase freeze-dried powder and functional red yeast rice powder) was performed by BY-HEALTH Co, Ltd, confirming a nattokinase content of ≥2,000 FU/g and a lovastatin content of 1.4% to 1.7%. The products are manufactured in a Good Manufacturing Practice–certified facility operated by BY-HEALTH Co, Ltd. The production process, comprising weighing and preparing raw materials, blending, and capsule filling, is simple and does not alter the raw materials composition. Each batch conforms to the health food specification of the National Medical Products Administration (Approval No: Guo Shi Jian Zhu G20200358), guaranteeing a lovastatin content of 0.308∼0.512 g per 100 g of product. The products are sealed and stored in a cool, dry place, with a 2-year shelf life. Data were collected using electronic data capture system that retained all modification records. During the intervention period, participants were asked to maintain medication log cards. Treatment adherence was assessed by pill count, and good adherence was defined as consumption of at least 80% of the provided products. In the laboratory testing process, the central laboratory has participated in the Lipid Standardization Program of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Duplicate serum samples with known concentrations (high and low values) were analyzed, and both intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation were calculated to ensure analytical reliability.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the change from baseline in LDL-C level at 3 months. The secondary outcomes included changes in other lipid profiles (TC, triglyceride [TG], HDL-C, non–HDL-C, small dense [sd]LDL-C, apolipoprotein [Apo] A1, ApoB, and ApoE), and the 10-year risk of CVD. Safety outcomes include elevated liver transaminases (defined as alanine transaminase and/or aspartate aminotransferase levels more than 3 times the upper limit of normal), elevated serum creatinine (defined as >133 μmol/L), hepatic or muscular symptoms (hepatalgia, myalgia, myositis, rhabdomyolysis, hemoglobinuria, muscle discomfort and/or weakness), an elevated creatine kinase (defined as more than 5 times the upper limit of normal), and gastrointestinal symptoms (abdominal pain, diarrhea and/or indigestion).11,27,28 In addition, participants are asked to report any adverse experiences throughout the trial.

Statistical analysis

Given an average difference in LDL-C reduction of 0.3 mmol/L between the placebo group and the NRYR group after 3-month interventions, a total of 588 participants were required for these 2 groups with a 2-tailed significance level of 0.05, a power of 0.90, and considering a 20% dropout rate. Additionally, assuming an interaction of 0.35 mmol/L and an average difference in LDL-C reduction of 0.8 mmol/L between the double placebo group and the simvastatin combined with NRYR group, with the same significance level and power, a total of 992 subjects were finally required for the trial after accounting for a 20% dropout rate.

For baseline characteristics of participants, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were presented as frequency with percentages. The primary analyses were conducted according to the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle, which excluded participants who did not receive the intervention after randomization.29 Linear mixed models were used to evaluate the effects of the NRYR supplement, simvastatin, and their combination on lipid profiles at 1 and 3 months. For both the primary and secondary outcomes, the models included fixed effects for the 3-way interactions of simvastatin, NRYR, and time, with random effects of interindividual intercepts. If a significant multiplicative interaction was detected, the respective effects were presented by comparing each intervention group with the placebo group, as well as by comparing the NRYR plus simvastatin group with the simvastatin group. To adjust for multiple comparisons, a Holm-Bonferroni test (step-down procedure) was used. Results were presented as least-squares means with their 95% CIs. The incidence of adverse events was also reported by group. In prespecified sensitivity analysis, the patterns of missing data were examined to assess the plausibility of a random missingness mechanism, and missing outcome data were handled by chained multiple imputation.30 Specifically, 5 imputed data sets were generated, and the combined results were estimated using the Rubin’s rules.31 The per-protocol set analysis was also conducted, which included only participants who adhered to the protocol during the trial period. Furthermore, a complete-case data analysis was performed. To account for between-center heterogeneity, we also conducted another sensitivity analysis using random intercepts for individual nested within center level in the models.

Detailed statistical analysis plan could be found in the Supplemental Material. The analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute), with the PROC MIXED, PROC MI, PROC MIANALYZE procedure. Statistical significance was considered at a 2-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Trial participants

Overall, among the 4,713 participants screened at the 6 centers, 1,136 eligible participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 4 groups: placebo (n = 285), NRYR (n = 284), simvastatin (n = 283), and the NRYR and simvastatin (n = 284). Of these, 1,110 participants received the intervention and were included in the mITT analysis. Over the 3-month period, 883 participants completed the study per protocol (Figure 1). As shown in Table 1, the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well-balanced across the groups. The mean age was 56.2 ± 8.5 years, the mean LDL-C level was 150.5 ± 30.8 mg/dL, and the mean China-PAR risk score was 6.1% ± 3.9% with the median of 5.3% (P25, P75: 3.1%, 8.7%), 28.7% of the participants were men, and 38.3% were at moderate risk. A total of 35 participants (3.2% of total population) took <80% of the provided products.

Primary outcomes

A significant interaction was identified (P < 0.001); therefore, the results for their respective effects are presented. At the 1- and 3-month follow-up visits, LDL-C levels decreased significantly in all intervention groups compared with the placebo group. Compared with placebo, the mean differences in the absolute/percent change in LDL-C levels from baseline to 3 months were −21.02 mg/dL (95% CI: −27.98 to −14.06 mg/dL)/−13.21% (95% CI: −17.88% to −8.54%) for the NRYR group, −25.80 mg/dL (95% CI: −32.64 to −18.97 mg/dL)/−17.08% (95% CI: −21.67% to −12.50%) for the simvastatin group, and −32.74 mg/dL (95% CI: −39.74 to −25.73 mg/dL)/−21.22% (95% CI: −25.92% to −16.52%) for the NRYR and simvastatin group, respectively (P < 0.001 for all comparisons). Of note, compared with the simvastatin group, the combination of NRYR and simvastatin resulted in a significantly greater reduction in LDL-C levels at 1 month, with a mean difference in absolute/percent change of −7.95 mg/dL (95% CI: −13.79 to −2.11 mg/dL; P = 0.008)/−5.05% (95% CI: −8.87% to −1.24%; P = 0.009). Furthermore, although a greater reduction was also observed at 3 months, it did not reach statistical significance, with a mean difference of −6.93 mg/dL (95% CI: −13.90 to 0.03 mg/dL; P = 0.051)/−4.14% (95% CI: −8.81% to 0.54%; P = 0.083) (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses using mITT imputation, per-protocol set data sets, complete-case data sets and with additional adjustment for center effects all yielded similar results (Supplemental Tables 1 to 4). Figure 2 demonstrated the individual participants’ percentage changes in LDL-C levels from baseline. At the 3-month follow-up, 9.4% of participants in the combination group achieved a ≥50% reduction in LDL-C, a proportion significantly higher than the 4.3% observed in the simvastatin group (P = 0.028).

Secondary outcomes

The use of NRYR, simvastatin, or their combination led to significant reductions in TC, TG, non–HDL-C, sdLDL-C, and ApoB levels at both 1 and 3 months, compared with placebo. Furthermore, HDL-C and ApoA1 levels significantly increased in 3 intervention groups (Figure 3). Specifically, compared with placebo, the mean percentage changes in TC at 3 months were −9.26% (95% CI: −12.26% to −6.27%; P < 0.001), −11.87% (95% CI: −14.80% to −8.93%; P < 0.001), and −14.33% (95% CI: −17.34% to −11.32%; P < 0.001) in the NRYR group, the simvastatin group, and the combination group, respectively. Furthermore, the combination intervention resulted in a greater reduction in TC than simvastatin alone at 1 month, with a between group difference of −3.33% (95% CI: −5.77% to −0.89%; P = 0.008) (Supplemental Table 5). Similarly, the combination group showed a significantly greater reduction in non–HDL-C than the simvastatin group at 1 month, with a mean difference of −4.44% (95% CI: −7.58% to −1.30%; P = 0.006) (Supplemental Table 6). Changes in other lipid profiles were largely similar between these 2 groups over the 3-month period (Supplemental Tables 7 to 12). The results of sensitivity analyses were generally consistent with the primary analyses (Supplemental Tables 5 to 12).

Adverse events

Safety outcomes are summarized in Table 3. During the intervention period, the incidence of adverse events was numerically higher in the NRYR and simvastatin group than in the simvastatin group (13 [4.7%] vs 9 [3.2%]), although the difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of any specific type of adverse events across the 4 groups. Changes in laboratory safety indicators at 1 and 3 months were also similar across all groups.

Discussion

In this 3-month trial, NRYR significantly reduced LDL-C levels compared with placebo, and the combination of NRYR with simvastatin yielded a numerically but nonsignificant further reduction in LDL-C beyond using simvastatin alone. Similar beneficial effects were observed on other lipid profiles, including reductions in TC, TG, non–HDL-C, sdLDL-C, and ApoB, as well as increases in HDL-C and ApoA1, across the 3 intervention groups. These findings indicate that NRYR has the potential to lower lipid levels for individuals with mild-to-moderate elevated LDL-C or a moderate risk of CVD (Central Illustration).

As a dietary supplement, NRYR has gained increasing attention in recent years as an effective, safe, and economical option for lipid management.32 Several clinical trials with small sample size have assessed its lipid-regulating effects. For example, a 6-month randomized double-blinded study of 47 patients with dyslipidemia showed that a supplement containing nattokinase and red yeast rice significantly reduced TC, LDL-C, and TG levels, and increased HDL-C.19 Another randomized controlled trial involving 113 participants found that the nattokinase-monascus supplement significantly lowered TC and LDL-C, while it did not affect TG, HDL-C, and carotid intima-media thickness.18 Neither study reported significant differences in adverse effects between groups.18,19 However, study participants in previous studies were mainly patients with dyslipidemia. In contrast, our study included individuals with mildly elevated LDL-C levels between 3.4 and 4.1 mmol/L as well as participants with LDL-C exceeding 4.1 mmol/L, prioritizing those with a moderate 10-year CVD risk. Therefore, our findings provide additional evidence that NRYR also confers lipid-lowering benefits to individuals with only mild-to-moderate LDL-C elevation or a moderate CVD risk profile.

A major distinctive feature of our randomized clinical trial is its evaluation of the lipid-lowering effects of combining NRYR with statins compared with statin monotherapy. As recommended by international guidelines, high-intensity statin therapy is the cornerstone and first-line choice for lipid management, as well as the foundation of combination regimens.33,34 However, statin intolerance affects approximately 7.46% of Chinese population, and often prevents patients from achieving their lipid targets.11 In this study, we found that adding NRYR to statin therapy yielded additional LDL-C reductions of 5.05% at 1 month and 4.14% at 3 months, without a significant increase in adverse events.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to investigate the combined lipid-lowering effect of NRYR and statins in a Chinese population. Although previous studies have compared RYR-containing supplements plus statin therapy to statin monotherapy, the synergistic role of nattokinase when combined with natural products on regulating lipid profiles merits another study to determine the health benefits.35,36 The strengths of our study include its multicenter design, large sample size, rigorous quality control, and high adherence to the intervention. The factorial allocation allowed for a direct comparison of the independent and combined effects of NRYR, providing valuable evidence for its potential clinical application.19,37,38

Study limitations

First, the 3-month intervention and follow-up period precluded assessment of effects on CVD events, and the long-term cardiovascular benefits of the intervention warrant further investigation. Second, our findings could not be generalized to patients with body mass index ≥30 kg/m2. Third, despite the relatively high dropout rate, the sample size was calculated to account for a 20% attrition, thus ensuring adequate statistical power. The last, the molecular mechanism underlying the lipid-lowering effect of NRYR requires further exploration.

Conclusions

This trial demonstrates that NRYR supplement alone provides obvious lipid-lowering benefits in a Chinese population with mild-to-moderate LDL-C elevation or a moderate predicted CVD risk, and its combination with simvastatin was associated with numerically but not significantly lower LDL-C levels than using simvastatin alone. Our findings fill an evidence gap regarding the use of nutritional supplement for lipid management and support its potential role in the primary prevention of CVD.
