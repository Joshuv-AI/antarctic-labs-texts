Pulmonary sclerosing pneumocytoma: a case report of a challenging preoperative diagnosis
Authors: Keisuke Tetsumoto, Teruya Komatsu, Akira Hara, Takenobu Gomyo, Tatsuo Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Pulmonary sclerosing pneumocytoma (PSP), formerly known as sclerosing hemangioma, is a rare tumor that is typically found incidentally and can closely mimic malignancy on imaging and biopsy, making it challenging to distinguish from lung malignancy.Presentation of case:A 67-year-old man presented with an incidental finding of a nodule in the left upper lobe. Chest computed tomography revealed a 2.2-cm, well-circumscribed nodule adjacent to the pulmonary hilum of the upper lobe. Bronchoscopic biopsy suggested adenocarcinoma, and the patient underwent left upper lobectomy with lymph node dissection via posterolateral thoracotomy. Final histopathological findings resulted in a diagnosis of PSP.Discussion:Accurate preoperative diagnosis of PSP is difficult because the tumor contains heterogeneous histologic components, and limited biopsy samples may capture only areas resembling malignancy. As illustrated in this case, preoperative biopsy may lead to misdiagnosis, and complete resection is often required for a definitive diagnosis.Conclusion:This case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of PSP and underscores the importance of considering PSP when evaluating solitary pulmonary nodules. A brief review of the relevant literature is also provided.

Introduction

Pulmonary sclerosing pneumocytoma (PSP) is a rare tumor accounting for approximately 1% of lung tumors and is most commonly observed in middle-aged female patients in Asia[1]. PSP is frequently detected incidentally on chest radiography or computed tomography (CT) and may be mistaken for adenocarcinoma, carcinoid, or other solid tumor malignancies[2,3]. Herein, we describe a case of PSP that was definitively diagnosed only after surgical resection, despite a preoperative diagnosis of adenocarcinoma, which, due to its malignant implication, directly informed the decision for a more extensive surgical resection: lobectomy with lymph node dissection. This underscores the critical impact of diagnostic accuracy in managing solitary pulmonary nodules. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE checklist[4].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old man was referred to National Hospital Organization Nagara Medical Center after an abnormal shadow was detected on a chest radiograph, despite the absence of symptoms. The patient’s medical history included hypertension, glaucoma, and spinocerebellar degeneration, for which he was undergoing follow-up. The patient had a 30-pack-year history of smoking and quit at 50 years of age. The family history was unremarkable.

Peripheral blood counts and biochemical examinations, including serum tumor markers, were within normal limits: carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), 2.1 ng/mL; cytokeratin fragment 21-1 (CYFRA), 1.2 ng/mL; and pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP), 49.6 pg/mL. Physical examination findings were unremarkable.

Contrast-enhanced chest CT showed a 2.2-cm well-circumscribed nodule adjacent to the pulmonary hilum in the left upper lobe, without swelling in any lymph node stations or distant metastases (Fig. 1). Whole-body positron emission tomography showed no significant fluorodeoxyglucose uptake. Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) indicated adenocarcinoma in histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses (Supplemental Digital Content Figure, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A90). The patient underwent a left upper lobectomy with lymph node dissection via posterolateral thoracotomy. Upon entering the left thoracic cavity, no pleural effusion or dissemination was observed. The nodule was well-circumscribed rather than infiltrative but was located adjacent to the left lower bronchus, prompting a left upper lobectomy (Fig. 2a and b). Histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses showed epithelial cells expressing anion exchanger 1/anion exchanger 3 (AE1/AE3), cytokeratin 7 (CK7), and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF-1), arranged in papillary, tubular, and solid patterns (Fig. 3a–d). The sample showed a heterogeneous pattern of hot spots, and Ki-67 staining was overall less than 5%, with focal areas showing 10–15% positivity. These findings supported a final pathological diagnosis of PSP. No lymph node metastasis was observed. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient has remained recurrence-free during 1 year of follow-up.

Discussion

PSP was first described as pulmonary sclerosing hemangioma (PSH) by Liebow and Hubbell in 1956[5]. In 2015, the World Health Organization officially renamed this entity PSP and classified it as a pulmonary adenoma, recognizing its intermediate malignant potential in its histological classification and distinguishing it from typical benign tumors[6]. PSP is predominantly observed in adults over 50 years of age, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 5:1[7].

Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging because PSP exhibits diverse histological patterns. Four major components – papillary, solid, sclerosing, and hemorrhagic – may appear in varying proportions[8]. When biopsy specimens contain only one of these components, diagnosis is difficult[9]. Shang et al reported that solid and papillary growth patterns most commonly lead to misdiagnosis[10], and patients correctly diagnosed preoperatively with PSP typically show two or more histological types in their biopsy samples[11]. In the present case, the EBUS-TBNA specimen was diagnosed as an adenocarcinoma, likely because of a predominantly papillary component in the sampled area.

Iyoda et al reported that only 7 out of 26 patients with PSP were preoperatively diagnosed using percutaneous needle or transbronchial biopsy[12]. Depending on the region sampled, cytology may range from hypocellular, bloody, or sclerotic to hypercellular[7], underscoring the need for an adequately sized specimen for definitive preoperative or intraoperative diagnosis. Indeed, another case diagnosed with PSP by EBUS-TBNA had samples large enough to allow immunohistochemical evaluation[13].

At present, no consensus exists regarding the optimal surgical procedure for PSP. Wedge resection or anatomical lung resection like segmentectomy or more, with sufficient margin, is appropriate[3]. The decision for anatomical lobectomy in this case was primarily driven by the preoperative suspicion of adenocarcinoma, highlighting how diagnostic uncertainty can affect surgical strategy. Although PSP typically has a favorable prognosis, cases of recurrence and lymph node metastasis have been reported[14,15].

Moreover, as in the present case, the partially elevated Ki-67 index raised concerns regarding malignant potential, highlighting the importance of careful postoperative surveillance, similar to that recommended for lung cancer. While typically low, elevated Ki-67 indices have been reported in some PSP cases and may correlate with a higher potential for recurrence, reinforcing the need for long-term surveillance even after complete resection[16].

Conclusion

We described a case of PSP that was diagnosed only after surgical resection. PSP is difficult to diagnose preoperatively, and a specimen of sufficient size is critical for an accurate diagnosis.
