Retrospective cross-sectional study of clinical and laboratory profiles of paediatric tuberculosis at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia (2018–2021)
Authors: Tekae Gebru Girmay, Tariku Yigremachew Gossaye, Haftu Hailu Gebrihiet, Niguse Tsegay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric Tuberculosis (TB) remains a diagnostic challenge owing to its paucibacillary nature and diverse clinical presentation. We aimed to characterise the clinical pattern, diagnostic yields, and predictors of disease forms among children managed at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH), Mekelle, Ethiopia.ObjectiveTo describe the disease form distribution, clinical features, and laboratory profiles of pediatric tuberculosis at a tertiary Ethiopian hospital, and to identify independent predictors of extrapulmonary and disseminated disease using multivariable logistic regression.MethodsWe conducted an institution-based, retrospective analytical cross-sectional study of 123 children (< 18) diagnosed with TB at ACSH between May 2018 and April 2021. From 180 screened charts, we excluded 57 cases (prior treatment n = 27, incomplete records n = 20, insufficient evidence n = 10). Using validated data extraction checklist, we analysed sociodemographics, clinical presentation, nutritional status, comorbidities, laboratory results (GeneXpert, ESR, haemoglobin), and TB disease forms via SPSS version 23, applying bivariable and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe mean age was 8.8 ± 5.3 years; adolescents (11–17 years) comprised 42.3% (n = 52). Females were slightly predominant (52%). Common presenting symptoms included fever (78%), weight loss (71.5%), and cough (55.5%). Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) affected 37.7% (n = 40/106), with the highest burden in children aged < 5 years (45.7%). Anaemia was prevalent in 62.7% (n = 74/118); ESR was elevated in 84% (n = 89/106). Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) was the most common form (43.9%; 95% CI 35.2–52.9). GeneXpert positivity was 31.3%. Younger age was significantly associated with EPTB (p < 0.001); children aged 0–4 years had over 3.5 times higher odds of EPTB compared with adolescents (AOR 3.57; 95% CI 1.28–10.00; p = 0.015).ConclusionPaediatric TB in this referral setting is characterised by high rates of extrapulmonary and disseminated disease, severe malnutrition, and low bacteriological confirmation. Younger age was independently associated with disease dissemination, a finding consistent with the immunological characteristics of early childhood. Early, age-stratified screening and adoption of more sensitive diagnostic tools are urgently needed in similar high-burden settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07391-4.

Introduction

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains the leading infectious cause of death globally, with an estimated 10.8 million incident cases and 1.4 million deaths in 2023, including approximately 450,000 children; reaffirming TBs position as deadliest infectious disease surpassing HIV/AIDS [1–5]. The burden is disproportionally high in low and middle income countries (LMICs), particularly Sub-Saharan Africa which contribute to more than 95% of global TB cases with incidence rates exceeding 100 per 100,000 population [5, 6]. The convergence of HIV epidemics, malnutrition, poverty, and health system gaps exacerbates TB transmission and poor outcome [6]. Globally, 6.6% of TB patients are co-infected with HIV, but in Africa this figure rise to 20%. TB accounts for 68% of deaths among people living with HIV, highlighting the synergistic burden of duel epidemics [5, 7, 8]. Furthermore, the emergence of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) has intensified global challenge in TB control, with 3.5% of new and 18% of retreatment cases exhibiting resistance, resulting in an estimated 160,000 deaths annually [5, 6].

Children bear a disproportionate and frequently overlooked burden of TB. Paediatric TB is often underdiagnosed and underreported due to its non-specific clinical presentation, paucibacillary nature and difficulties in specimen collection. Only 10–15% of childhood TB cases are smear-positive, while confirmatory tests like GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra detect just 50–60% of clinically diagnosed cases even with optimized sampling condition [9–11]. Younger children (< 8 years) more commonly present with extrapulmonary or disseminated disease forms, while adolescents tends to develop adult-type pulmonary disease with cavitation [4, 12]. Paediatric TB cases constitute approximately 11% of the global TB caseload [13]; however, only about 49% of these cases are diagnosed and reported globally, with the diagnostic gap being substantially larger among children under five years of age, in whom an estimated 58% of cases go undetected [5]. These figures indicate that the commonly cited 20–30% underreporting estimate likely represents a conservative lower bound; more recent surveillance data suggest diagnostic and reporting gaps of 40–58%, driven principally by the paucibacillary nature of paediatric disease, difficulty obtaining adequate sputum samples, non-specific clinical presentations frequently misattributed to other childhood illnesses, and limited access to advanced diagnostics such as GeneXpert MTB/RIF and chest radiography in resource-limited settings [5, 13].

Ethiopia is among the top five high-burden countries for TB, TB/HIV co-infection, and DR-TB, with approximately 144,000 cases, and 24,000 deaths annually (2023 estimates). In 2022 there was an estimated 119,000 TB/HIV co-infections, contributing to a significant morbidity and mortality [14, 15]. The Tigray region, with a population of 7.3 million people, faces amplified challenges due to ongoing conflict, displacement, and disruptions of health system, malnutrition spikes, likely shifting TB pattern to severe EPTB at referral centers [16]. Although Ethiopia has achieved a 42% decline in TB incidence since 1990 (177 per 100,000 in 2016), substantial gaps remain in pediatric case detection, diagnosis and management [17].

Diagnostic challenges are further compounded by the limited availability of gold-standard investigations. While sputum AFB microscopy, culture, GeneXpert, chest radiography, and histopathology are currently in use, their diagnostic sensitivity in children remains low owing to the paucibacillary nature of paediatric disease. Age-related differences are notable: just 0.5% of children aged 0–4 years are smear-positive, compared with 14% among those aged 5–14 years [18]. This highlights a persistent diagnostic gap, particularly in young children, where diagnosis continues to rely heavily on meticulous clinical history-taking, physical examination, and epidemiological knowledge. A sound understanding of local disease patterns and clinical presentations is crucial for early case identification, timely treatment initiation, and control of further transmission.

Prior studies describe adult or mixed-age TB but underexplore paediatric clinical/laboratory pattern in tertiary settings, hindering context specific guideline. This gap is critical in conflict zone, where referral bias may inflate EPTB and inform integrated nutritional/HIV screening. This study aimed to describe the clinical and laboratory profiles of paediatric TB at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, a referral centre in northern Ethiopia, to inform context-specific diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Objective of the study

General objective

To describe the disease form distribution, clinical features, and laboratory profiles of paediatric tuberculosis cases at ACSH (2018–2021), and to identify independent predictors of extrapulmonary and disseminated disease.

Specific objectives

To determine tuberculosis forms (pulmonary vs. EPTB) and its anatomical site of involvement.

To describe presenting symptoms, sign, and comorbidities.

To report haematology, microbiologic, and imaging findings.

To identify independent predictors of extrapulmonary and disseminated tuberculosis using multivariable logistic regression.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and study period

We conducted an institution-based, retrospective analytical cross-sectional study at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH), a 500-bed tertiary referral centre serving the Tigray, Afar, and northern Amhara regions of Ethiopia (approximate catchment population: 8 million). Data were collected between May and July 2021, covering medical records from 1 May 2018 to 30 April 2021.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

All children aged < 18 years diagnosed with any form of TB (pulmonary or extrapulmonary) attending ACSH paediatric services (outpatient clinics, inpatient wards, or ICU) during the study period were eligible for inclusion. All TB diagnoses were recorded at or prior to treatment initiation.

Exclusion criteria

Children were excluded if they had a prior history of anti-TB treatment (n = 27), incomplete clinical documentation (n = 20), or insufficient diagnostic evidence to confirm a TB diagnosis (n = 10).

Sample size determination

All eligible paediatric TB cases identified during the three-year study period were enrolled after applying the pre-specified exclusion criteria, constituting a total population (consecutive) sample of available records (n = 123). A sample size calculation was performed post-hoc to confirm statistical adequacy: using the formula n = Z²p(1 − p)/W² (Z = 1.96; p = 0.5 as a conservative assumption applied in the absence of a prior local prevalence estimate for paediatric EPTB at tertiary referral facilities; W = 0.05) with finite population correction applied (N = 180), the minimum required sample was 123, confirming that complete enrollment of eligible cases provided sufficient power to detect associations of clinical interest.

Operational/standard definitions

Clinically diagnosed tuberculosis

A clinically diagnosed TB case was defined as a child with clinical, laboratory, or radiological findings consistent with TB, but without microbiological or histological confirmation, in whom the treating physician initiated anti-TB therapy in accordance with the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health National TB, Leprosy, and HIV Prevention and Control Program guidelines [19], which are aligned with WHO recommendations. For such cases, diagnosis required the presence of at least two of the following: (i) a compatible clinical history including documented close contact with an infectious TB case; (ii) tuberculin skin test positivity (where performed); (iii) compatible chest radiographic findings; or (iv) clinical and radiological response to empirical anti-TB therapy.

Tuberculosis disease forms

Pulmonary TB (PTB) was defined as TB affecting the lung parenchyma, with or without intrathoracic lymphadenopathy. Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) was defined as TB affecting organs or tissues other than the lung parenchyma. Disseminated TB was defined as TB involving two or more non-contiguous organ systems or miliary TB confirmed radiologically; for analytical purposes, disseminated TB was treated as a subset of EPTB. These categories were mutually exclusive: cases were classified as PTB, EPTB (including disseminated TB), or the combination of both forms (EPTB/disseminated TB). The binary outcome variable used in logistic regression was EPTB or disseminated TB (coded 1) versus isolated PTB (coded 0). Radiological and histopathological reports were those generated by attending radiologists and pathologists at ACSH as part of routine clinical care.

Nutritional status

Nutritional status was classified using WHO weight-for-height z-scores for children aged 6–59 months and WHO BMI-for-age z-scores for children aged ≥ 5 years. Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) was defined as a z-score < − 3 SD; moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) was defined as a z-score between − 3 SD and − 2 SD; and normal nutritional status was defined as a z-score ≥ − 2 SD.

Anaemia

Anaemia was defined as a haemoglobin, haematocrit, or mean corpuscular volume (MCV) value below the 2.5th percentile for age and sex according to age-specific standardised reference values [20]. Age-specific thresholds are presented in Table 3.

Elevated ESR

An erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) > 20 mm/hr was considered elevated.

Data collection tool and procedure

A structured data extraction checklist was developed to capture pertinent clinical information from patients’ medical records. The tool was designed following a review of relevant literature, WHO guidelines, and the national TB protocol, and was validated with paediatric and public health experts. It comprised four domains: (1) patient identification and sociodemographic characteristics (medical record number, age, sex, residence, nutritional status, immunization history, and TB contact history); (2) clinical information, including presenting symptoms, duration of illness, prior TB treatment or prophylaxis, and TB form with site of extrapulmonary involvement; (3) physical examination findings, including nutritional assessment, respiratory signs, lymphadenopathy, and neurological abnormalities; and (4) laboratory and diagnostic investigations, including complete blood count, ESR, HIV serology, body fluid analyses (CSF, ascitic, or pleural fluid), radiological findings (chest X-ray, ultrasound, MRI/CT scan), and bacteriological results (smear microscopy, GeneXpert MTB/RIF).

Data were collected retrospectively from medical charts, TB registers, laboratory logbooks, and radiology reports for all paediatric patients aged 0–17 years diagnosed with any form of TB at ACSH during the study period. Chest X-ray (CXR) findings were extracted from formal radiology reports documented in the patients’ medical records by attending radiologists at ACSH as part of routine clinical care; actual radiographic images were not independently reviewed for this study. Trained paediatric residents and public health professionals extracted data using the standardised checklist after receiving orientation on the study objectives, variable definitions, and operational procedures. Data quality was ensured by pretesting the checklist on 10% of records prior to use, and supervisors re-checked 10% of randomly selected records for accuracy. Double data entry was performed, and inconsistencies were resolved by cross-referencing the original records. Missing information was documented, and cases with critical data gaps were excluded per the study eligibility criteria. Completed checklists were coded, anonymised, and entered into a spreadsheet, while hard copies were stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to the research team.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded, cleaned, and verified in Microsoft Excel before being imported into SPSS version 23 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD), medians with interquartile range (IQR), and frequencies with percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as appropriate.

The primary dependent variable for logistic regression analysis was EPTB or disseminated TB (versus isolated PTB). Independent variables were screened using bivariable logistic regression; those with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariable analysis, together with clinically important variables identified a priori (age group, sex, nutritional status, and HIV status), were entered into the multivariable model. Data were missing for anthropometry (13.8%), HIV status (43%), and several laboratory variables due to non-documentation in clinical records an expected limitation of retrospective chart review. Complete-case analysis was used for all regression models; the denominator for the final multivariable model was 118 of 123 cases with complete covariate data. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Associations in bivariate analyses were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate; p-values are reported without chi-square statistics in accordance with standard clinical reporting practice.

Results

The study analysed 123 paediatric TB cases identified from 180 screened charts covering the period 2018–2021; 57 cases were excluded owing to prior anti-TB treatment (n = 27), incomplete records (n = 20), and insufficient diagnostic evidence (n = 10).

Socio-demographic background of the study participants

The mean age was 8.8 ± 5.3 years (range 0.3–17 years). Adolescents aged 11–17 years constituted the largest age group at 52/123 (42.3%), followed by children aged 0–4 years at 37/123 (30.1%) and those aged 5–10 years at 34/123 (27.6%). The male-to-female ratio was 0.92:1, reflecting a slight female predominance. Regarding residence, 77 (62.6%) were from rural areas, 43 (35.0%) from urban areas, and three (2.4%) from internally displaced persons (IDP) centres; formal temporary settlements housing individuals displaced by the Tigray conflict (2020–2022). Clinical characteristics

Constitutional symptoms were highly prevalent. Fever was the most common presenting symptom (96/123; 78.0%), followed by weight loss (88/123; 71.5%), anorexia (80/123; 65.0%), cough (68/123; 55.3%), and night sweats (67/123; 54.5%). A history of close TB contact within the preceding two years was documented in 32/123 cases (26.0%), with the index case being an adult in 29 of 32 (90.6%) and a sibling in three. The relatively low rate of documented contact history compared with 32–41% reported in Asian cohorts likely partly reflects underascertainment in retrospective records in addition to possible genuine epidemiological differences.

HIV co-infection was identified in 8/123 patients (6.5%); of five who underwent CD4 cell count measurement, the mean CD4 count was 237 cells/µL.

Among the 106 patients for whom anthropometric data were available, malnutrition was present in 73 (68.8%). Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) affected 40 of 106 patients (37.7%), and moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) was present in 33 of 106 (31.1%). The burden of SAM was highest among children aged < 5 years (16/35; 45.7%). On physical examination, lymphadenopathy was identified in 38/123 patients (30.9%), with cervical nodes predominating (62.2%) followed by inguinal nodes (13.5%). Hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were present in 20 (16.3%) and 7 (5.7%) patients, respectively. Clinical and physical findings are summarized in Table 2.

Diagnostic investigations and yield

Bacteriological confirmation using GeneXpert MTB/RIF on respiratory samples was achieved in 15 of 48 tested patients (31.3%; 95% CI 19.5–45.8). Radiological abnormalities were detected on chest X-ray in 36 of 41 patients who underwent imaging (87.8%); consolidation was the most frequent finding (36.6%), followed by unilateral or bilateral pleural effusion (14.6%) and hilar lymphadenopathy (12.2%). Miliary pattern and cavitation were each observed in two patients.

Anaemia was highly prevalent, affecting 74 of 118 patients (62.7%; 95% CI 53.7–71.1). Normocytic anaemia accounted for 62.1% of all anaemia cases. ESR was elevated in 89 of 106 patients (84.0%); of these, 15.9% had values exceeding 100 mm/hr. Overall, definitive confirmation (bacteriological or histological) was achieved in 50 cases (40.7%). Histological or cytological confirmation by fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) or biopsy was obtained in 36 patients (29.3%), of whom 34 (94.4%) had lymph node confirmation, one had omental confirmation, and one had bone biopsy confirmation. Among the 13 patients with spinal TB, MRI or CT imaging was performed where available; 11 (84.6%) demonstrated gibbus deformity consistent with TB spondylitis, of whom six had evidence of spinal cord compression with associated clinical paraparesis. Among the 35 patients with gastrointestinal or disseminated TB involving the gastrointestinal system, abdominal ultrasound revealed the following findings: significant intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy (n = 24; 68.5%), ascites (n = 17; 48.5%), hepatomegaly (n = 13; 37.1%), bowel thickening or matting (n = 13; 37.1%), splenomegaly (n = 9; 25.7%), and splenic hypoechoic lesions (n = 6; 17.1%). Nearly three-quarters of this subgroup (74.2%) had two or more of these findings in combination.

TB Disease forms and statistical associations

EPTB was the most frequent clinical form, occurring in 54 of 123 patients (43.9%; 95% CI 35.2–52.9). The most common EPTB sites were the lymph nodes (n = 22; 17.9%) and the skeletal system (n = 16; 13.0%), followed by gastrointestinal TB (n = 11; 8.9%) and central nervous system (CNS) TB (n = 5; 4.1%). Disseminated TB was observed in 34.1% of cases (n = 42), while isolated PTB was the least common presentation at 22.0% (n = 27).

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of 123 paediatric TB cases at ACSH demonstrates a striking predominance of extrapulmonary (43.9%) and disseminated (34.1%) disease, a high burden of malnutrition (68.8%), and low bacteriological confirmation (40.7%). Younger age was significantly and independently associated with EPTB and disseminated disease. These findings collectively underscore the diagnostic and clinical challenges inherent to paediatric TB in a high-burden, conflict-affected referral setting.

Demographic and referral patterns

The slight female preponderance (52.0%) in our cohort is consistent with hospital-based studies from Hubli, India (51.6%) and Nepal [21, 22], and most likely reflects local healthcare-seeking behaviours rather than a true biological difference in TB incidence. However, our study did not directly assess differential healthcare access by sex, and this explanation warrants further investigation in prospective studies. The relatively older mean age (8.8 years), with adolescents comprising the largest group (42.3%), differs from the younger predominance reported in community-based cohorts in Bangladesh (where the 1–5-year age group predominated) but is consistent with data from Nepal [22, 23]. This pattern is consistent with the role of ACSH as a tertiary referral centre, which disproportionately receives older children with adult-type pulmonary disease or those in whom diagnosis was delayed.

Clinical manifestation and diagnostic challenges

Constitutional symptoms including fever (96/123; 78.0%), weight loss (88/123; 71.5%), appetite loss (80/123; 65.0%), and cough (68/123; 55.3%) were the primary presenting features, consistent with studies from Nepal and India [21, 22]. However, we noted a lower prevalence of convulsions(3.3%) compared to 17.2% reported Hubli, India [21]. Furthermore, history of close TB contact was reported in only 26% of our cases; lower than 32–41% reported elsewhere in Asia [21–23]. This lower rate should be interpreted cautiously: in a retrospective chart review, contact history is subject to under ascertainment if it was not consistently documented in clinical records, and prospective studies with standardised contact tracing would be needed to accurately characterise this parameter.

The low GeneXpert positivity (31.3%), compared with 42% in a prospective Indian study [24], is consistent with the known paucibacillary nature of paediatric TB. Contributing factors include non-cavitary parenchymal lesions, deep perihilar nodal disease with limited bronchial erosion, weak cough effort in young children, and a high proportion of extrapulmonary disease in the cohort. These factors collectively limit the sensitivity of sputum-based molecular diagnostics in children.

The nutritional syndemic and age-dependent risk

Nearly two-thirds of children in this cohort had acute malnutrition, with SAM affecting 37.7% overall and 45.7% of children aged < 5 years. This pattern is consistent with the nutritional vulnerability of young children in food-insecure settings and is likely compounded by the socioeconomic disruptions associated with the Tigray conflict. Malnutrition and TB have a well-established bidirectional relationship: malnutrition impairs cell-mediated immunity and increases TB susceptibility, while active TB worsens nutritional status [25, 26].

Although SAM trended toward higher prevalence in children aged < 10 years in our sample, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.124). Multivariable analysis revealed that younger age rather than malnutrition alone was the primary factor associated with extrapulmonary and disseminated disease. Children aged 0–4 years had over 3.5 times higher odds of EPTB compared with adolescents (AOR 3.57; 95% CI 1.28–10.00; p = 0.015). This association is consistent with the known immunological characteristics of early childhood, during which T-cell-mediated responses are less well developed, potentially facilitating hematogenous dissemination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis [25, 26]. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, we cannot establish causation; the observed association may also reflect differences in disease severity at presentation or in diagnostic access across age groups.

Physical examination findings

The commonest physical findings in our study were conjunctiva pallor 45(36.6%), auscultatory alterations/findings 41(33.3%), lymphadenopathy 38(30.9%), hepatomegaly 20(16.3%) and splenomegaly 7(5.7%). In a study done in Brazil altered auscultation was found in 36.6% and lymphadenopathy in 31% which is consistent with the current study [27]. But this study contradicts to some extent with another study done in Nepal showed hepatomegaly 45%, splenomegaly 11.67% and lymphadenopathy 23.3% [28]. The discrepancies in organomegaly could be the attention gaps on physical examination unlike prospective studies.

Haematological and inflammatory markers

Anaemia was identified in 62.7% of cases, with normocytic anaemia comprising the majority (62.1%), a pattern consistent with anaemia of chronic disease. An Indian study reported a slightly higher prevalence of anaemia (74%) in pulmonary TB, with normocytic anaemia in 66.2% findings broadly comparable to our results [29]. The mechanisms underlying TB-associated anaemia likely involve impaired erythropoietin responsiveness and suppression of erythropoiesis by inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α [29].

Elevated ESR was observed in 84.0% of patients, with 15.9% having values > 100 mm/hr. This is lower than the 99% reported in an Indian cohort, though the latter was a prospective study and may have used different ESR thresholds [29]. ESR is a non-specific inflammatory marker; while it may support the diagnosis of active TB, its wide individual variability limits its diagnostic utility in isolation [21].

Microbiological and radiological findings

In the current study GeneXpert yield from respiratory samples was 31.23% (n = 48). In a laboratory based prospective study done in India reported a GeneXpert yield of 42% from respiratory samples. This degree of low level mycobacterial yield in pediatrics has several factors. The noncavitary parenchymal lesion and confinement of the organisms deep to the perihilar nodes with less chance of erosion in to bronchus is an important reason. In addition to this, the weak tussive capacity and higher extrapulmonary TB contributes to the poor detection of MTB in children [24].

In this study the chest x ray was abnormal in 87.8% of patients whom they had respiratory complaints. About 36.6% had consolidation followed by consolidation and unilateral or bilateral effusion in 14.6% and hilar lymphadenopathy in 12.2%. Cavitation and miliary pattern were each observed in two patients, and isolated pleural effusion in three. These findings underscore the diverse radiological presentations of paediatric TB and highlight the importance of clinical awareness: consolidation without response to standard antibiotics, hilar lymphadenopathy, and miliary patterns in a febrile child in a high-burden setting should prompt early consideration of TB. Sensitising clinicians to these common paediatric TB radiological patterns may reduce diagnostic delay, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced imaging is unavailable.

A prospective study done in Nepal had similar chest x-ray findings to the current study. The findings in order of frequency were consolidation 24.4%, pleural effusion 14.6%, hilar lymphadenopathy 12.2%, cavitation 7.3% and miliary pattern in 4.9%. A study done in Nepal found the most common Chest x ray finding being lymphadenopathy 23.3%, unilateral pleural effusion 23.3% and consolidation 15% which contradicts to the current study [22].

In another study done in Brazil chest x ray abnormality was 81% and pulmonary parenchymal opacity 22.3%, hilar adenopathy 9% and cavitation 5.5% being the frequent findings. This discrepancy could be due to differences in study population and interpersonal differences in image reading [27].

TB disease forms and statistical associations

The predominance of EPTB (43.9%) over isolated PTB (22.0%) in our cohort contrasts with community-based data from Bangladesh, where pulmonary TB accounted for 62.8% of cases, but is more consistent with EPTB rates reported from Nepalese referral centers (78.3%)(EPTB 78.3%) [22, 28]. This difference most likely reflects referral bias inherent to our tertiary centre setting, where children with complex or diagnostically challenging extrapulmonary presentations are disproportionately represented. The high proportion of disseminated TB (34.1%) is particularly notable and may indicate delayed healthcare access in the context of conflict-related health system disruption, malnutrition-related immune compromise, or both.

The significant independent association between younger age and EPTB/disseminated disease (p < 0.001), with children aged 0–4 years demonstrating over 3.5 times higher odds of EPTB compared with adolescents (AOR 3.57; 95% CI 1.28–10.00), is consistent with prior literature [4, 25, 26]. This association likely reflects developmental differences in host immunity, but may also partly represent differences in the types and sites of TB exposure, or in the referral patterns of younger versus older children. Prospective studies with detailed immunological profiling would be needed to distinguish these possibilities.

Strength and limitation

This study has several strengths, including a comprehensive retrospective chart review covering all TB forms over a three-year period, a validated data extraction tool, and the application of multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of disease form in a context where such analyses are limited.

This study adds to the literature by (1) offering a rare, comprehensive description of pediatric TB clinical and laboratory profiles from a conflict-affected, resource-limited referral centre in sub-Saharan Africa; (2) quantifying a high burden of extrapulmonary and disseminated TB (34.1%), likely driven by malnutrition, delayed care, and conflict-related system disruption; (3) showing younger age as an independent predictor of dissemination via multivariable analysis; and (4) highlighting a large gap between national TB notifications and the likely true regional burden, with implications for programme strengthening and active case finding.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, referral bias is a significant concern: as a tertiary centre, ACSH disproportionately receives severe, complex, or diagnostically uncertain cases, which may inflate the observed proportions of EPTB and disseminated TB and limit generalizability to the broader population. Second, the retrospective design resulted in substantial missing data, particularly for anthropometry (13.8% missing) and HIV status (43.0% missing), which may have introduced selection bias and limited the power of subgroup analyses. Third, the high proportion of clinically diagnosed cases (59.3%) introduces a risk of diagnostic misclassification, as the clinical criteria applied though guideline-based cannot exclude non-TB conditions in all instances. Fourth, the single-centre design precludes inference about population-level incidence. Fifth, the study period partially overlaps with the onset of the Tigray conflict (November 2020), and the external validity of these findings for the post-truce period (after 2022) is uncertain, given likely shifts in patient population and healthcare access patterns. Sixth, the relatively modest case count over three years in a high-TB-burden country warrants acknowledgement. This likely reflects a combination of factors: incomplete case ascertainment within a single referral facility (community and primary-level diagnoses are not captured here), structural disruptions to the national programme resulting from the Tigray conflict, and significant diagnostic underreporting inherent to passive surveillance in resource-limited settings. These gaps are compounded by the paucibacillary nature of paediatric TB, which reduces bacteriological confirmation rates. Addressing these missing cases requires both active household contact investigation and screening of all child contacts of bacteriologically confirmed adult index cases, in line with WHO guidelines, as well as strengthening of the national programme’s paediatric case registration and follow-up systems.

Conclusion and recommendation

In this cohort of 123 children with TB at a tertiary Ethiopian hospital, extrapulmonary TB was the most frequently observed disease form (43.9%), with lymph node involvement (17.9%) and skeletal disease (13.0%) representing the predominant EPTB subtypes. Disseminated disease was present in 34.1% of cases, acute malnutrition was nearly ubiquitous (68.8%), bacteriological confirmation was achieved in fewer than half of cases, and younger age was independently associated with extrapulmonary and disseminated disease. These findings highlight the diagnostic and clinical challenges of paediatric TB in a high-burden, resource-constrained, and conflict-affected setting.

Based on these findings, we make the following recommendations:

Clinical diagnostic threshold: In the absence of bacteriological confirmation; achievable in fewer than half of cases in this cohort, clinicians in endemic settings should maintain a low threshold for TB diagnosis in children presenting with prolonged fever (> 2 weeks), unexplained weight loss or failure to thrive, persistent cough, documented close contact with an infectious TB case, or evidence of acute malnutrition. The use of tuberculin skin testing where available, chest radiography, and clinical scoring systems should be integrated into the diagnostic pathway.
