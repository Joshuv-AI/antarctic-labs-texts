Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease mimicking hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in a young white male: a case report
Authors: Weng Kit Chan, Charelle Manning, Simon Rushbrook, Charlotte Pither, Hameed Rafiee, Sophia Neda, Joy Staniforth, Syed Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKikuchi-Fujimoto disease (KFD) is a rare, generally self-limiting cause of necrotizing lymphadenitis, predominantly affecting young women of East Asian descent, and uncommon in Western populations. Although usually benign, KFD can rarely present with systemic inflammatory features that overlap with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), creating significant diagnostic uncertainty and risk of misdiagnosis.Case presentationWe report the case of a 23-year-old white male in the United Kingdom who presented with a two-week history of intermittent fever, significant unintentional weight loss, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, and poor dentition. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukopenia with neutropenia and lymphopenia, mild transaminitis, and marked hyperferritinemia (8757 µg/L). Extensive infectious, autoimmune, and malignant workup was negative. Imaging revealed isolated cervical lymphadenopathy without disseminated disease. Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy showed hypocellularity with hemophagocytosis, raising concern for HLH. However, only four of eight HLH-2004 criteria were met, insufficient for reaching a diagnosis. Excisional lymph node biopsy demonstrated necrotizing histiocytic lymphadenitis with abundant karyorrhectic debris and crescentic histiocytes, consistent with KFD. Following multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient was treated with a short course of oral corticosteroids, resulting in rapid clinical and biochemical improvement, and no evidence of relapse or complications on follow-up.ConclusionThis case highlights an atypical presentation of KFD in a young white male in a non-endemic setting, complicated by marked hyperferritinemia and HLH-like features. It underscores the importance of a structured, multidisciplinary approach in integrating laboratory trends, marrow findings, and definitive histopathological confirmation to distinguish KFD with HLH-like features from true HLH. Awareness of such presentations is essential to avoid unnecessarily aggressive immunosuppression while ensuring timely and appropriate management.

Background

Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease (KFD), also known as histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis, is a rare, generally self-limiting inflammatory condition characterized by fever and localized lymphadenopathy, first described independently by Kikuchi [1] and Fujimoto [2]. It preferentially affects young adults, particularly women of East Asian descent [3]. KFD remains uncommon in Western populations, contributing to significant diagnostic uncertainty and frequent misdiagnosis [4]. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination of affected lymph nodes [3]. Although usually benign, KFD may rarely present with systemic inflammation mimicking hyperinflammatory syndromes such as hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) [5]. We report a case of KFD in a young white male in a non-endemic setting presenting with overlapping HLH-like features.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old white male was referred to gastroenterology at the Norfolk and Norwich University Hospital with a two-week history of intermittent pyrexia, abdominal discomfort, nausea and vomiting, and unintentional weight loss of 10 kg. He was previously fit and well. Past medical history included medium-chain acyl-Coenzyme A dehydrogenase deficiency and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. He took no regular medications and reported no recent travel, sick contacts, illicit drug use, or known tuberculosis exposure.

On examination, he was febrile at 38.3 °C. A large, tender solitary cervical lymph node was palpable in the left posterior chain. Poor dentition was noted. Cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, with no hepatosplenomegaly, rash, or peripheral stigmata of infective endocarditis.

Admission blood tests showed leukopenia (3.4 × 109/L), neutropenia (1.90 × 109/L), lymphopenia (0.93 × 109/L), and mild transaminitis (ALT 46 U/L). Serum ferritin was markedly elevated at 8757 µg/L (reference range 23–300 µg/L), while fibrinogen, hemoglobin, renal function, and other liver function tests were within normal limits. Viral serologies (EBV, CMV, HIV, and Hepatitis A-E), toxoplasma, and autoimmune screens (ANCA, AMA, ASMA, Anti-LKM, Anti-GPC, and Anti-Reticulin R1) were negative. Blood, urine, and stool cultures were negative. QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus testing was indeterminate, and peripheral blood film showed a reactive picture.

An orthopantomogram raised concern for early dental abscesses involving the lower left six (LL6) and lower right seven (LR7) molar teeth. Review by the maxillofacial team identified dental decay with features of chronic infection requiring extraction, but no evidence of an acute abscess. Following extraction, a one-week course of ciprofloxacin and metronidazole failed to improve his symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography excluded infective endocarditis. Computed tomography (CT) of the neck revealed multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes, predominantly within the left posterior cervical chain (Fig. 1A). CT thorax, abdomen, and pelvis did not reveal any suspicious masses, lymphadenopathy, or organomegaly. An excisional cervical lymph node biopsy was undertaken to exclude lymphoma. Histopathological examination revealed numerous crescentic histiocytes with abundant karyorrhectic debris, without neutrophilic infiltration or evidence of malignancy, consistent with necrotizing histiocytic lymphadenitis (Fig. 1B). Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative for acid-fast bacilli, and subsequent mycobacterial culture was also negative. The case was reviewed at the hematology multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting. Although features including hyperferritinemia and marrow hemophagocytosis raised concern for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), only four of eight HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria [6] were met, and a diagnosis of HLH was not established.

In the context of characteristic histopathological findings, localized posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, prolonged fever refractory to antibiotics, repeatedly negative blood cultures, and the absence of identifiable infectious, autoimmune, or malignant pathology, a diagnosis of KFD was made.

Differential diagnoses

The symptoms of prolonged fever, weight loss, cervical lymphadenopathy, poor dentition, cytopenias, and hyperferritinemia prompted a broad list of differentials (Table 1). Management and follow-up

Following MDT review, the patient was commenced on a course of oral prednisolone 40 mg once daily. After a week, he became apyrexial, his inflammatory markers had improved, his cytopenias and elevated serum ferritin had normalized, while oral intake and body weight had returned to near baseline. His cervical lymphadenopathy had also subsided, with repeat ultrasound confirming resolution. He was discharged on a reducing dose of corticosteroids, and outpatient rheumatology follow-up. Discussion

Epidemiology and demographic considerations

KFD typically affects young women of East Asian descent. Cases occurring outside this patient demographic are less common and may pose a diagnostic challenge, particularly in non-endemic regions like the United Kingdom, where fewer than 30 cases have been reported to date [4]. Limited familiarity with the condition may increase the likelihood of delayed diagnosis or initial misdiagnosis as lymphoma, chronic infection, or systemic autoimmune disease, especially when constitutional features and cytopenias predominate [3]. This is further complicated by the often non-specific laboratory and radiological findings associated with KFD. [3]

Our case demonstrates these challenges clearly. The patient was a young white male in the United Kingdom, outside the demographic group classically associated with KFD. Indeed, the diagnosis was only established after extensive investigation and histopathological confirmation. Recognition that KFD can occur even in atypical demographic groups is therefore essential to avoid unnecessary invasive investigations, inappropriate escalation of therapy, and prolonged diagnostic uncertainty.

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology behind KFD is not fully understood, although current evidence points to an immune-mediated rather than an infectious or malignant process [14]. Histopathological analysis of excised lymph nodes demonstrates characteristic patchy paracortical necrosis with abundant karyorrhectic debris, numerous crescentic histiocytes, predominantly cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, and absent neutrophils and plasma cells, in keeping with immune-mediated cell death [3, 14]. This process is believed to represent an exaggerated immune response to an unidentified trigger, potentially autoimmune or infectious in origin [3, 14]. Activated T lymphocytes and histiocytes then contribute to local tissue injury by releasing pro-inflammatory mediators, resulting in lymph node necrosis [3]. The absence of neutrophils further suggests a non-bacterial inflammatory process, and is useful in distinguishing KFD from suppurative lymphadenitis. [15]

Moreover, genetic and autoimmune factors have also been implicated. In particular, KFD has an established association with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which may precede, coincide with, or complicate the disease course [14]. Importantly, SLE-associated lymphadenopathy is a distinct pathological entity but may show overlapping histological features with KFD, including necrosis and karyorrhectic debris [8]. Distinguishing features favoring SLE-associated lymphadenopathy include elevated anti-dsDNA antibodies, as well as histological findings such as hematoxylin bodies, vasculitis, deposits of nuclear material in the vessel walls (Azzopardi phenomenon), and prominent plasma cell infiltration [8]. In our patient, the absence of clinical features of SLE, negative autoimmune serology, normal complement levels, and lack of these supportive histological features favored KFD over SLE-associated lymphadenopathy.

Certain HLA class II alleles, including HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DPB1, are more commonly identified in affected individuals, supporting an underlying genetic predisposition, and potentially explaining the increased prevalence noted in ethnic Asian populations [16]. Furthermore, several viral agents, such as the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), human herpesvirus (HHV) types 6, 7, and 8, herpes simplex virus (HSV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), have been proposed as potential triggers, although no definitive infectious cause has been found. [14]

While KFD is usually limited to lymphoid tissue, systemic immune activation may rarely occur, manifesting as pyrexia, cytopenias, transaminitis, and raised inflammatory markers [17]. In severe cases, marked macrophage activation and cytokine release may result in laboratory derangement mimicking hyperinflammatory syndromes, including profound hyperferritinemia and hemophagocytosis [18]. This highlights the biological overlap between KFD and other immune-mediated inflammatory conditions and serves to illustrate the diagnostic complexity in patients presenting with more severe systemic features [14, 18, 19].

Overlap with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis

Differentiating between KFD with HLH-like manifestations and true HLH represented a significant diagnostic challenge in our case, given the overlapping clinical features of persistent fever, cytopenias, and marked hyperferritinemia. A recent literature review [20] identified 35 unique cases of HLH in association with KFD, along with a mortality rate of 11% despite aggressive treatment, thus highlighting the aggressive course of this overlap syndrome.

The link between KFD and HLH is likely underpinned by shared immune dysregulation pathways [20]. Both conditions are characterized by excessive activation of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, leading to a hypercytokinemia state driven by interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interleukin-6 [20, 21]. In HLH, impaired cytotoxic function results in sustained immune activation, cytokine storm, and uncontrolled macrophage activation, whereas in KFD, this process appears more self-limited [20]. Nevertheless, transient macrophage activation in KFD has been found to be sufficient to produce features such as hemophagocytosis and hyperferritinemia, contributing to the clinical overlap [21]. This mechanistic continuum may explain why distinguishing severe KFD from early or partial HLH remains clinically challenging.

A large retrospective cohort study by Yu et al. [5] systematically characterized blood and marrow abnormalities in 367 patients with KFD, helping distinguish it from HLH. In patients with KFD alone, cytopenias were common but typically mild and restricted to a single lineage, most often anemia (22%), lymphopenia (17%), and neutropenia (11%), whereas pancytopenia was uncommon (2%) [5]. In patients who underwent bone marrow examination, hypocellularity with minimal compensatory hematopoiesis was predominant, supporting transient myelosuppression rather than immune-mediated peripheral destruction. Conversely, true HLH occurred in fewer than 1% of cases and was characterized by severe multi-lineage cytopenias, hypercellular marrow, and prominent histiocytic infiltration with hemophagocytosis. [5]

Our patient’s hematological profile, comprising leukopenia driven by neutropenia and lymphopenia, in the absence of anemia or thrombocytopenia, along with a hypocellular bone marrow trephine, closely resembled the pattern described in uncomplicated KFD [5]. Nevertheless, the persistent fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, and hemophagocytosis appropriately prompted urgent MDT discussion. The patient fulfilled only four HLH-2004 criteria [6], while at least five are required for diagnosis (Table 3). Several key features of HLH were notably absent, including low serum fibrinogen, bicytopenia, and splenomegaly. Marked hyperferritinemia (8757 µg/L) further contributed to diagnostic uncertainty. While ferritin levels above 10,000 µg/L have been shown to be 90% sensitive and 96% specific for HLH [22], elevated ferritin alone is a non-specific marker of systemic inflammation and may also occur in infections, malignancy, and autoimmune conditions, including KFD. Importantly, the trajectory of ferritin may be more informative than a single measurement. In our patient, rapid normalization (from 8,757 µg/L to 105 µg/L) following corticosteroid therapy supported a reactive inflammatory process rather than the persistent immune dysregulation characteristic of HLH [23]. In addition, excisional cervical lymph node biopsy confirmed necrotizing histiocytic lymphadenitis consistent with KFD, thereby providing a unifying diagnosis and an alternative explanation for the patient’s hyperinflammatory state. Distinguishing KFD with HLH-like features from true HLH is critical, as overdiagnosis of HLH risks unnecessary exposure to aggressive immunosuppressive or cytotoxic therapies with substantial morbidity, while failure to recognize evolving HLH may delay life-saving treatment [24]. This case highlights the importance of integrating the pattern of cytopenias, marrow cellularity, laboratory evolution, and tissue diagnosis, rather than relying on isolated abnormalities alone.

Management and disease monitoring

Management of KFD is usually supportive, with symptomatic relief being the mainstay of treatment [25]. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and analgesia are usually sufficient in mild cases, while patients with more severe biochemical derangements (for example, significant cytopenias) or systemic manifestations may require short courses of corticosteroids [25, 26]. In our patient, the rationale for initiating corticosteroids was due to persistent fever, significant weight loss, marked biochemical inflammation, and lack of response to initial supportive and antimicrobial management. Furthermore, the presence of hyperferritinemia and hemophagocytosis raised concern for a more severe inflammatory phenotype. Corticosteroids have been reported to accelerate symptom resolution in patients with systemic or severe manifestations of KFD [25, 27], and their use in our patient was supported by multidisciplinary consensus.

Other successful treatment options reported in observational studies include intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) [28], hydroxychloroquine (either as monotherapy or in combination with systemic corticosteroids) [29, 30], and certain biologics (for example, Anakinra) [31], although the latter two are typically reserved for recurrent or steroid-resistant disease.

In particular, we wish to highlight the critical role of the MDT approach in establishing both diagnosis and management. Close collaboration between the gastroenterology, hematology, radiology, and histopathology teams enabled a systematic assessment of hyperinflammatory features, supported differentiation of KFD from HLH and lymphoma, and informed the decision to initiate corticosteroid therapy.

KFD is generally associated with a favorable prognosis, with the majority of patients experiencing spontaneous resolution within 1–6 months. [32] Recurrence is uncommon (3–13%) in adults, but has been reported as high as 42.4% in children [33, 34]. In addition, recurrence has been described up to eight years after initial presentation [33], although it tends to respond well to the same management as the initial episode [33, 35]. Disease progression may also be complicated by autoimmune conditions, in particular SLE, again highlighting the unpredictable nature of long-term disease evolution [36]. Severe or fatal outcomes, while very rare, are typically associated with concurrent hyperinflammatory syndromes (for example, HLH) or autoimmune disease (for example, SLE, mixed connective tissue disease, Graves’ disease) [14, 36]. This underscores the importance of ongoing surveillance after KFD, even in patients with complete initial resolution such as in our patient.

Conclusion

This case highlights KFD as an important differential in patients presenting with prolonged fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, cytopenias, and marked hyperferritinemia, even in atypical demographic groups and non-endemic settings. It also illustrates the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing KFD with HLH-like features from true HLH. In this context, MDT assessment and lymph node histopathology were central in establishing the diagnosis and guiding appropriate treatment. Clinicians should remain cognizant of the risk for recurrence and autoimmune complications on long-term follow-up.
