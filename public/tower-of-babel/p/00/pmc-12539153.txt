Daptomycin versus beta-lactam based therapy for the treatment of enterococcal endocarditis and other endovascular infections: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Vindya Perera, Lynn Wardlow, Mohammad M Sobhanie, Kelci Coe, Jessica M Leininger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDaptomycin has a favorable efficacy and safety profile against enterococcal infections, but it is currently a last line treatment for enterococcal infective endocarditis (EIE) due to limited evidence supporting its use.MethodsThis study aimed to investigate clinical outcomes among patients with EIE who received daptomycin versus beta-lactam based therapy. This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study of hospitalized patients with documented EIE and definitive treatment with daptomycin or beta-lactam based therapy between 2011 and 2023. The primary outcome was clinical failure (composite of inpatient infection-related mortality and progression of disease leading to change in therapy). Descriptive statistics assessed baseline demographics, clinical data, and outcomes between patients who received daptomycin versus beta-lactams.ResultsTwenty-six patients receiving daptomycin (median 10 mg/kg/dose) were compared to 102 receiving beta-lactam based therapy (81% ampicillin and ceftriaxone). No significant difference in clinical failure was observed between groups (0% daptomycin vs. 6% beta-lactam; p = 0.35). Individual components of the primary outcome, all-cause mortality, and adverse events leading to therapy discontinuation were similar between groups.ConclusionsOutcomes were similar among patients with EIE treated with daptomycin versus beta-lactams. Larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm this finding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11697-6.Keywords: Endocarditis, Enterococcus spp., Lipopeptide, Beta-lactam

Background

Infective endocarditis (IE) is one of the most common life-threatening infections in the United States. Representing about 10% of cases, Enterococcus spp. are the third most common cause of IE [1]. First line regimens for enterococcal endocarditis (EIE) from the 2015 American Heart Association (AHA) and Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines are (1) ampicillin or penicillin with gentamicin for patients with creatinine clearance >50 milliliters per minute (mL/min), or (2) dual beta-lactam therapy (ampicillin with ceftriaxone) for patients with suboptimal renal function or who cannot tolerate gentamicin [1]. Vancomycin with gentamicin is second line for beta-lactam intolerance and penicillin resistance [1]. Linezolid and daptomycin are considered last line for patients with EIE resistant to penicillin, aminoglycosides, and vancomycin, regardless of valve status [1]. However, beta-lactam allergies, intolerance to vancomycin or aminoglycosides, logistics of monitoring outpatient, and increasing antimicrobial resistance prompt the need for alternatives.

Daptomycin is favored for bloodstream infections caused by vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) due to once daily dosing, tolerability, and activity against enterococci [2, 3]. However, there are limited and conflicting data regarding its use in EIE, with previous studies noting that patients treated with daptomycin had similar in-hospital mortality and 30-day all-cause mortality rates but longer durations of bacteremia compared to those treated with alternative antibiotics [4–6]. Additionally, previous studies have been limited by the inclusion of small cohorts, infections other than endocarditis, combination therapy with daptomycin, and non-beta-lactam agents as comparators [4–6].

The present study investigated outcomes of daptomycin therapy compared to beta-lactam-based therapy in the treatment of EIE.

Materials and methods

Setting and design

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study of patients admitted to The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center (OSUWMC) inpatient facilities from November 1, 2011, through August 31, 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A query of our institution’s information warehouse identified patients 18 years or older with either (1) an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 or 10 code corresponding to IE plus ≥ 1 blood culture positive for Enterococcus spp. or (2) ICD 9 or 10 codes corresponding to both IE and enterococcal infection. Patients identified using these criteria were further reviewed by the study investigators to confirm documentation of an EIE diagnosis and the presence of ≥ 1 blood culture positive for Enterococcus spp., including E. faecalis and E. faecium. Patients with other endovascular infections (e.g., septic thrombophlebitis) without valvular involvement were permitted as treatment options and duration would be similar. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: incarcerated, did not receive definitive therapy with daptomycin or a beta-lactam for at least 7 days and at least 50% of their total treatment course, index culture demonstrated resistance to definitive antibiotic received, polymicrobial endocarditis, daptomycin dose < 6 milligram per kilogram (mg/kg) of total body weight (or adjusted body weight if total body weight exceeded 120% of ideal body weight), or received definitive therapy with both daptomycin and an anti-enterococcal beta-lactam. The criterion for at least 7 days of definitive therapy was chosen to exclude patients who died or were lost to follow up within days of EIE diagnosis while still capturing patients who had a negative outcome on therapy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was clinical failure, defined as a composite of inpatient infection-related mortality and progression of disease leading to a change in therapy.

Secondary outcomes included individual components of the primary outcome and all-cause mortality before the end of therapy. To assess safety, adverse drug reactions (ADRs) leading to discontinuation of therapy were collected.

Definitions

Definitive therapy was defined as the antimicrobial regimen received for greater than 50% of the treatment course and at least 7 days. Polymicrobial endocarditis was an endovascular infection caused by multiple unique organisms determined from provider documentation, assessment of blood cultures, and determination of possible contaminants. Inpatient infection-related mortality was defined as mortality during hospitalization prior to the planned end of therapy that was attributed to EIE as assessed by an infectious diseases (ID) physician. Progression of disease was defined as one or more of the following resulting in a change in therapy: recurrence of bacteremia, progressive heart failure or valvular dysfunction on echocardiogram, need for urgent surgery, or embolization. Safety outcomes were collected via provider documentation and included elevated creatinine kinase, severe acute kidney injury, or any other event leading to discontinuation of therapy.

Statistical methods

Study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) to securely store all data. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing (1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; (2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources [7, 8]. Descriptive statistics assessed baseline demographics, clinical data, and outcomes between patients who received daptomycin versus beta-lactams. Demographic and clinical variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges [IQR] for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Continuous data were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, and categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square or the Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all comparisons. All analyses were completed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). Sample size was determined via available patients during the study period rather than a power calculation.

Results

Baseline and clinical characteristics

The final study population included 26 patients receiving daptomycin and 102 patients receiving beta-lactams (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were relatively balanced between groups with notable exceptions described below (Table 1). The daptomycin group had significantly more patients with diabetes (p < 0.01), and the beta-lactam group had significantly more patients with a history of injection drug use (p = 0.04) and pre-existing valvular heart disease (p = 0.02). Though not statistically significant, patients within the daptomycin group had lower baseline creatinine clearance and a higher median Charlson comorbidity index. Most patients had native valve endocarditis (72%), with the aortic valve being most common (32%). Additionally, 19% of patients in the daptomycin group were treated for an endovascular infection other than endocarditis (e.g., septic thrombophlebitis) compared to only 1% of patients in the beta-lactam group. The most common pathogen was E. faecalis (88%), but E. faecium was more common in the daptomycin group than in the beta-lactam group (p < 0.01). Patients in the beta-lactam group were more likely to have a vancomycin and ampicillin-susceptible infection (p < 0.01) and more likely to undergo surgery for their acute EIE episode (p < 0.01). Patients received a median daptomycin dose of 750 mg (IQR 550–800 mg), corresponding to 10 mg/kg (IQR 8–11 mg/kg). Patients in the beta-lactam group most often received ampicillin with ceftriaxone (81%); beta-lactam based regimens are described in the supplement (Table S2). The median duration of definitive therapy was 42 days (IQR 34–44 days).

Outcomes

There were no differences between groups in the primary outcome or its individual components, all-cause mortality before the end of therapy, or ADRs (Table 2). No patients within the daptomycin group met the primary outcome. Seven patients in the daptomycin group and 15 in the beta-lactam group died before the end of therapy, but only 5 deaths in the beta-lactam group and none in the daptomycin group were attributed to EIE. Additionally, very few patients in both groups experienced an ADR leading to a discontinuation of therapy.

Discussion

Treatment with daptomycin for acute EIE over an 11-year period did not increase hospital length of stay, incidence of ADRs, infection-related mortality, or progression of disease compared to beta-lactam therapy. These results are consistent with previous studies [4, 6]. Carugati, et al.., found similar rates of in-hospital mortality between patients who received daptomycin or standard of care for left-sided IE caused by Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, or E. faecalis [4]. Peghin, et al.., found no difference in 30-day all-cause mortality between patients who received daptomycin-based and daptomycin-sparing therapy for EIE [6]. In contrast to the study by Cerón, et al.., that found a longer duration of bacteremia with daptomycin compared to dual beta-lactam or conventional therapy, our study observed no difference in the duration of bacteremia [5].

None of the patients receiving daptomycin met the primary outcome. While reassuring that daptomycin is an efficacious treatment, it is possible that selection bias and small sample size could have influenced these results. The patients in the daptomycin group were numerically less likely to have a history of prior endocarditis, artificial heart valve, or prosthetic valve endocarditis – which has been associated with a worse prognosis compared to native valve IE [9]– [10]. However, patients receiving daptomycin were more likely to have underlying comorbidities as assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity index and to have E. faecium infections. Though E. faecalis tends to be more virulent and more likely to cause IE, E. faecium is recognized as an increasing cause of healthcare-associated infections and is often difficult to treat due to multidrug resistance [11].

No differences were observed in the safety of daptomycin compared to beta-lactam based therapy. However, ADRs may have been underestimated since data collection was only performed on patients who discontinued antibiotic therapy because of toxicity.

Strengths

With 26 patients treated for EIE with daptomycin, our study is the largest to date comparing daptomycin to beta-lactams for the treatment of EIE. Lübbert, et al.., included 58 patients who received daptomycin for treatment for EIE with a reported success rate of 82.8%; however, a lack of a comparator arm limits interpretation [3]. Other studies have been limited by small sample size and the inclusion of infections other than endocarditis as well as organisms other than enterococci [4–6].

At OSUWMC, guidelines recommend daptomycin 8–10 mg/kg if used for enterococcal bloodstream infections or EIE. The median weight-based dose of daptomycin in this study was 10 mg/kg, which is consistent with the 2015 AHA/IDSA guideline recommended dose of 10–12 mg/kg [1]. In Cerón (2014), which found patients treated with daptomycin for EIE were more likely to require a change in therapy due to inadequate clinical or microbiologic response, the mean daptomycin dose was 8.5 mg/kg (range 6–10 mg/kg). Chuang, et al.., (2017) found that daptomycin doses of 9 mg/kg or greater were associated with decreased mortality in patients with VRE bloodstream infections [12]. In our study, 18 (69%) patients received daptomycin doses ≥ 9 mg/kg, and only one patient received a dose < 8 mg/kg.

Additionally, by permitting the inclusion of E. faecium infections as well as endovascular infections other than endocarditis, our study was reflective of real-world practice. Lastly, infection-related mortality was assessed by an ID physician to increase validity.

Limitations

Our study was limited by small sample size. With only 128 patients and few receiving daptomycin, it may not have been powered to detect a difference in treatment outcomes. Additionally, the few primary outcomes observed within our sample precluded the use of multivariable logistic regression to control for potential confounders.

Treatment groups had notable differences in baseline and infection characteristics. Patients in the daptomycin group were more likely to have diabetes and had more infections caused by E. faecium. Patients in the beta-lactam group were more likely to have a history of injection drug use and valvular heart disease, and they underwent surgery for the acute infection more often, which may have influenced outcomes. Additionally, patients in the daptomycin group were more likely to have been treated for an endovascular infection other than endocarditis (e.g., septic thrombophlebitis), and a higher percentage of those treated for endocarditis did not have definite evidence of valvular involvement (27% vs. 10% in the daptomycin vs. beta-lactam groups, respectively).

Lastly, the present findings were limited by the retrospective design and potential selection bias. Prescribing choices may have been influenced by factors not recorded in the medical record. At OSUWMC, therapy choice for EIE is largely provider preference and patient characteristic driven. Furthermore, patients lost to follow up after hospital discharge were counted as surviving beyond the duration of EIE therapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, daptomycin may be a viable alternative to beta-lactam therapy for the treatment of EIE; however, more and larger studies are needed to confirm these findings
