Cost-utility of pneumococcal strategies for adults aged 50 and older in Colombia: a model-based economic evaluation

Background

Pneumococcal disease remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults globally, leading to significant healthcare costs and adverse health outcomes. Invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) and community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae disproportionately affect adults aged 65 years and older, the risk of IPD and CAP increases especially in those who have comorbidities and immunosenescence [1, 2]. In Colombia, pneumococcal disease represents a significant public health challenge, with national surveillance data indicating substantial disease burden among older adults [3].

Vaccination is the most effective strategy to prevent pneumococcal disease [4]. Traditionally, the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) has been used in adult immunization programs. However, conjugate vaccines, such as the 13-valent (PCV13) and the newer 20-valent (PCV20), offer potential advantages by inducing immunological memory and broader serotype coverage [5–7]. Given the epidemiological shift in serotype prevalence, with non-PCV13 serotypes contributing to disease, PCV20 may provide enhanced protection against the circulating strains in Colombia [8].

Despite these advantages, in Colombia, no current pneumococcal vaccine is included in adult immunization schedules as the Colombian health system faces budgetary constraints and competing health priorities [9]. To introduce any pneumococcal vaccine in the adult immunization schedule, decision-makers must assess not only the clinical effectiveness of vaccination strategies but also their cost-effectiveness and long-term affordability. The Ministry of Health and Social Protection in Colombia, as well as other public and private payers, such as large EPS, are increasingly relying on economic evaluations to guide resource allocation decisions [10, 11].

Economic evaluations of pneumococcal vaccination in adults have yielded varying results, depending on the vaccine selected, the targeted age group, local disease burden, and the time horizon used in the analysis [12, 13]. Short- and medium-term analyses may underestimate the value of vaccination because these interventions typically involve a one-time cost, whereas health and economic benefits can accrue and accumulate over many years [14]. Conversely, long-term analyses can demonstrate potential cost savings as prevented cases reduce treatment costs over time [15]. For policymakers, understanding both economic implications is crucial for designing effective and sustainable vaccination strategies.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to evaluate the cost-utility of available pneumococcal vaccination strategies for Colombian adults aged 50 years and older, comparing medium-term (5-year) and long-term (10-year) time horizons. By providing clear evidence of cost-effectiveness and potential long-term cost savings, this analysis aims to inform immunization policy decisions in Colombia and support rational resource allocation within the health system.

Methods

We conducted a model-based cost-utility analysis comparing pneumococcal vaccination strategies for adults aged 50 years and older in Colombia. The study adopted the perspective of the Colombian public health system, focusing solely on direct medical costs relevant to the payer [10].

The target population consisted of adults aged 50 years or older, reflecting the primary age group for whom pneumococcal vaccination is recommended in Colombia [16]. Demographic information by age groups was extracted from the National Department of Statistics in Colombia [17]. The setting is the Colombian healthcare system, characterized by a mixed public-private insurance structure with defined benefit packages and a centralized immunization policy-making process under the Ministry of Health and Social Protection [8, 9].

We compared the following vaccination strategies to no vaccination:

PCV13: single-dose administration at year 0.

PCV13: single-dose administration at year 0.

PCV15: single-dose administration in year 0.

PCV15: single-dose administration in year 0.

PCV20: single-dose administration at year 0.

PCV20: single-dose administration at year 0.

PPSV23 alone: initial dose at year 0 plus a booster at year 5.

PPSV23 alone: initial dose at year 0 plus a booster at year 5.

PCV13 + PPSV23: PCV13 administered first, followed by PPSV23 after 8 weeks.

PCV13 + PPSV23: PCV13 administered first, followed by PPSV23 after 8 weeks.

PCV15 + PPSV23: PCV15 administered first, followed by PPSV23 after 8 weeks.

PCV15 + PPSV23: PCV15 administered first, followed by PPSV23 after 8 weeks.

For strategies involving PPSV23, the model incorporated the additional cost of the booster dose at year 5, applying discounting appropriately. The PCV13 + PPSV23 and PCV15 + PPSV23 strategies accounted for the price of two separate vaccine applications within the first year.

To represent both the short-term budget impact relevant to decision-makers and the long-term value of vaccination in preventing pneumococcal disease [14], two time horizons were analyzed: 5 and 10 years. For the discount rate, consistent with Colombian pharmacoeconomic guidelines [10], a 5% annual discount rate was applied to both costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) in the 10-year analysis.

Model structure and rationale

A decision-tree model was developed to compare the costs and health outcomes of alternative pneumococcal vaccination strategies in adults aged 50 years and older. The model simulates a hypothetical cohort of healthy individuals at baseline who receive one of the vaccination strategies or remain unvaccinated. Branches represent possible outcomes over the chosen time horizon, including remaining disease-free, developing community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), developing invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD), experiencing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), or dying.

The tree structure captures the one-time administration of vaccines (including boosters where applicable) and the downstream consequences of disease events, without transitions back to health. This approach is appropriate given that vaccination occurs in a healthy cohort and prevents incident disease, aligning with standard methods for evaluating vaccines [18] (Fig. 1).

Epidemiological parameters

The model incorporated age-specific annual incidence rates of pneumococcal disease, adjusted for the demographic distribution of adults aged 50 years and older in Colombia [19]. These incidence rates reflect the expected probability of developing pneumococcal infection in the target population over the analysis horizon. Additionally, the model accounted for the clinical distribution of pneumococcal disease manifestations, assigning 26.1% [20] of cases to CAP, 42.8% [21] to meningitis, and 10.0% [22] to BSI. This stratification enables the model to capture differences in clinical severity, treatment costs, and health outcomes across infection types.

The probabilities of developing severe complications following pneumococcal disease, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), epilepsy, and hearing loss, were incorporated to capture the long-term health impact and associated disutilities, as well as antimicrobial resistance rates for pneumococcal infections, as reported by Colombia’s national surveillance system [8] (Table 1).

The model also incorporated case fatality rates (CFRs) to capture the probability of death following different clinical presentations of pneumococcal disease. These rates reflect the severity of each outcome and were applied to patients who developed the disease during the simulated time horizon. Specifically, the CFR was estimated at 5.5% for CAP, 8.7% for meningitis, and 11.4% for BSI [19] across all age groups, weighted by population size. Including these mortality risks ensures that the model adequately represents the potential loss of life and its impact on QALYs in the vaccinated and unvaccinated populations.

Measurement and valuation of outcomes

Outcomes were measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which combined life-years and health-related quality-of-life weights for each health state. Health utility values were derived from the published literature on older adults with and without pneumococcal disease and its complications [25–30].

Baseline utility values were assigned to adults aged 50 years and older to reflect general age-related health status. Disutilities were then applied to capture the reduction in health-related quality of life associated with pneumococcal disease outcomes, including hospital-treated CAP, outpatient CAP, and IPD. Additionally, the model incorporated disutility weights for disease-related complications, such as MACE, epilepsy, and hearing loss, which can occur following severe pneumococcal infections. Measurement and valuation of costs

Vaccine acquisition costs were primarily obtained from the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) vaccine price list, which provides standardized regional procurement prices [31]. As of the date of this analysis, there was no publicly available price for the PCV15 vaccine; the price was extracted from the Colombian Drug Price Information System (SISMED) [32]. Administration costs per dose were also included, representing the expenses incurred in delivering vaccines through the national immunization program; they were extracted from the technical appendix derived from the Sufficiency study [33].

For strategies with PPSV23, booster doses in year 5 were included with appropriate discounting. The PCV13 + PPSV23 and PCV15 + PPSV23 strategies incorporated the costs of both vaccines and two separate administration events within the first year.

Expenses related to the medical management of CAP and IPD, as well as associated complications, were sourced from published cost-of-illness studies, economic evaluations previously conducted in Colombia and reported in peer-reviewed medical journals [34, 35] and expert-consensus, they were adjusted to 2025 Colombian pesos according to the corresponding inflation rate [36] and exchange rate (COP $3,891) [37]. These sources provided locally relevant estimates of the direct medical costs of hospitalizations, outpatient care, diagnostics, and the treatment of complications. Cost estimates for specialized healthcare, neuropsychological deficit, epilepsy, and antimicrobial resistance were derived via expert consensus due to the absence of public prices for these inputs in Colombia. The model incorporated estimates of vaccine efficacy against pneumococcal CAP and IPD based on available clinical trial evidence. PCV efficacy against CAP and IPD was included based on pooled clinical trial data, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals included in Table 2.

For conjugate vaccines, efficacy estimates were primarily based on the CAPiTA trial, the only randomized controlled trial evaluating PCV13 in older adults (aged 60 years or older) [6]. Additionally, no vaccine efficacy waning is assumed, as previous studies have shown no evidence of waning [40]. The model assumed that PCV15 and PCV20 share the same efficacy for preventing CAP and IPD, given the lack of clinical efficacy data or real-world effectiveness evidence in adults at the time the study was conducted. Table 2 summarizes the point estimates and 95% confidence intervals of vaccine efficacy assumptions used in the model, which were adjusted based on the local epidemiology of circulating serotype coverage in Colombia for each vaccine. The serotype coverage associated with each vaccine was sourced from national serotype surveillance data from 2022 to 2024, thereby minimizing the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic, which was characterized by abnormally low reporting rates [8] (Table 3). These coverage rates are assumed to be the same across pathologies, as the National Institute of Health does not differentiate CAP cases.

Heterogeneity and uncertainty analysis

While the model allows subgroup analyses by age bands (50–64, 65–74, 75–84, ≥ 85), for this evaluation, the main results were presented for the entire cohort aged 50 years and above, which is the primary target group for vaccination policy in Colombia. Deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) was conducted using variations of ± 25% on key parameters to assess the robustness of the results and Probabilistic Sensitivity analysis (PSA). DSA included vaccine costs, disease incidence, vaccine effectiveness, and healthcare costs. Additionally, scenario analyses evaluated the impact of age group (including the population aged 65 years and older) and the effects of complications, which were not included in the base case analysis.

Results

Deterministic base-case results

The model generated deterministic cost-utility results comparing pneumococcal vaccination strategies to no vaccination in adults aged 50 years and older in Colombia. Results are presented separately for two time horizons: a 5-year horizon to reflect the medium-term budget impact and cost-effectiveness, and a longer-term horizon of up to 10 years to capture the sustained health benefits and cost savings associated with vaccination over time.

To account for differences in life expectancy across age groups, the model applied a 10-year horizon to cohorts aged 50–64, 65–74, and 75–84 years, while using a 5-year horizon for adults aged ≥ 85 years, consistent with an assumed life expectancy of approximately 90 years in that subgroup. This approach improves the realism of the analysis by aligning follow-up duration with expected survival in each age band, ensuring that estimates of costs and benefits appropriately reflect the demographic structure of the target population.

The deterministic results presented in Table 4 indicate that PCV13 and PCV20 were cost-effective at both time horizons. PCV15, PCV13 + PPSV23, and PCV15 + PPSV23 became cost-effective over the longer time horizon; on the other hand, PPSV23 showed no cost-effectiveness at any time horizon, exceeding the WTP threshold by 7 times GDP per capita. Notably, the PCV13 and PCV20 strategies demonstrated ICERs lower than 30.4% of one GDP per capita, highlighting their potential to reduce overall healthcare spending while improving health outcomes. This cost-saving result reflects the one-time vaccine administration that prevents high-cost hospitalizations and complications over time, making it particularly attractive for long-term budget planning.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analyses reveals that for conjugated vaccines the main drivers of incremental costs and cost-effectiveness results are vaccine acquisition cost, serotype coverage, CAP incidence and cost of inpatient CAP, while for PPSV technologies the main observed drivers are serotype coverage, vaccine price and incidence of BSI (see Fig. 2). In particular, PCV20 was a robust strategy, reductions in serotype coverage and higher prices increased the ICER but did not exceed the WTP threshold (Fig. 2). Assumptions related to vaccine uptake and coverage influenced the magnitude of health gains, with higher coverage translating into a larger reduction in pneumococcal disease burden and a greater likelihood of achieving favorable economic outcomes.

Figure 4 shows the cost-effectiveness favorability acceptability curves (CEACs) for the vaccination strategies across a range of willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds per QALY gained in Colombia. At a threshold of $8,266 (equivalent to 1 GDP per capita), PCV20 has the highest probability of being the most cost-effective strategy at 78%, followed by PCV13 (22%). The likelihood of no vaccination being the preferred option when there is a willingness to pay higher than 0 is low. In contrast, PPSV23 alone has negligible probabilities. At a higher WTP threshold, approximately 15 times GDP per capita, the likelihood of PCV13 + PPSV23 increases modestly to 5.4% and for PCV15 + PPSV23, it rises to 0.9%. However, both remain lower than PCV20 and the other conjugate vaccine options. These curves highlight the robust cost-effectiveness profile of PCV20 under uncertainty, especially at WTP thresholds commonly considered in the Colombian health system.

Discussion

This economic evaluation demonstrates that implementing PCV13 and PCV20 vaccination for adults aged 50 years and older in Colombia is not only cost-effective but also becomes cost-saving when complications like MACE and AMR are included. Compared to no vaccination and alternative strategies (including PPSV23 alone and the sequential PCV13 + PPSV23 schedule), PCV20 consistently showed the highest health benefits at a low long-term cost. These findings support the adoption of PCV20 as the preferred pneumococcal vaccination strategy for adults in Colombia.

The deterministic results showed that PCV13 and PCV20 achieved greater reductions in QALYs lost at lower cost increases than all other strategies, even at conservative willingness-to-pay thresholds. In the deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (DSA and PSA), PCV20 cost-effectiveness showed robustness to exogenous shocks and maintained the highest probability of being the most favorable strategy across a wide range of willingness-to-pay thresholds, with a probability of 73.4% at a threshold of $8,266 (1 GDP per capita). This robust performance under uncertainty strengthens confidence in PCV20’s value proposition for Colombia’s health system.

Beyond economic metrics, the model estimated substantial public health benefits of implementing PCV20. Over the modeled horizon, PCV20 averted the highest number of pneumococcal disease cases, deaths, and severe complications (including MACE, hearing loss, and neurological sequelae) compared to all alternative strategies. By preventing IPD and CAP, PCV20 would reduce the clinical burden on health facilities, improve patient quality of life, and decrease mortality among older adults, a population with high baseline risk that typically presents multiple comorbidities.

These findings are consistent with international economic evaluations supporting the adoption of higher-valency conjugate vaccines in adults. For example, Smith et al. demonstrated that PCV13 was cost-effective compared to PPSV23 in US adults aged 65 years or older [14]. More recent modeling studies have presented even stronger economic and clinical arguments for PCV20 over PCV13, PCV15, and PPSV23 in various settings [7, 42–52].

From a policy perspective, procurement mechanisms such as the PAHO Revolving Fund play a central role in stabilizing vaccine prices and securing long-term supply agreements. This strengthens purchasing power and contributes to the sustainability of National Immunization Programs [53]. Although the analysis does not include a separate procurement scenario, sensitivity analysis supports robustness of PCV20 cost-effectiveness to the price and, indirectly, to PAHO procurement conditions. Additionally, maximizing coverage, particularly in high-risk groups and adults aged ≥ 65 years, would increase the number of prevented cases and hospitalizations and improve the overall population-level impact.

Strengths of this study include the use of Colombian-specific epidemiological and cost data, age-stratified incidence rates, and realistic life expectancy-based horizons. The model employed a probabilistic approach to capture parameter uncertainty and presented results in both economic terms (ICERs) and tangible health outcomes (cases and deaths prevented), which is crucial for public health planning and decision-making.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although age-specific incidence rates were used, the model assumed that complication probabilities were constant across age groups due to the lack of stratified data, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of disease severity in older subgroups. Second, the vaccine efficacy assumption was based on data from previously published clinical trials. Although they were adjusted using serotype coverage specific to Colombia, they were not explicitly generated for the country, reflecting limitations in the available data. Finally, the model focused on direct medical costs from the health system perspective and did not include indirect costs or societal benefits, potentially underestimating the full economic value of vaccination.

In addition, excluding indirect and societal costs likely underestimates the overall value of adult pneumococcal vaccination in Colombia. Preventing pneumococcal CAP and IPD may reduce productivity losses due to work absenteeism and presenteeism among adults aged 50–64 years, as well as caregiver time and informal care needs in older age groups. Furthermore, avoiding hospitalizations and long-term sequelae (e.g., neurological impairment, hearing loss, and cardiovascular complications) may translate into broader economic benefits beyond the healthcare budget, including reduced out-of-pocket expenditures, transportation costs, and household financial burden. From a societal perspective, vaccination may also yield additional benefits through reduced antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance-related costs, which were only partially captured in our scenario analyses.

We acknowledge that there may be relevant heterogeneity in baseline pneumococcal disease risk among adults aged ≥ 50 years, driven by age strata, comorbidity burden (e.g., chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease), frailty, and differences in healthcare utilization. Such variation may influence the absolute number of cases and deaths prevented and, therefore, the magnitude of the clinical and economic benefits. In higher-risk subgroups, greater baseline incidence and complication rates would likely translate into larger health gains and more favorable cost-effectiveness ratios for vaccination, whereas in lower-risk subgroups, smaller absolute benefits and higher ICERs may result. Although our model uses population-average inputs, the overall conclusions are expected to remain directionally consistent, particularly for older age groups and individuals with underlying risk conditions.

Despite these limitations, the results provide robust evidence in support of including PCV20 in Colombia’s adult immunization programs. By preventing thousands of pneumococcal disease cases and deaths over the coming decade while reducing overall costs, PCV20 represents a desirable public health investment that aligns with national health priorities of improving care for older adults and ensuring efficient resource use in the Colombian health system.

Implementation considerations to maximize uptake and real-world impact

To translate the projected health and economic benefits of PCV20 into real-world outcomes, implementation strategies to maximize vaccine uptake among adults aged ≥ 50 years are essential. In Colombia, adult immunization has historically achieved lower coverage than childhood vaccination programs [54], therefore, proactive delivery mechanisms are required. Potential strategies include integrating pneumococcal vaccination into routine care pathways for older adults and individuals with cardiometabolic or chronic respiratory conditions, leveraging existing primary care encounters, and administering opportunistic vaccination during outpatient visits, at hospital discharge, and during chronic disease follow-up. In addition, simplifying the adult schedule through a single-dose strategy may facilitate implementation, reduce missed opportunities, and improve completion rates compared with sequential regimens.

Finally, ongoing pneumococcal disease surveillance and periodic reassessment of vaccination recommendations are essential to sustain real-world impact over time. Serotype distribution, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and vaccine effectiveness may evolve due to changes in circulating strains, vaccine uptake, and potential serotype replacement. Therefore, national and regional surveillance systems should continuously monitor pneumococcal epidemiology and outcomes in older adults, including the contribution of vaccine-covered serotypes and trends in invasive and non-invasive disease. This evidence can inform timely updates of immunization strategies, ensuring that recommendations remain aligned with the local burden of disease and the evolving pneumococcal landscape in Colombia.

Conclusion

This economic evaluation demonstrates that PCV20 vaccination for adults aged 50 years and older in Colombia offers substantial clinical and financial advantages over alternative strategies, including PPSV23 alone and the sequential PCV13 + PPSV23 schedule. PCV20 was cost-effective at a 10-year horizon, with the highest probability of being the most favorable option under uncertainty analyses.

Beyond favorable economic outcomes, PCV20 was associated with the most significant reduction in pneumococcal disease burden, preventing more cases, deaths, and severe complications than any other strategy. These results support PCV20 as a highly valuable public health intervention that aligns with Colombia’s priorities of protecting older adults and ensuring efficient use of health system resources. Policymakers should consider prioritizing PCV20 implementation in national immunization schedules to achieve both cost savings and meaningful improvements in population health.
