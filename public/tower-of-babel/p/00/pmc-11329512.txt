MksB is a novel mycobacterial condensin that orchestrates spatiotemporal positioning of replication machinery
Authors: Hanna Bułacz, Joanna Hołówka, Wiktoria Wójcik, Jolanta Zakrzewska-Czerwińska
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCondensins play important roles in maintaining bacterial chromatin integrity. In mycobacteria, three types of condensins have been characterized: a homolog of SMC and two MksB-like proteins, the recently identified MksB and EptC. Previous studies suggest that EptC contributes to defending against foreign DNA, while SMC and MksB may play roles in chromosome organization. Here, we report for the first time that the condensins, SMC and MksB, are involved in various DNA transactions during the cell cycle of Mycobacterium smegmatis (currently named Mycolicibacterium smegmatis). SMC appears to be required during the last steps of the cell cycle, where it contributes to sister chromosome separation. Intriguingly, in contrast to other bacteria, mycobacterial MksB follows replication forks during chromosome replication and hence may be involved in organizing newly replicated DNA.Subject terms: Bacterial genes, Chromosomes, Replisome

Introduction

The bacterial chromosome must be efficiently compacted to fit into the tiny cell volume. Its architecture is not shaped solely by physical forces (i.e., cell size, molecular crowding, depletion forces); many DNA-binding proteins function to ensure proper compaction of bacterial chromatin and its coordination with ongoing cellular processes1–4. Among these DNA-binding proteins are the condensins, which are large-scale DNA-organizing proteins that are found throughout all domains of life5–9. In bacteria, three families of condensins have been identified to date: SMC10, that occur the most commonly, enterobacterial MukB11, and MksB12, which is scattered in various bacterial genera/species that also encode SMC. All three families of condensins are relatively similar in structure, consisting of polar head domains, coiled-coil arms, and a hinge that allows for dimerization13,14. Moreover, all condensins bind chromosomal DNA in complexes with their partner proteins: ScpAB, MukEF, and MksEF partner with SMC, MukB, and MksB, respectively. The ATPase activity of head domains enables the condensin complex to undergo conformational changes, forming a closed ring upon binding ATP and an open complex, called the ‘V form’, upon ATP hydrolysis15,16. This causes relatively distant DNA fragments to be brought together via DNA-binding modes called loop extrusion and loop entrapment for the modes involving SMC and MukB, respectively6,17–20.

Similar to the situation in eukaryotes, bacterial condensins seem to be crucial for proper chromosome organization and segregation. For example, in Bacillus subtilis, Caulobacter crescentus, and Streptomyces venezuelae, SMC-ScpAB complexes work together with the ParABS segregation system to mediate chromosomal arms cohesion starting from the oriC region, where ParB binds and recruits SMC-ScpAB complexes21–25. In Escherichia coli, which does not possess the ParABS segregation system, MukBEF complexes are recruited stochastically onto the chromosome and condense DNA through intra-arm interactions7,11,26. MksBEF complexes usually occur in bacteria that also possess SMC-ScpAB12,27–29. The B. subtilis JetABCD system, which is a member of the SMC complex superfamily, uses DNA cleavage to defend cells against foreign plasmids30. A similar role is played by the MksBEF complex in the recently described MksBEFG system of Corynebacterium glutamicum29. In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, two MksBs and SMC contribute to chromosome organization and influence the expression of many genes, including those related to virulence27,31.

The genomes of pathogenic mycobacteria (e.g., M. tuberculosis) encode an SMC homolog but no MksB-like protein32, whereas saprophytic species (e.g., M. smegmatis) encode SMC and two MksB-like proteins: the recently identified MksB and EptC33,34. Similar to other MksBEF homologs, EptC does not take part in chromosome organization, but rather contributes to defending against foreign DNA. To allow for efficient genetic modifications and transformation, researchers developed the laboratory strain, M. smegmatis mc2 155 (currently named Mycolicibacterium smegmatis35), which harbors a point mutation that inactivates the eptC gene28.

To date, relatively few studies have focused on the mycobacterial condensins28,33,34,36. Both SMC and MksB reportedly bind DNA in vitro, but deletion of the smc gene from M. tuberculosis and M. smegmatis yielded rather mild phenotypes with little or no effect on cell growth or chromosomal DNA condensation32. No previous report has assessed how condensins affect mycobacterial nucleoid compaction during the cell cycle. The mycobacterial chromosome has a unique bead-like structure37,38, so we speculated that it would be worthwhile to investigate how condensins might influence its architecture. In the present study, we characterized the chromosome organization and dynamics in living M. smegmatis cells deprived of SMC or MksB condensins and reveal that MksB follows replication forks during chromosomal DNA synthesis.

Results & discussion

Unlike MksB, SMC influences the last steps of sister chromosome separation

In mycobacteria, in contrast to other bacteria, the SMC gene does not occur in an operon together with scpAB genes encoding the SMC partner proteins, ScpAB (Fig. S1). As mentioned above, the M. smegmatis genome also encodes two other condensins: EptC (inactivated in the M. smegmatis mc2 155 laboratory strain28), and MksB, whose gene is organized in an operon with genes thought to encode MksEF partner proteins (Fig. S1). Our previous RNA-Seq results39 showed that in exponentially growing M. smegmatis cells, condensin-encoding genes are expressed at relatively low levels compared to other genes involved in chromatin organization (e.g., the highly expressed hupB gene, Fig. S2). During the growth of M. smegmatis, the genes encoding SMC and MksB are expressed at similar levels (Fig. 1); they peak in the lag and late-exponential phases and are lowest in the stationary phase. This may suggest that SMC and MksB play roles in actively dividing cells.

To examine how deletion of the smc or mksB genes affects chromosome organization, we constructed deletion mutants that additionally produced the HupB-EGFP fusion protein as a chromosome marker37. Studies of mutant strains revealed that deletion of the smc or mksB genes yielded rather mild phenotypes: The growth rates of deletion mutants were very similar to that of the wild-type strain (see Fig. S3A). For Δsmc cells, FM5-95 membrane staining showed that there was a slight lengthening relative to wild-type cells (5.5 ± 0.9 µm and 4.8 ± 0.8 µm, respectively; n = 150; p = 1.6×10-8), suggesting a potential increase in the cell cycle duration. This lengthening was also seen upon cell wall staining with the dye, NADA-green (3.8 ± 0.9 µm and 3.1 ± 0.8 µm for deletion mutant and wild-type, respectively; n = 400; p = 8.1×10-33 (Fig. S3B). While we acknowledge that incorporation of NADA-green in cross-links of newly synthesized PG may act as a stress factor that results in slightly shorter cells, we note that this effect would be proportional for both wild-type and mutant strains, and thus would not alter our conclusions. In the case of the ΔmksB strain, in contrast, we did not detect any significant difference in cell length using either FM5-95 (4.6 ± 0.7 µm and 4.8 ± 0.8 µm for deletion and wild-type cells, respectively; n = 150; p = 0.058) or NADA-green (3.1 ± 0.8 µm for deletion and wild-type cells; n = 400; p = 0.53) (Fig. S3C).

Next, we examined the condensation and global morphology of chromosomes in strains expressing the chromosome marker, HupB-EGFP. Microscopic analysis showed that Δsmc and ΔmksB populations exhibited no significant alteration in chromosome (HupB-EGFP) global morphology (Fig. 2A) or condensation (Fig. 2B) compared to the wild-type control strain. To assess for possible chromatin architecture changes observable under high-resolution imaging, we employed Lattice SIM microscopy combined with Z-stacking to create 3D models of the analyzed nucleoids (Fig. 2C). Once again, our data did not show any significant alteration, with chromosomes exhibiting similar beaded-like structure in mutant and control strains.

Since Δsmc cells were longer than wild-type cells, we examined whether the lack of SMC leads to defects in chromosome dynamics during the cell cycle. Using the HupB-EGFP fusion, we compared the dynamics of daughter chromosome separation in deletion mutant and wild-type control cells (Fig. 3A). Time-lapse experiments revealed that the process of sister chromosome separation (i.e., the time between separation of the sister chromosomes in a mother cell and another separation in the next generation) is prolonged in cells deprived of SMC, taking 149 ± 13 min for Δsmc cells versus 138 ± 15 min for the wild-type strain (n = 150; p = 2.3×10-7; Fig. 3B). Moreover, experiments using FM5-95 membrane dye showed that sister chromosome separation in the deletion mutant was delayed in relation to cytokinesis: In Δsmc and wild-type cells, chromosome separation occurred at 35 ± 7 min and 25 ± 7 min after cytokinesis, respectively (n = 50; p = 1.2×10-10; Fig. 3B). In the case of ΔmksB cells, the sister chromosome separation time was comparable to that of control cells (141 ± 12 min and 138 ± 15 min, respectively; n = 90; p = 0.06; Fig. 3C). These data suggest that SMC, but not MksB, affects chromosome dynamics in the final steps of the cell cycle. To better understand this phenomenon, we performed a real-time study of forced chromosome condensation in the deletion mutant versus the wild-type strain under antibiotic treatment. We used nalidixic acid (NDX) to induce chromosome condensation40, which we tracked utilizing the HupB-EGFP fusion in Δsmc and control cells (see Fig. S4). Following the addition of NDX to the medium, chromosome condensation began after 25 ± 6 min in the deletion mutant and 24 ± 7 min in the wild-type strain (n = 100; p = 0.21), while chromosome decondensation occurred after 112 ± 17 min and 111 ± 18 min, respectively (n = 50; p = 0.73). No significant between-strain difference was observed, suggesting that the role of SMC in chromosome condensation may not be critical.

In sum, our data suggest that the analyzed condensins do not influence the morphology or organization of the chromosome. Deletion of smc yielded a slight lengthening of cells and a small delay in the final steps of the cell cycle, specifically during the separation and subsequent segregation of sister chromosomes to daughter cells; this may explain that the cell cycle was prolonged in the deletion mutant. We speculate that this defect might at least partially reflect that mycobacterial SMC, similar to its homologs in other bacteria (e.g., B. subtilis)21,23, is responsible for maintaining cohesion along the chromosomal arms. Future studies are warranted to test this hypothesis, such as with Hi-C (chromosome conformation capture with sequencing) studies of Δsmc cells.

MksB follows replisomes during the synthesis of chromosomal DNA

To further elucidate the function of mycobacterial condensins in chromosome organization, we decided to examine their subcellular localization. For that purpose, we constructed an M. smegmatis strain producing MksB-HaloTag (Fig. S5) fusion proteins instead of the native MksB. Unfortunately, despite numerous attempts, we were unable to create any SMC fusion.

Snapshot analysis of the MksB-HaloTag cell population stained with the fluorescence ligand, R110 direct (Fig. 4A), revealed that MksB-HaloTag localized in the cell center, with a slightly asymmetric distribution that was closer to the one of the cell poles. Tracking the localization of Mks-HaloTag foci in time-lapse experiments enabled us to visualize the dynamic positioning of this protein during the cell cycle (Fig. 4B): Each MksB-HaloTag fluorescent focus appeared after cell division and was localized in the mid-cell, closer to the old pole. As the cell cycle progressed (indicated by increasing cell length), the MksB-HaloTag focus frequently split into two foci and then merged back together until, finally, the complexes disappeared near the new cell pole. Intriguingly, this choreography of the MksB complexes resembled the positioning and dynamics of replisomes during the cell cycle36,41.

Subcellular localization of MksB-HaloTag fusion protein during the cell cycle. (A) Demograph presenting the subcellular localization of MksB-HaloTag fusion protein (n = 350). (B) Time-lapse images of a representative MksB-HaloTag cells. Scale bar, 1 μm.

To examine whether MksB colocalizes with replisomes in M. smegmatis cells, we constructed a strain producing both a replisome marker (DnaN-mCherry41) and MksB-HaloTag (DnaN-mCherry/MksB-HaloTag strain). Both dnaN-mCherry and mksB-HaloTag were expressed from native promotors in native loci, and DnaN-mCherry/MksB-HaloTag cells exhibited a growth rate similar to that of the wild-type strain (Fig. S5). Real-time monitoring (images taken at 10-min intervals) revealed that MksB-HaloTag (green signal) and DnaN-mCherry (red signal) colocalized during the M. smegmatis cell cycle (see Fig. 5, Movie 1), with their foci appearing simultaneously during replication initiation. The foci colocalized throughout the process of chromosomal DNA synthesis; thereafter, the replisomes and MksB-HaloTag complexes frequently split into two foci, then merged and, after replication termination, finally disappeared. Upon the initiation of a new replication round in daughter cells, the fluorescent foci of both MksB-HaloTag and DnaN-mCherry once again appeared simultaneously (Fig. 5).

Time-lapse imaging of representative DnaN-mCherry/MksB-HaloTag cells with fluorescence profiles along the long cell axis presenting colocalization of DnaN-mCherry and MksB-HaloTag fusion proteins during the M. smegmatis cell cycle. Scale bar, 1 μm.

Similar results were obtained when we analyzed DnaN-mCherry/MksB-HaloTag cells using time-lapse imaging with a shorter time interval (3 min instead of previously used 10 min intervals): The DnaN-mCherry foci colocalized with the MksB-HT foci in 99% of the analyzed cells (n = 300; Movie 2).

Since MksB appeared to accompany replisomes during chromosomal DNA synthesis, we questioned whether the appearance of the MksB-HaloTag complex is related to this process. To address this question, we used griselimycin (GM) treatment to force replisomes to disassemble (Fig. 6A, Movie 3). GM inhibits the interaction of DnaN (a β-clamp subunit of DNA polymerase III) with the catalytic subunit of DNA polymerase III to cause replisome disassembly and premature replication termination42. We previously demonstrated that GM treatment is associated with continued loading of new DnaN subunits onto the oriC region. This implies that up to several duplication events may have occurred in this region, which lead to the generation of multiple DnaN-mCherry foci in a single cell40. In the present work, TLFM experiments showed that, upon GM treatment, the DnaN-mCherry and MksB-HaloTag foci (red and green fluorescence, respectively; Fig. 6A) indeed disappeared simultaneously after 33 ± 8 min (n = 150) post-treatment. Upon removal of GM, the cells restarted chromosome replication and the DnaN-mCherry and MksB-HaloTag foci appeared after 230 ± 24 min (n = 108). Moreover, in cells in which multiplication of the oriC region occurred upon GM treatment, each additional DnaN-mCherry complex was always accompanied by an MksB-HaloTag complex (see Fig. S6).

(C) Boxplots presenting the differences in replisomes mobility during the C phase in ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry (ΔmksB) and DnaN-mCherry (WT) cells.

These results suggest that the condensin, MksB, accompanies replisomes during DNA synthesis in M. smegmatis cells and its chromosomal localization depends entirely on the presence of replisomes. This subcellular localization of MksB is unique among bacteria, as previous reports indicated that MksB is located at the cell poles, such as in C. glutamicum22, or both in the cell center and at the poles, as seen in P. aeruginosa27. We speculate that the MksB complex might be required for the proper organization of newly replicated DNA, which would be a seemingly unusual role for a condensin that has global-range chromosome organizing function.

MksB ensures proper dynamics of the replication forks during replication

Based on our results indicating that the MksB-HaloTag complex is inseparably related to the assembly and maintenance of replisomes during chromosomal DNA replication (see Figs. 5, 6A, Movie 1, Movie 2, Movie 3), we used the DnaN-mCherry fusion protein to compare replication dynamics in MksB-deprived and wild-type cells (ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry and DnaN-mCherry strains, respectively; Fig. S7A). TLFM experiments revealed that the replication time (i.e., phase C of the cell cycle) was comparable in the deletion mutant and control strains (121 ± 10 min vs. 122 ± 10 min, respectively; n = 150; p > 0.05, Fig. S7B) and that cells lacking MksB reinitiated replication43 as often as wild-type cells (11.4% vs. 9.4% reinitiating cells; n = 500; see Movie 4). Intriguingly, the D + B phase, which represents the time between replication termination and the initiation of a new replication round in daughter cells, was shorter in ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry cells compared to control DnaN-mCherry cells (23 ± 9 min and 30 ± 9 min, respectively; n = 150; p = 3.6×10-7; Fig. S7B). The shorter D + B phase in the deletion mutant may suggest that MksB-mediated chromosome organization facilitates the last step of the cell cycle. This is a rather unexpected scenario in light of our observation that MksB complexes accompany replication forks during chromosomal DNA synthesis.

We thus examined whether MksB might also affect replisomes dynamics. In mycobacteria, replisomes frequently undergo splitting and merging, and often appear as two distinct complexes for most of C phase41. Our analysis of replisome positioning during the replication process showed that there were some differences between the mksB deletion mutant and the control strain. Replisomes (indicated by the DnaN-mCherry fusion protein) were less dynamic in the deletion mutant compared to the control strain: In ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry cells, the replisomes colocalized for most of the time, appearing as one fluorescent focus instead of the two foci typically seen in control DnaN-mCherry cells (Fig. 6B). More specifically, DnaN-mCherry cells exhibited two replisome complexes for 56 ± 9% of the replication duration, while ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry cells exhibited separated replisomes for 19 ± 8% of this period (n = 100; p = 2.0×10-10; Fig. 6C). These results may imply that MksB-mediated chromosome organization in the proximity of replisomes affects their dynamics, presumably by organizing the newly replicated DNA and/or by enabling proper spatiotemporal positioning of the replisomes to ensure their dynamic behavior during DNA synthesis.

To determine whether MksB contributes to the disassembly of replisomes and/or is involved in the proper spatiotemporal positioning of replication forks during C phase, we: (1) assessed replisome disassembly upon exposure to GM (see also Fig. 6A) or nalidixic acid (NDX) (Fig. S8A, B), and (2) compared replication dynamics in ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry and control DnaN-mCherry cells upon decreased level of chromosomal DNA superhelicity using novobiocin (NOVO). GM is a DnaN inhibitor, while NDX and NOVO are gyrase inhibitors that cause relaxation of chromosomal DNA40,42. GM and NDX target different enzymes, but both trigger replisome disassembly. NOVO treatment, in contrast, generates a more heterogeneous response, wherein the degree of a cell’s progression through the replication process determines whether it will finish the current replication round or undergo premature termination of replication. Time-lapse experiments showed no significant difference in the replisomes disassembly dynamics of the analyzed strains upon exposure to GM or NDX (for ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry and DnaN-mCherry cells, disassembly took 41 ± 5 min and 40 ± 5 min, respectively, under GM treatment and 42 ± 6 min and 41 ± 5 min, respectively, under NDX treatment; n = 120; p > 0.05; Fig. S8C). This suggests that MksB is not directly involved in replisomes disassembly. In experiments involving NOVO treatment, we observed that, compared to control cells, those deprived of MksB completed the replication process more often under decreased DNA superhelicity. More specifically, 54% of ΔmksB/DnaN-mCherry cells (n = 250) were able to complete chromosomal DNA replication under NOVO treatment, compared to 42% of DnaN-mCherry cells (n = 250; Movie 5). We speculate that under decreased DNA superhelicity, a lack of MksB-mediated DNA organization may facilitate the passage of replication forks during the replication process.

Taken together, our results indicate that the condensin, MksB, might locally organize chromosomal DNA contributing to proper positioning of the replication forks.

MksB and Lsr2 mediate chromosomal DNA organization near replisomes

Our experiments suggested that the mycobacterial condensin, MksB, may play a previously unexpectedly role in chromosome replication. The evidence suggesting this includes our finding that the positioning of MksB-HaloTag is inseparably related to that of the replisome (tracked using DnaN-mCherry; see Figs. 5, and 6A) and our interesting observation that lack of MksB does not disturb the replication process, but rather affects replisome dynamics (Fig. 6B, C). We previously showed that the nucleoid-associated protein (NAP), Lsr2, forms large and dynamic nucleoprotein complexes that follow the replisomes during the first half of the cell cycle and, similarly to our present findings with MksB, lack of Lsr2 limits the dynamics of replisomes45. Moreover, it was recently reported that Lsr2 might act as a roadblock on DNA regulating the replisomes flexibility; in the cells lacking Lrs2, catalytic subunit of DNA polymerase III exchanges less frequently, and the chromosome is replicated more faithfully44. To further explore MksB-mediated local transactions of chromosomal DNA near replisomes, we analyzed the positioning of MksB relative to that of Lsr2. Since these two proteins localize near replication forks, we hypothesized that they may cooperate in organizing the newly replicated DNA and/or assuring the proper spatiotemporal orientation of replisomes. We constructed strains that allowed us to track MksB-HaloTag in a Δlsr2 background and Lsr2-Dendra2 in a ΔmksB genetic background (Δlsr2/MksB-HT and ΔmksB/Lsr2-Dendra2 strains). The lsr2-dendra2 and mksB-HaloTag fusion genes were expressed from their native loci, and the constructed Δlsr2/MksB-HT and ΔmksB/Lsr2-Dendra2 strains exhibited growth rates similar to those of the control strains (Fig. S9A). TLFM experiments revealed that MksB deletion did not appear to alter the dynamics of Lsr2-Dendra2, compared to that in control cells: The splitting time of Lsr2-Dendra2 foci was 151 ± 15 min (n = 99) and 155 ± 10 min (n = 100) for ΔmksB and wild-type cells, respectively (p = 0.046; Fig. S9B). Similarly, our analysis of MksB-HT dynamics in the Δlsr2 background revealed that the time between the appearance and disappearance of MksB-HT was 121 ± 9 min in both strains (n = 200 for Δlsr2 and n = 199 for control; p = 0.5; Fig. S9C). However, the time between the disappearance of MksB-HT and its reappearance in daughter cells was slightly prolonged (31 ± 10 min for Δlsr2, n = 200, and 27 ± 8 min for control, n = 200; p = 1.1×10-5; Fig. S9D). Consistent with our previous results on the dynamics of replisomes45, MksB-HaloTag complexes, similarly to the replisomes, exhibited decreased mobility during the cell cycle of Δlsr2 cells (two MksB-HaloTag complexes were visible for 25 ± 8% versus 50 ± 10% of the replication duration for Δlsr2 cells and control cells, respectively; n = 150, p = 2.0×10-16; Fig. S9E).

In further studies aimed at detecting disruptions of the Lsr2- and MksB-DNA interactions in ΔmksB and Δlsr2 cells, respectively, we employed Single Particle Tracking (SPT). The results of SPT experiments comparing MksB-HaloTag in Δlsr2 versus wild-type cells revealed differences between the analyzed strains (Fig. 7A–C). The mean-squared displacement analysis (MSD) showed that the average diffusion of MksB-HaloTag particles was only a bit lower in Δlsr2 cells compared to control cells (diffusion coefficient, D = 0.22 µm2 s−1 vs. D = 0.26 µm2 s−1, respectively; Fig. 7A). However, because MksB is a DNA-binding protein that is expected to undergo constant transactions, its particles do not represent a homogenous population. Hence, to characterize the mobility of MksB-HaloTag particles, we used the squared-displacement (SQD) analysis (see Fig. S10A). SQD analysis (Fig. 7B) revealed that MksB-HaloTag molecules can be divided into three fractions: fast mobile molecules (D = 0.87 ± 0.002 µm2 s−1), slow mobile molecules (D = 0.166 ± 0.001 µm2 s−1), and confined molecules (D = 0.0402 ± 0.0009 µm2 s−1). In cells lacking Lsr2, the fast and slow mobile populations of MksB-HaloTag protein were smaller than those seen in the control strain (see Fig. 7B), while the confined molecule fraction was almost three times greater in Δlsr2 cells compared to control cells (2917 tracks from 91 cells of Δlsr2/MksB-HaloTag and 3121 tracks from 60 cells of MksB-HaloTag strain; p < 0.001). Moreover, dwell time analysis performed using a two-component model showed that more MksB-HaloTag molecules resided for longer periods of time in a 100-nm radius in Δlsr2 cells compared to control cells (49% and 51% of MksB-HaloTag particles had dwell times of 0.081 ± 0.0022 s and 0.26 ± 0.0072 s, respectively, in Δlsr2 cells compared to 61% and 39% of particles having dwell times of 0.078 ± 0.0018 s and0.22 ± 0.0092 s, respectively, for control cells; p < 0.001). The decreased mobility of MksB particles in the lsr2 deletion mutant may suggest that the bridging activity of Lsr2 is required to maintain proper chromosomal DNA structure near replication forks, and in turn influences the DNA-binding activity of MksB. Additionally, heat maps presenting the likelihood of confined molecule localization (Fig. 7C) showed that MksB-HaloTag localization was limited to the cell center in Δlsr2 cells, whereas it was more scattered along the long cell axis in the control strain. This observation corroborates our TLFM-based observations that the mobility of MksB complexes was decreased in Lsr2-depleted cells, and is consistent with our previous report that replisomes mobility is decreased in the lsr2 deletion mutant45.

Next, we performed analogous analyses of Lsr2-Dendra2 fusion proteins in ΔmksB versus wild-type cells. Our SPT experiments showed that Lsr2-Dendra2 particles were less dynamic in cells lacking MksB compared to wild-type cells. Both mean-squared displacement (MSD) and square displacement (SQD) revealed between-strain differences in the mobility of Lsr2-Dendra2 particles. The MSD results showed that the average diffusion of Lsr2-Dendra2 particles was significantly lower in ΔmksB versus control cells (D = 0.035 µm2 s−1 vs. D = 0.058 µm2 s−1, respectively; Fig. 7D). SQD analysis (see Fig. S10B and Fig. 7E) also revealed that the mobility of Lsr2-Dendra2 was slower in the absence of MksB. In the ΔmksB cells, the fast mobile population of Lsr2-Dendra2 particles was slightly smaller (approx. 21% vs. 28% at D = 1.02 ± 0.004 µm2 s−1, respectively), the slow mobile population was comparable in the two strains (approx. 55% vs. 58% at D = 0.176 ± 0.001 µm2 s−1, respectively), and the confined molecule fraction was 10% larger in ΔmksB cells compared to control cells (approx. 24% vs. 14% at D = 0.0418 ± 0 µm2 s−1, respectively). The differences in population distributions were statistically significant (3809 tracks from 131 ΔmksB/Lsr2-Dendra2 cells, and 4456 tracks from 130 Lsr2-Dendra2 cells; p < 0.001), suggesting that some Lsr2-DNA interactions were altered in the mksB deletion mutant. The increase in the confined molecule fraction and decrease in the fast mobile fraction may indicate that Lsr2 complexes are less dynamic in ΔmksB cells. As we previously showed, efficient DNA binding (i.e., bridging activity) by Lsr2 critically requires the oligomerization domain45. Thus, the locally altered organization of chromosomal DNA under MksB deletion may enhance the bridging activity of Lsr2 to, for example, maintain the topology and/or local organization of chromatin, thereby enlarging the confined particle fraction seen in SPT experiments. Furthermore, the dwell time analysis also showed that a larger subpopulation of Lsr2-Dendra2 particles resided for the longer time period in a 100-nm radius in ΔmksB cells compared to control cells (11% and 89% of Lsr2-Dendra2 particles had dwell times of 0.39 ± 0.002 s and 0.091 ± 0.0005 s, respectively, in ΔmksB cells, whereas 16% and 84% had dwell times of 0.16 ± 0.008 s and 0.082 ± 0.0006 s, respectively, in the control strain; p < 0.001). Thus, more Lsr2 particles stayed on the DNA and remained bound for longer in ΔmksB mutant cells compared to control cells.

In summary, we herein show that cells lacking the condensin, MksB, or the DNA-bridging protein, Lsr2, exhibited local alterations of chromatin structure and/or topology, as reflected by alteration of their DNA-binding dynamics in SPT experiments. Since chromosomal regions near replication forks undergo dynamic transactions during DNA synthesis, we propose that MksB and Lsr2 are reciprocally involved in maintaining local chromatin dynamics. In the Δlsr2 genetic background, the proper MksB-mediated spatiotemporal dynamics of the replication machinery appear to be disrupted. In the lsr2 deletion mutant strain, the mobility of MksB complexes is diminished and single molecules of MksB are less dynamic. In the opposite scenario, the lack of MksB seems to enhance the bridging activity of Lsr2 to maintain local chromatin structure near replication forks. The possible interplay between MksB and Lsr2 proteins will require further investigation.

Conclusions

Our present results suggest that, unlike the situation in other bacteria21,22,24, mycobacterial condensins do not appear to be crucial for maintaining chromosome organization (Fig. 2); instead, they seems to affect chromosome dynamics during the cell cycle (see Fig. 8). The SMC homolog in M. smegmatis appears to play a role in the last steps of sister chromosome separation, which was prolonged in the smc deletion mutant (Fig. 3). The recently identified mycobacterial MksB also seems to play a unique role compared to its homologs from other bacteria29,31. Our data suggest that M. smegmatis MksB may organize chromatin only locally, as it colocalizes with replication forks during chromosomal DNA synthesis (Figs. 5, 6A). In cells lacking MksB, we observed decreased replisomes mobility, which may indicate that MksB is involved in organizing newly synthesized DNA and/or ensuring the proper spatiotemporal positioning of replisomes (Fig. 6B).

Proposed model for the actions of the condensins, MksB and SMC, during the M. smegmatis cell cycle. MksB assists replication forks during chromosomal DNA synthesis, while SMC seems to be required for daughter chromosome separation.

Progression of DNA synthesis lies on DNA topology intertwining topological problems generated by passing replication forks. Our super-resolution microscopy experiments suggest that MksB and Lsr2 have reciprocal influences on the topology of DNA near replication forks (Fig. 7). Together, our novel observations create a unique picture of the involvement of the condensin, MksB, in sustaining chromatin local structure and its dynamic adjustments during DNA synthesis.

Materials & methods

DNA manipulations, bacterial strains, and culture conditions

All plasmids used to construct the M. smegmatis mc2 155 strains were propagated in E. coli DH5α and grown in LB broth or on LB agar plates (Difco) supplemented with the proper antibiotic(s) (ampicillin at 100 μg/ml, kanamycin at 50 μg/ml) and/or other compounds (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-d-galactopyranoside [X-Gal] at 0.004%, isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside [IPTG] at 0.5 mM). DNA manipulations were performed using standard procedures46. Reagents and enzymes were obtained from Thermo Scientific, Roth, and Merck. Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Merck and Genomed, and sequencing was performed by Microsynth. M. smegmatis mc2 155 strains were grown either in 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% ADC (albumin-dextrose-catalase; BD) and 0.05% Tween 80 or on 7H10 agar plates (Difco) supplemented with 10% OADC, 0.5% glycerol, 0.004% X-Gal, and/or kanamycin (50 μg/ml) or 2% sucrose. The M. smegmatis mc2 155 strains and details on their construction are presented in Table S1 and Text S1, and all utilized plasmids and oligonucleotides are listed in Tables S2, S3.

RNA isolation

RNA was isolated as described previously using TriReagent (Invitrogen)39. Each M. smegmatis culture (50 ml) was centrifuged (5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C). The collected cells were resuspended in 300 µl RNase-free water and 900 µl of TriReagent, and disrupted with MP FastPrep (3 × 45 s with 5 min intervals, on ice). The obtained RNA was purified, treated with DNase I (TURBO™ DNase, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and re-purified using the Clean–Up RNA Concentrator (A&A Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and purity of the obtained RNA were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis and using a NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific).

Reverse-transcription and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

For cDNA synthesis, 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed (Maxima First Strand cDNA synthesis kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a final volume of 20 μl. The obtained cDNA was diluted in RNase-free water (1:5) and used for quantitative PCR performed with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The relative expression level of each analyzed gene was quantified using the comparative ΔΔCt method, with the sigA gene amplified as the endogenous control (StepOne Plus real-time PCR system, Applied Biosystems). The optimized oligonucleotides used in this study were synthetized by Sigma-Aldrich (see Table S1).

Fluorescence microscopy

Snapshot imaging was performed using exponential-phase cells (OD600 = 0.8). M. smegmatis cultures were grown overnight in liquid medium and 2 ml of culture was stained with FM5-95 at 0.05 µg/ml and NADA-green at 1.52 µg/ml to visualize the cell membrane and cell wall, respectively, or with Hoechst 33,342 at 0.091 µg/ml to stain the nucleoid. After a 20-min incubation, the cells were centrifuged (6000 rpm, 5 min), smeared onto microscopic slides, and examined with a Leica DM6 fluorescence microscope equipped with a 100 × /1.32 Oil PH3 objective. Images were taken using phase contrast and filters under the following conditions: for DAPI and EGFP, 150-ms exposure time and 30% intensity; and for mCherry, 200-ms exposure time and 35% intensity.

Time lapse fluorescent microscopy (TLFM)

For TLFM, early log-phase M. smegmatis cultures (OD600 = 0.3) were grown in liquid medium. The cells were further grown either in liquid medium using an ONIX microfluidic system or on solid medium utilizing IBIDI dishes, as previously described37,40. Cells were loaded to the ONIX observation chamber (B04A-03-5PK CellASIC ONIX plates for bacteria) and cultured in fresh 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% ADC and 0.05% Tween 80 for 6 h; where indicated, the cells were exposed to an inhibitor (5 µg/ml novobiocin, 500 µg/ml griselimycin (GM), or 50 µg/ml nalidixic acid, (NDX)) for 5 h and then washed for 10 h. For strains carrying HaloTag fusion proteins, the fluorescent ligand R110Direct (Promega) was added at a final concentration of 0.058 µg/ml. For capture of time-lapse images on solid medium, cells were cultured in IBIDI dishes on 7H10 medium supplemented with 10% OADC and FM5-95 membrane dye at 0.05 µg/ml for 20 h. Images were recorded using a Delta Vision Elite inverted microscope equipped with a 100 × /1.4 oil objective at 10-min intervals, under the following parameters: 30-ms exposure time and 5% transmittance on the transmitted light channel; 80-ms exposure time and 32% transmittance on the EGFP channel; and 80-ms exposure time and 50% transmittance on the mCherry channel.

Microscopic data analysis

Microscopic data were analyzed using the Fiji and R software packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria; http://www.r-project.org), including the ggplot2 package47. Data exhibiting Gaussian distribution were analyzed with a two-sided parametric Student’s t test. For non-normal distributions, the statistical significance of differences in measured values was confirmed with the nonparametric two-sided Wilcoxon test using minimum confidence intervals of 0.995.

Lattice SIM imaging

Lattice SIM imaging was performed using an Elyra 7 (Zeiss) inverted microscope equipped with an sCMOS 4.2 CL HS camera and an alpha Plan-Apochromat 100 × /1.46 oil DIC M27 objective. Fluorescence was excited with a 488-nm laser (100 mW), and signals were observed through multiple beam-splitter (405/488/561/641 nm) and laser-block (405/488/561/641 nm) filters. Samples of log-phase cells were prepared on agar pads (1% agarose in 7H9 poured into 1.0 × 1.0-cm GeneFrames; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were stained with SYTO11 chromosomal dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 0.002 µg/ml, and illuminated with a 488-nm laser (100 mW; 0.2% intensity). To generate 3D models of the analyzed nucleoids, each Z-plane was illuminated in Lattice-SIM mode comprising 13 phases, and cells were imaged for 30 ms per phase. Image reconstruction was performed with the ZEN 3.0 SR software (Zeiss) using standard parameters, and 3D models of the obtained data were prepared with the VolumeViewer plugin (Fiji).

Single-particle tracking (SPT)

Cells for SPT experiments were cultured to mid-log phase in rich medium (7H9 supplemented with 10% ADC and 0.05% Tween 80). Slides and coverslips were cleaned with 1 M KOH, washed with milli-Q water, and dried with pressurized air. For experiments involving HaloTag fusions, cells were stained with TMRdirect at 2.5 nM for 1 h in a shaker (37 °C, 180 rpm), and then washed with fresh medium. Before imaging, cells were spread onto agar pads (1% agarose in 7H9 poured into 1.0 × 1.0 cm GeneFrames; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and covered with clean 18 × 18 0.17-mm coverslips. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss Elyra 7 inverted microscope equipped with an sCMOS 4.2 CL HS camera and an alpha Plan-Apochromat 63 × /1.46 Oil Korr M27 Var2 objective. The samples were pre-bleached, the fluorescence was excited with a 561-nm laser (500 mW) as follows: 40% intensity for Lsr2-Dendra2, and 30% for MksB-HaloTag stained with TMRdirect. The signals were observed through multiple beam-splitter (405/488/561/641 nm) and laser-block (405/488/561/641 nm) filters. For each experiment, 10,000 frames were taken in TIRF mode (62° angle) with 10-ms exposure per frame (with transfer time included, the total was 15 ms for Dendra2 fusions and 13 ms for HaloTag fusions) with constant 405-nm excitation (0.02% intensity) for Lsr2-Dendra2. The Z-axis was stabilized via the Definite Focus.2 system (Zeiss), and the temperature was maintained at 37 °C during imaging.

For SPT analysis, spots were identified and merged into tracks using the LoG Detector of the TrackMate v6.0.1 plugin (Fiji). Spot identification was carried out within a diameter of 0.5 μm with sub-pixel localization and median filter activated, and the threshold was set at 5. Track reconstruction was performed with a maximum linking distance of 0.5 μm; no frame gap was allowed, and only tracks consisting of more than four frames were used for further analysis. Comparison and statistical analyses (by default Levene variance test or Student’s t-test) of the resulting single-molecule trajectories were performed in SMTracker 2.0 as described previously48. The data were subjected to dwell time, mean-squared displacement (MSD), squared-displacement (SQD), and clustering analyses. Dwell time was determined for a confinement radius of 100 nm and fitted with two components. MSD was calculated for five time points (with the last time point excluded to avoid track-ending-related artifacts) and fitted to the linear equation. In SQD analysis, the diffusion coefficients (D) were determined from jump distance (JD) data using simultaneous fitting.
