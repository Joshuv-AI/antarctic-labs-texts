Nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes: a retrospective study
Authors: Yi-Hsien Chen, Yu-Wei Fang, Mon-Ting Chen, Hung-Hsiang Liou, Ming-Hsien Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsNonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists such as finerenone mitigate cardiorenal risks in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Real-world evidence comparing finerenone with spironolactone and eplerenone remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the cardiovascular outcomes in T2DM patients treated with finerenone vs. spironolactone or eplerenone using real-world data.Methods and resultsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network database. Adult patients with T2DM who were newly prescribed finerenone, spironolactone, or eplerenone were included (2021–2024). One-to-one propensity score matching was applied to eligible participants, resulting in 2957 finerenone users matching with spironolactone users and 1603 finerenone users matching with eplerenone users. Cardiovascular outcomes, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), heart failure, and mortality, were assessed over 24 months of follow-up. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox regression models. Finerenone users had significantly lower rates of MACE compared with spironolactone users (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.43–0.66) and eplerenone (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.50–0.87). Mortality was also reduced with finerenone vs. spironolactone (HR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.35–0.57) and eplerenone (HR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.41–0.75). Heart failure events were fewer with finerenone than with spironolactone (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.55–0.90) and eplerenone (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.50–0.99). Differences in acute myocardial infarction and stroke rates were not statistically significant.ConclusionFinerenone demonstrated superior cardiovascular outcomes compared with spironolactone and eplerenone in patients with T2DM with significant reductions in MACE, mortality, and heart failure events.

Introduction

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) include the steroidal spironolactone and eplerenone and the nonsteroidal finerenone. They have all been used to manage hypertension and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Mineralocorticoid receptors (MRs) influence electrolytes, fluids, and haemodynamic homeostasis, and MR overactivation has also been shown to lead to inflammatory and fibrotic pathways in the kidney and heart.1 The mechanism of MRAs may come from the modulation of these effects through MR antagonism.

According to previous trials, the steroidal MRAs (spironolactone and eplerenone) improved the outcomes of patients with HFrEF2,3 but offered limited renal benefits and increased hyperkalaemia risk.4 Finerenone, a nonsteroidal MRA, is more selective and has stronger anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects than eplerenone.5,6 In the phase 2B Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonist Tolerability Study (ARTS) trial, finerenone matched spironolactone’s cardiorenal benefits with less hyperkalaemia7 and showed comparable cardiac outcomes to eplerenone in the ARST-Heart Failure trial.8

Previous trials, namely the FIDELIO-DKD (Finerenone in Reducing Kidney Failure and Disease Progression in Diabetic Kidney Disease)9 and FIGARO-DKD (Finerenone in Reducing Cardiovascular Mortality and Morbidity in Diabetic Kidney Disease)10 trials, have shown that finerenone effectively improved the cardiorenal outcomes of patients with T2DM. Finerenone has subsequently been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the indications of reducing the risk of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline, end-stage kidney disease, cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and hospitalization for heart failure in adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and T2DM.

While clinical trials have demonstrated finerenone’s efficacy, real-world data remain limited.11,12 Furthermore, not many studies have compared finerenone with spironolactone and eplerenone to the best of our knowledge. The few that have done so include the ARTS trials, which evaluated the safety and efficacy profile of finerenone against spironolactone or eplerenone. Thus, studies comparing the cardiovascular efficacy of finerenone to that of its steroidal counterparts are in high demand.

To address this knowledge gap, we utilized the TriNetX database to compare the cardiovascular outcomes of T2DM patients on finerenone to those on spironolactone or eplerenone.

Methods

Data sources

TriNetX, LLC, founded in 2013, aims to use real-world data to improve trial design efficiency, enhance site selection, streamline patient recruitment, and generate real-world evidence to facilitate clinical trials addressing global health issues. By building a network of over 220 healthcare organizations in 30 countries, TriNetX connects different healthcare institutions through specialized networks, strictly complying with privacy laws like HIPAA and GDPR to protect patient information. Researchers can use built-in tools to analyze patient data and measure treatment effectiveness.13 The TriNetX database is a widely accepted, trusted resource for publishing clinical findings in healthcare literature.14,15

Study design and study population

This retrospective cohort study with a new user and intention-to-treatment design utilized data from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network to evaluate the cardiovascular outcomes of MRAs use in patients with T2DM. Figure 1 illustrates the flow diagram of included patients. We included patients who were aged ≥18 years, had T2DM, and were users of steroidal (spironolactone or eplerenone) or nonsteroidal (finerenone) MRAs. The initial cohort included 3648 new finerenone users, 208 724 new spironolactone users, and 9070 new eplerenone users from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023. We excluded patients with a history of malignancy and transplant status before the index date or dialysis within 3 months before the index date. Thereafter, the cohorts of participants were refined to 3050 finerenone users, 113 248 spironolactone users, and 4750 eplerenone users. The index date was the first prescription date of the MRAs. A 3-month lag applied to all exposures to minimize protopathic bias and ensure adequate latency after cohort entry.

The schema of patient enrolment in the study. eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate.

To minimize confounding effects, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. This process matched 2957 finerenone users with an equal number of spironolactone users and 1603 finerenone users with an equal number of eplerenone users. This matching ensured that the groups were comparable in terms of baseline characteristics, allowing for a more accurate outcome assessment.

Baseline covariates

In this study, baseline parameters were collected during the 2 years before the index date. We selected parameters for the propensity score model that, although not always direct predictors of the outcome, may influence prescribing practices, patient risk profiles, or healthcare utilization. This comprehensive approach is consistent with current methodological guidance and is intended to enhance the real-world validity and robustness of our findings. They included demographic characteristics [age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and race categories including White, African American, Asian, and Others], comorbidities (hypertensive diseases, dyslipidaemia, nervous system disorders, ischemic heart diseases, upper gastrointestinal disorders, vitamin D deficiency, electrolyte imbalance, disorders of thyroid gland, chronic lower respiratory diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, peripheral vascular disease, COPD, CHF, chronic rheumatic heart diseases, and overweight), medications [lipid-modifying agents, antithrombotic agents, antiarrhythmic drugs, renin-angiotensin system blockers, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, vasodilators, diuretics, vasoprotective agents, insulin, sulfonylureas, biguanides, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitor, drugs for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, psycholeptics, antiepileptics, and analgesics], and laboratory data [eGFR, blood urea nitrogen, serum sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, magnesium, bicarbonate, and chloride levels, haemoglobin level, leukocyte count, platelet count, and albumin, haemoglobin A1c, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, intact parathyroid hormone, iron, ferritin, urine protein and creatinine ratio (UPCR), natriuretic peptide B, and C-reactive protein levels]. (The diagnosis codes are shown in Supplementary Material online, eMethod.)

Study outcomes

In this study, we examined several parameters associated with cardiovascular outcomes, among which were major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) including acute myocardial infarction (AMI), stroke, mortality, and cardiac arrest. In addition, the study focused on heart failure as a major outcome of interest. Furthermore, all-cause mortality was evaluated to provide a comprehensive understanding of the overall risks and impacts related to cardiovascular health. The study focused on outcomes occurring within 3–24 months after the index event, with follow-up data collected until 20 November 2024.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess robustness, we conducted both positive and negative outcome controls. Evidence suggests that finerenone reduces hyperkalaemia and gynaecomastia compared to spironolactone2,16 but increases hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury compared to eplerenone8,17 in patients with T2DM; therefore, these were used as positive outcome controls. Melanoma and ketoacidosis, which have no significant associations with finerenone, spironolactone, or eplerenone in the literature, served as negative outcome controls.

Furthermore, to ensure the reliability of our findings, we performed three distinct sensitivity analyses aimed at addressing potential limitations and verifying the consistency of our results. First, we conducted an outcome analysis using a per-treatment design to account for variations in treatment exposure and its potential impact on the results. Second, we applied various PSM models, stepwise adjusted for multiple confounders, to reduce bias and better isolate the effects of the variables under study. Finally, we analyzed the risk profile across various time frames to account for situations where the proportional hazard assumption was violated, ensuring that our findings remained valid and applicable over different periods.

Statistical analysis

Variables are presented as counts and percentages for categorical data or mean values with standard deviations for numerical data. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess group differences, with an SMD of 0.2 indicating a small effect, 0.5 a moderate effect, and 0.8 a large effect.18 PSM was applied to create two comparable groups (finerenone vs. spironolactone users and finerenone vs. eplerenone users), minimizing baseline confounding. This method ensured group similarity for accurate treatment effect comparisons. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were evaluated, with an SMD of <0.1 indicating proper balance in a given group.19 Our primary PSM model employed all baseline covariates for matching. Sensitivity analyses used models that incrementally added covariates. In the matching process, age at index was analyzed as a continuous variable, while sex, race, and comorbidities were treated as categorical (present or absent). Laboratory data were coded as present if available within the time window and absent otherwise. Propensity scores were calculated via logistic regression and matched using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a 0.1 pooled SMD caliper, utilizing Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and R version 3.4.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The Cox proportional hazard regression model was employed, from which hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated to assess the risk of clinical outcomes between MRAs. The assumption of proportional hazards was assessed using the generalized Schoenfeld approach integrated into the TriNetX platform. The follow-up period started 90 days after the index date, with a maximum duration of 21 months. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to estimate event-free probabilities.

All statistical analyses were performed on the TriNetX platform. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered significant. R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the ggplot2 packages, was used to generate Figures for this study.

Cardiovascular outcomes of finerenone vs. spironolactone users

According to the KM plot of Figure 2, Finerenone consistently shows better outcomes with higher event-free rates across all categories, supported by statistically significant log-rank P-values (<0.001 for MACE and mortality, 0.005 for heart failure). Table 2 compares the cardiovascular and renal outcomes of patients with diabetes on finerenone vs. spironolactone after matching. Finerenone users had significantly lower rates of MACE (5.5% vs. 10.6%, HR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.43–0.66), mortality (3.1% vs. 7.1%, HR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.35–0.57), and heart failure (5.6% vs. 8.3%, HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.55–0.90). Although AMI and stroke rates were lower with finerenone, the differences were not statistically significant.

Cardiovascular outcomes of finerenone vs. eplerenone users

The KM plot in Figure 3 demonstrates that finerenone achieved higher event-free rates across all outcomes, with statistically significant differences (log-rank P-values: 0.003 for MACE, 0.048 for heart failure, and <0.001 for mortality). The occurrence of heart failure events was also reduced with finerenone (5.9% vs. 8.9%, HR 0.70, 95% CI: 0.50–0.99). However, differences in AMI and stroke rates were not statistically significant.

Subgroup analysis

Finerenone significantly reduced MACE and mortality across sex, age, and eGFR subgroups (all P < 0.05), with less pronounced effects on heart failure in females and patients aged 18–64 years (see Supplementary material online, Table S2). Finerenone showed better outcomes than eplerenone in reducing MACE, mortality, and heart failure in males and patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2, with significant HRs and P-values. However, no significant differences were observed in females, younger patients, or those with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2. Results varied across subgroups. (see Supplementary material online, Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis

Positive outcome control analyses demonstrated that finerenone had a significantly lower risk of hyperkalemia and gynaecomastia compared with spironolactone, but higher risks of hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury compared with eplerenone. Negative outcome control analyses found no significant differences in melanoma or ketoacidosis among the groups (see Supplementary material online, Table S4). Our findings remained valid after comparing the risks between nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists using a per-treatment design (see Supplementary material online, Table S5). The results remained consistent across different PSM models after adjusting for multiple confounders (see Supplementary material online, Table S6) across different time frames (see Supplementary material online, Table S7).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that finerenone provides significant reductions in MACE, mortality, and heart failure when compared to spironolactone and eplerenone in patients with T2DM. These findings highlight the superior cardiovascular efficacy and safety profile of finerenone to its steroidal counterparts. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that finerenone could be considered a more favourable treatment option for patients with T2DM who are at high risk of cardiovascular complications. These results reinforce the potential of finerenone to address the unmet needs of this high-risk patient population while maintaining a strong safety profile.

Guidelines recommend finerenone, based on the FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD trials, for patients with T2DM and CKD to reduce kidney disease progression and heart failure hospitalization.20 By following these guidelines, clinicians aim to improve outcomes related to both renal and cardiac health for individuals affected by these comorbid conditions. In contrast, spironolactone and eplerenone are primarily used to treat cardiovascular conditions, including heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, resistant hypertension (high blood pressure unresponsive to multiple medications), and left ventricular dysfunction following myocardial infarction.21–23

Our study contributes to the literature by comparing finerenone to spironolactone and eplerenone for currently approved indications, an area that is understudied. In the ARTS trial, finerenone was shown to lower albuminuria and cardiac biomarkers with similar efficacy while having a significantly lower increase in serum potassium in patients with HFrEF and mild-to-moderate CKD.7 In the ARTS-HF trial, finerenone was shown to induce a ≥ 30% decrease in NT-proBNP levels in a similar proportion of CKD or T2DM patients on eplerenone.8 The FIDELIO-DKD trial showed a lowered risk of composite cardiovascular outcomes (HR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.75–0.99) in patients with CKD and T2DM9; the FIGARO-DKD trial reported a lowered risk of composite cardiovascular outcomes (HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.76–0.98) in patients with T2DM and stage 2–4 CKD with moderately elevated albuminuria or stage 1 or 2 CKD with severely elevated albuminuria.10 The Finerenone in CKD and T2DM: Combined FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD Trial Program Analysis (FIDELITY) similarly reported a reduced risk of composite cardiovascular outcomes though SGLT2i use did not reduce the risk further.16,24 While analyses of the FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD are among the studies examining the efficacy of finerenone, most are conducted within the context of these two trials.25,26 Finerenone was compared with spironolactone in a subgroup comprising patients with treatment-resistant hypertension from the FIDELITY analysis; finerenone was associated with a lower SBP reduction but a lower risk of hyperkalemia and treatment discontinuation compared with spironolactone with or without a potassium binder.27 Combined with the findings of our study, this suggests that a lower rate of treatment discontinuation may contribute to improved outcomes with finerenone use.

In our study, finerenone, when compared to both spironolactone and eplerenone, had significantly lower MACE, mortality, and heart failure events but did not show a difference between AMI and stroke rates. This may be attributed to the short follow-up period, considering that finerenone use is not currently widespread. The results of our subgroup analysis show that finerenone is associated with significantly lower risks of all outcomes in all subgroups compared to spironolactone, except for heart failure in females and in people aged 18–64 years. However, when compared to eplerenone, finerenone only showed significantly lower risks of all outcomes in the male subgroup and lowered mortality risks regardless of eGFR levels. Regardless, considering that finerenone shows superior composite MACE outcomes and heart failure outcomes, our study suggests that it may be preferred as the MRA of choice over spironolactone and eplerenone for T2DM patients with CKD, although the benefits compared to eplerenone may be more subgroup specific.

Currently, several other trials regarding finerenone use are currently underway. The Finerenone in Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced or Preserved Ejection Fraction (FINEARTS-HF) trial demonstrated that in patients with heart failure and mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction, finerenone use resulted in a significantly lower rate of worsening heart failure events and cardiovascular deaths compared to placebo28 The FINE-ONE trial will explore the efficacy and safety of finerenone in patients with type 1 diabetes and CKD29 The CONFIDENCE study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the combination of finerenone and empagliflozin in patients with T2D and CKD.30 As evaluation of the use of finerenone is explored in more populations that would potentially benefit from finerenone use, the use of this drug may be expanded to cover more indications and reach a wider array of heart failure patients within different subgroups. As data on finerenone use becomes more robust, future studies should be conducted to address the need to explore the safety and efficacy of finerenone compared with spironolactone and eplerenone in these cohorts to optimize the treatment strategies used on these patients with unmet needs.

The study has strengths and limitations. Among its strengths, it utilizes the TriNetX database, providing real-world data from a large and diverse population, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The use of PSM minimizes baseline confounding, ensuring comparability between treatment groups and improving the reliability of the results. Additionally, the study offers a comprehensive analysis of multiple cardiovascular outcomes, including MACE, heart failure, and mortality, providing a holistic view of finerenone’s effects compared to those of spironolactone and eplerenone. Robust statistical methods, such as sensitivity analyses and Cox proportional hazard models, further strengthen the validity of the findings. Subgroup analyses also provide valuable insights into the differential effects of treatments across patient populations.

Although our findings are robust, this study has several limitations. First, This retrospective study is subject to bias from using pre-existing electronic health records, which may contain misclassified or incomplete data. The lack of randomization limits causal inference, and unmeasured confounders such as medication adherence, over-the-counter drug use, lifestyle, disease severity, or socioeconomic status—often missing from the database—may have influenced the results. Second, using the TriNetX database may result in incomplete or inaccurate data due to institutional differences in data collection and the lack of cause-of-death information, which may affect the accuracy of the MACE definition. Third, a 24-month follow-up period may not capture long-term cardiovascular and renal outcomes. Furthermore, renal outcomes were not as extensively evaluated as cardiovascular outcomes, leaving a gap in understanding finerenone’s full benefits. Fourth, The TriNetX database lacked information on why MRAs were prescribed, possibly causing confounding. Results should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies should include indication data to clarify patient benefits. Fifth, we could not perform competing risk analysis, which would better account for events like mortality that affect cardiovascular outcomes, because the TriNetX platform does not support it. We recommend that future studies use competing risk methods to validate our findings. Sixth, our new-user design was based on available electronic records, which may have included patients with prior exposure to the studied medications outside the TriNetX network or before the database’s time window. This could compromise the validity of the new-user design and affect our study results. Lastly, the smaller sample size of the eplerenone group may limit the study’s statistical power, and imbalanced parameters between groups could further affect the validity of the comparisons.

Conclusion

Finerenone demonstrated superior cardiovascular outcomes compared with spironolactone and eplerenone in patients with T2DM, significantly reducing MACE, all-cause mortality, and heart failure events. These results indicate that finerenone may offer potential advantages as a nonsteroidal MRA for patients in this context. However, these findings are preliminary, and additional research with larger and more diverse populations is required to confirm these outcomes and to provide more definitive guidance for clinical practice.
