BACE1 Expression Is Required for Proper Synaptic Vesicle Dynamics in the Hippocampus
Authors: John Zhou, Srdjan D Antic, Brati Das, Wanxia He, Dang Minh Tran, Xiangyu Hu, Rafael Fernández‐Chacón, Riqiang Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBACE1 is an indispensable enzyme for the production of β‐amyloid peptides by initiating the cleavage of amyloid precursor protein at the β‐secretase site. Targeting BACE1 inhibition is therefore a therapeutic strategy for treating patients with Alzheimer's disease. However, several clinical trials using brain‐penetrable BACE1 inhibitors have failed due to a lack of efficacy. Previous studies, including our own, have shown that both global and neuron‐specific BACE1 inhibition in mice leads to impairments in synaptic strength and spine density. In this study, we investigate the effects of BACE1 inhibition on activity‐dependent synaptic vesicle exocytosis and endocytosis using a synapto‐pHluorin mouse model. Our results demonstrate impaired synaptic release in BACE1‐deficient mice. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis reveals a significant downregulation of genes related to synapse structure and function. Pathway analysis suggests that BACE1 deficiency significantly downregulates neurexin‐neuroligin pathway, which can modulate docking and release of synaptic vesicles at the presynaptic compartment. Our findings suggest that BACE1 inhibition may lead to deficits in synaptic vesicle exocytosis due to the downregulation of key synaptic proteins. Keywords: BACE1, synaptic plasticity, synaptic vesicles, synapto‐pHluorin, transcriptomic analysis

Introduction

BACE1, also known as β‐site APP (amyloid precursor protein) cleavage enzyme 1, is the sole Alzheimer's β‐secretase to initiate the generation of β‐amyloid peptide (Aβ), the arguably causative agent in Alzheimer's disease (Vassar et al. 2009; Hampel et al. 2020; Bao and Shen 2023). Therefore, BACE1 inhibition has been a main therapeutic target for Alzheimer's disease treatment (Yan and Vassar 2014). Five brain‐penetrable compounds were independently developed and tested in human trials (Das and Yan 2019; McDade et al. 2021). However, several complications have arisen from BACE1 inhibition in clinical trials, largely due to BACE1 cleavages of its neuronal substrates related to synaptic strength and maintenance (Barao et al. 2016; Patel et al. 2022). Ultrastructural analysis has revealed that BACE1 inhibition by two clinically tested compounds in mice, or genetic deletion of Bace1 in mice, both lead to a reduction of synaptic vesicles docked on the synaptic active zones in the hippocampus (Das et al. 2021), concurring with the localization of BACE1 in presynaptic terminals (Kandalepas et al. 2013). These synaptic changes likely contribute to altered long‐term potentiation (LTP) and depression (LTD) of the CA1 and CA3, as well as behavioral deficits (Wang et al. 2008; Hu et al. 2018; Lombardo et al. 2019; Vnencak et al. 2019; Das et al. 2021).

To determine whether reduced docking of synaptic vesicles on the presynaptic active zones due to BACE1 inhibition or deficiency will affect the release of synaptic vesicles and synaptic vesicle pool dynamics, we bred Bace1 +/− mice with Bace1 +/− ;Thy1‐spH mice to generate Bace1 −/− ;Thy1‐spH (Bace1KO‐spH) and Bace1 +/+ ;Thy1‐spH (WT‐spH) as previously done to monitor the synaptic vesicle cycle in knock‐out mice (Rozas et al. 2012). The Thy1‐spH mouse model allowed for direct and real‐time measurement of synaptic vesicle release by utilizing synapto‐pHluorin, a genetically encoded optical indicator of vesicle release that consists of a pH‐sensitive green fluorescent protein (GFP) fused to the luminal side of a vesicle‐associated protein synaptotagmin (Miesenböck et al. 1998; Araki et al. 2005; Li et al. 2005; Burrone et al. 2006). Within the acidic pH environment of synaptic vesicles (pH 5.6), synapto‐pHluorin, the GFP (spH) signal is quenched (Miesenböck et al. 1998). During synaptic vesicle exocytosis, spH is exposed to the neutral extracellular space resulting in an increase in fluorescence at the presynaptic terminal. Following endocytosis, the vesicles become re‐acidified and spH fluorescence is quenched.

By comparing the fluorescent signal intensity between two groups of mice, we showed that Bace1KO‐spH mice exhibited a significant reduction in evoked spH signal amplitude in both the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus compared to WT‐spH mice. Furthermore, recovery of spH following stimulation took a longer time to reach baseline in the CA1 region in Bace1KO‐spH mice compared to littermate controls. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanism, we conducted unbiased transcriptomic analysis, which showed alterations of many synaptic genes including: piccolo (Pclo), activity‐regulated cytoskeleton‐associated protein (Arc), syntaxin‐binding protein 1 (Stxbp1), synapsin‐2 (Syn2), syntaxin‐1 (Syn1), and Synaptotagmin‐11 (Syt11). The reduced expressions of these synaptic genes combined likely contributed to the synaptic deficits observed in the models of BACE1 deficiency. Altogether our data suggest that BACE1 inhibition results in specific presynaptic alterations affecting synaptic vesicle release and vesicle recovery.

Methods

Mouse Strains and Breeding Strategy

Bace1 +/− mice were originally generated in the laboratory of Phillip Wong at Johns Hopkins University as previously described (Cai et al. 2001), and we acquired Bace1 +/− mice from Dr. Wong. Bace1 +/− were crossed with B6;CBA‐Tg(Thy1‐spH) (briefed as Thy1‐spH) (a generous gift from Dr. Weichun Li) to generate Bace1 +/− ;Thy1‐spH. Bace1 +/− ;Thy1‐spH mice were further bred with Bace1 +/− to obtain Bace1 −/− ;Thy1‐spH (designated Bace1KO‐spH) and Bace1 +/+ ;Thy1‐spH (designated WT‐spH) littermates for our experimental comparisons. Resultant mice were confirmed by genotyping. All mice were socially housed in a climate‐controlled environment with a 12:12 light: dark cycle and fed standard rodent chow and water ad libitum. A maximum of 5 mice (a total of less than 125 g/cage) of the same gender were housed in a standard mice GM500 cage (Tecniplast). Male and female mice at the age of 7 months or older were selected for phenotypic analyses. On average, mouse body weight was approximately 33 g (male) and 30 g (female) at this age.

All experimental protocols (AP‐200998‐0826) were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Connecticut Health in compliance with the guidelines established by the Public Health Service Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory animals. For electrophysiology, immunohistochemical and biochemical experiments: head decapitation under an overdose of isoflurane. Animals from 2 to 12 months of age were used for various purposes. At least 3 pairs of Bace 1 −/− mice and WT mice (about 20 g) of mixed gender were used for microarray experiments. Numbers of mice used in individual studies were specified in the relevant section.

Confocal Imaging

For confocal imaging of Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH hippocampi, 200 μm, horizontal brain sections were cut by vibratome (Camden Instrument 7000smz‐2) in ice‐cold ACSF (160 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM D‐Glucose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM KH2PO4, and 1 mM MgSO4 with pH adjusted to 7.4). Afterwards, sections were recovered in N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine solution (160 mM N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM D‐Glucose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM KH2PO4, and 1 mM MgSO4 with pH adjusted to 7.4) at 30°C for at least 30 min. Sections were then transferred to a cover glass bottom 35 mm petri dish, anchored with a harp; the chamber was perfused with 37°C ACSF (1 mL/min) followed by imaging with Zeiss 800 confocal microscope.

spH Recordings

Sample sizes were not calculated a priori but were based on previous publications. For in vivo spH analysis, we adopted the protocols discussed in the following studies (Nicholson‐Fish et al. 2016; Taipala et al. 2022). For acute slice spH optical recordings, brain slices were prepared the same way as slices used in confocal imaging. A total of at least 3 sections from Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH littermates were used for optical recordings of evoked synaptic release. For each genotype group, 4 female (~25 g for female mice) and 2 male mice (~30 g for male mice) were used. No significant sex differences were found. By using a 60× objective lens, each field of view was placed in dendritic layers proximal to either the CA1 or CA3 regions (approximately 100 μm from the pyramidal cell layer). A stimulating glass electrode was placed near the center of the field of view. For the recordings of evoked synaptic release, the stimulation pattern consisted of one train of 20 Hz pulses at 1 ms duration per pulse, 135 nA intensity. Train durations were varied between 3 (60 pulses) and 9 s (270 pulses). Fields of view (FOVs) containing between 3 and 10 viable puncta (responding to stimulation with optical signals) were treated as regions of interest (ROI) (Figure S1). Sections without viable puncta were excluded from recordings. The LED light source (CoolLed, UK, 470 nm) intensity was set at 3%. spH fluorescence was measured with a NeuroCCD camera (RedShirtImaging, Decatur, GA) using a filter cube, excitation: 480/40 nm; dichroic 510 nm, and emission: 535/50 nm. Frame interval was set at 72 ms and 700 frames were recorded for each optical trial, yielding a total trial duration of 50.4 s. Recordings of the resting fluorescence “F” (mV) and fractional changes in the fluorescence intensity of spH (ΔF/F in %) were both performed with NeuroCCD controlled by the Neuroplex software. Optical signals measured with no stimulation (“no stimulus” recordings) were used for bleach corrections.

Analysis of spH Optical Signal

For baseline recordings, mean values of resting fluorescence intensity (F) for each ROI of the CA1 and CA3 were compared between Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH using a Mann–Whitney test in GraphPad.

For evoked recordings, both resting fluorescence intensity (F) and change in fluorescence intensity data (ΔF/F) were processed in Excel. Statistical analysis and graphs were created in GraphPad. Scatterplot graphs represent average values for all ROIs from each genotype in either the CA1 or CA3 region and for the indicated stimulus duration. The mean values of the average peak amplitudes from each mouse were compared between Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH using a Mann–Whitney test. These comparisons included either the CA1 or the CA3 region. In the bar graphs for peak amplitude, each data point represents the average peak ΔF/F amplitude per mouse. For evaluating evoked spH response dynamics, a binomial filter (Neuroplex) was applied to signal traces; then fluorescence intensities were normalized to the maximum fluorescent intensity of the ROI trace and converted to percentage (% of the peak spH fluorescence).

During an electrical stimulus, vesicle exocytosis is the dominant process resulting in spH increase (Miesenböck et al. 1998; Sankaranarayanan et al. 2000). To mathematically describe the changes in spH intensity during exocytosis induced by stimulation, % of the peak spH fluorescence traces during the stimulus period were fitted to a one‐phase exponential association curve model using GraphPad (R 2 values > 0.85). Gene Microarray and Analysis

For gene microarray, each hippocampus from 3 pairs of P60 female Bace1 −/− (designated Bace1KO) and Bace1 +/+ (designated WT) was dissected and total RNA was extracted by the standard Trizol protocol. Samples were then submitted to Cleveland Clinic Genomics Core, which processed total RNA to create biotinylated cDNA using the Ambion kit. Biotinylated cDNA was hybridized to the Illumina Mouse Ref‐8 array then stained and visualized using fluorescently labeled streptavidin. Arrays were then scanned with an Illumina BeadArray Reader. Gene expression data (normalized total input cDNA) was then subjected to prefiltering, where gene expression intensity less than 30 was considered background and omitted from downstream analysis. Two Way ANOVA and post hoc Šídák's correction for multiple comparisons and adjusted p‐values were used to compare means of normalized gene expression values between genotypes. Data normality/sphericity was not assumed and was corrected using Geisser–Greenhouse. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a p < 0.05 were considered significant. DEGs related to synapse function and maintenance according to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) annotations (Qiagen) were selected for comparing data obtained from single nuclei RNA seq. Selected genes were further tested by Western blotting experiments.

Single Nuclei RNA Seq

For single nuclei RNA seq analysis, previously published single nuclei data sets of Bace1 fl/fl and Bace1 fl/fl;Thy1‐cre mice hippocampi were used (GSE217960) (Benoit et al. 2023). Briefly, to generate single nuclei data, nuclei from eight hippocampi from P192 male and female Bace1 fl/fl and Bace1 fl/fl;Thy1‐cre mice were extracted and loaded onto 10× Chromium controller to perform single‐nuclei partitioning and barcoding. Raw sequencing from the NovaSeq6000 was aligned and annotated using the CellRanger v3.1.0 pipeline. These services were performed by the Single Cell JAX Genomics Core. For single nuclei RNA analysis, Seurat v4.0 was used as described (Benoit et al. 2023). Expression profiling based on 2000 genes were used for UMAP clustering and SCTransform was used to normalize gene expression and data analyses as suggested (Wang et al. 2022). Differential gene expression analysis from excitatory neuron clusters (clusters with positive expression for Syt1, Slc17a7, and Neto1) was then performed using Wilcoxon Ranked Sum with Bonferroni correction for adjusted p‐values. Previously identified synaptic genes of interest that were differentially expressed from bulk gene microarray data comparing Bace1KO and WT were then selected for the single nuclei data set. Synaptosome Isolation and Western Blotting

Synaptosomes were isolated as previously described (Das et al. 2021). In brief, ~1‐year‐old female Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH hippocampi (N = 3) were dissected and synaptosomes were extracted using the Syn‐PER kit (Millipore). Synaptosomes were washed with ice‐cold PBS before being lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.4], 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, 1 μg/mL pepstatin, and 10 μg/mL aprotinin) for 10–30 min at 4°C with rotation. The lysates were collected, and further bath sonicated on ice for 30 s on and off cycles at 60 Hz for 5 min and then centrifuged at 15000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were loaded on 4%–12% SDS‐PAGE (NuPAGE system, Life Technologies) gels with MES running buffer (Thermofisher) for electrophoresis. Gels were run with Seeblue Plus 2 ladder and electrophoresis continued at 120 V for 3 h, until the 19 kDa marker was run off the gel. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 120 V for 3 h. The membranes were washed with PBS with 1:2000 20% Tween‐20 and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.5% evaporated milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were probed with primary antibody (see table for antibody list), followed by incubation with appropriate secondary HRP‐conjugated antibody (1:2000). The antibody‐bound proteins were detected by an iBright 1500 imaging system (Invitrogen). To ensure equal loading, the blots were reprobed with monoclonal anti‐actin (1:10000). For quantification purposes, band intensities of immunoblots were analyzed using image J software.

Statistics and Data Analysis

The Neuroplex software (RedShirtImaging) was used for extraction of F values and spH traces (intensity versus time) as described (Popovic et al. 2015). Organization of data points and initial analyses were performed in Excel 365. Statistical comparisons (mean, standard deviation, count, standard error of mean) and graphs were created using GraphPad 10. For analysis of electrical recordings, no significant sex differences were detected. For baseline recordings, average fluorescence intensity of ROIs for each field of view is displayed as an individual data point. For evoked recordings, average values for each mouse are displayed as data points within histograms. Histograms indicate mean values; error bars indicate one SD. For baseline and evoked 3 and 9 s duration recordings, Mann–Whitney test was used and p < 0.05 was considered significant. No outliers were excluded in this study. For gene expression analysis, Mann–Whitney with Bonferroni correction for adjusted p‐value was used to compare means of normalized gene expression values between genotypes. As discussed in the publication (Benoit et al. 2023), due to the experiments consisting of multiple samples, the neighborhood graph was batch‐corrected using the batch correction software BBKNN (Polański et al. 2020). DEGs with a p < 0.05 were considered significant. For Western Blot Results, D'Agostino and Pearson test determined that the data was normally distributed; then a Two‐way ANOVA with Sidak multiple comparison test was used to measure differences between genotypes. Except for transcriptomic assays which were conducted blindly, all other assays were not performed in a blind manner.

Results

BACE1 Deletion Does Not Alter Resting spH Signal or Distribution

Previously, electron microscopy studies showed a marked reduction in docked vesicles at presynaptic active zones of the hippocampus of Bace1 −/− mice compared to WT (Bace1 +/+ ) littermates (Das et al. 2021). To understand whether this reduction would affect the release of synaptic vesicles, we investigated neural transmission activity at the synapse by introducing the GFP‐based synaptopHluorin (spH) sensor in Bace1‐null mice to monitor vesicular exocytosis. This sensor is expressed under the control of the neuron‐specific Thy‐1.2 promoter (Tabares et al. 2007). By crossing Bace1 +/− mice with Bace1 +/− ;Thy1‐spH mice, we obtained Bace1KO‐spH (Bace1 −/− ;Thy1‐spH) mice and WT‐spH (Bace1 +/+ ;Thy1‐spH) mice. We first compared the distribution of synaptic spH labeling in CA1 and CA3 regions of 1‐year‐old Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice by confocal microscopy. Our results revealed no discernible differences (Figure 1A). Resting spH fluorescence intensity (F) at CA1 and CA3 was also similar between Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice (Figure 1B). These observations suggest that BACE1 deficiency does not appear to affect synaptic vesicle distribution in the hippocampus at the meso scale.

BACE1 Deletion Results in Altered Evoked spH Signal

Because BACE1 deficiency results in altered synaptic function and fewer vesicles near the active zone (Das et al. 2021), we hypothesized that BACE1 deficiency may alter activity‐dependent synaptic vesicle dynamics. To test this hypothesis, we performed optical recordings of spH fluorescence levels while electrically stimulating in acute slices from Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH. In acute brain slices, extracellular electrical stimulation induces population synaptic potentials—synchronized release of glutamate causing widespread depolarizations (Milicevic et al. 2023). Resting synaptic vesicles are acidic, resulting in quenched spH signal. Electrical stimulation results in spH vesicle exocytosis and exposure to the more basic extracellular environment, which in turn unquenches modified GFP. During and after stimulation, vesicle endocytosis and reacidification result in reduced spH signal. Although the net evoked spH signal represents the balance of vesicle exocytosis and endocytosis, an increase in the spH signal during the stimulus is primarily due to the exocytosis of vesicles, and the recovery of the spH signal to baseline after the end of stimulation is due to endocytosis.

Brain slices were prepared from Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice and stimulated with a train of pulses (20 Hz) for 3 s (a total of 60 stimulation pulses). The peak spH, signal expressed as ΔF/F, was reduced for Bace1KO‐spH to only half of that found in WT‐spH. Both hippocampal regions, CA1 and CA3, were affected (Figure 2A,B). These results suggested a BACE1‐associated deficit in the vesicle release dynamics.

Next, we examined the temporal dynamics of the evoked spH signals, which rise during a 3‐s stimulation train—in the period of time when synaptic vesicles undergo exocytosis (Figure 2C, Figure S1A,B). Following the cessation of a stimulus train, in the period of time when synaptic vesicles undergo endocytosis, the spH signals decay as the vesicles' pH recovers and returns to base line levels (Figure 2D, Figure S1A,B). To quantify the ON and OFF dynamics of vesicle releases in a population of hippocampal neurons, the recorded spH signals were normalized to the peak amplitude of individual traces (each sample expressed as a % of the peak spH), averaged across experimental measurements in multiple brain slices, and fit to a one‐phase association curve (see methods for details). During the stimulus‐evoked release of vesicles, the time constant (tau) of this association curve is primarily due to exocytosis (Nicholson‐Tomishima and Ryan 2004), with shorter tau values suggesting increased rates of vesicle releases. To measure the recovery of spH, the decay of the spH optical signal after stimulation was fit to a one‐phase decay curve (see methods for details). A post‐stimulation spH recovery is primarily due to endocytosis, with increased tau values suggesting decreased vesicle recovery rates (Nicholson‐Tomishima and Ryan 2004). In experiments using the 3‐s stimulation paradigm, the rates of both vesicle release and vesicle recovery were similar between the two experimental groups (Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice), in two hippocampal regions examined: CA1 and CA3 (Figure 2C,D and Figure S1).

To further validate this observation, we examined the steady state of the exocytosis/endocytosis process with a modified stimulation paradigm encompassing a longer stimulus train of 9 s (180 pulses). With a 9‐s stimulation paradigm, we again observed peak‐amplitude differences between Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice (Figure 3A). On average, the CA1 and CA3 peak spH amplitudes in Bace1KO‐spH mice were only 55% ± 11% and 44% ± 11% of the equivalent signal obtained in the WT‐spH mice (Figure 2B). With 9 s stimulation trains, we once again observed no differences in exocytosis tau (ON tau) between the Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH conditions (Figure 3C, Figure S2A,B), confirming the lack of a significant effect of the BACE1 status (e.g., inhibition) on the rate of vesicle release.

While the exocytosis phase (ON tau) appeared unaffected by the BACE1 activity, the endocytosis phase (OFF tau) showed an effect. In both CA1 and CA3 regions, the lack of BACE1 activity caused prolongation of recovery. The average tau values were markedly larger in BACE1‐KO compared to control (Figure 3D). Furthermore, in the CA1 hippocampal region, the post‐stimulus recovery endocytosis tau was almost 75% greater in Bace1KO‐spH compared to WT‐spH. Altogether, our results suggest that BACE1 deficiency alters the stimulus‐evoked net vesicle release causing a robust loss in the optical signal amplitude (Figure 3B). While the number of vesicles is reduced, the average rate of exocytosis is not affected (Figure 3C), but the post‐stimulus vesicle recovery at synaptic terminals is significantly slowed in at least one region of the hippocampus (Figure 3D).

Bace1 Deletion Results in Reduction of Synaptic Vesicle Assembly Components

To explore possible mechanisms underlying deficits in synaptic vesicle dynamics upon Bace1 deletion, we conducted unbiased microarray experiments using three pairs of 2‐month‐old female hippocampal tissues from Bace1KO and control littermates. By analyzing the sequencing results, we noted that Bace1 expression was reduced by approximately 75% in Bace1KO mice when compared to WT controls (Figure 4A), in line with the expectation. After filtering for “nervous system development” (GO:0007399) genes, we highlighted genes that were differentially expressed in Bace1KO hippocampi compared to controls as shown in the volcano plot and heatmap (Figure 4A,B). Among these DEGs, Fxyd6, Enpp2, and Clic6 were elevated while synaptic genes were downregulated in Bace1KO hippocampi compared to the controls (Figure 4A). The notable synaptic genes are Syp (synaptophysin), Polo (piccolo), Syn1 (synapsin 1), Syn2 (synapsin 2), Syt1 (synaptotagmin 1), Ppp1r9b (spinophilin).

We also compared our microarray data with single nuclei RNA seq data (snRNA‐seq) of hippocampi from neuronal specific deletion of Bace1 (Bace1 fl/fl;Thy1‐cre) that has been previously published by our group (Benoit et al. 2023). Specifically, we compared synaptic‐related DEGs identified from the microarray dataset to that performed by pseudo‐bulk RNAseq analysis of excitatory neuronal nuclei from Bace1 fl/fl and Bace1 fl/fl;Thy1‐cre dataset. We found a number of consistently altered genes, which are common to both microarray and snRNA‐seq (highlighted in Table 1). Divergent gene expression between microarray and snRNAseq datasets was also detected, and this divergency could be due to a number of experimental factors (differences in age, global vs. neuronal‐specific knockout of Bace1, whole tissue vs. single nuclei), as well as differences between sensitivity of microarray and RNAseq.

A select number of altered genes from microarray or snRNAseq datasets were chosen for validation of changes in the protein levels. To this purpose, we performed Western blot of a number of these genes using hippocampal synaptosomes from Bace1KO‐spH and WT‐spH mice. We found that protein levels of Pclo, Ppp1r9b, Syn2 (Synapsin 2), and Arc were significantly decreased on the western blots (Figure 5). Pclo, Ppp1r9b, and Arc were reduced by approximately 60% in Bace1KO‐spH mice, while Syn2 reduction was more modest (~15%). The reduction of these synaptic proteins is likely to contribute to the deficits in synaptic physiology revealed by population optical imaging of vesicle release (Figures 2 and 3). Syt1 (Syntaxin 1) and Syp (Synaptophysin) levels were not significantly different in Bace1KO‐spH, despite a 10% reduction of gene expression according to microarray results from Bace1KO mice. Protein levels of Stxbp1 (also known as Munc18) monomers were not obviously changed, but a possible Stxbp1 heterodimer appeared to be reduced in Bace1KO‐spH mice. Interestingly, the Western blot analysis of synaptosomes reveals significant differences in synaptic proteins that did not reach significance during transcriptomic analysis i.e., Pclo, Syt11, Stxbp1, and Arc. This could potentially be due to the dichotomy of synaptic proteomics versus whole tissue transcriptomics.

To further explore the potential underlying mechanisms that may contribute to this deficit, we performed Ingenuity Pathway Analysis based on genes from microarray experimental results. As shown in (Figure 5C), Neurexins and Neuroligins, Synaptogenesis Signaling Pathway, SNARE Signaling Pathway, Netrin Signaling, Acetylcholine Receptor signaling, CREB Signaling and Clathrin‐mediated Endocytosis were top gene sets that were suppressed in BACE1KO mouse hippocampi. GABAergic Receptor Signaling Pathway (Enhanced) and Corticotropin Releasing Hormone Signaling were gene sets that were activated in BACE1KO mouse hippocampi. Since neurexin, neuroligins and netrin are known to regulate presynaptic vesicle dynamics and could act as substrates of BACE1 (Lichtenthaler et al. 2018), BACE1 cleavage may regulate their roles in synaptic transmission. Hence, this pathway analysis adds additional evidence that BACE1 inhibition can alter presynaptic vesicle dynamics.

Discussion

Alzheimer's β‐secretase, mostly well known as BACE1, is the most richly expressed by neurons in the brain. In addition to cleaving APP to generate Aβ peptides, as a type I transmembrane aspartyl protease, BACE1 will also cleave multiple membrane‐bound substrates such as Sez6, neuregulin‐1 and ‐3, Jagged‐1 and CHL1 (Hampel et al. 2020). Intriguingly, many of these BACE1 substrates have a role in the control of synaptic functions, in line with the observed impaired synaptic strength and impaired cognitive functions in mice with BACE1 deficiency.

In this study, we investigated whether BACE1 deficiency will affect the physiological release of synaptic vesicles. By examining Bace1‐null mice expressing GFP‐based synaptopHluorin (spH) under the control of neuron‐specific Thy‐1.2 promoter (Tabares et al. 2007), we were able to monitor synaptic release upon stimulation. Uniform and region‐wide expression of the fluorescent indicator combined with an excellent sensitivity of the optical recording setup allows for robust measurements of spH population signals. By examining multiple brain slices, we noted that synaptic transmissions are indeed altered, reflected by decreased spH optical signal amplitudes (Figures 2 and 3), in line with fewer synaptic vesicles docked on the active zone. Intriguingly, the rate of vesicle release was not altered in our observations (Figure 2C) while endocytosis of released vesicles was also decreased upon BACE1 deficiency (Figure 2D). The reduced spH optical signal amplitudes in BACE1‐deficient mice during both 3‐ and 9‐s duration trials could be the result of a smaller vesicle pool size, as suggested by previous studies based on high‐resolution fluorescence imaging (Rozas et al. 2012).

Reduced spH peak amplitude in BACE1‐deficient mice could also be due to defects in vesicle release, as previously suggested by paired pulse facilitation observed in BACE1‐deficient mice (Laird et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2008; Vnencak et al. 2019). Furthermore, BACE1 deficiency resulted in disrupted vesicle recovery after stimulation in a CA1‐specific manner. This result could reflect region‐specific changes of LTD in Schaeffer collateral vs. mossy fiber presynaptic terminals previously observed in BACE1‐null mice.

While it is challenging to pinpoint the exact molecules responsible for the deficiency in synaptic transmission by BACE1 deficiency, we decided to gain insights from unbiased transcriptomic assays. In our microarray experiments, Bace1 mRNA levels are reduced by ~75%, measured by microarray probes covering the entire transcript. However, the reduction of Bace1 mRNA, detected by a poly‐A tail selected transcript for snRNAseq experiments, was significantly less substantial. We decided to use the DEGs found in the microarray results to compare DEGs in the snRNAseq experiments reported by our lab recently (Benoit et al. 2023). We found that many molecules, which are important for synaptic transmission, endocytosis or membrane trafficking, are reduced upon BACE1 deficiency in mice (see volcano plot in Figure 4 and Table 1). The most significantly reduced gene in the microarray dataset is Tmem25, a gene encoding a protein shown to induce acidification changes in lysosome compartments and accelerate the degradation of NMDA receptor subunit 2B in neurons (Zhang et al. 2019). The reduction of Tmem25 in the brains was linked to hyperexcitation and epilepsy, a phenotype which is also seen in Bace1‐null mice (Hu et al. 2010; Hitt et al. 2012; Yan et al. 2012). Noticeably, the reduction of Tmem25 was not obvious in the snRNAseq dataset; likely this difference is related to bulk‐Seq vs. snRNAseq and a global vs. neuronal deletion of Bace1.

By comparing two transcriptomic datasets, we identified several synaptic functional genes that are consistently downregulated due to BACE1 deficiency in neurons. Among them, Pclo and Syn2 are both synaptic active zone scaffold proteins, which regulate synaptic vesicle fusion assembly to ensure docking, fusion, and recycling (Südhof and Rizo 2011; Jin and Zhai 2023). Downregulation of these proteins could underlie functional changes in vesicle dynamics observed in Bace1KO‐spH mice. Syt11 is a known essential component of the neuronal vesicular trafficking pathway, and its reduction has been shown to cause deficits in LTP (Shimojo et al. 2019). Presynaptic reduction of Syt1 (Figure 4) could explain a decrease in exocytosis (Geppert et al. 1994; Fernández‐Chacón et al. 2001); if localized in the postsynaptic compartment, it may reduce LTP by decreasing levels of synaptic or extrasynaptic AMPA receptor trafficking events (Wu et al. 2017).

While not a focus in this study, BACE1 deficiency can also cause a reduction in spine density as shown by another group (Pratsch et al. 2023). We also identified downregulation of Arc, which has been identified as a regulator of the trafficking of neurotransmitter receptors to the membranes of post synaptic terminals (Urbanska et al. 2012). Ppp1r9b (spinophilin), a dendritic spine‐enriched protein, is shown to regulate NMDAR phosphorylation and function (Salek et al. 2023). Its reduction or deficiency will limit GluN2B surface expression.

How Bace1 deficiency will lead to altered expression of these synaptic proteins remains to be elucidated in future as many BACE1 substrates can regulate signaling changes. In our pathway analysis, we found that the neurexin and neuroligin pathway is one of the top three pathways, downregulated by BACE1 deficiency in the hippocampus (Figure 5C). It has been shown that presynaptic neurexin 3 is shown to be cleaved by α‐ and γ‐secretase to release its intracellular domain for cellular functions (Bot et al. 2011). However, its cleavage by BACE1 remains to be fully established. Many type I transmembrane proteins such as APP, neuregulin‐1, CX3CL1 were shown to be cleaved by both α‐ and β‐secretase (Lichtenthaler et al. 2018). Both neurexins and neuroligins are type I transmembrane proteins and have been suggested to be BACE1 substrates based on proteolytic proteomic assays (Kuhn et al. 2012). Neurexins are localized and enriched at synapses while neuroligins are localized on the postsynaptic membranes. The binding of neuroligin on the postsynaptic membrane to neurexin on the presynaptic compartment controls synaptogenesis and neural transmission (Nam and Chen 2005; Südhof 2018). Upon its binding by neuroligin, the intracellular domain of neurexin, upon cleavages by secretases, is shown to mediate the recruitment of synaptic vesicles to the active zone (Dean et al. 2003; Gokce and Südhof 2013). Neurexin ICD directly interacts with Syt1 (Hata et al. 1993), Mint/Cask, which in turn recruits Stxbp1 (Biederer and Südhof 2000; Sun et al. 2009; Lin et al. 2019), Syt11 (Trovò et al. 2024), and Ppp19rb (Khalaj et al. 2020). Given that our microarray and/or synaptosome Western blot data suggest that BACE1 inhibition downregulates Syt1, Stxbp1, Syt11, and Ppp19rb, it is speculated that abrogated BACE1 cleavage of neurexin will disrupt presynaptic terminal function. If pan‐neurexin function is disrupted, synapse stability is compromised along with a reduction in readily releasable synaptic vesicle pool size (Quinn et al. 2017). Since the neurexin family has three family members (Reissner et al. 2013), it will be intriguing to know the precise role of these members to be regulated by BACE1‐mediated cleavage in future.

Several other BACE1 substrates can also control synaptic plasticity. For example, Netrin 1 is another type I signaling molecule which is a key driver of axonal growth and synaptic maturation (Glasgow et al. 2021). BACE1 inhibition may also contribute to presynaptic development through modulation of its binding to a classical Netrin 1 receptor DCC (Deleted in colorectal carcinoma), which is cleaved by BACE1 (Taylor et al. 2022). Alternatively, APP is shown to act as a functional Netrin 1 receptor (Lourenco et al. 2009); its cleavage by BACE1 will disrupt the Netrin‐APP interaction. Furthermore, the extracellular domain of neuroligin 1, after cleavage by α‐secretase or BACE1, has been shown to play a role in stimulating presynaptic terminal maturation through binding to the ErbB4 receptor (Mei and Nave 2014). Hence, future studies will be required to pinpoint how BACE1 governs the release of synaptic vesicles, and this knowledge is likely helpful for developing a strategy for minimizing synaptic side effects due to strong BACE1 inhibitions in AD patients.
