Fatty-acid-based antimiR-23b delivery in the DMSXL model: A potential therapeutic strategy for brain dysfunction in myotonic dystrophy type 1
Authors: Diego Piqueras-Losilla, Andrea Garcia-Rey, Aline Huguet-Lachon, Argimiro Mayoral-Olmos, Isabel Campillo, Melanie Nufer, Mouli Chakraborty, Ana Díaz-Maqueda, Nuria Barquero, Anchel Gonzalez-Barriga, José Martinez-Hernandez, María Gracia de Garnica García, Geneviève Gourdon, Ruben Artero, Beatriz Llamusí, Estefanía Cerro-Herreros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMyotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a severe neuromuscular disorder caused by CTG repeat expansions in the DMPK gene, leading to the formation of toxic RNA foci that sequester essential splicing regulators MBNL1/2. Beyond muscle impairment, DM1 affects also the brain, leading to significant cognitive deficits, behavioral abnormalities, and intellectual disabilities. This study evaluates the therapeutic potential of the lipid-conjugated antimiR-23b, X82108, designed to promote MBNL1/2 upregulation through inhibition of miR-23b. Systemic administration of X82108 in mice and non-human primates efficiently crosses the blood-brain barrier, increasing MBNL1 in the brain. In DMSXL transgenic mice, treatment increases Mbnl1/2, reduces toxic DMPK, and restores normal splicing patterns across all brain regions. These molecular improvements correlate with improved behavioral outcomes, including reduced impulsivity and normalized exploratory activity. Collectively, the findings highlight X82108 as a promising systemic therapy for DM1, targeting not only muscular features as we have previously shown but also DM1-related CNS alterations.Keywords: myotonic dystrophy, antisense oligonucleotides, miR-23b, MBNL, alternative splicing, brain delivery, blood-brain barrier, CNS alterations, behavior correction, rare disease

Introduction

Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a rare genetic disease with severe neuromuscular symptoms and is the most common form of muscular dystrophy in adults. The congenital phenotype, CDM1, is particularly aggressive, affecting not only muscles but also various organs, including the central nervous system (CNS).1,2,3 Among the various symptoms, cognitive deficits are particularly prominent in congenital cases, including intellectual disability and impairments in memory, attention, and processing speed. Excessive daytime sleepiness is also widely seen.4,5 The complex CNS profile of DM1 indicates involvement of multiple brain regions, with frontal lobe dysfunction linked to executive impairments and hippocampal neuropathology associated with visuospatial memory and learning deficits.6,7 These neurological symptoms, due to CNS involvement, significantly impact patients’ quality of life.8

DM1, including CDM1, is caused by the abnormal expansion of CTG repeats in the 3′ untranslated region of the DMPK gene. In healthy individuals, the repeat number ranges from 5 to 37, but pathogenic expansions exceed 50 CTG and can surpass 1,000 in congenital cases, with larger repeats leading to more severe symptoms and earlier onset of clinical symptoms. CTG repeat lengths >500 may manifest as childhood DM1, which typically presents with cognitive and behavioral problems.7 The expanded CTG repeats result in the accumulation of toxic mRNA in nuclear foci, sequestering essential RNA-binding proteins MBNL1 and 2 and reducing the level of functional MBNL proteins, which disrupts various gene expression processes, including alternative splicing, miRNA biogenesis, mRNA localization, and mRNA stability.9,10 Interestingly, both MBNL1 and MBNL2 can partially compensate for each other’s functions. This functional redundancy highlights the regulatory complexity of the MBNL family.11

The DMSXL mouse model carries approximately 45 kb of the human DM1 locus with over 1,000 CTG repeats in the human DMPK gene, which is ubiquitously expressed. Homozygous DMSXL mice exhibit multisystemic phenotypes and a high rate of neonatal mortality, consistent with what is reported in congenital DM1.12 In homozygous DMSXL mice, these toxic transcripts interfere with brain function by forming RNA foci and causing mis-splicing in defined gene transcripts.13,14,15 The presence of mutant DMPK mRNAs in the CNS leads to reduced short-term synaptic potentiation and behavioral alterations such as impaired exploratory activity, deficits in spatial memory, increased anxiety-like behavior, and an anhedonia-like phenotype.15

Nucleic-acid-based therapeutics (NBTs) represent a promising class of drugs for treating CNS disorders. NBTs encompass a range of modalities, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), antimiRs, and advanced gene-editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas13.16,17 These therapies offer targeted disease-specific gene modulation with advantages over traditional small-molecule drugs, such as reduced off-target effects and potentially faster development timelines.18 Despite the clinical use of two NBTs (nusinersen and tofersen) for CNS indications,19,20 their clinical application is hindered by the low permeability of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), necessitating direct brain administration.16 Currently, most NBTs are delivered via intrathecal injection, which, while effective, is invasive and logistically complex. To address this, less invasive methods such as intranasal or intravenous (i.v.) administration of NBTs are under investigation.16,21,22

Multiple approaches are being tested to enhance NBT delivery and stability in the brain, and several promising improvements have already been achieved, which include divalent siRNAs (di-siRNAs), which show greater CNS efficacy23,24; C16 conjugation, which increases durability after intrathecal injection25; cholesterol DNA/RNA heteroduplexes and receptor-targeted ligands, such as transferrin or GLUT-1, which enhance brain uptake26,27,28; and nanoparticle systems, which improve NBT stability and brain distribution.22 Nevertheless, further optimization is still needed to achieve efficient, widespread, and long-lasting delivery to the CNS.

Currently, multiple therapeutic strategies for DM1 are in development, including ASOs aimed at releasing sequestered MBNL proteins by targeting DMPK transcripts for degradation or inhibiting the binding of DMPK mRNA and MBNL proteins.29,30 An alternative approach focuses on enhancing MBNL1 protein levels,31,32,33 which are essential for proper alternative splicing of muscle-related (and other) transcripts, and ultimately, improving muscle function. MBNL proteins are naturally repressed by miR-23b,34 so by utilizing specific antimiRs to block miR-23b by perfect sequence complementarity, MBNL1 levels can be increased, thereby aiding in the correction of the splicing abnormalities associated with DM1.35,36,37 Additionally, targeting miR-23b triggers a dual mechanism of action by increasing MBNL1 and downregulating DMPK transcripts in primary cell lines with different CTG repeat lengths.34 Furthermore, recent observations indicate that antimiRs targeting miR-23b and carrying an oleic acid moiety demonstrate greater efficacy and biodistribution compared to those that are non-conjugated or conjugated with palmitic acid or cholesterol, as evidenced in both cellular and HSALR mouse models of DM1.34,38

Considering that DM1 is a multisystemic disorder with considerable neurological deficits and that previous studies have demonstrated significant limitations in the ability of unconjugated ASOs to efficiently cross the BBB following systemic administration,39 this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the oleic-acid-conjugated antimiR X82108 in crossing the BBB in various animal models. The ability of X82108 to reach the CNS was evaluated in both HSALR and DMSXL mice, as well as in higher non-rodent species such as non-human primates (NHPs). Furthermore, the study focused on evaluating the efficacy of the antimiR in correcting DM1 phenotypes, including CNS impairments in DMSXL mice, while also assessing the potential toxicity of this therapeutic oligonucleotide.

Results

Oleic-acid-conjugated antimiR-23b reaches the brain and produces significant MBNL1 upregulation in HSALR mice and NHPs

Previous studies have demonstrated the ability of OA-conjugated antimiR-23b (X82108) to increase Mbnl1 levels in HSALR muscle, improving molecular and functional muscular DM1-related phenotypes.38 However, whether the same molecule injected intravenously could exert an effect on Mbnl1 expression in the brain was unknown. To address this, single (SAD) and multiple (MAD) i.v. administrations were performed in HSALR mice using a dose range of 6, 9, 12, and 24 mg/kg.

In the SAD study (Figure 1A), mice were sacrificed 2 weeks after the injection, and X82108 was detected by enzyme-linked oligosorbent assay (ELOSA) in brain extract samples in the cohort dosed at ≥12 mg/kg, while samples from lower doses remained below the quantification limit. In addition, X82108 was still detected in the brain 14 days post-injection at concentrations of approximately 300 ng/g, indicating sustained presence over time (Figure 1B). This was accompanied by a significant, dose-dependent increase in Mbnl1 protein levels at doses ≥12 mg/kg (Figures 1C and S1A), with a Spearman correlation of 0.72 between dose and protein upregulation in this organ (Figure S1B). At this same terminal time point, miR-23b levels in the brain were not significantly reduced compared to PBS-treated animals at the 12 and 24 mg/kg doses (Figure S1C), suggesting that although residual compound exposure was sufficient to induce a downstream increase in Mbnl1, repression of the target miRNA was no longer robustly detectable 14 days after a single administration, potentially reflecting steric blocking of the target without measurable changes in total miRNA levels.

In the MAD study, mice received a total of three doses, one every 2 weeks (Figure 1D), resulting in detectable brain levels of X82108 at all doses. Fourteen days after the last injection, the antimiR was detected by ELOSA within a concentration range of approximately 400–1,700 ng/g (Figure 1E). The antimiR was detected in a dose-dependent manner, leading to a significant Mbnl1 increase at all doses tested (Figures 1F and S1D), with a strong Pearson correlation of 0.80 (Figure S1E). Following repeated administration, miR-23b levels showed a modest, non-significant reduction of approximately 20% at 6, 9, and 12 mg/kg, whereas a marked and significant reduction of approximately 60% was observed at 24 mg/kg (Figure S1F), consistent with the highest compound exposure detected in the brain at this dose.

These findings confirm that repeated administration of X82108 increases brain exposure and elevates Mbnl1 levels in HSALR mice, demonstrating that the amount of OA-conjugated antimiR-23b reaching the brain after i.v. administration is sufficient to achieve target engagement in the brain, as reflected by increased Mbnl1 levels.

To confirm whether the biodistribution potential of the OA conjugation was conserved in a higher-order species, two i.v. administrations of X82108 at a dose of 20 mg/kg were given to NHPs (Figure 1G). In the brain cortex of treated animals, X82108 administration resulted in a marked reduction in miR-23b levels compared with PBS-treated controls (Figure 1H). All treated animals showed detectable levels of X82108 in the brain cortex (∼750 ng/g), whereas control animals had no quantifiable compound levels (Figure 1I). In addition, both MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein levels were increased in treated animals relative to controls (Figures 1J, 1K, S1G, and S1H). These findings confirm that systemic administration enables brain exposure of X82108 in NHPs and supports target engagement and molecular effects in a translationally relevant species.

To determine whether oleic acid conjugation contributes to the intrinsic activity of the antimiR or primarily facilitates its entry into the brain after systemic administration, we performed additional control experiments using the antimiR X82102, which is the unconjugated form of the antimiR X82108. Intrathecal administration (0.4 mg/animal) in three mice resulted in an approximately 1.5-fold increase in brain Mbnl1 protein levels 24 h after dosing, indicating that miR-23b targeting is sufficient to induce Mbnl1 upregulation when directly delivered to the CNS (Figures S1I and S1J). In contrast, following a single i.v. administration of unconjugated X82102 (24 mg/kg), no compound was detected in the brain, and no increase in Mbnl1 protein levels was observed 14 days after dosing (Figures S1I and S1K), whereas the oleic-acid-conjugated antimiR X82108 induced an approximately 2-fold increase in Mbnl1 under the same conditions (Figure 1C).

To assess sequence specificity, an oleic-acid-conjugated scrambled antimiR (SC-OA) was evaluated. This sequence, previously shown to be inactive in its cholesterol-conjugated form in HSALR mice,36 did not increase MBNL1 protein levels in DM1 transdifferentiated fibroblast-derived myotubes transfected with SC-OA (Figure S1L), in contrast to antimiRs targeting miR-23b, which consistently induced MBNL1 upregulation in vitro.38

Together, these results indicate that oleic acid conjugation enables systemic brain delivery, while MBNL1 upregulation is dependent on sequence-specific inhibition of miR-23b.

Systemic antimiR X82108 crosses the blood-brain barrier and spreads throughout the brain without compromising BBB integrity

HSALR mice are not a suitable model for evaluating whether the amounts of X82108 reaching the CNS after i.v. administration are sufficient to rescue DM1-related defects, because the long CTG repeat is not expressed in the brain of these animals.40,41 Therefore, we selected the DMSXL mouse model, which carries >1,000 CTG repeats with CNS expression,15 in order to assess X82108 effects and therapeutic potential in a pathological context in the brain.

The BBB is a dynamic and physical barrier that avoids the free diffusion of substances from blood to brain. Its integrity is key to maintaining brain homeostasis, but it is also the main obstacle for the development of antisense therapies for neurological diseases, given that the BBB is not permeable to oligonucleotides, except in situations in which it is damaged, such as some neurological disorders in which there is tight junction loss and disrupted transport of substances across the BBB (e.g., Alzheimer disease and multiple sclerosis).42 Assessing BBB integrity is essential in diseases with a significant neurological component, such as DM1, as is investigating the effect of any proposed therapeutic agent on BBB integrity.

We assessed BBB permeability by measuring the brain-to-blood ratio of 4 kDa FITC-dextran in wild-type (WT) and DMSXL mice, 24 h after i.v. administration of 25 mg/kg X82108. FITC-dextrans of variable sizes (4–70 kDa) are commonly used as BBB permeability markers due to their minimal plasma protein binding and inability to cross an intact BBB.43,44 As part of this BBB integrity assessment, we quantified FITC-dextran levels in serum, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and brain tissue from WT and DMSXL mice, with or without X82108 treatment.

Dextran-FITC was readily detectable in serum across all groups, with no significant differences between WT and DMSXL mice, indicating comparable systemic circulation of the tracer. In contrast, dextran-FITC levels in CSF and brain tissue were minimal. In brain samples, concentrations were below 0.05 mg/mL in some individual mice, further supporting the presence of an intact BBB in both genotypes (Figure 2A). Similarly, brain fluorescence scanning in mice treated for 24 h with X82108 and FITC-dextran revealed the widespread presence of the antimiR (red) in the brain detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), a highly sensitive technique for detecting single-stranded RNA or DNA sequences in tissue sections. Meanwhile, FITC-dextran (green) was restricted to small capillaries and absent in brain tissue (Figure 2B), both in wild-type (WT) and DMSXL mice. This confirms that the low FITC-dextran signal detected in the CSF or brain likely resulted from trace contamination or residual dextran within the cerebral vasculature. The data obtained suggest that the permeability of the BBB in the DMSXL model is not altered compared to WT and that X82108 treatment does not increase BBB permeability, either in WT or in DMSXL mice.

The antimiR (green signal) was detected by FISH across multiple brain regions and showed clear colocalization with both major brain cell types—neurons (NeuN, yellow) and astrocytes (GFAP, red) (Figures 3B–3D).

Specifically, in mice treated with antimiR, even if the highest intensity appears on the meninges and blood vessels, we observed the presence of X82108 across all brain regions. The highest accumulation on the brain appears in the cortex (CTX), especially on the outer layers of the neocortex; olfactory bulbs (OBs), especially in the glomerular and mitral cell layers; and cerebellum (CB), in the Purkinje and granular layers of the whole cerebellum, but with higher intensity on the flocculus and paraflocculus. (Figures 3B–3D). The compound was also detected in significant amounts in subcortical brain regions, particularly in the basal ganglia and limbic system, including the ventral striatum (STRv), caudoputamen (CP), pallidum (PAL), amygdala, and hypothalamus (HY) (Figure S2).

Notably, in saline-treated DMSXL mice, no fluorescent hybridization signal was detected in the CTX, CB, or OB, nor in any other brain region, indicating both the absence of antimiR and the specificity of the technique in detecting the antimiR (Figure S3).

These findings indicate that after i.v. injection, X82108 effectively penetrates the CNS without compromising BBB integrity, and it is widely distributed to different CNS areas and cell types.

Reduction of miR-23b expression raises Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 levels in DMSXL mouse brain after antimiR X82108 treatment

The brains of 2-month-old DMSXL mice that had received four i.v. injections of antimiR X82108 at 25 mg/kg over a period of 8 weeks (Figure 3A) were dissected into six regions (frontal and temporal cortex, cerebellum, hippocampus, brainstem, and rest of the brain) for molecular analysis (Figure 4A).

Mbnl protein levels, expressed as mean of Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 protein levels, were upregulated in all brain regions except cerebellum (C). The treatment led to a significant 70%–90% reduction in miR-23b levels across all brain regions compared to saline-injected mice. While a strong decrease was observed in all areas, the greatest reductions occurred in the frontal and temporal cortex and cerebellum, correlating with the highest accumulation of antimiR X82108 in these tissues (Figure 4B). Overall, these results confirm a robust reduction in the detected levels of the target miRNA across all brain regions.

Previous studies reported a pronounced reduction of Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 protein levels in astrocytes of DMSXL mice compared to WT controls.45 Since miR-23b represses Mbnl proteins,35 which are essential factors in producing the disease phenotype in the brain,46,47,48 we assessed their expression in different brain areas by western blot.

Treatment with antimiR X82108 resulted in a significant increase in Mbnl protein levels (referring to both Mbnl1 and Mbnl2) in most brain regions as compared to saline-treated animals (Figure 4C). Specifically, X82108 treatment significantly increased Mbnl1 protein levels in the frontal and temporal cortex, hippocampus, and brainstem, with no increase detected in the cerebellum or the rest of the brain at a dose of 25 mg/kg (Figures S4A and S4B). Similarly, X82108 treatment led to a significant increase in Mbnl2 protein levels in the frontal and temporal cortex, brainstem, and rest of the brain, but no increase was observed in the cerebellum and hippocampus at the same dose (Figures S4C and S4D).

Overall, these findings indicate that antimiR X82108 increased Mbnl1/2 protein expression in key brain regions, particularly in the prefrontal and temporal cortex, supporting its potential therapeutic effect by counteracting miR-23b-mediated repression.

DMPK mutant transcript levels are reduced in the DMSXL mouse brain upon antimiR X82108 treatment

The reduction of DMPK using anti-DMPK ASOs is a promising therapeutic approach for treating DM1.49,50,51 However, this effect is not exclusive to these ASOs, as the reduction of miR-23b, as well as the inhibition of GSK3β or sodium channels, also indirectly and significantly decreases DMPK levels in DM1 models.34,52,53

In order to characterize the expression pattern of mutant human DMPK in the group of DMSXL mice we used in our experimental set, we first evaluated whether all treated mice had the same number of repeats to confirm that the observed effects were due to the treatment itself rather than to an inherently milder phenotype in some animals. As shown in Figure 5A, there were no significant differences in the average number of repeats between saline-treated mice and those treated with antimiR X82108. We next examined whether transgene expression varied across the different brain regions analyzed in the untreated DMSXL animals. As shown in Figure S5B, DMPK expression was higher than the expression of the endogenous mouse Dmpk in all brain areas, with the highest DMPK expression relative to endogenous mouse Dmpk detected in the hippocampus, where DMPK levels were approximately 10-fold higher. In contrast, other brain regions showed lower DMPK/Dmpk ratios, ranging from ∼6-fold in cortex and brainstem to ∼4-fold in the cerebellum and remaining brain areas. Notably, this supports the DMSXL mouse as a particularly suitable model to study cognitive and brain-related phenotypes of DM1, due to the high expression of the transgene in the brain compared to the previously reported expression in skeletal muscle, where the transgene is expressed to a lesser extent than the endogenous Dmpk.41,45

Finally, to explore whether the reduction of DMPK through miR-23b inhibition also occurs in the CNS, we analyzed the expression of mutant DMPK in the brain regions of DMSXL mice treated with either saline or antimiR X82108.

The reduction of miR-23b by X82108 resulted in a significant decrease in DMPK transcripts in the frontal and temporal cortex, cerebellum, brainstem, and other brain areas—while a nonsignificant decrease was observed in the hippocampus. The reduction of DMPK seen in the CNS is consistent with the antimiR-23b dual mechanism of action previously described in patient myotubes34 (Figure 4D).

In summary, X82108 administration at 25 mg/kg significantly reduced mutant human DMPK expression, particularly in cortical regions (60%–70%), highlighting its therapeutic potential in mitigating DM1 molecular pathology in the CNS.

Aberrant splicing in the DMSXL mouse brain was rescued upon antimiR X82108 treatment

Up to 128 transcripts with splicing errors, including exon skipping and changes in splice site selection, were previously identified in astrocytes from DMSXL mice.45 To further investigate the impact of X82108 treatment on splicing regulation, we selected four MBNL-dependent splicing events—Itga6 Ex27, Numa1 Ex16, Fermt2 Ex4a, and Sorbs1 Ex2745—and analyzed their splicing patterns across six brain regions in DMSXL mice treated with X82108. Since antimiR-23b reduced DMPK and increased Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 expression (Figure 4), thereby enhancing the active pool of Mbnl, we assessed its ability to restore normal splicing of these target events. We observed a moderate rescue of Itga6 Ex27 inclusion across all brain regions studied (Figures 5A and 5B); a partial rescue of Numa1 Ex16 inclusion in the temporal cortex, brainstem, and cerebellum (Figures S6A–S6D); and a partial rescue of Fermt2 and Sorbs1 Ex4a and Ex27 inclusion, respectively, in the frontal and temporal cortex (Figures S6B–S6D). Overall, treatment with antimiR X82108 resulted in an improved percentage of splicing recovery (PSR) compared to saline-treated mice, with a 28% rescue in the frontal cortex, 30% in the temporal cortex, 10.5% in the cerebellum, 10% in the hippocampus, 12% in the brainstem, and 25% in the rest of the brain, when the drug was administered intravenously at a dose of 25 mg/kg (Figure 5C).

These findings support that antimiR X82108 effectively rescues splicing abnormalities of Itga6, Numa1, and Sorbs1 in the DMSXL mouse brain, particularly in cortical areas. This correction highlights the potential therapeutic benefit of antimiR X82108 in ameliorating brain missplicing previously described in DM1 patients.45,54

Systemic antimiR X82108 treatment corrects behavioral abnormalities in DMSXL mice

Previous studies had demonstrated that homozygous DMSXL mice exhibit altered exploratory behavior, novelty-induced inhibition, anxiety, spatial and working memory impairment, and anhedonia, which are characteristics reminiscent of the neurological manifestations of DM1.15 To investigate the potential therapeutic effects of antimiR X82108 on rescuing behavioral abnormalities in adult DMSXL mice (∼4 months old), we performed the open field test. This widely used behavioral assay assesses general locomotor activity, exploratory behavior, and anxiety-related responses in rodents exposed to a novel environment. In this preclinical assay, DMSXL mice displayed a significant reduction in exploratory activity, as indicated by a decreased distance traveled during both the first 5 min and the full 30-min session (Figures 6A and 6C) as compared to WT mice. In addition, these mice exhibited altered exploration patterns, spending a higher proportion of time and covering more distance in the center zone. However, this effect was not consistently sustained throughout the entire duration of the test (Figures 6B and 6D). These results suggest that, under the conditions of this test, DMSXL mice exhibit both reduced exploratory drive and altered anxiety-related behavior during the initial exposure to a novel environment, as indicated by increased time and distance spent in the center of the arena, which may reflect reduced thigmotaxis and abnormal risk assessment.

Treatment of these DMSXL mice with antimiR X82108 significantly normalized exploratory behavior by reducing the time spent in the center of the open field arena, thereby reversing the abnormal exploratory pattern observed in untreated mutant mice and making their behavior more similar to that of WT controls (Figure 6B). However, the treatment did not fully rescue the total distance traveled by the mice during the test (Figure 6A).

In conclusion, antimiR X82108 treatment rescued the behavioral abnormalities observed in DMSXL mice by restoring exploratory activity and normalizing risk assessment to levels comparable to WT controls. These findings confirm that sufficient amounts of the drug reach the relevant brain regions to achieve not only molecular but also functional phenotypic rescue.

Evaluation of potential toxicity of antimiR X82108

To assess the potential toxicity of systemic antimiR X82108 treatment in the adult DMSXL mice, we conducted a histopathological evaluation of four organs commonly assessed in toxicological studies. Examination of liver, kidney, heart, and brain sections by a histopathologist revealed no evidence of treatment-related adverse findings at doses of up to 25 mg/kg administered intravenously once every 2 weeks for 8 weeks (Figure 7A). More specifically, histopathological evaluation did not indicate significant toxicity associated with the test compound after 8 weeks of treatment. Mild and sporadic alterations were observed in liver and kidney of individual mice from both the saline- and antimiR-treated groups, but no consistent findings suggested toxicity related to antimiR X82108 (Figure 7B). However, renal histological analysis revealed potential treatment-related findings, specifically basophilic intracytoplasmic granules in the proximal tubules of a single animal. These granules, which are common in oligonucleotide treatments, were not associated with any signs of necrosis or cell degeneration and are likely due to intralysosomal accumulation of the test product or its metabolites (Figure S7).

Analysis of predicted off-target transcripts (miR-23a, Aopep, Shld2, Akap8l, Thbs4, and Snhg14) showed no significant changes in expression following X82108 treatment, with the exception of miR-23a (saline-treated DMSXL, n = 10; X82108-treated DMSXL, n = 9) (E). Relative expression levels represent the mean across all brain regions and were quantified by RT-qPCR. Unpaired t test or two-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s test ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data are represented as mean ± SEM. See also Figures S7 and S8.

To evaluate the potential neuroinflammatory effects of X82108 in adult DMSXL mice, we assessed whether its administration increased astroglial and/or microglial activation across multiple brain regions49 by quantifying the expression levels of Gfap and Aif1, respectively, by RT-qPCR in distinct brain regions. As shown in Figures 7C and 7D, no significant differences were observed in the expression of Aif1 or Gfap in most analyzed regions of the brain between mice treated with saline and those treated with X82108, except for a slight decrease in Aif1 expression in the temporal cortex (p < 0.05). This result suggests that X82108 does not induce a significant neuroinflammatory response in the brain, as evidenced by the absence of widespread activation of these markers across different brain regions.

Additionally, a chronic 26-week toxicology study was conducted in CD-1 WT mice to further characterize the toxicological profile of antimiR X82108. Mice received 25, 80, or 260 mg/kg of X82108 by i.v. administration every 2 weeks for 26 weeks. The Irwin and motor activity test performed at the end of the treatment period showed no treatment-related effects on general behavior or locomotion. Microscopic analysis revealed non-adverse X82108-related basophilic granules in all examined brains, predominantly at higher doses, resembling those previously observed in DMSXL mouse kidneys. The absence of histopathological alterations and normal behavioral outcomes suggests that chronic administration is well tolerated with no evidence of any toxic effect in the nervous system. In summary, antimiR X82108 was well tolerated in adult mice, with no observed toxic histopathological brain alterations, a key finding given the confirmed presence of the antimiR in this organ.

In addition to histopathological, behavioral, and neuroinflammatory assessments, we evaluated the molecular safety profile of X82108 by assessing sequence-dependent off-target effects. To this end, an in silico analysis of sequence complementarity was performed across the mouse transcriptome and genome following established guidelines for oligonucleotide safety evaluation.55

This analysis identified a limited number of transcripts with sequence complementarity to X82108, including perfect matches to members of the miR-23 family (miR-23a and miR-23b), as well as to Aopep, the host gene containing the miR-23b transcript. Beyond these expected matches, only a small number of additional candidates showed partial complementarity, resulting in a total of additional nine predicted transcripts (Figure 8A). Several of these corresponded to poorly characterized or predicted genes, including olfactory receptor family members (Or2y11, Or2y8, Or2y6, and Or2y10) and unannotated loci such as Gm51405, which lack functional annotation, clear human orthologues, and show minimal or no expression in the mouse brain, suggesting limited biological relevance. Among all predicted transcripts with sequence complementarity to X82108, four genes with partial complementarity (Shld2, Akap8l, Thbs4, and Snhg14) additionally to the perfect matches miR-23a and Aopep were found to be expressed in brain tissues and were therefore selected for further validation (Figure 8B). Notably, these genes are not classified as dosage-sensitive and have not been associated with Mendelian disease phenotypes based on available genetic databases.

To validate in vivo the potential off-target candidates expressed in the brain, RT-qPCR analysis was performed across multiple brain regions of X82108-treated DMSXL mice. As expected, a robust and consistent reduction of both members of the miR-23 family (miR-23a and miR-23b) was observed across all analyzed regions, with an approximate decrease of 70% for miR-23b and 55% for miR-23a. In contrast, no significant changes were detected in the expression of any of the other selected candidates, including Aopep, in treated animals compared to controls (Figure 7E). Notably, the reduction of miR-23a was not uniform across all brain regions, reaching statistical significance only in the frontal cortex, temporal cortex, and hippocampus (Figure 8C).

Together, these results indicate that X82108 selectively inhibits miR-23b and, to a lesser extent, its closely related family member miR-23a, which shares near-identical sequence homology, without inducing broader off-target effects across brain tissues.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of systemic administration of oleic-acid-conjugated antimiR-23b, X82108, in facilitating oligonucleotide distribution to the CNS and modulating key molecular targets associated with DM1 pathology.

The brain uptake observed in both HSALR mice and NHPs, along with the increase of MBNL1 and MBNL2, key hallmark proteins in the disease, and the reduction of DMPK transcript levels in DMSXL mice, support the therapeutic potential of this approach. This is further demonstrated by the ability of X82108 to correct splicing defects in different brain areas and the behavioral improvements seen in DMSXL mice when administered X82108.

Poor cellular uptake and suboptimal pharmacokinetics often limit the effectiveness of many therapeutic molecules, making chemical modifications necessary to optimize their properties and improve efficacy.56 Our findings suggest that systemic delivery of the oleic-acid-conjugated, chemically modified antimiR X8210838 enables its presence in the CNS, potentially by improving access across the BBB. This effect could be related to general properties of oleic acid itself in biological systems57,58,59 and its potential interactions with lipid transport pathways,60,61,62,63,64,65 in combination with the impact of other chemical modifications that are present in artificial oligonucleotides. However, it remains uncertain whether OA-conjugated oligonucleotides share mechanistic features with free oleic acid.

In this context, although alterations in lipid metabolism have been described in DM1, there is no clear evidence that oleic acid alone directly modulates MBNL expression. Consistent with this, our results using an oleic-acid-conjugated scrambled AntimiR indicate that the oleyl moiety alone is not sufficient to induce MBNL1 upregulation, even in cellular systems where alterations in oleic acid metabolism have been reported.66 To clarify this point, our comparative analyses indicate that oleic acid conjugation primarily facilitates brain entry following systemic administration rather than enhancing the intrinsic activity of the antimiR. Indeed, while direct CNS delivery of the unconjugated molecule was sufficient to induce Mbnl1 upregulation, no effect was observed after i.v. administration, highlighting the critical role of the conjugation in enabling CNS exposure.

Due to their inherent biophysical properties, naked oligonucleotides are rapidly cleared from circulation and cannot efficiently cross the BBB, often requiring repeated intrathecal or intra-cerebroventricular administration to reach the CNS. However, these delivery methods have limitations, including uneven biodistribution, reduced drug penetration into deeper brain regions,26,67 logistical difficulties, and procedural risks. Previous studies evaluating intracranial administration of DMPK ASOs demonstrated their efficacy in DM1 models,49 but systemic delivery of these oligonucleotides at muscle-effective doses, which can exert an effect on the CNS, have not been reported.68 In our study, a noteworthy increase in X82108 exposure in the brain was observed after triple administration, indicating a prolonged effect of the X82108 oligonucleotide in the brain (Figure 1E). These findings confirm that repeated administration of X82108 increases brain exposure and elevates Mbnl1 levels in HSALR mice. Importantly, while a significant reduction in miR-23b was only observed at the highest dose (Figure S1F), Mbnl1 upregulation was detected at all doses with measurable brain exposure (Figures 1C and 1F), suggesting that functional inhibition of miR-23b can occur without measurable changes in its total levels, consistent with the steric-blocking mechanism of action of the antimiR, whereas higher exposure may additionally promote detectable miRNA reduction.69,70 Importantly, this increased exposure is not associated with any observable toxicological effects, as demonstrated in Figure 7 and corroborated by the results of the Irwin test conducted in CD-1 mice after chronic administration of antimiR X82108 at doses of 25, 80, or 260 mg/kg for 26 weeks.

Brain fluorescence scanning confirmed the broad distribution of antimiR X82108 across different brain regions after i.v. administration, as detected by FISH, and was supported by molecular changes linked to DM1 pathology, including miR-23b and DMPK downregulation, Mbnl upregulation, and splicing correction (Figure S9). Notably, Mbnl upregulation was observed not only for Mbnl1 but also for Mbnl2 (Figures 1K and S4), a protein that plays a more significant role in the CNS than in other organs, particularly during neural development,11 and whose downregulation in the brain has previously been reported to lead to the cognitive impairment seen in DMSXL mice.45 The frontal and temporal cortices showed the strongest Mbnl upregulation, correlating with the highest and most uniform presence of antimiR X82108 (Figure 3C). In contrast, brainstem, cerebellum, hippocampus, and other brain regions exhibited more moderate effects, likely due to lower delivery and distribution of X82108 (Figures 3D and 3E).

Interestingly, although a general trend between Mbnl upregulation and DMPK downregulation can be observed, this relationship is not consistent across all brain regions, suggesting that these processes may be partially linked but also influenced by region-specific factors. Notably, the cerebellum and hippocampus exhibited complementary therapeutic responses: splicing correction in the cerebellum appeared to result primarily from downregulation of DMPK transcription, while in the hippocampus it was associated with increased Mbnl1 protein levels. This effect may reflect a mechanism previously observed in DM1 myotubes, whereby antimiR-23b treatment led to stronger DMPK reduction in cells with shorter CTG repeats and greater MBNL1 upregulation in those with longer expansions.34 A similar scenario could occur in the hippocampus, which shows higher transgene expression, and the cerebellum, where expression is lower (Figure S5). However, additional mechanisms may contribute to the regional differences in Mbnl and DMPK levels. For instance, astrocytes express higher levels of transgenic DMPK, Mbnl1, and Mbnl2 in DMSXL mice compared to neurons,45,54 which might mask or skew apparent changes in Mbnl levels depending on the cellular composition of each brain region. Moreover, if the antimiR distributes more efficiently into specific cell types, differential uptake could also shape the response. Taken together, these findings suggest that miR-23b inhibition by X82108 may activate region-specific mechanisms in the brain, consistent with prior observations in ASO-treated adult mouse models of Angelman syndrome, where hippocampus and cerebellum responded through distinct molecular pathways.71

ASOs do not efficiently cross an intact BBB; for this reason, they are typically administered directly into CSF or parenchyma for treating brain and spinal cord diseases.67 Evaluating BBB integrity in neurological disorders is crucial to ensuring effective drug delivery to the brain, as previous studies have reported BBB alterations in disease models and patients, such as those with Alzheimer disease.42,43 In contrast to findings from other models, DMSXL mice maintain an intact BBB, as demonstrated by FITC-dextran permeability assays. Using the same method, we have confirmed that X82108 does not alter the BBB permeability in treated DMSXL animals, indicating this is not the mechanism used by oleic-acid-conjugated oligonucleotides to cross this membrane.

X82108 treatment significantly corrected aberrant splicing in multiple MBNL1-dependent transcripts, achieving a 30% PSR across temporal and frontal cortices. Notably, the mis-splicing of Itga6 Ex27 was rescued in all brain areas. Aberrant splicing of Itga6 in DM1 mice disrupts integrin-mediated cell adhesion, potentially impairing astrocyte attachment and migration, which are crucial for neuronal-glial interactions and CNS homeostasis. Postmortem analyses of brains from patients with DM1 have revealed a high prevalence of RNA foci in astrocytes, correlating with the mis-splicing of adhesion-related transcripts, including ITGA6, SORBS1 and NUMA1, suggesting a potential role in the neuropathological manifestations of the disease.45,54,72 Moreover, FERMT2 is known to regulate region-specific astrocyte-neuron interactions, particularly in regulating synapse formation and function in the hippocampus. Downregulation of Fermt2 results in a significant reduction in hippocampal astrocyte territory size in C57BL/6NTac mice.73 Astrocytes emerge as a particularly vulnerable cell population in the DMSXL brain, suggesting that aberrant splicing events, in addition to higher levels of DMPK transgene and Mbnl proteins depletion, promote glial dysfunction, which contributes to DM1 neuropathology. Interestingly, we observed X82108 uptake in both astrocytes and neurons throughout the brain regions (Figure S2) suggesting that the observed splicing rescue may result from both cell types, restoring aspects of their physiological function. Compared to previously tested ASOs, oleic acid conjugation enhances CNS uptake and facilitates spliceopathy correction in the DM1 mouse model brain.

Behavioral testing confirmed that X82108 rescues exploratory deficits, consistent with molecular recovery. However, total distance traveled remained lower than in WT mice, indicating that some functional impairments persist despite these improvements. Similar findings have been reported with ASOs in mouse models of MECP2 duplication syndrome, A30P∗A53T∗α-Syn (Parkinson disease), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), where transcriptomic normalization and partial phenotype rescue did not fully restore all motor and behavioral functions.74,75,76 The persistent reduction in total distance traveled suggests that neural circuit alterations or brain regions less sensitive to X82108 may contribute to this deficit. Treatment timing also appears crucial, as a 70% reduction in DMPK rescues this phenotype when administered at postnatal day 19,49 whereas treatment in 2-month-old mice (50%–70% DMPK reduction) does not. This suggests that some functional impairments may only be reversible during early development due to the severity of the model, as observed in other congenital disorders such as spinal muscular atrophy.77

Histopathological evaluation revealed no significant treatment-related toxicity in major organs, including the brain, liver, kidney, and heart. The detection of basophilic granules in kidney tubules is a well-documented hybridization-independent effect of oligonucleotide treatments, associated with intra-lysosomal accumulation of oligonucleotide, and in absence of any other histopathology findings, is considered non-toxic.78,79 Moreover, a chronic toxicology study in CD-1 mice (26-week i.v. dosing) confirmed the long-term tolerability of X82108, with no evidence of neuroinflammation or adverse behavioral effects. Although these findings support the long-term safety of X82108, future studies will be needed to determine the durability of its therapeutic efficacy in disease-relevant CNS settings. Additionally, a comprehensive off-target assessment in the brains of DMSXL mice demonstrated a favorable specificity profile, as no relevant expression changes were detected for prioritized candidates, with the only consistent modulation observed for miR-23a. This finding was expected given that miR-23a and miR-23b belong to the same miRNA family, share an identical seed sequence, and differ by only a single nucleotide at the 3′ end,35 suggesting that antimiR action, including the effect on Mbnl1, is driven by targeting both miRNAs. This result is not an off-target effect but an additional potential mechanism of action of increasing of Mbnl transcripts, since miR-23a is also expected to regulate Mbnl.37 Collectively, these results support the high specificity of X82108 while highlighting miR-23a as a relevant molecular node that warrants further investigation to better define its contribution to the compound’s mechanism of action in DM1. Importantly, these findings establish a strong safety profile, supporting the further clinical development of X82108 as a viable DM1 CNS-targeting therapy.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that oleic-acid-conjugated antimiR X82108 efficiently crosses the BBB, enabling widespread CNS distribution and molecular correction of key DM1 hallmarks. By facilitating brain delivery through systemic administration, this approach offers a clinically relevant alternative that is less invasive than intrathecal ASO administration and highlights the potential of lipid-conjugated ASOs as a promising strategy for CNS-targeted therapies.

Limitations of the study

While the present study supports the therapeutic potential of X82108 for CNS manifestations of DM1, some questions remain to be addressed. Although the DMSXL model reproduces key molecular and behavioral features of the disease and is widely used to investigate DM1-associated CNS dysfunction, it may not capture the full complexity of human disease, and additional studies will be valuable to further define how these findings translate across the broader spectrum of DM1 pathology. Furthermore, while brain delivery of X82108 was demonstrated in both mice and non-human primates, efficacy studies were performed only in DMSXL mice. Future studies will therefore help further establish the translational potential of this approach and inform clinical development strategies.

The precise mechanisms underlying the enhanced CNS delivery mediated by oleic acid conjugation also remain incompletely understood. Although our data indicate that oleic acid primarily facilitates brain exposure rather than directly contributing to target engagement, the molecular pathways involved in BBB transport require further investigation. Similarly, regional differences in molecular responses across brain areas suggest that additional factors, including cell-type composition, differential transgene expression, and tissue-specific oligonucleotide distribution, may influence therapeutic outcomes.

Although X82108 produced robust molecular correction and improved several behavioral abnormalities, some functional deficits persisted after treatment. These findings suggest that certain disease manifestations may require earlier intervention, higher levels of target correction, or may reflect irreversible developmental alterations that cannot be fully reversed in adult animals. Finally, while the favorable safety profile and specificity observed in the present studies support continued development of X82108, longer-term studies will be necessary to define the durability of therapeutic benefit and further characterize its mechanism of action, including the relative contribution of miR-23a and miR-23b.

Experimental model and study participant details

HSALR mice

Mouse handling and experimental procedures for HSALR mice followed European regulations on laboratory animal care and experimentation (2003/65/CE) and were approved by the Conselleria de Agricultura, Generalitat Valenciana (A20220322163151). Homozygous transgenic HSALR (line 20b) mice80 were provided by Prof. C. Thornton (University of Rochester Medical Center, Rochester, NY, USA).

A total of 13 male and 12 female HSALR mice were used for single ascending dose (SAD) and 13 male and 12 female HSALR were used for multiple ascending dose (MAD). The mice used in this study were approximately 3.5–4 months old at injection. A sample size of five mice per experimental group was used for both SAD and MAD studies. HSALR mice were treated with PBS as a vehicle or with X82108 at doses of 6, 9, 12, or 24 mg/kg. All groups received a single intravenous (tail vein) injection of X82108 in the SAD study, whereas in the MAD study, mice received three injections every two weeks. Fourteen days after the last injection, mice were sacrificed, and tissues of interest were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular assays.

In addition, to evaluate the contribution of oleic acid conjugation to brain delivery and pharmacological activity, separate cohorts of a total of 9 male mice of approximately 3 months old (n = 3 per group) were treated with the unconjugated version of the compound (antimiR X82102). For intrathecal administration, mice received a single dose of 0.4 mg per animal and were sacrificed 24 h post-injection to assess acute target engagement in the brain. In a parallel experiment, mice received a single intravenous (tail vein) injection of X82102 at 24 mg/kg and were sacrificed 14 days post-administration to evaluate systemic delivery and central nervous system exposure. Age-matched saline-treated male mice served as control subjects. Brains were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular analyses.

Non-human primates (NHPs)

The in vivo assessment in cynomolgus monkeys was conducted at Labcorp Early Development Services GmbH (Germany) in compliance with the German Animal Welfare Act, Directive 2010/63/EU, and Commission Recommendation 2007/526/EC, with approval and oversight by the attending laboratory animal veterinarian.

A total of three male and three female cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) with approximately 24–50 months old were acclimated to handling procedures to facilitate intravenous administration. Throughout the study, food was provided daily in amounts appropriate for body weight, and water was available ad libitum. Animals were assigned to one of two treatment groups and received either vehicle (PBS) (n = 2) or X82108 at a fixed dose of 20 mg/kg (n = 4), administered intravenously via the saphenous vein at a dose volume of 5 mL/kg on days 1 and 22. Treatments were well tolerated, with no adverse clinical signs observed. Necropsies were performed on day 43, corresponding to 21 days after the last administration. Monkeys were euthanized following institutional guidelines, and brain cortex tissue was collected for ELOSA and western blot analysis.

Evaluation of oleic acid-conjugated scrambled AntimiR in DM1 transdifferentiated fibroblast-derived myotubes

To assess the sequence specificity of X82108, an oleic acid–conjugated scrambled antimiR (SC-OA) was evaluated in vitro using DM1 fibroblasts transdifferentiated into myotubes. Immortalized MyoD-inducible fibroblasts derived from individuals with DM1 and unaffected controls were kindly provided by Dr. Denis Furling (Institute of Myology, Paris, France). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 4.5 g/L glucose; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.38 Cells used in this study were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

DM1 cells were treated with SC-OA across a range of concentrations (10 nM, 50 nM, 200 nM, 1 μM and 5 μM) to match the conditions used previously for active compounds.38 Compounds were delivered by lipofection using X-tremeGENE HP Transfection Reagent (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 4 h of incubation, transfection medium was replaced with fresh muscle differentiation medium (MDM) in order to promote fibroblast transdifferentiation. MDM was prepared with DMEM 4.5 g/L glucose (Gibco) supplemented containing 1% P/S (Gibco), 2% horse serum (HS; Gibco), 1% apo-transferrin (10 mg/mL; Sigma), 0.1% insulin (10 mg/mL; Sigma), and 0.02% doxycycline (10 mg/mL; Sigma). Cells were maintained under differentiation conditions and harvested on day 4 for downstream analyses.38

DMSXL transgenic mice

All experimental procedures involving mice were performed in strict accordance with the European Union Directive 86/609/EEC concerning the protection of animals, and followed the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used. The experimental protocols received approval from the Charles Darwin Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation (CEEA 5), under the authorization numbers APAFIS #30275–2021030414389550, granted by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research. Animals were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. Temperature and humidity were kept within the recommended range (20°C–24°C and 40–60%, respectively).

DMSXL transgenic mice, on a >99% C57BL/6 background, possess a 45 kb fragment of human genomic DNA from a DM1 patient. The DMSXL mice used in this study were 2 months old male mice at injection and carried over 1000 CTG repeats within the DMPK transgene. Age-matched C57BL/6 male mice served as control subjects. The DMSXL status was confirmed by multiplex PCR using specific oligonucleotide primers designed to target the DMPK transgene and the integration site within the mouse Fbxl7 gene: FBF (forward), FBFBR (reverse), and FBWDR (reverse) (Table S1). The FBF and FBFBR primers anneal to the mouse Fbxl7 gene, producing a 167 bp sequence in wild-type mice. The FBWDR primer specifically targets the human transgene, resulting in a 236 bp PCR product. On the other hand, the size of the CTG repeat was determined by PCR of tail DNA with oligonucleotide primers ST-300 (Table S1), followed by electrophoresis of the amplification products through a 0.8% (m/v) agarose denaturing gel.

Intravenous bolus injections were administered via the retroorbital vein at a volume of 5 μL/g of either saline solution (PBS) or antimiR X82108. The treatment regimen comprised a total of four injections, given biweekly over an 8-week period. For the antimiR X82108 group, each injection was delivered at a dose of 25 mg/kg. The experimental groups included: DMSXL saline (n = 10), DMSXL antimiR-X82108 (n = 9), and wild-type saline controls (n = 8). Brain tissues were collected in RNase-free tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at (−80 ± 10°C). Liver, kidney, heart, and brain tissues from 4 mice treated with saline and 4 mice treated with X82108 were collected in formalin for histological analysis at the Diagnostic Anatomopathological and Toxicological Laboratory at Micros Veterinaria (MicrosVet) in León, Spain.

Intravenous administration was performed via the tail vein in albino strains or via the retro-orbital sinus in C57BL/6-background mice to ensure reliable venous access, as both routes provide comparable systemic exposure.

26-Week chronic toxicology study of X82108 in CD-1 mice

The study was conducted at the European Research Biology Center (ERBC) facilities in Italy in accordance with the Rules Governing Medicinal Products in the European Community and the Guideline on Repeated Dose Toxicity (CPMP/SWP/1042/99). All procedures and facilities complied with Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

The chronic administration study in mice was conducted on strain CD1 due to its widespread acceptance by regulatory authorities and the extensive background data available for this species. The study was designed to assess the potential toxic effects of X82108 on the brain (histopathological evaluation) and general behavior and locomotor activity (Irwin test) after intravenous administration every two weeks for 26 weeks (14 total administrations). A total of 52 CD-1 mice (26 males and 26 nulliparous, non-pregnant females), aged approximately 6–8 weeks, were used in the study. Animals were distributed into four experimental groups: one control group and three treatment groups receiving 25, 80, and 260 mg/kg of X82108. The test item was administered by intravenous bolus injection into the tail vein at a dose volume of 5 mL/kg.

Method details

Enzyme-Linked OligoSorbent Assay (ELOSA)

Custom hybridization-based ELISA were performed in order to determine X82108 and X82102 concentrations in the brain of treated and untreated HSALR mice and NHPs.81 The following phosphorothioate probe was used: miR-23b [BIO] TCACATTGCCAGGGAT [DIG], double-labeled with digoxigenin (DIG) and biotin (BIO). A control sample (HSALR mice or NHPs treated with saline) was used to subtract the background.

The samples were previously homogenized and denatured at 55°C overnight. The prepared samples were incubated in a 96-well transparent plate during 30 min at 37°C with the hybridization probe, previously denaturalized at 65°C for 15 min, at a dilution of 1:1. Afterward, the mix samples/probe was transferred to a NeutrAvidin plate for 30 min at 37°C, where occurs the hybridization of the biotin from the probe with the NeutrAvidin binded of the plate, followed by a wash step. In order to discard those probes that were not bound to the compound, an incubation was performed with the Micrococcal Nuclease enzyme (1:10000) for 1 h at 37°C, a specifically ssDNase, followed by a wash step. The remaining hybrids were detected with an anti-digoxigenin antibody (1:5000) conjugated with an alkaline phosphatase enzyme during 30 min at 37°C, followed by a wash step. In the end, it was incubated with Attophos substrate (1:1). After an hour of incubation at room temperature, fluorescence was measured in a microplate reader at 444 nm of excitation and 555 nm of emission at multiple time points (60′, 70′, 80′ and 90′). X82108 concentration was determined by fluorescence interpolation in a standard curve.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (1 tablet to each 10 mL, Roche), and determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific) using bovine serum albumin as standard.

For immunodetection of MBNL1, MBNL2, or GAPDH (internal control), 15 μg of total protein from mice and NHPs brains was denatured at 100°C for 5 min, separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS-T for 1 h, then incubated with primary anti-MBNL1 (1:200; MB1a(4A8), DSHB) or anti-MBNL2 (1:750; MB2a(3B), DSHB) antibodies for 24 h at 4°C. After washing, membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; ABIN1589977, AntibodiesOnline) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by visualization using ECL substrate and imaging with ImageQuant 800. After detecting MBNL1/MBNL2, membranes were stripped and reprobed for GAPDH using HRP-conjugated anti-GAPDH antibody (1:3500; sc-365062, Santa Cruz). All images were quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California), normalizing MBNL1/MBNL2 levels to GAPDH, and then to the control group (Saline).

Jess Simple Western

MBNL1 of mice administered with X82102 intrathecally and intravenously and MBNL2 of NHPs protein levels were quantified using the Jess Simple Western system (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne). One microgram for detecting MBNL1 and 2 mg for detecting MBNL2 of total protein was loaded into the 12–230 kDa Separation Module (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne). MBNL1 and MBNL2 were detected using mouse anti-MBNL1 monoclonal antibody (1:10 dilution, MB1a(4A8), DSHB) and mouse anti-MBNL2 monoclonal antibody (1:10 dilution, MB2a(3B), DSHB) respectively, and goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase using the Anti-Mouse Detection Module (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne). Chemiluminescent detection was performed using the RePlex Module (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Normalization was done through total protein chemiluminescence analysis using the Total Protein Detection Module (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne). Data were analyzed using Compass software for Simple Western (version 6.2.0). MBNL1 protein levels were normalized to total protein for each sample, and this ratio was expressed relative to the average of untreated animals.

MBNL1 quantification using quantitative dot blot (QDB) assay

MBNL1 protein levels of trans-differentiated myoblast were quantified using quantitative dot-blot (QDB) assay. Protein extracts were diluted to load 1 μg of total protein per well, mixed with 4× loading buffer (240 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 40% glycerol, 8% SDS, 0.4% bromophenol blue, and 40% β-mercaptoethanol), heated at 95°C for 5 min, and spotted onto QDB plates (Quanticision Diagnostics) in quadruplicate. After air-drying, membranes were washed with TBS-T (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 137 mM NaCl, and 0.5% Tween 20), blocked for 1 h in 5% skim milk in TBS-T, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-MBNL1 (1:1000, ab77017, Abcam). Following washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:2000, ABIN1589977, AntibodiesOnline) for 2 h at room temperature. Signal was developed using ECL substrate and quantified using an Infinite M200 Pro plate reader (Tecan) in luminescence mode. MBNL1 values were normalized to GAPDH using HRP-conjugated anti-GAPDH antibody (1:500; sc-365062, Santa Cruz) and subsequently to mock-transfected DM1 cells to obtain fold-change values.82

DMSXL blood-brain barrier integrity evaluation

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability was investigated using X82108 in combination with 4 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (TdB Labs) in order to assess BBB integrity43 in DMSXL mice compared to WT mice and the effects of X82108 treatment on membrane permeability. Intravenous bolus injections were administered to WT and DMSXL mice via the retroorbital vein at a volume of 5 μL/g of either PBS or X82108 at a dose of 25 mg/kg. After 24 h, 4 kDa FITC-dextran was injected intravenously via the same route at a dose of 600 mg/kg. 10 min after the FITC-dextran administration the animals were sacrificed. The serum, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain were collected and homogenized in PBS containing protease inhibitors and directly load into a black low-binding 96-well plate to measure FITC fluorescence using Infinite 200 pro (Tecan) plate reader. A standard curve was used to perform the analysis and quantification.

Moreover, imaging methods were performed to confirm the BBB permeability. Brain sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and permeabilized prior to FITC fluorescence amplification with anti-FITC Alexa Fluor 488 (200-542-037, Jackson ImmunoResearch) at 1/100. 0.3% Sudan Black B (Sigma) was used for autofluorescence quenching. Subsequently, for FISH, the sections were pre-hybridized in hybridization buffer (prepared according83) and incubated with Cy3 labeled X82108-probe ([5 Cy3] TCACATTGCCAGGGAT) diluted 1/50 in hybridization buffer for 2 h at 37°C. Finally, it was incubated with NuclBlue (R3705, Invitrogen) for 10 min at 37°C for nucleus staining. Brain imaging was performed with TCS-SP2-AOBS confocal microscope (Leica Biosystems).

AntimiR detection by fluorescence brain scanning

DMSXL brain, cerebellum and spinal cohort sections were stained by fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for X82108 detection. NeuN and Gfap were used as markers to identify neuron and astrocyte cell populations,84,85 respectively, by IHC. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and permeabilized prior to antigen retrieval with 10 mM citrate. For the IHC, sections were blocked and incubated with a cocktail of anti-NeuN (MAB377, Sigma) and anti-Gfap (AB45541, Sigma) at 1/50 and 1/500 overnight. After washing, tissues were incubated with specific labeled secondary antibodies anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (A32728, Invitrogen) and anti-chicken IgG Cy3 (703-165-155, Jackson ImmunoResearch) at 1/200 and 1/500 for 2 h 0.3% Sudan Black B (Sigma) was used to quenching of autofluorescence. Subsequently, for FISH, the sections were pre-hybridized in hybridization buffer (prepared according83) and incubated with FAM labeled X82108-probe ([56 FAM] TCACATTGCCAGGGAT) diluted 1/50 in hybridization buffer for 2 h at 37°C. Finally, it was incubated with NuclBlue (R3705, Invitrogen) for 10 min at 37°C for nucleus staining. Brain, cerebellum and spinal cohort scanners were performed with Apeiro VERSA Scanner Microscope (Leica Biosystems).

RNA extraction, semiquantitative RT-PCR and RT-qPCR

Total RNA from HSALR mice brain, NHPs brain cortex and the frontal cortex, temporal cortex, cerebellum, hippocampus, brain stem, and other brain regions of DMSXL mice was isolated using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of RNA was digested with DNase I (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California) and reverse-transcribed with SuperScript IV (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California) using random hexanucleotides.

For subsequent PCR reactions, 20 ng of cDNA was used with GoTaq polymerase (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin). Specific primers were used to analyze the alternative splicing of Itga6 Ex27, Numa1 Ex16, Sorbs1 Ex27 and Fermt2 Ex4a in mouse samples (Table S1). Gapdh levels established the endogenous reference levels using 0.2 ng of cDNA. PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel and quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California). The percent spliced index (Ѱ) was calculated to determine the proportion of inclusion of a specific exon in mature mRNA compared to the total splicing events for that region. The Percentage splice recovery (PSR) index was defined as the value: %EI minus X¯ %DEI, divided by X¯%HEI minus X¯%DEI (EI: exon inclusion of each sample; DEI: disease exon inclusion; HEI: healthy exon inclusion). The overall PSR indicates the average rescue for the set of four splicing events studied in each brain region.

DMPK, Dmpk, Aif1, Gfap, Aopep, Shld2, Akap8l, Thbs4, and Snhg14 and Gapdh internal control transcripts were quantified using a LightCycler 480 Instrument II and software (Roche Diagnostics) with a 5× HOT FIREPol EvaGreen qPCR mix plus (ROX) (Solis BioDyne, #08-24-00001) using specific oligonucleotide primers (Table S1). Samples were quantified in triplicate and experiments were repeated at least twice. Relative expression with respect to the endogenous transcript and the control group was obtained by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

microRNAs were retro-transcribed using miRCURY LNA RT Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. miR-23b and miR-23a relative expression in brain tissues was quantified and normalized to RNU1A and U6 snRNA as internal controls using a LightCycler 480 Instrument II and software (Roche Diagnostics) and miRCURY LNA SYBR Green PCR Kit with specific miRCURY-locked nucleic acid microRNA PCR primers (Qiagen).37

Open field test

Mouse activity was assessed on days 56 and 57 post-treatment in open boxes (open field: 45 cm wide, 45cm long, 40 cm high) made of white, matte-finish plastic. An infrared detection system (SMART v3.0 software, Panlab) recorded the animals’ activity. Mice were transferred to the testing room at least 30 min prior to the experiment to allow habituation to the environment. During this period, animals remained in their home cages. The floor of the arena was virtually divided into peripheral and central zones. At the start of the test, each mouse was gently placed in the peripheral zone of the arena, facing the wall, and allowed to explore freely for 30 min. Behavior was recorded using a top-mounted video camera and analyzed with the automated tracking software. Between each trial, the arena was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues. The following parameters were collected to assess activity and spatial exploration: total distance traveled, percentage of time spent in each zone, percentage of resting time, and number of entries into the different areas of the open field (center and periphery). Inhibition of exploration due to novelty and anxiety-related behavior was assessed by measuring the time spent in the center versus the periphery of the arena, the number of entries into the center zone, and the proportion of time spent immobile (resting time) during the initial phase of the test.

Histopathological examination of DMSXL mice

The DMSXL organs were sent to Micros Veterinaria S.L. (Spain) and processed using standard histological procedures, including trimming, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin-eosin staining. The stained sections were examined by a pathologist following standardized histopathology procedures established by the Society of Toxicologic Pathology (STP) and the International Harmonization of Nomenclature and Diagnostic (INHAND) guidelines.86,87,88,89

Microscopic findings were recorded by the pathologist during the histopathological examination. For each tissue type, a comprehensive evaluation was conducted to assess both non-proliferative and proliferative lesions. Histological changes were described in terms of distribution, severity, and morphological characteristics. Severity scores were assigned on a scale from 1 to 5 as follows:

Grade 1: Minimal. Tissue changes are barely perceptible, small, or infrequent. Includes multifocal or extensive lesions affecting less than 10% of the tissue in an average high-power field (hpf).

Grade 2: Slight. Histopathological changes are noticeable but not prominent. Multifocal or extensive lesions affecting 10–25% of the tissue in an average hpf.

Grade 3: Moderate. Prominent tissue changes that are not the dominant feature of the tissue. Multifocal or extensive lesions affecting 25–50% of the tissue in an average hpf.

Grade 4: Marked. Dominant histopathological change that is not overwhelming. Multifocal or extensive lesions affecting 50–95% of the tissue in an average hpf.

Grade 5: Severe. Overwhelming tissue change. Multifocal or diffuse lesions affecting more than 95% of the tissue.

Histopathological examination of brain tissue from CD-1 mice was performed at Novaxia Pathology Laboratory (France). Brains were processed into paraffin blocks, sectioned (4–5 μm), and stained with H&E at ERBC. Microscopic evaluation was conducted by the study pathologist using a light microscope, and findings were graded on a 1 to 5 severity scale (minimal to severe).

In silico evaluation of hybridization-dependent off-targets in mice brain

Potential hybridization-dependent off-targets of X82108 were systematically assessed through sequence complementarity searches against the mouse genome and transcriptome according established guidelines for oligonucleotide safety assessment.55 Analyses were performed using reference datasets from the NCBI RefSeq database, which provides a comprehensive and curated collection of genomic DNA and RNA sequences, including annotated splice variants. Similarity searches were conducted using the fuzznuc tool from the EMBOSS package (v6.6.0), allowing up to five mismatches and considering both sense and antisense complementarity. Independent searches were carried out against the mature mouse transcriptome and the mouse genome assembly to capture potential interactions with both processed transcripts and primary RNA species.

For genomic analyses, sequence matches identified in the mouse genome were further annotated to determine their localization within transcribed regions. Genomic coordinates of identified hits were converted to BED format and intersected with annotated gene features using BEDTools (v2.28.0). This approach enabled the identification of potential off-target sites within gene bodies, including intronic and exonic regions, while excluding pseudogenes and non-relevant genomic annotations. Only complementary strand matches were prioritized for downstream evaluation, as these are more likely to mediate hybridization-dependent effects.

To contextualize the biological relevance of identified candidates, gene expression patterns across mouse tissues were assessed using the Bgee database (v14.1), which integrates curated transcriptomic datasets from healthy samples. Expression scores were used to prioritize genes with detectable or enriched expression in central nervous system tissues.

In addition, potential functional and disease relevance of candidate genes was explored through cross-species annotation, including homology mapping to human orthologs and interrogation of OMIA, OMIM, and ClinGen databases to assess associations with genetic disorders and dosage sensitivity. This integrative strategy enabled the prioritization of biologically relevant off-target candidates for experimental validation.

Irwin test in CD1 mice and motor activity assessment

The Irwin test is a systematic observational method used to assess and quantify the behavioral and physiological state of mice in response to drug administration.90 This test allows for the identification of effects of the test substance on the central nervous system should be assessed appropriately. The following parameters were evaluated: locomotor activity, passivity, curiosity, vocalization, irritability, pain response (tail), straub tail, tremor, twitches, convulsions, startle reaction, ataxic gait, limb position, bizarre behavior, righting reflex, body tone, abdominal tone, grip strength, pinna reflex, corneal reflex, pain response (toe), piloerection, heart rate, respiration, cyanosis, redness, pallor, pupil size, palpebral ptosis, salivation, lachrymation, hypothermia, immediate death, and delayed death. Each parameter was scored from 0 to 8 based on intensity.

The Irwin test and the motor activity assessment was conducted on Days 85 (week 13) and 169 (week 25) of treatment. Each animal was observed at pre-dose, and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-dose.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with Prism 10 (GraphPad). Results are presented as means ± S.E.M. An unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups. A one-way ANOVA test was performed to compare various groups and conditions, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. Where data did not follow normality, Kruskal-Wallis’s test was applied followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. For off-target evaluation in grouped assays, statistical significance was assessed using two-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s post hoc test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Details about the statistical analysis used for each quantification are described in the corresponding figure legend. Sample sizes (n) are included in each figure, where each data point represents an individual animal.
