Learning to stand with delays alters sensorimotor control but does not cause instability when returning to natural balance
Authors: Liam H. Foulger, Xiyao Liu, Amin M. Nasrabadi, Calvin Z. Qiao, Mark G. Carpenter, Lyndia C. Wu, Jean‐Sébastien Blouin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo maintain a bipedal posture, humans must compensate for inherent sensorimotor delays from neural conduction times and electromechanical delays. Ageing and certain neurological disorders increase these delays, so it is crucial that we adapt our control of balance to compensate for the uncertainty associated with acting on sensory information from the past. Although humans can adapt to imposed delays of 400 ms, the mechanisms underlying the adaptation process remain unknown because gross balance instability or errors are absent when returning to balancing without delays. To investigate this, we used a robotic balance simulator to impose delays of 250 ms while participants balanced upright. We characterized and modelled the adjustments in motor commands required to adapt to the addition and removal of delays. Following 20 min of adaptation, participants successfully maintained their balance with the imposed delay. When the delay was abruptly removed, participants remained upright with minimal changes in their whole‐body oscillations, but we observed transient (5–20 s) spectral power increases between 1 and 2 Hz in the net ankle torque and lower limb muscle activity. Our computational model revealed that increased sensorimotor gains led to spectral changes in the balance motor commands. Our results indicate that increased sensorimotor gains are necessary to adapt balance control to longer delays and that these gains remained transiently elevated after the removal of the delays without resulting in postural instability. This highlights the remarkable adaptability of human balance control, revealing that the nervous system can flexibly adjust sensorimotor strategies to maintain balance under changing conditions. Key points The human nervous system can adapt to sensorimotor delays, allowing us to maintain balance even though there are delays between sensed stimuli and our corrective motor actions.While balancing on a robotic simulator, participants exposed to a 250 ms delay between their self‐generated motor commands and resulting whole‐body motion exhibited initial difficulty maintaining balance and increased muscle (co)activation but adapted within minutes of exposure.Despite no postural instability following the abrupt removal of the 250 ms delay, participants exhibited transient (5–20 s) increases in leg muscle activation and ankle torque power (1–2 Hz).These changes in the neuromuscular control of balance after delay removal suggest increased sensitivity to sensory feedback, as supported by a computational model representing key physiological features of balance control.By revealing how the brain adapts when facing rapidly changing environments, our results highlight the flexibility of the neural control of balance to ensure robust bipedal stability.

Introduction

The ability to adapt our balance to maintain a bipedal posture is crucial for achieving many movement goals related to daily living. As sensed stimuli arise from past movements and current motor commands lead to actions in the future due to neural conduction times and electromechanical delays in force generation (Forbes et al., 2018; Milton, 2011; Milton et al., 2009; More & Donelan, 2018; van der Kooij et al., 1999), the control of balance experiences delays estimated between 100 and 160 ms. These delays can change across our lifespan or following neurological disorders (Cameron & Nilsagard, 2018; Dorfman & Bosley, 1979; Eyre et al., 1991; Lin & Woollacott, 2002; Ramdharry, 2018; Woollacott et al., 1986), so it is imperative that we adapt our motor control to evolving standing balance conditions. Recent work has shown that humans can learn to balance with imposed delays of up to 400 ms, learning persists for at least 3 months, and generalizes across directions and limbs (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024). Despite evidence of our ability to update how we control standing balance with delays, removing these delays induces very little disturbance to the control of balance. Consequently, the mechanisms underlying how our motor commands facilitate this adaptation and how they change following the removal of imposed delays (i.e. after‐effects) while enabling a stable upright posture remains unclear.

Muscle activation patterns represent a critical tool to reveal the neural mechanisms underlying sensorimotor adaptation. For example, antagonistic muscles typically exhibit increased co‐contraction at the initiation of a novel and challenging task, which gradually decreases as adaptation progresses (Huang & Ahmed, 2014; Huang et al., 2012). Increased muscle co‐contraction also occurs during challenging balance tasks (Alizadehsaravi et al., 2020; Allum et al., 2002; Iwamoto et al., 2017; Nelson‐Wong et al., 2012; Oomen et al., 2015; Reynolds, 2010), which may be beneficial to increase ankle stiffness and reduce electromechanical delays (Le Mouel & Brette, 2019). Although changes in muscle activation and co‐contraction patterns during adaptation to standing with imposed delays have not been quantified, spectral changes in ankle torques after the removal of a delay provide indirect evidence that balance adaptation alters muscle activation patterns (Rasman et al., 2024). Motor errors (i.e. after‐effects) that occur immediately following the removal of a perturbation are considered a hallmark of sensorimotor adaptation (Kim et al., 2021; Krakauer et al., 2019). However, the link between a neural adaptation process and alterations in muscle activation is not clear given that balance adaptation to delays induces minimal postural instability after the delay is removed (i.e. minimal behavioural after‐effects) (Rasman et al., 2021). Computational modelling may help reconcile these observations: large overlaps exist in sensorimotor gain combinations enabling baseline (non‐delayed) and delayed standing balance, indicating that sensorimotor control changes induced by delays could still result in successful balance under baseline conditions (van der Kooij & Peterka, 2011). Thus, experimental data from ankle motor commands (including co‐contraction) are crucial to identify the active mechanisms responsible for previously reported spectral torque changes while computational approaches will reveal the sensorimotor gain adaptions required to balance with imposed delays and potential after‐effects resulting from delay removal.

Here, we investigated the balance behaviour and muscle activation patterns during and following adaptation to a delay imposed during standing balance to reveal the physiological mechanisms of balance control adaptation. Participants stood on a robotic balance simulator with 250 ms imposed delays for a 20‐min adaptation period. Following this adaptation period, the delay was abruptly removed. We quantified changes to whole‐body angular velocity, net ankle torque, and muscle activity before, during, and after the adaptation. Based on our prior work showing increases in the frequency content of the net ankle torque between 1 and 2 Hz (Rasman et al., 2024), we predicted that muscle activity would also present similar oscillations (i.e. after‐effects) following the removal of the imposed delay. To reveal how sensorimotor gain changes were responsible for the adaptation and related after‐effects, we modelled the control of standing balance with and without the 250 ms imposed delay. As a secondary aim, we also quantified how muscle activation and co‐contraction patterns changed across and after the adaptation period. We predicted that muscle activity and muscle co‐contraction would initially increase when exposed to imposed delays and then gradually decrease throughout the adaptation period, mirroring changes to the variance in whole‐body angular velocity and net ankle torque.

Methods

Ethical approval

This experiment was reviewed by the University of British Columbia's Clinical Research Ethics Board (H22‐00523) and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki, with the exception of registration in a database. Participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolling in the study.

Participants

We recruited 19 healthy young adult participants without any neurological or musculoskeletal impairments to participate in this study. We removed data from one participant due to incomplete data, so the final sample size consisted of 18 participants (9 female and 9 male, 23.4 ± 3.1 years old, 173.6 ± 11.4 cm, 67.3 ± 11.7 kg, means ± SD).

Experimental set‐up

This experiment took place on a custom‐designed robotic balance platform to simulate human standing balance (Fig. 1A ; Qiao et al., 2023; Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024). The movements of the robot involved a real‐time controller (PXIe‐8133; National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA) while a data acquisition module (PXI‐6289; National Instruments) digitized the analog data from two force plates on the platform (BP250500‐2K‐3874; AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) and a field‐programmable gate array module (PXIe‐7846R; National Instruments) recorded the position of the motor, communicated and sent digital commands to the servomotor. The simulation, commands and data acquisition were run in LabVIEW 2019b (National Instruments) at 500 Hz. While the robotic platform can simulate motion in both anteroposterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) directions (Qiao et al., 2023), we only simulated AP balance for this experiment to focus on the ankle plantar and dorsi‐flexor muscles. AP tilt of the robotic platform, which is coupled to the AP whole‐body motion of participants (Qiao et al., 2023), was controlled by a rotary servomotor (R88M‐K1K530H‐BS2; self‐contained encoder: 131,072 counts and 16 mm per revolution; OMRON, Kyoto, Japan) and a linear actuator (TH 110 SP4 16 dia × 16 lead; Rollon, Olney, UK). The force plates on the robotic balance platform remained stable in space, and hence participants rotated their whole‐body around their ankle joints to balance upright.

The movement of the robotic balance platform was controlled by the torques that the participants applied to the two force platforms. The forces and torques from the participants were combined and transformed to the midpoint of the ankle joints to determine the net AP ankle torque. The movement of the robotic platform (and resulting whole‐body motion of the participants), was based on the dynamics of a single‐link inverted pendulum, a simplification often used to model standing balance in the AP direction (Gage et al., 2004; Loram & Lakie, 2002; Loram et al., 2006). While standing balance is a complex, multi‐segmental task, Gage and colleagues (Gage et al., 2004; Winter et al., 2003) demonstrated that an inverted pendulum model provides a valid approximation of whole‐body oscillations in the AP direction for human standing balance. As participants leaned forward, a plantarflexion torque was required to stabilize the body against gravity. If this torque was greater than the torque due to gravity, participants would then accelerate backwards (Forbes et al., 2016). To mimic the dynamics of an inverted pendulum in real‐time, we converted a continuous transfer function to a discrete‐time equivalent using the zero‐order hold method (Luu et al., 2011): Iθ¨−mmgLθ=T θT=1Is2−mmgL Where θ and θ¨ represent the angular position and acceleration, respectively, of the whole‐body centre of mass, I is the mass moment of inertia of the body around the ankle joint from a distributed mass model (Luu et al., 2011; Qiao et al., 2023; Winter, 2009), mm is the effective moving mass of each participant, which is estimated at 0.971 of the total body mass to remove the mass of the feet, L is the distance from the ankle joint (axis of rotation) to the whole‐body centre of mass, g is the gravitational acceleration of 9.8 m/s2, and T is the ankle torque applied to the body. When an artificial delay was introduced, the torque produced by the participants was delayed by the desired time lag before being fed into the state‐space equation. The baseline delay of the control system for AP sway is 9.4 ms (Qiao et al., 2023), which is much smaller than the imposed delay humans are able to perceive (150 ms) (Rasman et al., 2021).

We performed the following anthropometric measurements for each participant to program the simulation and set up the experiment: mass, whole‐body height, centre of mass height, trunk length and hip joint centre coordinates. We calculated the hip joint centre coordinates from the participants’ hip width (distance between bilateral anterior superior iliac spines), hip depth (average distance from anterior superior iliac spines to posterior superior iliac spines) and leg length (medial malleolus to anterior superior iliac spine, via the medial femoral epicondyle) using a regression equation (Harrington et al., 2007). Participants stood upright on the robotic balance platform with their stance width at 50% of their hip joint centre width.

To measure lower limb muscle activity, we recorded surface electromyography (EMG) bilaterally from the soleus (Sol), medial gastrocnemius (mGas) and tibialis anterior (TA). We positioned surface electrodes (Blue Sensor M series, 40.8 × 34 mm; AMBU, Ballerup, Denmark) following SENIAM guidelines (Leis & Trapani, 2000), although this was modified slightly by placing the Sol electrodes below the lateral gastrocnemius. The EMG signals were amplified (2000×) and band‐pass filtered (10–500 Hz) with a NeuroLog system (NL900D, NL 820 and NL136; Digitimer, Welwyn Garden City, UK). Following this, the EMG data were collected at 2000 Hz by a separate data acquisition board (PXI‐6289; National Instruments) running LabVIEW 2019b (National Instruments). To synchronize between the robotic system and EMG recording system, we sent a common trigger signal to both at the start and end of each standing balance period as well as a continuous multi‐sine signal throughout the recording for redundancy.

Robotic balance platform protocol

Following the anatomical measurements and EMG electrode application, participants stood upright on the robotic balance platform (Fig. 1A ). We aligned the participants’ ankle AP axis of rotation with the axis of rotation of the robot and secured them to pelvis and shoulder brackets using seatbelts. To minimize the initial ankle torque applied to the ground by the participants at neutral position (Fx/Fy < ±15 N and Mx/My < ±5 N m), we ensured that they were relaxed, their feet position was aligned with the robot axis of rotation, and their body was aligned with the earth‐vertical line passing through this axis. Participants received standardized instructions regarding how the torques and forces that they applied to the ground actuated the robot and that they were always in control of the robot motion until they hit a motion limit. They also received specific instructions for each phase of the experimental protocol (Fig. 1B ). Following these instructions participants were given an opportunity to balance naturally on the robotic platform for 2–5 min and were then encouraged to explore the full movement of the robot until they were comfortable with the movement of the robotic platform.

Each period of standing balance was considered an episode. During all episodes, we imposed virtual angular position limits to the self‐generated balance movements of 6° anterior and 3° posterior as well as angular velocity limits of ±12.5°/s. These positional limits were chosen to replicate limits normally observed during natural quiet standing balance (Luu et al., 2011). When participants reached or exceeded the imposed position limits, we determined they experienced a virtual fall. To replicate the consequences of a fall during real‐world standing balance, episodes were ended when a virtual fall occurred, unlike previous studies that have allowed participants to continue to balance even if they reached (or exceeded) the virtual limits (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024). Following a virtual fall, the robotic simulation ended, and participants were passively moved back into their neutral position (0°) by the balance platform. Alternatively, when the participants exceeded the virtual angular velocity limits, the velocity was held constant at ±12.5°/s until it fell below the limit again to ensure participant safety.

EMG normalization

To normalize muscle contraction amplitude, participants held near static positions of 4° forward (targeting Sol and mGas) and 2° backward (targeting TA) for 10 s. Each target position was repeated three times. This resulted in submaximal contractions, which we used for EMG normalization to express EMG activity levels in a balance‐relevant context, given that muscle activity levels are comparatively low during quiet standing (Joseph & Nightingale, 1952; Portnoy & Morin, 1956; Smith, 1954).

Experimental phases

To determine the neuromuscular adaptations to an imposed standing balance delay and after‐effects following the removal of the delay, the main experiment consisted of three phases: pre‐adaptation, adaptation and post‐adaptation (see Fig. 1B ). No control group was included as recent work has shown that simply balancing on the robotic platform without delays does not lead to any adaptation to imposed delays (Rasman et al., 2024).

Pre‐adaptation

During the pre‐adaptation phase of the experiment, we instructed participants to relax and balance naturally for 2 × 120 s trials. Each trial was a single episode as no falls occurred. This allowed us to quantify their baseline standing balance characteristics (kinetics, kinematics and muscle activation patterns). No artificial delay was imposed for these episodes; hence they balanced with the baseline delay of the system (9.4 ms).

Adaptation

During the adaptation phase, participants performed the same standing balance task while we imposed a 250 ms delay between their self‐generated torque and resulting body position during which we characterized their adaptation through their kinetic, kinematic and EMG responses. This delay was chosen as it enabled participants to adapt within a 20‐min session, thus allowing us to quantify the immediate after‐effects of the adaptation. Participants were not provided with any information about the changes to their standing balance control, other than informing them that it may become more difficult to balance, but we reminded them they always maintained control of their whole‐body motion. Participants balanced for 20 min with this imposed delay. The adaptation time only included time during an episode, so the timer was stopped each time a virtual fall occurred. For example, a 15 s episode followed by a 30 s break after a virtual fall and then a 20 s episode would only count towards 35 s of adaptation time.

Post‐adaptation

Following the adaptation phase, participants balanced for two final trials to characterize the after‐effects of the adaptation to the imposed delay. In the first trial, participants balanced for the first 15 s with the 250 ms delay before it was abruptly removed so they could balance without the delay for another 120 s. This abrupt transition allowed us to quantify the immediate after‐effects of adaptation on the net ankle torque, muscle activity and whole‐body oscillations. Participants also performed a second trial involving 120 s of quiet standing without the imposed delay.

Data reduction and analysis

All analyses were performed using custom‐made scripts in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Neuromechanical changes across adaptation protocol

To quantify the changes to standing balance and muscle activation before, during, and following the 20‐min adaptation period, we measured the changes to standing balance parameters and muscle activity during the pre‐adaptation, adaptation and post‐adaptation periods.

Standing balance parameters

To determine the changes to standing balance, we calculated the number of virtual falls (i.e. how many times the participant reached the virtual limit), whole‐body angular velocity variance, and ankle torque variance for each participant. The number of falls was determined by summing the total number of occasions each participant hit a virtual position limit for consecutive 30‐s segments in the 20‐min adaptation period, resulting in a 30 s temporal resolution for the adaptation changes over the 20‐min period. We chose a shorter time window than the 60‐s segments used previously (Rasman et al., 2024, 2021) to allow for greater time resolution given the shorter adaptation period we used. We calculated whole‐body angular velocity by first determining the whole‐body angular position using the linear encoder displacement and known geometry of the backboard. Following this, the data were lowpass filtered (4th order, 20 Hz dual‐pass Butterworth) and the first time‐derivative was taken to determine whole‐body angular velocity. Net ankle torque was calculated from the forces and moments from the two force platforms on the robotic platform (see ‘Experimental set‐up’). Whole‐body angular velocity variance has commonly been used to quantify adaptations to imposed standing balance delays and exhibit an exponential decrease with training (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023). Given that maintaining balance in the AP direction requires active ankle torque modulation (Loram & Lakie, 2002; Loram et al., 2006, 2005), ankle torque variance can also be used to estimate adaptations to imposed standing balance delays and similarly decreases throughout the adaptation (Rasman et al., 2023).

For the angular velocity variance and ankle torque variance, we calculated the variance in non‐overlapping 2 s windows (Rasman et al., 2021) and then averaged all the variances within non‐overlapping 30 s segments. For example, if an episode lasted 5 s, two 2 s windows would be included. Alternatively, if an episode was <2 s, no data were included from this episode. We also counted the number of virtual falls and calculated the variances during the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases by averaging the 2 s window variances across the entire two 2‐min trials. To characterize any immediate (and potentially transient) postural changes following the removal of the delay, we also calculated 2 s window angular velocity and ankle torque variances across participants for the 10 s period (i.e. five 2 s windows) prior to and following the removal of the delay in the post‐adaptation phase.

Muscle activation patterns

Given that muscle activity represents the underlying active neural control of balance, we also characterized muscle co‐contraction and activation before, during and after the adaptation period. First, the EMG data were band‐pass filtered at 10–500 Hz with a second‐order dual‐pass Butterworth filter. We then applied a 100 ms moving root mean square (RMS) to each of the EMG channels. To normalize the EMG data, we divided each signal by the corresponding muscle RMS from a 100 ms window during the EMG normalization phase. This normalization window was chosen as the continuous 100 ms period within ±0.1° of the target body angles (4° for Sol and mGas, −2° for TA) with the lowest whole‐body angular velocity variance. This allowed us to normalize muscle activity to baseline levels that are relevant for quiet standing balance. We then averaged the normalized EMG data between the bilateral muscles (i.e. mean of right and left sides) to yield a normalized average bilateral EMG for the Sol, mGas and TA. Consequently, all analyses and results referring to Sol, mGas and TA imply an averaging between the activity of bilateral muscles.

To determine how participants altered their motor command patterns during the balance adaptation to imposed delays, we computed the co‐contraction index (CCI) of the bilateral antagonist muscle pairs (Sol–TA and mGas–TA). Co‐contraction of antagonist ankle muscle pairs is an indicator of task difficulty during standing balance (Alizadehsaravi et al., 2020; Allum et al., 2002; Nelson‐Wong et al., 2012; Oomen et al., 2015; Reynolds, 2010) and CCIs have been used to quantify sensorimotor adaptations to various arm reaching tasks (Gribble et al., 2003; Huang et al., 2012; Thoroughman & Shadmehr, 1999). The CCI was calculated with the following equation for each antagonist muscle pair (Sol–TA and mGas–TA) (Lewek et al., 2004; Rudolph et al., 2000): CCIi=lowihighi×lowi+highi Where CCIi is the CCI at the current sample, with a smaller value indicating less co‐contraction (i.e. the antagonist muscles are not activated at the same time) and a larger value indicating greater co‐contraction (i.e. the antagonist muscles are activated simultaneously). Unlike other implementations of this equation, the CCI was not bound between 0 and 1 because we did not normalize EMG to maximal voluntary contraction. In this equation, lowi and highi are the muscles with the lowest and highest EMG amplitude, respectively, within the antagonist muscle pair. An advantage of using this approach is that muscle co‐activation at low amplitudes results in a lower CCI than muscle co‐activation at larger amplitudes because the ratio of low to high muscle activation is multiplied by the net muscle activity, something not considered in other co‐contraction indices (Falconer & Winter, 1985; Vitali et al., 2021).

For the changes to CCI, we extracted the mean CCI for each 30 s window across the 20‐min adaptation period as well as the mean CCI for the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases. During the 30 s windows, we also extracted peak normalized muscle activation for the Sol, mGas and TA to quantify the changes to overall muscle activity during the adaptation task. We extracted peak muscle activity from each 30 s window to quantify the self‐generated balance‐correcting motor commands without considering the periods of little or no muscle activity that occur during standing balance.

Stationary frequency analyses

To determine how the spectral properties of the balance parameters and muscle activity changed throughout the adaptation, we calculated the auto spectra of the net ankle torque, whole‐body angular velocity, and the RMS‐normalized muscle activations (see previous section for normalization procedure) (Rasman et al., 2024). The auto spectra of the muscle activity were calculated for the net ankle muscle activity (Sol + mGas − TA) to provide insight into the overall muscle activity that drives the net AP ankle torque as well as for each bilateral muscle pair (mean RMS normalized EMG from Sol, mGas, TA) to determine how each muscle pair contributed to the overall muscle activity and associated changes. For each participant, we extracted 5 s non‐overlapping windows of data within episodes, which provided a frequency resolution of 0.2 Hz. This 5 s window was chosen as a 2 s window would result in only a 0.5 Hz resolution, whereas previous results have found a 0.2 Hz resolution is needed to adequately characterize ankle torque spectral changes during balance delay adaptations (Rasman et al., 2024). Similar to the variance calculations (although the window sizes were different; see section ‘Standing balance parameters’), if an episode lasted 11 s, two 5 s windows were included. Alternatively, if an episode was <5 s, no data were included from this episode. We defined the last 10 extracted windows from the pre‐adaption phase as the baseline, the first 10 windows from the adaptation phase as early adaptation, the last 10 windows from the adaptation phase as late adaptation, and the first 10 windows from the post‐adaptation phase (not including the 15 s with the 250 ms delay) as post‐adaptation. Given that many balance episodes were shorter than 5 s near the start of the adaptation phase (i.e. due to virtual falls), the early adaptation phase windows included the first 75 s to 174 s of the total adaptation phase. We then calculated the average auto spectra across these 10 windows for each participant. We also normalized the auto spectra by their sum from 0.2 to 5 Hz to visualize how the frequency distribution changed across the adaptation and minimize effects related to magnitude‐only changes.

Time–frequency analyses

To identify the time course of any after‐effects related to the balance adaptation task, we used a time–frequency approach to quantify the non‐stationary spectral properties of the ankle torques and the following RMS normalized ankle EMG combinations: net muscle activity (Sol + mGas − TA), Sol, mGas and TA. This allowed us to determine how the spectral properties of the net balance‐correcting motor commands and their contribution from each muscle changed over time, with a specific focus on the delay–no delay transition. We applied Morlet wavelet transformations (Cohen, 2019; Zhan et al., 2006) with a frequency resolution of 0.2 Hz from 0 to10 Hz to the net ankle torque and muscle activity. This was done for data during the pre‐adaptation phase (to illustrate the baseline behaviour) as well as the post‐adaptation phase prior to and following the removal of the 250 ms delay (to highlight after‐effects associated with adaptation). The full‐width at half‐maximum (i.e. the width of the Gaussian between the 50% amplitude points) for the Morlet wavelets was set to 3 s to improve the frequency resolution, meaning the resulting temporal precision of the time–frequency analysis is slightly lower than 3 s due to some power in the wavelets remaining outside the full‐width at half‐maximum (Cohen, 2019). Time‐dependent spectral power was calculated by multiplying the result of the Morlet wavelet convolution by its conjugate. The pooled estimates were then calculated by taking the mean of the time‐dependent spectral power across the participants.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks) and JASP 0.18.3.0 (JASP Team, 2023). The significance threshold was set to P = 0.05. Most of the data were determined to not be normally distributed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, so non‐parametric statistical tests were performed.

Changes across adaptation period

To determine if the number of falls, whole‐body angular velocity variance, net ankle torque variance, peak Sol, peak mGas, peak TA, Sol–TA CCI and mGas–TA CCI changed throughout the experimental phases, we performed Friedman's test with the data from the entire pre‐adaptation phase (pre), the first 30 s of the adaptation phase (early), the last 30 s of the adaptation phase (late), and the entire post‐adaptation phase following the removal of the delay (post). Post hoc Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests (with Holm's correction) were performed for the following comparisons: pre–early, early–late, and pre–post (except for number of falls given that we did not observe a fall during the pre and post phases). To determine the immediate changes to whole‐body angular velocity variance and net ankle torque variance following the removal of the delay in the post‐adaptation phase, we performed a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test to compare the variance in the final 2 s with the delay with the variance in first 2 s without the delay. This was repeated with the subsequent 2 s periods following the removal of the delay, until a significant difference was found. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using the Holm correction.

For each participant, we also fitted the changes in these variables across the adaptation period to an exponential decay function: fx=aebx Where a and b were fitted with least‐squares, b<0 to model a decay function, x is the time (from 0.5 to 20 min in 0.5‐min increments), and f(x) is the predicted measure. For each participant fit, we determined the time constant (τ=1−b) and the coefficient of determination (R2).

Stationary frequency analyses

To determine if after‐effects were present in the post‐adaptation phase, we compared the changes to the spectral properties of the net ankle torque, whole‐body angular velocity, and the normalized muscle activity between the pre‐adaptation and post‐adaptation phases using a bootstrapping approach (Foulger et al., 2024; Hannan et al., 2021). We randomly selected 18 participants (with replacement) and extracted the auto spectra between 0.2 and 10 Hz of the pre‐adaptation and post‐adaptation for the whole‐body angular velocity and net ankle torque, as well as net muscle, Sol, mGas and TA activity. We then calculated the group mean auto spectra of the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases and determined their difference (i.e. post minus pre). We repeated this process 10,000 times. Following this, we sorted the auto spectra in ascending order at each frequency and determined the 99% confidence interval by taking the 50th and 9950th data points. The post‐adaptation auto spectra were determined to be significantly larger than the pre‐adaptation auto spectra when the lower end of the 99% confidence interval was >0, and vice versa. If the 99% confidence interval crossed zero, there were no significant differences between the pre‐ and post‐adaptation auto spectra.

To characterize the changes to the spectra properties across the adaptation protocol with a single indicator, we also extracted the median power frequency between 0.2 and 10 Hz from the auto spectra of each measure for the baseline, early adaptation, late adaptation and post‐adaptation. This allowed us to extract changes to the overall frequency distribution. To determine how the median power frequency of each measure changed throughout the adaptation, we used Freidman's tests and a post hoc Wilcoxon signed‐rank test as described above (see ‘Changes across adaptation period’).

Bootstrapped time‐frequency differences

To determine the temporal and spectral properties of the after‐effects following the removal of the 250 ms delay on the net ankle torque and RMS normalized muscle activity, we performed a bootstrapping procedure on the time‐dependent auto spectra (Foulger et al., 2024; Hannan et al., 2021). We randomly selected 18 participants (with replacement) and extracted their time‐dependent auto spectra for each of the measures (net ankle torque, net muscle activity, Sol activity, mGas activity and TA activity) in the last 60 s of the pre‐adaptation and first 60 s of the post‐adaptation (following the removal of the delay). We used a 60 s period to characterize the duration of the adaptation after‐effects following the removal of the delay as opposed to the 30 s period used for stationary analyses because we did not know if changes would last longer than 30 s. The time‐dependent auto spectra were down‐sampled to 20 Hz to reduce the computational load of the bootstrapping. Given that we expected the representative auto spectra to be relatively stationary during the pre‐adaptation (i.e. the overall balance behaviour was not expected to change throughout the pre‐adaptation in the absence of a transition), we also randomly circularly shifted (from 0° to 360°) each participant's pre‐adaptation auto spectra time series to remove the effects of random fluctuations in net torque and muscle activity that occur when standing naturally (Loram et al., 2005). We then determined the pooled average time‐dependent auto spectra for the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases and calculated the difference (post‐adaptation minus pre‐adaptation). We repeated this process 10,000 times. Following this, we sorted the time‐dependent auto spectra in ascending order at each frequency and time point and determined the 99% confidence interval by taking the 50th and 9950th data points. Significant differences across time and frequency were determined similar to the stationary auto spectra (see ‘Stationary frequency analyses’).

Model of standing balance

We implemented a linear quadratic regulator (LQR) to reveal the changes in sensorimotor gains explaining the control of balance following a transition from delayed standing to normal standing balance (Fig. 2A ). Modelling from proportional‐derivative controllers has suggested a decreased stability region for the gains required to balance with increased delays (van der Kooij & Peterka, 2011). Previous experimental results, however, have shown increased torque auto spectra between 1 and 2 Hz (Rasman et al., 2024) suggestive of larger sensorimotor gains to control standing balance with imposed delays. To investigate this apparent contradiction and reveal the mechanisms of adaptation, we extracted the gains from LQR controllers optimized with and without the 250 ms imposed robotic delay to determine how sensorimotor gains (i.e. LQR angular position and velocity gains) are modulated as we adapt to balancing with imposed delays.

Balance model and exemplar simulated data

A, linear‐quadratic‐regulator (LQR) model for the control of standing balance. We represented body dynamics as an inverted pendulum based on the average anthropometrics of the participants, set passive stiffness at 70% of load stiffness (Loram et al., 2007) and damping ratio at 5.73 N m s/rad (Loram & Lakie, 2002). To emulate the physiological control of standing balance, we modelled angular position and angular velocity noises as pink noise based on perceptual thresholds for balance perturbations (RMS values: 0.002 rad and 0.0036 rad/s, respectively) (Fitzpatrick & McCloskey, 1994; Tisserand et al., 2022). The torque generated by the controller was influenced by signal‐dependent noise, modelled using bi‐linear curves to induce motor noise proportional to torque magnitude. These profiles were informed by isometric experiments measuring ankle torque during plantarflexion and dorsiflexion, which demonstrated distinct signal‐dependent motor noise scaling at torque levels below and above 10% of maximum voluntary contraction (Tracy, 2007). We incorporated prediction errors and feedback errors in the control of standing balance. First, we compared the predicted state to the set point at t = t + ΔtL, where t indicates when torque is introduced into the plant (inverted pendulum), subsequently generating both pendulum angular position and velocity to generate an error signal relative to the desired future state (comparison 1). Second, we determined the feedback error by comparing the noisy state against controller‐predicted delayed states at t = t* – Δt S (see comparison 2). The two errors were summed and supplied as input to the LQR controller, which then generated an action tailored for the present state, optimized for time t = t* + Δt M + Δt R. B, sample standing balance without delays simulation data from the models optimized with no delay (to simulate pre‐adaptation; light blue) and with a 250 ms delay (to simulate post‐adaptation; black). We directly compared simulated data between the models trained with and without the 250 ms delay by starting the simulations with the same randomization seed. To facilitate comparisons, we have only included the matching feedback gains (k = 0.7) for post and pre‐adaptation. Note that the y‐axis scaling has been increased compared to Fig. 1C .

We integrated physiological sensory (Δt S = 60 ms) and motor (Δt M = 60 ms) delays as well as artificial robotic delay (Δt R = 250 ms or no delay) to match the experimental procedures. To solve the control problem (balancing an inverted pendulum), we derived the LQR balance controller's angular position and velocity gains via an optimal solution that minimized the following cost function: J=∫0∞xTtQxt+uTtRutdt Where Q represents a positive semi‐definite matrix that assigns weights to the system states (angular position and velocity), R denotes a positive definite matrix attributing weights to control efforts (torque). The state vector for the inverted pendulum is represented by x, and u represents the applied control effort. The pendulum control problem yielded controller gains for angular position and velocity, accounting for delays represented as Padé approximations in the continuous‐time algebraic Riccati equation. The controller obtained by the LQR solution acts on a summation of (1) feedback error with a gain (k; set heuristically) and (2) an error based on a predicted state (body angle and angular velocity) and setpoint. We incorporated a Smith predictor in the control to anticipate the consequences of motor actions in the presence of delays and subsequently regulate the system (Miall et al., 1993). The simulation accommodates the total delays (Δt L) to maintain an upright posture. Typically, the total delay (Δt L) consists of both motor and sensory delays (Δt S and Δt M combined; 120 ms), but an additional term (Δt R = 250 ms) was added to replicate the imposed delay experienced by the participants during the adaptation phase of the experiment (Δt L = 370 ms).

We optimized the LQR controller to model both normal balance control and balance control following adaptation to a 250 ms delay, representing analogues to the pre‐adaptation and late adaptation conditions of the experiment, respectively. We first optimized and simulated the control of balance using the LQR controller with our Smith predictor compensating only for the normal sensory and motor delays (i.e. Δt L = 120 ms to model the pre‐adaptation experimental condition). We then optimized the controller with an additional robotic delay (Δt R = 250 ms) introduced, which was accounted for in the simulation control scheme (Δt L = 370 ms). To simulate the immediate adaptation after‐effects when participants returned to the balancing condition without an imposed delay (i.e. the post‐adaptation experimental condition; Δt S + Δt M = 120 ms), we removed the robotic delay (Δt R = 250 ms) from both control and plant structures while preserving the controller optimized for the total learned delay (i.e. summation of the three delays). This was done to represent the abrupt transition from the imposed delays to the natural balancing while the controller may have initially used the sensorimotor balance control (i.e. the control gains) adapted to the imposed 250 ms condition (Δt L = 370 ms), before transitioning back to the natural control of balance (Δt L = 120 ms). Using the torques generated by the controllers balancing an inverted pendulum with no added delays (Δt L = 120 ms), we then compared their spectral components (5 s windows, 0.2 Hz; replicating the procedures for the stationary frequency analyses of the experimental data) for the data simulating the pre‐ (control gains optimized for Δt L = 120 ms) and post‐adaptation (control gains optimized for Δt L = 370 ms) periods.

Results

Balance and muscle activity changes across adaptation

All participants maintained upright balance without any virtual falls in the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases. When participants balanced with the delays, they all experienced virtual falls (i.e. they reached the limits of the balance motion) as well as increases in body kinematics and kinetics, which decreased with time. Compared to the pre‐adaptation phase, participants maintained their upright balance with increased (all P < 0.001) whole‐body angular velocity variance (178×) and net ankle torque variance (126×) during the early adaptation phase. Falls (from 7 to 0), angular velocity variance (0.24×) and ankle torque variance (0.17×) all significantly decreased (all P < 0.001) from early to late adaptation, but these variables showed no significant difference between the pre‐ and post‐adaptation phases (Table 1). The decreases observed during the adaptation phase were well fitted to exponential decay functions (median R2 = 0.51–0.81) with median time constants ranging from 2.0 to 10.8 min (falls: 2.0 min [range: 0.7–4.2 min]; angular velocity variance: 10.8 min [5.3–20.8 min]; ankle torque variance: 10.2 min [4.0–19.1 min]; Fig. 3). In the 2 s windows immediately following the removal of the delay (Fig. 3 insets), there was an immediate decrease in the angular velocity variance (z = 3.593, P < 0.001) and ankle torque variance (z = 3.462, P < 0.001), which then reached pre‐adaptation levels within 10 s. Thus, no obvious changes occurred to the balance behaviour following the removal of the delay, aside from the immediate return to the baseline standing balance control.

Spectral properties of balance and muscle activation across adaptation

To determine how the spectral properties of the whole‐body angular velocity (Fig. 5A ), net ankle torque (Fig. 5B ) and muscle activity (Fig. 6) changed throughout the adaptation, we extracted the auto spectra from the pre‐adaptation, early adaptation, late adaptation and post‐adaptation experimental phases. For all the measures, there was an overall increase in the spectral power from the pre‐adaptation to early adaptation, which decreased in late adaptation. In post‐adaptation, the spectral power returned to similar levels to the pre‐adaptation, except for a noticeable increase in the power between ∼1 and 2 Hz.

To determine whether these observed increases between ∼1 and 2 Hz were significant, we bootstrapped the differences between pre‐ and post‐adaptation auto spectra. For both whole‐body angular velocity and net ankle torque, we observed a significant increase in power from pre‐adaptation to post‐adaptation between 1.2 and 2 Hz (Fig. 5). Similarly, we observed increases in power between 1.2 and 2 Hz for net muscle activity and mGas activity with an additional increase at 2.6 Hz. For TA activity, spectral power post‐adaptation only increased at 1.6 Hz, but Sol activity did not increase in the 1–2 Hz frequency band (Fig. 6). The bootstrapping analyses were also supported by the changes to the median power frequency (Table 2). From pre‐ to post‐adaptation, there was a significant increase in the median power frequencies of the ankle torque (Δ = 0.10 Hz [range: −0.14 to 0.90 Hz], P = 0.004) as well as the net ankle muscle (Δ = 0.45 Hz [−0.45 to 1.19 Hz], P < 0.001) and mGas (Δ = 0.39 Hz [−0.46 to 1.27 Hz], P < 0.001). Overall, these results supported the hypothesis that muscle activity would present similar increases in 1–2 Hz oscillations as ankle torque following the removal of the imposed delay.

Time‐frequency auto spectra

To characterize the time course of the spectral after‐effects associated with balance adaptation to an imposed delay, we implemented a time–frequency analysis on the net ankle torque and muscle activity (Fig. 7). Following the removal of the imposed delay, the average of the pooled ankle torque power between 1 and 2 Hz increased (2.4×) when comparing the 10 s period following the removal of the delay to the previous 10 s period. A similar increase in power was observed in the net EMG (3.9×), mGas and TA (3.6–4.2×), but the increase was smaller in Sol (1.4×). To determine the duration of these spectral changes after the abrupt removal of the delay, we performed a bootstrapping analysis to compare the power spectra differences between the pre‐ and post‐adaptation periods that both included standing balance behaviour under no delay conditions. For the net ankle torque, the significant increases in power in the 1–10 Hz band (only shown up to 4 Hz in Fig. 7) from pre‐adaptation to post‐adaptation lasted approximately 5 s following the removal of the 250 ms delay but lasted up to 20 s for frequencies between 1 and 2 Hz. Similarly, the net muscle activity spectral power remained significantly elevated for approximately 10 s after the removal of the delay between the 1.2 and 2.2 Hz frequency band. We also observed increases in power lasting 5–10 s after delay removal in mGas (1.2–2.2 Hz) and TA (1.2–1.8 Hz), but these changes only lasted ∼2 s in the Sol (1.2–1.8 Hz).

LQR model results

The current results, and previous work on a similar balance simulator (Rasman et al., 2024), revealed increases in angular velocity and torque power between 1 and 2 Hz after the removal of a delay during standing balance. These after‐effects characterized by a shift to higher frequencies for balance imply larger control gains for the maintenance of upright standing, a suggestion that may appear at odds with the predicted reduced stability region for gains from proportional‐derivative controllers (Peterka, 2018; van der Kooij & Peterka, 2011) and the perception of participants reporting ‘calming their motor actions’ (Rasman et al., 2024, 2021). To investigate this apparent incongruence, we utilized an LQR to model the control of standing balance while incorporating a predictor designed to account for delays (Rasman et al., 2024).

An LQR controller equipped with a Smith predictor successfully maintained a pendulum upright (i.e. no falls occurred) for imposed robotic delays of 250 ms (Δt L = 370 ms), resulting in an ∼ 98% increase in the position and velocity control gains compared to the same model optimized without imposed delays (Δt L = 120 ms). To model the after‐effects of balance adaptation to an imposed delay, we removed the robotic delay from the simulated control loop while keeping the larger gains (i.e. those optimized for the control of balance with the imposed 250 ms delay). Both models maintained the inverted pendulum upright. Similar to the experimental results, the model with the higher gains revealed an increase in the torque and velocity auto spectra within the 0.5–2 Hz frequency range compared to the LQR controller that was optimized without the imposed delays when both balanced the inverted pendulum without imposed delays (Δt L = 120 ms). Although this replicated an important feature of the experimental data, the increase in 0.5–2 Hz power was accompanied by a reduction in power for frequencies ranging from 2 to 5 Hz absent from the experimental data. To further tune the model, we then modified the feedback gain (k) that was heuristically set to 0.7. Results from the simulations showed that adjusting feedback gains to 0.55 (pre‐adaptation) and 0.7 (post‐adaptation) reproduced the main frequency features observed in the torque and velocity auto spectra after‐effects (Fig. 8).

Angular velocity (A) and ankle torque (B) auto spectra from the LQR model simulation of an inverted pendulum balancing with only physiological delays (120 ms)

The results show an increase in power in the frequency range of 0.5–2 Hz due to the higher control gains when the LQR controller was optimized with a 250 ms delay (Gainangle = 327 vs. 645 N m/rad for pre‐ vs post‐adaptation; and Gainvelocity = 167 vs. 330 N m s/rad for pre‐ vs. post‐adaptation). The insets show the angular velocity and torque auto spectra for the tuned feedback gains. These results suggest that, in addition to increasing controller gains, adjusting feedback gains during the learning period could be a strategy to balance in the presence of the delay.

Discussion

Here, we investigated the physiological mechanisms responsible for adapting the control of standing balance to sensorimotor delays. When participants initially stood on a robotic balance platform with a 250 ms imposed delay, they exhibited increased balance oscillations and muscle (co)activation while also experiencing virtual falls. As participants adapted to the 250 ms delay, the number of falls, angular velocity variance and ankle torque variance decreased along with ankle muscle activation and co‐contraction. After the delay was removed, participants maintained their upright balance without falls and after‐effects manifested as transient (10–20 s) increased spectral power between 1 and 2 Hz in the net ankle torque which were primarily driven by increases in mGas and TA activity. These findings suggest that alterations in the frequency content of the motor commands are necessary to maintain an upright posture with delays. Simulation results from an LQR model showed that the increased sensorimotor gains necessary to balance with delays resulted in the observed increases in motor command and ankle torque spectral power after the removal of the delay. The observed after‐effects indicate that sensorimotor adaptations occur to facilitate balancing with imposed delays, and that these adaptations can last for 10–20 s before we transition back to our baseline (or preferred) standing balance control.

When a 250 ms delay was imposed, participants initially exhibited large balance instabilities and experienced virtual falls, replicating previous findings (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024) and supporting predictions from computational models of standing balance (Bingham et al., 2011; Milton & Insperger, 2019; van der Kooij & Peterka, 2011). To adapt motor control to new contexts, the nervous system generates probabilistic estimates of self‐motion to infer the relationship between motor and sensory signals (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Krakauer & Mazzoni, 2011; Wolpert et al., 2011). In the presence of imposed sensorimotor delays, the sensory consequences of self‐generated motor commands are interpreted by the central nervous system as an external stimulus (Blakemore et al., 1999; Farrer et al., 2008; Haering & Kiesel, 2015; Wen, 2019) and for standing balance, as unexpected balance motion (Rasman et al., 2021). This results in a mismatch between our predicted self‐motion and sensory feedback, which persists until we update our sensorimotor estimates to the new delay (Kilteni et al., 2019; Rasman et al., 2024, 2021; Stetson et al., 2006; Wolpert et al., 2011). Similarly, during visuomotor tracking with imposed delays, participants demonstrate large initial errors which improve as they adapt to the visual delays (Foulkes & Miall, 2000; Miall & Jackson, 2006; Miall et al., 1985). This underlies the importance of sensory predictions for the control of balance and movement, and how our ability to update these predictions is crucial for adaptation to novel environments.

Increased sensorimotor gains following adaptation results in brief after‐effects

We observed increased power between ∼1 and 2 Hz in the stationary auto spectra of the whole‐body angular velocity and net ankle torque in the post‐adaptation compared to pre‐adaptation phase. These results confirm previous after‐effects observed after balance adaptation to a 300 ms delay (Rasman et al., 2024). Given the similarity in delays between experiments (250 ms vs. 300 ms), we expected to replicate these previous results. The current observations extend these findings in multiple ways. First, our computer simulations revealed that this increase in 1 and 2 Hz power was driven by larger controller and feedback gains in the model optimized for standing with the delay. These simulations further suggest that larger sensorimotor gains are required to balance when an additional 250 ms delay is added. Second, the increase in ankle torque power at ∼1–2 Hz was associated with corresponding increases in spectral power in the TA and mGas. This may be a result of increased paradoxical contractions in the calf muscles, which occur between 1 and 2 Hz (Loram et al., 2005), due to the increased sensorimotor gains needed to balance with imposed delays. Given that postural sway increased in the 250 ms delay condition, it is predicted (based on work from Loram & Lakie, 2002) that intrinsic ankle stiffness decreased, as supported by observations from Sakanaka et al. (2016). This proposed reduction in intrinsic ankle stiffness would require an increased reliance on the active control of balance that extends after removal of the imposed delay. There were, however, only minimal changes to Sol spectral content following the return to normal standing. These differences in lower limb muscle contributions to the overall ankle muscle activity suggests a change in the distribution of motor commands following the transition to no delay. This is possibly as a result of the higher recruitment threshold for mGas during standing balance (Héroux et al., 2014) and increased proportion of type II fibres in TA and mGas (Johnson et al., 1973). Differences between Sol and mGas responses may also be due to the biarticular nature of mGas which crosses both the ankle and knee joints (Bobbert & van Ingen Schenau, 1988; Jacobs et al., 1993). When the knee is fully extended (as it is during upright balancing), increased activation of mGas motor units and reduced activation of Sol motor units occur during plantarflexion (Kennedy & Cresswell, 2001; Lauber et al., 2014).

The time–frequency decomposition of the signals revealed that the spectral changes were present for up to 20 s in the ankle torque while these effects lasted up to 10 s for the net muscle, mGas and TA activity. We propose this represents the duration it takes to update the sensorimotor gains following the removal of the delay. While vestibular‐evoked balance responses re‐engage in less than 1 s following the reinstatement of participant‐controlled motion (Luu et al., 2012), participants in the present study did not need to instantly update their sensorimotor gains following the removal of the delay given that they were able to maintain balance (and avoid falls) with the larger gains. Supporting our results, previous work (Teasdale & Simoneau, 2001) has shown it can take 10 s or longer to recalibrate our global balance control to altered sensory conditions. While increased sensorimotor gains allow us to maintain balance under baseline conditions, remaining in this state is likely suboptimal from both control and energetic perspectives. This is supported by the lower position and velocity control gains in the baseline (no imposed delay) LQR model, which was optimized based on control error (i.e. position and velocity errors) and energetic cost (i.e. active torque). Thus, the gradual transition in muscle activity spectral content reflects the return to sensorimotor gains that are optimized for balance control under baseline conditions.

Balance adaptations occur with decreasing muscle activity and co‐contraction

Over the adaptation protocol, we observed decreases in angular velocity variance and ankle torque variance as typically seen during adaptation to standing with imposed delays (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024). Rasman et al. (2023) found that young adults adapted to 250 ms delays with time constants of 7.3 min for both angular velocity variance and ankle torque variance, which is slightly shorter than the 8.3–9.1 min group time constants we observed. This may be due to methodological differences between studies; we stopped the balancing task when participants reached a virtual positional limit (i.e. experienced a virtual fall), whereas Rasman et al. (2023) allowed participants to continue to learn from the balance environment even when they exceeded the balance limits. Thus, our current approach provided a more ecological balance task by preventing participants from exploring near (or past) their biomechanical balance limits. The number of virtual falls decreased more rapidly throughout the adaptation protocol (group τ = 2 min), providing another indication of adaptation that is highly relevant for real‐world balancing. Indeed, given that avoiding falls is the primary goal for standing balance, participants prioritized this as the initial parameter to adapt to. Once the number of virtual falls was limited and participants could remain upright, they fine‐tuned their overall balance behaviour as reflected by the changes to angular velocity and ankle torque variance.

We also observed increases to all ankle muscle activity and co‐contraction at the start of the adaptation protocol, similar to previous findings that muscle activity and co‐contraction increase in response to a novel and challenging balance task (Alizadehsaravi et al., 2020; Allum et al., 2002; Iwamoto et al., 2017; Nelson‐Wong et al., 2012; Oomen et al., 2015; Reynolds, 2010). It has been suggested that increased muscle co‐contraction during arm reaching and postural tasks serves to increase stability and reduce movement errors (Gribble et al., 2003; Pienciak‐Siewert et al., 2020). Throughout the adaptation period, participants decreased their muscle activity and co‐contraction. These observations are similar to those by Sozzi et al. (2016) where ankle muscle activation and co‐contraction decreased as participants adapted to repeated balance perturbations. Our study expands on these findings given that this previous study involved external perturbations which are not equivalent to the self‐generated (but delayed) movements that occurred here on the robotic balance simulator. As participants adapted to the imposed delay, ankle muscle activity and co‐contraction decreased, with adaptation time constants longer (group τ = 10.5–25.2 min) than the angular velocity variance and ankle torque variance. Given that ankle muscle activation is required to remain upright and regulate the net ankle torque (Loram & Lakie, 2002; Loram et al., 2005; Winter et al., 1998), this concomitant decrease was expected. Such decreases in muscle activity and co‐contraction have also been reported as adaptation progresses for arm reaching tasks (Huang & Ahmed, 2014; Huang et al., 2012). A potential benefit to reductions in muscle activity and co‐contraction as we adapt to reaching and balancing tasks may be related to the improved manoeuvrability and decreased metabolic cost (Gribble et al., 2003; Pienciak‐Siewert et al., 2020).

Balance after‐effects are subtle and do not cause instability

Despite the strong body of literature on motor adaptation tasks, it is challenging to relate previous findings to standing balance adaptations. In classical arm reaching adaptation tasks, adaptation has been defined to comprise two components: explicit and implicit adaptation. Explicit adaptations are the changes to our movement that we consciously make to adjust to a novel task whereas implicit adaptations are automatic changes to our movement that occur as our sensorimotor control is updated (Kim et al., 2021; Krakauer et al., 2019). Given the continuous nature of balance and its simplified goal being to avoid falling (as opposed to a specific movement, i.e. to reach a certain target), parsing out the explicit and implicit contributions using this pre‐existing framework becomes challenging. While the goal of avoiding falls is clearly explicit and participants are able to report alterations in balance during challenging scenarios, the motor actions required to balance are mostly unperceived, and self‐generated balance‐correcting responses can even be mis‐attributed as perturbations (Dalton et al., 2017; Luu, 2010; Luu et al., 2012; Tisserand et al., 2022). In addition, vestibular‐evoked balance responses, which are largely subcortical (Marsden et al., 2005), can be modified in the absence of conscious perception when the relationship between motor commands and sensory feedback is altered or removed (Héroux et al., 2015; Luu et al., 2012; Rasman et al., 2021). While these principles regarding the control of upright standing suggest that adaptations when balancing with delays are mainly driven by implicit adaptation, such adaptations in arm reaching tasks result in gross movement after‐effects (e.g. overshooting of the target) which were not observed here, or in previous papers, following the removal an imposed delay (Rasman et al., 2021, 2023, 2024). Instead, after‐effects (following the removal of the delay) manifested as changes to the spectral content of the balance oscillations and motor commands, which did not, however, lead to overt balance changes. This highlights that while measuring gross movements may be suitable for observing after‐effects in arm reaching adaptation tasks, studies measuring changes to balance control should also characterize changes to the spectral content of the balance oscillations to identify sensorimotor control alterations.

Discrepancies between perception and sensorimotor gain

Previous perceptual reports have suggested that we must ‘calm’ our motor activity to adapt to balancing with imposed delays (Rasman et al., 2024). Our simulation results, however, indicated that increased sensorimotor gains explain the increased power in kinematics, kinetics and muscle activation at frequencies between 1 and 2 Hz observed when a 250 ms delay was removed. These computer simulations suggest that humans learn to balance with a delay by increasing the gain of the balance controller and that these gains remained initially elevated during the rapid transition associated with the delay removal. While these increased sensorimotor gains may seem contradictory with perceptual reports of reduced motor actions, humans exhibit poor conscious perception of their own motor actions while balancing (Luu, 2010; Tisserand et al., 2022). It is also possible that we do not perceive the increased sensorimotor gains required to balance with delays during late adaptation because there is an overall decrease in balance oscillations and muscle activity that occurs across the adaptation period. We note, however, there is still an overall increase in balance oscillations and muscle activity from baseline to late adaptation because of the increased sensorimotor gains required to remain upright in the presence of imposed delays.

The current simulated and empirical observations may also appear at odds with previous simulations from van der Kooij & Peterka (2011), who showed smaller stability regions (i.e. combinations of proportional and derivative gains resulting in stable balance) for proportional‐derivative controllers balancing an inverted pendulum with imposed delays. Their results, however, indicate that, despite the smaller stability regions, a proportional‐derivative controller can balance an inverted pendulum using larger gains when additional delays are present compared to when no additional delays are added. Consequently, our results do not oppose those from van der Kooij & Peterka (2011) and adding a state predictor (as we did here) to a proportional‐derivative controller would further increase the region of stability for controller gains. As predicted from our balance control model, oscillations in balance behaviour and ankle torque (in addition to muscle activity) shifted towards higher frequencies following the removal of a delay. These observations contrast with adaptations reported for a visuomotor reaching task (Miall & Jackson, 2006), where no change in corrective motor response frequency occurred. Miall & Jackson (2006) argued that the lack of changes in frequency content during visuomotor control provided evidence against a Smith predictor for visuomotor control. We propose that these differences in sensorimotor adaptation for balance compared to a visuomotor reaching task may reflect differences in the neural control of these movements. For example, the vestibular system contributes to balance control but is typically less relevant for reaching tasks (Forbes et al., 2015; Smith & Reynolds, 2017).

Clinical implications

Age‐related increases in sensory and motor conduction times can introduce delays in muscle activation for balance‐correcting responses ranging from approximately 20 to 30 ms (Allum et al., 2002; Lin & Woollacott, 2002), while individuals with neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis or Friedreich's ataxia may experience even longer delays: up to 109 ms (Cameron et al., 2008) and 165 ms (Diener et al., 1984), respectively. Although these delays are shorter than the 250 ms imposed in our experiment, they do not include force production delays. Furthermore, the underlying adaptation mechanisms – particularly the modulation of sensorimotor gains – remain relevant. A better understanding of how these gains are adjusted to maintain balance in the presence of sensorimotor delays could inform the development of targeted rehabilitation strategies aimed at restoring stability and reducing fall risk in populations affected by delayed sensorimotor responses.

Limitations

One limitation of our study is that while the robotic balance platform can impose delays on the visual, vestibular and somatosensory cues of self‐motion, it does not impose delays on the muscle contractions and how they are sensed (i.e. with muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs and foot sole receptors), potentially inducing a sensory conflict (Rasman et al., 2021). Additionally, it is unclear how hip and ankle muscles contribute to mediolateral balance adaptations given that we chose to only allow movement in the AP direction. Recent work, however, has shown similar after‐effects for AP and ML balance, and balance adaptations to imposed delays generalize across directions (Rasman et al., 2024). AP sway was constrained as an inverted pendulum for both the robotic balance platform and the LQR model. Although quiet standing in the AP direction has been adequately modelled as an inverted pendulum (Gage et al., 2004; Winter et al., 2003), this represents a simplification of standing balance biomechanics. For example, there may be biomechanical and sensing advantages to actuating multiple joints when balancing upright. Results from computational models suggest that stable balance under linear control may be possible at longer sensorimotor delays for a dual‐link (Kuo, 1995) than a single‐link (van der Kooij & Peterka, 2011) inverted pendulum. Future work should expand on the balance platform and LQR model to enable the exploration of multi‐joint strategies.

EMG crosstalk between antagonist muscles is also a potential limitation of our methodology. However, we observed periods of large plantar flexor (Sol or mGas) activation without concurrent TA activity (see Fig. 1C ), and vice versa, indicating that any potential crosstalk was minimal. Furthermore, crosstalk interference will proportionally decrease as the recorded muscle activity increases (Germer et al., 2021). Thus, any potential EMG crosstalk would have had minimal influence on our results. We also note that two participants did not increase their mGas activity or ankle muscle co‐contraction at the introduction of the imposed delay. This suggests that certain participants may not need to adapt their ankle muscle activity across a training protocol to sensorimotor delays. Future studies are required to address potential inter‐individual muscle responses and adaptations to the presence of delays in a balancing task.

Conclusion

In the present study, we used a robotic balance simulator to impose a 250 ms delay in the control of standing balance and characterized participants’ balance and muscle activity as they adapted to the balancing task. Participants adapted their balance to the imposed delay by decreasing their ankle muscle activation and co‐contraction across a 20‐min adaptation protocol. When the imposed delay was abruptly removed, we observed increases in the 1–2 Hz spectral content of the angular velocity and ankle torque driven mainly by changes to medial gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior activity, rather than large movement after‐effects due to the continuous nature of standing balance. Our results, supported by computer simulations from an LQR standing balance model, provide evidence that increased sensorimotor gains are needed to maintain upright balance with delays. Furthermore, we found that it takes 10–20 s to transition back to a baseline regulation of balance motor commands following the removal of the delay, possibly because higher sensorimotor gains are associated with suboptimal balance control and increased energy expenditure. These findings underscore the remarkable adaptability of human balance control, revealing that the nervous system can flexibly adjust sensorimotor strategies to maintain balance under changing conditions.
