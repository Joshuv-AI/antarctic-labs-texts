Left-handed cardiac surgery and training: a survey-based assessment
Authors: Eric E Vinck, Peyman Sardari Nia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESThere is a lack of guidance and scant learning resources for left-handed (LH) cardiac surgery residents and surgeons. No objective data exists to evaluate the reality of the training experience of LH cardiac surgeons and residents.METHODSA 32-question survey was designed for LH cardiac surgeons and residents. The survey questions were aimed towards understanding the experiences of LH cardiac surgeons and residents in order to identify and determine where the challenges of LH in cardiac surgery lie. The survey was disseminated by the European Association for Cardio-thoracic Surgery (EACTS) through online platforms, social media and the EACTS website.RESULTS74 total responses were gathered from the survey; 73% were true LH operators. During residency, 78.1% of LH cardiac surgery residents had no access to LH faculty. Of those with LH mentors, only 53.3% were supportive and helped teach LH techniques. As trainees, 49.3% considered coronary artery bypass grafting anastomosis to be the most difficult portion of LH technique. Upon initiating independent practice, LH cardiac surgeons consider being LH an advantage in comparison to residency.CONCLUSIONSIn LH cardiac surgery, there is a lack of tailored surgical exposure, training guidance, standardization, learning tools and teaching resources. Training resources for LH cardiac surgeons and residents should be developed.Keywords: Cardiac Surgery, Left-handed, Southpaw, Training, Survey

INTRODUCTION

Left-handedness (LH) has been antagonized throughout history. Conversely, LH has also been linked to intelligence and skillfulness. In the world of sports, such as in baseball and martial arts, LH offers significant advantages [1]. The left-handed surgical population accounts for 10% of surgeons worldwide who have highly specific training needs and challenges inherent to left-handed (LH) cardiac surgery [1]. Being left-handed has certain advantages in surgery; however, there is a lack of guidance and scant learning resources resulting in obstacles to left-handed cardiac surgeons and residents. The 1st paper on LH in cardiac surgery was published in 1986 by Pezzella followed by a letter-to-editor on this same paper by Reece [2, 3]. Pezzella described cardiopulmonary bypass cannulation techniques for LH cardiac surgeons [2, 3]. Ever since, little attention has been given to the training obstacles faced by southpaw cardiac surgeons and residents. Today, there are 11 papers available on PubMed aimed for LH cardiac surgery [1–11]. There are also only 7 instructional videos for LH cardiac surgery techniques provided by the Cardiothoracic Surgery Network (CTSNet) in comparison to an innumerable amount of instructional and technical videos for right-handed cardiac surgeons (https://www.ctsnet.org/content-areas/left-handed-surgery) [1–11]. Despite the lack of left-handed surgical instruments, scarce learning resources and limited access to left-handed cardiac surgery faculty, LH cardiac surgeons and residents must push through these training barriers. Achieving proficiency in LH cardiac surgery is mostly reached during later stages of training and during early career practice [1]. Although this is a general understanding, no objective data exists to evaluate the reality of the training experience of LH cardiac surgeons and residents. In this paper, we present the results of an online survey conducted to identify and detail training experiences of LH cardiac surgeons and residents.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

A 32-question survey was designed for left-handed cardiac surgeons and residents. The survey included questions on training experiences from being LH, left-handed technical perceptions, training support from faculty and surgical strengths and weaknesses. The survey was aimed for left-handed cardiac surgery residents and surgeons regardless of whether they operate with their left or right hand. The questions were then responded according to each surgeons’ dexterity preferences. During the month of June and July 2024, the survey was disseminated by the European Association for Cardio-thoracic Surgery (EACTS) through online platforms, social media and the EACTS website. Additionally, this survey was further relayed to and by members of the Association of Future African Cardiothoracic Surgeons (AFAC), The Brazilian Society of Cardiovascular Surgery, the Latin American Association of Cardiac and Endovascular Surgery (LACES) and The Asian Society for Cardiovascular and Thoracic Surgery for further dissemination. The survey was closed ending July, and final responses were gathered and analysed.

Statistics

Final survey results were expressed both in the number of respondents/answers per each question and according to the percentages of answers obtained for each question. Results were gathered using the online platform SurveyMonkey® and distributed in percentages according to the number of respondents per question. Final values are stated in percentiles. Additional statistical analysis was not required.

RESULTS

Survey responses were collected ending July 2024. Throughout the months of June and July 2024, 74 (100%) total responses were gathered from the survey. Responses came from Europe (29; 39.2%), followed by Latin America (19; 25.7%), North America (11; 14.9%), Asia (8; 10.8%), Africa (6; 8.1%) and the Caribbean (1; 1.4%). The majority of responses (32; 43.2%) came from attending cardiac surgeons with more than 2-years-experience, followed by residents (23; 31.1%), early career <2-year attendings (9; 12.2%) and fellows (10; 13.5%). The majority of responses (53; 72.6%) were from integrated (5–7-year-pathway from medical school) programs and 20 (27.4%) were from traditional programs (general surgeons).

Left-handed relation with faculty

Of those having finished a traditional (prior general surgery) program, 45 (84.9%) had no exposure to left-handed general surgery faculty and 32 (68.1%) had pressure from their faculty to use the right-hand during general surgery training. During residency application, 4 (5.4%) of cardiac surgery applicants chose a specific training program based on the presence of LH faculty. Upon entering training, 57 (78.1%) had no access to LH faculty during their cardiac surgery residency. While training, 35 (47.3%) of LH residents felt the lack of guidance and desired more access to LH faculty. Whereas 25 (33.8%) of LH residents had ambidextrous abilities and felt adequately guided by right-handed faculty. Fourteen (18.9%) found right-handed faculty sufficient and helpful despite being LH. When LH faculty was present, 46.7% of LH faculty were either right-handed operators and reluctant to train LH techniques or easily irritated. The other 53.3% of LH faculty were understanding and supportive in teaching LH techniques. Thirty-nine (53.4%) of right-handed faculty were supportive and understanding to LH operators/residents. Forty (54.8%) had unsupportive/impatient/quickly-frustrated right-handed faculty. Some residents were ambidextrous (19; 26%) and converted to right-handed surgery. Overall, 47 (63.5%) of LH operating cardiac surgery residents were either obligated or advised to use their non-dominant hand during training. During surgery with right-handed faculty, 48 (64.9%) of residents had their LH technique corrected due to the surgeon’s dexterity preferences and 25 (52.1%) had their LH technique corrected >5 times per surgery. While in residency, only 4 (5.4%) of LH trainees had the opportunity to perform surgery with more than 2 LH instructors, 12 (16.2%) had exposure to 2 LH surgeons, 27 (36.5%) had scrubbed in with only 1 LH cardiac surgeon and 31 (41.9%) of residents never scrubbed in with LH faculty. Overall, 44 (60.3%) of cardiac surgery residents consider being LH as an advantage during residency, while 29 (39.7%) considered it to be a disadvantage. These perceptions are affected by ambidexterity.

Left-handed residents’ technical skill perceptions

As trainees, 35 (49.3%) considered coronary artery bypass grafting anastomosis to be the most difficult portion of LH technique, followed by valvular and annular suture placement (23; 32.4%); the other 7 (9.9%) and 6 (8.5%) were conduit harvesting and cannulation, respectively. With regard to LH internal mammary artery takedown, 25 (35.7%) considered the right internal mammary artery (RIMA) to be more challenging due to its infrequent harvesting and less practiced and not due to anatomy (more geometric for LH). When comparing individual left internal mammary artery (LIMA) versus RIMA takedowns by LH operating surgeons and trainees, LIMA takedowns had a higher difficulty level and technical demand in comparison to RIMA, 11.4% versus 5.7%, respectively. During training, 17 (23%) of OR staff responded negatively to the technical LH requests of the trainees. While in training, 16 (21.6%) of LH residents sought LH faculty at other centres for guidance and training. Forty-two (57.5%) of LH residents and surgeons consider an elective rotation or short fellowship with a LH operating cardiac surgeon necessary to learn LH techniques.

Left-handed cardiac surgeons’ dominance

Upon initiating independent practice as attending cardiac surgeons, 60 (83.3%) consider being LH an advantage in comparison to residency. However, 12 (16.7%) still consider being LH as a disadvantage in cardiac surgery and continue to struggle technically. Seventy (94.6%) of LH surgeons had no access to LH cardiac surgery instruments. Fifty (67.6%) of LH cardiac surgeons struggle to a degree with right-handed instruments. Fifty-seven (75.7%) of LH cardiac surgeons still have to remind scrub nurses of their LH dexterity, needle direction preferences and driver-mounting approaches. Of them, 26 (45.6%) have to remind scrub nurses >5 per surgery. Thirty-six (48.7%) of LH cardiac surgeons consider that extra care must be taken to avoid needle stick injuries due to their LH dominance during passing and receiving of surgical instruments.

For LH operators, 8 (14.8%) perform cardiac surgery 100% left-handed, 29 (53.7%) use their right hand for up to 5–25% of surgeries and 17 (31.5%) for up to 50%. Therefore, true LH operators were 54 (73%). Specifically for LH operators placing valve/annular sutures, 21 (40.4%) place sutures 100% left-handed, 17 (32.7%) use their right hand for 5–25% of valve/annular sutures and 14 (26.9%) for up to 50%. LH ambidextrous yet right-handed operators were 19 (26.8%) for valve suture placements. Thirty-eight (52.8%) of LH cardiac surgeons were almost exclusive LH operators, while 21 (29.2%) use their right hand due to their ambidexterity. The other 13 (18.1%) were forced into using their non-dominant hand despite not being ambidextrous. With regard to minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS), 40 (56.3%) consider being LH an advantage in handling MICS needle drivers and in manoeuvring through small working spaces for valvular/annular suture placements. Sixty-three (85.1%) consider a short LH simulation course or fellowship in LH MICS/endoscopic cardiac surgery beneficial to learn minimally invasive left-handed tricks and skills. DISCUSSION

Left-handedness is mentioned throughout history, even dating back to ancient biblical scriptures referring to the distinctiveness of LH; ‘Among Benjamin’s elite soldiers were 700 chosen men who were left-handed; every one of these could sling a stone at a hair without missing’—Judges 20:16. Today, left-handedness is still finding its place in the field of cardiac surgery. This is the 1st survey conducted specifically for LH cardiac surgeons and residents. In this survey, the majority of responses came from Europe (29; 39.2%) and Latin America (19; 25.7%). Responses showed that respondents from traditional tracks had scarce access to LH faculty since general surgery; (45; 84.9%), and (32; 68.1%) were pressured into using the right hand. This agrees with a previous surgery survey showing a 90% lack of LH faculty support [12]. In cardiac surgery training, up to 57 (78.1%) had no access to LH faculty. Also, when present, only 53.3% of LH faculty were supportive, while supportive right-hand faculty were only 39 (53.4%). Also, only 4 (5.4%) of LH residents were exposed to more than 2 LH instructors.

True LH cardiac operators were 54 (73%) with certain variability in the assistance of their right hand. This is in accordance with previous literature in that LH surgeons are considered “universal adapters” in surgery [13]. However, 38 (52.8%) of LH cardiac surgeons were almost exclusive LH operators and 21 (29.2%) prefer to use their right hand due to their ambidexterity. The other 13 (18.1%) were forced into using their non-dominant hand. As a result, there are 18.1% of LH cardiac surgeons not meeting their surgical potential due to unsupportive faculty and pressure during training. In essence, not only are there few LH cardiac surgeons, there are even fewer LH operators and therefore a smaller instructor pool. LH residents must therefore struggle and endure finding their own technique in a very solitary fashion or seek guidance and mentorship elsewhere. Right-handed cardiac surgeons and residents have many patterns, templates and examples to follow. However, LH cardiac surgeons have few resources and faculty to guide them. In fact, 41.9% of LH residents have never scrubbed in with a LH instructor. In consequence, surgical independence and confidence can take longer for left-handers in the absence of proper guidance from faculty even though there is no skill disadvantage. Some cardiothoracic surgery training programs even refuse to train left-handed residents as they are not willing to put in the effort to training LH surgeons. In residency applications and interviews, this is a common question, ‘are you left-handed’? A previous survey showed that 97% of LH surgery applicants do not mention their dexterity during interviews due to the expected negative reactions [12]. As a result, many LH surgeons and residents are forced to develop ambidextrous skills during training in fear of discrimination from the right-handed faculty. In this survey, 63.5% cardiac surgery residents were told or forced to use only their right hands at the risk of being scrubbed out of surgery. This experience has also been reported previously by other authors [13].

The myth of the RIMA being more difficult than the LIMA has also been answered here. Most LH cardiac surgeons and residents consider RIMA to be easier to takedown with the left hand, only, it is less harvested and therefore less practiced. This survey shows that standardization of LH techniques still requires objectivity with regard to the technical aspects such as which side of the operating table to operate from, which sternal opening techniques to use, conduit harvesting approaches, cannulation strategies, coronary anastomosis styles (backhand to forehand switching) and valve suture placements [1]. Coronary anastomosis continues to be the most challenging portion to learn as a LH resident (49.3%) and thus requires extra mentorship and guidance from LH faculty.

Apart from the skill development process, LH surgical instruments remain a basic frustration for LH operators. This survey showed that 94.6% LH cardiac surgeons lack access to LH instruments. Some authors found that although LH instruments lack greatly, many LH surgeons adapt successfully without them [13]. However, this conclusion relies on the ambidexterity of LH surgeons. Standard needle drivers have a rack-and-pinion system, which is designed for either right-handed or left-handed surgeons. Castroviejo needle holders, however, have a buckling-latch mechanism, which locks to the left and releases to the right. Therefore, although they have an ambidextrous universal usage, there is still directional dexterity in their lock-release system, which is designed for the right-handed. Also, MICS (endoscopic) needle drivers have a spring-hinge system either with a straight or right curved beak. This mechanism is levered by the right thumb and index finger opening the beak upward, which turns opposite if used by a left-handed surgeon (opening downwards). Therefore, although these are considered minor details and obstacles, right-hand-designed instruments still demand a level of effort and pose a degree of discomfort to left-handed surgeons. According to this survey, 67.6% of LH cardiac surgeons struggle to a degree with right-handed instruments.

LH cardiac surgeons and residents are a minority and have very specific training needs. Since cardiac surgery in itself is a very individual art and process, left-handedness adds to this narrow scope of training demand. Few methods exist to objectively measure LH dexterity in cardiac surgeons. As more digital, simulation and endoscopic data-acquiring systems become available, a more streamlined approach to LH cardiac surgical education will develop. Since general LH cardiac surgical recommendations are still very individual and generic, there are various areas for training development. These include wet labs, skills labs and endoscopic simulation training to objectively analyse LH performances. Left-handed cardiac surgical techniques should begin to be standardized to facilitate residents’ experiences and achieve surgical proficiency earlier in training. 57.5% of survey responders consider that LH surgical rotations and LH skills lab training are essential as well as LH endoscopic simulation courses (85.1%) to help develop LH dexterity and keep on advancing learning tools for the LH cardiac surgeon.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations, which include response bias since results depend on the subjectivity of the responders. These are self-reported and not observed. Additionally, most responses came from Europe and Latin America, which may represent specific continental patterns and perhaps not global trends. Number of responses may also not be representative of the entire LH cardiac surgical community. These results do however provide a view into the reality faced by LH surgeons and show similar results, patterns and trends as other left-handed studies reported in the literature.

CONCLUSION

In Left-handed cardiac surgery, there is a lack of tailored surgical exposure, training guidance, standardization, learning tools and teaching resources. Training resources for LH cardiac surgeons and residents should be developed. Left-handed skills labs, courses and short fellowships can help LH residents and young surgeons develop their LH technique.
