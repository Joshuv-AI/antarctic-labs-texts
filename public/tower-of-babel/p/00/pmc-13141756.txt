Molecular characterization, evolutionary dynamics, and pathogenic assessment of chicken infectious anemia virus isolates circulating in Egyptian poultry
Authors: YA Soliman, M S El-Nagar Eman, AN Gamal Maha, MS Khattab, M Salem Heba, Mohamed G Seadawy, Doha Abd Alrahman Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a result of its severe immunosuppressive nature, CIAV persists as a critical pathogen causing substantial economic concerns in global poultry production. The emergence of genetic variants and reports of suspected vaccine sub-optimal protection in Egyptian broiler flocks necessitates an urgent investigation of currently circulating strains. In this study, 165 samples were collected from six Egyptian governorates (2024–2025). PCR screening revealed 38 positive cases, and six isolates were selected for full-length sequencing of the VP1 and VP2 genes. Molecular analysis identified the isolates as Genotype II, showing significant genetic drift from Genotype I vaccinal strains. Key nonsynonymous mutations, notably H22Q in VP1, suggested potential antigenic escape, while the VP2 gene maintained 100% amino acid identity despite multiple silent mutations. Experimentally, the Genotype II isolate exhibited extreme virulence in SPF chickens; infected birds suffered from severe growth retardation (p < 0.0001) and profound lymphoid organ atrophy, with thymus weights dropping to 1.1 ± 0.07 g. Severe aplastic anemia was confirmed by a dramatic decline in PCV% (19.33 ± 1.45%) and pale, gelatinous bone marrow. Viral load quantification revealed massive replication in the thymus (9.5 Log₁₀) and bone marrow (9 Log₁₀), leading to a total collapse of humoral immunity, with antibody levels falling below the positivity threshold by the 4th week and reaching undetectable levels (S/P 0) by the 5th week post-infection. Histopathology corroborated these findings, showing systemic lymphocyte depletion and characteristic intranuclear inclusion bodies. These findings confirm the emergence of highly pathogenic Genotype II CIAV in Egypt. The ability of these variants to bypass existing immunity highlights the critical need for updating current vaccination strategies and implementing more robust biosecurity measures.Keywords: CIAV, Genotype II, VP1 gene, Egypt, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Chicken Infectious Anemia Virus (CIAV) is recognized as one of the most economically disruptions agents in the global poultry industry. Its critical importance arises from its role as a potent immunosuppressive pathogen that acts as a "silent destroyer," predisposing flocks to a myriad of secondary infections and leading to catastrophic vaccination failures (Schat and Vicky, 2020). CIAV is a member of non-enveloped viruses with icosahedral symmetry in the genus Gyrovirus belonging to Anelloviridae family. Its genome is a single-stranded closed circular DNA, of about 2.3 kb which contains three major overlap open reading frames (ORFs) encoding for VP1, VP2 and VP3 proteins (Siddell et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024).

The molecular pathogenicity is orchestrated by these proteins; VP1, is essential for viral replication, transmission, and virulence since host-neutralizing antibodies primarily target it. During viral assembly, the non-structural protein VP2 supports the correct structure of VP1 by acting as a scaffolding protein and dual-specific protein phosphatase (Aziz et al., 2025; Peters et al., 2002). The main virulence factor that causes p53-independent apoptosis in chicken thymocytes, T lymphoblastoid cells, and myeloid cells is VP3, or apoptin (Shulman and Davidson, 2017; Schat and Vicky, 2020). Being non-enveloped, CIAV is remarkably resistant to common disinfectants and environmental stressors, necessitating the use of potent, specialized disinfection protocols (Karki et al., 2025).

Aplastic anemia, hemorrhagic syndrome (Blue Wing Disease), bone marrow shrinkage, and thymic atrophy are among the severe pathological alterations caused by CIAV, which mainly affects chicks between two and four weeks of age (Yan et al., 2024). The invasion of T lymphoblasts in the thymus and hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow is its most significant effect, leading to morbidity and mortality rates in vulnerable young chicks that can reach as high as 55% and 80%, respectively (Alispahic et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024). Although all ages of chickens are at risk, young birds are more likely to develop clinical illness. Older birds, on the other hand, usually develop subclinical infections with few clinical symptoms, yet they act as risky virus reservoirs that enable the pathogen's continuous circulation (Orakpoghenor, 2019). A key epidemiological challenge is that CIAV is transmitted both horizontally and vertically. Vertical transmission from infected breeder hens via the egg is particularly devastating, as it ensures chicks are infected from day one. Therefore, the immunization of breeder flocks is of paramount importance to ensure the transmission of maternal antibodies to offspring (Karki et al., 2025). Historically, CIAV strains circulating in Egyptian poultry flocks were predominantly classified as Genotype I. For instance, Hussein et al. (2016) demonstrated that field isolates from multiple Egyptian provinces exhibited high genetic identity with the Cux-1 vaccine strain, clustering firmly within Genotype I. Further characterization by Abdel-Mawgod et al. (2018) of full-genome sequences of Egyptian field isolates also confirmed their position within the Genotype I lineage, despite harboring specific pathogenicity-linked mutations. However, recent epidemiological surveillance indicates a significant evolutionary transition; Elsamadony et al. (2025) recently documented the emergence and increasing prevalence of Genotype II variants in Egypt. This shift is of paramount concern as the widely used Cux-1 vaccine (Genotype I) may no longer provide sufficient cross-protection. The accumulation of nonsynonymous mutations in the hypervariable regions of the VP1 capsid protein a process of genetic drift likely enables these emerging Genotype II strains to bypass vaccine-induced immunity, leading to the clinical breakthroughs currently witnessed in the field. Our findings offer vital insights into the molecular changes and pathogenic potential of circulating strains, highlighting the necessity for continuous and systematic surveillance to guide future vaccination and control measures.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The current investigation was carried out in accordance with the rules governing the use and care of laboratory animals. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of [Faculty of veterinary medicine, Assiut University] authorized all experimental protocols under the following approval number: 06/2025/0397. To minimize animal distress, all birds were humanely anesthetized using an appropriate dose of Ketamine/Xylazine combination (25/5 mg/kg BW) before any invasive handling or sampling. At the conclusion of the study, using cervical dislocation, birds were humanely put to death following deep general anesthesia, ensuring strict adherence to international animal welfare standards.

Field samples and clinical history

A total of 165 clinical samples (bursa, thymus, spleen, and liver) were collected between January 2024 and May 2025 from various poultry sectors, including broilers, commercial layers, and breeder flocks, across six Egyptian governorates (Table 1). The age of the sampled birds ranged from two to 27 weeks. Regarding the vaccination history, the breeder and commercial layer flocks were immunized with a single dose of live attenuated CIAV vaccine (Cux-1 strain) via drinking water between 13 and 15 weeks of age to ensure maternal antibody transfer, while the broiler flocks remained unvaccinated, the sampled flocks exhibited clinical signs of stunted growth, generalized weakness, and pale combs, with mortality rates ranging from 3% to 10%. Necropsy findings revealed pathognomonic lesions, including marked atrophy of the bursa of Fabricius and thymus, watery pale bone marrow, and petechial hemorrhages in the proventriculus and skeletal muscles.

Molecular detection and screening of CIAV by qPCR

From all collected field samples (5 mg per sample), total viral DNA was extracted using the FAVORGEN Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Cat# FAVNK002) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were homogenized using a mechanical homogenizer (PRO 200, Pro Scientific, USA) at 4°C. 50 µL of elution buffer were used to elute the purified DNA and kept at −20°C until needed. The presence of CIAV was determined using a TaqMan-based qPCR assay as described by Wu et al. (2022) to identify positive cases for subsequent isolation. The reaction utilized specific primers: CAV1F (5′-CTC AAG CGA CTT CGA CGA AGA-3′) and CAV1R (5′-AGC CTC ACA CTA TAC GTA CCG-3′), alongside a specific TaqMan probe: CAV-probe (5′-FAM-ACA GCG GTA TCG TAG ACG AG-TAMRA-3′). The 20 µL reaction mixture consisted of 10 µL of 2x QuantiNova Probe Master Mix (Qiagen, Germany), 0.5 µL of each primer, 0.25 µL of the specific probe, and 5 µL of purified DNA template. The thermal profile comprised an initial denaturation at 95°C for two minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 seconds, and combined annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 seconds. The assay was carried out using a real-time thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). Samples exhibiting the lowest Cycle Threshold (Ct) values were selected for virus isolation and subsequent genetic characterization.

Absolute viral DNA quantification

To track viral load dynamics and tissue tropism during the pathogenicity study, absolute quantification of CIAV DNA was performed in the liver and lymphoid organs (thymus, bone marrow, and spleen) as described by Su et al. (2025). Total DNA was extracted from 5 mg of each tissue sample, and the assay was conducted using the AriaMx qPCR System (Agilent Technologies, USA). Absolute quantification was achieved using a standard curve generated from ten-fold serial dilutions of a certified CIAV vaccine with a known genomic concentration. The standard curve demonstrated high linearity (R2 = 0.994) and a slope of −3.077, with a calculated amplification efficiency of 111.33% and a y-intercept of 12.81. All experimental samples were run in triplicate, and the viral loads were expressed as log10 DNA copies per µL of template DNA.

VP1 and VP2 full-length gene amplification

For the selected samples showing high viral titers, conventional PCR was conducted to amplify the full-length coding sequences of the VP1 (∼1300 bp) and VP2 (∼650 bp) genes (Table 2). EasyTaq® DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Cat# AS111) was used to conduct a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in a total volume of 25 µL. 2.5 µL of 10x EasyTaq® Buffer, 2 µL of dNTPs (2.5 mM each), 0.5 µL of forward and reverse primers (10 µM each), and 0.25 µL of EasyTaq® DNA Polymerase made up each reaction mixture. Lastly, nuclease-free water was used to adjust the volume after adding 2 µL of the isolated DNA template. Dhama et al. (2008) guided the optimization of the thermal cycling conditions, which included an initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 minutes, 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 seconds, specific annealing temperatures (60°C for VP1 and 63°C for VP2) for 30 seconds, extension at 72°C (1.5 min for VP1 and 1 min for VP2), and a final extension at 72°C for 10 minutes. To verify the anticipated amplicon sizes, the PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide.

Purification, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis

The full-length VP1 and VP2 PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1% low-melting-point agarose gel. Using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 28704), the precise target bands were removed and purified in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations. After being eluted in 30 µL of elution buffer, the purified amplicons were kept at −20°C until they were sequenced. The ABI PRISM 3730XL Analyzer and BigDyeTM Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kits with AmpliTaq DNA polymerase were used in a MJ Research PTC-225 Peltier Thermal Cycler to carry out sequencing procedures (Applied Biosystems, USA). Bi-directional sequencing was conducted for six representative isolates (one from each governorate) using the same primers employed in the initial PCR amplification to ensure maximum accuracy and sequence coverage. To verify their identity, the resultant nucleotide sequences were examined using the National Center for Biotechnology Information's (NCBI) Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The Lasergene sequence analysis program (DNAStar, version 10; DNAStar, Inc.) and BioEdit version 7.2 (Hall, 1999) were used to compile and modify the raw sequence data for bioinformatics analysis. The Clustal W module was used to align nucleotide and amino acid sequences. MEGA X software was used to create phylogenetic trees using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method and the Tamura-Nei substitution model. A bootstrap approach with 1000 replicates was used to evaluate the evolutionary link and dependability between the current Egyptian isolates and different global/vaccinal strains. Following submission of the completed sequences to the NCBI GenBank database, accession numbers were assigned for the full-length VP1 gene (PX704673 to PX704678) and the VP2 gene (PX704679 to PX704684), which are compiled in (Table 1(.

In vivo pathogenicity assessment

The Al-Qalyubia isolate (Accession No. PX704675 for VP1 and PX704681 for VP2) was selected for the in vivo pathogenicity trial. This strain was chosen as a representative of Genotype II, exhibiting high genetic similarity (over 99.2%) to the other sequenced field isolates in this study, and was further characterized by its consistent molecular profile and high viral load. 120 SPF chicks) Kom Osheem SPF project, Fayoum, Egypt(that were one day old were split into two equal groups at random (n = 60 each For the pathogenicity trial, the challenge inoculum was prepared as a 10% (w/v) tissue homogenate from the thymus and liver of naturally infected chicks. Due to the fastidious nature of CIAV in cell culture, the viral load in the clarified homogenate was determined using absolute quantification via qPCR) following the standardized protocol and curve parameters described in Section Absolute Viral DNA Quantification. To achieve a standardized challenge dose, the clarified tissue homogenates were serially diluted and quantified via qPCR to achieve a final inoculum dose of 4 × 10³ viral DNA copies per 0.1 ml. The infected group received an intramuscular (I/M) inoculation of 0.1 mL of this suspension per bird, while the control group was injected with an equal volume of sterile saline. For 35 days, birds were observed every day for death and clinical symptoms. The pathogenicity was comprehensively evaluated by calculating the relative organ-to-body weight ratios of the spleen, bursa of Fabricius, and thymus at weekly intervals to quantify lymphoid atrophy (AbdAlla et al., 2022). Tissue samples including thymus, bone marrow, spleen, and liver were collected at 3, 7, 14, and 21 dpi for viral load tracking. The absolute quantification of CIAV DNA in these tissues was performed via qPCR as described in section Absolute Viral DNA Quantification. All viral loads were calculated as log10 DNA copies per mg of tissue to ensure consistency across all experimental time points.

Furthermore, Serum samples were tested for anti-CIAV antibodies using the BioChek CIAV Antibody test kit (BioChek B.V., Reeuwijk, Netherlands) following the manufacturer’s instructions (Shao et al., 2023). The impact on the hematopoietic system was assessed through comprehensive hematological profiling, focusing on Packed Cell Volume (PCV) and Hemoglobin (Hb) levels to confirm the induction of anemia (Chandrashekaraiah et al., 2020). This was supplemented by a detailed histopathological evaluation of the bone marrow and thymus to detect microscopic alterations and lymphoid depletion (Schat and Vicky, 2020). For this purpose, collected tissue specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated in a graded series of alcohols, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Subsequently, sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) for standardized microscopic examination.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was confirmed by Levene’s test. For data involving multiple time points, a Two-way ANOVA was conducted using Group and Time as the main factors, followed by Sidak’s or Tukey’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons between groups. Results are presented as mean ± SEM, with statistical significance established at p < 0.05; with higher levels of significance indicated as p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001 (Motulsky, 2014).

Results

Clinical findings and gross pathological lesions

The investigated poultry flocks exhibited a clinical picture highly suggestive of Chicken Infectious Anemia (CIA), with a clear distinction between age groups. Young broiler flocks (2–3 weeks old) displayed acute clinical signs, including severe depression, ruffled feathers, and stunted growth. A hallmark observation was the striking pallor of the combs, wattles, and skin, reflecting a state of severe systemic anemia. In these flocks, morbidity was high, while mortality rates ranged from 3% to 10%. Conversely, in older breeder and layer flocks (22–27 weeks old), the infection was primarily subclinical, presenting no overt symptoms but serving as a source for vertical transmission. Upon necropsy, the most pathognomonic lesions were centered on the lymphoid and hematopoietic systems. All examined carcasses showed marked atrophy of the thymus (often reduced to small, translucent remnants) and involution of the Bursa of Fabricius. The bone marrow in the femur and tibia was consistently pale, watery, or yellowish-white, indicating the replacement of hematopoietic tissue with fat. Furthermore, systemic hemorrhages were a frequent finding, ranging from petechiae on the proventricular mucosa to extensive ecchymotic hemorrhages in the skeletal muscles and subcutaneous tissue of the wings, the latter being characteristic of "Blue Wing Disease." Visceral organs, particularly the liver and kidneys, were moderately enlarged and pale in the majority of acute cases.

Molecular detection and gene amplification

Out of the 165 examined tissue samples, 38 (23.03%) that were analyzed had positive results for the Chicken Infectious Anemia Virus (CIAV) DNA using the TaqMan-based qPCR assay. Samples exhibiting high viral loads, characterized by low Cycle Threshold (Ct) values (<25), were prioritized for further genetic characterization. The full-length coding portions of the VP1 and VP2 genes were amplified using conventional PCR on six representative isolates, one from each sampled governorate. The PCR reaction yielded distinct and specific amplicons at approximately 1300 bp for the VP1 gene and 650 bp for the VP2 gene, as visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure S1, Supplementary Material). In the negative control, there was no amplification., confirming the high specificity of the designed primers and the success of the optimized thermal cycling protocol. These six positive amplicons were subsequently purified and processed for bi-directional sequencing to investigate their molecular characteristics.

Sequence and phylogenetic analysis of VP1 and VP2 genes

The full-length sequences of the VP1 (∼1300 bp) and VP2 (∼650 bp) genes were successfully obtained for six representative Egyptian isolates (designated as Al-Qalyubia, Menoufia, Damietta, Mansoura, Arish, and PortSaid). The nucleotide sequences obtained in this study have been deposited in the GenBank database under the following accession numbers: PX704673 to PX704678 for the VP1 gene, and PX704679 to PX704684 for the VP2 gene. Phylogenetic analysis relied on the complete coding sequence of the VP1 gene revealed that all six Egyptian field isolates from the 2024–2025 period clustered firmly within Genotype II. As illustrated in Fig. 1, these isolates (marked with red circles) formed a distinct monophyletic group that is genetically distant from the Genotype I cluster, which encompasses the widely used vaccinal strains, including Cux-1 (MN079036.1), Nobilis P4 (MN079038.1), and Del-Ros (MN079037.1). The Egyptian isolates shared high genetic similarity with previous regional strains (e.g., MW286467) and international Genotype II isolates from Israel and Bangladesh. Similarly, the phylogenetic tree constructed using the VP2 gene sequences (Fig. 2) corroborated the VP1 findings. All current isolates (marked with red triangles) were positioned within Genotype II, clustering alongside field variants from China and South Korea. The consistency between both VP1 and VP2 phylogenetic topologies confirms that the circulating CIAV in the sampled Egyptian governorates belongs to a non-vaccine-like lineage (Genotype II). The genetic divergence observed between the Egyptian field isolates and the vaccinal strains (Genotype I) provides a molecular explanation for the continued clinical outbreaks and vaccination breakthroughs reported in the field. Amino acid sequence analysis further identified specific substitutions in the hypervariable regions of VP1, which are known to influence viral antigenicity and neutralizing antibody binding.

Nucleotide and amino acid identity and variation analysis

VP1 gene analysis

The pairwise sequence identity matrix for the full-length VP1 gene revealed that the nucleotide identity between the current Egyptian field isolates and the classical Genotype I vaccinal strains (Del-Ros, Cux-1, and Nobilis P4) ranged from 94% to 96%. Interestingly, the amino acid identity was slightly higher, ranging from 95% to 97%. This discrepancy between nucleotide and amino acid identity indicates the presence of both silent (synonymous) and nonsynonymous mutations. The majority of the observed variations were silent mutations, which do not alter the protein sequence but contribute to the genetic drift of the virus. However, the 3%–5% divergence at the amino acid level suggests the occurrence of nonsynonymous mutations within the hypervariable regions of the VP1 capsid protein. These substitutions are critical as they may alter the viral antigenicity and potentially lead to vaccine escape (Table 3).

VP2 gene analysis

Regarding the VP2 gene, the nucleotide identity between the field isolates and the vaccinal lineages was significantly lower, dropping to approximately 64%. Surprisingly, the amino acid identity remained 100% among all field isolates and showed a very high conservation with reference strains. This high amino acid conservation despite the lower nucleotide identity (approx. 36% divergence) emphasizes a high frequency of silent mutations within the VP2 gene. This suggests that while the nucleotide sequence has evolved significantly, the functional integrity of the VP2 protein is strictly maintained due to its essential role as a scaffolding protein and protein phosphatase during viral replication (Table 4).

Analysis of nucleotide and amino acid substitutions

VP1 protein analysis

Multiple sequence alignment of the full-length VP1 protein revealed significant genetic variation between the 2024–2025 Egyptian field isolates and the classical Genotype I vaccinal strains (Cux-1, Nobilis P4, and Del-Ros). A total of 20 to 25 nucleotide substitutions were identified across the VP1 gene compared to the Cux-1 strain. Approximately 30% of these were nonsynonymous mutations, resulting in key amino acid changes concentrated within the hypervariable regions. Significant amino acid replacements were observed at several positions, including H22Q (in most field isolates) and substitutions in the aa 139–151 and aa 370–420 domains. Specifically, the substitution of Histidine (H) by Glutamine (Q) at position 22 was consistent across the Genotype II isolates. These nonsynonymous mutations are situated in regions associated with viral replication efficiency and capsid assembly, suggesting an antigenic drift that may compromise the neutralizing efficacy of antibodies elicited by Genotype I vaccines (Figure S2(a, b, c), Supplementary Material).

VP2 protein analysis

In contrast, the VP2 protein exhibited a high degree of evolutionary conservation at the proteomic level despite substantial nucleotide divergence. While the nucleotide identity between field isolates and vaccinal strains dropped to approximately 64%, the amino acid identity remained 100% among all Egyptian isolates. This indicates that nearly all nucleotide substitutions in the VP2 gene were silent (synonymous) mutations, which do not alter the primary structure of the protein. The strict preservation of the amino acid sequence across the VP2 protein (216 aa) highlights its critical functional role as a dual-specificity protein phosphatase and a scaffolding protein, where any structural change might be detrimental to viral viability (Figure S3 (a, b), Supplementary Material).

Pathogenicity, hematological, and immunological analysis

Experimental infection of SPF chickens with the isolated Genotype II CIAV strain induced severe growth retardation and profound immunopathology. The isolate demonstrated high virulence, resulting in a 25% cumulative mortality rate (15/60) by the end of the experiment, with the majority of deaths occurring between 14 and 24 days post-infection (dpi). A significant reduction in total body weight was observed starting from 7 days post-infection (dpi), reaching 1800 ± 34.36 g compared to 2500 ± 34.36 g in the control group by 35 dpi (p < 0.0001). This growth depression was accompanied by marked atrophy of primary and secondary lymphoid organs; specifically, the bursa of Fabricius and thymus weights significantly declined to 1.1 ± 0.069 g and 0.50 ± 0.17 g, respectively, at the peak of infection) Fig. 3, Table 5). These pathological changes were further corroborated by severe hematological deviation (Fig. 4, Table 6). The infected birds developed clinical anemia, evidenced by a significant drop in Packed Cell Volume (PCV%) to 19.33 ± 1.45% (p < 0.0004), alongside a marked reduction in RBC counts and hemoglobin levels. Furthermore, profound leukopenia and lymphopenia (51.67 ± 1.95%; p = 0.0001) were recorded, highlighting the virus's lymphocytotropic nature and its devastating impact on the host's immune competence. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the high virulence of the circulating Genotype II field variants and their ability to induce severe systemic disease. The humoral immune response, as assessed by ELISA, demonstrated a distinctive pattern of initial seroconversion followed by profound suppression in the infected group. While the control group remained consistently seronegative (S/P < 0.6) throughout the 35-day period, the infected birds exhibited a significant rise in antibody titers at 2 weeks post-infection (1.50 ± 0.17; P < 0.001). However, this was followed by a sharp and progressive decline, with titers dropping below the positive detection threshold by the 4th week (0.05 ± 0.01) and reaching undetectable levels by the 5th week. This rapid collapse in antibody production reflects the high pathogenicity of the 2024–2025 Egyptian Genotype II isolates, suggesting that the viral-induced destruction of lymphoid tissues compromised the host's ability to sustain a humoral response, thereby explaining the vaccination breakthroughs observed under field conditions (Fig. 5, Table 7(.

Gross pathological examination

The clinical manifestations and high viral loads were consistent with the severe gross lesions observed during post-mortem examination. From 7 dpi, infected birds exhibited multi-organ involvement, characterized by hepatomegaly with subcapsular hemorrhages, nephritis with ureates in the ureters, and splenomegaly. Systemic hemorrhages were prominent, particularly in the proventriculus and thigh muscles. The hallmark of these Genotype II isolates was the progressive impact on lymphoid and hematopoietic tissues. The thymus exhibited marked atrophy accompanied by congestion, peaking between 14 and 21 dpi. Similarly, the femoral bone marrow appeared pale and gelatinous, indicative of severe aplastic anemia. While initial signs of fibroblastic proliferation were noted at the later stages of the experiment (28–35 dpi), the hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues did not achieve full functional recovery, confirming the profound lymphocytotropic and erythrotropic impact of the circulating Egyptian isolates (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10(.

Histopathological findings

Microscopic examination of the bursa of Fabricius in the infected group at 3 dpi revealed variably sized follicles with widened interfollicular septa and minimal leukocyte infiltration. By 7 dpi, the bursa displayed progressive interstitial fibrosis and atrophy of lymphoid follicles. The damage reached its peak by 28 dpi, characterized by severe diffuse lymphoid depletion and marked interfollicular heterophil infiltration. This peak in histological severity followed the high viral replication phase observed at 14 dpi, suggesting that tissue remodeling and inflammatory responses continue to progress even after the initial viral surge. Regarding the thymus, the infected group showed moderate lymphocytolysis, interfollicular edema, and heterophil infiltration as early as 3 dpi. By 7 dpi, mild diffuse lymphoid depletion was noted, which progressed into severe lymphocytolysis and lymphoid depletion by 21 and 28 dpi. Crucially, the onset of severe thymic lymphoid depletion at 21 dpi coincided with the decline in viral load from its 14 dpi peak, indicating that immune tissue collapse is a downstream consequence of intense earlier viral replication. Persistent interfollicular heterophil infiltration remained a consistent feature in the late stages of infection, while the control group showed no detectable lesions (Fig. 11). The bone marrow of infected chickens at 3 dpi exhibited moderate depletion of hematopoietic cells and erythrocytes, which were replaced by loose connective tissues. Large atypical cells containing eosinophilic nuclear inclusion bodies characteristic of CIAV were observed in the extravascular spaces. Although the depletion was marked at 7 and 21 dpi, mild focal fibrosis was observed by 28 dpi, suggesting a chronic state of marrow aplasia consistent with the prolonged presence of the virus in hematopoietic tissues. In the spleen, the infected group showed severe hyperplasia of reticular cells accompanied by progressive depletion of the white pulp, starting from 3 dpi. The severity of white pulp depletion increased over time, reflecting the systemic lymphocytotropic impact of the virus, whereas the control group remained histologically normal. Microscopic analysis of the liver in infected chickens at 3 dpi demonstrated moderate vacuolar degeneration and hepatocyte apoptosis, along with sinusoidal dilation. By 7 dpi, small foci of leukocyte infiltration and Kupffer cell hyperplasia were prominent. By 21 and 28 dpi, the lesions persisted as mild periportal mononuclear leukocyte infiltrations and diffuse hepatocyte degeneration. The persistence of these hepatic alterations aligns with the sustained viral detection in systemic organs throughout the experimental period. The control group showed normal hepatic cords and intact architecture throughout the study (Fig. 12).

Discussion

Chicken Infectious Anemia (CIA) remains one of the most critical immunosuppressive diseases in the global poultry industry, leading to significant economic losses and vaccination failures (Karki et al., 2025; Pohuang et al., 2025). The present study addressed this challenge by collecting 165 field samples from broiler flocks across six Egyptian governorates, resulting in 38 PCR-positive cases (23%). This high prevalence in vaccinated flocks aligns with Orakpoghenor (2019) and confirms that CIAV is enzootic in Egypt, capable of bypassing current biosecurity and vaccination protocols (No et al., 2021; Bhatt et al., 2013). Given the time-consuming nature of virus isolation on embryonated eggs or specialized cell lines like MDCC-MSB1 (Mohandas, 2019; Zeng et al., 2023), qPCR and full-length sequencing of the VP1 and VP2 genes proved to be the most reliable tools for characterization.

Our phylogenetic analysis revealed that these isolates cluster within Genotype II, exhibiting high genetic homogeneity (98.5%–99.8% identity) but significant divergence from classical Genotype I vaccinal strains (88.5%–90.2% identity). This finding is in harmony with Song et al. (2024) and Elsamadony et al. (2025), but contrasts with earlier Egyptian reports by Aziz et al. (2025), who identified Genotype I as the primary circulating strain. This discrepancy suggests a rapid evolutionary transition in the field, possibly driven by prolonged vaccination pressure using Genotype I-based vaccines, which may favor the selection of Genotype II variants.

Molecular analysis identified the H22Q mutation in VP1, a key substitution in the hypervariable region associated with neutralizing epitopes. As noted by Xu et al. (2023), mutations at such critical positions can alter antibody binding affinity, facilitating antigenic drift and vaccine escape. Interestingly, while our isolates showed high variability in VP1, the VP2 protein exhibited strict amino acid conservation (up to 100%). This reflects a strong purifying selection on VP2, likely due to its indispensable dual role as a protein phosphatase and a molecular scaffold required for the correct assembly of the VP1 capsid. This high conservation suggests that VP2 could be a viable target for developing pan-genotype CIAV vaccines.

Experimentally, our Genotype II isolate exhibited extreme virulence in SPF chickens, inducing severe clinical signs and pathognomonic pale, yellowish, and gelatinous bone marrow. This gross lesion was quantitatively confirmed by a dramatic drop in Packed Cell Volume (PCV%) to 19.33 ± 1.45%, falling significantly below the clinical anemia threshold of 20% (Govindhasamy, 2022). This hematopoietic failure directly correlates with the high viral loads detected via qPCR in the bone marrow (9.00 ± 0.25 log10) and thymus (9.50 ± 0.30 log10), where the virus targets and destroys erythroid and myeloid progenitor cells (Mostafa et al., 2021).

Our findings regarding peak mortality at 5 dpi are consistent with Alispahic et al. (2024), underscoring the acute nature of this genotype. Furthermore, the infection caused significant body weight reduction and profound lymphoid organ atrophy, culminating in undetectable ELISA antibody titers by the 4th week. This total humoral collapse aligning with Pohuang et al. (2025), Mostafa et al. (2021), and Elsamadony et al. (2025) confirms that these emerging Egyptian Genotype II variants induce a persistent "immunological void" that leaves the host highly vulnerable to secondary infections.

Finally, the histopathological findings characterized by systemic lymphoid depletion and pathognomonic intranuclear inclusion bodies corroborated the irreversible damage caused by these emerging Egyptian Genotype II variants (Rimondi et al., 2009; Nighil Rosh, 2015). The observed synchrony between peak viral loads and the subsequent progression of tissue aplasia further underscores the high virulence of these strains. These results highlight the urgent need for developing genotype-matched vaccines based on prevalent local strains to mitigate the devastating impact of CIAV on the Egyptian poultry sector and to overcome the potential vaccine escape associated with current Genotype I-based vaccination programs.

The continuous use of live attenuated vaccines in the Egyptian poultry sector may exert a selective pressure that facilitates the emergence of Genotype II strains, which appear to possess superior fitness and environmental stability compared to the previously dominant Genotype I.

Conclusion

This study highlights the emergence and dominance of highly pathogenic Genotype II CIAV variants in Egypt (2024–2025). Our findings demonstrate that these variants have undergone significant antigenic drift, enabling them to bypass immunity in vaccinated broiler flocks. The experimental infection in SPF chickens confirms their extreme virulence, characterized by severe aplastic anemia (PCV < 20%) and a total collapse of humoral immunity. Given the massive viral replication in the bone marrow and thymus, these isolates pose a continuous threat to poultry health and vaccine efficacy. Consequently, we recommend an urgent update of national vaccination strategies to include genotype-matched, local strains. Furthermore, continuous molecular surveillance is essential to monitor the viral evolution and mitigate the economic impact on the Egyptian poultry industry.
