Comprehensive proteomic and pathological profiling identifies PRAS40 as a novel biomarker and mediator of primary immune checkpoint blockade resistance in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Ning Mao, Cui He, Jia Kuang, Chunguang Wang, Zhao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has revolutionized the treatment landscape of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet primary resistance remains a significant clinical challenge. Recent evidence implicates PRAS40 (AKT1S1) in regulating cellular survival and immune responses, but its role in immunotherapy resistance is not fully understood.MethodsTranscriptomic data from TCGA and GTEx cohorts were analyzed to assess PRAS40 expression. Prognostic value was evaluated using Cox regression. Immune microenvironment features were characterized with CIBERSORT and TIMER. Predictive efficacy for ICB response was examined using TIDE and IPS. Plasma PRAS40 levels in 66 NSCLC patients receiving ICB were quantified by proximity extension assay (PEA), and multiplex immunohistochemistry assessed associations among PRAS40, PD-L1, and CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues.ResultsHigh PRAS40 expression was associated with poor prognosis, reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration, and downregulation of immune checkpoint genes. Elevated circulating PRAS40 predicted primary ICB resistance and shorter progression-free survival, independent of PD-L1 or CD8+ T cell status.ConclusionPRAS40 is strongly associated with primary ICB resistance in NSCLC and may serve as a novel predictive biomarker. These findings support its potential to guide personalized immunotherapy in lung cancer.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04166-1.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most prevalent and deadly malignancies worldwide, posing a significant global health burden [1, 2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for most cases and represents the predominant subtype [3–5]. With advances in medical technology, NSCLC treatment strategies have undergone substantial innovations, among which immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has emerged as a promising immunotherapeutic approach [3, 6, 7]. The mechanisms of ICB resistance remain complex and involve multiple factors, including T-cell exhaustion, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and heterogeneous Programmed Death Ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression [8–10]. Moreover, some patients exhibit primary resistance to ICB therapy [8]. Current biomarkers, including PD-L1 and tumor mutational burden (TMB), are limited by tissue dependency, temporal variability, and spatial heterogeneity. These limitations reduce their accuracy in predicting therapeutic resistance in NSCLC patients [11]. The clinical challenges underscore an urgent need for the development of novel biomarkers.

AKT1S1, also referred to as PRAS40, is a key regulatory protein involved in the modulation of cellular apoptosis and survival pathways [12]. As a central regulatory component of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, PRAS40 contributes to the dynamic balance of cell growth and proliferation by directly inhibiting mTORC1 activity [13]. In recent years, its abnormal expression and functional heterogeneity of PRAS40 in tumors have received increasing attention. In hepatocellular carcinoma, PGK1 was reported to enhance tumor cell proliferation by phosphorylating PRAS40, thereby suppressing autophagy-dependent cell death [14]. In colorectal cancer, activation of RSK and PRAS40 has been implicated in TIFA-mediated tumor progression [15]. These findings suggest that PRAS40 functions not only as a key driver of tumorigenesis but also as a potential therapeutic target. PRAS40 has been implicated in both tumorigenesis and therapeutic resistance in lung cancer, with recent evidence linking its expression to reduced efficacy of ICB [15–18]. Therefore, elucidating the role of PRAS40 in NSCLC is of substantial clinical importance, particularly in predicting treatment response and optimizing therapeutic strategies.

This study integrates bioinformatics and experimental approaches to investigate the significance of PRAS40 in NSCLC. Transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) were analyzed to evaluate the association between PRAS40 expression and patient prognosis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated its potential as an independent prognostic marker.

Immune infiltration within the TME was assessed using CIBERSORT and TIMER, while Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) and Immunophenoscore (IPS) algorithms were employed to predict the impact of PRAS40 on ICB response. In addition, plasma samples were analyzed using Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) to explore the potential of PRAS40 as a circulating biomarker.

Overall, this study aims to clarify the relationship between high PRAS40 expression and primary resistance to ICB in NSCLC, providing a potential biomarker to guide personalized immunotherapy and improve clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of gene expression profiles from TCGA and GTEx databases

Gene expression data were obtained from TCGA (https://cancergenome.nih.gov/) and the UCSC XENA database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), specifically including datasets for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). The collected datasets contained mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) profiles, proteomic data, and corresponding clinical information.

The mRNA-seq data included 396 normal lung tissue samples and 1,016 NSCLC tumor samples (515 LUAD and 501 LUSC cases). Proteomic data covered 365 LUAD and 328 LUSC cases, totaling 693 NSCLC tumor samples.

During preprocessing, clinical cases with missing survival information, defined as survival status recorded as 0 or NA, were excluded. A total of 970 NSCLC cases were retained for downstream survival analysis. To establish a normal control group, we downloaded 288 normal lung tissue RNA-seq profiles from the GTEx database via the UCSC XENA platform.

After merging the TCGA and GTEx datasets, intersecting genes were retained, and normalization was applied. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified employing the R package limma, with thresholds set at |log2 fold change|> 2 and p < 0.05. Additionally, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) pathways were implemented utilizing the clusterProfiler package (version 4.6.2) in R.

Cox regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on a dataset of 693 tumor samples, including 365 LUAD and 328 LUSC cases, obtained from TCGA to identify genes associated with prognosis in NSCLC. Candidate genes were subsequently entered into a LASSO-Cox regression model to construct a prognostic risk signature. To optimize the regularization parameter (λ), tenfold cross-validation was applied, and the optimal λ = 0.045 was selected by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Only genes with non-zero coefficients were retained for the final model, reflecting its sparsity. LASSO regression applied L1 regularization, where λ determined model complexity. Larger values of λ increased penalization of variable coefficients and forced some coefficients to shrink to zero. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and survival analysis

The TCGA proteomic dataset was randomly divided into training and testing cohorts using the createDataPartition function in R. ROC curves were prepared with the pROC package to evaluate the predictive performance of the selected gene expression profiles in distinguishing disease status. A risk score (RS) was computed for each individual by summing the weighted expression values of ten selected genes and their corresponding LASSO coefficients. Survival outcomes were then analyzed utilizing Kaplan–Meier (K–M) estimations with the survfit function from the survival package. Statistical differences in overall survival between high- and low-risk groups were evaluated with the log-rank test, which revealed significant differences in survivalrobabilities between the two cohorts.

Construction of prognostic signature and nomogram development

To evaluate the prognostic performance of candidate genes identified via the LASSO algorithm, K–M survival analysis and ROC curve assessments were conducted. Genes demonstrating strong associations with both prognosis and diagnostic value were incorporated into the construction of the RS-based prognostic model. Patients were classified into high- or low-risk groups in light of the optimal RS cutoff. K–M and ROC analyses were implemented to test the prognostic and diagnostic potential of the RS.

To test whether the RS serves as an independent prognostic indicator, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were applied, with adjustments made for relevant clinical covariates. Independent predictors were then integrated into a nomogram. The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was assessed by calculating the concordance index (C-index), which measured agreement between predicted and observed survival outcomes. Calibration curves were generated to further illustrate the degree of concordance.

Immune infiltration

Immune cell infiltration was quantified using the CIBERSORT method, which deconvoluted gene expression data to estimate the relative proportions of 22 distinct immune cell populations. All analyses were carried out in R (v4.2.1), with data preprocessing performed using the e1071 and preprocessCore packages.

Correlation analyses were then performed to examine the association between AKT1S1 expression and immune infiltration. Results were visualized with the ggpubr and ggExtra packages, and scatter plots were generated using ggplot2 to display the relationships between AKT1S1 expression and infiltration levels of individual immune cell subtypes.

TIDE analysis

The TIDE web platform was utilized to predict responses to ICB therapy. Differences in TIDE scores between the high-risk and low-risk groups were tested utilizing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Protein expression analysis in clinical samples

Protein expression data were gathered from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), which provides immunohistochemistry (IHC) profiles across 46 normal tissue types and more than 20 cancer types. Tissue sections were stained using antibodies against over 11,000 human proteins. Staining intensity was classified as negative, weak, moderate, or strong, based on visual assessment and imaging parameters such as laser power and detector sensitivity.

Clinical sample collection

This study included 66 NSCLC patients who received ICB therapy. Eligibility criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, histologically confirmed malignancy, unresectable and/or metastatic disease, at least one radiologic assessment post-treatment, and availability of pre-treatment formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples from the MATCH-R study. Patients treated with ICB combined with chemotherapy were excluded. All participants received either anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy or combination regimens based on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Treatment response was evaluated using RECIST or iRECIST criteria. Durable clinical benefit (DCB) was defined as an objective response or stable disease lasting ≥ 12 months. Patients exhibiting progressive disease or stable disease with progression-free survival (PFS) ≤ 12 months were classified as having no durable benefit (NDB). PFS was calculated from treatment initiation to disease progression, death, or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was measured from the initiation of treatment to the date of death or last follow-up.

Plasma proteomics analysis

Plasma protein profiling was performed using the Olink® PEA technology (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden), as previously established. The method employed matched pairs of antibodies, each conjugated to a unique oligonucleotide. When both antibodies bound to their target protein, the oligonucleotides were brought into proximity, enabling hybridization followed by a DNA polymerase-driven extension reaction. This process generated unique DNA barcode sequences corresponding to specific proteins, which were quantified using next-generation sequencing (NGS) or real-time PCR. In this study, baseline plasma samples from 66 NSCLC patients were analyzed using the Olink® Explore 1536 panel, which included 1472 protein targets and 48 internal controls distributed across four 384-plex panels covering inflammation, oncology, cardiometabolic, and neurology. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 2 × 50 bp reads using an S1 flow cell. Data were processed through Olink’s proprietary quality control pipeline, and protein abundance was reported as Normalized Protein Expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale. Internal controls and inter-plate normalization were applied to ensure data reliability. Quantification of DNA barcodes was performed using the Biomark HD real-time PCR system (Fluidigm), and NPX values reflected relative protein expression, with higher NPX indicating greater abundance.

Evaluation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) in the TME

All tissue sections suspected to contain TLS were reviewed by a pathologist and subjected to multiplex IHC staining following standard hematoxylin–eosin-saffron (HES) staining protocols. TLS was identified as lymphoid aggregates primarily composed of B lymphocytes, frequently accompanied by plasma cells and T lymphocytes. Primary antibodies used for IHC included anti-CD20 (a B cell marker) and anti-CD3 (NB600-1441, Bio-Techne, China, Shanghai) for T cell identification. TLS were defined as lymphoid aggregates consisting of more than 50 cells, located within tumor nests or at the invasive margin—defined as fibrous tissue within 1 mm of tumor cells. For the evaluation of TLS within lymphoid organs (e.g., lymph nodes, spleen, and tonsils), only TLS intermixed with tumor cells or situated at a distance from residual lymphoid tissue was considered in order to exclude pre-existing lymphoid follicles.

IHC staining

IHC was conducted on 3.5 μm-thick FFPE tissue sections using the Ventana Discovery Ultra automated staining system (Roche Diagnostics). Dual staining was performed with the following antibody pairs: (i) CD3 (1:50, rabbit anti-human, NB600-1441) and CD20 (1:150, mouse anti-human, NBP2-45,454), (ii) CD8 (1:100, mouse anti-human, NBP2-25,195) and PD-L1 (1:50, rabbit anti-human, NBP1-76,769). All antibodies were purchased from Bio-Techne (Shanghai, China). Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was completed via heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER). To prevent non-specific binding, a blocking reagent provided with the platform was applied. Primary antibodies were incubated at ambient temperature for 2 h, followed by 30 min of incubation with secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using OmniMap anti-rabbit HRP (760–4311) and anti-mouse HRP (760–4310) reagents (Ventana, Roche). For CD3 and CD8, DAB was applied as the chromogen, generating a brown signal, while for CD20 and PD-L1, an alkaline phosphatase (AP) system with BCIP/NBT substrate generated a purple signal. After chromogenic development, slides were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped with mounting medium. All stained sections were reviewed under a light microscope, and the entire procedure followed validated manufacturer protocols and previously established conditions.

Tumor PD-L1 expression assessment and stratified analysis

To evaluate whether AKT1S1 serves as a predictive biomarker independent of PD-L1 expression status and to control for the potential confounding effect of PD-L1 in clinical stratification, the standardized Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) was applied to quantify PD-L1 expression. TPS is defined as the percentage of viable tumor cells showing partial or complete membranous PD-L1 staining relative to the total number of viable tumor cells. Only tumor cells with partial or complete membranous staining were included, whereas PD-L1–positive immune cells or tumor cells with cytoplasmic staining alone were excluded to minimize bias from non-specific signals.

Each tumor sample was independently reviewed by two pathologists, with at least 100 viable tumor cells assessed, following previously published methodologies (PMCID: PMC8612948; PMCID: PMC10480627). PD-L1 expression levels were classified according to TPS as follows: TPS ≥ 1% was defined as PD-L1–positive, and TPS < 1% as PD-L1–negative, for use in clinical stratification analyses.

For statistical analysis, plasma AKT1S1 expression levels were combined with PD-L1 status for subgroup analysis of PFS. In the PD-L1–negative subgroup (TPS < 1%, n = 41), prognostic differences between the AKT1S1High and AKT1S1Low groups were compared to determine whether AKT1S1 exhibited predictive value independent of PD-L1 status. A corresponding analysis was also performed in the PD-L1–positive subgroup (TPS ≥ 1%, n = 25).

Semi-automated quantification of T cell infiltration

Tissue sections were digitized using the Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging platform (Akoya Biosciences). CD8+ T cell density within tumor lesions was quantified through image analysis. Tissue segmentation and cell phenotyping were performed with Inform software (version 2.4.1, Akoya Biosciences), enabling automated identification of CD8+ T cells annotated by a pathologist. CD8+ lymphocyte density for each sample was calculated as the number of CD8+ T cells per unit surface area of the tumor lesion.

Survival and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.1. Wilcoxon and chi-square tests were used for group comparisons, as appropriate. K–M survival curves were generated and compared with the log-rank test (survival package, v3.3.1), with PFS as the primary endpoint. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated utilizing Cox proportional hazards regression. Optimal cutoff values for stratification were determined with the maxstat package based on maximally selected rank statistics. Multivariate Cox analysis was conducted with the survivalAnalysis package (v0.3.0) to assess the independent prognostic value of candidate biomarkers for ICB. Statistical significance was established as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

The determinant role of AKT1S1 expression in prognosis and immunotherapeutic response in NSCLC

To clarify the clinical relevance of AKT1S1, prognostic and immunotherapy analyses were conducted. Time-dependent ROC curves confirmed its strong predictive accuracy for prognosis (Fig. S7A). Correlation analysis in the TCGA-NSCLC cohort showed that AKT1S1 expression significantly varied with clinical stage and TNM classification (Fig. S7B). A prognostic nomogram demonstrated strong predictive performance (Fig. S7C), with calibration curves validating its accuracy (Fig. S7D). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified AKT1S1 as an independent prognostic factor for OS in NSCLC (Fig. S7E). These results suggest that AKT1S1 is a clinically relevant prognostic protein in NSCLC.

Baseline AKT1S1 levels are linked to poor clinical outcomes in ICB therapy

To identify potential peripheral biomarkers predictive of ICB responsiveness, we performed high-throughput plasma proteomic profiling in 66 cancer patients. Proteomic measurements were obtained, which target 1,472 proteins alongside 48 internal controls. All plasma samples were harvested prior to the initiation of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy (Fig. 6A), and patient characteristics are detailed in Table S1. We then evaluated the association between individual protein markers and DCB. Notably, AKT1S1 expression was evidently lower in patients with DCB compared to those with NDB (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, plasma AKT1S1 levels positively correlated with AKT1S1 expression in matched tumor tissues (Fig. 6C). Patients in the AKT1S1Low group exhibited significantly improved OS (p = 0.047) (Fig. 6D) and PFS (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6E). Overall, patients who achieved DCB had substantially lower baseline plasma AKT1S1 levels than those who did not (Fig. 6F). Specifically, the DCB rate was 30% in the AKT1S1Low group versus 21% in the AKT1S1High group (Fig. 6G). Discussion

One of the core findings of this study is the association between PRAS40 expression and the efficacy of ICB in NSCLC patients. High PRAS40 expression was significantly correlated with primary resistance to ICB, suggesting its potential role in regulating tumor immune evasion. Unlike previous studies, this work innovatively explored the role of PRAS40 in NSCLC immunotherapy, providing a new theoretical basis for personalized treatment [20]. Multivariate analysis confirmed PRAS40 as an independent prognostic factor, consistent with other established predictors [21].

High PRAS40 expression was linked to reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and lower immune checkpoint gene expression in the TME, indicating that PRAS40 may influence ICB response by modulating immune evasion. This observation deepens our understanding of the interplay between the TME and immunotherapy efficacy, though additional functional studies are warranted. TIDE and IPS analyses demonstrated that PRAS40 outperformed conventional biomarkers in predicting ICB response, underscoring its translational potential as a novel prognostic and predictive biomarker in NSCLC.

The link between high PRAS40 expression and primary ICB resistance in NSCLC suggests its involvement in multidimensional immune regulatory networks. Beyond its known mTORC1 inhibitory function [22], AKT1S1 may promote immune evasion through autophagy regulation, glycolytic reprogramming, and disruption of mTOR feedback loops [23–25]. Given the reported role of the CXCL13/CCR7 axis in TLS formation and our observation of TLS reduction, AKT1S1 may inhibit B cell recruitment and TLS development, thereby weakening antitumor immunity. Furthermore, AKT1S1 overexpression may enhance AKT/mTOR signaling, upregulate PD-L1 expression, suppress CD8+ T cell activation and infiltration, and modulate B cell and Treg function, ultimately driving immune escape and ICB resistance (Fig. S9).

Compared with PD-L1 expression and TMB [26], AKT1S1 demonstrates distinct prognostic advantages. Multivariate analyses identified AKT1S1 as an independent prognostic factor, underscoring its potential to complement established biomarkers. Notably, integrating AKT1S1 expression with TIDE scoring further improved predictive accuracy and may help identify PD-L1–negative but ICB-sensitive subgroups.

Plasma PRAS40 levels were also closely associated with treatment response, supporting its potential as a peripheral biomarker [27, 28]. Unlike conventional markers, PRAS40 can be directly and rapidly measured, enabling real-time monitoring of therapeutic efficacy and timely optimization of treatment strategies. As a minimally invasive biomarker, circulating PRAS40 not only simplifies longitudinal monitoring but also provides immediate clinical insights, thereby supporting rapid and individualized decision-making in the context of immunotherapy [16, 29].

Overall, this study identifies AKT1S1 as a novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for NSCLC, providing new clinical directions while underscoring the need for further functional studies. Immune profiling revealed that high-risk patients, characterized by elevated AKT1S1 expression, exhibited poor responses to both immunotherapy and chemotherapy, highlighting the importance of individualized treatment strategies. These findings not only deepen our understanding of NSCLC pathogenesis but also offer practical tools for clinical application.

The study also examined the association between AKT1S1 expression and primary resistance to ICB in NSCLC patients, providing a new direction for lung cancer immunotherapy. By integrating pathological and proteomic analyses, high PRAS40 expression was identified as a predictor of poor prognosis and a potential regulator of ICB response through modulation of immune evasion in the TME. The findings offer a theoretical basis for personalized treatment and may guide the development of more precise immunotherapy strategies to improve patient outcomes and survival.

Several limitations should be noted, including the relatively small sample size for plasma PRAS40 analysis and the cross-sectional study design, which may reduce the stability and reliability of the findings. Future investigations should enlarge the sample size, incorporate long-term follow-up, and include more comprehensive clinical data to validate results. Although the risk model demonstrated favorable performance, prospective validation remains essential for assessing clinical applicability. Prospective cohort studies could overcome the inherent limitations of retrospective analyses, such as temporal bias, unmeasured confounding, and inconsistencies in data collection, thereby providing stronger causal evidence and improving clinical translation.

Further studies should investigate the mechanistic role of PRAS40 in tumor immunotherapy, identify additional biomarkers, and integrate multi-omics approaches to construct more comprehensive predictive models. Functional experiments, including CRISPR-Cas9 or shRNA-mediated AKT1S1 knockdown in murine tumor models, may clarify its causal impact on CD8+ T cell infiltration and ICB efficacy. PRAS40 can already be detected rapidly using ELISA (detection limit: 0.1 ng/mL, CV < 10%), and when combined with PEA technology, holds potential for development into a point-of-care testing kit for dynamic treatment monitoring. Additional clinical trials are required to confirm PRAS40 as a predictive biomarker for ICB response in NSCLC, thereby providing stronger evidence and facilitating clinical translation.
