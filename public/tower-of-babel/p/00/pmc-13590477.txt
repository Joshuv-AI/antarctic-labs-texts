Deep transfer learning for detection of placenta-mediated diseases from ultrasound images
Authors: Salman Jubair Jim, Sina Lotfian, Minhaj Nur Alam, Robert Rohling, Farah Deeba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Placenta-mediated diseases, such as preeclampsia (PE) and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) neonates, are associated with structural and functional changes in the placenta. While ultrasound is routinely performed during pregnancy, its potential for detecting such diseases using deep learning remains underexplored. This study presents a deep transfer learning approach using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to detect placenta-mediated diseases from ultrasound B-mode images of the human placenta ex vivo. This study is a secondary analysis of data from a prospective case-control study, where placentas were collected from women who delivered at BC Women’s Hospital, Vancouver, Canada, from April 2018 to April 2020. Ultrasound data obtained from ex vivo human placentas were used to perform transfer learning using three deep learning architectures: DenseNet-121, ResNet-50, and InceptionV3, each pretrained on one of two datasets: ImageNet or RadImageNet, to detect SGA and/or PE outcome. The dataset consisted of 46 placentas obtained from 40 participants, of which 25 placentas were complicated by SGA and/or PE (including 12 SGA-only placentas, 2 PE placentas, and 11 placentas affected by both SGA and PE). Experimental results demonstrate that DenseNet-121, when pretrained with ImageNet, achieved the most promising performance, with accuracy of 0.76 ± 0.12, F1-score of 0.78 ± 0.09, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.86 ± 0.10. Among the RadImageNet pretrained models, ResNet-50 yielded an accuracy 0.75 ± 0.10, F1-score 0.77 ± 0.07, and AUC of 0.84 ± 0.09. Our proof-of-concept results demonstrate that standard B-mode ultrasound images of the placenta ex vivo contain sufficient information to distinguish between normal pregnancies and pregnancies affected by SGA and PE.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a life-threatening pregnancy disorder that clinically manifests as new onset of hypertension accompanied by proteinuria with other common maternal organ dysfunctions, after 20 weeks of gestation1. PE affects about 2–5% of pregnancies worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of small-for-gestational-age (SGA) neonates, along with significant risk of maternal morbidity and mortality2. Placental malperfusion, dysfunction and abnormal tissue architecture play a key role in the development of PE and SGA3. Identifying the anatomic and structural changes of the placenta in PE and associated SGA pregnancies could potentially enable early detection and improved clinical management. However, due to the lack of accessible and effective tools, structural damage is typically assessed post-delivery using ex vivo tissue, when no intervention is possible.

Conventional B-mode ultrasound can examine the gross anatomy of the placenta, but this qualitative evaluation lacks sensitivity to detect relevant microstructural changes which contribute to placental dysfunction4. As an alternative, we have developed a quantitative ultrasound (QUS) imaging method, which has successfully detected QUS parameter changes associated with SGA and/or PE5. Prior studies have also demonstrated the utility of QUS for placental tissue characterization in animal models of preeclampsia. For example, envelope statistics derived from the homodyned K-distribution were used to characterize placental microstructural alterations in the reduced uterine perfusion pressure (RUPP) rat model of preeclampsia, demonstrating significant differences between RUPP and control placentas6. In another study, QUS effective scatterer diameter (ESD) measurements correlated with histological measurements of placental cell nuclei size and identified structural differences between RUPP and control placentas7. While promising, QUS imaging requires access to raw ultrasound data and calibration using external test phantoms8, which may not be available in clinical settings. In such cases, standard B-mode ultrasound imaging may offer a more accessible alternative for placental assessment.

Deep learning-based methods offer new possibilities for detecting placental abnormalities from routinely acquired ultrasound B-mode images during prenatal ultrasound. Prior work has primarily focused on tasks such as placenta segmentation9–12, and detection of abnormal placental adhesion13,14, anatomical structure localization and recognition15, and placental maturity grading16, with limited application to the detection of placenta-mediated pregnancy diseases. Particularly, a previous work showed promising results in detecting hypertension disorders of pregnancy using a combination of ultrasound B-mode images and microflow images17. Machine learning and deep learning techniques have also been successfully applied to ultrasound imaging in other organ systems, such as the liver, to detect tissue abnormalities and disease patterns. Notably, many of these applications, such as fatty liver detection, fibrosis detection and tumor localization, were first developed and validated in ex vivo or animal studies before in vivo clinical translation18–20. The application of deep learning for the detection of placenta-mediated diseases from placental B-mode ultrasound images remains relatively underexplored.

To explore the feasibility of using B-mode placental images to automate classification of PE and/or SGA, we present a deep learning framework to detect placenta-mediated pregnancy disorders: SGA and/or PE, from ultrasound B-mode images acquired from the human placenta ex vivo. We applied three convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures: DenseNet-12121, ResNet-5022, and InceptionV323. To address the limited availability of labeled data for training deep learning models from scratch, we applied a transfer learning strategy24, using models pretrained on two datasets: ImageNet, a large-scale natural image dataset25, and RadImageNet, a domain-specific medical imaging dataset26 and compared their performances. Finally, to understand the model interpretability, we used gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)27 to visualize the important regions in an image contributing to the model’s prediction. Although ultrasound images of placentas are routinely obtained during clinical care, they are generally optimized for macroscopic structural evaluation rather than quantitative analysis. The ex vivo imaging framework therefore provides a controlled environment with minimized confounding factors such as motion, maternal habitus, and variable placental position, to evaluate feasibility, establish baseline imaging characteristics, and refine model parameters before translation to in utero applications. This work establishes a foundation for in utero placental assessment with the potential for automated risk prediction in routine clinical practice.

Results

Participant and placental characteristics

A total of 46 placentas (including 14 placentas from 7 dichorionic twin pregnancies and 32 placentas from singleton pregnancies), collected from 40 pregnant women, were included in the analysis. Of the 46 placentas, 25 placentas (54.35%) were complicated by PE or SGA, or both, including 12 SGA-only placentas, 2 PE placentas, and 11 placentas affected by both SGA and PE. These included placentas from two dichorionic twin pregnancies; all other cases were singleton pregnancies. Among the PE cases for which the gestational age at diagnosis were recorded, 6 were early onset (diagnosed at < 32 weeks) and one was late onset. 4 of these placentas were from pregnancies complicated by gestational diabetes mellitus. Among all cases affected by SGA, 15 were categorized as early onset (diagnosed at < 32 weeks), 2 were late onset (diagnosed at > 32 weeks) and the rest were not recorded. Early onset PE and PE co-occurring with SGA are both associated with severe phenotype of PE, predominantly driven by underlying placental dysfunction28.

Control cases included placentas from five dichorionic twin pregnancies; the remaining placentas were from singleton pregnancies. Among the control placentas, one singleton and four twin placentas were affected by gestational diabetes mellitus. Placental pathology was reviewed by a perinatal pathologist (J.T.) according to the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus Statement29. Maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM) was present in 18 of 25 (72%) SGA and/or PE group and absent in all control placentas. Fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM) occurred in 8 of 25 (32%) SGA and/or PE group and 2 of 21 (9.5%) control placentas. Inflammatory lesions, such as chronic villitis, chronic deciduitis and subchorionitis, were observed in 3 of 25 (12%) SGA and/or PE group and 4 of 21 (19%) control cases. Placental abruption was identified in 1 of 25 (4%) SGA and/or PE cases. Non-specific findings, including chorangioma, increased perivillous fibrin/fibrinoid deposition, and intervillous thrombus, were observed in 6 of 25 (24%) SGA and/or PE cases and 3 of 21 (14%) control placentas.

Model performance

The performance metrics across the 5-fold cross-validation for all evaluated model configurations are summarized in Table 2. Across all architectures, ImageNet-pretrained models achieved slightly higher AUC values than their RadImageNet-pretrained counterparts. DenseNet-121 with ImageNet pretraining achieved the highest mean AUC (0.86 ± 0.1). Sensitivity was highest for DenseNet-121 with RadImageNet pretraining (0.80 ± 0.04), while precision was highest for InceptionV3 with ImageNet pretraining (0.83 ± 0.16). Overall, performance was comparable across architectures, with mean accuracies ranging from 0.74 to 0.76 and F1-scores ranging from 0.76 to 0.78. ROC curves for the three architectures with ImageNet and RadImageNet pretraining are shown in Fig. 2. Further, as an exploratory sensitivity analysis of the binary classification results, the best-performing model in the full cohort, DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet, achieved an AUC of 0.92 ± 0.07 in the singleton-only cohort, compared with 0.86 ± 0.10 in the original cohort.

Figure 3 presents Grad-CAM visualizations for representative cases associated with control and SGA and/or PE outcomes. Grad-CAM maps represent class-specific activation for individual examples, where warmer colors indicate image regions that most influenced the model’s prediction. Because these maps are generated from the final convolutional layer and upsampled to the original image size, they appear smooth and can extend slightly beyond tissue boundaries. Despite this limitation, the highlighted regions corresponded to the placental area in most cases across all architecture and pretraining strategies. This suggests that the models focus on the B-mode appearance of placentas when making predictions. In misclassified examples, such as the InceptionV3 (ImageNet) models for SGA-only, PE-only, and combined SGA & PE cases, the activation maps showed attention to non-placental tissue regions.

Multiclass classification model performance

We evaluated two best-performing models (in terms of AUC) from the binary classification stage: DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet and ResNet-50 pretrained on RadImageNet for three-class prediction across control, SGA only and SGA with PE. The performance metrics across the 5-fold cross-validation for all evaluated model configurations are summarized in Table 2. ResNet-50 pretrained on RadImageNet achieved improved Control-class sensitivity, precision, and F1-scores of 0.77 ± 0.21, 0.74 ± 0.15, and 0.72 ± 0.06, respectively, compared to DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet (0.70 ± 0.16, 0.62 ± 0.05, and 0.65 ± 0.09). In the SGA-only class, ResNet50 achieved a sensitivity of 0.52 ± 0.20 and F1-score of 0.54 ± 0.17, compared to DenseNet121’s 0.44 ± 0.32 and 0.36 ± 0.20. The most challenging class, SGA with PE, showed the lowest sensitivity for both models; however, ResNet50 yielded better performance (0.48 ± 0.33 vs. 0.26 ± 0.21). Furthermore, ResNet-50 achieved the highest overall accuracy and average AUC, outperforming DenseNet121.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates the feasibility of applying deep learning-based methods to automate the detection of placenta-mediated diseases from B-mode ultrasound images obtained from ex vivo human placentas. By leveraging transfer learning with three convolutional neural network architectures and two pretraining datasets (ImageNet and RadImageNet), we developed proof-of-concept models capable of detecting SGA and/or PE with notable classification performance. We did not observe any performance advantage of using RadImageNet pretraining over ImageNet, with both approaches yielding comparable results.

The inclusion of both singleton and dichorionic twin placentas may also introduce biological heterogeneity, as twin placentas can exhibit structural characteristics distinct from singleton placentas. To address this concern, we performed an exploratory singleton-only sensitivity analysis. Although this analysis was limited by the reduced sample size, the overall classification trend was retained.

We also assessed model attention maps using Grad-CAM visualization, which was useful in identifying image regions that influenced the automated decision. In most cases across the evaluated architecture and pretraining combinations, the placenta region was highlighted, indicating that the models often focused on clinically relevant structures. However, there were a few examples, particularly among incorrectly classified cases, where non-placental regions were highlighted. These regions may indicate that the models utilized contextual image features, such as placental boundaries and shape during classification. This behavior may also reflect differences in feature extraction strategies across architectures. The Grad-CAM approach could therefore serve as a useful complementary tool for assessing classifier behavior and identifying cases where model attention may extend beyond the primary placental region. Future studies comparing full-image analysis with segmented placental region-based analysis approaches may help further clarify the extent to which contextual image features contribute to classification performance and attention map generation.

The multiclass classification results show that distinguishing SGA-only from SGA & PE remains challenging. Across all classes, ResNet-50 pretrained on RadImageNet outperformed DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet, with higher sensitivity, F1-scores, and AUROC, particularly for the SGA & PE group. These findings suggest that domain-specific pretraining improves multiclass discrimination.

Previously, we developed a logistic regression model, placenta-QUS, using QUS parameters, which were estimated using the ultrasound raw radiofrequency data. The model achieved an AUC of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.78–0.98) for SGA and/or PE prediction5. The current deep learning models reached slightly lower AUC values (with a maximum of 0.86 ± 0.10). The advantage of the current approach over the previous method is that they operate directly on the ultrasound B-mode images, which are routinely obtained for clinical care, without requiring access to raw RF data or specialized software for QUS parameter computation. The ultrasound system in this study is a standard clinical scanner, in addition to a research interface providing access to the raw RF data. In routine clinical systems, such data are internally acquired but not always accessible. The current deep learning framework relies only on B-mode images, which are available on all ultrasound machines, and directly compatible with existing obstetric workflows. Therefore, this approach could serve as a practical alternative in settings where access to RF data or the capability to compute QUS parameters is not available.

Our study has several limitations. The main limitation is the relatively small number of cases in the dataset, which prevented dividing the data into separate training, validation, and test sets. Although several bias control measures (cropping, data augmentation, stratified cross-validation, and transfer learning) were applied, risk of shortcut learning and overfitting to the data cannot be excluded due to the limited sample size. Additionally, the study was conducted on placentas ex vivo and is therefore a diagnostic model predicting existing outcomes. A future in utero study on an adequately powered sample will be necessary to validate the proposed model for prognostic use, i.e., prediction of adverse outcomes before onset. Another limitation of this study is the difference in gestational age at delivery between the study groups. Gestational age may influence placental morphology and histopathological appearance and therefore represents a potential confounding factor in the interpretation of the image classification results. Future in utero studies with imaging performed within matched gestational age ranges will be important to isolate disease-specific imaging characteristics from gestational age-related variability. Additionally, unlike the system-independent QUS-based approaches, no measures have yet been taken to make the current model system independent. It is unknown whether the proposed deep learning models will generalize across ultrasound machines, settings, or acquisition protocols. All data in this study were acquired under a consistent imaging setup, and performance may vary when applied to images from different hardware or clinical environments. Additionally, in utero imaging would introduce substantially greater variation due to maternal habitus, placental position and size across gestation, and scanner-specific acquisition parameters. Future studies will evaluate the performance of the proposed framework under real-world imaging conditions, such as variation in placental positions and operator dependent systems settings, as well as assess cross-machine and multi-site generalizability to ensure applicability for broader clinical use. Additionally, domain adaptation strategies will be required to improve robustness across varying imaging platforms and acquisition conditions. Future in utero studies with adequately powered, matched cohorts and inclusion of relevant clinical covariates will be essential to confirm its imaging-specific contribution to early disease prediction.

Conclusion

In this study, we presented multiple deep learning architectures, each trained with two different pretraining strategies, for detecting placenta-mediated diseases using B-mode ultrasound images of the placenta ex vivo. The models achieved good diagnostic performance in distinguishing between normal pregnancies and those affected by SGA and/or PE. Further validation in in utero studies is the next step toward developing a reliable, non-invasive tool for early prediction of placenta-mediated diseases.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a secondary analysis of data from a prospective case-control study, where multimodal imaging data were acquired from the placenta ex vivo30,31. In this paper, we analyze the ultrasound B-mode images of the placenta. Placentas were collected from pregnant participants (≥ 19 years) with normal pregnancy or a diagnosis of SGA and PE, who delivered at BC Women’s Hospital, Vancouver, Canada, from April 2018 to April 2020. Eligibility criteria included participants aged 19 years or older and had either a normal pregnancy scheduled for elective cesarean section at BC Women’s Hospital, or a diagnosis of SGA fetus or PE with planned delivery at BC Women’s Hospital, either by cesarean section or vaginal birth. Participants identified with a gestational abnormality or other pregnancy-related conditions (such as congenital anomalies, genetic abnormalities, maternal or fetal infection, or aneuploidy) and treated with investigational medication were excluded. The study (H17-00331) was performed under written informed consent of all participants after approval by the University of British Columbia/Children’s and Women’s Health Center of British Columbia Research Ethics Board. Secondary analysis and data sharing with the University of North Carolina at Charlotte were approved under IRB-23-0615. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data acquisition protocol

After delivery, the placentas were stored at 4o C prior to imaging. Before imaging the amniotic sac and the umbilical cord were removed and the placenta was then placed in a plastic container. A layer of acoustic absorbing pad (Aptflex F28, Precision Acoustics, UK) was placed beneath the maternal surface of the placenta to reduce reverberation artifacts. A sonographer (V.L.) first scanned the placenta to locate two regions-of-interest: (i) homogeneous region without macroscopic or sonographically visible abnormalities and anatomical structures (such as large vessels or stem villi), and (ii) an inhomogeneous region with suspected lesions identified during gross examination or ultrasound scan, using a SonixTouch ultrasound machine (Analogic Corp., Richmond, BC) and an L14-5/38 linear transducer at the PRIME (Perinatal Research and Imaging Evaluation) center, located at BC Women’s Hospital and Health Centre. The regions of interest were marked using India ink and photographed for future reference (Fig. 4). For the present analysis, the homogeneous ROI was used to investigate microstructural differences undetectable by conventional ultrasound or gross inspection.

Acquiring all the scans under identical ultrasound settings ensured that the system-dependent factors, such as diffraction effects, focusing and imaging depth remained uniform across specimens. The same imaging setup, including the temperature-controlled water bath, container, and acoustic absorbing pad, was used for all placentas to maintain consistent coupling and acoustic conditions between the probe and tissue. The objective was to minimize variability arising from the imaging system itself.

After imaging, each placenta underwent pathological examination, including measurement of placental weight and dimensions. The placenta was sampled such that full-thickness sections included the volumes of interest acquired with SWAVE and were aligned with the plane imaged with ultrasound (Fig. 4). Up to four sections (2 μm each) were obtained per placenta. After routine formalin fixation, processing, and hematoxylin and eosin staining, the slides were scanned (Fig. 4) with the Aperio ScanScope system (Aperio, Vista, CA) at 400× microscope resolution at the BC Children’s Hospital Department of Pathology. The pathologic slides were reviewed by an experienced perinatal pathologist (J.T.) according to a routine protocol based on the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus Statement29.

The models were developed using ultrasound images from the placenta ex vivo to detect the outcome: occurrence of small-for-gestational-age (SGA) and/or preeclampsia (PE). SGA was defined as fetal abdominal circumference < 10th percentile measured from routine ultrasound examination. PE was defined as gestational hypertension (systolic pressure or diastolic pressure) and proteinuria (0.3 g/day in a 24-h urine collection)32.

The ultrasound images were cropped at the top and bottom to remove irrelevant data, such as header information and dark background. Input images were resized to a dimension of 224 × 224 pixels for DenseNet-121 and ResNet-50, and to 299 × 299 pixels for InceptionV3. Because pretrained convolutional neural networks (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet) require three-channel RGB input, each grayscale B-mode image was replicated across three channels to form an RGB-equivalent input. Pixel values were subsequently normalized using the ImageNet channel statistics (mean = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406]; standard deviation = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]). For transfer learning, the pretrained convolutional base of each network was utilized as a feature extractor. The original fully connected classification layer was replaced with a new sequence of layers suitable for binary classification. The layers included a dropout layer (with a dropout rate of 0.5) to reduce overfitting, followed by a linear layer that mapped the extracted features to two output logits representing two classes: (i) control and (ii) SGA and/or PE.

For model development, five-fold stratified cross-validation (CV) was applied at the patient (placenta) level using scikit-learn’s StratifiedKFold with shuffle=True and a fixed random_state = 42 to ensure reproducibility. Stratification preserved the binary class proportion (Control vs. SGA/PE) across folds within ± 1 case. To prevent any placenta-level information leakage, all 30 ultrasound frames from a given placenta were always assigned together to the same fold within any iteration; no frames originating from the same placenta ever appeared in both training and held-out splits. Table 3 summarizes the five-fold stratified cross-validation partitions used for binary classification, including the number of placentas and ultrasound frames assigned to the training and held-out validation sets in each fold. To prevent cross-fold contamination of model parameters, an independent model instance was instantiated at the start of each cross-validation iteration, with pretrained backbone weights reloaded fresh, the classification head re-initialized, and a new optimizer and scheduler constructed; five entirely independent models were therefore trained, one per fold. Models were trained with a batch size of 32 using the AdamW optimizer34 with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay of 1 × 10⁻², and a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule that decayed the learning rate from 1 × 10⁻⁴ to a minimum of 1 × 10⁻⁶ over the course of training. The classification objective was the standard cross-entropy loss; for the InceptionV3 architecture, an auxiliary-classifier loss with a weighting factor of 0.4 was additionally included, consistent with the original InceptionV3 training protocol. DenseNet-121 and ResNet-50 were each trained for 50 epochs, while InceptionV3 was trained for 100 epochs to accommodate its slower convergence on this dataset. For each cross-validation fold, the model checkpoint achieving the highest validation accuracy across all training epochs was retained for evaluation.

Because placentas from twin pregnancies may exhibit structural characteristics distinct from singleton placentas, we performed an exploratory singleton-only sensitivity analysis to assess whether the main classification trends observed in the full cohort were retained in a more clinically homogeneous subset. For this analysis, 14 placentas from dichorionic twin pregnancies were excluded from the original cohort of 46 placentas, leaving 32 placentas from singleton pregnancies. We then re-trained the selected models using the same procedure as in the primary analysis.

Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)27 was applied to selected validation images from representative folds to generate heatmaps highlighting the regions weighted most strongly by the models for SGA and/or PE detection.

Multiclass classification model analysis

SGA accompanied by PE exhibit more severe placental morphological abnormalities compared to cases of SGA alone35. Therefore, we performed a multiclass classification analysis for three groups: (i) controls, (ii) SGA only, and (iii) SGA & PE. Training and validation were conducted using the same procedures established for the binary classification model.

Statistical analysis

Comparison of demographic and clinical characteristics between control and diseased cases were performed with the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, as the sample size was relatively small and the distributions of the estimated measures were not strictly Gaussian. We consider a p value < 0.05 as indicating statistical significance.

Model performance for each fold was assessed on the held-out validation set using classification metrics: accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), and F1-score, computed by applying a probability threshold of 0.5 at the final layer. The overall performance was reported as the mean and standard deviation of these metrics across the 5 folds. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for each fold, and the mean and standard deviation of area under the curve (AUC) across 5 folds were reported.

All experiments were implemented using PyTorch (version 2.5.1.post308)36. Training and inference were performed on NVIDIA (Model RTX A5500) GPUs.
