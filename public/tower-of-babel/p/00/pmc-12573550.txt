Mental health problems, traumatic experiences and subjective treatment goals of treatment-seeking refugee minors
Authors: Joschi Dewitz, Dana Barthel, Lars Dumke, Areej Zindler, Levente Kriston, Lea Schumacher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Forced displacement is globally increasing, with children and adolescents being particularly affected. Exposure to armed conflict, the flight itself and challenges of settling in new environments put them at risk of experiencing potentially traumatic events and developing mental health problems, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression. Still, limited information is available regarding the traumatic experiences, psychological stress and treatment goals of treatment-seeking refugee children and adolescents.Methods: This cross-sectional study evaluated data from refugee minors seeking treatment at an Outpatient Health Care Center for Refugee Children in Germany. The standard diagnostic procedure included assessing sociodemographic characteristics, traumatic experiences before, during and after flight and mental health problems, including the diagnosis of PTSD and depression. Treatment goals were also examined.Results: From October 2016 to December 2023, a total of 716 refugee minors participated in the study. They were on average 15.13 years old, 56.3% of the sample were unaccompanied, and 72.1% were male. Most participants were from Afghanistan and Syria. Repeated interpersonal violence was frequently experienced in the home country (65.5%), during flight (59.3%), and in Germany (13.7%). The majority of children and adolescents fulfilled the diagnostic criteria of PTSD (57.8%), and the prevalence of depressive episodes was 44.2%, with a comorbidity rate of 35.9%. The majority of refugee minors identified specific treatment goals, particularly for reducing trauma-related symptoms such as negative thoughts, sleep issues, and nightmares.Conclusions: Our findings provide novel data on the mental health problems, pre-, peri- and post-migration traumatic experiences and treatment expectations in a treatment-seeking sample of refugee minors. The findings highlight the importance of developing trauma-focused interventions and improving clinicians' skills to address effects of complex, potentially ongoing, traumatic experiences and to tailor treatment to personal treatment goals. This is essential for providing high quality care for refugee minors.

Introduction

The global crisis of forced displacement has been intensifying for decades due to armed conflicts, natural disasters, unequal economic resources, and a growing world population (UNHCR, 2025). By the end of 2024, the number of refugees – people forced to flee their own country in search of safety – totalled 43.4 million worldwide. Children are over-represented among refugee populations, accounting for more than 40% of the world’s refugees, despite representing less than a third of the global population (UNHCR, 2022, 2024). Similarly, in Germany, over half of the 1.17 million refugees hosted in 2015/2016 were under the age of 18 (BAMF, 2019; bpb, 2023). A considerable number of refugees from the Middle East, North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa, Eurasia, and Ukraine were registered in Germany, also after the main refugee movements in 2015/2016, many of them children (BAMF, 2023; BMI, 2024; UNICEF, 2022a). Since nearly all regions of origin share the commonality of ongoing armed conflicts, refugee minors were likely exposed to experiences of war, loss, hunger, and various forms of insecurity (UNICEF, 2022b).

Due to the trauma and instability associated with forced displacement, refugee children and adolescents are likely to experience significant levels of mental health problems (Müller et al., 2019). Studies consistently show that refugee children in general, and especially those who are unaccompanied, have a substantially higher likelihood of developing mental disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety compared to youth without flight experiences (Bean et al., 2007; Kien et al., 2019; Mannhart & Freisleder, 2017; Oleimat et al., 2023). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported pooled prevalence rates of 22.7% for PTSD and 13.8% for depression in refugee minors (Blackmore et al., 2020). Symptoms such as nightmares, sleep disturbances, persistent sadness, social withdrawal, and concentration difficulties can substantially impair psychosocial functioning and influence help-seeking behaviour (Namer et al., 2022). However, little is known about the clinical severity and symptom patterns with which refugee minors present in routine mental health services. Gaining a clearer understanding of their treatment needs and presenting complaints could help to improve the quality and appropriateness of care provided.

One factor that has consistently been associated with mental health problems in refugee minors is the extent of their traumatic experiences, with a higher number of traumatic experiences markedly increasing the risk of developing mental health problems (Bogic et al., 2015; Müller et al., 2019; Scharpf et al., 2021; Tam et al., 2017). Next to experiencing traumatic events in the home country, a longer duration of flight was also associated with a higher number of traumatic experiences (Bogic et al., 2015; Tam et al., 2017; van Os et al., 2016). During their flight, unaccompanied children and adolescents are even more likely to experience violence and life-threatening situations in comparison to accompanied minors (Müller et al., 2019). Accompanied children and adolescents can also be indirectly exposed to persistent stressors as a result of their parents’ traumas (Back Nielsen et al., 2019; Dalgaard et al., 2020). Childhood stress, such as neglect and maltreatment, is frequent among refugee children and adolescents (Teicher & Samson, 2013). While little is known about traumatizing experiences in Western home countries, refugee children and adolescents are likely to face several insecurities also after their flight (Hornfeck et al., 2023; Höhne et al., 2022; Vervliet et al., 2014). This emphasizes that refugee minors are likely to encounter a complex array of traumatic experiences, characterized by multiple, interrelated events rather than a single incident. Recognizing this complexity is essential to develop adequate care for this vulnerable population.

Adequate mental health care for refugee children and adolescents is both effective in alleviating various mental health problems and crucial for their integration (Cowling & Anderson, 2023; Della Rocca et al., 2023; Iversen et al., 2014). Still, most host countries primarily focus on acute medical care and often disregard the mental health needs of refugee children and adolescents (Smith Jervelund et al., 2020). Previous research showed that refugee minors have limited access to mental health services and only few receive appropriate treatment (Boettcher et al., 2021; Colucci et al., 2015; Dumke et al., 2024). For example, in a study of more than 200 refugee children and adolescents in Germany, only 15% of those reporting emotional difficulties had consulted a mental health professional (Namer et al., 2022). Even when contact to mental health services has been established, refugee minors are at heightened risk of receiving inequitable and potentially lower quality treatment. A nationwide study involving over 2,000 mental health professionals in Germany, including those working in child and adolescent services, found that refugee patients received 20% shorter treatment durations compared to the host population (Dumke et al., 2023). Mental health professionals often express uncertainty and difficulty in working with refugee populations, which may result in lower levels of engagement and continuity of care for these patients (Peñuela-O'Brien et al., 2023). In line with this, refugee children often report that treatment providers do not respond to their individual needs and often do not feel taken seriously or understood (Demazure et al., 2022).

A recent scoping review examining the quality of care for refugee minors across European countries identified notable quality deficiencies, including the provision of ineffective, inappropriate, or even harmful services (Dumke et al., 2025). A key contributing factor was the failure to engage with young people’s perspectives and treatment preferences. In line with this, a systematic review on refugee minors’ perceptions of mental health services further emphasize the importance of identifying relevant therapeutic goals to foster a strong working alliance and improve quality of care (Demazure et al., 2022). Notably, however, none of the studies included in the review directly assessed the treatment goals of refugee children and adolescents, highlighting a critical gap in current research and practice.

Taken together, limited knowledge on the mental health problems, the complex traumatic experiences, and treatment goals of treatment-seeking refugee minors is available. This directly impedes the development of appropriate care for treatment-seeking refugee minors, as their symptom burden is unclear, and wishes of the refugee minors were hardly considered. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to answer the questions (1) how frequent are traumatic experiences in refugee children and adolescents seeking treatment; (2) how prevalent are mental health problems, mainly PTSD and depression, in this population; and (3) what are the subjective treatment goals of these children and adolescents? To this end, we conducted an exploratory study, evaluating data collected during routine mental health care services.

Methods

The current study was conducted as part of the routine care at the Outpatient Health Care Center for Refugees Children at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf. This health care centre has been providing outpatient psychiatric, psychotherapeutic, and psychosocial care for refugee children since 1998. A key focus of the work is treating trauma-related disorders, such as PTSD, depression, anxieties, and sleep disorders. A team composed of medical doctors, psychologists, psychotherapists, art therapists, music therapists, educators, social workers, and interpreters is dedicated to treating trauma-related disorders in these children. Refugee minors are often referred from their school, youth services or other (non-specialized) health services. Within the region, it is the only service that provides such extensive mental health care specialized on the needs of refugee minors (very few other services focus on providing psychotherapeutic treatment to refugee minors).

Procedure

Since October 2016, data on sociodemographic characteristics and mental health problems of refugee minors who seek treatment at the Outpatient Care Center were collected during a standardized diagnostic process. All refugee minors that approached the clinic, who showed no signs of suicidality, psychosis, intoxication or signs of a developmental disorder, took part in the diagnostic procedure. If suicidality or psychotic symptoms were present, a stabilization phase preceded the diagnostic process. Originally, all minors that were at least 8 years old underwent the complete diagnostic procedure. From 2023 on, only children of at least 11 years were included. Children and adolescents were assessed by self-reports, proxy-reports by parents/caregivers were not assessed. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardian of the refugee minor (for unaccompanied minors, a legal guardian is provided by the youth welfare service in Germany). Sociodemographic information and information on mental health problems were collected through modules of a structured diagnostic interview and standardized questionnaires during an interview with a clinical psychologist. The structured diagnostic interview mainly focused on PTSD and depression-related symptoms as these disorders are most prevalent in this population. This interview was conducted in German or the mother tongue of the respective patient, often with the help of an interpreter. Minors did not receive any compensation for their participation. The ethics committee of the Hamburg Chamber of Psychotherapists approved the study (05/2017-PTK-HH).

Measures

The study collected data on traumatic experiences, PTSD diagnosis and severity of PTSD symptoms, depression diagnosis and severity of depression symptoms, risk behaviour, and treatment goals.

Traumatic experiences

The refugee minors were asked to self-report the frequency of different traumatic experiences using a questionnaire developed by the Child Public Health research group at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf. It measures how often a specific potentially traumatic event occurred for a given person, using a Likert scale with response options of ‘never’ (1), ‘once’ (2), ‘several times’ (3), and ‘unclear’ (4). Following the concept of sequential traumatization of children by Keilson (Keilson, 1979), it was assessed how often each traumatic event occurred in the home country, during the flight, and in the host country (Germany). The assessed traumatic events were adopted from the Stressful Life Events Questionnaire (Bean et al., 2004) and the University of California at Los Angeles Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Reaction (Steinberg et al., 2004) with modifications. The survey includes 15 items that covered (a) life-threatening illnesses (1 item), (b) serious accidents/natural disasters (1 item), and, (c) interpersonal violence (13 items). The 13 items assessing interpersonal violence cover experiences of violence, abuse, neglect, prostitution, war, kidnapping, separation, witnessing, perpetration, flight, and imprisonment. Further, patients could report the frequency of other traumatic experiences and rate which event was perceived as the most burdensome in two free text fields.

Post-traumatic stress disorder

Diagnostic criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) were assessed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI-KID) Module K. This module includes a structured diagnostic interview with a dichotomous response format (yes/no) that assesses a PTSD diagnosis in children and adolescents according to the ICD-10 criteria (Sheehan et al., 1998). The MINI-KID Module K includes 16 items of which 15 items assess feelings during the last month in relation to a stressful life event. The first item of the MINI-KID module K was not administered since the detailed questionnaire regarding traumatic events was administered prior to the MINI-KID module. Therefore, the interviewer already knew whether or not a child has reported to have experienced such an event.

In addition, the severity of PTSD symptoms were measured using the Child Revised Impact of Events Scale (CRIES-13; CAW, 2023). This questionnaire consists of 13 items, assessing intrusion (4 items), avoidance (4 items), and arousal (5 items). Patients were asked how often they experienced a specific symptom on a four-point Likert scale with response options ranging from ‘not at all’ (scored as 0), ‘rarely’ (scored as 1), ‘sometimes’ (scored as 3) to ‘often’ (scored as 5). A score for the sub-scales intrusion, avoidance, and arousal was calculated by summing the scores of the corresponding items. The total score was calculated by summing the avoidance and intrusion score, whereby a score of 17 or higher was interpreted as a high probability of PTSD. The internal consistency of the CRIES-13 was found to be good in our (Cronbach's α = .89) and in previous studies (Verlinden et al., 2014).

Depression

A depression diagnosis was assessed with the Module A of the MINI-KID, based on DSM-IV and ICD-10 criteria (Sheehan et al., 1998). The MINI-KID Module A consists of eleven items, evaluating the presence of depressive symptoms in the past two weeks using dichotomous answer options (yes/no). We added one additional item (‘Have you experienced any unusual events during this time, such as hearing voices, seeing phenomena, or feeling that strange things are happening around you?’), to allow for the diagnosis of a severe depressive episode with psychotic symptoms (F32.3). Another additional item allows for the differentiation of depressive disorder from grief or medication- or substance-induced depressive symptoms: Were you ill just before feelings of sadness or irritability began? Did you take any medications, drugs, or alcohol at the onset? Did a close person to you pass away, and were the feelings of sadness or irritability only present in the two months following the loss?Four diagnoses could be established in accordance with the ICD-10 criteria: mild depressive episode (F32.0), moderate depressive episode (F32.1), and severe depressive episode without or with psychotic symptoms (F32.2 or F32.3).

Were you ill just before feelings of sadness or irritability began? Did you take any medications, drugs, or alcohol at the onset? Did a close person to you pass away, and were the feelings of sadness or irritability only present in the two months following the loss?

Additionally, the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Depression Pediatric Item Bank was used as a screening instrument to assess the severity of depressive symptoms within the last seven days (Irwin et al., 2010). The severity of various typical depressive symptoms (e.g. sadness, stress, loss of interest) was assessed using a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ (1), ‘almost never’ (2), ‘sometimes’ (3), ‘often’ (4), to ‘almost always’ (5). A score can be obtained ranging from a minimum of 14 to a maximum of 70. The following cut-off values were used: < 32 = ‘no depression’; 32–38 = ‘mild depression’; 39–53 = ‘moderate depression’; and <53 = ‘severe depression’. Varni and colleagues (2014) found good internal consistency for the scale (Cronbach's α = .86; Varni et al., 2014), similar to the internal consistency in our study (Cronbach's α = .92).

Risk behaviour

Risk behaviour was assessed using three items from the Borderline Symptom List (BLS-23; Bohus et al., 2009; Wolf et al., 2009) which assesses the frequency of medication, alcohol, and drug abuse (e.g. cannabis or cocaine) over the previous two weeks. The items were rated on a five-point Likert scale, including ‘never’ (0), ‘once’ (1), ‘twice’ (2), ‘daily’ (3), and ‘several times a day’ (4).

Suicidal and self-injurious behaviour

Suicidal and self-injurious behaviour in the last six month was assessed using three items based on questions from the Child Behavior Checklist (Achenbach & Edelbrock, 1991). Specifically, refugee minors were asked to rate the following three statements: ‘I intentionally injured myself (e.g. hitting the wall)’; ‘I am thinking about killing myself’; and ‘I tried to kill myself’. Possible answers were: ‘Not applicable’, ‘Somewhat or sometimes applicable’, ‘Exactly or often applicable’, ‘I don't know’, or ‘No answer’.

Treatment goals

Each patient was prompted to articulate two personal treatment goals in free-text format, in response to the query: ‘What changes should occur through treatment?’ For each treatment goal, the refugee minors rated their perceived likelihood of achieving the goal (‘How likely do you think it is that you will achieve the treatment goal?’) on a seven point Likert scale ranging from ‘unlikely’ (1) to ‘very likely’ (7). Additionally, the importance of attaining each treatment goal (‘How important is it to you to achieve the treatment goal?’) was rated on a seven point Likert scale ranging from ‘not important’ (1) to ‘very important’ (7).

Data analysis

The sociodemographic information of the sample and their mental health problems were described with descriptive statistics. We analyzed frequencies, ranges, means, and standard deviation of all included variables. Data were analyzed using IBM's statistics software SPSS Version 27.0.0.0 (IBM Corp, Armon, NY).

The qualitative content analysis of the treatment goals was carried out according to Mayring (Mayring, 2010). Based on an analysis of the free text, 14 categories were formed inductively. The subjective treatment goals were then assigned to the categories. To strengthen reliability, two persons (a doctoral student and a clinical researcher) independently categorized the treatment goals. Then a comparison was made, and a consensus decision was reached in the event of discrepancies/ambiguities.

In Germany, about one in five refugee minors reported interpersonal violence (n = 112; 21.1%). Treatment goals

Most of the children and adolescents (n = 532, 74.3%) stated at least one treatment goal. A second treatment goal was reported by 291 (40.6%) of the children and adolescents. The most frequently stated first treatment goal related to ‘negative thoughts and emotions about the past’ (n = 115, 21.2%). The second most frequent first treatment goal was ‘nightmare and sleeping problems’ with (n = 94, 17.7%). The most frequently stated second treatment goal was also ‘nightmare and sleeping problems’ (n = 55, 18.9%). All treatment goals were coded into categories which are presented in Table 4. On average, refugee minors rated the likelihood of achieving their first treatment goal as M = 5.19 (SD = 1.65; range = 1-7; n = 364). Furthermore, they indicated the importance of achieving this goal with an average rating of M = 6.63 (SD = 0.98; range = 1-7; n = 364). For the second treatment goal, the children and adolescents also assessed the likelihood of achieving it as M = 5.25 (SD = 1.61; range = 1-7; n = 192) and its importance to them as M = 6.57 (SD = 0.95; range = 1-7; n = 202). Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of PTSD, depression, and some other mental health problems, the frequency of traumatic experiences, and subjective treatment goals in a large sample of refugee minors seeking treatment at an outpatient care centre in Germany. Refugee minors presented with high rates of trauma-related mental health problems. Regarding point prevalence, more than half of the children and adolescents fulfilled diagnostic criteria of PTSD and 44.2% experienced a depressive episode. Comorbidity of PTSD and depression was rather common, and suicidal thoughts were reported by almost one in four refugee minors. Nearly every child and adolescent had experienced traumatic events, the vast majority of them even multiple times. Physical and psychological abuse, neglect, and witnessing violence were the most prevalent forms of interpersonal violence. The refugee children and adolescents reported frequent traumatic experiences not only in their home country and during flight but also after arrival in the host country. Addressing negative thoughts and feelings about past experiences, as well as nightmares and sleeping problems, were the most reported treatment goals. Our findings provide novel information on the lived realities regarding symptom burden, traumatic experiences and treatment expectations of refugee minors and may be relevant to improving the quality of care for this vulnerable group.

In the current study, we observed high rates of PTSD, depression, and suicidal and self-injurious behaviours among refugee children and adolescents seeking mental health treatment. Due to the focus on a service-seeking sample, the rates of mental health problems were significantly higher than those reported in studies using community samples (Kien et al., 2019). However, our findings are in line with previous studies on refugee minors seeking mental health treatment in Germany (Höhne et al., 2023). The twice as high rates of suicidality and self-harm in this study compared to community samples (Kien et al., 2019) may indicate that access to the health care system is often only sought or facilitated in the presence of an acute crisis or severe psychological distress. This indicates that preventive and early support may be unavailable or not accessed until psychological distress reaches a critical point. In line with this, refugee minors in our study also had lived, on average, nearly two years in Germany before they approached the clinic. For many refugee children and adolescents and their families, barriers such as language-related difficulties, stigmatization of mental illness and help-seeking, lack of availability of services, and legal or financial restrictions are common reasons for not accessing support (Colucci et al., 2015).

Nearly all of the children and adolescents who presented to the outpatient centre had experienced traumatic events, with the large majority having been exposed to multiple events. Alarmingly, more than one-fifth reported additional traumatic experiences after arriving in Germany. These findings are consistent with previous studies assessing trauma after arrival in host countries and add to a growing body of research that emphasize the cumulative trauma exposure that refugee minors face, not only before and during migration, but also after migration (Pfeiffer et al., 2022). Given the strong link between trauma exposure and mental health problems, our findings underscore the importance of comprehensive trauma assessment in both research and clinical practice, encompassing pre-, peri-, and post-migration experiences. Post-migration stressors (e.g. discrimination, uncertain residence status, poor living conditions) and traumatic experiences not only seem to negatively affect the processing of past experiences but have also been found to exacerbate existing mental health problems (Behrendt et al., 2022; Bronstein & Montgomery, 2011; Hornfeck et al., 2023; Montgomery & Foldspang, 2008; Scharpf et al., 2021; Vervliet et al., 2014). It is assumed that daily stressors can have a more substantial long-term impact on the mental health of children and adolescents than stressful life events occurring during pre- and peri-migration periods (Vervliet et al., 2014). At the same time, mental health professionals often feel inadequately prepared to deal with the ongoing stressors in the lives of their patients with a refugee background (Peñuela-O'Brien et al., 2023). In addition to improving the safety and protection of refugee minors, future research is needed to explore effective interventions and improve clinicians’ skills and knowledge to address the post-migration stressors and traumatic experiences identified in the current study.

The majority of refugee minors seeking treatment articulated clear treatment goals. The high-perceived probability and importance of reaching these treatment goals highlight refugee minors active role and motivation in addressing their mental health challenges. The most frequent reported subjective treatment goals concerned trauma-related symptoms, which is in correspondence with the mental health problems objectively assessed with standardized instruments. Furthermore, the third most frequently reported treatment goal in our study related to the refugee youth’ current living conditions, underlining the significant impact of post-migration stressors on mental health. These findings highlight the importance of multidisciplinary and integrated care approaches in addressing the needs of refugee children and youth. In line with World Health Organization guidance (WHO, 2024), child and youth mental health services should be supported by a diverse workforce and embedded within a network of interconnected services, including schools, community organizations, and primary care. Close collaboration with representatives of agencies responsible for housing, legal status, and child welfare can further be beneficial in addressing refugee youth’s broader needs (Versteele et al., 2024). Thus, multi-professional teams in refugee care and close collaborations with other relevant positions and authorities handling relevant issues such as living conditions and residence status are crucial for the care of refugee children and adolescents. In sum, mental health professionals need to carefully identify treatment goals and reach mutual agreement with their patients on what is important to work on to build a working alliance and provide quality mental health care (Demazure et al., 2022). The personalized formulation of treatment goals assists treatment planning, has a motivating effect and encourages the patient's cooperation (Driessen et al., 2001).

Limitations

First, the prevalence of mental health problems reported in this study cannot be generalized to the general population of refugee minors, as the sample consists of a treatment-seeking population. However, as the participants were seen in a routine care setting, the findings may be particularly relevant to other mental health services operating in similar contexts, where patients are likely to present with comparable characteristics. Second, the assessment of traumatic experiences, although detailed, does not provide a complete list of all possible traumatic events. Third, most interviews were conducted with an interpreter, which might have led to underreporting of particularly distressing and shameful experiences. Fourth, this study focused on the most prevalent mental health problems found in the literature, namely, PTSD and depression. We needed to balance comprehensiveness of the diagnostic assessment with the burden of participating in this study. However, there might be other relevant mental health problems faced by this population that we did not investigate in this study. Finally, this study is exploratory and based on a single group. Therefore, the generalizability and replicability of our findings need to be addressed in future studies. Nevertheless, this study is based on a very large sample of a vulnerable and hard-to-reach population and can, thereby, provide important insights into the needs of refugee children and adolescents presenting in routine care.

Conclusions and implications

Our results reveal that refugee children and adolescents seeking mental health care present with high rates of PTSD and depression. This may indicate that the existing health care system is struggling to respond to the high need for psychosocial support among refugee minors at an early stage. Reducing organizational barriers to care and establishing stepped care models using innovative treatment approaches such as peer support, brief psychological interventions and digital mental health interventions could help to provide timely support to refugee children and adolescents with mental health problems (Höhne et al., 2024).

Our findings on the frequency and complexity of traumatic events and the ongoing exposure to potentially traumatic events among refugee children and adolescents, even after their arrival in Germany, needs to be considered in the care of refugee children, to ensure their safety and protection. All persons involved in the care of refugee children and adolescents as well as authorities must be trained in child protection practices and trauma-informed work in order to prevent further traumatic experiences and to recognize traumatic symptoms in time.

Lastly, the reported treatment goals underscore the need for mental health interventions tailored to address trauma-specific symptoms in child and adolescent refugee populations. The findings also support the importance of integrating evidence-based therapeutic approaches that target trauma-related cognition and sleep disturbances, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy. Identifying refugee children’s and adolescents’ subjective treatment goals and emphasizing these areas in treatment planning may improve engagement and treatment outcomes, given that they align directly with the children's and adolescents’ own objectives.

Taken together, this study showed that treatment-seeking refugee minors experience a large symptom burden and encounter a complex array of traumatic experiences. Refugee children and adolescents indicated improving several trauma-related symptoms as important treatment goals. These findings emphasize the need for effective mental health services to prevent and treat trauma-related problems of this highly vulnerable patient group.
