Ivonescimab: The Next United States Food and Drug Administration Project Optimus Flashpoint?
Authors: Garth W Strohbehn, Mark J Ratain
Published: 2026

Since the late 2010s, lung cancer researchers have searched, largely in vain, for treatment strategies for advanced/metastatic NSCLC that could improve on the outcomes achieved with anti–programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1) receptor immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Ivonescimab, a bispecific antibody targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor and PD1, has offered encouraging findings. Ivonescimab is approved for use in the People's Republic of China based on progression-free survival (PFS) gains in first-line treatment of programmed death-ligand 1–positive squamous NSCLC and second-line treatment of EGFR-mutated NSCLC (in combination with chemotherapy).1 Furthermore, a recent presentation at the European Society of Medical Oncology Congress on October 19, 2025 reported that ivonescimab offered superior PFS over the combination of chemotherapy and the anti-PD1 therapy tislelizumab in squamous NSCLC.2 Ivonescimab’s global development program has proceeded rapidly and now spans multiple tumor types, including colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and pancreatic cancer, as well as a range of combination partners, including cytotoxic chemotherapy, radiation, and antibody-drug conjugates.3,4 To gain approval in the European Union and the United States, ivonescimab will need to exhibit benefit in Western populations against a Western standard of care. If FDA is indeed serious about its Project Optimus, its effort to ensure that the doses of cancer drugs are optimized at the time of approval, then the sponsor will also need to justify ivonescimab’s dose through a robust dose-finding process.5 At present, the dose of ivonescimab currently being used in registration trials, 20 mg/kg every 3 weeks (q3w), does not appear to meet this standard.

The phase 1A dose-escalation trial of ivonescimab evaluated 0.3 mg/kg to 30 mg/kg every 2 weeks (q2w) in a heavily pretreated population (n = 2 NSCLC) using a “3+3+3” design.6 Both pharmacologic activity (as defined by PD1 receptor saturation and declines in VEGF) and clinical activity (as defined by tumor response) were observed at the 3 mg/kg q2w (1.5 mg/kg/wk) dose level. Treatment-related adverse events appeared less often at 3 mg/kg q2w and below. The subsequent nonrandomized phase 1B trial assessed 10 mg/kg q3w (3.3 mg/kg/wk), 20 mg/kg q3w (6.7 mg/kg/wk), 20 mg/kg q2w (10 mg/kg/wk), and 30 mg/kg q3w (10 mg/kg/wk) in both treatment-naive and treatment-resistant (but ICI-naive) patients with NSCLC.7 Although objective response rates (ORR) appeared lower at the lowest average weekly dose of 3.3 mg/kg/wk (Table 1), 10 mg/kg q3w provided half of recipients with long-term disease control on par with that observed in phase 3 trials. The disconnect between response and control should not be particularly surprising, since ORR is a poor surrogate end point after ICI treatment.9 In a separate, nonrandomized phase 2 trial, 10 mg/kg q3w (3.3 mg/kg/wk) and 20 mg/kg q3w (6.7 mg/kg/wk) were evaluated in combination with chemotherapy in treatment-naive, driver mutation-negative NSCLC (cohort 1); osimertinib-refractory, EGFR-driven NSCLC (cohort 2); and platinum-resistant, driver mutation-negative NSCLC (cohort 3).8 The first half of each cohort received 10 mg/kg q3w alongside chemotherapy; the combination seemed safe and tolerable. The second half of each cohort received 20 mg/kg q3w with chemotherapy. Neither ORR nor disease control rate reliably increased as ivonescimab's dose increased from 10 mg/kg to 20 mg/kg q3w (Table 2).

Ivonescimab has been taken forward in pivotal phase 3 trials—and many single-arm phase 2 trials in different disease states—at a dose of 20 mg/kg q3w.10 Yet, the early-phase data suggest its minimum effective dose to be much lower than 20 mg/kg q3w in both the monotherapy and the combination settings (Table 3). There is reason to believe that: (1) 10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg q3w have equivalent efficacy; (2) lower doses may confer a lower risk of adverse events; and (3) the optimal dose may be even lower than 10 mg/kg q3w. Taken together, this provides sufficient rationale for the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to mandate a dose optimization trial before approving ivonescimab for use in the United States, or perhaps as a postmarketing requirement should the data otherwise warrant accelerated approval. This has implications for the monotherapy component of the ivonescimab development program and, perhaps to a greater extent, the combination trials currently underway. Administering excessively dosed ivonescimab in combination with other therapies (which have their own toxicities) may push toxicity and tolerability beyond acceptable limits, sacrificing time on treatment and, consequently, clinical outcomes for recipients.

What form should such a trial (or trials) take? The guiding principles for dosage optimization trials were elucidated in collaborative sessions between the U.S. FDA and American Society of Clinical Oncology.11 First, it should be a randomized trial devoid of bias favoring higher doses (i.e., it should not utilize a noninferiority design). Because it is incumbent on the higher dose to prove its incremental efficacy advantage over lower dosage(s), the trial should assess whether the highest proposed dose offers clinically meaningful and statistically significant efficacy benefit over the lower dose(s). Second, treatment arm assignments can be engineered to make the trial maximally safe for participants, which would help accrual, particularly if conducted in the postmarketing space. Crossover to the higher dose after the primary end point (e.g., disease progression) is reasonable; judicious use of interim analysis would help avoid overallocation of patients to (potentially ineffective) lower doses while still answering the research question, and more elegant Bayesian adaptive designs may improve efficiency.12,13 Third, activity end points such as ORR or tumor size reduction would be poor end points for this trial, not only because ORR is a poor surrogate for overall survival with anti-PD1 therapies9 but also because ivonescimab’s dual mechanisms of action make the provenance of responses inherently ambiguous (i.e., was the response because of anti-PD1 activity, anti-VEGF activity, or both?). Duration of response or PFS would seem better choices.11 Fourth, ivonescimab provides an excellent use case for model-informed drug development,14 as it possesses dual mechanisms of action (akin to a combination therapy) and VEGF- and PD1-mediated responses occur over different timescales, its binding to the two targets is cooperative, and its development has moved forward in a wide array of diseases despite relatively limited phase 1 and phase 2 data (i.e., there has been no large basket trial). Finally, and most obviously, given both the challenges associated with conducting dose optimization trials in the postmarketing setting13 and the number of patients who would be placed at risk of dose-related adverse events if ivonescimab were approved at an unnecessarily high dose, ivonescimab’s optimal dose should be established before regulatory approval and widespread use.5,11

Optimizing ivonescimab’s dose does not seem to be one of the sponsor’s major concerns. Between September 17, 2024 and September 17, 2025, the number of clinical trials involving ivonescimab (or AK112 or SMT112) registered at ClinicalTrials.gov rose from 40 to 117. Of the 11 that are actively recruiting at U.S. sites as of September 17, 2025, only one (in colorectal cancer) alludes to more than one dose level being tested. Ivonescimab may end up testing the FDA’s resolve on this issue. First, FDA itself has described how well-publicized presentations can complicate clinical trial equipoise, particularly in areas of high unmet need.15 Second, FDA credibility, especially on this issue, may be called into question if its own guidance is not being enforced. Of note, the sponsor has suggested, in U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission filings, that the FDA supports the current development plan, including dose.16 Finally, ivonescimab’s sponsor (and the sponsors of other bispecific antibodies currently in development) should consider the economic consequences of failing to optimize the dose. To this end, sotorasib may be a cautionary tale: if, ultimately, FDA grants accelerated approval to the 20 mg/kg q3w dose but—either as an industry-sponsored trial to fulfill a postmarketing requirement or a third party–funded trial—a lower dose is found to be near-effective,17 there is a risk that payers will refuse to cover the higher dose owing to the lack of marginal benefit, even if it is FDA-approved.18

The FDA introduced Project Optimus to minimize toxicities and promote efficacy-oriented dose optimization before a drug’s approval. In so doing, the FDA acknowledged that most cancer drug doses were optimized to prespecified toxicity thresholds and that the “more is more” bias that pervades cancer drug development harms patients. The large number of potential ivonescimab recipients underscores the need to optimize its dosage. Indeed, it is because ivonescimab is a potential future standard of care across an array of disease states that it should be held to a high standard.
