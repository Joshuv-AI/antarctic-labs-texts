Multidisciplinary Management and Hemodynamic Rescue of Cardiogenic Shock in RCA STEMI With Severe Mitral Regurgitation
Authors: Afek Kodesh, Ivan Aivasovsky, Matteo Sturla, Noah Markewitz, Louis Verreault-Julien, Daniel J Goldstein, Azeem Latib, Francesco Castagna, Ulrich P Jorde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical ConditionA 55-year-old man was admitted with inferior-wall ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. He was found to have multivessel disease with a culprit lesion not amenable for revascularization, new ischemic mitral regurgitation, and cardiogenic shock, prompting the initiation of mechanical circulatory support (MCS). His course was complicated by conduction abnormalities.Key QuestionsSome questions for consideration were: 1) Which criteria guide the choice of MCS in patients with complex cardiovascular anatomy and cardiogenic shock? 2) Is coronary artery bypass grafting with concomitant mitral valve repair/replacement superior to percutaneous coronary intervention and mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair in patients with poor target vessels and severe mitral regurgitation?OutcomeAfter initial stabilization with intra-aortic balloon pump, the patient underwent staged percutaneous coronary intervention and mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, leading to resolution of symptoms and disease. He also received a prophylactic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator in the setting of variable block and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction.Take-Home MessageThis case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary discussions for a patient with complex cardiovascular anatomy requiring MCS and a high level of acute care.Key words: cardiogenic shock, conduction disease, mechanical circulatory support, myocardial ischemia, mitral regurgitation, papillary muscle

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old Caucasian man with no known past medical history presented to the emergency department with 3 days of malaise and chest pain. The initial electrocardiogram demonstrated an inferior wall ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Figure 1), and an emergency left heart catheterization showed a distal right coronary artery (RCA) culprit lesion (Video 1) that could not be easily crossed, as well as severe diffuse disease in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and left circumflex artery (LCx) (Figures 2 and 3). Given challenging and technical difficulties, no attempt to revascularize the RCA was performed. A simultaneous right heart catheterization demonstrated a cardiogenic shock picture with elevated biventricular pressures and reduced cardiac output (Table 1). A marginally reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% to 45%, severe right ventricular dysfunction with RCA territory wall motion abnormality, and moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation (MR) were noted at intraoperative point-of-care cardiac ultrasound. Serum creatinine, lactic acid, and cardiac enzymes were significantly elevated (Table 2). As initial steps, inotropic and vasopressor supports were started to allow a multidisciplinary discussion to determine the most appropriate type of mechanical support, with the eventual decision to place an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP).

The patient was then transferred to the cardiac care unit, with a consequent improvement in organ perfusion markers (Table 2). Formal transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% and severe functional MR due to leaflet tethering (Videos 2 and 3). Over the next few days, vasopressor support was no longer needed, but given the severity of MR, dobutamine and IABP were unable to be weaned (Video 4). Considering the patient's multivessel coronary artery disease, lack of revascularization, and severe MR, multidisciplinary discussion among the cardiothoracic, interventional-structural, and heart failure teams was held again to determine whether to pursue coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with mitral valve replacement or staged percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER), with the final decision to pursue the latter given his risk profile. Postoperatively, IABP was successfully removed, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35% and mild-to-moderate residual MR (Video 5, Table 3).

Throughout his treatment course, the patient was also noted to have paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and variable high-degree atrioventricular block on telemetry, eventually requiring dual-lead implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement. He was discharged home in stable condition.

In a patient with acute myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, and severe MR requiring mechanical circulatory support, what factors determine the type of mechanical circulatory support device: ECMO, IABP, or Impella CP?

In patients with acute myocardial infarction and cardiogenic shock, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines do not recommend (Class 3) routine use of IABP or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), given a lack of survival benefit.1 However, in certain situations and especially in patients with mechanical complications of acute coronary syndrome, such as in the current case, mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices are beneficial for hemodynamic stabilization.1 For this patient with severe MR due to ischemic papillary muscle, we preferred IABP over ECMO or Impella CP. Although Impella CP provides better left ventricular unloading, which in turn reduces the severity of MR, it does not provide the same level of coronary perfusion compared with IABP.2 Given acute severe MR and the lack of revascularization, we opted for a combination of afterload reduction and improved perfusion with IABP. Although ECMO provides the most comprehensive support, its retrograde flow would increase afterload and exacerbate left ventricular distension and pulmonary congestion in this patient with already elevated left-sided pressures.

Additionally, randomized controlled trials have evaluated the utility of these MCS devices in various scenarios, including the IABP-SHOCK II trial, which assessed the use of IABP in patients with acute myocardial infarction and cardiogenic shock undergoing early revascularization. The investigators found no significant differences in 30-day mortality for those receiving IABP.3 However, this trial did not include patients with mechanical complications related to their acute coronary syndrome, such as MR.

The unique characteristics of this case are not an entity that has been evaluated at scale by randomized controlled trials. We believed that, given all the factors of this case, IABP would provide the patient with the most benefit in terms of hemodynamic stabilization while also providing the least risk of complication. We opted for an 8-F catheter for insertion of a 50-mL balloon, given that we had to optimize hemodynamic support while also minimizing risk. The 50-mL balloon provided ideal support, as between the time of IABP implantation and removal after M-TEER, the patient continued to have excellent organ perfusion and hemodynamic parameters. The 8-F sheath did not lead to any vascular complications in the 7 days that he was requiring IABP.

What is the incidence of acute ischemic MR after RCA infarct?

Infarction-related MR may quickly arise from either papillary muscle rupture or functional mechanisms, such as ischemic tethering of the mitral valve apparatus in the setting of ischemic cardiomyopathy, and it can cause abrupt decline in cardiac function. MR accounts for approximately 7% of cardiogenic shock postinfarction, with mortality rising to 55% if untreated.4

Papillary muscle rupture is a rare but life-threatening complication after acute myocardial infarction, which usually occurs in the first week postinfarction and especially in the first 24 hours. In the current era of widespread reperfusion therapy, the incidence of papillary muscle rupture and severe MR has declined to <1% of patients with acute myocardial infarction. Among papillary muscles, the posteromedial muscle ruptures more frequently (69% incidence), as it has a single blood supply, in contrast to the anteromedial muscle, which has a dual blood supply. The posteromedial muscle is typically perfused by the posterior descending artery (from the RCA) and less often by an obtuse marginal branch (from the LCx).4

In contrast, functional mechanism leading to left ventricular remodeling, and eventual leaflet tethering and annular dilation, are more common than papillary muscle rupture. Although most cases of leaflet tethering are seen in the setting of a remote infarction and chronic remodeling, acute instances occur as well, in which revascularization alone may be able to resolve the damage.5 In our clinical case, leaflet tethering occurred from an acute infarct, but immediate revascularization was not feasible. Consequently, management relied on medical stabilization, including MCS followed by staged revascularization and mitral valve repair.

What hemodynamic factors indicate readiness for weaning from IABP support, and how does severe MR affect this process?

Successful weaning from IABP support relies on overall improvement of hemodynamic, metabolic, and clinical parameters. Key hemodynamic stability indicators include mean arterial pressures of >70 mm Hg, heart rate of <100 beats/min, cardiac index of >2 L/min/m2, central venous pressure of <12 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of <18 mm Hg; metabolic parameters should demonstrate improvements of end-organ perfusion (lactate: <2.5 mmol/L, urine output: ≥0.5-1 mL/kg/h); and clinical assessment should confirm the absence of low-output symptoms such as altered mental status and dyspnea, and minimal vasoactive agent requirements.6

Three primary weaning strategies have been described: standby mode (abrupt discontinuation of IABP), ratio weaning (gradual reduction of IABP inflation frequency from 1:1 to 1:2 to 1:3), and volume weaning (progressive reduction of balloon inflation volume). Each approach aims to assess the heart's ability to maintain hemodynamic stability without mechanical support.6

In this patient with severe MR, IABP provided crucial left ventricular unloading and afterload reduction, improving both coronary perfusion and forward flow. Reduction or complete cessation of IABP support immediately resulted in a marked increase in the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure v-wave, reflecting worsening left atrial pressures and severe MR burden (Video 4). Despite successful revascularization, the patient remained dependent on IABP and dobutamine. It was only after M-TEER that left ventricular unloading improved and IABP was able to be removed. This highlights how, in MR-related cardiogenic shock, addressing the primary valvular pathology is essential for successful mechanical support weaning.

What factors determine the choice between CABG with concurrent mitral valve surgery or a staged strategy of PCI followed by transcatheter edge-to-edge repair?

In patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock and mechanical complications such as MR, decision-making is difficult and requires the support of a multidisciplinary heart team.1 In our patient scenario, the RCA lesion could not be crossed easily, and acute revascularization was deferred. In cases of severe MR with cardiogenic shock, urgent surgical repair or valve replacement remains the reference standard of care given the high mortality associated with conservative medical therapy.1 However, in patients deemed poor surgical candidates, transcatheter interventions such as M-TEER have emerged as promising alternatives in the acute setting, and the procedure is given a Class 2a recommendation if mitral valve anatomy is favorable.7,8

In patients who also need combined revascularization, multidisciplinary discussions are needed to understand the risks and benefits of CABG and repair compared with staged PCI and M-TEER, as studies have not documented clear patient profiles that would benefit from each respective procedure. In our scenario, the cardiothoracic surgery, heart failure cardiology, and interventional structural teams were involved in joint discussions and agreed that the patient's severe right ventricular dysfunction, in conjunction with poor targets for bypass, made surgical repair of his mitral valve with CABG a less favored option. Given these insights as well as his mitral anatomy, all teams elected to proceed with M-TEER. However, in preparation for M-TEER, the decision was made to undergo revascularization of his LAD and LCx. The choice to revascularize or repair MR is controversial given the lack of current guidelines or clinical studies to guide these decisions. Consequently, the approach is individualized and driven by clinical experience. In our center's experience with M-TEER in patients with ischemic MR and untreated coronary artery disease, favorable outcomes have been achieved when the coronary artery disease is addressed before treating MR. Additionally, we believe that performing revascularization before the intervention reduces the risk of periprocedural complications during M-TEER. Although the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction was 55%, this was in the setting of acute severe MR and severely reduced forward flow by left ventricular outflow tract assessment. Without revascularization, the patient may have struggled to tolerate the increased workload after the removal of the “pop-off” valve.

PCI was therefore performed to the LAD and LCx, and M-TEER was performed 2 days later. The patient was maintained on IABP and dobutamine between procedures, and after M-TEER, IABP was removed in the catheterization laboratory, and dobutamine was weaned over the next 12 hours. The patient's left ventricular ejection fraction on transthoracic echocardiography was 35% in the setting of MR elimination, and the mitral valve was functioning well, with a post clip gradient of 2.8 mm Hg.

When is the appropriate time to intervene for complete heart block secondary to RCA STEMI?

Despite advances in reperfusion therapy, high-degree atrioventricular block remains a clinically significant complication after STEMI, with complete heart block occurring in 3.7% of inferior/posterior myocardial infarctions and 1.0% of anterior/lateral myocardial infarctions.9 The presence of second- or third-degree atrioventricular block in STEMI is associated with significantly higher in-hospital mortality, primarily serving as a surrogate marker for larger infarct size rather than heart block itself being a direct cause of mortality.1 Inferior myocardial infarction–associated atrioventricular block is often transient and self-resolves, and thus can be treated conservatively.9

The patient's initial rhythm upon arrival to the unit was paroxysmal atrial flutter with complete heart block. Given that he remained asymptomatic, temporary pacing was not required. It is possible that maintaining him on inotropic support with dobutamine and IABP prevented any symptoms from his bradyarrhythmia, thereby avoiding pacing. Additionally, through his course with circulatory support and eventual revascularization, complete heart block improved to 2:1 atrioventricular block. Earlier guidelines do not provide an exact timeline, stating that a pacemaker is indicated after myocardial infarction if atrioventricular block is persistent for 5 to 9 days and not expected to recover.10 The 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association report advises pacemaker implantation if high-degree atrioventricular block persists beyond 72 hours despite reperfusion efforts.1 Ultimately, the decision was made for a dual ventricular pacemaker placement with persistent block 8 days after revascularization.

After acute coronary syndrome, guidelines recommend waiting at least 40 days for recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction before ICD placement.9 Given that the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction was low and he required pacemaker implantation, there was a multidisciplinary discussion to pursue prophylactic ICD instead. This practice of ICD consideration in patients after myocardial infarction with indication for permanent pacemaker is supported by guidelines.9 At the time of home discharge, the patient was hemodynamically stable and asymptomatic, with close subspecialty follow-up.
