CLIP2: a novel functional player in AQP5 trafficking dynamics and implications for Sjögren’s disease
Authors: Claudia D’Agostino, Nikola Filipovic, Egor Zindy, Louise Conrard, Zhen Wang, Stefania Moscato, Fiona Engelke, Angelica Nash, Carl Johan Hagströmer, Helin Strandberg, Dorian Parisis, Azine Datlibagi, Françoise Gregoire, Nargis Bolaky, Maxime Tarabichi, Muhammad Shahnawaz Soyfoo, Kevin L Schey, Thomas Dörner, Torsten Witte, Jason Perret, Letizia Mattii, Susanna Törnroth-Horsefield, Christine Delporte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSjögren’s disease (SjD) is an autoimmune condition affecting exocrine glands, resulting in decreased saliva and tear production. Aquaporin 5 (AQP5) is a transmembrane water channel playing a central role in water transport. In SjD, deregulation of AQP5 expression, trafficking and interactions with various proteins contribute to the underlying disease mechanisms.MethodsCo-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry, proximity ligation assay, microscale thermophoresis and in-silico modelling were carried out to investigate protein-protein interaction. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout was performed in a salivary gland cell line stably transfected with AQP5. Protein colocalization and AQP5 trafficking were studied by immunofluorescence. Autoantibodies were quantified by ELISA.ResultsWe identified CAP-Gly Domain Containing Linker Protein 2 (CLIP2) as a novel AQP5-interacting partner. Structural modeling of the AQP5-CLIP2 interaction revealed that the interaction is similar to other CAP-Gly domain complexes. Functional investigations indicated CLIP2 significantly influences AQP5 trafficking to the plasma membrane. Furthermore, interactions of CLIP2 with other AQP5 partners reveal the possible formation of multiprotein complexes in salivary glands. In SjD patients, alterations in these complexes and the presence of autoantibodies against AQP5, CLIP2, and other AQP5-interacting partners such as Ezrin and prolactin-inducible protein (PIP) may also contribute to disease pathogenesis.ConclusionOur study identifies CLIP2 as a novel player in the AQP5 interactome and sheds light on its crucial role in AQP5 trafficking. In SjD, the alterations in AQP5-CLIP2 interactions and the presence of autoantibodies against both proteins highlighted their potential as targets for future therapeutic interventions in SjD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02476-6.Keywords: Aquaporin 5, Autoantibodies, CAP-Gly Domain Containing LinkerProtein, Protein-protein interaction, Salivary gland, Sjöögren's disease

Introduction

Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a complex chronic autoimmune systemic disease primarily characterized by dysfunction of exocrine glands, including lacrimal and salivary glands. This dysfunction manifests as a sicca syndrome, characterized by reduced salivary and lacrimal secretions [1, 2]. In approximately 30% of patients, systemic manifestations may affect other organs such as the lungs, kidneys and central nervous system [3]. The disease is often diagnosed several years following its onset, and to date, patients can only benefit from palliative treatments to alleviate the exocrinopathy [4].

In salivary glands (SG), the integral transmembrane water channel aquaporin 5 (AQP5) - a member of the aquaporin (AQP) family - plays a pivotal role in water transport involved in saliva secretion [5–8]. Upon nerve stimulation, AQP5 bearing vesicles translocate to the apical membrane of secretory acinar cells (ACs), where AQP5 ensures transmembrane water flow to the acini lumen down the osmotic gradient and actively contributes to saliva formation [9, 10]. The AQP5 interactome plays a role in coordinating and regulating AQP5 translocation and function that in turn affects water transport [11]. To date, several AQP5-interacting proteins have been identified [11, 12], including the Transient Receptor Potential Cation Channel Subfamily V Member 4 (TRPV4; [13]), anion exchanger 2 (AE2) and Na-K-Cl cotransporter 1 (NKCC1) [14], Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC; [15], prolactin-inducible protein (PIP; [16, 17]), Ezrin [16, 18, 19] and junctional proteins [20]. However, the molecular basis of these protein-protein interactions and their specific roles in AQP5 translocation remain largely unknown. In SG ACs from SjD patients, alterations in the expression and/or subcellular localization of AQP5 [5, 21–23] and/or AQP5-interacting proteins [5, 16, 24] may contribute to SjD pathogenesis. Our previous studies have shown modified distribution of AQP5-PIP interactions in SG ACs from SjD (mostly cytoplasmic) compared to controls (mainly at the apical plasma membrane) with no significant modification in the number of AQP5-PIP interactions [24]. In addition, we have detected significant decrease in the number of AQP5-Ezrin interactions and altered distribution of these interactions in SG ACs from SjD (mostly absent or localized at the basolateral side) compared to control (apical region) [16]. In addition, both PIP and Ezrin expression were reduced in SG ACs in SjD [16, 24]. These studies suggest that the modified expression of PIP and Ezrin (the latter acting as a linker between the cytoplasmic membrane and the cytoskeleton) may account for the altered distribution of AQP5 in ACs of human minor salivary gland biopsies (hMSGB) from SjD patients [16, 24]. Considering the limited knowledge of the AQP5 interactome [11, 12], it remains essential to pursue its study to gain further insight in the regulation of AQP5 trafficking. This will allow a deeper understanding of the deregulation of AQP5 translocation in the pathogenesis of SjD and hopefully offer new targets for therapeutic intervention. In this work, we aim to address this research gap by identifying novel AQP5 interaction partners, their interactions with some previously identified AQP5-interacting proteins, their roles in AQP5 trafficking as well as their possible involvement in SjD.

We identified CAP-Gly Domain Containing Linker Protein 2 (CLIP2, also known as CLIP115) as a new AQP5-interacting partner. CLIP2 is a member of the cytoplasmic linker protein family (CLIP) that mediates interactions between the microtubule and organelles and has been proposed to play essential roles in microtubule-dependent processes such as vesicular trafficking. CLIPs share a common structural feature consisting of two remarkably conserved cytoskeleton-associated protein glycine-rich (CAP-Gly)- or microtubule binding (MTB)-domains (Supplementary Fig. S1) that acts as the binding site for microtubule as well as other proteins [25, 26]. The direct interaction between AQP5 and the CLIP2 MTB-domains was confirmed and quantified in vitro by microscale thermophoresis (MST) using recombinant protein and computer modelling of the AQP5-CLIP2 complex suggests that the AQP5 C-terminus binds between the two MTB-domains in a manner that resembles how CAP-Gly domains bind other proteins [27–29]. We revealed the functional impact of CLIP2 on AQP5 trafficking, whereby CLIP2 impairment results in a notable reduction in induced-AQP5 trafficking to the plasma membrane, providing comprehensive insights into its role. Furthermore, we showed that CLIP2 also interacts with previously identified protein partners of AQP5 such as PIP and Ezrin [16, 24] suggesting the possible existence of multiprotein complexes involving CLIP2, AQP5, PIP, and Ezrin and the perturbation of such complexes and CLIP2 in SjD SGs. Lastly, we also identified autoantibodies against AQP5, CLIP2, Ezrin, and PIP in SjD patients, as well as their associations with clinical features. The latter suggests the potential for novel biomarkers for the diagnosis of SjD, especially in cases where the classical panel is not sufficiently informative with respect to the diagnosis of underlying SjD.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Normal SG-SV40 transformed squamous cells resembling acinar cells (referred to as NS-SV-AC) were provided by Prof. Azuma (Second Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Tokushima University School of Dentistry) [30]. These cells were grown in DMEM/F12 supplemented with inactivated 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin, 4mM of L-glutamine and 1mM of sodium pyruvate (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). The cultures were maintained in 5% CO2 balanced air at 37 °C. Cells were certified free of mycoplasma contamination using the LookOut Mycoplasma PCR kit (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). The identity of the cell line was confirmed by Short tandem repeat (STR) DNA profiling (European Collection for Authenticated Cell cultures, Public Health England, England).

Human minor salivary gland biopsies

Paraffin-embedded human minor salivary gland biopsies (hMSGB) from SjD patients (SICCA-SD; meeting the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria for SjD [31]) and from patients with non-specific sialadenitis (SICCA-NS; used as negative controls) were obtained from the Erasme Hospital Biobank (Brussels, Belgium; BE_BERA1; Biobanque Hôpital Erasme-ULB (BERA); BE_NBWB1; Biothèque Wallonie Bruxelles (BWB); BBMRI-ERIC). The paraffin-embedded hMSGB were sectioned by Diapath (part of the Center for Microscopy and Molecular Imaging (CMMI)). The study was approved by the ULB Erasme Hospital ethics committee (P2020/351), with no patients opposing to the use of the human residual clinical material for research purposes, and no need for informed consent in compliance with Belgian law.

Plasmid constructs and cell transfection

A plasmid expressing SNAP-hAQP5 was designed to contain a SNAP-tag (a small protein derived from mammalian O6-alkylguanine-DNA-alkyltransferase, with a molecular weight of approximately 19kDa [32]), attached at the N-terminus of the complete human AQP5 (hAQP5) sequence, as previously described [33]. For the CRISPR/CAS9 approach, dual guide RNAs (dual gRNAs), were designed in silico to target the NM_003388.4 exon 10 of hsCLIP2 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Two pairs of dual gRNAs guides, each pair targeting the same DNA site, having a high fraction of efficacy (VectorBuilder gRNA collection) were used. Each dual gRNA pair was expressed from a single modified CRISPR/CAS9 plasmid, where Cas9 is a nickase mutant (hCas9-D10A) (VectorBuilder, CA, USA). The hCas9-D10A mutant nickase enzyme used in conjunction with two gRNAs targeting the two opposite strands of a specific site will generate a single-strand cut on both strands, resulting in double-strand breaks (DSBs) at the target site. This approach minimizes off-target effects associated with CRISPR/Cas9 expression, as DSBs are only produced when both gRNAs effectively target the designated site.

All plasmid DNAs were isolated from transformed recombinant Escherichia coli cultures using the E.Z.N.A Maxiprep Plasmid Purification DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). NS-SV-AC cells were transfected by electroporation (parameters set to 270 V and 700µF) using a Gene Pulser II System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Cells were resuspended in Ingenio solution (Madison, WI, USA) and 5 µg of the SNAP-hAQP5 plasmid, 8 µg of a plasmid encoding human Prolactin-inducible protein (hPIP) (pEZ-M94 vector, GeneCopoeia Rockville, MD, USA), or 2.5 µg of each CRISPR/CAS9 CLIP2 dual gRNA vectors were added. Stable transfection of NS-SV-AC cells with SNAP-hAQP5 or CRISPR/CAS9 CLIP2 dual gRNA vectors was achieved through selection with respectively 5 µg/ml of puromycin or 10 µg/ml of blasticidin (InVivo Gen, San Diego, CA, USA).

Amplicon sequence analyses

CRISPR/CAS9 deletions were objectivized by analysis of PCR products amplified with primers flanking human CLIP2 genomic targets (gRNA1, gRNA2, gRNA3 and gRNA4): hsClip2_QC-A_Cas9-1 F (forward) GGTGAAATCGAGGAGCTCCA; hsClip2_QC-A_Cas9-1R (reverse) ATTAGCCCCATGTTCTCGCT; hsClip2_QC-B_Cas9-1 F (forward) GGAGGTGGACAAGCTCCG; hsClip2_QC-B_Cas9-1R (reverse) CTCCTCCTGGGCCTCCTG.

To perform amplicon sequence analysis, we ran CRISPResso version 2.2.14 on the compressed fastq files for the paired reads R1 and R2 (parameters – fastq R1 and fastq R2), providing our amplicon as input with the parameter - amplicon_seq, and with other parameters set as default. We ran it separately on both our control and engineered cells.

As an independent custom analysis, we also aligned both the control and engineered sequences to the reference genome hg38 with bwa v0.7.17-r1188 mem, and parameters -w 100 -A 1 -B 4 -O 1 -E 0 -L 5, which set the penalization to zero for long indels. We then sorted and indexed the BAM files with Samtools v1.17, after keeping properly aligned pairs with the flag -f 0 × 2, and then counted the number of read pairs in the following categories:{perfect matching, soft clipped, deletions or insertions} in the region of our amplicon chr7:74375950–74376230.

In the hCLIP2-KO vs. the hCLIP2 WT cells, amplicon sequence analysis showed 83.0% vs. 99.5% aligned reads (12,175,415; 8,193,729 reads); 54.5% vs. 36.5% modified reads; 49.2% vs. 3.1% deletions in modified reads; 70.0% vs. 97.9% substitution in modified reads. These results indicate that at least around 25% of reads have a deletion in the engineered cell lines, which do not appear in control. Since CRISPResso2 considers read pairs, this would mean between 25% of edited cells had both alleles hit or 50% of edited cells had only one allele hit. However, there is a substantial difference in the percentages of unaligned reads which is still unaccounted for. While 98.4% of all read pairs aligning in the amplicon region were perfect matches to the reference in the control, only 53.1% fragments were perfect matches in the engineered cells, and thus 46.9% showed some kind of editing. In our control, the percentage of read pairs with a deletion over the total number of read pairs with either a deletion or a perfect match was low (1.48%); in contrast, the hCLIP2-KO cells showed 24.24% read pairs with a deletion, similar to the percentage seen in CRISPResso2. However, a large fraction of soft-clipped reads was also seen in the amplicon region (42.54% vs. 0.14% in the control), which would indicate an additional fraction of fragments were impacted by the construct and not necessarily translating in deletion after alignment using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment algorithm. Thus, around 47% of DNA fragments could have had some events that led to either deletions or soft clipping in the alignment data in the engineered cells, which were not seen in the control cells.

Immunoprecipitation and Western blotting (WB)

Total proteins from mouse parotid gland (mPG), mouse submandibular gland (mSMG), SNAP-hAQP5 NS-SV-AC cells, and HA-hAQP5 NS-SV-AC cells [24] were extracted as previously described using as lysis buffer consisting of 180 mM Tris, 0.1 μm CaCl2, 0.8 mM MgCl2, 0.01% SDS and 0.05% sodium deoxycholate pH 7.2 containing either 0.1% Triton X-100 [24]; or 0.1% NP-40; 0.1% Tween 20, 0.1% CHAPS and 0.05% NP-40; 0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.05% NP-40. Mouse salivary glands were obtained from C57Bl6 mice (Janvier-Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) with all animal care and experiments approved by the local Animal and Welfare Committee (Protocol 489 N) and carried out in agreement with the local and national guidelines. For immunoprecipitation, 800 µg total proteins from mouse salivary glands or 500 µg of total proteins from either SNAP-hAQP5 NS-SV-AC cells or HA-hAQP5 NS-SV-AC cells [24] were incubated overnight at 4 °C under rotary mixing without (negative control) or with 1 µl of rabbit anti-AQP5 antibody (#AB15858, EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Then, protein A-coated Sepharose beads (20 µl for mouse SGs, 25 µl for cells) were added to the mixtures that were further incubated for 1 h at 4 °C under rotary agitation. Additional negative controls were performed in the absence of total proteins. The beads were washed three times with protein extraction buffer, incubated for 30 min in Laemmli buffer containing 10 mg/mL dithiothreitol (20 µl for mouse SGs, 25 µl for cells) and centrifuged at 17,000 g for 5 min at room temperature (RT) to collect the supernatant containing the eluted immunoprecipitated proteins. Both the immunoprecipitated proteins and total input proteins (used as positive controls) were separated by electrophoresis on an 8% SDS-PAGE Tris-Glycine gel and then electrotransferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Additionally, the expression of hCLIP2 in NS-SV-AC cells was assessed via WB analysis. To this end, 20 µg of proteins underwent electrophoresis on an 8% SDS-PAGE Tris-Glycine gel before being transferred to a PVDF membrane. The PVDF membranes were blocked for 1 h at RT with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBS-T) and 5% skimmed milk, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-CLIP2 antibody (1/1000, #25088-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), rabbit anti-CLIP2 (1/1000, HPA020430, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MI, USA), rabbit anti-AQP5 antibodies (1/1000, AB15858, Merck-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), mouse anti-β-actin (1/2000, #MAB1501, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The membranes were washed three times with PBS-T and incubated for 1 h at RT with goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated diluted in PBS-T containing 5% skimmed milk (1/3000; #7074, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) or goat anti-mouse StarBright blue 700 diluted in TBS containing 1% BSA containing 5% skimmed milk (1/2500, #12004158, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). Finally, when using secondary antibodies coupled to HRP, the membranes were incubated for 5 min at RT with ECL detection reagent, and chemiluminescent detection was performed using X-OMAT blue films (mouse SG samples; Kodak, Rochester, NY, USA) or Amersham Imager 600 (cell samples; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA). When using secondary antibodies coupled to fluorophore, fluorescence was detected using an Amersham Imager 600 (cell samples; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA). Densitometric analysis of WB images was performed with the FIJI ImageJ software using integrated density and background removal [34].

Mass spectrometry analysis of immunoprecipitated proteins

Mouse parotid and submandibular SG immunoprecipitated proteins were loaded onto a 4–12% NuPAGE Novex Bis-Tris gel (ThermoFisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) and allowed to run into the gel for 1.5–2 cm. The gel was stained with GelCode Blue Stain Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) and the stained area was excised for in-gel digestion. In-gel digestion was performed as previously described [24, 35]. Tryptic peptides were separated on a one-dimensional fused silica capillary column (200 mm × 100 μm) packed with Phenomenex Jupiter resin (3 μm mean particle size, 300 Å pore size). One-dimensional liquid chromatography was performed using the following gradient at a flow rate of 0.5 µL/min: 0–2 min: 2% ACN (0.1% formic acid), 2–75 min: 2–35% ACN (0.1% formic acid), 75–95 min: 35–95% ACN (0.1% formic acid) balanced with 0.1% formic acid. The eluate was directly infused into an LTQ Velos Pro mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) equipped with a nanoelectrospray source. The instruments were operated in a 15-step data dependent mode with one precursor scan event to identify the top 15 most abundant ions in each MS scan, which were then selected for fragmentation. Dynamic exclusion (repeat count 1, exclusion list size 300, and exclusion duration 15 s) was enabled to allow identification of less abundant ions. The raw data were searched using the TagRecon algorithm [36] against a Uniprot mouse database (Jan, 2017) with a static modification of carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues, variable modifications of oxidation of methionine and maximum two missed cleavage sites. The search results were filtered by IDPicker [37] by controlling protein false-discovery rate (FDR) to less than 1%. Spectral counts for each protein detected with two or more unique peptides among control and AQP5 immunoprecipitation samples were analyzed by CANVS to determine significance [38].

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well on Millicell EZ Slide 8-well glass (0.7 cm²/well) (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and fixed with methanol for 15 min at −20°C. The hMSGB sections were deparaffined. PLA was performed using Duolink kit (#DUO9210, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MI, USA; according to manufacturer’s protocol) with a set of two primary antibodies selected from the following: goat anti-AQP5 (1/100; produced by goat immunization using a 26 amino acid peptide corresponding to the 25 C-terminal amino acids of hAQP5 with an additional cysteine residue on the N-terminal for coupling to the keyhole limpet hemocyanin carrier protein; Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium), rabbit anti-CLIP2 (1/200; #25088-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), mouse anti-PIP (1/100, #ab218935, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and mouse anti-Ezrin (1/100; #MA5-13862, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Negative controls were performed in the absence of one or both primary antibodies. After completing the PLA, the samples were mounted using Duolink in Situ Mounting Medium with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Louis, MI, USA). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Axio Observer LSM-710) with a x63/1.4 Plan Apochromat lens (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and processed as previously described [24]. Data were expressed as the number of positive spots (indicative of protein-protein interaction) per number of ACs (regions of interest (ROIs)), determined by DAPI counterstaining that labels cell nuclei.

AQP5-trafficking

hCLIP2 KO-SNAP-hAQP5- and SNAP-hAQP5- (positive control) transfected cells were preincubated for 24 h with 50µM indomethacin (#I7378, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MI, USA) prior to subsequent incubation for 8 h in the absence or presence of either 10µM forskolin (FK) (#1099, Tocris, Bristol, UK), 0.1µM thapsigargin (TH) (#1138, Tocris, Bristol, UK) or a combination of both. Following stimulation, the cells were fixed with 0.8% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at RT, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 min, and blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (#AB_2337258, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at RT. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary goat anti-AQP5 antibody (1/100, Eurogentec Seraing Belgium; see above) followed by a 1 h incubation at 4 °C with secondary Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated donkey anti-Goat IgG (1/500, #A32758, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Nuclei were labelled using 1 µg/ml DAPI. Confocal microscopy images were acquired using a 63x/1.4 Plan Apochromat objective on an Axio Observer LSM-710 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Quantification of AQP5 labeling at the cell plasma perimembrane area was performed as previously described [33].

Double Immunofluorescence (IF) and image analysis

Paraffin-embedded hMSGB sections were deparaffined, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 6. Thereafter, hMSGB sections were washed in PBS, incubated for 1 h at RT in PBS containing 10% donkey serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with two primary antibodies (among goat anti-AQP5 (1/100; Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium), rabbit anti-CLIP2 (1/200; #25088-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), mouse anti-PIP (1/100, #ab218935, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and mouse anti-Ezrin (1/100; #MA5-13862, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Then, hMSGB sections were incubated for 1 h at RT with appropriate secondary antibodies among donkey anti-goat (1/500 Alexa Fluor™ Plus 594, #A32758, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), goat anti-rabbit (1/1000 Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate, #4412, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) and goat anti-mouse (1/500 Alexa Fluor™ 594, #A-11005, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To perform double immunofluorescence of AQP5 and CLIP2, secondary antibodies (donkey-anti goat for AQP5 and goat anti-rabbit for CLIP2) were sequentially applied in the indicated order for 1 h, with three PBS washes in between to prevent cross-reactivity. Samples were counterstained with 1 µg/mL of DAPI and mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Immunofluorescent labelling of AQP5, CLIP2, PIP and Ezrin was quantified from images captured at 20X magnification using a Leica DM 2000 microscope equipped with fluorite plan objective and DFC425C Digital 5 Megapixel color microscope camera (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) set to monochrome acquisition, using multichannel imaging, and exposure settings that avoid pixel saturation. The image resolution was optimized by increased DPI and adjusting contrast levels to enhance visual clarity. Two microscopic fields, generally covering the entire tissue section, were analyzed for each sample. Semi-quantitative image analysis was conducted using CellSens Imaging Software (Olympus, Düsseldorf, Germany), as previously described [24]. Specifically, the color threshold was determined based on negative controls, and the percentage of the reacting area in gland parenchymal tissue was assessed for each antigen, excluding the stromal compartment, including the infiltrate. Moreover, AQP5/CLIP2, CLIP2/PIP, CLIP2/Ezrin and PIP/Ezrin co-localizations were evaluated in images that had their intensity values stretched in a consistent manner across all analyzed samples. This adjustment facilitated the identification of the yellow area, indicating red and green co-localization. The semi-quantification of AQP5/CLIP2, CLIP2/PIP, CLIP2/Ezrin and PIP/Ezrin co-localization was expressed as the percentage of yellow area in gland parenchymal tissue.

Quantification of autoantibodies

IgG and IgA autoantibodies against the full-length hAQP5 (recombinantly produced in Pichia pastoris; see below), CLIP2 (residues 69–304 corresponding to MTB-domains plus connecting linker, recombinantly produced in Escherichia coli; see below), Ezrin (#MBS954384, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA) and PIP (#abx167865, Abbexa LTD, Cambridge, UK), were examined in patients with SjD (n = 347; recruited in Hannover, Berlin and Udine), and in healthy controls (HC; n = 123) using Luminex antigen array as previously described [39]. The autoantibody study was approved by the local ethics committee (Vote Hannover Medical School ethical committee No. 5582; Berlin ethical committee agreement EA1/002/16; Udine ethical committee agreement CEUR-2017-Os-027-ASUIUD). Briefly, antigens were covalently coupled to carboxylated color-coded beads (#MC10021-01; #MC10072-01; #MC10067-01; #MC10025-01; Luminex® MagPlex® microspheres, Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA). In addition, the AQP5 fusion protein which was used in a recent publication [40] and which contains the extracellular loop peptide amino acids 190-265 (human AQP5 Ag14514, Proteintech, Planegg-Martinsried, Germany) was bound to Luminex beads as above, and IgG antibodies against this antigen were measured in all 123 HC and in 223 patients with SjD.

The antigen-loaded beads were then incubated with human serum samples diluted 1/100 in assay buffer (PBS, 0.5% BSA, 50% Low-Cross buffer (#100500; Candor Biosciences, Wangen, Germany) and subsequently incubated with 5 mg/ml secondary phycoerythrin-labeled goat anti-human IgG/IgA antibody (#109–116−098 for IgG; #109–115−011 for IgA; Dianova, Hamburg, Germany), after three washing steps with PBS/0.05% Tween 20. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) representing IgG/IgA reactivity was determined by Luminex 200 instrument (Luminex Corporation, Austin, Texas). Antigen profile in SjD and HC were compared by Fisher’s exact test using R (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The percentage of positive classified SjD patients were defined by 98% quantile of the intensity values of HC as cut-off. Spearman correlation tests were performed to evaluate potential associations, determined by R, between clinical characteristics from SjD patients recruited in Hanover and Berlin and antigen targets. Patient characteristics of combined Hannover and Berlin cohorts are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Recombinant production of AQP5 and CLIP2

Full-length hAQP5 was recombinantly expressed in Pichia pastoris and purified as previously described with the exception that the alkali membrane wash step was replaced by washing in membrane buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 20mM NaCl, 10% glycerol) [41]. Following purification and analysis of the fractions using SDS-PAGE, AQP5-containing fractions were pooled, concentrated and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen until further use.

The hAQP5 C-terminus (residues 228–265) were expressed and purified as a GST-fusion protein in Escherichia coli as previously described [18]. The GST-tag was cleaved off using thrombin and the hAQP5 C-terminus was isolated by running the sample through a Vivaspin 6 concentrator with 10,000 kDa MWCO (Cytiva Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) and collecting the flowthrough.

The codon-optimized gene for human CLIP2 MTB1 and MTB2-domains with the joining linker (Genscript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) was cloned into pET15b using the NdeI/BamHI restriction sites for expression in Escherichia coli. The final CLIP2-construct contained amino acid residues 69–304 with an N-terminal His-tag followed by a Thrombin cleavage site. In addition, constructs for the individual MTB-domains (residues 73–148 for CLIP2-MTB1 and 214–288 for CLIP2-MTB2) were created in the same manner. The plasmids were transformed into Ca2+-competent BL21 Escherichia coli for expression under the T7 promotor. A 5 ml LB-culture supplemented with 100 µg/ml ampicillin was inoculated with a small amount of the glycerol stock culture and grown overnight at 37 °C. The overnight culture was added to a 2 L baffled flask containing 1 L LB + 100 µg/ml ampicillin and grown at 37 °C with 200 rpm shaking. At OD600 ± 0.8 (approximately 3 h), isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 1mM to induce expression. After 3 h, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 15,000xg for 15 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 ml lysis buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 100mM NaCl, 10mM ß-mercaptoethanol (MeOH)) supplemented with one cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (#5892791001, Roche, Bazel, Switzerland) and the cells were broken by sonication on ice (8 × 30 s with 30 s waiting in between). Cellular debris were spun down at 45,000xg for 45 min and the supernatant was injected on a 5 ml His-Trap column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) that had been equilibrated with lysis buffer. After washing the column with lysis buffer + 75mM imidazole, proteins were eluted using lysis buffer + 300mM imidazole. The fractions were analyzed using SDS-PAGE, pooled and concentrated using a Vivaspin 6 concentrator (10 kDa cutoff). The sample was injected on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL-column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) equilibrated with lysis buffer and fractions were collected and analyzed using SDS-PAGE. Fractions containing pure CLIP2 were pooled, concentrated as above, aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Microscale thermophoresis

CLIP2 was fluorescently labelled with cysteine reactive C5 maleimide-Alexa 488 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To prevent cysteine bridges, Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP, 0.5mM final concentration) was added to 100µM CLIP2 and the sample was incubated for 15 min at RT. Alexa-488 and CLIP2 were mixed at a 10:1 molar ratio and incubated on a tipping table at 4 °C overnight, while being protected from light using aluminum foil. The following day, the sample was added to a PD10 desalting column equilibrated with 20mM Tris (pH8), 100mM NaCl and 0.5mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to separate labelled CLIP2 from free Alexa488. The eluted CLIP2-Alexa488 was concentrated, aliquoted and stored at −20 °C or used directly.

For the MST-experiment, a 1:1 dilution series of hAQP5 was made in MST buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100mM NaCl, 0.4% n-Nonyl-Beta-D-Glucopyranoside (NG)) resulting in 12 samples between 0.0068 and 7.0 µM. The samples were mixed with equal volumes of 109 nM labelled CLIP2 (diluted in MST-buffer) and transferred to premium-coated MST-capillaries. MST-traces were recorded in a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany) with the LED and MST-power set to 20% and 80% respectively. MST-experiments with CLIP2-MTB1 and CLIP2-MTB2 were carried out in the same manner but with an hAQP5 concentration between 0.0079 and 16 µM and the LED and MST power were set to 20% and 60% respectively. The MST-experiments with the isolated hAQP5 C-terminus were performed using regular MST capillaries, a concentration range of 0.0059 and 12 µM and with the LED and MST power were set to 20% and 80%.

The MST data from three individually prepared dilution series were merged and analyzed using the MO Affinity analysis software. For the full-length hAQP5 and the hAQP5 C-terminus, the data was normalized to generate values for fractional occupancy (fraction bound). Curve fitting was performed by analyzing the data in GraphPad Prism v10 (GraphPad Software LLC). The fitting process utilized the equation for one-to-one specific binding (Y = Bmax*X/(Kd + X)).

In silico modelling

The docking simulations were performed using the AlphaFold Multimer option in AlphaFold v2.3.1 [42]. The best docking results were obtained using only the C-terminal tail of hAQP5 (residues 226–265) and the two MTB-domains of human CLIP2 (residues 77–149 and 222–249). Initial results using the full-length sequence of hAQP5 showed an interaction strongly influenced by previously solved AQP structures, particularly a rigid conformation of the proximal C-terminal region. However, isolating the C-terminal domain of hAQP5 allowed for a docking model without bias from the training dataset, resulting in a subset of predictions in which the hAQP5 C-terminal domain, including the proximal region, with high probability was predicted to play a role in the interaction with CLIP2. The model was analyzed in PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC) and the electrostatic surfaces were calculated using the Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS) electrostatics plugin.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA, USA). One-tailed Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction and one-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test (more robust tests to detect an increase or decrease in the parameter than two-tailed tests) and Kruskal-Wallis test with post-hoc Dunn tests. Data are considered significant at p ≤ 0.05. Results are expressed as the mean ± S.D. (standard deviation) or as the median with interquartile range of n samples for data.

Results

Identification of CLIP2 as a novel AQP5-interacting protein partner

AQP5 co-immunoprecipitation combined with mass spectrometry analysis was used to detect potential novel members of the AQP5 interactome. Analysis of various detergent conditions produced six samples where AQP5 was successfully immunoprecipitated (Supplementary Table S2). Statistical analysis by CANVS [38] revealed CLIP2 as novel member of the AQP5 interactome in mouse parotid gland (mPG) (p value = 0.048) along with SLC12A2 (Supplementary Table S2). Although the presence of CLIP2 in mouse submandibular gland (mSMG) (Fig. 1A, B) samples did not reach significance, spectral count data showed its presence in five out of six samples and its absence in control samples (Supplementary Table S2). In total, 12 CLIP2 peptides were identified in co-immunoprecipitation samples (Supplementary Fig. S3). The AQP5-CLIP2 interaction was confirmed by WB analysis of AQP5 co-immunoprecipitated proteins using anti-CLIP2 antibody in mSMG and mPG (Fig. 1C, lanes C and D). WB analysis of AQP5 co-immunoprecipitated proteins using anti-AQP5 confirmed the antibodies were suitable for immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. S4). CLIP2 also co-immunoprecipitated with AQP5 in human salivary gland NS-SV-AC cell line stably expressing HA-hAQP5 (HA was used as another tag) or SNAP-hAQP5 (as native cells lack AQP5 expression; Fig. 1D, lanes C and E). As positive controls, CLIP2 was consistently detected in the input proteins from both mSMG and mPG (Fig. 1C, lanes A and B), as well as from NS-SV-AC stably expressing HA-hAQP5 or SNAP-hAQP5 (Fig. 1D, lanes A and B) indicating the tag type does not influence the hAQP5-hCLIP2 interaction and further strengthening the fact that the N terminal end of hAQP5 is not involved in the interaction with hCLIP2. Conversely, no CLIP2-immunoreactive band was detected in samples when the co-immunoprecipitation was conducted in the absence of anti-AQP5 antibodies (Fig. 1C, lanes E and F; Fig. 1D, lanes D and F) or input proteins (Fig. 1C, lane G; Fig. 1D, lane G). Based on both the detection of CLIP2 among the AQP5 co-immunoprecipitated proteins using various detergent conditions, as well as one of its known functions to participate in intracellular organelle and vesicle transport, CLIP2 was selected as a promising candidate for further characterization and possible involvement in AQP5 trafficking.

The interaction between AQP5 and CLIP2 was further characterized, validated, and quantified by MST, using recombinantly expressed full-length AQP5 and CLIP2. The CLIP2-construct consisted of the two MTB-domains and the serine-rich linker in between, as the MTB-domains have been shown to be responsible for mediating protein-protein interactions [27–29]. Therefore, we also made constructs of the individual CLIP2 MTB-domains (MTB1 and MTB2). For the MST-experiment, a 1:1 dilution series of AQP5 was mixed with an equal volume of CLIP2 that had been fluorescently labelled with Alexa Fluor-488 and MST-traces were recorded. Analysis of the data for CLIP2 with both MTB domains resulted in a binding curve which was best described by a one-to-one binding model with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 106 ± 17.7 nM (Fig. 2A). In contrast, binding could not be observed in the MST-experiments with the individual MTB-domains, suggesting that both MTB-domains are necessary for the interaction to occur (Fig. 2B).

Previously, we have showed that the interaction between AQP5 and proteins involved in its trafficking is mediated by the AQP5 C-terminus [18, 24, 33], a common site for regulatory AQP protein-protein interactions [43]. To explore if this is also the case for the interaction between AQP5 and CLIP2, we repeated the MST-experiment using an AQP5 C-terminal peptide. This resulted in a binding curve that could be fitted to one-to-one binding model with an estimated Kd of 467 ± 248nM which is similar to what is observed for full-length AQP5 (p = 0.128) (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Although the certainty of the Kd value is reduced due to a lower signal-to-noise level in the MST data, this supports the involvement of the AQP5 C-terminus in the interaction with CLIP2.

In silico modelling of the AQP5-CLIP2 interaction

To further investigate the interaction between AQP5 and CLIP2, we performed in silico modelling of the AQP5-CLIP2 complex using AlphaFold-Multimer (AlphaFold v 2.3.1 [42]),. Initial attempts to use full-length AQP5 failed to produce a reliable docking model. We have previously observed that the predicted structure of the AQP2 and AQP5 C-termini by AlphaFold is likely to limit the residues available for complex formation with the Ezrin FERM-domain due to a lack of flexibility [18]. This is likely due to bias from the training data set in which crystal structures of mammalian AQPs most often display a rigid conformation of the proximal part of the C-terminus. The AQP5 C-terminus is likely to be disordered beyond residue 240 as predicted by the PrDOS server [44] (Supplementary Fig. S5B) and this is also supported by crystal structures of human AQP5 in which structural information for this region is largely absent [41, 45]. Therefore, docking of the isolated AQP5 C-terminal domain (residues 226–265) to CLIP2 was performed. The best result (as estimated by predicted local distance difference test, pLDDT) was obtained by docking the AQP5 C-terminal domain to the two CLIP2 MTB-domains without the joining linker (residues 77–149 and 222–249) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). In this model, the AQP5 C-terminus binds between the two MTB-domains arranged in an antiparallel manner (Fig. 3A). Specifically, residues 243–248 of AQP5 contain an acidic stretch that binds in a basic groove on MTB1 lined by Asn88 and Lys91, Lys119 and Lys144 (Supplementary Fig. S6). In addition, hydrophobic residues on either side of the acidic stretch (Ile238 and Tyr 43 on the proximal side and Trp249 on the distal side) bind in equivalent hydrophobic pockets on both MTB1 (Phe103, Trp108, Val124) and MTB2 (Phe243, Trp248, Val264) (Fig. 3B). The predicted AQP5-binding site on MTB1 is highly conserved within CAP-Gly domains and structural studies have shown it to be responsible for the interactions between CLIP1 MTB1 and SLAIN motif family member 2 (SLAIN2) [27], between CLIP1 MTB2 and tubulin [28], as well as between the dynactin large subunit p150Glued and end-binding protein 1 (EB1) [29]. Comparison of the binding sites of the modelled AQP5-CLIP2 complex with the CLIP1-SLAIN and CLIP1-tubulin experimental structures reveals distinct similarities, specifically the involvement of Asn67 in CLIP1-SLAIN, the corresponding tubulin acidic stretch in CLIP1-tubulin and the hydrophobic patch consisting of F82/F236 and W87/W241 in both (Fig. 3C and D). Taken together this suggests that CLIP2 binds AQP5 in a similar binding mode as observed in other CAP-Gly complexes.

Validation of Crispr-Cas9-mediated knockout of hCLIP2

To determine the role of hCLIP2 in the stimulation-dependent trafficking of AQP5 to the plasma membrane, a CRISPR/CAS9 gene knockdown approach was used on hCLIP2 gene using two dual gRNAs, designed to selectively target all isoforms of hCLIP2, focusing on the shared exon 10 for variant 1 and exon 9 for variant 2. After Illumina sequencing of the amplicons, we ran CRISPResso2 and a custom pipeline to analyze the data (see detailed analysis results under Material and Methods). Altogether, this revealed between 47% and 94% edited cells depending on whether the hits were biallelic or monoallelic, respectively. In addition, the hCLIP2 disruption in hCLIP2-KO cells was validated by WB and PCR and did not modify the expression of hAQP5 (Supplementary Fig. S7). Indeed, compared to CLIP2 WT cells, densitometric analysis of WB images indicated a reduction of the relative CLIP2 expression in CLIP2 KO cells (77%), with no noticeable modification in AQP5 expression (97%).

Functional involvement of hCLIP2 in AQP5 trafficking

We then investigated the effect of hCLIP2-KO on the induced AQP5 trafficking (Fig. 4). AQP5 trafficking can be induced by both forskolin (FK) [33, 46], inducing cAMP levels by activating adenylyl cyclase, and thapsigargin (TH) [33, 47, 48], inducing intracellular calcium increase by antagonizing sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) pump from the endoplasmic reticulum). Indomethacin (INDO) can set al.l cells to a similar basal intracellular level of cAMP and calcium by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis and prostaglandins-induced cAMP and calcium increase. Therefore, cells were subjected to 24 h preincubation with 50µM INDO prior to their incubation for 8 h without or with 10 µM FK, 0.1 µM TH, or a combination of both (FK + TH). In the hCLIP2 WT cells, there was an observable increase in AQP5 at the plasma perimembrane area induced by FK, TH and FK + TH (Fig. 4B). In hCLIP2-KO cells, the presence of AQP5 at the plasma perimembrane area was slightly higher than in hCLIP2 WT cells in unstimulated cells (CTRL). In addition, in hCLIP2-KO cells treated with FK, TH or FK + TH, there was a significant decrease in AQP5 at the plasma perimembrane area compared to the unstimulated cells (CTRL), as well as compared to the corresponding stimulated conditions in hCLIP2 WT cells (Fig. 4B). Our data therefore strongly supports the involvement of CLIP2 in AQP5 trafficking.

Alteration of CLIP2-AQP5 interaction in SjD hMSGB

The existence of AQP5-CLIP2 complexes was shown by PLA in NS-SV-AC cells expressing SNAP-AQP5. The number of red fluorescent spots, indicative of AQP5-CLIP2 complexes, was very high and evenly distributed within the cells, while they were very sparse in the negative control (Fig. 5A). The limited resolution of the acquired images using a 63x/NA 1.4 objective, which provides an axial resolution of approximately 700 nm at 594 nm, may account for cytoplasmic spots that sometimes appear to be located within the nucleus on single optical sections with maximal intensity projection. However, individual optical section examination shows only a few spots present in the same focal plane as the nucleus, most likely due to limited axial resolution, while most spots are located in sections either above or below the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S8). Both the AQP5-CLIP2 interaction and co-localization were then examined using PLA and double immunofluorescence, respectively, in paraffin-embedded hMSGB from SjD patients (SICCA-SD) and control patients with non-specific sialadenitis (SICCA-NS), both presenting sicca symptoms. The red fluorescent spots of PLA were dispersed within the ACs (ROIs) as well as at the apical plasma membrane from SICCA-NS hMSGB while predominantly localized at the basal membrane of the ACs from SICCA‐SD hMSGB (Fig. 5B). The number of AQP5‐CLIP2 complexes was significantly decreased in ACs from SICCA‐SD hMSGB compared to SICCA‐NS hMSGB (p = 0.032; Fig. 5C). Using double immunofluorescence, AQP5 was mostly expressed at the apical-lateral membranes of ACs in SICCA-NS hMSGB (red, Fig. 5D). In contrast, the AQP5 expression was decreased at the apical membrane of ACs in SICCA-SD hMSGB. Moreover, the AQP5 expression area (Fig. 5E) was significantly decreased in ACs from SICCA-SD hMSGB (p = 0.050). CLIP2 was primarily localized at the plasma membrane of both serous and mucous ACs and excretory ducts, but also in the cytoplasm of serous ACs and excretory ducts from SICCA‐NS hMSGB (green, Fig. 5D). The CLIP2 expression area was not modified in ACs from SICCA-SD hMSGB as compared to SICCA-NS hMSGB (p = 0.200; Fig. 5E). Our data indicate that AQP5 and CLIP2 can co-localize in ACs from SICCA-NS hMSGB (Fig. 5F) and that this co-localization, predominantly localized at the apical plasma membrane, was significantly impaired in ACs from SICCA-SD hMSGB (p = 0.050; Fig. 5G).

Altered CLIP2 interactions with other AQP5-interacting protein partners in SjD hMSGB

Previous studies showing the interactions between AQP5 and PIP [24] and AQP5 and Ezrin [16], as well as the aberrant localization of PIP and Ezrin in SICCA-SD hMSGB. Consequently, we evaluated the interactions between CLIP2 and these two other AQP5-interacting protein partners, as well as the interactions between PIP and Ezrin to explore the possible existence of a multiprotein complex. To this end, PLA was first performed on NS-SV-AC cells to detect possible CLIP2-PIP, CLIP2-Ezrin and PIP-Ezrin complexes. Then, the protein-protein interactions and co-localization of protein couples were investigated in SICCA-SD and SICCA-NS hMSGB using PLA and double immunofluorescence.

In PIP-transfected NS-SV-AC cells (as NS-SV-AC does not naturally express PIP), scattered PIP-CLIP2 complexes were detected (Fig. 6A). In addition, PIP-CLIP2 complexes were dispersed throughout the ACs from SICCA-NS hMSGB, while they were primarily located at the basal lamina of ACs in SICCA-SD hMSGB (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, there was a notable reduction in PIP-CLIP2 complexes in ACs from SICCA-SD hMSGB compared to SICCA-NS hMSGB (p = 0.042) (Fig. 6C). These findings strongly suggest that CLIP2 can interact with PIP, in addition to AQP5. Using double immunofluorescence, PIP was found in the cytoplasm and to a weaker extent to the apical membrane of ACs from SICCA-NS hMSGB (Fig. 6D). The PIP-CLIP2 co-localization appeared widespread and reduced in ACs from SICCA-SD hMSGB compared to SICCA-NS hMSGB (p = 0.050) (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

The cytoplasmic linker protein family known as CLIPs has gained recognition for its pivotal role in connecting specific organelle membranes to microtubules [49–52]. Recent research has shed light on the structural and functional aspects of CAP-Gly domains, particularly their interactions with binding partners, such as microtubules and microtubule-plus-end-tracking proteins (+ TIPs) [53]. Understanding the structural intricacies of these domains is essential for deciphering the precise mechanisms through which CAP-Gly domains contribute to a wide range of cellular processes including but not limited to chromosome segregation, establishment and maintenance of cell polarity, intracellular organelle and vesicle transport, cell migration, and intracellular signaling functions [53]. CLIP2 is expressed in a wide range of tissues, with particularly high levels in the brain, gastrointestinal tract and the pancreas [54]. Of notable importance, CLIP2 is prominently expressed in neuronal dendrites and appears to play a role in regulating the translocation of brain-specific organelles, specifically of dendritic lamellar bodies. Structurally, CLIP2 contains two N-terminal CAP-Gly or microtubule binding (MTB) domains which are surrounded by serine-rich regions and are connected to an extended α-helical region that is predicted to form a coiled-coil domain (Supplementary Fig. S1). These structural features are conserved in the close human homologue CLIP1 (also known as CLIP170). However, CLIP1 contains an additional Zinc-knuckle domain in its C-terminus that is proposed to interact with the MTB domains, thereby causing auto-inhibition [50]. Both CLIP1 and CLIP2 form homodimers that dimerize via the coiled-coil domain and preferentially bind to and stabilize the growing ends of the microtubule via their CAP-Gly domains [50, 55]. However, at high expression levels, CLIP2 affects the microtubule network differently, causing bundling of the microtubule into fibers [50]. Their different structures, roles in microtubule organization and tissue-specific expression patterns [54] suggest that CLIP1 and CLIP2 may have specialized roles in the translocation of organelles and protein complexes in specific cellular contexts [49, 50].

Our study identifies CLIP2 as a novel interacting partner of AQP5. This is the first time that a member of the CLIP family has been shown to interact with AQPs, giving new insights into how human AQPs are regulated by trafficking in a tissue-dependent manner through the interactions with other proteins. Specifically, our work adds a new piece to the AQP5 interactome puzzle and a layer of complexity to the regulation of AQP5 in ACs of human SGs. The functional role of CLIP2 in AQP5 trafficking further provides important insights into its role in SG. Furthermore, decreased CLIP2 expression and interaction with AQP5, as well as autoantibodies against CLIP2, AQP5 and other AQP5-interacting partners may participate in the pathogenesis of SjD. These data warrant additional studies to further understand the possible role of CLIP2 in SjD.

Using mouse SG proteins, AQP5-induced co-immunoprecipitation coupled to mass spectrometry analysis revealed the presence of multiple proteins, including AQP5 and SCL12A2, previously identified as an AQP5-interacting protein partners [14]. The presence of PIP, previously detected as an AQP5 interacting protein [24], was also detected in four out of six parotid gland IP samples when the required two unique peptides per protein was reduced to only one peptide per protein in the database searching algorithm, in agreement with the low PIP abundance [24]. In addition, CLIP2 was identified as a new AQP5-interacting protein partner. The presence of CLIP2 reached significance in mPG but not in mSMG even though spectral count data showed its presence in five out of six samples, depending on the lysis buffer used to prepare the proteins used for immunoprecipitation. WB analysis confirmed that CLIP2 co-immunoprecipitated with AQP5 in mouse SGs, as well as in NS-SV-AC cells expressing AQP5. Despite the limitation of the co-immunoprecipitation experiment related to the lack of an IgG isotype control in the co-immunoprecipitation experiments, other negative controls were performed in the absence of antibodies or proteins supporting the specificity of the CLIP2 immunoprecipitation by AQP5 and thereby their interaction. Furthermore, the presence of AQP5-CLIP2 interaction was evidenced by at least two other separate techniques, i.e. PLA and MST.

The direct interaction between AQP5 and CLIP2 was further validated in vitro using MST which showed that AQP5 binds CLIP2, a method that has proven highly successful for characterizing AQP-interactions, including the interaction between AQP5 and PIP as well as Ezrin [18, 24, 56–58]. The MST-results showed that AQP5 binds CLIP2 MTB-domains with sub-micromolar affinity (Kd=0.11 ± 0.018 µM) and that this is likely to be mediated by the C-terminal tail. The estimated affinity is similar to what has been obtained for previous studies of CAP-Gly complexes, such as the interaction between CLIP1 and tubulin (0.51 ± 0.25 µM) [59] as well as the interaction between AQP5 and PIP (Kd=0.57 ± 0.07 µM) [24], whereas the affinity between AQP5 and Ezrin FERM-domain is lower (14 ± 5.7 µM) [18]. PIP and Ezrin have also been shown to interact with the AQP5 C-terminal tail, and although structural modelling of AQP5-Ezrin complex suggests the involvement of different residues, further studies will be needed to explore if these proteins compete with each other for binding to AQP5. Moreover, as our work suggests CLIP2 to be a binding partner for both PIP and Ezrin, future work exploring the possible existence of multi-protein complexes will give valuable new insights into the AQP5 trafficking mechanism.

Structural modelling of the AQP5 C-terminal tail with the CLIP2 MTB-domains suggested a similar mode of interaction as observed in structures of other CAP-Gly domain complexes. Commonly, these interactions involve a highly conserved basic groove on the CAP-Gly domain that binds acidic residues followed by an aromatic residue as seen in the CLIP1-tubulin (EExEEY/F) and p150Glued-EB1 complexes respectively (EEY) [28, 29]. In the AQP5-CLIP2 model, the motif interacting with the basic groove of MTB1, EPDEDW, is highly similar, thus supporting the modelling. It is interesting to note that, despite the substantial sequence and structural similarity between the CLIP2 MTB1 and MTB2-domains, the modelling suggests a closer interaction between AQP5 and MTB1. This could potentially be explained by differences in sequence within basic groove where in MTB1, Asn 88 as part of an NGV-motif interacts with AQP5, resembling the interaction observed between CLIP1 MTB1 and SLAIN [27]. In CLIP2 MTB2, Asn88 is replaced with glycine (resulting in GGT motif instead of NGV), making it unlikely to participate in binding in a similar manner. Nevertheless, as seen from our MST-experiments with the isolated CLIP2 MTB-domains, both MTB-domains are necessary for the interaction between AQP5 and CLIP2 to occur.

Considering the involvement of CLIP2 in connecting specific organelle membranes to microtubules and in various cellular processes, including intracellular protein trafficking [53], we hypothesize that the AQP5-CLIP2 interaction may play a role in the regulated trafficking/translocation of AQP5 to the cellular membrane, which is crucial for AQP5 function ensuring transmembrane water flow. In comparison to CLIP2-WT cells, the CLIP2-KO cells were characterized by impaired AQP5 trafficking to the plasma membrane upon FK, TH and FK + TH treatments, strongly supporting the key role of CLIP2 in this cellular process, and ruling out that the altered distribution of AQP5 may be attributed to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress upon TH treatment. The use of polarized NS-SV-AC cells with apicobasal polarity, overcoming some limitations of 2D-epithelial cells, may be useful to further study the role of CLIP2 in AQP5 trafficking and localization. The slight increase in AQP5 expression at the cellular membrane in CLIP2-KO cells compared to CLIP2 WT is unexpected and requires further investigation. In addition, as hCLIP2-KO cells are a heterogeneous population consisting of edited mono-allelic (94%) and biallelic (47%) cells, future studies will be required to assess both hCLIP2 and SNAP-hAQP5 expressions at the same time by confocal live-cell imaging and/or by Western blot on plasma membrane preparations. Furthermore, to overcome clonal variation and potential adaptive changes, the generation of stable cell lines through lentiviral delivery of multiple hCLIP2-specific shRNAs could offer a robust alternative to suppress CLIP2 expression, ensuring both efficiency and specificity. Moreover, given the CLIP2–PIP and CLIP2–Ezrin interactions detected by PLA in NS-SV-AC cells, it would be of interest to examine how FK, TH, and FK + TH treatments influence these interactions using complementary techniques such as PLA, Western blotting of membrane fractions, dual-label immunofluorescence, or live-cell imaging approaches [59, 60]. Together, these studies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of CLIP2 function in the molecular networks governing AQP5 trafficking.

Noteworthy, AQP5 has been shown to modulate microtubule dynamics. A correlation was shown between reduced levels of AQP5 and diminished assembly of microtubules, leading to a decrease in paracellular permeability. Conversely, when AQP5 was overexpressed, the microtubule assembly was increased and accompanied by enhanced microtubule stability [60]. Hence, as AQP5-vesicular trafficking is regulated by microtubules, which are in turn modulated by microtubule-plus-end trafficking proteins (+ TIPs), CLIP2 may serve as a bridge between AQP5 and microtubules. Our findings align with the well-established functions of CAP-Gly proteins, particularly their interaction with tubulin monomers, tubulin dimers, and microtubules and their involvement in vesicular trafficking [49]. The CLIP proteins stabilize microtubules and participate in dynein-dependent processes, such as microtubule capture at kinetochores and cargo targeting at the centrosome [53]. In addition, actin and microtubules can interact during the exocytosis process through cross-linking proteins [61]. Interestingly, our study offers a new insight into CLIP functions (namely CLIP2), by revealing the participation of CLIP2 in AQP5 trafficking. The reduction in induced-AQP5 trafficking upon CLIP2-KO emphasizes the dependency of AQP5 trafficking on the intact function of CLIP2. Considering the high sequence homology between AQP5 and AQP2, CLIP2 may also interact with AQP2 and participate in its trafficking and the regulation of AQP2-driven water reabsorption in the kidney. Indeed, the C-terminal sequence that was proposed to mediate the interactions between AQP5 and CLIP2 (EPDEDW) is also conserved in AQP2 (EPDTDW), suggesting that the two proteins may bind CLIP2 in a similar manner. More broadly, CLIP2 may prove to be a player in the trafficking of other AQPs as well. Further studies to probe the interaction between CLIP2 and human AQPs will be required to confirm these hypotheses, including mutagenesis of CLIP2 to precisely identify the amino acids involved in the AQP5-CLIP2 interaction and determine their role in AQP5 trafficking.

To investigate the involvement of CLIP2 in AQP5 deregulation in SGs of patients with SjD, we investigated the localization CLIP2, AQP5 and AQP5-CLIP2 complexes. AQP5-CLIP2 complexes were altered in hMSGB from SjD patients as compared to controls. Indeed, the AQP5-CLIP2 complexes were predominantly located at the basal membrane in hMSGB from SjD patients, while widely distributed within the ACs in the hMSGB from control patients. This observation indicates a significant change in the distribution of the AQP5-CLIP2 complexes in the context of SjD. Additionally, the expression level of AQP5, but not of CLIP2, was significantly reduced in ACs from SjD hMSGB. However, the colocalization of AQP5 and CLIP2 within the cytoplasm of ACs in SICCA-NS hMSGB and at the basal membrane of ACs in SICCA-SD hMSGB, as detected by PLA, was not observed when investigated by double immunofluorescence. This discrepancy may be attributable to the lower sensitivity of the immunofluorescence technique compared to PLA as well as differences in signal accuracy due to image acquisition using a widefield microscope for double immunofluorescence and a confocal microscope for PLA analysis. However, although immunofluorescence performed using confocal microscopy provides higher resolution and image clarity compared to conventional optical microscopy, the images obtained with the widefield microscope were of sufficient quality to allow an appropriate image analysis comparing different samples within the same methodology. Even though paraformaldehyde used to fix hMSGB is known to promote the formation of intra- and inter- protein crosslinking, and possibly altering the distance between proteins, appropriate PLA controls and the use of SICCA-NS hMSGB have been used to validate the presence of AQP5-CLIP2 interaction, and such interaction was also documented in methanol-fixed NS-SV-AC cells. Additional co-immunoprecipitation studies performed on both control and SjD hMSGB may be warranted to corroborate AQP5-CLIP2 interaction but may face ethical dilemmas and practical limitations related to limited tissue availability.

The unaltered hCLIP2 expression yet modified localization of hAQP5-hCLIP2 complexes at the basal region of ACs in SjD MSGB may point to scaffolding or trafficking defects contributing to abnormal AQP5 distribution [21, 22, 62, 63] and reduced saliva secretion. However, further investigation will be necessary to corroborate this hypothesis and determine the mechanisms involved. Considering AQP5 interacts with PIP [24] and Ezrin [16], we also examined the interactions between CLIP2 and these other AQP5-interacting partners. Our results showed that CLIP2 could interact with PIP and Ezrin, in addition to AQP5. Therefore, we hypothesize that AQP5, PIP, Ezrin, and CLIP2 could form a multiprotein complex that may be crucial for the proper function of SGs. To corroborate the existence of such multiprotein complex, customized multiplexed PLA may represent a useful approach to tackle this question [64, 65]. The perturbation of the interactions between some of the AQP5-interacting protein partners in SjD hMSGB provides further evidence of the complexity of AQP5 regulation and its implications in SjD pathogenesis. In SICCA-SD hMSGB, unlike the reduction of PIP-CLIP2 and PIP-Ezrin complexes, no significant difference was detected in Ezrin-CLIP2 interactions (detected by PLA) although reduced co-localization of both proteins was observed (studied by double IF). This apparent discrepancy may be due to the inherent technical differences and sensitivities of the two methodologies. The perturbation in Ezrin-CLIP2 interaction in SICCA-SD hMSGB may either not occur or occur at a distance greater than 40 nm, rendering them undetectable by PLA. Additional co-immunoprecipitation experiments are warranted to confirm the existence of multiprotein complexes.

Considering that SjD is a complex autoimmune disease with diverse clinical manifestations, the presence of autoantibodies against AQP5, CLIP2, PIP and Ezrin was evaluated as well. Our study sheds light on the presence of autoantibodies against some of the proteins studied in SjD patients, in particular IgG autoantibodies against CLIP2 and Ezrin. It remains to be clarified to what extent these autoantibodies play a role in the interactions of previously described interacting partners and in AQP5 trafficking. However, the observation that a considerable proportion of patients produces autoantibodies against AQP5, CLIP2, PIP and Ezrin emphasizes the importance of these proteins in the pathogenesis of SjD. Weak correlation between these autoantibodies and clinical complications of SjD suggest that the newly identified autoantibodies may participate in the disease process. Furthermore, it is interesting to note that in a subset of SjD patients lacking antibodies against Ro/SSA, approximately 22% of the patients could be identified based on their seropositivity for at least one the novel antibody among the combined panel. Several studies revealed that the presence of autoantibodies against AQP5 in the sera of SS patients was associated with a marked reduction in resting salivary flow compared to SjD patients lacking such antibodies [66, 67]. In most of the previous studies on AQP5 autoantibodies, the complete protein was used as an antigen [66–68] and the cut off was calculated by a Receiver-operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis. If a cut off definition based on a high specificity >98% as in our measurements would have been used, AQP5 antibodies would have been positive in 2–15% of SjD patients, similar to our findings. In a recent publication, a peptide representing part of an intermembrane region, extracellular loop E, helical transmembrane domain and cytoplasmic C-terminal tail of hAQP5 (aa190-265) was used as an antigen in an ELISA and 61/63 SjD patients were found to have antibodies against this peptide [40]. In our analysis using this antigen on Luminex beads and European SjD patients, antibodies against the loop region were more common (14.3%) than against the complete protein, but less common than in the recent study using Chinese SjD patients (97%). These distinct findings may possibly be due to distinct patient sampling and methods of detection of autoantibodies. Further studies using other SjD cohorts are warranted to confirm if antibodies against AQP5 could represent an interesting diagnostic marker for SjD, and if the detected autoantibodies contribute to the dysfunction of SGs.

Conclusions

This study has uncovered CLIP2 as a novel AQP5-interacting partner within human SG, contributing functionally to the regulation of AQP5 trafficking. Furthermore, our findings show that CLIP2 can also interact with other AQP5-associated protein partners. Moreover, the presence of autoantibodies targeting these proteins is found to correlate weakly with various clinical parameters. The detection of anti-CLIP2 antibodies underscores the need for in-depth investigations into CLIP2 gene polymorphisms that may be implicated in triggering autoimmunity. Collectively, our results suggest a potential involvement of CLIP2 in SjD pathogenesis through its modified expression and interaction with AQP5 and PIP. Overall, this research enhances our knowledge regarding AQP5 cell biology and sheds light on SjD pathogenesis, opening avenues for additional fundamental and clinical investigations.
