Impact of Initial Axillary Microaxial Flow Pump on Post-Myocardial Infarction Ventricular Septal Defect Management
Authors: Kenta Murakami, Shin Hasegawa, Akinao Miki, Hirotaka Yamauchi, Akimitsu Tanaka, Takeki Ohashi, Soh Hosoba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe mortality rate of ventricular septal defect (VSD) following acute myocardial infarction remains high. Mechanical circulatory support devices have been shown to facilitate delayed surgery and improve postoperative survival, although clear guidelines for device selection are lacking.MethodsThis study presents a retrospective cohort analysis of 18 patients with post-infarction VSD treated with microaxial flow pump (mAFP) from March 2017 to November 2024. Patients were divided into 2 groups: the percutaneous group utilized Impella 2.5 or CP (Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA) and the axillary access group (Impella 5.0 or 5.5). Their clinical outcomes and early to midterm survival rates were analysed.ResultsThe survival discharge rate was 31% (4 in 13 patients) in the percutaneous group and 100% (5 patients) in the axillary access group, with a significant difference (P = .029). Seven patients who were initially managed with percutaneous mAFP and later required the addition of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation all died perioperatively. The 100-day Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a significant improvement in survival rates in the axillary access group compared to the percutaneous group (log-rank, P = .020).ConclusionsThese findings illustrate variation in surgical timing and device selection and suggest that the early use of an axillary access mAFP may warrant further investigation in post-myocardial infarction VSD patients.

INTRODUCTION

Advances in revascularization have reduced post-myocardial infarction ventricular septal defect (post-MI VSD) incidence to 0.2%-0.3%.1–4 However, early mortality remains 40%-80%, 1-year mortality 75%,2,5,6 and without surgery, 30-day mortality 94%.2 Thus, post-MI VSD remains fatal.

The primary cause of poor prognosis is heart failure (HF) due to acute MI and right HF from left-to-right shunting. Additionally, myocardial fragility and multiorgan failure from cardiogenic shock (CS) complicate immediate surgery. While optimal timing is debated, mechanical circulatory support (MCS) allows delaying surgery 3-4 weeks,7 promoting stabilization and repair, potentially reducing early mortality and recurrent shunting.8,9

However, late complications such as infection, HF, and renal failure may worsen outcomes, sometimes necessitating early surgery. Impella (Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA) improves haemodynamics by reducing shunting and enhancing circulation. It decreases left-to-right shunt, systolic pulmonary artery pressure, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure while increasing cardiac index. Compared to intra-aortic balloon pumps (IABP), microaxial flow pump (mAFP) improves CS outcomes,10,11 making it a preferred bridging device.

Nonetheless, mAFP poses risks, including haemolysis, bleeding, infection, right-to-left shunting, VSD expansion, and embolism. Axillary access mAFP may reduce bleeding and hemolysis12 while enabling early mobilization and preoperative optimization, but evidence remains limited. The European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend MCS for post-MI VSD,10 though device selection and timing remain unclear.

This study aims to improve post-MI VSD outcomes by evaluating optimal MCS selection and management.

METHODS

Ethics and regulations

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institution’s data protection officer. Anonymized data sharing was permitted by the institutional review board (TGE01327-016, approved on 6 September 2024). Informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective nature. All relevant institutional and national regulations were followed.

Study design

This retrospective cohort study included 34 consecutive patients diagnosed post-MI VSD between March 2017, when mAFP became available in Japan, and November 2024 at a single institution in Japan (Figure 1).13 Ten patients treated only with IABP, 3 who did not undergo MCS, 2 with concurrent myocardial rupture, and 1 who did not provide informed consent were excluded. The study enrolled 18 patients treated with mAFP devices. The Impella 2.5 and CP devices were assigned to the percutaneous group, and the Impella 5.0 and 5.5 devices were assigned to the axillary access group. The primary aim was to observe the management and prognosis according to mAFP type.

Population

Adult patients who required MCS support with mAFP for unstable haemodynamics due to post-MI VSD were included. CS was defined by systemic hypotension, signs of systemic hypoperfusion, and radiological evidence of pulmonary oedema assessed by board-certified cardiologists based on imaging findings and clinical judgement.

Device selection, including axillary access mAFP or veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO), was considered in cases of advanced CS, as defined by haemodynamic and metabolic criteria (Table S1).

The final decision regarding device choice and timing, as well as MCS combination, was made through multidisciplinary discussions involving interventional cardiologists and cardiovascular surgeons, based on both clinical and anatomical considerations.

In cases where axillary access was technically challenging—due to small vessel diameter or severe calcification—and urgent haemodynamic support was required, percutaneous mAFP was selected. Conversely, axillary access mAFP was used when the approach was feasible and the patient’s clinical condition permitted surgical insertion. With accumulating institutional experience and procedural proficiency, our strategy gradually shifted towards prioritizing early use of axillary access mAFP in eligible patients (Supplementary Figure).

Data collection

Data were collected from patient medical records, including clinical history, symptoms, haemodynamics, echocardiographic and angiographic findings, treatments received during hospitalization, treatment-related complications, discharge status, and follow-up data.

Follow-up

Data were collected from patient records, including haemodynamic and biochemical parameters, medication use, and organ support at 24 hours post-admission and within 24 hours before surgery. In-hospital complications included dialysis-requiring renal failure, major bleeding (Bleeding Academic Research Consortium grade ≥3) from insertion sites, stroke, gastrointestinal or intracranial haemorrhage, haemolysis requiring transfusion/dialysis, acute limb ischaemia, re-exploration for bleeding, cardiac arrest, HF, respiratory events (pneumonia, reintubation, tracheostomy), sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and multiple organ failure. Haemolysis was defined as an elevation of lactate dehydrogenase to more than twice the upper limit of normal, accompanied by clinical signs such as hemoglobinuria or unexplained anaemia. Mortality was evaluated at discharge and 100 days, with cumulative survival rates evaluated during the follow-up period based on medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (first to third quartiles), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Group comparisons used Student t-test for normal and Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normal variables. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and analysed using Fisher’s exact or chi-squared test. Kaplan-Meier curves estimated 100-day survival after post-MI VSD diagnosis, and distributions were compared with the log-rank test. Analyses were conducted using EZR version 1.68 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Tokyo, Japan), with significance set at P < .05.

Hypertension was significantly more prevalent in the percutaneous group (77% vs 20%; P = .047). Two patients (15.3%) in the percutaneous group had cardiac arrest before surgery.

Pulmonary oedema was observed in 14 patients (78%). The median lactate level was 2.55 mmol/L (first to third quartiles: 2.05-6.125). Transthoracic echocardiography showed an left ventricular ejection fraction of 53.1% ± 10.2% and pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) of 1.06 (first to third quartiles: 0.65-1.57). The VSD size was 8.9 ± 4.1 mm, with a trend towards larger defects in the axillary access group. The median time from onset of acute coronary syndrome symptoms to medical intervention was 0.5 days (first to third quartiles: 0-2.75 days). Coronary revascularization was performed in 50% of patients, all of whom underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The remaining 50% were treated conservatively without revascularization.

The pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow ratio (Qp/Qs) was 1.95 ± 0.76. The VSD location was the anterior wall in 12 patients (67%) and at the inferior wall base in 6 patients (33%). Qp/Qs, systolic pulmonary artery pressure, and PAPi each had one missing value due to suboptimal echocardiographic imaging or lack of right heart catheterization. These missing data were assumed to be missing at random and were excluded from the respective analyses.

Severity at presentation was assessed using the CardShock Score and IABP-SHOCK II Score. Calculation of these scores requires specific parameters, including the Glasgow Coma Scale and post-PCI thrombolysis in myocardial infarction flow grade. Therefore, patients who were sedated on arrival or had not undergone PCI were excluded from these assessments. Among patients with calculable scores, the mean CardShock Score was 3.8 ± 2.1 in the percutaneous group (n = 11) and 1.5 ± 0.5 in the axillary access group (n = 4). The mean IABP-SHOCK II Score was 4.5 ± 2.5 (n = 7) and 4.6 ± 2.9 (n = 3), respectively.

Despite intensive management, 3 patients (20%) in the percutaneous group experienced haemodynamic deterioration and died before surgical intervention could be performed. Inotropes were used in 1-3 agents up until the time of surgery in 8 patients (61.5%) from the percutaneous group, compared to only 1 patient (20%) in the axillary access group.

Regarding device usage, the Impella 5.5 was used in 4 patients, the Impella 5.0 in 1 patient, the Impella CP in 8 patients, and the Impella 2.5 in 5 patients. In the percutaneous group, 3 patients (17%) escalated to axillary access mAFP, and 6 patients (33%) required V-A ECMO. In contrast, no escalation occurred in the axillary access group. The average duration from mAFP insertion to surgery was 10.4 ± 6.5 days. Additionally, the clinical course of the 3 patients who underwent escalation from percutaneous mAFP to axillary access mAFP was as follows.

The first patient, a 74-year-old woman, received an Impella 2.5 on admission, which was maintained for 12 days and then escalated to an Impella 5.0 for enhanced HF management and rehabilitation. After 8 additional days of support, surgical repair was performed. The second patient, a 71-year-old woman, presented 7 days after symptom onset and received an Impella 2.5 on the same day. Due to worsening hepatic and renal function, the device was escalated to an Impella 5.0 on day 3. V-A ECMO was added on day 5, and surgery was performed after 6 days of support. The third patient, a 72-year-old man, received an Impella CP on the day of admission. However, due to haemolysis at higher support levels and rising lactate levels, sufficient circulatory support could not be achieved. On day 2, the device was changed to Impella 5.0 with concomitant V-A ECMO. Despite escalation, organ dysfunction persisted, and surgery was performed the following day.

In all cases, the initial selection of percutaneous mAFP was based on haemodynamic instability and the urgency of support. Decisions regarding escalation, MCS addition, and surgical timing were made on a case-by-case basis through multidisciplinary discussion involving cardiovascular surgeons and interventional cardiologists. Clinical indicators included sustained lactate elevation, hypotension, and worsening end-organ function, all of which were considered to reflect inadequate flow support.

Preoperative laboratory tests on the day before surgery showed significantly higher estimated glomerular filtration rate (25 vs 69 mL/min/1.73 m2; P = .011) and lower lactate levels (1.9 vs 0.8 mmol/L; P = .038) in the axillary access group. The surgical method involved a single patch in 11 patients (73.3%) and a double patch in 4 patients (26.6%). The Maze procedure was additionally performed in one patient (6.6%), and tricuspid valve repair was performed in 1 patient (6.6%). A residual shunt was observed in 4 patients (26.6%), with Qp/Qs of 1.41 ± 0.23. There was no significant difference between the groups.

The surgical technique (single patch or double patch) was determined at the discretion of the operating surgeon, based on local anatomical features such as defect size and shape, extent of necrosis, and fragility of surrounding tissue. The type or duration of MCS did not influence surgical technique selection.

Among the 11 patients who were managed with mAFP alone, 10 (91%) were operated at P-8 to P-9 settings. During concurrent V-A ECMO support, mAFP was downregulated to P-3 to P-4 for left ventricular unloading. Qp/Qs was monitored daily to prevent pulmonary overcirculation due to left-to-right shunting. Pump flow settings were adjusted based on Qp/Qs trends, blood pressure, and signs of pulmonary oedema. Volume management was also performed as necessary to avoid volume overload. In cases of persistently high Qp/Qs or difficulty in haemodynamic control, early surgical intervention was considered.

Complications

mAFP-related preoperative complications are shown in Table 3, while postoperative complications are shown in Table 4. Three patients (20%) in the percutaneous group died before surgery. Major bleeding was observed in 10 patients (56%) and represented the most common complication. Bleeding sites included the insertion site (8 patients, 44%) and the gastrointestinal tract (2 patients, 11%). A median of 17 units of red blood cells (first to third quartiles: 10.5-31.5 units) was transfused postoperatively. Nine patients (50%) required renal replacement therapy, with no significant inter-group differences. No abrupt pump stoppage occurred with the mAFP device; however, pump exchange was required in one patient (20%) in the axillary access group. This was due to displacement of the mAFP from the left ventricle into the aorta.

Postoperative complications were similar between groups, with 5 patients (33.3%) experiencing bleeding. In the axillary access group, 2 patients (13%) required re-exploration and 1 patient (7%) had intracranial haemorrhage. In the percutaneous group, 2 patients (13%) experienced gastrointestinal haemorrhage. Acute kidney injury was observed in 6 patients (40%), all of whom required renal replacement therapy. Cardiac arrest occurred in 1 patient (7%) in the axillary access group, while 3 patients (20%) experienced HF. Respiratory complications included pneumonia in 5 patients (33%), reintubation in 3 patients (20%), and tracheostomy following prolonged intubation in 4 patients (27%). Sepsis was observed in 2 patients (13%). Disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiple organ failure were observed in 4 patients (27%).

DISCUSSION

Surgical management of post-MI VSD lacks consensus. Tissue support improves about 3 weeks post-MI, reducing leakage and improving survival after patch closure, supporting bridging therapy. Several studies highlight prolonged MCS use, aiding elective surgery by promoting tissue healing. Yet, as shown here, sepsis and multiorgan failure can complicate delayed surgery.

The mean duration from mAFP implantation to surgery in this study was 10.4 days, longer than the 6-day period in previous studies. While prior studies reported 75% in-hospital mortality, ours showed 50%. The lower mortality and longer implantation duration may be due to earlier mAFP implantation compared to another centre (median 1-day post-diagnosis), leading to early haemodynamic stabilization and improved HF management. mAFP may reduce shunt volume, lower LV end-diastolic pressure, and relieve pulmonary oedema, potentially aiding post-MI VSD management. While it has been suggested to worsen hypoxemia, our findings indicate a reduction in left-to-right shunt volume, with no cases of worsened hypoxemia observed.

Axillary access mAFP showed potential for improved long-term survival. Ikeda et al reported fewer adverse events and lower mortality in the axillary access group than in the percutaneous group.12 Our study demonstrated no significant differences complications, including haemolysis, related to mAFP use between the groups. However, the percutaneous group exhibited higher rates of renal replacement therapy, insertion-site bleeding, and access-site ischaemia, which may have adversely affected clinical outcomes. Preoperative lactate levels were higher and estimated glomerular filtration rates were lower in the percutaneous group, indicating poorer end-organ perfusion. Moreover, fewer patients in the axillary access group required preoperative discontinuation of catecholamines, suggesting more stable haemodynamics and sufficient circulatory support. These findings imply that inadequate cardiac output and insufficient tissue perfusion in the percutaneous group may have hindered optimal HF management. In comparison to the Impella CP, the Impella 5.5 delivers a higher cardiac output, potentially facilitating more effective haemodynamic stabilization. Furthermore, axillary access enables earlier mobilization and rehabilitation than femoral access, which may contribute to improved overall survival. For illustrative purposes, 2 cases from each group are presented in the Supplementary Case.

A concerning observation was 100% mortality in patients who had extracorporeal membrane oxygenation added after initial percutaneous mAFP use. These patients experienced worsening hypoxia, hypotension, and inadequate tissue perfusion, likely compromising myocardial healing before ECMO support. Our findings highlight a potential association that early selection of a high-support device like axillary access mAFP may inform future strategies for bridging therapy by stabilizing circulation and promoting myocardial recovery, allowing an extended wait before surgery.

Limitations

First, group differences may have influenced outcomes: the percutaneous group had more elderly patients, renal dysfunction, arrest, and higher lactate, while the axillary access group had more smokers, larger VSDs, higher CPK, and lower EF.

Second, axillary access mAFP insertion via the axillary artery requires surgical access, which can be time-consuming and challenging in unstable patients with respiratory distress or low BP. Additionally, insertion is difficult if the vessel diameter is <7 mm, with a 4.1%-5.0% failure rate.14,15

Third, Impella CP and 5.5 were introduced later in the study, potentially introducing treatment timing biases. Fourth, our small sample (5 patients in the axillary access group) limited statistical power, possibly explaining the lack of significant differences in complications. Fifth, 3 patients initially supported with percutaneous mAFP were escalated to Impella 5.0 or 5.5 due to insufficient haemodynamic support or progression of organ dysfunction. Although these patients were classified within the percutaneous group based on initial access, such treatment crossover may have introduced confounding in inter-group comparisons. Given the limited sample size, statistical adjustment for this effect was not feasible.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the importance of early circulatory support in post-MI VSD. Early axillary access mAFP use may improve haemodynamic stabilization and myocardial repair, potentially enhancing survival. Further multicentre studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings.
