Pediatric Pneumococcal Eye Infection and Changes in Antibiotic Susceptibility: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Ui Yoon Choi, Soo-Han Choi, Doo Ri Kim, Kil Sung Bae, Areum Shin, Yae-Jean Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study analyzed the antimicrobial susceptibility of pneumococcus isolated from eye discharge of pediatric patients. It included 65 patients, stratified by age (<2 years vs. ≥2 years) and date of isolation of pneumococcus (earlier period, 1994–2013 vs. later period, 2014–2024). There were no differences between the age groups in antimicrobial susceptibility. Between the periods, antibiotic susceptibility showed no significant difference for cefotaxime (83.3% vs. 63.8%) and levofloxacin (100% vs. 95.5%), but there was a significant difference for penicillin susceptibility (48.5% vs. 16.7%, P=0.035). The results suggest an increase in non-susceptibility to penicillin in the later period.

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) causes invasive diseases in children such as meningitis, bacteremia, and pneumonia, as well as milder illness, such as sinusitis, acute otitis media, and eye infection. Pneumococcus infection can be life-threatening, particularly in children younger than 2 years, elderly persons, and immunocompromised patients [1]. Although the introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) resulted in a marked decrease in invasive pneumococcal infections, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in pneumococcus is still an important health concern [2]. Since nasopharyngeal colonization is the first step in the pathogenic process of respiratory diseases, most studies have been conducted on nasopharyngeal isolates. Overall, studies have reported a reduction in the prevalence of AMR by reducing the total number of pneumococcal diseases, serotype replacement (vaccine type [VT] to non-vaccine type [NVT]), and emergence of non-encapsulated S. pneumoniae (NESp) [34]. In a nasopharyngeal study conducted with children in the United States during 2011–2020, a significant increase in resistance to ≥1 antibiotic class was observed, and children ≥2 years of age seemed to have a higher proportion of penicillin-nonsusceptible S. pneumoniae (PNSP) isolates than that of younger children [5].

In Korea, the seven-valent PCV (PCV7) was introduced for optional use in 2003, and was replaced with an updated 13-valent PCV (PCV13) in 2010. That same year, a 10-valent PCV (PCV10) was also introduced, and PCV10 and PCV13 were implemented as a national immunization program (NIP) vaccines in 2014. A significant decrease in the VT proportion of PNSP isolates has been reported in nasopharyngeal carriage studies conducted during the post-vaccination period [6].

To date, antimicrobial resistance of pneumococcus has been investigated based on nasopharyngeal colonization. Contagious eye infections are important clinical disease entities often treated with antibiotics which may cause serious complications such as blurred vision and vision loss [7]. Pneumococcal eye infections are clinically significant, yet data is limited. A few studies have indicated that pneumococcus in the nasopharynx and eye infections are largely identical. For example, a recent study showed that serotypes and genotypes in the conjunctiva and nasopharynx were matched in 87% of study participants [89]. However, specific data on the eye may be of additional value for selecting appropriate antibiotics for pneumococcal eye infections. In this study, we investigated the antimicrobial susceptibility of pneumococcal isolates in eye discharge from pediatric patients. This study will provide insights in the treatment of pneumococcal eye infections.

This retrospective study was conducted among pediatric patients who were hospitalized or visited the outpatient clinics of Samsung Seoul Medical Center, Pusan National University Hospital, and Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital from November 1994 to October 2024. Patients were included if pneumococcus was confirmed in culture from an eye discharge sample. Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were collected through a retrospective medical record review. The patients were stratified by age (<2 years, known to be more vulnerable to pneumococcal infection vs. ≥2 years) and date of isolation of pneumococcus [earlier period (1994–2013), prior to introduction of PCV in NIP vs. later period (2014–2024), after introduction of PCV in NIP]. Antimicrobial susceptibility was compared between age groups and periods.

Eye discharge was collected using a calcium alginate swab and transferred to a transport medium. Microbiological analysis was conducted in the department of laboratory medicine of each hospital. Samples were cultured on blood agar and were incubated for 24–72 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 chamber. Bacterial identification and microbial susceptibility testing were performed by an automatic system, either MicroFlex LT (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) or Vitek2 (bioMérieux, Mary-l Etoile, France). The MIC was calculated according to the 2023 criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. Susceptibility was defined as follows: penicillin (non-meningitis, parenteral) MIC ≤2 mg/mL, cefotaxime (non-meningitis) MIC ≤1 mg/mL, and levofloxacin MIC ≤2 mg/mL [10].

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0, SPSS®, Chicago, IL, USA) with a P-value <0.05 considered statistically significant. The Chi-square test was employed for the comparison of categorical variables, and the results are reported as a proportion (%). Continuous variables were compared using the t-test and were summarized as mean and standard deviation.

A total of 65 pediatric patients had pneumococcal eye infection. Baseline characteristics of these patients is presented in Table 1. Of these patients, 30 (46.2%) were male, and the overall median age was 1.35 years (range 0–16 years, interquartile range 0.20–3.50 years). In the age-stratified comparison, there were no significant differences in antimicrobial susceptibility for penicillin, cefotaxime and levofloxacin between the two age groups (Table 2). The earlier period and later period were comprised of 43 and 22 pediatric patients, respectively, and there were no significant differences in mean age or proportion of males and individuals with underlying diseases (Table 3). Antimicrobial susceptibility data showed that there were no significant differences between the two periods for cefotaxime (83.3% vs. 63.6%, P=0.105) or levofloxacin (100% vs. 95.5%, P=0.458). However, there was a significant difference in penicillin susceptibility between periods 1 and 2 (58.5% vs. 16.7%, P=0.035), with lower susceptibility observed in period 2 (Table 3).

Studies examining pneumococcal antibiotic nonsusceptibility have yielded variable results. A synthesized analysis of pediatric invasive pneumococcal diseases and nasopharyngeal colonization studies demonstrated a reduction in nonsusceptibility to penicillin and third-generation cephalosporins over the 10 years following the introduction of PCV immunization [11]. The PCV program may have contributed to this trend by reducing disease burden and the consequent need for antibiotic use [12]. Oh et al. also reported no increment of PNSP in the postvaccination period based on airway specimens collected during the period of 2009 to 2018 in Korea [13]. In a nasopharyngeal carriage study conducted by Lee et al., an increase of the NVT proportion in PNSP was observed [6].

Only a few studies have addressed pneumococcal eye infections. Antimicrobial nonsusceptibility varies by region, reflecting differences in antibiotic use practices. In the Antibiotic Resistance Monitoring in Ocular Microorganisms (ARMOR) study, a multicenter, prospective surveillance program conducted in the United States, no discernible change in resistance to penicillin or quinolones was noted among pneumococcal isolates between 2009 and 2016 [14]. Another report from the ARMOR study found that resistance to third-generation cephalosporins remained low, at approximately 1.5%, from 2014 to 2020 [15]. By contrast, a study conducted at a tertiary eye center in Saudi Arabia revealed a marked increase in pneumococcal resistance to penicillin (from 1.6% in 2003 to 20.8% in 2019) and quinolones (from 0.8% in 2003 to 80% in 2019), a trend potentially driven by widespread self-medication practices [16].

In this study, we investigated the antimicrobial susceptibility of pneumococcus isolated from eye discharge during 1994–2024. No difference was found between the age groups (<2 years vs. ≥2 years), but an increase of PNSP from 51.5% up to 83.3% was noted in later periods (1994–2013 vs. 2014–2024). This study has certain limitations inherent to its retrospective design that need to be addressed. Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed for only three antimicrobials. Serotyping or multilocus sequence typing associated with nonsusceptibility was not performed, thus interplay between PCV vaccination, serotype distribution changes, and antimicrobial susceptibility was not studied. Additionally, the small participant size limits generalizability. Although pneumococcus isolated in this study was not serotyped between VT, NVT or NESp, the increase in PNSP in the later period may be a reflection of the persistently high rate of antibiotic prescriptions in Korea.

Acute respiratory infections account for a significant proportion of antibiotic use, and unnecessary antibiotic use facilitates AMR [17]. In Korea, antibiotic use for upper respiratory infections has steadily declined (total population, 73% in 2002, 49% in 2011, and 35% in 2021) [18]. However, Korea remains among the highest antibiotic-consuming countries within the Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), with average antibiotic use 1.2 times higher than that of OECD members [19]. Pediatric patients seemed to receive more antibiotics for upper respiratory infection (41.3% in infants, 32.5% in children, 30.2% in adults, and 21.9% in adults aged ≥65 years in 2021, respectively) [20]. Extended-spectrum penicillin is the most commonly used antibiotic in Korea, particularly for acute respiratory infections in children [1720]. This sustained use may exert continuous selective pressure and rapid bacterial genome evolution, such as alterations in pneumococcal penicillin-binding protein, leading to increased PNSP.

This study is the first investigation in Korea to specifically examine the antimicrobial susceptibility of pneumococcal isolates obtained from eye discharge. Antimicrobial susceptibility can vary by region, study population, and specimen. Selecting antibiotics based on data of a different geographical region or nasopharyngeal carriage may be unproductive in treatment of pneumococcal eye infections in Korea. This study documented an increase in PNSP in pneumococcal eye infections and highlights these infections as a clinically significant disease entity requiring the attention of clinicians. The findings will aid in selecting appropriate antibiotics for treating pneumococcal eye infections. Continuous monitoring of pneumococcal antimicrobial susceptibility is essential for guiding evidence-based antibiotic selection and for maintaining effective regional surveillance.
