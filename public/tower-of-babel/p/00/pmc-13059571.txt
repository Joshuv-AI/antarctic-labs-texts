Chondrolaryngoplasty in gender-affirming surgery: presentation of a modified technique and retrospective clinical evaluation
Authors: Andrey L. Istranov, Yuliya I. Isakova, Maria V. Plotnikova, Sergey N. Teyfukov, Eleonora M. Ratmanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChondrolaryngoplasty is an important stage in gender- affirming surgery. As demand for this procedure grows, a safe and reliable surgical technique, adaptable to individual patient anatomy and aesthetic goals, is essential.MethodsWe present a retrospective review of patients who underwent gender incongruence chondrolaryngoplasty using an original technique. The study included 30 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of gender incongruence. The age of the patients ranged from 20 to 34 years. The modified procedure involved resection of the prominent thyroid cartilage and the creation of a rigid neoframework using sutures. Outcomes were assessed using the five-point Likert scale for satisfaction and the SF-36 questionnaire for quality of life. Outcome assessments were conducted via an online survey platform. The average follow-up period was 78.5 ± 7.2 months. All 30 patients were successfully contacted and completed the survey, resulting in a 100% response rate at long-term follow-up.ResultsThe results indicate a high level of patient satisfaction. On the Likert scale, 90% of patients reported a “very good” outcome. The SF-36 questionnaire, administered at long-term follow-up, demonstrated high self-reported levels of functioning and well-being across all domains. The mean scores were notably high in the domains of Social Functioning (90.2 ± 7.5) and Physical Functioning (88.5 ± 8.1). There were no major postoperative complications.ConclusionsThis modified chondrolaryngoplasty technique is an effective and safe component of gender-affirming surgery, associated with high levels of long-term patient-reported body satisfaction and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03588-7.

Introduction

Gender- affirming surgery represents a significant and evolving field within plastic surgery. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the global prevalence of gender incongruence was estimated at 0.3–0.5% of the population as of 2020, representing approximately 25 million individuals [1]. It is crucial to note, however, that these WHO figures primarily include individuals who have formally sought medical recognition of their gender identity. Consequently, these statistics likely underrepresent the true prevalence, as they do not account for individuals who do not seek surgical intervention or those undergoing hormone therapy outside the established healthcare system. The actual number of people with gender incongruence is therefore believed to be substantially higher. In addition to genital reconstruction, many of these patients seek facial and vocal feminization procedures, including chondrolaryngoplasty [2].

Chondrolaryngoplasty comprises surgical techniques aimed at reshaping the thyroid cartilage to reduce the laryngeal prominence (Adam’s apple). The procedure was first described by Wolfort and Parry in 1975 [3], and numerous technical modifications have been reported since [4–6]. The anterior thyroid cartilage prominence is typically more pronounced in males and individuals with an asthenic body habitus [7]. By reducing this prominence, chondrolaryngoplasty effectively feminizes the neck contour and is a commonly requested intervention among transfeminine individuals [8]. Beyond experiencing gender dysphoria, this population faces a high risk of social rejection and physical violence. A 2015 U.S. study found that 48% of respondents reported experiencing denial of services, verbal harassment, and/or physical assault due to their gender identity. Those who were openly identified as transgender were significantly more likely to face such discrimination compared to those who were not [4].

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study was to present a modified chondrolaryngoplasty technique and detail its surgical steps. The secondary objectives were to evaluate long-term postoperative outcomes, including patient satisfaction, quality of life, and complication rates, using standardized questionnaires.

Anatomical features of the laryngo-tracheal complex in men and women

The larynx is a complex anatomical and physiological structure composed of various tissue types. The thyroid cartilage, the largest cartilaginous structure in the laryngotracheal complex, consists of hyaline cartilage [9]. It comprises two quadrangular laminae that fuse anteriorly in the upper two-thirds, forming the laryngeal prominence. The angle of fusion differs between males and females, measuring approximately 90° in males and 120° in females. The vocal folds, situated posterior to the thyroid cartilage, are generally larger and longer in males, accounting for the differences in vocal tonality and timbre between genders [10]. Additionally, a more defined layer of subcutaneous fat in the anterior neck of females renders the laryngeal prominence less conspicuous. Thus, the thyroid cartilage serves both a protective function and contributes to a secondary sex characteristic [11]. Sex hormones play a major role in the development of the laryngotracheal cartilaginous apparatus. During puberty, as secondary sexual characteristics develop, this region undergoes a transformative period. This period lasts approximately six months, though full laryngeal maturation is typically not achieved until ages 17 to 20. These transformations dramatically alter the larynx’s structure, particularly its cartilaginous components. n males, the thyroid cartilage grows and projects anteriorly, forming a prominent bulge on the neck [12]. Laryngeal development in males is rapid and occurs primarily in the sagittal plane [13]. In contrast, laryngeal growth in females is slower and progresses mainly in the transverse direction. Following its maturation, the larynx undergoes calcification, a process regulated by the parathyroid glands. This calcification reduces elasticity and increases the stiffness of the laryngeal framework. By age 50, the male larynx is typically fully ossified. In females, this ossification process is significantly slower and may not be complete until age 80 [14]. In addition to sex and parathyroid hormones, the growth and development of the laryngeal cartilage are influenced by pituitary hormones, which regulate the production of sex and thyroid hormones. Consequently, delayed laryngeal cartilage development can result from impaired thyroid gland function.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective review of all patients, who underwent modified chondrolaryngoplasty as part of gender-affirming surgery.

Inclusion criteria were: a diagnosis of gender incongruence (ICD-11: HA60.XX), age ≥ 18 years, and provision of written informed consent for the medical intervention.

Exclusion criteria were: patient refusal to proceed, active local infection in the surgical field, and any somatic contraindications to surgery.

This study included 30 patients, aged 20 to 34 years, with a confirmed diagnosis of gender incongruence. The sample size was determined by the total number of eligible patients who underwent the procedure during the defined study period, as this was a descriptive study of a novel technique without a formal sample size calculation. All procedures were performed between January 2018 and January 2020 in the Plastic Surgery Department of University Clinical Hospital No. 1 at Sechenov University by a single surgeon (A.L. Istranov). Prior to surgical intervention, all patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team, including a psychiatrist, a sexologist, and an endocrinologist, to confirm the diagnosis. As part of this assessment, patients completed questionnaires documenting their sex assigned at birth and their current gender identity. Based on the multidisciplinary review, all included patients had a formal diagnosis of gender incongruence.

All patients had been on feminizing hormone therapy for at least two years prior to surgery. To mitigate the risk of thromboembolism and facilitate optimal wound healing, hormone therapy was suspended four weeks preoperatively and was reinstated following complete wound healing, provided no postoperative complications occurred.

No patient had a history of prior surgical interventions in the laryngotracheal complex, including vocal fold procedures.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of the Sechenov First Moscow State Medical University approved the study protocol (No. 52). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for both the surgical procedure and the use of their anonymized data for research publication.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was patient satisfaction with the surgical result. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of postoperative complications, the surgeon’s assessment of the treatment outcomes and postoperative quality of life.

To assess quality of life, the SF-36 questionnaire was administered postoperatively. All 30 patients who underwent surgery during the defined period were eligible for follow-up. Outcome assessments were conducted via an online survey platform. Contact was attempted via the email and phone numbers recorded in the medical charts. All 30 patients were successfully contacted and completed the survey, resulting in a 100% response rate at long-term follow-up.

Postoperative complications were categorized as local or systemic.

Systemic complications included thromboembolism, respiratory failure, acute vascular failure (shock), and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).

Local complications included pain, hematoma, surgical site infection, severe edema, wound dehiscence, dysphonia, and temporary vocal weakness. Complications were assessed through physical examination.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). No comparative statistical tests were applied as this was a single-cohort descriptive study.

Preoperative evaluation

The preoperative workup, in addition to standard tests for general anesthesia (complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, screening for HIV, hepatitis B/C, and syphilis, blood typing, urinalysis, coagulogram, lower extremity venous ultrasound, chest radiograph, and electrocardiogram), included laryngeal stroboscopy (Atmos Strobo 21Led, Germany). Stroboscopy was used to evaluate vocal fold closure, oscillation amplitude, and symmetry. This examination was critical for determining the extent of safe cartilage resection, as it allowed for the indirect assessment of vocal fold level relative to the thyroid cartilage. A single dose of intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis (Сefazolin 1 g) was administered preoperatively. Perioperative corticosteroids were not routinely used.

Surgical technique

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. The objective was to reduce the laryngeal prominence by resecting the thyroid cartilage, thereby feminizing the neck contour.

With the patient in a seated position, preoperative marking was performed. The incision was planned within a natural cervical skin crease superior to the thyroid cartilage. The boundaries of the cartilaginous prominence were palpated and marked (Fig. 1).

Operating marking of the midline, cut level and projection of the angle of thyroid cartilage

A 1.5 cm transverse skin incision was made with a No. 15 scalpel. Soft tissues were dissected meticulously in a layered manner using blunt and sharp techniques to expose the thyroid cartilage prominence. The perichondrium was partially elevated from the most projecting portion of the cartilage. The protruding edges of the thyroid cartilage laminae were resected using a No. 11 scalpel at a 90-degree angle, creating a new thyroid cartilage angle (Fig. 2A, B). The size of the resected cartilage fragment ranged from 3 to 5 mm.

Crucially, the resection was performed superior to the attachment points of the vocal and thyroepiglottic ligaments to avoid iatrogenic injury (Fig. 3).

In cases involving ossified thyroid cartilage in older patients, resection was performed using a piezoelectric ultrasonic system (VarioSurg LED, NSK Nakanishi, Japan). Piezosurgery allows for selective cutting of mineralized tissue while preserving adjacent soft tissues, enabling more precise dissection.

The free edges of the cartilage were then approximated and sutured with a PDS 5 − 0 thread (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) to create a smooth, rounded anterior arch. This step ensured structural stability at the ligamentous attachment sites. The wound was closed in layers, and an intradermal suture was placed with Monocryl 5 − 0 thread (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA). Skin adhesive (Dermabond) could be used as an adjunct (Figs. 4A-E and 5A and B). A supplementary video file provides further detail (Video 1).

Results

Patient satisfaction was assessed via questionnaire between July and August 2025, at a mean follow-up of 78.5 ± 7.2 months. All 30 patients (100%) completed the survey. On the Likert scale, 90% of patients (27/30) rated the outcome as “very good,” and the remaining 10% (3/30) as “good” (Fig. 9).

The mean hospitalization duration was 2,5 ± 0,5 days. During the early postoperative period (first 14 days), 12 patients (40%) reported mild pain at the surgical site, which resolved completely in all patients by the end of the second week. No patient reported symptoms of persistent dysphonia or was diagnosed with permanent voice changes during the follow-up period. However, seven patients (23%) reported self-limiting temporary vocal weakness. This symptom was self-limiting and resolved completely in all seven patients within 7 to 21 days (mean 14 ± 5 days) postoperatively, confirming its transient nature and association with postoperative edema rather than nerve injury. The absence of postoperative objective voice assessment (e.g., stroboscopy) is a limitation of this retrospective study. No patient required revision surgery, and there were no systemic complications.

Discussion

Multiple surgical approaches for chondrolaryngoplasty exist, most deriving from the seminal technique described by Wolfort in 1975, which involves resection of the anterior thyroid cartilage angle [3]. Continued refinements to this method have been reported.

One notable modification, described by Sturm and Chaiet [4], involves resecting the superior border of the thyroid cartilage at the superior thyroid notch and prominence following perichondrial elevation. This technique can yield satisfactory outcomes; however, its principal drawback is the risk of intraoperative injury to the thyrohyoid membrane during superior dissection. Such injury potentially compromises the superior laryngeal nerve, leading to dysphonia. Our technique mitigates this risk by confining perichondrial elevation and cartilage resection strictly to the anterior angle. Nevertheless, a review of the literature and our clinical observations indicate that the cranial aspect of the thyroid cartilage can be prominent in some patients. Despite the high overall satisfaction rate, two patients (6.7%) reported a residual laryngeal prominence, perceiving the neck contour as insufficiently feminine. A retrospective review of these patients’ medical records and intraoperative photographs indicated that both cases featured a prominent cranial projection of the thyroid cartilage that extended beyond the area resected using our technique. In these cases, isolated anterior angle resection may be insufficient, resulting in residual prominence and potential patient dissatisfaction postoperatively. For such anatomical variations, the combined resection of the superior border and the anterior angle, as described by Sturm and Chaiet, remains justified. Our modified technique demonstrates optimal outcomes in patients with an isolated prominent anterior thyroid cartilage angle, thus providing clearer boundaries for its application.

A closed, transoral chondrolaryngoplasty technique via the sublingual space has also been described. Its main advantage is the absence of a visible cervical scar, a common patient concern. However, this approach is technically demanding, requires specialized endoscopic equipment, and carries a risk of injury to anatomical structures in the submandibular region and upper neck.

Spiegel and Rodriguez introduced a technique utilizing a laryngeal mask airway with concurrent fiberoptic bronchoscopy under general anesthesia [6]. This method provides direct visualization of the internal thyroid cartilage surface and, crucially, the vocal fold attachment sites, thereby facilitating a more radical resection while minimizing complication risks. Their cohort comprised 31 patients. According to the authors, no complications were observed in any patient. A single case of insufficient satisfaction was noted, attributed to residual prominence of the thyroid cartilage.

The retrospective analysis by Cohen MB et al. initially included 198 patients, with follow-up data available for 45. Among them, 13% reported suboptimal satisfaction, citing either scar conspicuity or residual cartilage projection as the cause [8].

The choice of surgical instruments is another critical consideration. While a scalpel is standard, Al-Jassim and Lesser recommend using rongeurs for ossified cartilage in older patients [5]. In our practice, we prefer a piezoelectric ultrasonic system (VarioSurg LED, NSK Nakanishi, Japan) for such cases, as it enables precise, tissue-selective cutting, facilitates the creation of smooth cartilage edges, and minimizes soft tissue trauma.

Secure re-approximation of the cartilage edges is essential. We utilize a PDS 5 − 0 suture on a round needle; its prolonged absorption time ensures adequate healing, and its tensile strength provides reliable fixation. Alternatively, miniplates may be used, typically in cases involving complete cartilage separation or during vocal pitch-raising procedures that require cricothyroid approximation.

Preoperative planning is vital for preventing vocal fold injury. Jazayeri et al. describe using direct laryngoscopy and computed tomography for vocal fold visualization [15]. Similarly, preoperative laryngoscopy is used to assess vocal fold mobility and anatomy. While it does not provide direct metric measurements, it informs the safe superior resection margin in conjunction with established surgical anatomical landmarks (e.g., the vocal ligament attachments.

Study limitations

The interpretation of our findings must consider several limitations. The retrospective design, absence of a control group, limited cohort size (n = 30), and single-center origin of the data may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our results. Furthermore, it is important to note that quality of life is a multifactorial outcome. The reported improvements likely reflect the combined effect of various gender-affirming interventions (such as hormone therapy and other surgical procedures) that patients underwent, and cannot be attributed solely to chondrolaryngoplasty. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger sample sizes and comparative control groups are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, the modified chondrolaryngoplasty technique presented herein is straightforward and reproducible, requiring no specialized equipment, which enhances its accessibility for surgical practice. The procedure is associated with a low risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications, which, as demonstrated by long-term follow-up, are overwhelmingly transient and self-resolving. Objective assessment at various rehabilitation stages demonstrates that the technique reliably produces a stable, predictable, and cosmetically favorable feminization of the neck contour. Patient-reported outcomes at long-term follow-up indicate high satisfaction with the aesthetic result and high overall quality of life.
