Extended applications of chest wall perforator flaps in breast surgery: case series and systematic literature review
Authors: Serene Si Ning Goh, Xinyan Zhou, Qin Xiang Ng, Dhananjay Kulkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChest wall perforator flaps (CWPFs) are integral to modern oncoplastic breast surgery, offering volume replacement without muscle sacrifice. While their role in partial breast reconstruction is established, data on extended and non-conventional applications remain limited. This study aims to synthesize current clinical experience and delineate the extended applications of chest wall perforator flaps beyond conventional oncoplastic contexts.MethodsThis study combined a prospective institutional case series from two tertiary centers (Singapore and UK) with a systematic literature review of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases. Eligible studies described CWPF use for extended oncologic, reconstructive, or aesthetic indications. Data were synthesized descriptively according to PRISMA guidelines.ResultsSix women underwent LICAP-based reconstruction for complex indications including skin-replacing excisions, implant salvage, chronic sinus closure, and total autologous reconstruction with adjunct lipofilling. All flaps were fully viable with good contour restoration and symmetry, and adjuvant therapy proceeded without delay. The systematic review identified 38 studies (≈ 324 patients, 349 flaps). CWPFs, most commonly LICAP, LTAP, and TDAP, were used for defects following oncologic resection, radiotherapy injury, implant failure, or post-bariatric contour correction. Overall flap success exceeded 95%, with major complications under 5%. Donor-site morbidity was minimal, with high aesthetic satisfaction (> 90%).ConclusionsCWPFs represent muscle-sparing reconstructive options whose utility may extend beyond conventional oncoplastic indications; this descriptive series supports their feasibility and short-term outcomes across a range of selected clinical settings, suggesting that CWPFs warrant further consideration in the management of complex breast and chest wall defects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10549-026-08030-3.Keywords: Chest wall perforator flaps (CWPFs), Intercostal perforator flaps (LICAP, LTAP, TDAP), Oncoplastic breast surgery; Skin-sparing mastectomy; Implant salvage; Total autologous breast reconstruction; Lipofilling

Background

Local perforator flaps have become an important adjunct in oncoplastic breast conserving surgery, particularly where volume replacement is necessary to achieve optimal cosmetic outcomes. Anatomical and clinical studies have thoroughly mapped chest-wall perforators arising from the intercostal and axillary artery systems, specifically the anterior, medial, and lateral intercostal branches and the lateral thoracic and thoracodorsal arteries [1]. These flaps are described according to their dominant vessel: lateral intercostal artery perforator (LICAP), anterior intercostal artery perforator (AICAP), medial intercostal artery perforator (MICAP), thoracodorsal artery perforator (TDAP), and lateral thoracic artery perforator (LTAP). Among these, the LICAP flap is the most frequently used, representing 55–70% of all local perforator flap reconstructions in contemporary series [2, 3].

Ideal candidates for local perforator flap reconstruction are women with small to moderate breast volumes and suitable Doppler-identified perforators. Such flaps avoid muscle harvest, resulting in lower donor-site morbidity and reduced pain compared with traditional musculocutaneous or myocutaneous options. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies involving 1,893 chest wall perforator flaps by Nava et al. reported a pooled recipient-site complication rate of 13%, flap loss rate of 1%, and donor-site complication rate of 4%, with consistently high patient satisfaction scores across studies [2]. In cases with close or uncertain margins, a staged (two-procedure) approach, performing wide local excision first and proceeding to reconstruction once clear margins are histologically confirmed, has been shown to enable flap preservation and margin re-excision while maintaining stable reconstructive outcomes. In a prospective series, Roy et al. (2017) demonstrated that this two-stage strategy allowed breast conservation in women otherwise recommended for mastectomy, achieving good-to-excellent cosmetic results in 90% of cases and high patient satisfaction, with low complication rates [4].

Beyond these conventional indications, CWPFs have potential utility across a broader range of complex reconstructive scenarios. In this manuscript, the term “extended” refers to expanded clinical indications rather than a modification of flap design, encompassing non-standard applications including skin-replacing excisions, nipple-areolar complex replacement, salvage of implant-based reconstructions following capsular contracture or radiation injury, total autologous breast reconstruction augmented by lipofilling, post-radiation wound salvage, and fistula closure. Despite a growing body of evidence for conventional use, these extended applications remain underexplored, with limited data on surgical outcomes, aesthetic durability, and integration into multidisciplinary treatment pathways.

Hence, this study aims to address this evidence gap by synthesizing current clinical experience and delineating the extended applications of chest wall perforator flaps beyond conventional oncoplastic contexts. Drawing upon a prospective institutional series from two tertiary breast units in conjunction with a systematic review of the international literature, this study sought to (i) map the evolving indications for extended CWPF use, (ii) describe key technical refinements, (iii) evaluate surgical outcomes, and (iv) identify remaining knowledge deficits to guide future research and clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and overview

This study comprised two complementary components: (1) a prospective institutional case series jointly conducted at the Western General Hospital (United Kingdom) and the National University Hospital (Singapore), and (2) a structured literature review of extended and non-conventional applications of chest-wall perforator flaps (CWPFs) in breast and chest-wall reconstruction. The study adhered to the CARE (CAse REport) checklist [5] for clinical case reporting and followed PRISMA principles in conducting and reporting the literature review [6]. All patients gave written informed consent permitting use of their clinical photographs for research and publication. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained under the Caldicott Guardian principles and reviewed by the relevant institutional ethics committee in the United Kingdom and Singapore.The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251162961).

Institutional case series

All consecutive patients who underwent CWPF reconstruction for indications beyond standard partial breast reconstruction between 2024 and 2025 were included. Eligible indications comprised: skin-replacing resections, salvage of implant-based reconstructions after capsular contracture or radiation injury, and complex wound or fistula management after prior breast surgery. Patients undergoing CWPF solely for conventional volume replacement after wide local excision were excluded.

Regarding data collection, clinical data were prospectively entered into institutional databases and verified against operative and postoperative records. Extracted variables included: patient demographics and comorbidities, oncologic diagnosis and indication for reconstruction, flap type and design, operative details (positioning, perforator mapping, pedicle characteristics, intra-operative findings), postoperative complications (graded using Clavien–Dindo), timing to adjuvant therapy, and functional and aesthetic outcomes documented at follow-up.

In terms of operative techniques, preoperative handheld Doppler ultrasonography identified dominant perforators, typically from the sixth or seventh intercostal spaces, 3–4 cm anterior to the latissimus dorsi border. Markings were performed in the upright position to delineate the inframammary fold, lateral mammary fold, and posterior axillary line. Tumour excision and sentinel-node biopsy were completed in the supine position, after which the patient was repositioned laterally or semi-laterally for flap harvest. A lazy-S or elliptical skin paddle centred over the mapped perforator was designed to allow primary donor-site closure. Dissection was performed in the subfascial plane, preserving the perforator with minimal skeletonisation. Flap perfusion was confirmed by handheld Doppler and direct visualisation. The flap was inset without tension, slightly over-corrected for anticipated radiotherapy-related volume loss, and secured in the cavity. A small dermal monitoring island was retained when feasible. Donor sites were closed primarily; drains were used only if axillary clearance was performed. Patients were discharged the same day or after overnight observation. Postoperative care included a supporting bra, and early follow-up for wound and cosmetic assessment. Aesthetic outcomes were assessed by the operating surgeon at the latest clinic follow-up using a four-point qualitative scale (poor, fair, good, or excellent), based on breast contour, symmetry, scar quality, and patient-reported satisfaction as documented in the clinical notes. Functional outcomes were defined as the absence of shoulder movement restriction, upper limb weakness, or donor-site symptoms at follow-up review.

Systematic literature review

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (last updated May 2025). The search combined the terms: (“chest wall perforator flap” OR “LICAP” OR “LTAP” OR “TDAP” OR “IMAP” OR “MICAP” OR “AICAP”) AND (“breast surgery” OR “breast reconstruction”) NOT (“partial breast reconstruction”). Reference lists of included articles were screened manually to capture additional relevant studies. No date restrictions were applied. Only papers written in English were included. The full search strategy can be found in the Appendix (Table S1).

Eligible studies were peer-reviewed clinical reports describing the use of CWPFs for extended or unconventional indications, including: whole-breast reconstruction after mastectomy, implant salvage, chest-wall or skin-replacing reconstruction after radiotherapy, burns, or trauma, and aesthetic or post-bariatric breast reshaping. Eligible designs included prospective or retrospective series, cohort studies, and case reports. Cadaveric, animal, purely technical papers without outcome data, reviews, and conference abstracts were excluded.

All identified citations were imported into Rayyan (Qatar Computing Research Institute) for deduplication and blinded screening. Two reviewers (XY and SSNG) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (QXN). Data were extracted using a standardized Excel® form, capturing study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), patient demographics, surgical indications, LICAP flap technique, reconstructive goals, complications, cosmetic outcomes, and oncological follow-up.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical-appraisal checklists for case reports, case series and single-armed cohort studies [7, 8]. Quality grading focused on clarity of inclusion criteria, completeness of outcome reporting, and duration of follow-up. Because most studies were small and descriptive, formal meta-analysis was not feasible. Findings were synthesized narratively and summarized in tabular form, stratified by flap category (LICAP / non-LICAP). The outcomes assessed were the novel applications of CWPFs, perioperative and flap-related complications, and qualitative aesthetic and functional outcomes (as reported by the original authors).

Synthesis of findings

Data from the case series and literature review were integrated descriptively. Results are presented in summary tables (Tables 1 and 2) and visually in a PRISMA flowchart. Where available, numeric data on complication rates and satisfaction were collated, but no quantitative pooling was undertaken due to heterogeneity in study design and reporting.

Results

Institutional case series

Between 2024 and 2025, six women (age range 30–70 years) underwent CWPF reconstruction at the Western General Hospital and National University Hospital Singapore. All patients had LICAP-based reconstruction with indications beyond conventional partial breast reconstruction such as to replace excised skin, replace nipple-areolar complex, augment soft-tissue bulk for implant salvage in irradiated or thinned skin, and, when combined with lipofilling, to achieve full autologous breast volume replacement (Table 1) and (Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary Figures S1, S2 and S3). Comorbidities included prior radiotherapy (n = 2), none were smokers. Follow-up ranged from 18 to 26 months (median 24 months) across the six cases.

Case 1

A patient in her fifties presented with an ulcerated, bleeding nipple. Punch biopsy confirmed Paget’s disease, and imaging revealed no underlying DCIS or invasive carcinoma. She underwent a full-thickness wide local excision of the nipple–areola complex, with the LICAP flap used to fill the resulting central defect and provide skin cover. Margins were histologically clear, and the flap settled well after adjuvant radiotherapy with good cosmetic outcome. Patient may consider areolar tattooing in the future.

Case 2

A patient in her thirties presented with a left breast mass proven to be invasive carcinoma NST grade 3 with nodal involvement and extensive associated microcalcifications consistent with DCIS. Following partial response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, she underwent a large lateral wide excision crossing the nipple–areola complex with axillary clearance. The defect was reconstructed using a combination approach with a LICAP flap for volume replacement, supplemented with abdominal fat grafting in the surrounding breast tissue to add volume to restore contour. She required a margin re-excision for focal involvement, which was successfully performed by retracting the flap to access the cavity, achieving a clear final margin. Final symmetry and aesthetic outcome was preserved.

Case 3

A patient in her late sixties presented with an inflamed fistulous tract at a previous lumpectomy site twelve years with a hard underlying lump after treatment for breast cancer. Imaging excluded recurrence, and histology confirmed fat necrosis. Excision of the fistula and superficial thin skin and fat necrosis tissue was performed, and the defect was resurfaced using a LICAP flap. The flap provided both skin cover and volume to fill the cavity left after excision of the fat necrosis mass. A LICAP flap was chosen over a random-pattern transposition flap because the defect involved irradiated tissue from prior radiotherapy, where random-pattern flaps based on uncharacterized local vascularity carry a higher risk of wound-healing complications. By contrast, the LICAP flap was based on a reliable, Doppler-confirmed perforator and provided well-vascularized soft tissue from outside the irradiated field, favouring better wound healing.

Case 4

A woman in her seventies presented with a 2.5 cm superficial right upper-outer-quadrant tumor associated with mild skin puckering. This was a very superficial ER positive cancer with skin involvement. She underwent wide local excision, including the overlying skin, together with sentinel lymph node biopsy. A LICAP flap was used for simultaneous volume replacement and skin resurfacing. The patient achieved excellent aesthetic results and clear oncologic margins, with no complications.

Case 5

A woman in her sixties, previously treated with nipple-sparing mastectomy and implant reconstruction, presented with post-radiation capsular contracture and thinned upper-outer-quadrant skin over the implant. As there was no plane between the skin and the capsule, lipofilling/ fat grafting was not an option. She underwent capsulectomy and implant exchange, with interposition of a LICAP flap between the skin and the new implant to augment soft-tissue thickness and improve contour. Postoperatively, the skin quality and implant pliability improved significantly.

Case 6

A 59-year-old woman with a 5.2 cm central, hormone receptor–positive, node-negative tumor involving the nipple declined neoadjuvant chemotherapy and muscle flap reconstruction. She underwent skin-sparing mastectomy with immediate LICAP flap reconstruction supplemented by intraoperative lipofilling to enhance volume. 100 mL of processed lipoaspirate was injected in a single session, representing the maximum harvestable volume from the available donor sites (bilateral thighs and flanks). The lipoaspirate was distributed across the pectoralis muscle, overlying skin flaps, and reconstructed breast envelope in a multilayer fanning technique to optimize contour and volume. Histology confirmed clear margins, and the patient achieved acceptable symmetry with preserved breast contour following adjuvant radiotherapy.

Overall, all six flaps demonstrated complete viability with no necrosis or wound complications. Functional recovery was good in all patients. Three patients (Cases 1, 2, and 6) underwent curative resection for primary breast cancer and received adjuvant radiotherapy within six weeks of initial surgery. The LICAP flap consistently provided reliable volume replacement, durable skin coverage when required, and aesthetically favourable outcomes across a spectrum of complex oncologic and reconstructive scenarios.

In both centers, a standardized operative workflow was followed for all reconstructions, emphasizing meticulous preoperative planning, perforator mapping, and tension-free flap inset to ensure reproducible results. Each patient underwent preoperative outpatient Doppler assessment to identify dominant perforators, most commonly arising from the 6th or 7th intercostal spaces approximately 3–4 cm anterior to the latissimus dorsi border. Markings were performed in the upright position to delineate the inframammary fold, lateral mammary fold, and posterior axillary line. Wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy were performed in the supine position, followed by flap harvest in the lateral position. A “lazy-S” or elliptical skin paddle centered over the mapped perforator was designed using the skin displacement technique to facilitate direct donor-site closure. Flaps were raised in the subfascial plane, with careful preservation of the intercostal perforator and avoidance of pleural breach. Intraoperative perfusion was confirmed using handheld Doppler and direct visualization of the pedicle. The flap was inset without tension and slightly overcorrected to compensate for the anticipated volume reduction following adjuvant radiotherapy. A small skin island was retained for perfusion monitoring, and transient venous congestion when encountered was relieved by releasing kinks or widening the subcutaneous tunnel. All donor sites were closed primarily, and patients were discharged on the same day; surgical drains were placed only when axillary clearance was performed.

Results of systematic review

Based on the literature retrieval, a total of 406 records were screened, before 38 studies were finally included. The reports contained approximately 324 patients and 349 (25 bilateral) CWPF were identified, encompassing both oncologic and non-oncologic indications. There were 18 case series, 16 case reports, one retrospective cohort study, and three prospective observational studies summarized in Table 2 [9–46]. The majority of studies originated from Europe and Asia, with a smaller number from North America. Eleven studies (29%) described the use LICAP or LICAP-based flaps, while the remaining 27 (71%) reported other CWPF variants, including anteroinferior (AICAP), lateral thoracic (LTAP), thoracodorsal (TDAP), internal mammary (IMAP), dorsal intercostal (DICAP) and hybrid combinations such as LICAP–AICAP or TDAP–DICAP.

Risk of bias and reporting quality

Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for case series and case reports, and single-arm cohort studies (Supplementary Tables S1-4). Overall methodological quality was moderate, with generally clear descriptions of operative technique and outcomes but limited follow-up and lack of objective cosmetic or volumetric assessment. Common sources of bias included small sample size, single-surgeon or single-centre design, short follow-up, and subjective aesthetic grading.

Indications

The indications for reconstruction were diverse: oncologic defects following mastectomy or wide local excision involving resection of overlying skin, which accounted for nearly half of the cases (46%), while post-radiation ulcers and salvage reconstructions comprised 26%. Aesthetic or post-bariatric contouring indications represented 21%, and the remaining 7% addressed trauma, infection, or soft-tissue contracture. Several novel adaptations were described, including supercharged DICAP flaps, delayed TDAP flaps, and contralateral IMAP pedicled flaps transferred across the midline for inner-quadrant and parasternal defects.

Timing of reconstruction

A review of the timing of reconstruction across the included studies demonstrates a strong historical and indication-based trend. Earlier applications of LICAP and CWPFs were predominantly delayed, reflecting their initial use in secondary reconstruction and salvage of complex or irradiated defects. These included post-radiation ulcers [25, 29], osteoradionecrosis [44], post-burn deformities [28], and contour restoration following massive weight loss [18, 20, 38]. In contrast, recent publications from 2019 onward [9, 15, 33, 43] increasingly describe immediate CWPF reconstruction following oncologic excision. A small subset of reports [40, 41] described two-stage or “surgically delayed” reconstructions, in which the flap was raised or transferred after a short delay (typically 5–7 days). This staged approach, akin to a vascular conditioning maneuver, enhanced flap reliability and allowed safe expansion of perforasomal territory for total autologous breast reconstruction.

Perforator mapping

Perforator mapping was performed in nearly all studies, most commonly using handheld Doppler, while one quarter incorporated computed tomographic angiography (CTA) or intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) angiography to guide dissection and assess flap perfusion. Flap dimensions ranged from 8 × 12 cm to 26 × 14 cm, and most were raised as islanded or propeller flaps. Primary donor-site closure was achieved in nearly all cases. Operative time varied according to complexity, ranging from 90 to 440 min, and patient positioning was typically lateral or supine for LICAP/AICAP designs, and prone–supine for TDAP-based reconstructions.

Complications

Across the included CWPF studies, there were only two cases of total flap loss: one IMAP flap in the Novo-Torres et al. series (1/7, 14%) [23] and one IMAP flap with total necrosis in the Finelle et al. cohort (1/23, 4.3%) [37]. No total losses were reported for LICAP, LTAP or TDAP flaps in the remaining series. Partial-thickness problems were uncommon and typically limited to small distal segments or early-experience cases: e.g. five minor tip necroses (< 10% of surface each) in Lorenzen et al. (all revised successfully) [45], three partial necroses (13%) in Finelle et al. [37], and single partial necrosis/venous congestion events in Gunnarsson et al. (both resolved) [22]. Other complications were mostly minor: seroma (isolated cases across series), delayed wound healing or small dehiscence (sporadic), and fat necrosis in IMAP “breast-sharing” cases (Novo-Torres et al., 3/7) [23]. Infection was rare and generally unrelated to the flap itself. Importantly, no study reported a systematic delay to adjuvant therapy attributable to the use of CWPFs. Overall, CWPFs, particularly LICAP/LTAP/TDAP, show a favorable safety profile, with IMAP losses confined to isolated reports.

Functional and aesthetic outcomes

Functional recovery was uniformly good across the pooled cohort. No study in this review reported significant limitation of shoulder movement or upper-limb function following CWPF harvest. Minor donor-site morbidity, such as seroma, localized wound dehiscence, or mild donor-site tightness, was occasionally noted but did not result in functional impairment. None of the series described chronic shoulder weakness, tethering, or activity-related discomfort, even in flaps requiring 180° rotation or large skin paddles. Cosmetic outcomes were favourable in almost all reports. Across the included studies, over 90% of patients achieved good to excellent aesthetic results, as judged by either surgeon or patient assessment. Most reconstructions achieved satisfactory breast mound projection and contour restoration, with scars well concealed along the bra line or inframammary fold. Aesthetic improvement was also noted in symmetry and upper-pole fullness, particularly in post-bariatric and hybrid reconstruction series. However, evaluation methods were largely subjective, relying on qualitative descriptors such as “good,” “satisfactory,” or “excellent.” Only a minority of studies incorporated objective or validated tools. Virzì et al. used the BREAST-Q scale [43], reporting high satisfaction scores (satisfaction 69, psychosocial 71, physical 68), and Hammad et al. performed sono-mammographic volumetry showing stable contour at one year [38].

Discussion

This review and accompanying case series demonstrate that the role of CWPF, particularly the lateral intercostal artery perforator flap, has evolved beyond its initial description as a reconstructive adjunct for wide local excision. The ideal candidates for local CWPF surgery are typically women undergoing primary breast-conserving surgery with small to moderate breast volumes and favorable Doppler signals from a suitably located perforator. A recent international multicentre evidence from Karakatsanis et al. (2024), encompassing over 600 patients across Sweden, the UK, and Australia, reinforces the safety and utility of CWPFs as an alternative to mastectomy [47]. In this cohort, 67.7% of procedures were performed specifically to avoid mastectomy, with an overall complication rate of 8.6% and no flap losses. Economically, CWPFs may also represent a cost-effective alternative to traditional reconstructive options. Comparative analyses have shown that thoracodorsal artery perforator flaps are more cost-effective than latissimus dorsi flaps, largely due to the preservation of muscle, reduced shoulder-related disability, and avoidance of implant-associated costs [48]. The ability to perform CWPF reconstruction as a day surgery in nearly 70% of patients [47] may further enhance their healthcare efficiency and accessibility, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Across most published studies in the oncologic setting, immediate rather than delayed reconstruction with CWPFs following wide local excision is increasingly being reported. However, delayed reconstruction remains an important option in complex scenarios [1, 47]. For instance, Feyta et al. (2022) described the successful use of enlarged and pre-expanded perforator flaps, based on anterior intercostal, thoracoacromial, and superior epigastric artery perforators, to correct post-burn chest deformities, achieving over 90% complete flap engraftment and durable contour restoration with minimal donor-site morbidity [28]. Complementing these, Hoffman et al. (2023) and Maier et al. (2024) introduced the surgically delayed thoracodorsal artery perforator flap as a reliable muscle-sparing option for patients unsuitable for abdominally based free flaps [40, 41]. Using a two-stage delay technique, the flap is partially elevated while preserving a distal skin bridge, allowing vascular conditioning through dilation of choke vessels and recruitment of collateral flow before final transfer. Doppler assessment confirmed increased perforator caliber and flow, translating to greater flap reliability and expanded harvestable volume. This approach may extend the perfusion limits of CWPFs, potentially enabling safe bilateral or high-volume autologous reconstructions in complex or previously irradiated fields.

Beyond the role of CWPFs in immediate and delayed reconstruction of breast following breast conserving surgery, they are increasing being used to address a wide range of complex oncoplastic problems. Their versatility makes them valuable in difficult situations such as scar contracture and breast distortion, skin loss, loss of nipple areola, chronic fistula tracts as described in our series. One advantage of CWPFs lies in their ability to provide skin replacement when the tumour is close to or involves the skin or NAC. In such cases, the overlying skin and NAC can be excised for oncologic clearance, while the skin paddle of the perforator flap simultaneously restores the defect and replenishes volume. When the NAC is sacrificed, the LICAP flap may offer an additional benefit: it provides skin and soft tissue coverage derived from outside the native breast envelope, preserving breast shape and projection while minimizing the need for contralateral symmetrization procedures required in techniques such as the Grisotti flap. Recent advances have further extended the reconstructive potential of CWPFs beyond partial breast defects to selected cases of total autologous breast reconstruction. Early anatomical and clinical work by Van Landuyt et al. (2004) and Hamdi et al. (2004) established the feasibility of using pedicled perforators from the thoracodorsal and intercostal systems to replace significant breast volume without muscle sacrifice, achieving durable, natural results with minimal donor-site morbidity [1, 21]. Building on this evolution, Yee et al. (2023) introduced the modified chest-wall lateral intercostal artery perforator (MCW-LICAP) flap [9], reinforcing that CWPFs can reliably achieve whole-breast reconstruction in appropriately selected patients. Five patients in their series underwent MCW-LICAP combined with Goldilocks mastectomy, and one had a stacked intercostal artery perforator configuration, highlighting the adaptability of CWPFs for total autologous reconstruction without microsurgery. The authors also observed a rapid learning curve, where operative efficiency improved after the sixth case, supporting reproducibility in general oncoplastic practice. Complementing these findings, our own series demonstrated that combining a LICAP flap with lipofilling can achieve total breast reconstruction without prosthetic support, highlighting the potential role of CWPFs as a standalone autologous option for comprehensive breast restoration. In terms of lipofilling, when performed immediately, fat is injected into the pectoralis muscle, and subcutaneous tissues; if performed later, fat can also be grafted directly into the flap to refine contour and projection. The goal of lipofilling is to optimize symmetry and augment volume, particularly in patients who have received radiotherapy, where additional sessions of fat grafting may be necessary. Increasingly, surgeons are combining multiple reconstructive techniques to achieve both aesthetic and functional excellence, especially in complex cases or when donor tissue is limited.

The reported advantages of CWPFs extend beyond their versatility in defect coverage. Several studies have reported that CWPFs achieve adequate volume and projection without sacrificing muscle or requiring prosthetic implants, thus preserving shoulder function and minimizing donor-site complications such as seroma or contour deformity [49, 50]. A concealed lateral or inframammary scar, together with operative characteristics reported in individual series such as shorter operating time and reduced blood loss may make these flaps an attractive option in early breast cancer and risk-reducing settings [1, 22]. In comparison with latissimus dorsi, transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous, or free flaps, CWPFs have also been reported to be associated with fewer complications and faster convalescence [21, 45, 50]. The avoidance of microsurgical anastomosis and the ability to close donor sites primarily make CWPFs feasible even in elderly or medically complex patients [9]. Innovative adaptations such as supercharged DICAP flaps, contralateral LICAP or IMAP transfers, and combined flap-lipofilling approaches, further underscore their versatility and expanding role in modern oncoplastic practice [23, 27, 31, 32, 39]. Some series have also suggested that perforator-based transposition techniques may offer improved projection, reduced tension, and better contour symmetry, however, these remain observations from non-comparative studies [4, 51].

This study is among the first to comprehensively examine CWPFs for non-conventional applications beyond partial breast reconstruction. While the institutional series focuses on LICAP-based reconstructions, the systematic review intentionally encompasses the broader CWPF family including LTAP, TDAP, IMAP, and AICAP variants. This was to provide the most clinically useful comparative context. These flaps share the same muscle-sparing principle and anatomical basis, and are frequently considered alongside one another in reconstructive decision-making. By adopting this broader scope, the review captures the full breadth of clinically impactful literature on extended applications, including delayed TDAP flaps for total autologous reconstruction and IMAP flaps for chest wall salvage. This is so as to offer surgeons a more comprehensive and practically applicable evidence synthesis. Several limitations of the available evidence warrant acknowledgement. The majority of included studies were retrospective case series with limited follow-up durations and inconsistent outcome reporting, which may underrepresent late complications or contour irregularities. The lack of randomized comparative studies precludes any firm conclusions regarding comparative superiority over established muscle-based or free-flap techniques and the lack of standardized patient-reported outcome measures (such as the BREAST-Q) further limits the ability to quantify aesthetic and functional outcomes objectively. The predominantly single-centre design of included studies may also limit generalisability across different patient populations and surgical volumes. Despite these limitations, this study establishes an important foundation for future multicentre research and contributes meaningfully to the growing recognition of CWPFs as feasible, versatile reconstructive options with promising short-term outcomes in selected patients. It also raises awareness among reconstructive and oncoplastic surgeons, expanding the surgical armamentarium available for managing increasingly challenging oncological indications.

Conclusion

CWPFs are a useful addition to the modern oncoplastic armamentarium, combining oncologic safety with acceptable aesthetic outcomes. This study suggests that, beyond conventional partial breast reconstruction, CWPFs can be adapted for more complex indications such as skin replacement, implant salvage, and selected cases of total autologous reconstruction, with good short-term outcomes. The current evidence base remains largely descriptive, drawn from small single-centre series with limited follow-up; nonetheless, the consistency of findings across a range of indications supports wider consideration of CWPFs as a reliable and accessible reconstructive option. These findings provide a basis for prospective multicentre studies with standardized outcome reporting, including validated patient-reported measures and longer oncological follow-up, which will be needed to more fully define the role of CWPFs in breast and chest wall reconstruction.
