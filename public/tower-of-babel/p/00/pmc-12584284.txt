Clinical profiling, antifungal drug susceptibility, and biofilm formation ability in pulmonary mucormycosis
Authors: Peng Wang, Yaqin Peng, Jun Liu, Tianmeng Li, Dan Zhang, Li Gu, Qiangyi Wang, Mingze Su, Ming Wei, Shuai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary mucormycosis (PM), a life-threatening infection caused by Mucorales, exhibits high mortality. Comprehensive data integrating clinical profiling, antifungal susceptibility, and biofilm formation ability are limited. This retrospective study characterized 26 adult PM patients at a Beijing tertiary-care hospital. Data on demographics, underlying diseases, co-infections, and outcomes were collected. Clinical Mucorales isolates underwent molecular identification via 18 S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. In vitro susceptibility against nine antifungals (amphotericin B, fluconazole, voriconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin, 5-flucytosine) was determined using Sensititre YeastOne YO10 panels. Biofilm biomass was assessed (24 h, 48 h, 72 h) via the crystal violet staining assay. Patients were predominantly older males (median age 66 years, 65.4% male) with high comorbidity burden (92.3%). All-cause in-hospital mortality was 38.5%. Strikingly, 80.8% had co-infections. Temporal analysis revealed that viral (77.8%) and fungal (62.5%) co-infections often preceded the detection of Mucorales. Molecular identification confirmed Rhizopus spp. (54%) predominated, followed by Rhizomucor spp. (19%), Mucor spp. (19%), and Lichtheimia spp. (8%). Antifungal testing showed amphotericin B possessed the most consistent activity (MIC50/MIC90: 1/2 µg/mL). Posaconazole was the most potent azole (MIC50/MIC90: 0.25/1 µg/mL), but profound genus-level heterogeneity was observed. Biofilm assessment at the 48-h peak revealed biofilm formation in 84.6% (22/26) of isolates. This study highlights the high prevalence of antecedent viral/fungal co-infections preceding Mucorales detection and significant mortality, despite in vitro susceptibility to amphotericin B/posaconazol. In addition, most of the strains demonstrated biofilm formation ability, with evident genus-level heterogeneity. These findings emphasize the imperative of species-level identification and consideration of genus-specific traits to guide effective management of this life-threatening infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04472-9.

Introduction

Pulmonary mucormycosis (PM), a highly lethal invasive fungal infection caused by fungi within the order Mucorales, has emerged as a significant and escalating threat to critically ill patients globally [1]. Marked by rapid tissue destruction, vascular invasion, thrombosis, and necrosis, PM carries an alarmingly high mortality rate, often exceeding 50% even with aggressive therapeutic interventions [2]. In recent decades, the incidence of mucormycosis, including its pulmonary manifestation, has shown an upward trajectory, driven by the increased use of immunomodulating therapies for cancer and autoimmune diseases, as well as advancements in diagnostic techniques that have improved the detection rate [3]– [4]. Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic acted as a potential new risk factor, contributing to unprecedented surges in mucormycosis cases, which may involve a complex interplay between viral damage, immune dysregulation (including corticosteroid use), and opportunistic fungal invasion [5]– [6].

The most common genera associated with human mucormycosis include Rhizopus, Mucor, and Rhizomucor [7]. Accurate identification to the species level is important for both clinical management and epidemiological surveillance [8]. Traditional methods of morphological identification, which rely on colony appearance and microscopic characteristics, demand a high level of expertise and are prone to misidentification, especially due to the subtle distinctions between different species. This diagnostic conundrum frequently leads to delays in initiating appropriate therapy, directly contributing to poor outcomes. Molecular techniques, such as PCR targeting ribosomal RNA genes (e.g., 18 S rRNA, 28 S rRNA, ITS regions) and increasingly, sequencing, offer improved sensitivity and the critical ability for species-level identification directly from clinical specimens or culture isolates [9].

Epidemiological data on PM in China remain limited, with most published studies lacking comprehensive microbiological characterization, including species identification and antifungal susceptibility profiles. Research characterizing the antifungal susceptibility of clinical Mucorales helps guide appropriate antifungal therapy [10]. Although standardized antifungal susceptibility testing methods exist for filamentous fungi, their application and interpretation for Mucorales are not as routine or well-established as for other fungal pathogens like Candida or Aspergillus species [11]– [12]. Empirical treatment decisions are frequently made without precise knowledge of the infecting strain’s susceptibility profile. However, antifungal susceptibility can vary significantly between and even within genera of Mucorales. Thus, comprehending the antifungal susceptibility profiles of Mucorales is crucial for optimizing therapeutic outcomes on an individual basis.

Beyond planktonic susceptibility, the role of biofilm formation in filamentous fungi is an emerging area of concern with profound clinical implications [13]. Fungal Biofilms are structured communities of microbial cells encased in an extracellular polymeric substance, adherent to mucosal or abiotic surfaces, confer significant resistance to both host immune defenses and antifungal agents, often orders of magnitude higher than their planktonic counterparts [14]. Consequently, in the context of PM, biofilm formation could be relevant on necrotic tissue surfaces, inhaled foreign bodies, or potentially on endotracheal tubes in ventilated patients, creating sanctuary sites that evade immune clearance and drug penetration, promoting persistence and treatment failure [15]. However, due to the rarity of the disease, there are few relevant studies on the biofilm of the clinical Mucorales strains. Understanding the biofilm-forming potential of clinically relevant Mucorales isolates is crucial for explaining recalcitrant infections and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) describe the demographic and clinical characteristics, including underlying conditions, co-infections, and outcomes of 26 PM patients in a tertiary-care hospital, over a recent four-year period; (2) determine the in vitro susceptibility profiles of clinical isolates against a panel of nine antifungal agents; and (3) investigate the biofilm formation ability of these isolates. By integrating clinical and microbiological data, this study provides valuable insights into epidemiology, antifungal resistance patterns, and biofilm-associated virulence of PM, informing future treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This retrospective study consisted of adult hospitalized patients (≥ 18 years of age) diagnosed as PM in a tertiary-care hospital in Beijing, China, between April 2021 and March 2024. Patients enrolled in this study should meet the following inclusive criteria: (1) diagnosed with PM based on the clinical guidelines from Medical Mycology Society of Chinese Medicine and Education Association (2022) [16]; (2) the culture results of sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), or other respiratory specimens were Mucorales. Patients were excluded if there was incomplete data in the medical records. Demographic information, underlying diseases, microbiologic data, and clinical outcomes were collected. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital, Capital Medical University (April 16, 2024).

Clinical strains and culture conditions

In this study, Mucorales isolates were obtained from a strain collection established by culturing clinical samples from patients diagnosed with PM. In addition to collecting clinical data, the microbiological characteristics of pathogenic Mucorales isolates were also analyzed. Standard strain Rhizopus arrhizus ATCC 56,536, purchased from China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center, was used as a control. All the strains were cultivated on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) at 35 °C for 2–5 days.

Molecular identification of fungi

Characterized with well-developed fluffy cotton or wool-like aerial hyphae, Mucorales isolates were first identified based on structures such as sporangia, columellae, sporangiophores, and rhizoids under a high-power microscope after being stained with reagents of lactophenol cotton blue. Morphologically confirmed Mucorales isolates were further identified by the molecular method. The genomic DNA of fungi was extracted using a PlantZol kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The 18 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers NS1 (5′-GTAGTCATATGCTTGTCTC-3′) and NS8 (5′-TCCGCAGGTTCACCTACGGA-3′) [17]. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed in a 30 µL reaction mixture containing 15 µL of 2× EasyTaq SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), 1 µL of each primer (10 mM), 2 µL of DNA template, and the remaining volume with ddH2O.The reaction conditions were 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 60 °C for 3 min.

To identify the isolates at the species level, all the sequences were subjected to BLAST analysis in NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) using the 18 S ribosomal RNA sequences (SSU) database from the Fungi type and reference material. All the sequence data were deposited in GenBank (accession numbers PV299927 to PV299952).

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic analysis of fungi was conducted in MEGA 12 software using the Neighbor-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [18]. The phylogenetic tree was subsequently visualized.

Antifungal susceptibility testing

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed for nine drugs, namely, amphotericin B, fluconazole, voriconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin, and 5-flucytosine, using the Sensititre YeastOne YO10 panels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The inoculum suspensions were diluted with YeastOne broth to a final inoculum density of 0.5–5.5 × 104 CFU/mL. The dried panels were rehydrated with 100 µL of the working suspension in each well. The panels were covered with seal strips and incubated at 35 °C for 24–72 h (until sufficient growth was present in the growth control well). The growth of fungi reduces the indicator, resulting in a visible color change: blue to pink. Wells without growth remain the original color. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of an antifungal agent that substantially inhibits the growth of fungi, as detected visually when testing most antifungal agents. Rhizopus arrhizus ATCC 56,536 reference strain was used as a quality control strain.

Biofilm formation assay

The biofilm formation assay was carried out in polystyrene, sterile, flat-bottomed 24-well microtiter plates (Corning, NY, USA). 2 mL of suspension of spores in potato dextrose broth (PDB; Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) was added (~ 105 spores/mL). PDB contains 5 g of potato dipping powder, 10 g of soybean meal, 15 g of glucose, and 5 g of sodium chloride per liter. The plate was cultivated statically at 35 °C for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h.

The total biofilm biomass was determined using a crystal violet (CV) staining assay according to Kulisova et al., with some modifications [19]. After removing fungal suspension from the microtiter plate wells, the wells were washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove nonadherent cells. Subsequently, the biofilms were fixed with 2 mL methanol for 15 min, stained with 2 mL CV (0.2%) for 20 min, and washed with distilled water. Then, the bound crystal violet was extracted with 1 mL of 33% acetic acid. 200 µL of the obtained solutions were transferred to a new flat-bottom 96-well plate, and the OD630 nm values were measured using a Multiskan EX microplate photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). The CV assays were performed in 3 independent biological replicates, with each treatment in 3 repetitions.

Wells with PDB medium were used as negative controls. The OD cut-off value (ODc) for biofilm formation was defined as three standard deviations above the mean OD value of the negative control. The isolates were categorized as strong biofilm producers (OD630 nm ˃ 4× ODc), moderate biofilm producers (2× ODc ˂ OD630 nm ≤ 4× ODc), weak biofilm producers (ODc ˂ OD630 nm ≤ 2× ODc), and non-biofilm producers (OD630 nm ≤ ODc) [20].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 software program (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), or median and interquartile range (IQR). A statistical comparison between the two groups was performed by the Chi-square test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way ANOVA, followed by the LSD test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients with PM

From 2021 to 2024, 29 patients with proven mucormycosis were admitted to our hospital, of which 3 patients, who had no microbiological culture results from respiratory samples, were excluded for further analyses.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of these 26 patients. The median age of those patients was 66 (IQR: 49–77) years. 17 patients were male (65.4%) and 9 were female (34.6%). 92.3% had underlying illnesses. Hypertension (53.8%), diabetes (46.2%), and chronic liver disease (42.3%) were the most common underlying illnesses in the total study population. The median hospital stay of these patients was 19 (IQR: 9–34) days. At the time of admission, 80.8% were co-infected with other pathogens. Nine (34.6%) patients were co-infected with viruses, 11 (42.3%) with bacteria, and 16 (61.5%) with other fungi. The types of viruses that cause co-infection include Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, influenza A, SARS-CoV-2, etc.; the bacterial species include Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, etc.; fungal species include Candida spp., Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus niger, etc. The all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was 38.5%. The clinical characteristics of patients with different outcomes were further analyzed, as shown in Table 2. The variables included age, gender, underlying diseases, co-infection, and length of hospital stay; the differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, timelines from the first positive Mucorales-positive sample collection are reported in Fig. 1. Respectively, 77.8% (7/9) of viral infections, 36.4% (4/11) of bacterial infections, and 62.5% (10/16) of fungal infections occurred before Mucorales were detected.

The 18 s rRNA gene sequences (~ 1,700 bp) of Mucorales isolates were compared with those of 15 other reference strains obtained from GenBank for difference comparison and phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 2). Overall, amphotericin B exhibited the lowest MICs against Mucorales isolates, the MIC range was narrow (0.5–2 µg/mL), with MIC50 = 1 µg/mL and MIC90 = 2 µg/mL. Among the azoles, posaconazole was the most potent, although the MIC range was broader (MIC range = ≤ 0.015->8 µg/ml), with MIC50 = 0.25 µg/mL and MIC90 = 1 µg/mL. Itraconazole showed variable antifungal activity (≤ 0.015->16 µg/mL); while the MIC50 was low (0.25 µg/mL), the MIC90 exceeded the highest concentration tested (> 16 µg/mL). Voriconazole (MIC range: 2 - >8 µg/mL, MIC50: 8 µg/mL, MIC90: >8 µg/mL) and fluconazole (MIC range: 128 - >256 µg/mL, MIC50 > 256 µg/mL, MIC90 > 256 µg/mL) demonstrated relatively poorer antifungal activities. All three echinocandin antifungal agents (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin) showed no appreciable activity, with MIC50 and MIC90 values exceeding 8 µg/mL for all agents. Similarly, 5-flucytosine was completely inactive against all isolates (MICs > 64 µg/mL).

Among Mucorales, significant differences in susceptibility profiles were observed between genera. For Rhizopus spp., amphotericin B (MIC50/MIC90: 1/2 µg/mL) and posaconazole (MIC50/MIC90: 0.25/1 µg/mL) were the most active agents. Itraconazole showed a low MIC50 (0.25 µg/mL) but a high MIC90 (> 16 µg/mL). Voriconazole was relatively ineffective (MIC50/MIC90: >8 µg/mL). Fluconazole, echinocandins, and 5-flucytosine displayed universally low levels of activity. For Rhizomucor spp., triazoles demonstrated the highest activity level. Both posaconazole (MIC50/MIC90: 0.03/0.12 µg/mL) and itraconazole (MIC50/MIC90: ≤0.015/0.25 µg/mL) exhibited potent activity. Voriconazole showed moderate MICs (MIC50/MIC90: 4/8 µg/mL). Amphotericin B was consistently active (MIC50/MIC90: 1/2 µg/mL). For Mucor spp., amphotericin B was highly active (MIC50/MIC90: 1/1 µg/mL). Posaconazole (MIC50/MIC90: 1/8 µg/mL) showed variable antifungal activity; itraconazole and voriconazole demonstrated very poor activity. For Lichtheimia spp., interpretation is limited by the small sample size. Itraconazole, posaconazole, and amphotericin B demonstrated relatively higher levels of activity against all the tested isolates (Table S1).

Biofilm formation ability

The biofilm biomass of the tested strains at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h was evaluated using the CV staining assay. The one-way ANOVA followed by the LSD test was employed to assess the statistical significance of the experimental data. Pairwise comparisons between groups revealed no statistically significant differences (Fig. 3A). As half of the strains (13/26) exhibited peak OD630 nm values at 48 h (Table S2), the experimental data at the 48-hour time point were chosen for further analysis of the biofilm formation ability of all strains. As shown in Figs. 3B and 84.6% (22/26) of the strains demonstrated biofilm formation ability. Among these, 20 strains were categorized as weak biofilm formers and two as moderate biofilm formers, with no strong biofilm-forming strains observed. Both strains with moderate biofilm formation ability belong to the genus Lichtheimia, while the 4 strains without biofilm formation ability belong to the genus Rhizopus.

Discussion

This retrospective study provides a detailed clinical and microbiological characterization of 26 adult patients with PM in a tertiary-care hospital in Beijing, China, over a recent four-year period. Our findings underscore the severity of PM, reveal complex microbial interactions, highlight the critical importance of species-level identification and susceptibility testing, and provide novel insights into the biofilm-forming potential specific to various genera of Mucorales.

This study observed that the predominance of older males (median age 66 years, 65.4% male) is consistent with the demographic information for mucormycosis reported by Ozbek et al. [6]. The exceptionally high burden of comorbidities (92.3%), particularly hypertension (53.8%), diabetes (46.2%), and chronic liver disease (42.3%), underscores the role of host factors in susceptibility. Diabetes remains a well-documented major risk factor, often associated with impaired neutrophil function and acidosis, which is favourable for Mucorales growth [21]. The significance of chronic liver disease and hypertension warrants further investigation into their specific immunological impacts. The all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was 38.5%. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in age, gender, comorbidities, co-infection status, or length of hospital stay between survivors and non-survivors (Table 2), potentially limited by the sample size, this finding underscores the inherent pathogenicity of Mucorales. It suggests that once mucormycosis is established, the mortality risk is substantial, irrespective of specific demographic characteristics or comorbidity profiles within this high-risk cohort. A striking finding was the high prevalence of co-infection (80.8%) at admission. The pathogen spectrum revealed a complex polymicrobial interaction, comprising viruses (34.6%), bacteria (42.3%), and notably non-Mucorales fungi (61.5%, primarily Candida and Aspergillus spp.). Crucially, temporal analysis (Fig. 1) indicated that viral (77.8%) and fungal (62.5%) co-infections more commonly preceded Mucorales detection. While bacterial co-infections were also frequent, they occurred more often after Mucorales were identified. The high co-infection burden necessitates comprehensive diagnostic workups in high-risk patients presenting with severe pneumonia.

The accurate identification of the causative pathogen species in PM is crucial to understanding its epidemiology and developing effective management strategies. This study employed near-full-length (~ 1700 bp) 18 S rRNA gene sequencing to molecularly characterize 26 clinical Mucorales isolates obtained from respiratory tract specimens of PM patients. Phylogenetic analysis incorporating GenBank reference strains robustly confirmed species identity and elucidated the species distribution within this population. While the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region is the most widely sequenced DNA region for fungi in general, observed heterogeneity in Mucorales in the ITS region has occurred due to insertions and deletions that lead to inaccurate species identification and classification [9]. Therefore, the more conserved 18 S rRNA gene fragment provides high-resolution data suitable for reliable genus-level identification and robust phylogenetic inference, as demonstrated in Fig. 2. The predominance of Rhizopus (54%, 14/26) aligns with its well-established status as the most common causative agent of mucormycosis globally [22]. Knowledge of the prevalent genera within a specific center or region can inform empirical therapeutic choices, particularly when species-level identification or susceptibility testing is delayed.

This in vitro susceptibility analysis of 26 clinical Mucorales isolates, representing four major genera (Rhizopus, Rhizomucor, Mucor, and Lichtheimia), against nine antifungal agents reveals critical patterns with significant implications for the management of PM. In this study, amphotericin B showed superior efficacy against the Mucorales isolates compared to other agents, which aligns with previous studies that have established amphotericin B as a mainstay in treating mucormycosis [23]. The drug’s mechanism of action, which involves binding to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane and disrupting membrane integrity, likely contributes to its broad-spectrum activity against these rapidly growing fungi [24]. However, the use of amphotericin B is often limited by its renal toxicity and the need for intravenous administration, which can complicate long-term therapy [25]. Of note, posaconazole exhibited the most potent activity among the azole antifungals in this study, suggesting its potential as a promising alternative to amphotericin B in certain clinical settings. Consistent with the known resistance patterns of Mucorales to many azoles, itraconazole showed variable activity against Mucorales, while voriconazole and fluconazole displayed relatively poor efficacy, which may be due to efflux pumps and differences in the structure of fungal enzymes [26]. In contrast, posaconazole exhibits extended-spectrum activity compared to other azoles (e.g., itraconazole, voriconazole, fluconazole), a property attributed to its structural modifications that enhance binding affinity for fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes. Given that Mucorales are known to be intrinsically resistant to echinocandins due to differences in their cell wall β−1,3-glucan synthase structure [27], it was unexpected that neither the echinocandin antifungals (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin) nor 5-flucytosine exhibited appreciable activity against the tested isolates in this study.

Furthermore, profound genus-level heterogeneity was found in antifungal susceptibility, particularly to azoles [28]. Rhizopus spp. exhibited variable responses to posaconazole and limited efficacy to itraconazole. In contrast, triazoles exhibited superior potency against Rhizomucor spp. compared to other genera, suggesting that infections caused by Rhizomucor spp. may allow for greater flexibility in triazole-based therapeutic strategies. For Mucor spp., amphotericin B was the most potent drug, while azoles (particularly itraconazole and voriconazole) demonstrated low efficacy. Lichtheimia spp. were vulnerable to amphotericin B, posaconazole, and itraconazole. However, the very small sample size limits the reliability of these antifungal assessments. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate the antifungal susceptibility profiles, particularly the genus-level heterogeneity observed.

Biofilm formation is a critical factor in fungal pathogenesis, contributing to persistent infections, increased antifungal resistance, and challenges in treatment [29]. Compared to other filamentous fungi, such as Aspergillus spp., there have been relatively few studies on the biofilm of Mucorales [13, 30]– [31]. The evaluation of biofilm biomass in the tested strains at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h using the CV staining assay provided valuable insights into the biofilm formation dynamics of Mucorales species. The statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in biofilm biomass across the three time points (Fig. 3A), suggesting a relatively stable biofilm formation pattern during this period. However, it is noteworthy that half of the strains (13/26) exhibited peak OD630 nm values at 48 h (Table S2), indicating that this time point might represent a critical phase in biofilm development for these strains. Therefore, the 48-hour data were selected for further analysis to assess the biofilm formation ability of all strains. As shown in Fig. 3B, most strains (84.6%, 22/26) demonstrated biofilm formation ability, with 20 strains categorized as weak biofilm formers and two as moderate biofilm formers. The absence of strong biofilm-forming strains in this study might be attributed to the distinct biological characteristics and growth patterns of Mucorales, which are known for their rapid hyphal growth and invasion rather than robust biofilm formation. The genus-level heterogeneity in biofilm formation ability is also evident in this study. Both strains with moderate biofilm formation ability belong to the genus Lichtheimia, while the four strains without biofilm formation ability are from the genus Rhizopus. This finding highlights the importance of considering genus-specific differences in biofilm formation when studying Mucorales infections. Lichtheimia spp., known for their potential to cause severe infections in immunocompromised hosts, might rely on biofilm formation as one of the strategies to enhance their pathogenicity and resistance to host defences and antifungal treatments. On the other hand, the lack of biofilm formation in some Rhizopus strains might suggest alternative virulence mechanisms, such as rapid tissue invasion and angioinvasion, which are hallmarks of Rhizopus-mediated mucormycosis.

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the study was conducted in a single center in Beijing, China, which might limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different demographics or medical practices. Secondly, the relatively small cohort size (n = 26) restricts the statistical power of analyses, particularly evident in the inability to identify significant predictors of mortality despite observed trends and the limited ability to draw robust conclusions for less common genera like Lichtheimia regarding susceptibility or biofilm formation. Thirdly, the biofilm formation assessment utilizing the CV staining assay offers valuable insights. Still, it may not comprehensively capture all aspects of biofilm complexity and its impact on antifungal resistance in clinical settings. Further studies with larger sample sizes, multi-centre collaboration, and more comprehensive methodologies are needed to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this comprehensive clinical and microbiological characterization of 26 patients with PM underscores the disease’s significant severity and complexity within a Beijing tertiary-care hospital. The exceptionally high burden of comorbidities, particularly diabetes and chronic liver disease, reinforces the critical role of host immunosuppression. The substantial all-cause in-hospital mortality rate (38.5%), with no identifiable demographic or comorbidity predictors, highlights the inherent lethality of established Mucorales infection. The remarkably high prevalence (80.8%) and complex nature of co-infections, often viral or fungal, preceding Mucorales detection, necessitate comprehensive diagnostic strategies in suspected cases. Crucially, in vitro susceptibility testing revealed profound genus-level heterogeneity in antifungal response: amphotericin B demonstrated consistent activity, while azole efficacy varied significantly. Furthermore, this study provides novel insights into the biofilm-forming potential of Mucorales, demonstrating genus-specific differences. Collectively, these findings emphasize the imperative of species-level identification, susceptibility testing, and consideration of genus-specific traits to guide effective management of this life-threatening infection. Further multicenter studies are warranted to validate and expand upon these observations.
