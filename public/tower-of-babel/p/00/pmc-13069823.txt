125Iodine brachytherapy stents for lung cancer with superior vena cava syndrome
Authors: Yipu Li, Zhanguo Sun, Haotian Duan, Mengyao Song, Rongna Hou, Xinwei Han, Yan Yan, Dechao Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo determine the feasibility and efficacy of 125Iodine brachytherapy stent (IBS) for lung cancer associated with superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS).MethodsFrom May 2018 to August 2022, a retrospective analysis of 76 patients diagnosed with SVCS who underwent SVC stenting in our department. The ISB group (n = 33) underwent self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS) combined with 125Iodine seed strand implantation (called ISS), while the conventional stent group (n = 43) underwent SEMS implantation alone. The primary endpoints were technical success, clinical success, complications, stent patency, Karnofsky score; secondary endpoints were progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).ResultsThere was a significant difference in the stent patency (93.93% vs. 76.74% at 4 months) and Karnofsky scores [(81.52 ± 7.95) vs. (77.44 ± 7.90) at 1 month, all P < 0.05] between the ISB and conventional stent groups (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between two groups in technical success (100% vs. 100%), clinical success (96.97% vs. 93.02%), complication rates (24.24% vs. 11.63%), median PFS (3.40 vs. 3.80 months), or median OS (20.40 vs. 20.40 months) (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe ISB can effectively relieve SVCS, improve functional status, and prolong stent patency, while showing no significance on PFS and OS.Trial registryEthics Committee of Scientific Research and Clinical Trial, The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University.Unique identifying number: Ethics Approval Number: 2023ky0106.Date of registration: 2023.01.12.Keywords: 125Iodine brachytherapy, Superior vena cava syndrome, Stenting, Lung cancer

Introduction

Superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS) is a clinical emergency characterized by partial or complete obstruction of the SVC due to diverse etiologies, resulting in venous reflux disorders [1]. Among all the causes, malignant tumors account for 80–90%, necessitating treatment strategies to be predicated on etiology, disease severity, and the overall health of the patient, thereby underscoring the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration among oncology, radiotherapy, interventional therapy and thoracic surgery [2–6].

Although local high-dose radiotherapy can swiftly diminish tumor burden, achieving a local tumor control rate of around 70%, radiotherapy is often not feasible for patients with severe head and neck swelling or respiratory distress [7]. SVC stenting can quickly restore blood flow, diminish SVC pressure, and alleviate head and neck swelling as well as respiratory difficulties, often representing the preferred intervention for critically ill patients. However, due to the tumor ingrowth within the stent, once the tumor control is failed, the SVC stent is susceptible to reobstruction, resulting in a recurrence of SVCS. Consequently, prolonging stent patency constitutes a significant clinical challenge.

Radioactive 125Iodine seeds implantation has become one of the important minimally invasive treatments for tumors, showing good local control rates in lung cancer [8–10], prostate cancer [11–13], and biliary duct cancer [14–18]. Some scholars have placed radioactive 125Iodine seed into a 3 F plastic tube individually, and then made a linear 125I seed strand (ISS). Through catheter and guide wire technology, the ISS are combined with conventional self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS) to form a new functional 125Iodine brachytherapy stent (IBS). This method has been used in hepatocellular carcinoma with portal vein tumor thrombus [19] and malignant biliary obstruction [20]. Based on the above methods, can this method be used to recanalize the obstructive SVC? After reviewing previously published literature, no prior reports have been found yet. Since 2018, our center has developed such an IBS to treat SVCS through the jugular or femoral vein approach, with the goal of investigating whether IBS can alleviate or even recanalize the obstructive SVC. The details are reported as follows.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

This retrospective single-center study was conducted in our department from May 2018 to August 2022, which divided into two time spans, all patients underwent SVC stenting between May 2018 to January 2020, and all patients underwent combined implantation of ISS and SEMS (ISB group) between January 2020 to August 2022. Inclusion criteria included: (1) definitive diagnostic evidence of malignant lung tumors; (2) typical presentation of SVCS; and (3) a Karnofsky performance status of ≥ 50. Exclusion criteria included (1) non-malignant primary lung tumors; (2) prior radiotherapy; (3) previous SVC stenting; (4) benign SVC obstruction; (5)Karnofsky performance status of ≤ 40; and (6) incomplete clinical data. Finally, 76 patients with SVCS were identified for inclusion (Fig. 1). Of these, 43 patients underwent only SVC stenting (conventional stent group), while 33 patients received the combined implantation of ISS and SEMS (ISB group). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding age, gender, primary diseases, time to diagnosis, obstruction site, Karnofsky score, and prior treatment (all P > 0.05, as shown in Table 1).

Interventional treatments

All patients underwent routine blood tests, liver and kidney function tests, electrolyte and coagulation panels, serologic screening for four infectious diseases,, an electrocardiogram, and contrast-enhanced chest CT before the procedure, For the ISB group, the ISS can be implanted through the jugular vein or femoral vein. The following describes the femoral vein approach as an example. The patient was positioned supine on the DSA (Artis Zeego, Siemens, Germany) examination table, with oxygen inhalation and continuous electrocardiographic monitoring. Bilateral femoral regions were prepared and draped in a sterile fashion. Under local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine and ultrasound guidance as required, the right and left femoral veins were punctured using a modified Seldinger technique. An 8 F long sheath (90 cm, COOK, USA) was introduced via the right femoral vein, and a 6 F long sheath (80 cm, Cordis, USA) via the left femoral vein. A 0.035-inch guidewire (260 cm, Terumo, Japan) and a straight-tip side-hole catheter (110 cm, Merit, USA) were advanced via the right femoral sheath. The catheter was positioned at the proximal segment of the SVC obstruction to measure intravascular pressure. Using the catheter and guidewire, the obstructed segment was traversed and the device was advanced into the internal jugular vein. Then, high-pressure angiography was performed using contrast agent: Ioversol (320 mgI/100 mL, Hengrui, China) with parameters: 4 mL/s, total volume 10 mL, pressure 400 psi. This assessment delineated the degree and extent of stenosis, evaluated collateral circulation, and measured distal pressure across the obstruction. Thereafter, a stiff guidewire (Amplatz Super Stiff, Denmark) was exchanged and introduced. When biopsy was indicated, a 6.0-mm biopsy forceps (Nanjing Micro-invasive, China) was introduced through the 8 F long sheath to obtain 2–6 tissue samples from the obstructive segment. The specimens, which were fixed in 10% formalin, were sent for pathological examination. Conversely, if a biopsy was not required, a balloon dilation catheter (8 mm × 60 mm, Bard, USA Rival) could be used to dilate the obstruction area according to the degree of obstruction. Via the left femoral approach, a 0.035-inch guide guidewire and 5 F vertebral artery catheter were used to re-establish a channel across the obstruction, with the 6 F long sheath positioned at the proximal end of the lesion. A 14–16 mm Wallstent stent (Bard, USA), which was delivered through the right femoral vein operation approach along the Amplatz guidewire, had a length that covered at least 1 cm at both the upper and lower ends of the obstruction. The stent was fully deployed under fluoroscopic monitoring. Then the pre-prepared ISS which were slowly pushed out of the stent through the inner core of the 6 F sheath through left femoral vein [0.8 mm seeds; The number of seeds was calculated as: obstruction length (mm) + 10 mm proximal + 10 mm distal margin, divided by seed spacing 4.5 mm; seed activity = 0.7 mCi (Tianjin Saide, China)] Under fluoroscopy, the ISS was successfully delivered and fixed between the SEMS and local tumor. The stent delivery system was then removed. Then a 5 F straight-tip side-hole catheter was exchanged and introduced. Final angiography assessed SEMS patency and measured pressures proximal and distal to the stent. If necessary, post-dilation with a balloon catheter was carried out (Figs. 2 and 3). All patients subsequently underwent systemic treatments after multidisciplinary team consultation.

Definitions

The technical success was defined as successful deployment of the SEMS and implantation of the ISS. The clinical success was defined as > 20% reduction in pressure gradient across the obstruction, accompanied by substantial alleviation or resolution of SVCS symptoms within 24 h. Contrast-enhanced chest CT or color Doppler ultrasound was rechecked every 1–2 months to assess the patency of the stent. PFS was defined as the interval from the procedure date to disease progression, death, or last follow-up and OS was defined as the interval from the procedure date to death or last follow-up.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using the statistical software SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared using Student's t-test. The enumeration data were compared by χ2 test or Fisher's exact probability method. Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test were used to calculate PFS and OS. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 76 patients, 16 stents were 14 mm, 43 were 16 mm, and 17 were 18 mm in diameter. There was no significant difference in the size of the implanted stents between the two groups (P = 0.98). Clinical success rates were 96.97% (32/33) in the ISB group and 93.02% (40/43) in the conventional stent group, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). A total of 481 radioactive seeds were implanted, with an average of (14.58 ± 1.97) seeds (ranging from 12 to 22 seeds per patient), and the seed activity was 0.7 mCi (Figs. 4 and 5). The ISB group had 8 cases (24.24%) of mild complications, while the conventional stent group had 5 cases (11.63%) of mild complications, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). In the ISB group, distal SVC pressure decreased from 29.46 ± 5.68 mm H2O preoperatively to 15.64 ± 4.60 mm H2O postoperatively. In the conventional stent group, distal pressure decreased from 29.72 ± 4.11 mm H2O preoperatively to 16.91 ± 3.80 mm H2O postoperatively. The above parameters showed no statistically significant differences in pretreatment pressure, posttreatment pressure and the magnitude of pressure reduction between the two groups (P > 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in the stent patency between the two groups at 1-month and 2-month after the operation, but a statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups at 4-month stent patency (93.93% vs. 76.74%) (P < 0.05). Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in median PFS and OS between the two groups. (P > 0.05; Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Lung cancer is a common cause of SVCS, and radiotherapy or SEMS implantation is priority treatment strategies with higher evidence-based medicine [1]. Nevertheless, in some patients, the swelling resulting from the necrosis of tissue cells during radiotherapy, which may exacerbate the venous return obstruction, and some patients with severe dyspnea, who may even be unable to complete the radiotherapy. As for SVC stenting, Aung EY et al. [5] conducted a meta-analysis of the data from 2,249 SVCS patients treated with stenting in 45 studies from 1993 to 2020, reporting the technical success rate of 96.8% (range, 79.7–100%), clinical success rate of 92.8% (range, 78.3–100%), and complication rate of 5.78% (range, 1.3–13.5%). The 1-year stent patency rate ranged from 65 to 92%, and the causes of stent occlusion were thrombosis within the stent and tumor growth inside the stent. Prolonging stent patency is a crucial issue for improving patient quality of life. SVC stenting has gradually become the preferred minimally invasive treatment for SVCS due to its advantages of wide indications, simple operation, symptom relief and low cost [21].

In order to prolong the patency time of SVC stents, many scholars have done a lot of works, Wang ZS et al. [22] used covered or uncovered stent insertion for SVCS on 64 patients, the covered stent patency period was significantly longer in the group treated with covered stents (374 vs. 317 days, P < 0.05). Covered stents can stop tumor ingrowth to a certain extent, but they have no therapeutic effect on tumors, poor stent compliance, and high cost, furthermore, there is a risk of occluding the azygos vein.

Other studies have focused on radiotherapy strategy, Park J et al. [23] had evaluated the efficacy of palliative radiation therapy (RT) for superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome from lung cancer and to compare the 2-week (30 Gy in 10 fractions) and 1-week (20 Gy in 5 fractions) schedules, but the results showed no significant differences in recanalization rates (62.5% vs. 60.0%, tumor response rates (75% vs. 60.0%), and 6-month overall survival rates (29.2% vs. 36.4%).

How to find a newly functional stent or a good anti-cancer partner has always been a hot topic in clinical and basic research. In some studies, ISS were implanted outside stents for the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma and main portal vein tumor thrombus, and the OS and stent patency of patients were improved [24]. Simultaneously, numerous scholars applied this technology to malignant biliary obstruction [25], esophageal obstruction [26], and ureteral obstruction [27], which opened up a new treatment domain. Inspired by the use of radioactive ISS for treating malignant obstructive diseases in the lumen, our team envisions the possibility of combining radioactive ISS with SEMS to form a functional stent that can solve the problem of SVC stenosis. The treatment of malignant lumen obstruction with ISS, which has the following technical advantages, is worthy of attention: (1) The radiotherapy range of a single 125I seed is 1.7 cm, and continuous low-dose-rate irradiation can persistently inhibit tumor cell division and proliferation for a long time, which has a biological dosimetric advantage over traditional radiotherapy; (2) The linear distribution, which is more suitable for the natural trajectory of the lumen, enables precise targeting of the tumor area; (3) The combination of the ISS and SEMS, which gives rise to a new type of functional stent, is innovative; (4) The technical operation, which is simple and can be accomplished concurrently with the stent implantation, achieves two objectives with one procedure.

In this retrospective study, technical and clinical success rates were 100% and 96.97%, respectively; 4‑month stent patency in the ISB group was 93.93%, significantly higher than in the conventional stent group (93.93% vs. 76.74%).Moreover, the 1-month Karnofsky Score in IBS group (81.52 ± 7.95) was higher than the conventional stent group (77.44 ± 7.90). These findings indicate superior short-term functional improvement in the ISB group, which may have implications for longer-term outcomes. While the SEMS provided physical support at the tumor obstruction site, the ISS acted as a close-range brachytherapy protective wall, which had a certain inhibitory effect on the growth of the tumor and hyperplasia. However, the combined treatment regimen did not significantly impact PFS or OS. This may depend on the limitations of ISS as a localized intervention. In addition, the mean interventional procedure time increased from 29.7 min to 37.5 min, an increase of 20.8%. Although the extension of operation time did not increase complications (Table 3), it is a serious technical burden for patients with acute and severe respiratory distress.

The preliminary experience is summarized, as follows: (1) Administering hormones and dehydration agents 15 min before the operation, along with judicious use of fentanyl for sedation, enhances patient tolerance (2) When the balloon is pre-expanded, the size of the balloon should be “small rather than large” to accommodate the stent delivery system and the 6 F sheath; (3) It is advisable that the length of the ISS exceed 1 cm above and below the obstruction length to reduce the dose cold zone caused by the displacement of the ISS; (4) For patients who need to take a biopsy in the obstructive area, clamp biopsy technique can be used through the 8 F sheath, which can reduce the damage and bleeding of the collateral circulation vessels by percutaneous puncture; (5) When selecting the diameter of the stent, the diameter of the patient's normal SVC should be taken into account, and it is appropriate for the stent diameter to be 10% larger than that of the normal SVC, ensuring that even if the tumor lesion shrinks, the intravascular stent can still adhere closely to the blood vessel wall without displacement; (6) Employing the bilateral femoral vein approach can reduce the complications associated with neck puncture to a certain extent.

This study has several limitations. First of all, the single-center retrospective design and small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, and future multi-center studies are needed to expand the cohort to strengthen the evidence level. Second, the optimal irradiation dose remains unclear, and the absence of commercial TPS design and application further complicates this issue. Consequently, the ideal number and spatial arrangement of radioactive seeds have not been standardized. Currently, dose distribution is estimated by geometric approximation, and the lack of a standardized TPS reduces reproducibility, so dosimetric uncertainties, and the need for standardized should also be concerned at our futrue study.Third, it is still unclear whether additional therapies, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, could synergistically improve efficacy. Fourth, the limitations in follow-up duration prevent us from confirming the longer-term stent patency.

In conclusion, the ISB can effectively relieve SVCS, improve short-term functional status, and prolong stent patency, while showing no significance on PFS and OS.
