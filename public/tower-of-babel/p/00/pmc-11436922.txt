Dataset of aquatic insects acquired using field-emission scanning electron microscopy and the NanoSuit method
Authors: Yasuharu Takaku, Chiaki Suzuki, Takahiko Hariyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A simple surface modification, called NanoSuit, by electron beam or by plasma irradiation can form a nanoscale layer, allowing to keep small animals alive and hydrous under the high vacuum required for field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). We previously applied NanoSuit to aquatic insects, Dixa longistyla larvae (Diptera: Dixidae), which always lie on their ventral surface just under the water surface. We found that the crown-like structures on the ventral side of the hind segments enable the larvae to reside in such ecological niche. Moreover, fine structures in the crown protected with NanoSuit appeared intact, unlike those subjected to conventional sample fixation. However, a fundamental understanding of these structures (living and/or not treated with conventional fixation) interacting directly with water should be established using FE-SEM. This data descriptor introduces a rich dataset of images acquired using NanoSuit for various aquatic insects. The image data can be accessed and viewed through Figshare.

Background & Summary

Evolutionary adaptations to diverse environments have led to a large structural variety and the development of functional surfaces in living organisms. As direct interaction with water is essential for aquatic organisms, the body wettability is an important factor, and its distribution on the body surface can be a survival strategy for these organisms. Several studies have been focused on how various aquatic insects reside in environments with specific properties1,2. For instance, on the water surface, the water strider stands with superhydrophobic legs and glides quickly on the water by a sculling motion that leverages surface tension3. Hydrophobicity in organisms is achieved by chemical modifications with wax or oil secreted on the outer surface and/or physical regulations of fine hairy structures3,4. On the other hand, within a water column, Micronecta sedula possess air stores with a layer of hydrophobic hairs on the ventral surfaces of the abdomen and thorax5. During a dive, the air stores increase buoyancy of these aquatic backswimmers6,7. Their air-bubble respiration also works as a hydrophobic physical gill that enables them to live underwater for long periods.

In contrast to these organisms, by mirroring water striders, Dixa longistyla (Diptera: Dixidae) larvae lie with their ventral surface just under the water surface. They never dive into water and are never completely submerged under a water column8,9. We have previously revealed that crown-like structures, which possess both hydrophobic and hydrophilic properties, on the ventral side of the hind segments allow the larvae to reside in such ecological niche (Fig. 1; Movie 1, available at https://figshare.com/articles/media/Supporting_Movies/26789482?backTo=%2Fcollections%2F_%2F6894889&file=48672175)10. We adopted the NanoSuit method to analyse the fine structures on the surface of living Dixa larvae10. The NanoSuit method uses a natural extracellular substance that covers some organisms, or in some cases, an added substance mimicking the extracellular substance and polymerises it by using electron beams or plasma irradiation10–18. By encasing living organisms in the thin vacuum-proof suit, the NanoSuit method allows small animals to survive even within the high-vacuum environment required for field-emission (FE) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) without the complex preparation required for conventional fixation (cf. Movie 2, available at https://figshare.com/articles/media/Supporting_Movies/26789482?backTo=%2Fcollections%2F_%2F6894889&file=48672181 and Movie 3, available at https://figshare.com/articles/media/Supporting_Movies/26789482?backTo=%2Fcollections%2F_%2F6894889&file=48672184)10–15. As NanoSuit maintains the integrity of the organism’s surface without interfering with high-resolution SEM16, it enables sophisticated observation to study biological materials at the horizontal field of view above 10 μm17,18.

This study was aimed to determine the image profiles of aquatic insects that were untreated with conventional fixation. The collected images allow users to inspect the three-dimensional configuration of fine surface structures in specimens under living conditions, providing a basis for complementary investigations. This approach would be applicable to the field of a biomimetic engineering, which develops various devices related to buoyancy. Methods

Experimental organisms

By sweeping the surface of water with a tropical fishnet, (1) Dixa longistyla (Takahashi, 1958, Diptera: Dixidae) larvae were collected from a pond at the National Museum of Nature and Science, Tsukuba, Japan (140°06′ E, 36°06′ N) and a stream near Lake Sanaru, Hamamatsu, Japan (137°41′ E, 34°42′ N); (2) water striders, Aquarius paludum, (Fabricius, 1794, Hemiptera: Gerridae) were also collected from a stream near Lake Sanaru, Hamamatsu, Japan; and (3) Micronecta sedula (Horváth, 1905, Hemiptera: Corixidae) were collected from a pond in the campus of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (137°72′ E, 34°70′ N).

Light microscopy

For light microscopic observations, we used a stereo dissecting microscope, SMZ800 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a compact digital camera, EOS 90D (Canon, Tokyo, Japan). We also used a phase contrast microscope, Ni-U (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) via objective lens 10x, 20x, and 40x, respectively. The images were obtained using a high-definition digital camera, DS-Ri2 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and the raw data was analysed using the NIS-Elements D software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Electron microscopy

For FE-SEM, we used a JEM-7100F system (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 1.0 kV for the specimens with the NanoSuit method or 5.0 kV for those prepared with the conventional fixation methods, respectively. The vacuum level of the observation chamber was 10−3–10−6 Pa, with approximately 10−6 Pa around the gun of the electron beam and approximately 10−4–10−5 Pa around the observation stub. In addition, we set aperture size φ to 100 µm, the scan speed for each beam to 10–15 frame/s, the emission current to 100–140 μA, the probe current to 10 pA, and the beam irradiation density was approximately 2.65 × 1017–9.56 × 1018 electrons/m−2, depending on the observation conditions. The working distances were 6.1–18.9 mm, indicated on the image data bar in each SEM image. To record the dynamic movements of living specimens, SEM data were directly transferred to a high-definition digital video recorder (BDZ-EW500, Sony, Tokyo, Japan).

Preparation for standard SEM observation

For the conventional fixation methods, specimens were prefixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Sorensen’s phosphate buffer (LSI Medience, Tokyo, Japan) (pH 7.4) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes for 10 min in the same buffer at room temperature. The specimens were postfixed in ice-cold 1% OsO4 in the same buffer for 1 h and then dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol at room temperature (50%, 70%, 90%, 95%, 99%, and 100% for 3 times and 10 min each), transferred to 100% t-butyl alcohol at room temperature (3 times for 10 min each), and freeze-dried (JFD300, JEOL). To make the preparation conductive, the specimens were ultra-thin coated with OsO4 (PMC-5000, Meiwa, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 2a).

Sample preparation for FE-SEM to observe living specimens

The NanoSuit method10–18 was used to observe the intact surface structure. Specimens were placed directly into the SEM instrument without any pre-treatment (e.g., chemical fixation, dehydration, or ultrathin electrically conducting material coating). Although various substances fabricated into the NanoSuit membrane were reported previously11,12,17, in the present research, natural extracellular substances (ECS) on the organisms were used as the components. When the ECS covering the surfaces of the living specimens were polymerised by electron beam irradiation, the NanoSuit was formed in the SEM instrument. A low magnification (20–30×) electron beam was used to irradiate the entire surface of the sample, and the areas in which a NanoSuit formed were used for subsequent SEM observations.

Technical Validation

We present a technical discussion of the images in the collected dataset. The validation of the ultrastructural details of aquatic insects was performed in several ways. The equipment used to acquire the FE-SEM data was maintained and aligned for optimal imaging conditions before dataset acquisition (see Methods).

As conventional fixation includes various treatments, it usually takes 2 days to prepare (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the NanoSuit method only takes a few minutes to prepare (Fig. 2b). Imaging using both methods showed that the crown-like structures in Dixa larvae consist of three different structures (Fig. 3). In the centre (region 1), flat leaf-like structures of approximately 3–5 μm in width with central folds are observed (Fig. 3a,b,e,f). In region 2, surrounding the central structures, dense hairs (microtrichia) of 300 nm–1 μm in diameter are observed (Fig. 3a,c,e,g). In region 3, sparse hairs (protrusion) of approximately 0.5–2 μm in diameter are observed on the periphery (Fig. 3a,d,e,h).

We previously reported the differences between images collected using conventional fixation (https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_b_i_Electron_microscopic_images_of_i_Dixa_i_larvae_conventional_fixation_/24433567?backTo=/collections/_/6894889).and NanoSuit methods (https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_b_ii_Electron_microscopic_images_of_i_Dixa_i_larvae_NanoSuit_/24433552?backTo=/collections/_/6894889)10. Following conventional fixation, shrinkage of specimens was inevitable, especially due to desiccation during sample preparation (Figs. 2a,d, 3e) because the water content of living organisms is approximately 70–80%. Under these conditions, the outline of each fine structure appears more clearly defined with conventional fixation (Fig. 3f–h) than with NanoSuit protection (Fig. 3b–d), which provides a much smoother appearance. In region 2, the tips of the hairy structures show various branches, which seem dispersed when prepared by conventional fixation (Fig. 3g) but neatly straight and aligned when using NanoSuit (Fig. 3c). Considering the tip of the hairy structures in region 3 at higher magnification, the images obtained using the NanoSuit method show that these structures are not hard spines but seem flexible even under the high-vacuum environment in the SEM, similar to the natural features observed in water and air (cf. Figure 3d,h). Furthermore, in conventional fixation, the specimens are usually attached to the observation stub with a double-sided tape for SEM imaging (Fig. 2d). This attachment is necessary because dried lightweight samples11,17,18 may be easily inhaled by the vacuum pump of the SEM system. These results suggest that unfixed specimens protected with NanoSuit remain wet and the natural surface structure of the living organism can be conserved through SEM. In the images obtained by light microscopy, the specimens treated with the NanoSuit method did not show observable changes in appearance after 20 min of SEM observations (Fig. 2c), illustrating the convenience of this method.

In conventional fixation, if the specimens are not coated with an electrically conductive material (e.g. osmium, gold, palladium, or platinum)20, the samples are electrostatically charged, precluding satisfactory SEM imaging16. On the other hand, specimens protected with NanoSuit show no electrostatic charging even without electrically conductive coating, further supporting the suitability of this method. We previously reported the following results: (1) specimens protected with NanoSuit do not show electrostatic charging if the specimens are alive13,16; (2) in experiments involving dead specimens, although NanoSuits can be formed on the specimen surfaces in the SEM instrument, an immediate accumulation of electrostatic charge on the surface occurs from the subsequent SEM observations16; and (3) water contained inside living organisms contributes to inhibit electrostatic charging14. These results suggest that living organisms possess electrical conduction and/or are endowed with charge-free properties on their surfaces. In addition, NanoSuit seems to be responsible for prolonging the survival time of living specimens under high vacuum and the charge-free conditions.

The dataset presented herein contains FE-SEM images acquired using the NanoSuit method for three species of aquatic insects that reside in different environments (i.e. on the water surface, under the water surface, and within a water column) and show characteristic hairy structures (Fig. 4). In water striders, Aquarius paludum, the surface of the legs exhibits several unbranched small hairs (microsetae) (Fig. 4a,b) (https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_c_i_Electron_microscopic_Images_of_i_Aquarius_paludum_i_front_leg_NanoSuit_/24427228?backTo=/collections/_/6894889, https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_c_ii_Electron_microscopic_Images_of_i_Aquarius_paludum_i_middle_leg_NanoSuit_/24427225?backTo=/collections/_/6894889, https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_c_iii_Electron_microscopic_Images_of_i_Aquarius_paludum_i_hind_leg_NanoSuit_/24427222?backTo=/collections/_/6894889). Such structures show super-hydrophobicity achieved by chemical modifications with wax or oil secreted on the outer surface and/or physical regulations of fine hairy structures3,4. In the images acquired using NanoSuit, in addition to physical regulations of the fine nanogrooves appearing on the hairy structures4, the hairy structures seem to be covered with secreted wax or oil (Fig. 4b). We have previously reported that, with the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), the NanoSuit method is not only helpful in obtaining the images but also enables us to examine the in situ elemental compositions of hydrous specimens14. Future studies to investigate the property of the hairy structures need to examine the surface materials. If these substances consisted of pure non-evaporated liquids, such as oils or waxes, the EDS value would show both high oxygen and high carbon peaks, suggesting the chemical modifications on the hairy structures. These properties enable water striders to stand on water by leveraging the surface tension (Fig. 4c). On the other hand, Dixa larvae cling to the water surface with flexible branched hairy structures (Fig. 4d,e), adhering beneath the water surface by leveraging the surface tension (Fig. 4f) (https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_b_ii_Electron_microscopic_images_of_i_Dixa_i_larvae_NanoSuit_/24433552?backTo=/collections/_/6894889)10. Moreover, Micronecta sedula use their fine unbranched hairy structures to store air under a water column (Fig. 4g,h) (https://figshare.com/articles/figure/_d_Electron_microscopic_Images_of_i_Micronecta_sedula_i_NanoSuit_/24427204?backTo=/collections/_/6894889)5. In the hairy structures, the bubble-free part is described by a Wenzel state, in which the roughness of the structures increases the surface area, causing hydrophobicity21. The place where the bubble is attached is described by a Cassie–Baxter state, in which air can remain within the rough structures below water, enhancing hydrophobicity22. According to these principles, in aquatic backswimmers, the assembly of small air bubbles trapped on the surfaces of fine hairy structures provides the buoyant force required for floating (Fig. 4i)5–7.

As the imaging system of the SEM instrument is intended for motionless objects, only the video mode (scan speed of 10–15 frames/s) could be used to observe the active movements of the animals in NanoSuit. If the specimen moved faster than the video frame rate, the images became blurry (Fig. 5b; Movie 2, available at https://figshare.com/articles/media/Supporting_Movies/26789482?backTo=%2Fcollections%2F_%2F6894889&file=48672181), and the same effect occurred for light microscopy (Fig. 5a). Thus, when using the NanoSuit method, observations with high resolution could only be obtained when the living animals remained still (Movie 3, available at https://figshare.com/articles/media/Supporting_Movies/26789482?backTo=%2Fcollections%2F_%2F6894889&file=48672184). The successfully acquired images showed fine structures in living conditions (Fig. 4h). As shown in the images in the collected dataset, morphological specificities in aquatic insects elicit their optimal functions in various environments. The NanoSuit method allows identifying specific features on the surface of hydrous specimens.
