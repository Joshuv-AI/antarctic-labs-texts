Daily adaptive radiotherapy in postoperative hypofractionated salvage radiothERapy for prostate cancer patients (DART-PHASER): Early clinical and dosimetric results

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate dose-volume histogram metrics and preliminary toxicity of prostate cancer (PC) patients affected by postoperative locoregional relapse treated with moderately hypofractionated salvage radiotherapy (sRT) using AI-assisted daily adaptive RT.MethodsThis is a prospective observational study (4181CESC, NCT05884632) on adaptive hypofractionated sRT in PC. Eligible patients (up to 80 years old, post-prostatectomy, PSMA PET-CT confirmed M0) received 20 daily fractions up to 59 Gy for macroscopic relapse and 55 Gy for biochemical-only relapse. When indicated, pelvis was included with a dose of 45 Gy. The treatment was administered using Ethos™ system which generates two plans: scheduled and adapted. The scheduled plan includes a dose recalculation on daily synthetic CT (generated from daily CBCT). The adapted plan consists on a reoptimization based on the daily anatomy. The user then select the preferred one for treatment. This is an interim analysis reporting on DVH metrics (scheduled versus adapted plan) and toxicity outcome.ResultsThe results of the first 840 treatment fractions in 42 patients are reported. Sixteen (37.2%) had biochemical relapse only, while 27 (62.8%) had macroscopic relapse. We reported a significant improvement between scheduled and adapted plan for rectum V52.8Gy (16.9% versus 12.8%); p ≤0.0001), rectum Dmean per fraction (1.54Gy/fraction versus 1.42 Gy/fraction; p ≤0.0001), prostate bed PTV95%, (92.1% versus 98.5%; p ≤0.0001), pelvis PTV95% (<0.0001), pelvis PTV107% (<0.0001), and bowel Dmax (<0.0001). There was no difference for prostate bed PTV107%, bladder Dmean, and bladder V40.8Gy. Grade 2 GI toxicity (overall) was 4.8%. Grade 2 GU toxicity (overall) was 9.5% of cases. In the univariate analysis, the only factor associated with diarrhea onset was the inclusion of the pelvis (p = 0.002). There was no difference between treatment dose and toxicity.ConclusionPreliminary data seems to show minimal acute toxicity. A longer follow up is needed to confirm these data and evaluate late toxicity.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Adaptive radiotherapy, Salvage radiotherapy, Ethos, Hypofractionated radiotherapy

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains the most common malignancy in men [1]. For patients undergoing radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy (RT) serves as a complementary treatment, either as adjuvant RT (aRT) or salvage RT (sRT); aRT has demonstrated benefits in biochemical relapse-free survival (BRFS) in trials such as EORTC 22911, ARO 96–02, and SWOG 8794, but up to 40% of patients in the observation arm did not experience recurrence, raising concerns about overtreatment and associated toxicities [2], [3], [4]. Conversely, sRT is commonly applied in cases of biochemical recurrence, observed in 20–40% of PCa cases post-surgery [5]. Recent trials, including RADICALS, RAVES, and GETUG-AFU 17, support delaying RT until recurrence due to lower genitourinary (GU) toxicity and better biochemical control when sRT is delivered at PSA levels of 0.1–0.2 ng/ml [5], [6], [7], [8].

Advances in imaging, particularly PET-CT with novel tracers, have improved the detection of disease even at low PSA levels, aiding better patient selection for sRT [9], [10]. Additionally, the optimal RT dose in the postoperative setting is debated [11]. While guidelines recommend a minimum of 66 Gy [12], [13], dose escalation studies suggest improved biochemical relapse-free survival (BRFS) [14], [15], [16], [17], though high-level evidence remains sparse [18]. PCa's low α/β ratio makes it sensitive to higher doses per fraction, supporting moderate and extreme hypofractionation as standard in curative settings [19]. However, its role in postoperative scenarios is less explored, with most data being retrospective [20]. Several published and ongoing trials [21], [22], [23], [24] are investigating hypofractionated regimens, but long-term data are awaited.

The risk of late complications has limited hypofractionation in postoperative settings, despite recent trial on extremely hypofractionated postoperative RT seems to show a good early tolerability [24], [25]. Daily adaptive radiotherapy (DART), which accounts for anatomical changes through technologies like MR-linac, offers a promising alternative. The SCIMITAR phase II trial demonstrated low rates of acute and late GU and gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities with DART in postoperative PCa patients [24].

Ethos™ (Varian Medical Systems), an AI-integrated software within a linear accelerator, adapts treatment plans based on daily cone-beam CT imaging, enabling rapid contouring and planning. This technology minimizes intrafraction changes and reduces patient time on the treatment couch. At our institution, the standard hypofractionated schedule is guided by cone-beam CT but lacks online plan adaptation. This prospective observational study evaluates the toxicity and efficacy of hypofractionated postoperative sRT using DART for PCa patients with postoperative recurrence.

Treatment characteristics

A simulation CT (3 mm slice thickness) without contrast medium was acquired in the supine position and with the arms on the chest with Combifix (Kalona, IA, USA). The clinical target volume (CTV) delineation was performed as follows: the CTV was the prostate bed only and included the bladder-urethral anastomosis, the bladder neck, and the retro-bladder space, and the seminal vesicles bed in case of infiltration at the histological report, as defined by EORTC guidelines [26]. Patients were requested to have their rectum empty, and to drink 500 ml of water just after having emptied the bladder and 30 min before simulation and each treatment session. When available, surgical clips were used to better define the prostate bed. The planning treatment volume (PTV) was obtained from the CTV by adding 5 mm in all directions. Moreover, the following organs at risk (OARs) were contoured: the rectum, the bladder, the small bowel, the femoral heads, and the penile bulb. The treatment was performed in 20 fractions 5 times per week for a total dose of 55 Gy to patients with biochemical relapse only, and 59 Gy to patients with macroscopical relapse.

RapidArc plans consisted of two to three full arcs using 6 MV photon beams. All dose distributions are calculated with the Acuros XB algorithm on an Ethos treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA), setting a dose grid size of 2.5 mm (version 16.1.0).

Each day before treatment, all patients were imaged with cone beam CT (CBCT) for daily-adaptive procedure. From November 2024 the Hypersight system, a software advancement in image quality and in image generation timing, with iCBCT was implemented in our Ethos™ treatment unit. In case of bladder or rectal volumes not adequate when daily CBCT was acquired, patients were asked to empty the rectum or fill the bladder, repeat positioning and checking.

Concomitant androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) was administered based on the current clinical practice depicted in the international Guidelines [27]. The study was conducted under the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients signed an informed consent.

Adaptive radiotherapy procedure

The adaptive radiotherapy procedure on Ethos™ is described hereafter: patients were placed on the treatment couch and a pre-treatment CBCT was performed. Once acquired, the OARs and the influencers were automatically calculated by the artificial intelligence or propagated by vectorial transformation (based on a 6 degrees of freedom registration between Planning CT and Synthetic CT generated from the previous CBCT acquisition) and checked for consistency by the treating physician (the workflow used in Ethos for online adaptive treatment involves the use of structures known as influencers. These can be either auto-contoured using AI or manually contoured. These structures are essential for this type of planning because modifications to these structures automatically affect the treatment goals for PTVs and any other contoured structures, both anatomically-geometrically and, consequently, in terms of online plan optimization). See details on influencers in Supplementary 1.

After that, the CTV (prostate bed with or without pelvis) was propagated from the original simulation CT to the actual CBCT using the accepted influencers and manually modified by the treating physician, if necessary. After the contouring phase, the plan calculation automatically started. At the end of the auto-planning procedure, Ethos™ system generates two available plans: the scheduled and the adapted. The scheduled plan consists on a dose recalculation on daily synthetic CT (generated from daily CBCT). The adapted plan consists on a re-optimization based on the daily anatomy. The treating physician selected which was administered based on the best dose-volume histogram and dose distribution. If the pretreatment session lasted more than 15 min a verification CBCT was performed before RT start. In case of significant anatomical changes (target volume outside from the treatment field) the entire procedure was repeated.

End-points definition and follow-up

The primary end-point of the present study was rectum DVH variation between scheduled and adapted plan for the following metrics: PTV95%, PTV107%, rectum V52.8Gy, rectum Dmean, bladder V40.5Gy, bladder Dmean, bowel Dmean). Secondary endpoints were: DVH variation (scheduled versus adapted plan) for PTVs, bladder, bowel, and acute toxicity. All DVH metrics were reported as a daily value of the 20 treatment fractions, since treatment plans were computed on a daily basis. Moreover, Dmeans for rectum and bladder, and V59Gy for the bowel were reported as daily value (Gy) as follows: rectum and bladder Dmean <1.5Gy/fraction (ref. 30Gy), bowel V2.95Gy <1 cc/fraction (ref. 59Gy).

Acute toxicity was reported in this analysis by physician-reported outcome according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE version 5.0). More specifically, acute toxicity was defined as any gastrointestinal event occurred within 90 days from RT with grade ≥ G2.

Baseline symptoms were evaluated in the accrual phase, and side effects were evaluated as a change from the baseline status. Physical examination including toxicity was recorded at baseline at 10th and last treatment fraction, and at 3, 6, 12 months and yearly afterwards. PSA was performed every 3 months for the first year, every 6 months for the second year and every year afterwards. In the present interim analysis acute clinical toxicity of the first 42 patients was reported.

Sample size calculation and statistics

Based on previously published data [8], it was assumed a proportion of acute gastrointestinal events of 10%. The number of patients required was calculated by considering a difference of 5 percentage points (approximately half) in the proportion of acute gastrointestinal events with a grade ≥ G2. Thus, a sample size of 184 patients was required to provide 80% power using the two-tailed z-test at 10% significance. Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients were summarized using descriptive statistics methods. Continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics, including number of observations, mean, minimum, maximum and median. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies.

The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare mean DVH parameters (OAR constraints and PTV coverage) between scheduled and adapted plan. Two-tailed tests with a significance level of 5% were used throughout. For each parameter, the median and IQR were reported for both plan types, along with the absolute difference in medians (Δ) and the corresponding p-value. Constraint compliance rates were computed for each dosimetric parameter as the percentage of treatment fractions in which the pre-specified dose-volume constraint was met, separately for the scheduled and adapted plan. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (IBM, version 25).

Inter-fraction and inter-patient dosimetric variability were visualized using paired violin plots and heatmaps. For each parameter, violin plots displayed the full distribution of fraction-level values for both the scheduled and adapted plan side by side, overlaid with a box plot showing the median, IQR, and 1.5 × IQR whiskers, and a dashed red line indicating the clinical constraint threshold. Heatmaps were constructed as patient-by-fraction matrices, with one map for the scheduled and one for the adapted plan displayed side by side. A tricolour scale was applied, centered on the clinical threshold for each parameter: green indicated compliance with the constraint, yellow indicated a borderline zone, and orange-to-red indicated constraint violation, enabling rapid visual identification of critical fractions and patients.

Agreement between each daily plan type (scheduled and adapted) and the approved reference offline plan was assessed using the Bland-Altman method. For each dosimetric parameter and each evaluable fraction, the difference between the daily plan value and the corresponding offline reference value was computed (Δ = daily plan − offline) and plotted against the mean of the two measurements. The mean difference (bias) was calculated as an estimate of systematic deviation from the reference, and the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were defined as bias ±1.96 × SD, where SD is the standard deviation of the pairwise differences. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were computed for both the bias (standard error = SD/√n) and the limits of agreement (standard error = √(3SD2/n)) and displayed as shaded bands on each Bland-Altman plot. A bias close to zero indicates absence of systematic deviation from the reference plan, while narrow limits of agreement indicate high reproducibility of the delivered dosimetry relative to the planned dosimetry. The width of the limits of agreement (upper LoA − lower LoA) was used as a summary measure of total agreement variability and compared between the scheduled and adapted plan for each parameter, with a narrower LoA in the adapted plan interpreted as evidence of superior fidelity to the approved reference dosimetry. Since measurements were obtained from multiple fractions nested within the same patients, observations are not fully independent; the Bland-Altman analysis was therefore performed at the fraction level, acknowledging that within-patient correlation may lead to a conservative estimate of true between-patient variability.

Because each patient contributed multiple treatment fractions, dosimetric observations were not statistically independent. To assess the potential impact of within-patient correlation, a sensitivity analysis was performed at the patient level. For each dosimetric parameter, the median value across all fractions was calculated for each patient, yielding one independent observation per patient. Comparisons between scheduled and adapted plans were then repeated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on these patient-level summaries. In addition, linear mixed-effects models were fitted for selected dosimetric endpoints, with patient included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to quantify the degree of within-patient clustering. The results of these analyses were compared with those obtained from the original fraction-level analysis to assess the robustness of the findings. Analyses and figures were performed and generated using Python (version 3.12) with the SciPy (version 1.11), NumPy (version 1.26), and Matplotlib (version 3.8) libraries.

Results

Sixteen (38%) had biochemical relapse only, while 26 (62%) had macroscopic relapse. Pelvis was treated in 17 (40.5%) patients. Regarding rectal sparing, the median rectum V52.8Gy was 16.9% (IQR 16.3%) and 12.8% (IQR 9.65%) in the scheduled and adapted plan, respectively (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3a), representing a clinically meaningful reduction of 4.1 percentage points in high-dose rectal exposure. Regarding bladder dosimetry, bladder Dmean did not differ significantly between the two plan types (scheduled: median 1.33 Gy [IQR 0.52 Gy]; adapted: median 1.23 Gy [IQR 0.55 Gy]; p = 0.353; Supplementary material 2), indicating that this metric is primarily driven by inter-fraction bladder filling variability rather than plan adaptation. Bladder V40.8Gy showed a small but statistically significant difference (scheduled: median 31.4% [IQR 17.82%]; adapted: median 31.8% [IQR 19.62%]; p = 0.005; Supplementary material 2), with the adapted plan marginally worse; however, given both medians are well below the 50% constraint and the absolute delta is negligible (+0.40%), this finding might not considered clinically relevant. The inter-fraction variability of bladder metrics and heatmap is illustrated in Supplementary material 2. See Table 3.

Regarding bowel sparing, the median bowel Dmax was 2.35 Gy (IQR 2.00 Gy) and 2.34 Gy (IQR 2.06 Gy) in the scheduled and adapted plan, respectively (p < 0.0001; Supplementary material 3), although the absolute difference was clinically negligible (Δ = −0.01 Gy). The corresponding fraction-by-fraction heatmap is shown in Supplementary material 3. See Table 3.

Sensitivity analyses accounting for repeated measurements demonstrated substantial agreement with the original fraction-level results. When median dosimetric values were calculated for each patient and statistical comparisons were repeated using 42 independent observations, significant differences between scheduled and adapted plans remained for prostate bed V95%, pelvic nodal PTV V95%, pelvic nodal PTV V107%, rectum V52.8Gy, rectum Dmean, and Bowel Dmax. Two parameters that were statistically significant in the fraction-level analysis, prostate bed PTV V107% and bladder V40.8Gy, were no longer significant in the patient-level analysis, suggesting that these findings were partially influenced by within-patient correlation. Linear mixed-effects models incorporating patient as a random effect yielded results consistent with the patient-level analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients indicated variable degrees of clustering across dosimetric endpoints, with higher within-patient correlation observed for rectal dose metrics and minimal clustering for most target coverage parameters.

Overall, accounting for patient-level clustering did not materially alter the main conclusions of the study regarding the dosimetric advantages of adaptive planning. See supplementary material 4.

Agreement of scheduled and adapted plans with the reference offline plan

To quantify the fidelity of each plan type to the approved reference offline plan, Bland-Altman analysis was performed for all nine dosimetric parameters, comparing scheduled−offline and adapted−offline differences across all evaluable fractions.

For prostate bed PTV V95%, the scheduled plan showed a large systematic negative bias relative to the offline reference (bias = −7.50%, 95%, LoA: −19.74% to +4.75%), indicating that the scheduled plan consistently underestimates target coverage compared to what was planned. In contrast, the adapted plan showed near-perfect agreement with the reference (bias = −0.06%, LoA: −2.11% to +2.00%), with a sixfold reduction in the standard deviation of differences (SD 6.24% vs 1.05%) (supplementary material 6). A similar pattern was observed for pelvic nodal PTV V95%, where the scheduled plan showed a negative bias of −3.36% (LoA: −17.83% to +11.11%) versus −0.27% for the adapted plan (LoA: −10.53% to +10.00%), and for pelvic nodal PTV V107%, where the scheduled plan overestimated hot-spots by +1.74% (LoA: −2.81% to +6.29%) compared to +0.18% for the adapted plan (LoA: −1.94% to +2.30%).

For rectum V52.8Gy, the scheduled plan demonstrated a clinically meaningful positive bias of +5.43% relative to the offline reference (LoA: −13.13% to +23.99%), indicating systematic overexposure of the rectum when the original plan is simply repositioned on the daily CBCT anatomy. The adapted plan showed a near-zero bias of −0.39% (LoA: −10.29% to +9.52%), with the standard deviation of differences halved (SD 5.05% vs 9.47%), confirming substantially better agreement with the reference dosimetry (supplementary material 6). For rectum Dmean, the scheduled plan bias was +0.19 Gy (LoA: −0.31 to +0.70 Gy) versus +0.03 Gy for the adapted plan (LoA: −0.23 to +0.28 Gy), again with a marked reduction in spread.

Bladder V40.8Gy showed wide limits of agreement in both plan types, with the scheduled plan bias at +2.62% (LoA: −20.31% to +25.55%) and the adapted plan at +3.20% (LoA: −18.28% to +24.69%). The comparable LoA widths in both groups confirm that bladder dosimetric variability relative to the reference plan is not meaningfully reduced by adaptation, driven instead by inter-fraction organ filling changes (supplementary material 6). For bladder Dmean, the scheduled plan bias was +0.09 Gy (LoA: −0.70 to +0.87 Gy) versus +0.07 Gy for the adapted plan (LoA: −0.53 to +0.66 Gy), with a modest but not dramatic reduction in variability.

For bowel Dmax, both plan types showed comparable agreement with the offline reference: scheduled plan bias +0.09 Gy (LoA: −1.02 to +1.20 Gy) versus adapted plan bias +0.01 Gy (LoA: −1.01 to +1.02 Gy). The near-identical LoA widths indicate that bowel dose variability relative to the reference plan is largely driven by daily bowel position and filling rather than by the choice of plan, consistent with the lack of clinically meaningful difference between scheduled and adapted plans for this parameter.

Across all parameters, the Bland-Altman analysis demonstrates a consistent pattern: the adapted plan restores agreement with the approved reference offline dosimetry for target coverage and rectal parameters, substantially reducing both systematic bias and random variability relative to the scheduled plan. For bladder and bowel metrics, the limits of agreement remain wide regardless of plan type, reflecting the dominant contribution of inter-fraction anatomical variability that cannot be corrected by plan adaptation alone. These findings support the clinical value of daily online adaptive radiotherapy on the Ethos platform in maintaining the intended dosimetric objectives across the full course of treatment. Discussion

The preliminary results of this prospective study indicate that daily adaptive radiotherapy (DART) in postoperative hypofractionated salvage radiotherapy (sRT) for prostate cancer patients provides significant dosimetric improvements while maintaining a favorable toxicity profile. The dosimetric analysis demonstrated that the AI-assisted adaptive approach yielded superior target coverage and reduced exposure to organs at risk (OARs) compared to the scheduled plan, in alignment with prior findings on MRI-guided adaptive radiotherapy [28]. Specifically, the rectum V52.8Gy and Dmean were significantly lower in the adapted plan, suggesting that daily anatomical adjustments mitigate rectal exposure and may potentially reduce late toxicity. This supports the hypothesis that adaptive techniques could enhance the therapeutic ratio of postoperative radiotherapy, a key concern in the management of prostate cancer patients undergoing sRT [29].

In fact, the necessity for personalized adaptive approaches in postoperative sRT has been highlighted in previous literature, particularly in studies exploring intra-fractional variations in prostate bed radiotherapy [30]. The current study corroborates these findings, emphasizing that anatomical and volumetric fluctuations, which are common in the postoperative setting, can be effectively managed using AI-assisted adaptive workflows. The significant improvement in target volume coverage with the adaptive plan, as seen in the prostate bed and pelvic PTV metrics, aligns with data from trials such as SCIMITAR, which demonstrated improved plan conformity with adaptive strategies [24]. Moreover, the toxicity outcomes in this study suggest that adaptive planning contributes to lower acute gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary (GU) toxicities compared to studies using non-adaptive hypofractionated sRT [6], [7], [8], [16], [31]. The reported rates of Grade ≥ 2 acute GI toxicity (4.8%) and GU toxicity (2.4%) are in line with, if not superior to, those observed in other postoperative RT trials, such as RADICALS-RT and GETUG-AFU 17 [7], [8]. In particular, by comparing actual preliminary data with those of the RADICALT-RT which use a similar 20-fraction regimen, we can notice that results are in line with the original hypothesis of keeping G2 GI toxicity ≤5%. Notably, the absence of Grade 3 or higher acute toxicity is an encouraging finding that reinforces the safety profile of this approach. The lack of significant toxicity differences between the 55 Gy and 59 Gy groups further suggests that hypofractionation in this setting may not necessarily lead to increased acute toxicity, a critical consideration given the historical hesitancy in adopting such regimens due to concerns over late effects [11]. The role of higher treatment dose on disease control requires a longer follow-up.

One of the most significant findings of this study is the observed dosimetric benefit in bowel and bladder dose reduction, which has been a major challenge in postoperative RT [16], [17]. The adaptive planning method effectively adjusted for daily anatomical variations, leading to a notable decrease in bowel Dmax and rectal exposure, which are crucial determinants of late toxicity [18]. These results align with data from MR-guided RT studies, where daily plan adaptation has demonstrated dosimetric benefits in normal tissue sparing while preserving tumor control [32].

The study also raises important considerations regarding the role of concurrent androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in salvage settings. While ADT remains a standard adjunct to sRT, its impact on toxicity and disease control in the context of adaptive radiotherapy warrants further investigation [5] Given that patient selection criteria for this study included PSMA-PET-CT staging, the integration of advanced imaging techniques in treatment decision-making also reflects the evolving landscape of prostate cancer management [9].

Despite these promising findings, this study has some limitations. The relatively short follow-up period precludes definitive conclusions regarding late toxicity and BRFS. Long-term outcomes will be critical in determining whether the dosimetric advantages observed with adaptive radiotherapy translate into sustained oncologic control [33]. Additionally, while the study highlights the feasibility of AI-driven adaptation workflows, its reproducibility in broader clinical settings requires validation through larger prospective trials. Lastly, although a statistically significant reduction in bladder V40.8Gy was observed in the fraction-level analysis, this finding was not confirmed in the patient-level sensitivity analysis. This discrepancy likely reflects the relatively small magnitude of the effect combined with substantial day-to-day variability in bladder filling and anatomy. Consequently, the impact of adaptive planning on this endpoint appears less consistent across patients than that observed for target coverage and rectal dose metrics.

Since this preliminary analysis showed no safety concern or toxicity exceeding the planned threshold, the study is continuing to recruit patients.

In conclusion, early results of the XXXXXXXX trial provides compelling early evidence that DART in postoperative hypofractionated sRT for prostate cancer improves target coverage while reducing exposure to OARs, thereby mitigating acute toxicities. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature advocating for adaptive radiotherapy in PCa management and highlight the potential of AI-driven treatment approaches to enhance precision and safety. Future research should focus on long-term oncologic outcomes and the integration of adaptive sRT into standardized clinical protocols to optimize patient selection and treatment efficacy.
