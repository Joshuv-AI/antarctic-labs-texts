Executive summary of American Radium Society Appropriate Use Criteria for the treatment of locoregionally recurrent rectal cancer
Authors: Eric D Miller, Krishan R Jethwa, Eric Dozois, Zhaohui Jin, Gerard Abood, Dmitriy Akselrod, Joseph Attallah, D Chamil Codipilly, Christopher J Anker, Christopher L Hallemeier, Leslie C Hassett, Timothy Kennedy, Percy Lee, Diane C Ling, Neil B Newman, Navesh Sharma, William Small, Leila Tchelebi, Suzanne Russo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis literature‐based systematic review and associated guidelines provide evidence‐based paradigms for the management of locoregionally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC). This multispecialty committee included gastrointestinal radiation and medical oncology, gastroenterology, radiology, and colorectal surgery. As is the standard, the previously described American Radium Society Appropriate Use Criteria methodology for this project was followed rigorously, with the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Timing, and Study Design framework and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses methodology to assess the evidence. RAND/University of California Los Angeles consensus methodology (modified Delphi) was used to rate the appropriateness of treatment options. Published between January 1, 2013, and July 16, 2025, 116 peer‐reviewed trials provided the evidence: 10 were well‐designed randomized phase 2/3 trials, 29 were moderately well designed trials that accounted for most common biases (matched cohort and phase 2), 76 trials had design limitations (retrospective), and one was a meta‐analysis. Clinical cases were created as examples to illustrate current acceptable management of LRRC. Treatment and prognosis are influenced by prior therapy and the site(s) and extent of LRRC. The ability to achieve a margin‐negative surgical resection is the ultimate determinant of survival and local control. Preoperative systemic therapy, radiation therapy, or a combination of the two can facilitate tumor downsizing and improve the likelihood of a margin‐negative resection. An individualized multidisciplinary approach is required to ensure the best outcome. Although this review does not suggest a major alteration of current practice, it provides reassuring evidence of the importance of combined‐modality therapy.Keywords: brachytherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, intraoperative radiotherapy, radiotherapy, recurrent rectal cancer, surgery

INTRODUCTION

The management of locoregionally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC) is complex, and can be associated with both poor prognosis and significant morbidity. For LRRC, treatment options depend on the location and extent of the recurrence, prior local therapies, the balance of patient goals, performance status, and anticipated treatment morbidities. Treatment may include surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and/or radiation therapy (RT).

In 2012, the American College of Radiology (ACR) published appropriate use criteria (AUC) for recurrent rectal cancer. 1 In 2017, the ACR AUC committees transitioned to the American Radium Society (ARS) for oversight. The present systematic review and guidelines provide an update to the original 2012 ACR recurrent rectal cancer AUC, and are intended to guide treatment decisions for patients with LRRC on the basis of the available evidence. Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome questions included the following. (1) What is the role of surgery in the treatment of LRRC? (2) What is the role of preoperative/perioperative therapy? (3) What is the role of nonoperative management (NOM) in the treatment of LRRC? (4) What is the role of RT/reirradiation? (5) What is the role of systemic therapy (chemotherapy/immunotherapy) in the treatment of LRRC?

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This ARS AUC multispecialty‐led committee is composed of all key specialties involved in the management of LRRC, including colorectal surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology, surgical oncology, gastroenterology, and radiology. With the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Timing, and Study Design framework, evidence regarding treatment outcomes was assessed via Cochrane and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses 2020 methodology. 2 , 3 Eligible studies included prospective observational, phase 2/3 trials, and retrospective trials including >25 patients that were identified by a comprehensive search published between January 1, 2013, and July 16, 2025 in the Ovid MEDLINE (1946+; including epublished ahead of print, in process, and other nonindexed citations), Ovid Embase (1974+), Ovid Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2005+), and Scopus via Elsevier (1970+) databases. Results were limited by study design and English language. Animal studies were excluded. Search strategies were designed and conducted by a medical librarian with input from the study investigators. Controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords was used to determine the final studies included. Two authors independently screened the comprehensive list of articles, and one assessed the full‐text articles to determine the final studies included in the summary of the literature review, which advised our recommendations. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by consensus. Of the 244 articles identified via the search strategy, 80 were selected that met all inclusion criteria. Twenty‐three additional studies were included via backward citation searching that were identified from the reference lists of articles found from the search strategy and significantly contributed to the literature. Of the 43 citations in the original 2012 bibliography, 13 citations were retained in the final document. Articles were removed from the original bibliography if they were more than 10 years old and did not contribute to the evidence, or if they were no longer cited in the revised narrative text. Nine additional studies referenced outside the summary of the literature review were included to provide context but were not used by the committee to guide recommendations. Study type and quality for the references were assessed. Of the 116 references used as evidence, 116 were categorized as therapeutic references, including 10 well‐designed studies (phase 2 randomized and phase 3), 29 moderately well designed studies that account for most common biases (matched cohort and phase 2 studies), 76 studies with design limitations (retrospective reviews), and one meta‐analysis. Well‐established RAND/University of California Los Angeles consensus methodology (modified Delphi) was used by the expert panel to rate the appropriateness of the treatment options, 4 with a total of two rounds of voting used by the group. Categories included (1) usually not appropriate, (2) may be appropriate, and (3) usually appropriate. The project proposal as well as this executive summary were reviewed and approved by the ARS AUC steering committee, which includes a librarian with expertise in systematic reviews.

Patient workup

The diagnostic evaluation of suspected LRRC requires a multimodal approach that integrates clinical assessment, biochemical testing, and advanced imaging. A detailed history and physical examination is important, with particular attention to new pelvic pain, altered bowel habits, urogenital symptoms, bleeding, or pelvic neuropathic symptoms that often accompany locoregionally recurrent disease. 5 Serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) is routinely obtained because rising levels can be an early indicator of suspected recurrence, although normal values do not exclude disease. Circulating tumor DNA is also considered an emerging tool in evaluating suspect recurrence. Endoscopic assessment—typically flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy—is used to directly evaluate the anastomosis or rectal stump, which allows biopsy of suspicious lesions and assessment for synchronous pathology. Computed tomography (CT)–guided biopsy is used to confirm the diagnosis of LRRC at the pelvic sidewall or presacral sites not amenable to endoscopic biopsy.

Imaging is crucial to defining the extent of disease and determining resectability. High‐resolution pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging modality because it provides superior soft tissue delineation that allows comprehensive evaluation of the extent of disease, including presacral and/or sacral involvement, pelvic sidewall extension, potential invasion of adjacent organs, and extension along sacral nerves and/or the lumbosacral plexus. 6 , 7 Specific MRI protocols with gadolinium administration, as opposed to a more standard rectal cancer protocol, may be required to fully clarify the extent of nerve involvement or degree of sacral involvement. It is important to consider points of potential fixation in the pelvis, compromised margins, and characterization of which anatomic compartments are involved because this will influence the surgical approach and suitability for an operation. Contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is obtained to evaluate for distant metastases, particularly to the liver and lungs. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–CT (FDG‐PET/CT) or FDG‐PET/MRI may be incorporated to differentiate postoperative fibrosis from active tumor, detect occult metastatic sites, and guide surgical planning. 8 , 9

RESULTS: SUMMARY OF THE LITERATURE REVIEW

Topic 1: What is the role of surgery in the treatment of LRRC?

Surgical management of LRRC remains one of the most complex domains in colorectal oncology, where the central therapeutic goal of achieving a margin‐negative (R0) resection must be balanced against substantial patient‐, tumor‐, and treatment‐related considerations. Appropriate patient selection is fundamental, and hinges on an understanding of disease biology, patterns of spread, and overall treatment intent. The natural history of LRRC demonstrates that recurrence occurring earlier after primary therapy, especially within the first 1–2 years, often reflects aggressive tumor biology. 10 As discussed in the Materials and Methods section, the extent of disease, including involvement of the pelvic sidewall, sacrum, or visceral structures, should be evaluated with imaging. Biomarkers such as CEA provide further prognostic information, with elevated or rising CEA correlating with risk of distant metastasis. Patient performance status similarly guides decision‐making, given that morbid procedures such as pelvic exenteration may have a significant burden on function and quality of life (QOL), and such risk must align with a patient’s goals of care.

Prognostication based on anatomic location and the extent of locally recurrent disease in the pelvis has evolved considerably over the last several decades, which reflects a deeper understanding of how tumor biology and anatomic constraints determine resectability. Some of the earliest and most influential frameworks were the fixation‐based classifications by Pilipshen and Suzuki, which stratified LRRC according to the degree of fixation to surrounding structures. 11 , 12 Suzuki et al. characterized tumors by degree of fixation, with no points of fixation, one site, two sites, or three or more sites, with potential fixation points being posterior, pelvic sidewalls, or anterior. 13 This system highlighted that fixation to the sacrum, pelvic sidewall musculature, and major vessels, and specifically the number of points of fixation, strongly predicted the difficulty of achieving a margin‐negative resection. However, foundational, fixation‐based approaches were limited by their subjective assessment and lack of anatomic specificity, and in particular the lack of distinction in regard to complexity based on anatomic compartment (i.e., posterior vs. lateral). As imaging capabilities improved, particularly with high‐resolution MRI, contemporary classification systems shifted toward compartment‐based anatomic mapping, which provided more predictability of surgical difficulty, operative risk, and margin status. Compartment‐based classifications divide recurrences into central/anterior, posterior, or lateral, with each associated with distinct differences in surgical approaches and the feasibility of complete resection. 11 Central/anterior recurrences are the least technically demanding and maintain the highest probability of R0 resection, whereas lateral, posterior, or composite recurrences (i.e., multiple compartments), often involving pelvic sidewall structures (nerves, vessels, and muscle), are substantially more challenging, and may require multistage approaches by experienced cross‐disciplinary surgical teams to achieve R0 resection. 14 , 15 Sorrentino et al. further validated that these compartment‐based systems outperform earlier classifications in predicting R0 versus R+ resections, which reinforces their utility in contemporary multidisciplinary preoperative planning. 12

Management pathways for nonmetastatic LRRC often diverge on the basis of prior therapy and recurrence pattern. Local regrowth after NOM may remain amenable to salvage resection alone if there is not a threatened circumferential margin. In contrast, extraluminal recurrences in previously unirradiated patients typically warrant neoadjuvant therapy followed by extended resection tailored to the involved anatomic compartment. When prior pelvic RT has been delivered, management is largely dependent on disease burden. Upfront surgery is generally limited to the small subset of patients with isolated anastomotic or luminal recurrence when there is a high level of confidence of achieving a wide surgical margin. Larger or extraluminal recurrences after prior RT present greater challenges, and require multimodality planning inclusive of reirradiation and potential consideration of intraoperative radiation therapy (IORT) to optimize local control (LC).

Regardless of the scenario, surgery is generally considered the only potentially curative treatment for LRRC, particularly in the setting of prior RT, with long‐term survival dependent on the ability to achieve an R0 resection. Comparative studies underscore this principle: nonsurgical management of LRRC—whether via chemoradiation (CRT) alone or systemic therapy—is associated with poorer outcomes. Across multiple series, NOM strategies generally have 5‐year overall survival (OS) rates of only 0%–20%. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Population‐based data from Norway demonstrate that patients who do not undergo resection experience a median survival of approximately 5–14 months, with a 5‐year survival of 3% after RT alone. 17 In one of the largest modern series, Hagemans et al. found that nonsurgically managed patients had a 5‐year survival of only 4%, compared with 51% in patients who underwent R0 surgical resection. 16

Multimodality approaches incorporating RT, chemotherapy, and surgery generally result in the most favorable LC and survival rates. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 When R0 resection is achieved, 5‐year OS commonly ranges from 30% to 50%, with some contemporary IORT‐supported series reporting survival approaching 55%–60% in select patients. Kusters et al. reported a 5‐year OS of 58% with R0 resection, whereas Denost et al. reported a 5‐year OS of 35% after R0 resection. 23 , 24 Similarly, Dresen et al. reported a 5‐year OS of 48% after R0 resection in patients treated with multimodality therapy incorporating IORT, with substantially poorer outcomes in those who underwent R1/R2 (positive margin/residual gross disease) resection (hazard ratio, 2.11; 95% CI, 1.37–3.24; p = .001). 20 Haddock et al. examined combined‐modality therapy with intraoperative electron radiation (IOERT), and reported a 3‐year LC of 77% and a 5‐year OS of 46% in those who underwent R0 resection, although survival outcomes were substantially lower after R1 (27%) or R2 (16%) resection. 21 Collectively, these data reinforce that R0 resection is strongly associated with favorable long‐term survival.

Historically, several patterns of disease spread were considered unresectable because of prohibitive surgical risk, high postoperative morbidity, or technical limitations, including recurrent disease extending above the third sacral body (S3), unilateral or bilateral pelvic sidewall involvement, and invasion of major vascular structures such as the external iliac vessels or aortoiliac axis. Moreover, location of the recurrence alone (i.e., high sacral involvement and bilateral sidewall) was thought to be a surrogate for poor oncologic outcomes, regardless of the therapy applied. However, contemporary series challenge these assumptions. Modern data show that en bloc vascular resection and reconstruction, including of the aortoiliac axis, can be performed safely in experienced centers, and may achieve meaningful long‐term survival in select patients. 25 , 26 Similarly, sacropelvic resections that include high sacrectomy (above S3) have been demonstrated to be feasible and safe, with experienced centers reporting low surgical mortality, acceptable morbidity, and encouraging oncologic outcomes in select patients. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 These studies indicate that site and extent of pelvic recurrences once viewed as absolute contraindications may now be selectively considered for curative‐intent oncologic resection via multidisciplinary coordination inclusive of chemotherapy, RT, advanced surgical resection and reconstructive techniques, and refined perioperative care.

Unfortunately, LRRC is a highly morbid disease state often related to the extent of locoregional infiltration in the pelvis, and the therapeutic options are also associated with acute and late side effects. The most common complications from surgery include wound infection, abscess, or fistula potentially requiring surgical re‐exploration, bleeding, bowel obstruction, ureteral obstruction, and neuropathy. 21 , 31 , 32 Risks of surgery are related to the extent of the operation, with sacrectomy, pelvic exenteration, and lateral/sidewall recurrences generally being more challenging and associated with greater risk. Although acknowledging the notable risks of pelvic exenteration while striving to achieve appropriate practice benchmarks, an international multicenter cohort study proposed benchmark thresholds for the major complication rate of ≤53% and 90‐day mortality rate of ≤6%. 33

Patients with LRRC generally have a poorer QOL when compared with patients who have had prior rectal cancer resection without disease recurrence. Series evaluating patient‐reported QOL before and after surgery for LRRC note the following: (1) patients who have surgery for LRRC have a poorer QOL for between 6 and 9 months after surgery compared to baseline; (2) those managed nonoperatively have a better QOL for the first 6 months compared with surgically treated patients, after which those who have surgery have a better QOL; (3) R0 resection is associated with better QOL beyond 12 months when compared with R1 or R2 resection, and R2 resection is not associated with any benefit in QOL when compared with palliative NOM; (4) surgery is commonly associated with chronic perineal pain, significant impact on sexual and urinary function, and musculoskeletal symptoms affecting the pelvis and lower limbs, particularly in those undergoing sacrectomy; and (5) most patients will have a permanent stoma. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Additionally, surgery has significant impact on financial and occupational status, which often limits a patient’s ability to return to work.

Taken together, surgery for LRRC represents a high‐risk but potentially curative intervention in which outcomes depend on patient selection, anatomic classification, prior therapy, and the feasibility of achieving negative margins. When centered around the core oncologic principle that survival benefit is maximized when R0 resection is achievable, contemporary data support a multimodality, compartment‐guided approach that extends surgical candidacy beyond traditional limits, although such approaches carry notable morbidity and should only be considered when patients understand the risks and benefits and they remain aligned with their goals and wishes. As treatment paradigms continue to evolve, careful integration of imaging, tumor biology, patient fitness, and technical expertise will remain essential to optimizing outcomes in this challenging disease state.

Topic 2: What is the role of preoperative/perioperative therapy in the treatment of LRRC?

For patients with LRRC, achieving an R0 surgical resection is by far the most important prognostic factor for long‐term survival. 16 , 17 , 20 , 38 Thus, the role of preoperative therapy is primarily to assist in achieving that goal. A study by Alberda et al. suggests that LRRC within a previously irradiated field is less responsive to systemic therapy than synchronous distant metastases outside the RT field, which implies that systemic therapy alone may not be sufficient to achieve substantial downstaging to facilitate resection. 39 The extensive surgery often required to achieve a margin‐negative resection in this patient group may result in postsurgical complications making it difficult to receive sufficient postoperative therapy, which thus adversely affects patient outcomes. 40

Although no randomized data currently exist, the use of neoadjuvant treatment, primarily RT, has been associated with improved outcomes. 22 , 38 , 41 , 42 In a retrospective study from Tang et al. that included 71 patients with LRRC, the combination of CRT and surgery was associated with improved progression‐free survival (PFS) when compared with patients who were treated with surgery alone or chemotherapy/CRT without surgery. 18 Similarly, larger series, including from the Cancer Registry of Norway, Mayo Clinic, and Catharina Hospital in The Netherlands, demonstrate that in patients with LRRC, neoadjuvant RT in combination with surgery provides the best opportunity to achieve an R0 resection and the potential for cure. 20 , 21 , 22 , 41

Topic 3: What is the role of NOM in the treatment of LRRC?

Although NOM is gaining momentum for patients with primary rectal cancer, patients with LRRC often present with disease that is more technically challenging or not amenable to resection, which makes NOM a consideration for this patient population. Sorrentino et al. demonstrated that in patients with LRRC receiving neoadjuvant treatment, a pathologic complete response (pCR) to therapy was associated with an improved 5‐year OS and a trend for 5‐year local recurrence‐free survival. 43 However, the pCR rate was only 16%. Nordkamp et al. reported similar results in a cohort of 345 patients with LRRC where 15% of patients had a pCR, which was associated with more favorable outcomes, and suggested that a NOM approach might be considered in select patients. 44 However, on the basis of radiographic response, LRRC appears to be radioresistant when compared with primary rectal cancer. 45 The current evidence, albeit largely limited to retrospective studies, favors an R0 surgical resection as the only feasible means to achieve long‐term LC and cure.

In the multicenter phase 2 study by Valentini et al. investigating the role of hyperfractionated reirradiation for patients with LRRC, completion of an R0 resection was independently associated with favorable LC and disease‐free survival (DFS). 19 In addition, multiple large retrospective series demonstrate that completion of an R0 resection in patients with LRRC is associated with improved outcomes, including OS. 16 , 20 , 21 , 38 , 41 However, in patients where an R0 or R1 resection cannot be achieved, NOM may be a consideration. Hagemans et al. found no difference in OS in patients with LRRC who underwent an R2 resection versus those who were managed nonsurgically. 16

Although this affects a smaller proportion of this patient population, patients with rectal cancer caused by a deficiency in mismatch repair (dMMR) have been shown to be highly sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. 46 , 47 , 48 For patients with locoregionally recurrent dMMR rectal cancer who received treatment with conventional CRT and surgery at primary diagnosis before the era of routine testing for dMMR, ICIs should be considered as primary treatment by extrapolating from the very high response rates observed in the de novo setting.

Topic 4: What is the role of RT in the treatment of LRRC?

Subtopic 1: What is the role of RT for patients with LRRC without prior RT?

Unlike in the de novo setting, there are no randomized data that demonstrate the potential benefits of neoadjuvant RT for patients with LRRC without prior RT. However, the limited available data suggest that this is a patient group where neoadjuvant RT should be strongly considered, given the suboptimal disease control outcomes with surgery alone. This is further illustrated by the CAPIRI‐IMRT phase 2 trial, which included 71 patients with LRRC without a prior history of pelvic RT. 49 Patients received pelvic RT with a whole‐pelvic dose of 45 gray (Gy) followed by a boost of 10–16 Gy for a total dose of 55–61 Gy in 30–33 fractions with concurrent capecitabine and weekly irinotecan followed by maintenance chemotherapy. The primary end point of the study was local response rate. The overall local response rate was 47% at 3 months, with 20% of patients ultimately completing surgical resection. The cumulative local PFS rates at 1 and 3 years after completion of CRT were 74% and 34%, respectively, with a 3‐year OS of 59%.

Several retrospective studies also investigated the potential role for RT for LRRC. Alberda et al. investigated the outcomes of patients with LRRC amenable to resection by comparing those who received neoadjuvant RT to those who did not for the primary tumor. 50 Both patient groups received multimodality treatment for the recurrence, including RT (44.6–52 Gy in 19–28 fractions for those without a prior history of RT; 27–30 Gy in 15–18 fractions for reirradiation), often with concurrent chemotherapy and IORT for patients with a close or positive margin. Despite a higher proportion of patients with initial stage I/II disease in the upfront surgery group (58% vs. 46%), there was no significant difference in 5‐year OS (28% vs. 43%; p = .81), recurrence‐free survival (55% vs. 48%; p = .5), or DFS (27% vs. 40%; p = .59) between patients who were treated with primary upfront surgery and those who received neoadjuvant RT, respectively. Tanaka et al. reported on 32 patients with LRRC treated with surgery alone as the primary treatment. 51 At the time of recurrence, patients received RT with doses of 39–74.25 Gy in 13–33 fractions with three‐dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT), with 63% receiving concurrent chemotherapy. The 1‐ and 5‐year LC rates were 52% and 13%, respectively, with a higher biological effective dose (BED) associated with higher LC rates. Similarly, the 1‐ and 5‐year OS rates were 83% and 23%, respectively, with a trend toward higher OS with higher BED.

Because of the adverse outcomes in this patient group and the difficulties in achieving a margin‐negative resection at recurrence, carbon‐ion RT has also been investigated as a potential definitive therapy. Yamada et al. performed a phase 1/2 study that included 180 patients with LRRC (including 186 lesions) without a prior history of pelvic RT. 52 Patients were treated with a 16‐fraction regimen in 4 weeks with doses from 67.2‐ to 73.6‐Gy relative biological effectiveness (RBE). Surgeries to the primary tumor were evenly split between low‐anterior and abdominoperineal resections. In the phase 1 portion of the trial, no grade >3 acute toxicity was observed at the highest dose, 73.6‐Gy RBE, and this dose was selected for the phase 2 portion of the study. The 5‐year LC and OS rates at 73.6‐Gy RBE were a promising 88% and 59%, respectively. The prospective observational GUNMA 0801 trial showed similar results in a similar group of patients with a dose of 73.6‐Gy RBE in 16 fractions for 4 days per week. 53 For the 28 patients enrolled, the 3‐year LC and OS rates were 86% and 92%, respectively. No grade 3 or higher acute toxicities were observed; two patients developed late grade 3 pelvic infections.

Subtopic 2: What is the role of RT in previously irradiated patients with LRRC?

Patients with LRRC often present with significant symptoms that may adversely affect QOL and be a challenge to manage. The option of reirradiation was historically not considered because of the fear of potential toxicity. However, with the advent of advanced planning and delivery techniques such as intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and particle therapy, the toxicity associated with reirradiation can be mitigated, and reirradiation is more frequently considered. Data to support reirradiation in this clinical scenario are limited to several small single‐arm prospective trials and a number of retrospective studies, which are summarized in Table 1.

Mohiuddin et al. established the preliminary safety of pelvic reirradiation in a phase 1/2 pilot study for patients with LRRC. 66 In this study, patients who had previously received RT doses to the pelvis ranging from 30 to 64.87 Gy (median, 45 Gy) received 30 Gy to the posterior pelvis with opposed lateral fields shielding the small bowel and bladder, followed by an additional 10‐Gy boost to gross disease in select patients. Patients who were treated with palliative intent received 1.8 Gy per fraction, whereas patients treated preoperatively received 1.2 Gy per fraction delivered twice daily. All patients also received concurrent continuous‐infusion 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU). Cumulative RT doses ranged from 70.6 to 111.6 Gy. Of the 17 patients treated with preoperative intent, 15 underwent radical resection, with a 2‐year OS of 66%. For patients treated with palliative intent, 87% had symptom relief, with 40% experiencing partial response to treatment. The median OS in this cohort was 14 months.

A phase 2 study by Valentini et al. included 59 patients with LRRC (with a previous pelvic RT dose of 30–55 Gy) treated with preoperative CRT receiving a dose of 30 Gy in 1.2‐Gy twice‐daily fractions to the gross tumor volume plus a 4‐cm margin, followed by a boost to the gross tumor volume, plus a 2‐cm margin of 10.8 Gy with the same fractionation schedule. 19 Treatment was delivered via 3DCRT, and concurrent continuous‐infusion 5‐FU was administered. Surgical resection was planned for 6–8 weeks after the completion of CRT, followed by adjuvant systemic therapy. The majority of patients (86%) completed CRT without treatment interruption, and no acute grade 4 toxicities were observed. The overall response rate was 44%, and 83% of patients with pelvic pain before treatment had improvement in their symptoms. Surgical resection was completed in 51% of patients, with R0 and R1 resection rates of 35.6% and 5.1%, respectively. At a median follow‐up of 36 months, 5‐year LC was 39% in all patients and 69% in patients who underwent an R0 resection. In patients who underwent an R0 resection, the 5‐year OS was 67%, whereas it was 22% in patients who did not receive surgery or had an incomplete resection.

Cai et al. completed a phase 2 study evaluating accelerated hyperfractionated IMRT. 56 The study included 22 patients with LRRC with a prior history of pelvic RT (dose range, 36–62 Gy) treated with preoperative RT to a dose of 39 Gy in 1.3‐Gy fractions delivered twice daily. Subsequent surgery or chemotherapy was not mandated in the study. Nearly all patients (91%) completed the planned RT course as scheduled. There were no acute grade 4 toxicities and late toxicity was infrequent, with two patients developing chronic diarrhea, one patient with a small bowel obstruction, and two patients with dysuria. Partial response to treatment was observed in 41% of patients, and complete symptom relief was observed in 27% of patients. Cumulative local PFS rates were 67% at 1‐year post RT but dropped to 11% at 2 years. The median survival after reirradiation was 19 months.

Additional retrospective studies have demonstrated that hyperfractionated RT in patients with LRRC previously treated with RT is safe and can facilitate a margin‐negative resection. Tao et al. reported on the MD Anderson experience, which included 102 patients with LRRC treated with pelvic reirradiation to a dose of 30–45 Gy (median, 39 Gy) in 1.5‐Gy twice‐daily fractions to the gross tumor volume with a 1.5‐ to 2.5‐cm planning target volume margin. 63 The majority of patients (91%) received concurrent 5‐FU. Preoperative‐intent treatment was planned for 65 of the patients, with 66% ultimately undergoing surgical resection and an R0 resection rate of 67%. IORT to the gross tumor volume with a 2‐ to 3‐cm margin was also delivered in 22 of the patients who underwent surgical resection. Freedom from local progression at 3 years was 40%, and higher for those patients who underwent surgical resection (49% vs. 30%; p = .013). The 3‐year OS was 39%, again higher in patients who underwent surgical resection (63% vs. 20%; p < .0001). The 3‐year rate of grade 3/4 late toxicity was 34%. Other studies report the use of similar external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) doses ranging from 15 to 56.4 Gy, most often delivered as hyperfractionated treatment to the gross tumor, with a margin sometimes also including the presacral space. 54 , 58 , 60 , 61 , 65 Similar to Tao et al., more favorable outcomes were observed in those patients able to undergo surgical resection, with both Mohiuddin et al. (5‐year OS, 22% vs. 15%; p = .001) and Al‐Haidari et al. (3‐year OS, 62% vs. 16%; p = .001) reporting an improvement in OS compared to those receiving reirradiation alone. 54 , 60 Although subject to the limitations of retrospective data, multiple studies report a late grade 4 toxicity of less than 10%. 60 , 61 , 65

The use of hyperfractionated RT may not always be feasible for patients, and multiple studies have reported the use of once‐daily fractionation, typically to doses of 30–49.2 Gy. 54 , 60 , 65 Youssef et al. reported no difference in OS on the basis of fractionation technique, although the analysis was limited to small patient numbers, whereas Mohiuddin et al. reported increased late toxicity with once‐ versus twice‐daily fractionation (60% vs. 43%; p < .05), although limited by its use of 3DCRT. 60 , 65 No formal comparison of 3DCRT with IMRT has been performed, and such a prospective study is not feasible. Although many of the studies report that 3DCRT is safe and feasible, IMRT should be used to limit the dose to adjacent normal organs.

Given the RT dose that adjacent organs at risk receive with the initial course of treatment for rectal cancer, particle therapy is also a consideration, given its superior dose conformation and potential for lower adjacent normal tissue doses. 67 For proton beam therapy, initial acute toxicity and QOL outcomes have been reported for the single‐arm phase 2 RERAD II trial. 68 This initial report included results of the first 25 patients with LRRC with prior pelvic RT treated with dose‐escalated intensity‐modulated proton therapy, with doses of 55 Gy in 44 fractions as neoadjuvant treatment or 57.5–65 Gy in 46–52 fractions delivered once daily as definitive treatment, both with concurrent capecitabine. Grade 3 ileus was reported in two patients but there were no grade ≥4 toxicities reported. Patient‐reported outcomes showed largely stable global health scores during and after treatment. Outcome data with proton therapy are largely limited to single‐institution retrospective reviews. 59 , 62 , 69 Koroulakis et al. reported on 28 patients treated with proton beam reirradiation to doses of 16–60 Gy. 59 The rate of grade 3 acute toxicity was 11%, with no acute grade ≥4 events. There was one late grade 5 event in a patient with a prior history of RT‐related injury. The 1‐year local PFS and OS rates were 34% and 82%, respectively.

Carbon‐ion RT has also shown potential as an effective therapy for patients with LRRC, given its higher linear energy transfer potential compared to other RT modalities. In the setting of reirradiation, the current data largely consist of retrospective studies. 55 , 57 , 64 , 70 In one of the larger series, Yamada et al. reported on 77 patients with LRRC treated with carbon‐ion RT receiving a dose of 70.4‐Gy RBE delivered in 16 fractions during 4 weeks without subsequent resection. 64 No grade ≥4 acute or late toxicities were observed, with grade 3 acute and late toxicities in 10% and 21% of patients, respectively. At 5 years, the LC rate was 62%, with a 5‐year OS of 38%. Cai et al. reported on carbon‐ion RT (48‐ to 75.6‐Gy RBE) for 25 patients with unresectable LRRC, with 68% of the patients receiving prior pelvic RT. 57 Again, no grade ≥4 toxicity was observed, and no acute toxicity greater than grade 2 was observed. The 2‐year LC and OS were 72% and 65%, respectively. Chung et al. and Jeans et al. performed retrospective comparison studies of carbon‐ion RT with photon‐based reirradiation in patients with LRRC with prior RT. 71 , 72 With acknowledgment of the limitations of such studies, treatment with carbon‐ion RT was associated with improved outcomes and lower rates of toxicity.

Although the benefit of reirradiation in patients with LRRC has not yet been proven in a prospective trial, the currently available, largely retrospective data highlight its utility. For patients with symptomatic recurrences, reirradiation is effective at palliation, with improvement in symptoms in up to 80% of patients. 19 , 61 , 65 For patients with potentially resectable disease, multiple studies demonstrate the role that reirradiation may play in facilitating an R0 resection, which is key to long‐term survival. Bosman et al. at Catharina Hospital compared the outcomes of patients with LRRC treated with reirradiation (most receiving 30 Gy with a 10‐ to 15‐Gy IORT boost) to those who did not. 42 In the reirradiated patients, 56% achieved an R0 resection, 38% achieved an R1 resection, and 6% achieved an R2 resection, compared to 42%, 25%, and 33% completing an R0, R1, and R2 resection, respectively, in the group without preoperative RT (p < .001). As anticipated, resection margin status was an independent predictor of improved outcomes, including distant metastasis–free survival and OS. In a pooled analysis between Catharina Hospital and the Mayo Clinic investigating outcomes of patients receiving multimodality treatment for LRRC, the R0 resection rate was only 26% for patients who received no preoperative treatment, compared to 43%–50% for those who received preoperative reirradiation. 22 Similarly, in a retrospective analysis from the Cancer Registry of Norway, patients with LRRC who received reirradiation before surgery had the best survival outcomes, with a 3‐year OS of 50% compared to 35% for those who did not receive reirradiation. 41

Subtopic 3: What is the role of intraoperative therapy in the treatment of LRRC?

For patients with LRRC, completion of an R0 surgical resection remains one of the strongest prognostic factors for both recurrence and OS. 73 , 74 , 75 The ability to complete an R0 resection is largely reliant on the anatomic location of the recurrence. Perianastomotic or intraluminal recurrences are associated with higher rates of R0 resection and improved outcomes compared to other anatomic locations in the pelvis. 23 , 76 , 77 , 78 Although repeat surgical resection is already challenging because of the loss of surgical planes and fibrosis from prior RT, extraluminal recurrences are particularly challenging for achieving an R0 resection because of the potential involvement of anterior or posterior structures, including the bladder, vagina, bony pelvis, and sacral nerves. Given the challenges in achieving an R0 resection in this patient group and the relative radiation sensitivity of adjacent bowel limiting reirradiation dosing, IORT presents an opportunity to deliver high targeted doses of RT to the areas at highest risk of recurrence while simultaneously displacing adjacent radiosensitive organs such as the small bowel. IORT can be delivered either with electrons (IOERT) or as high–dose rate (HDR) brachytherapy, with advantages of each approach including faster setup and treatment time with electrons versus a more homogeneous dose distribution for irregular surfaces with HDR brachytherapy. 79 The long‐term oncologic outcomes of IOERT versus HDR brachytherapy for the treatment of patients with LRRC was investigated retrospectively by Voogt et al. and showed no difference in OS between the two but rather an association with improved local recurrence‐free survival with HDR brachytherapy at the expense of potentially more major postoperative complications. 79 The data supporting the utility of IORT for patients with LRRC are largely limited to retrospective studies with limited prospective data to date. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 Select studies are summarized in Table 2.

Tan et al. performed a prospective single‐arm feasibility study at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre that included 30 patients with either locally advanced (15%) or recurrent (85%) rectal cancer. 90 Of the 26 patients with LRRC, six had a prior history of pelvic RT and completed reirradiation to a mean dose of 45 Gy. R0 resection was achieved in 70% of patients, whereas 22% and 7% of patients underwent an R1 and R2 resection, respectively. At a median follow‐up of 3.5 years, the 2.5‐year local recurrence–free, failure‐free, and OS rates were 68%, 37%, and 82%, respectively. The rates of local recurrence did not correlate with surgical margin status, which suggests a potential benefit of IORT in patients who underwent an R1 or R2 resection, although numbers were small, which potentially underpowered the results. Grade 3 or 4 acute or late toxicities were observed in 37% of patients.

A number of large retrospective series highlight the potential benefit of IORT in patients with LRRC. The largest series from the Mayo Clinic includes 607 patients with recurrent colorectal cancer who received IOERT as part of salvage treatment. 21 Of the 607 patients, 427 had a rectal primary tumor, and 41 patients had received prior RT. EBRT was delivered in 96% of patients, with most receiving it before surgery (median dose, 27.5 Gy). Surgical margin status was R0 in 37%, R1 in 37%, and R2 in 26% of patients. The median IORT dose was 15 Gy. The 5‐year local relapse and OS rates were 28% and 30%, respectively. Grade ≥3 toxicity partially attributable to IORT was 11%. A series from Catharina Hospital included 147 patients with LRRC treated with neoadjuvant RT and IOERT at the time of surgical resection. 20 The reported 5‐year LC and OS rates were 54% and 32%, respectively. The Mayo Clinic and Catharina Hospital also performed a pooled analysis that included 565 patients who received multimodality treatment, including IOERT, which reported a 5‐year OS of 33% and a 5‐year local recurrence rate of 45%. 22 Shorter waiting times between completion of preoperative RT and IORT were associated with a decreased risk of local recurrence. In both the Mayo Clinic and Catharina Hospital series as well as the pooled analysis, achieving an R0 resection was associated with improved OS. Other large series report similar results with the addition of IORT as part of the multimodality management of patients with LRRC, with 5‐year LC rates of 39%–56% and 5‐year OS ranging from 23% to 56%. 80 , 82 , 83 , 88

Comparative studies highlighting the potential benefits of IORT are limited both in number and scope, given that the current data are limited to retrospective studies with heterogeneity in prior treatment and at the time of recurrence. An earlier study from the Mayo Clinic by Suzuki et al. reported on the results of 106 patients who underwent palliative surgical resection, with 42 of the patients receiving IORT to a median dose of 15 Gy for those with microscopic residual disease and 20 Gy for those with macroscopic residual disease. 13 In addition, 41 of the patients treated with IORT received adjuvant EBRT, with 90% receiving 45 Gy or more, whereas 55% in the non‐IORT cohort received EBRT. Receipt of IORT was associated with improved LC and OS. Similarly, Valentini et al. and Kishan et al. have reported on the potential association of IORT with improved LC and OS. 84 , 92 Given the importance of resection margin status, data also support the use of IORT to reduce the risk of local recurrence associated with an R1 resection. Hyngstrom et al. reported on a cohort of 100 patients with locally advanced or recurrent colorectal cancer treated with IORT. 83 Patients received preoperative RT (50.4 Gy if there was no prior history of RT vs. 39 Gy if there was a prior history of RT), and IORT was delivered via HDR brachytherapy with a median dose of 12.5 Gy. An R1 resection was not associated with worse LC (p = .316) or OS (p = .109). A study from the Mayo Clinic also examined the utility of reresection of microscopic positive margins on intraoperative frozen sections in patients with LRRC treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, CRT, and IORT at the time of resection. 93 The results of the study showed no difference in OS or local recurrence in patients with an R1 resection that was converted to R0 after reresection versus those patients with an R1 resection that remained R1 after reresection, which highlights that treatment with IORT was potentially adequate as opposed to a more aggressive surgical approach.

Subtopic 4: What is the role of stereotactic body radiation therapy in the treatment of LRRC?

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) has emerged as a noninvasive method to provide durable LC for patients with unresectable LRRC involving pelvic lymph nodes. The data supporting SBRT in this setting are entirely based on small retrospective series, with select studies presented in Table 3. 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 , 99 Johnstone et al. reported the largest series, which consisted of 69 patients with LRRC who had received prior pelvic RT, with the pelvic recurrence either not amenable to surgical resection or the patient refusing surgery. 96 The majority of patients had received prior RT doses of 45–54 Gy in 25–30 fractions to the pelvis, and 86% of recurrences were in pelvic lymph nodes. Patients were treated with 30 Gy in five fractions. The median PFS and OS were 12.1 and 38.7 months, respectively. Of the 24 patients who died, 14 deaths were attributable to consequences of the local recurrence. In another cohort, from Smith et al., 30 patients with an inoperable local recurrence, primarily located in the pelvic lymph nodes, were treated with SBRT to 30 Gy in five fractions. 98 The median PFS and OS were 12.1 and 28.3 months, respectively. No patients experienced grade 3 acute toxicity, and one patient experienced a late grade 3 toxicity. Similar smaller single‐institution studies report favorable rates of LC, with 1‐year rates of 90%–100% and low rates of grade 3/4 toxicity. 94 , 95 , 97 The optimal SBRT dose and fractionation to treat recurrent pelvic disease to balance toxicity and LC are currently unknown. Isotoxic dosing, where the prescription dose is escalated until it reaches a maximum predetermined organ‐at‐risk constraint accounting for prior RT, has been proposed and shown to be feasible. 99 However, the clinical utility of such an approach has not been demonstrated to date.

Subtopic 5: What is the role of brachytherapy in the treatment of LRRC?

Brachytherapy is another method whereby high doses of RT can be delivered to the area of recurrence while minimizing the dose to adjacent normal tissues, which have often received prior RT. Both low–dose rate (LDR) and HDR brachytherapy have been reported as potentially effective treatment options for patients with LRRC. 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105 LDR brachytherapy has the advantage of potentially broadening the therapeutic index by permitting repair of sublethal damage in normal tissues, whereas HDR permits dose sculpting.

Retrospective series report on the utility of LDR interstitial brachytherapy as a salvage option for patients with LRRC. 100 , 103 In a large series from China, 101 patients with LRRC (88% previously treated with RT) were treated with CT‐guided 125I‐seed implants. 100 The median LC and OS were 10 and 20.8 months, respectively, with 9% of patients experiencing a grade ≥3 adverse event. Bishop et al. reported a single‐institution experience of 20 patients with locally recurrent anorectal cancer (85% rectal) treated with interstitial brachytherapy at the MD Anderson Cancer Center. 103 The 1‐year rates of LC and OS were 80% and 95%, respectively, with 54% of patients experiencing long‐term palliation of symptoms related to their recurrence. One patient experienced a late grade 3 complication. In one of the few prospective studies, Shi et al. performed a phase 2 study including 57 patients with LRRC who had received up to one prior line of chemotherapy (not containing irinotecan) who were randomized to either 5‐FU, irinotecan, and leucovorin (FOLFIRI) alone or with the addition of LDR brachytherapy 1 week before FOLFIRI. 106 Brachytherapy consisted of 125I seeds placed under CT guidance with a prescribed dose of 120 Gy. At a median follow‐up of 30 months, the primary end point of the study, LC time, was statistically significant in favor of the brachytherapy plus chemotherapy group (median, 12 vs. 4 months; p < .001). Similarly, PFS favored the brachytherapy plus chemotherapy group (10 vs. 4 months; p < .001), as did 2‐year OS (49.8% vs. 20.1%; p = .0464). There was no difference in treatment‐related toxicities between the groups.

HDR brachytherapy has also been reported for the treatment of LRRC, predominantly in retrospective series. 101 , 102 Kolotas et al. reported on a cohort of 38 patients with LRRC in Germany treated with either CT‐guided or intraoperative HDR brachytherapy. 102 The median OS was 15 months, with 72% of deaths related to distant metastases. Palliation of pain was reported in 90% of patients, with a median duration of 5 months. In a series from the University of Southern California, 27 patients with unresectable LRRC underwent debulking surgery followed by HDR brachytherapy to the remaining disease by receiving a dose of 12–25 Gy in three to five fractions. 101 Rerecurrences were observed in 67% of patients, with a mean time to recurrence of 11.3 months. Ultimately, nine patients were without evidence of disease long term. Complications related to treatment occurred in 19% of patients.

Topic 5: What is the role of systemic therapy in the treatment of LRRC?

Subtopic 1: What is the role of chemotherapy in the treatment of LRRC?

Chemotherapy remains a fundamental component of the multidisciplinary management of LRRC, whether as part of multimodality curative‐intent treatment or as a palliative modality. The aims of preoperative systemic therapy include cytoreduction of gross disease to help facilitate R0 resection, reducing the risk of distant metastasis, providing a test of time and tumor biology to aid in patient selection for a potentially morbid local treatment, and, logistically, providing a window of time to allow for the coordination of the necessary surgical team members tailored to the individual case.

There are no randomized trials definitively addressing whether initial chemotherapy, before CRT, improves oncologic outcomes, such as OS, in patients amenable to a curative‐intent operation. Nordkamp et al. performed a multicenter retrospective comparison in which one institution routinely used neoadjuvant chemotherapy before neoadjuvant CRT, whereas the other primarily used CRT alone. 107 Neoadjuvant chemotherapy use was not associated with improved OS, nor was it associated with improvement in local rerecurrence–free survival, metastasis‐free survival, or pCR.

The role of adjuvant chemotherapy after curative‐intent resection for LRRC is also undefined, given that it is not well addressed in the literature and there are no randomized trials addressing this issue. Treatment decisions are often individualized on the basis of a patient’s disease history, prior treatment, performance status, surgical recovery, and extrapolation from data informing primary management of high‐risk colorectal cancer, with consideration for offering fluoropyrimidine‐based or oxaliplatin‐containing regimens in patients with aggressive histopathology or close or positive resection margins. However, evidence demonstrating improvement in DFS or OS is lacking.

For patients who are not candidates for curative‐intent surgery, systemic chemotherapy plays a palliative role focused on alleviating symptoms, controlling locoregional disease, and prolonging survival. Banken et al. reported on a cohort of 58 patients who were treated with palliative‐intent therapy with either systemic therapy, RT, or a combination. 108 Those treated with chemotherapy alone had an LC rate of 43%, whereas those who received CRT or chemotherapy and CRT had LC rates of 70% and 68%, respectively.

Subtopic 2: What is the optimal chemotherapy regimen in the treatment of LRRC?

Selecting an optimal systemic therapy regimen for LRRC requires integration of prior treatment history, the interval since initial therapy, disease molecular and/or biological features, and the planned treatment intent. In the curative‐intent setting, intensified chemotherapy regimens may be chosen to maximize tumor regression before surgery, whereas in the palliative setting, systemic therapy aligns with metastatic colorectal cancer paradigms with the goal of providing symptom relief and prolonging survival.

In patients who are chemotherapy naive at recurrence—typically those whose initial treatment involved upfront surgery or CRT without systemic therapy—doublet systemic therapy options such as FOLFOX (5‐FU, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin) or CAPOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin) are the preferred first‐line choices, although triplet combinations such as FOLFIRINOX (5‐FU, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan) can also be considered. Targeted therapies—including anti‐EGFR antibodies (in RAS/BRAF wild‐type disease), anti‐HER2 therapies (HER2‐positive tumors), VEGF inhibitors, BRAF inhibitors (BRAF V600E mutation), and KRAS G12C inhibitors—can be incorporated in the first‐line and/or later lines of therapy depending on the individual patient’s disease molecular features.

For patients previously treated with oxaliplatin‐ or fluoropyrimidine‐based chemotherapy, regimen selection depends on the disease‐free interval and residual toxicity. Oxaliplatin rechallenge is commonly considered when there is a sufficient interval (e.g., >6 months) between initial therapy and subsequent recurrence. Conversely, early recurrence or persistent neuropathy generally favors an alternative regimen, such as irinotecan‐based regimens such as FOLFIRI.

Overall, optimal systemic therapy for LRRC is highly individualized, which reflects both the heterogeneity of recurrence biology and the lack of prospective trials specific to LRRC. However, the guiding principles are that intensification of therapy may be warranted in order to maximize response in the context of potentially curable disease, whereas treatment should be tailored to balance QOL and disease control when the intent is palliative.

Subtopic 3: What is the optimal chemotherapy regimen when combined with concurrent RT in the treatment of LRRC?

Fluoropyrimidine‐based chemotherapy remains the established concurrent radiosensitizer in the care of patients with LRRC, although this is largely based on data informing practice for primary management. The two primary regimens are continuous‐infusion 5‐FU and oral capecitabine, both of which enhance DNA damage during RT and improve local tumor control. Capecitabine at 825 mg/m2 twice daily on RT days or continuous‐infusion 5‐FU at 225 mg/m2/day on days 1–5 are the most commonly used dosing strategies. 109 , 110

Despite extensive investigation in primary rectal cancer, the addition of oxaliplatin to fluoropyrimidine‐based CRT has not consistently demonstrated a survival or LC benefit in randomized trials, whereas it has substantially increased toxicity (STAR‐01, ACCORD 12, and CAO/ARO/AIO‐04). 111 , 112 , 113 As a result, oxaliplatin‐containing concurrent regimens are not recommended for LRRC. Similarly, irinotecan has shown radiosensitizing promise in early‐phase studies but carries substantial gastrointestinal toxicity, and has not demonstrated clear benefit in LRRC or primary disease.

Targeted agents such as mTOR inhibitors, 114 sorafenib, 115 VEGF inhibitors, 116 , 117 EGFR inhibitors, 118 , 119 , 120 , 121 and anti–PD‐1 antibody (e.g., pembrolizumab) 122 have been tested in combination with pelvic RT for primary rectal cancer but results have been negative, with increased toxicity and no clear improvement in outcomes. LRRC‐specific evidence is minimal, and such combinations should be considered investigational. Given the heightened toxicity risk during pelvic RT, particularly in the setting of prior RT, fluoropyrimidine monotherapy remains the well‐established and best‐tolerated radiosensitizer.

In summary, 5‐FU or capecitabine remains the standard concurrent chemotherapy for CRT for LRRC on the basis of proven safety and efficacy in primary rectal cancer with decades of clinical experience. Multiagent or targeted radiosensitizing regimens lack supporting evidence, and carry risks that outweigh the potential benefit.

Subtopic 4: What is the role of immunotherapy in the treatment of LRRC?

Although LRRC‐specific immunotherapy data are limited, the role of immunotherapy in the treatment of patients with dMMR/microsatellite instability–high (MSI‐H) rectal cancer has transformed dramatically. An early‐phase study by Cercek et al. evaluating patients treated at initial diagnosis for dMMR/MSI‐H rectal cancer demonstrated a clinical complete response rate of 100% with dostarlimab (a PD‐1 inhibitor). Subsequent larger analyses confirmed durable responses, with long‐term sphincter preservation and no need for CRT or surgery in the majority of patients. 123

In the metastatic context, the KEYNOTE‐177 study demonstrated substantially improved PFS with first‐line pembrolizumab, with fewer treatment‐related adverse events when compared with fluoropyrimidine‐based chemotherapy for patients with MSI‐H metastatic colorectal cancer. 124 The CheckMate 8HW trial further demonstrated the superior PFS of nivolumab plus ipilimumab over nivolumab alone in MSI‐H metastatic colorectal cancer. 125 Combination therapy yielded response rates of 71% compared with 58% for nivolumab alone, and responses were durable, with the median duration of response not yet reached, despite a median follow‐up of 47 months. Treatment‐related adverse events were higher with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, with grade 3/4 events of 22% versus 14%. These data support the use of dual checkpoint blockade in patients with LRRC with dMMR/MSI‐H tumors, with the goal of maximizing cytoreduction to facilitate subsequent curative‐intent surgery or as a strategy in those who are not operable candidates to maximize disease response and longevity.

Although there are no studies specifically focusing on patients with LRRC, outcomes from primary management and/or the metastatic setting may be generalized to those with LRRC disease. Immunotherapy may be considered neoadjuvantly to induce tumor shrinkage and potentially help facilitate resection. The role of adjuvant immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy after R0 salvage surgery is unstudied but may eventually be informed by ongoing primary colorectal cancer trials evaluating PD‐1 blockade in the postoperative setting.

The use of immunotherapy and subsequent NOM may also be considered. Although surveillance without RT or surgery is well established for primary locally advanced dMMR rectal cancer treated with immunotherapy, there is minimal experience with organ‐preservation strategies in LRRC. Nevertheless, in patients who are medically inoperable or decline radical pelvic surgery, immunotherapy offers a promising, potentially definitive treatment option, with favorable toxicity profiles and high response rates in the appropriate molecular subset.

For microsatellite‐stable LRRC, ICIs remain relatively ineffective, and research is ongoing to explore patient selection and combination strategies to improve effectiveness.

Limitations of this study include the inclusion of a majority of retrospective studies with associated heterogeneity, primarily driven by high rates of pelvic control with neoadjuvant therapy resulting in low rates of locoregional recurrence and relatively small series. Some of the studies were also completed in the era before the development of more modern systemic therapy and RT, including IMRT and modern image guidance. Finally, the extent of disease at recurrence, including fixation‐based classification, was also not routinely reported in the vast majority of studies. Nonetheless, the available evidence strongly indicates that an individualized multidisciplinary approach with a strong preponderance of achieving a margin‐negative surgical resection, when feasible, results in the best possible patient outcomes.

SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATIONS

Treatment decisions under these circumstances are complex, and should take into account patient goals and anticipated outcomes associated with the modality used.

The panel strongly recommends that preoperative RT ± concurrent or sequential systemic therapy followed by surgery ± IORT (to facilitate tumor downsizing and improve the likelihood of R0 resection) may be appropriate for the typical case with localized pelvic recurrence of rectal cancer after total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) with long‐course CRT, consolidation chemotherapy, and surgery without sacral involvement in an operative patient. The panel also recommends that surgery ± IORT may also be appropriate in this clinical scenario; however, the preoperative RT‐containing regimen is slightly favored. Although preoperative systemic therapy alone followed by surgery ± IORT (without preoperative therapy) may also be appropriate, it is the least preferred of the three treatment options. (Variant 1)
