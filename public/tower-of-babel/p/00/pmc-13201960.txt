Navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach for cyst fenestration of a dorsal brainstem lesion: illustrative case

Cystic components in brain tumors located around the brainstem can cause progressive neurological deterioration due to mass effect and obstructive hydrocephalus. In particular, surgical fenestration of dorsal brainstem cystic lesions is commonly challenging to perform because of difficulties in selecting an optimal surgical corridor. Previous reports have described several endoscopic approaches for managing dorsal brainstem cystic lesions.1–6 Herein, we report a case of a dorsal brainstem cyst that was successfully treated with a navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported.

Illustrative Case

Clinical History

A 9-year-old boy undergoing treatment for an optic pathway pilocytic astrocytoma presented with truncal ataxia and somnolence. MRI revealed reenlargement of a brainstem cyst that caused compression of the brainstem and fourth ventricle, accompanied by hydrocephalus. The patient was then admitted to our institution (Fig. 1). He was diagnosed with an optic pathway pilocytic astrocytoma at the age of 3 years, and he initially presented with mild motor weakness of the left upper limb. He had been managed conservatively with chemotherapy for 2 years. At the age of 5 years, he was urgently admitted to the hospital due to tumor progression and worsening hydrocephalus, and he underwent microsurgical resection of the tumor using the transsylvian transinsular approach. Subsequently, he required multiple additional procedures, including repeated microsurgical resections for tumor recurrence and growth of the cystic component (Fig. 1A and B). Despite these treatments, the cystic component in the posterior fossa gradually enlarged, resulting in compression of the fourth ventricle and obstructive hydrocephalus (Fig. 1C–F). Therefore, a navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach for cyst fenestration was planned. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position on an intraoperative MRI (iMRI)–compatible surgical table. The initial iMRI study was conducted, and neuronavigation was performed to validate the trajectory to the cyst via the safe entry zone in the rostral portion of the fourth ventricle. A small craniotomy was performed in the superolateral portion of the left posterior fossa to partially expose the transverse sinus (Fig. 2A and B). A peel-off sheath (17.5-Fr Neurosheath, Medikit) was advanced toward the cyst under VarioGuide guidance. The cyst wall was widely fenestrated under rigid endoscopic visualization. An Ommaya reservoir was subsequently implanted to allow aspiration of the cyst contents in case of cyst reexpansion (Fig. 2C–F and Video 1). The second iMRI study confirmed cyst fenestration and placement of the Ommaya reservoir catheter within the cyst, which was also verified on postoperative CT imaging (Fig. 3A and B). Contrast-enhanced MRI conducted on postoperative day 1 revealed sufficient communication between the cyst and the fourth ventricle, accompanied by cyst shrinkage and improvement of hydrocephalus (Fig. 3C and D). Three-dimensional imaging showed that the catheter was inserted into the cyst via the suprafacial triangle, which is one of the safe entry zones via the floor of the fourth ventricle for brainstem lesions (Fig. 3E and F). The patient developed transient truncal ataxia after the surgery. However, he was able to ambulate independently. The patient was discharged home after 2 weeks. Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered for the residual solid tumor component. The patient remains clinically stable under outpatient follow-up. MRI at 12 months after surgery confirmed communication between the cyst and the fourth ventricle and no cyst reexpansion (Fig. 4). Discussion

Observations

Previous studies have reported several endoscopic approaches for managing dorsal brainstem cystic lesions.1–6 Jafarpour et al. have presented an endoscopic posterior suboccipital approach for fourth ventricular cysts.2 This method involves posterior suboccipital craniotomy and the advancement of a rigid endoscope via the foramen of Magendie into the fourth ventricle to directly visualize and fenestrate the cyst. Furthermore, it provides a midline, minimally invasive route that prevents traversing cerebellar tissues and allows bilateral or multisite cyst fenestration.2 Say et al. described an endoscopic suboccipital transaqueductal approach to the isolated fourth ventricle.4 This is a minimally invasive endoscopic technique in which a burr hole is created in the suboccipital region, the fourth ventricle is cannulated via the foramen of Magendie, and the endoscope is advanced upward to reach and fenestrate the cerebral aqueduct. This inferior-to-superior trajectory allows direct access to the aqueduct if the lateral ventricles are small or supratentorial approaches are not feasible, thereby providing an alternative route for treating an isolated or trapped fourth ventricle.4 Wang et al. also reported on the endoscopic transcerebellar medullary fissure approach.5 In this minimally invasive posterior fossa technique, a small midline suboccipital craniotomy is performed, and the cerebellar tonsils are gently separated via the medullary fissure. Then, an endoscope is advanced to directly visualize the dorsal aspect of the brainstem. This approach provides a natural midline corridor, prevents traversing cerebellar tissues, and allows safe access to dorsal brainstem lesions under direct endoscopic visualization.5 Some reports have also described the endoscopic trans–third ventricle approach. This transventricular endoscopic technique involves the following: 1) a frontal burr hole is established to access the lateral ventricle, 2) the endoscope is advanced via the foramen of Monro into the third ventricle, and 3) the cerebral aqueduct is traversed to reach the fourth ventricle. Under direct endoscopic visualization, the wall of the arachnoid cyst in the fourth ventricle is widely fenestrated to reestablish CSF flow. This method avoids suboccipital craniotomy and provides a minimally invasive route from above if the cerebral aqueduct is sufficiently dilated.6,7 Although the telovelar approach provides a well-established microsurgical corridor to the fourth ventricle, it remains relatively invasive and requires cerebellar manipulation, which may result in postoperative neurological deficits such as ataxia. In the current case, a small craniotomy was made in the left posterior fossa. A navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach for cyst fenestration was utilized. This strategy is associated with a low risk of CSF leakage, and it facilitates surgical manipulation. Moreover, the surgical corridor to the cyst is direct and linear, thereby allowing easy and reliable access. This technique is optimal for the patient in the current case, who had experienced multiple recurrences despite repeated treatments. The use of this approach for the surgical fenestration of brainstem cystic lesions has not been reported. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report showing the application of the endoscopic transcerebellar approach for brainstem cyst fenestration.

There are four key points that should be considered when performing the endoscopic transcerebellar approach for brainstem cystic lesions. First, a small craniotomy should be placed in the superolateral portion of the posterior fossa with partial exposure of the transverse sinus. Second, the surgical trajectory must be carefully planned to avoid injury to the cerebellar nuclei. Third, based on the 3D-reconstructed images and neuronavigation guidance, the entry point into the cyst should be at the site where the brainstem tissue overlying the cyst is as thin as possible and be located at a safe entry zone in the rostral portion of the floor of the fourth ventricle.8,9 Finally, to minimize the risk of recurrence, a sufficiently wide communication between the cyst and the fourth ventricle should be established at the end of the procedure. To facilitate surgical manipulation, the craniotomy should be placed cranially, as close as possible to the transverse sinus, rather than caudally. A more midline approach is associated with an increased risk of injury to the cerebellar nuclei. This type of injury may cause evident cerebellar deficits, such as ataxia, gait instability, and impaired motor coordination. A left-sided approach was selected in the current case. This is because the solid component of the tumor laid along the anticipated surgical corridor, thereby decreasing the risk of brainstem injury and enabling additional tumor resection. The suprafacial triangle in the rostral portion of the floor of the fourth ventricle is the widest among the safe entry zones located on the floor of the fourth ventricle. Therefore, the rostral portion of the floor of the fourth ventricle should be targeted under neuronavigation guidance.8–10 To reduce the risk of recurrence, it is important to establish a sufficiently wide and durable communication between the cyst and the ventricular system (or subarachnoid cistern), thereby restoring CSF circulation.11,12 In particular, the endoscopic transcerebellar approach is useful because it facilitates access to the cyst via the fourth ventricle, thereby enabling the establishment of a secure communication between the cyst and the fourth ventricle.

However, this approach carries several potential risks related to the transgression of cerebellar and brainstem structures. Injury to the cerebellar hemispheric cortex and subcortical white matter may result in limb ataxia, dysmetria, and coordination deficits. Involvement of the deep cerebellar nuclei, particularly the dentate nucleus, may lead to more pronounced and persistent cerebellar dysfunction, including impaired motor planning and cerebellar cognitive-affective symptoms. As the trajectory approaches the fourth ventricle, there is a risk of injury to the floor of the fourth ventricle, which contains critical nuclei of cranial nerves. Damage to this region may result in cranial nerve deficits such as diplopia, facial weakness, dysphagia, dysarthria, and impaired respiratory or autonomic function. Furthermore, direct or indirect injury to the brainstem represents a major concern, as this may cause severe neurological deficits including long tract signs, altered consciousness, and life-threatening cardiorespiratory instability. In addition, the transcerebellar corridor may be associated with the risk of hemorrhage from cerebellar vessels, which may further compromise neurological function. Therefore, the indications for this approach should be considered with great caution. In the current case, despite a history of using multiple supratentorial approaches, the cyst repeatedly enlarged over a short period of time, which led us to select the navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach. Therefore, this method can be a last-resort option if no other viable alternative is available.

Lessons

A navigation-guided endoscopic transcerebellar approach can be an effective treatment option for dorsal brainstem cysts. The surgical corridor to the cyst is direct and linear, thereby allowing easy and reliable access. The entry point into the cyst should be located in the rostral portion of the floor of the fourth ventricle. Hence, it is important to create a sufficiently wide communication between the cyst and the fourth ventricle.
