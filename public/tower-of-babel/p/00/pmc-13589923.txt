Molecular architecture responsible for specific inhibition of oncogenic PI3Kα mutants by RLY-2608 and STX-478
Authors: Xiao Liu, Yanyan Chen, Guanyi Li, Anqi Chen, Qingtong Zhou, Ming-Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHotspot mutations in phosphoinositide 3-kinase alpha (PI3Kα), such as H1047R, E542K, and E545K, drive tumorigenesis across multiple cancer types. Orthosteric PI3Kα inhibitors are effective but have toxicity against wild-type PI3Kα, the emergence of resistance, and a narrow therapeutic index. Allosteric inhibitors such as RLY-2608 and STX-478 offer a promising path toward mutant-specific suppression, yet the structural basis for their selectivity and mechanisms of action remain elusive. Here, we report high-resolution cryogenic electron microscopy structures of RLY-2608- and STX-478-bound H1047R, E542K, and E545K, revealing a shared cryptic allosteric pocket, accessible only through a major conformational rearrangement of the activation loop that is stabilized in oncogenic mutants. While both inhibitors occupy this pocket, they have distinct interaction networks and propagate divergent allosteric activities: RLY-2608 induces large-scale remodeling of catalytic and membrane-interacting elements, whereas STX-478 reinforces autoinhibitory interfaces between p110α and p85α subunits. Comparative analysis provides structural insights into their differentiated potency and selectivity.Key words: PI3Kα, Mutation, Inhibitor, Selectivity, Cancer

Introduction

Phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks) belong to a large family of lipid kinases that phosphorylate the 3′-hydroxyl group of phosphoinositides (PtdIns) on the plasma membrane1, 2, 3, 4. They play central roles in regulating critical cellular processes such as metabolism, cell survival, and motility, with their dysregulation implicated in cancer, inflammation, and metabolic disorders4, 5, 6. PI3Ks are categorized into three classes based on substrate specificity. Among these, phosphoinositide 3-kinase alpha (PI3Kα)—a class IA heterodimer composed of a catalytic subunit (p110α) and a regulatory subunit (p85α)—is of particular interest due to its established role in oncogenesis. This significance is underscored by the high prevalence of gain-of-function p110α mutations in human solid tumors7,8, with approximately 80% of the alterations clustering within three major hotspots9,10: H1047R in the kinase domain and E542K/E545K in the helical domain. Notably, the H1047R mutation enhances membrane binding and catalytic activity, whereas that of E542K/E545K disrupts the inhibitory interaction with p85α, both leading to constitutive PI3K/AKT pathway activation and driving tumor cell proliferation11,12.

A large number of small-molecule inhibitors targeting PI3K have been developed6,13,14, the majority of which are of orthosteric nature and compete with ATP for kinase binding, thereby suppressing the hyperactive PI3K/AKT pathway in PIK3CA-mutant tumors15,16. Some of them showed proven clinical effectiveness exemplified by alpelisib (BYL-719). However, the efficacy of these conventional agents is often limited by the constrained mutational landscape of the p110α active site, the propensity to acquire secondary resistance mutations17, 18, 19, and dose-limiting on-target toxicity towards wild-type (WT) PI3Kα. This narrows the therapeutic window and leads to adverse events such as hyperglycemia18, prompting the exploration of allosteric inhibitors (Fig. 1A). RLY-2608 and STX-478 have thus been developed with demonstrated preference for H1047R over WT (12- and 14-fold, respectively)18,20. Despite their therapeutic potential, the underlying structural basis of such selectivity remains elusive.

Here, we present high-resolution cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of RLY-2608- or STX-478-bound oncogenic PI3Kα mutants (H1047R, E542K, and E545K). Our comprehensive structural analysis, complemented by experimental validation, unveils the unique binding modes, allosteric regulation, and molecular architecture manifested by the two mutant-selective inhibitors, offering a structural framework and mechanistic insights into the development of next-generation therapeutic agents.

Methods

Expression and purification

Protein expression and purification followed the established methods10,21. Briefly, the p110α and p85α genes were cloned into the pFastBac Dual vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), incorporating an N-terminal 6×His tag for purification. Recombinant baculoviruses containing the above genes were prepared by the Bac-to-Bac system (Invitrogen) and then used to infect Trichoplusia ni HighFive insect cells. To maximize yield, the multiplicity of infection was increased. Expression was carried out in the presence of 150 nmol/L STX-478 or 250 nmol/L RLY-2608. Cells were harvested 48 h post-infection and stored at −80 °C until processing. Purification of mutant complexes mirrored the WT protocol10. However, due to the inherent tendency of the mutant to aggregate, resulting in lower yields, the salt concentration in the wash buffer was reduced to 150 mmol/L NaCl.

Cryo-EM data collection

Cryo-EM grids were prepared with the Vitrobot Mark IV plunger (FEI) set to 4 °C and 10% humidity. Compound STX-478 was added to the purified complex at a final concentration of 300 μmol/L and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min before flash-frozen. For RLY-2608, due to its lower solubility, the compound was added post-purification and incubated overnight to achieve a final concentration of 500 μmol/L. Three microliters of sample solution were applied to freshly glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 holey golden grids, allowed to incubate for 6 s, and then blotted for 4 s on both sides with a blot force of 1. After that, the grids were flash-frozen in liquid ethane for vitrification. Data collection was performed on an FEI Titan Krios (FEI) electron microscopy (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detection device and operated at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV. A nominal magnification of 105,000 × was used in counting mode with serial EPU Software (FEI Eindhoven), corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.824 Å. Images were obtained with a defocus span from −1.0 to −2.0 μm. An accumulated dose of 50 e−/Å2 was fractionated into a movie stack of 36 frames.

Image processing

MotionCor2 was used to perform the frame-based motion-correction algorithm to generate drift-corrected micrograph for further processing and defocus parameters were estimated by CTFFIND 4.022. Dose-weighted sums from each stack were then used for subsequent processing. Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters for individual micrographs were determined with Gctf (v1.06)23. Further processing including particle picking, extraction, 2D classification, 3D classification, ab initio reconstruction, and local refinement was conducted in CryoSPARC (v4).

For the RLY-2608-bound H1047R dataset, our previously determined WT PI3Kα structure (PDB ID: 7MYN) served as a reference for automatic particle picking, yielding 4,329,427 particles. They were then divided into five subsets, each containing approximately 870,000 particles, followed by 2D classification. Non-characteristic classes identified during 2D classification were subjected to ab initio reconstruction, generating three discrete initial models. Concurrently, particles exhibiting high-resolution features and numerical predominance were processed to establish a reference model for intact complexes. All particles subsequently underwent heterogeneous refinement, incorporating for each subset: the imported reference model and the three ab initio-derived initial models. This generated distinct particle classes per subset. High-resolution particle classes across all refinements were pooled and reconstructed, producing a final electron density map at 2.84 Å resolution.

For the RLY-2608-bound E545K and E542K PI3Kα datasets, processing followed the workflow established for the RLY-2608-bound H1047R. The BYL-719-bound E542K structure (PDB ID: 8GUA) served as a reference for particle selection, yielding 8,476,709 particles (E545K) and 6,290,648 particles (E542K), respectively. Particles were distributed to six subsets for 2D classification. Particles displaying definitive complex features were pooled across subsets and processed by ab initio reconstruction to generate an initial model. This model underwent successive homogeneous and non-uniform refinement, producing reference maps. Using these refined maps as templates, particles were re-extracted and subjected to 3D classification. Well-defined classes were merged and reconstructed. Low-pass filtration excluded particles contributing to resolutions below 15 Å. Final refinement yielded electron density maps at 3.01 Å (RLY-2608-bound E545K PI3Kα) and 2.89 Å (RLY-2608-bound E542K PI3Kα) resolutions, respectively.

For the STX-478-bound H1047R dataset, our previously resolved structure of WT PI3Kα (PDB ID: 7MYN) was used as a reference for automatic particle picking, yielding 6,307,262 particles. They were randomly partitioned into six subsets, each containing approximately one million particles. One subset was selected for 2D classification. Particles lacking complex features were identified during this step and subjected to ab initio reconstruction, generating five initial models. Heterogeneous refinement was then performed independently on each of the six randomly assigned subsets. The five initial models obtained from ab initio processing, along with the imported template (PDB ID: 7MYN), were used in a 3D classification step to automatically separate particles into two groups containing (i) the target complex and (ii) non-target particles. The selected particles identified across the classifications (i) were pooled. This combined particle set underwent further ab initio reconstruction, automatically separating into two distinct classes. Heterogeneous refinement was performed in these classes. Refinement of the selected class produced a final electron density map at 3.31 Å resolution.

For the STX-478-bound E545K and E542K datasets, processing mirrored the workflow used for the STX-478-bound H1047R. The previously determined BYL-719-bound E542K structure (PDB ID: 8GUA) served as the reference template for automatic particle picking. Particle selection yielded 4,947,175 particles for the STX-478-bound E545K and 6,328,286 particles for the STX-478-bound E542K. These particles were randomly partitioned into subsets, each containing approximately one million particles. Following 2D classification, subsets lacking characteristic complex features were selected for ab initio reconstruction. Subsequently, all particles underwent heterogeneous refinement. Each refinement incorporated the imported high-quality reference model alongside three lower-quality initial models generated by the preceding ab initio reconstruction. Particles identified as high-resolution classes across the refinements were pooled. Low-pass filtering excluded particles yielding resolutions below 15 Å or 20 Å. Refinement of the selected, dominant particle classes produced final electron density maps at resolutions of 3.01 Å (E545K) and 2.86 Å (E542K), respectively.

Model building and refinement

The WT structure (PDB ID: 7MYN) underwent rigid-body docking into the reconstructed density. Ligand coordinates and geometric restraints were prepared using the electronic Ligand Builder and Optimization Workbench (eLBOW)24 and docked into the cryo-EM density via the LigandFit GUI25 in PHENIX (v1.18.2-3874). Initial model placement was performed in UCSF Chimera, followed by multiple cycles of manual rebuilding in COOT (v8.0) and computational refinement in PHENIX. Model quality was evaluated using PHENIX's comprehensive cryo-EM validation suite, with full metrics detailed in Supporting Information Table S1. Molecular graphics were prepared using UCSF Chimera26, ChimeraX27, and PyMOL (Schrödinger, v4.6).

Western blot

T-47D and MCF-7 cells were cultured in DMEM (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% FBS (ThermoFisher). All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For H1047R and E545K signalling analysis, the cells were seeded at 200,000 cells/well in 12-well plates, followed by overnight adhesion. They were then treated with RLY-2608 or STX-478 (0, 50, 100, 200, and 400 nmol/L; DMSO ≤0.5%) for 6 h at 37 °C. Thereafter, cells were washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Yeasen, Shanghai, China, Cat# 20115ES60) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (TargetMol, Wellesley Hills, MA, USA, Cat# C0001) and 1 mmol/L PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# ST506). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were stored at −80 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified by BCA assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0012), and 30 μg of protein per sample was separated by SDS–PAGE. Proteins were transferred to 0.45-μm PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), blocked with 5% BSA, and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p-AKT (Cell Signaling Technology), AKT (Cell Signaling Technology), and p110α (Abclonal, Wuhan, China). After three 30-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 30 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence. Experiments included three independent replicates and were presented with ImageJ (v1.54g).

For residue validation, experiments were performed using 293T cells. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO2. They were then seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL, with 1 mL added per well. When the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were subjected to serum starvation for 12 h. Transfection was then carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were treated with compounds at the following final concentrations: RLY-2608 at 2 μmol/L, 200 nmol/L, 20 nmol/L, and 2 nmol/L, STX-478 at 1 μmol/L, 200 nmol/L, 40 nmol/L, and 8 nmol/L. The final concentration of DMSO in all treatments was kept below 0.5%. Following 48 h of compound exposure, the cells were harvested for subsequent analyses as described above.

Cell viability assay

MCF-7 and T-47D cells were cultured in DMEM medium (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% FBS, while SK-BR-3 cells were maintained in McCoy's 5A medium (ThermoFisher) also containing 10% FBS. They were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2, seeded into 96-well plates at densities of 8000, 8000, and 25,000 cells per well, respectively, and allowed to overnight adhesion. After 24 h, T-47D cells were treated with RLY-2608 or STX-478 at a maximum concentration of 20 μmol/L (with 0.5% DMSO) and five-fold serially diluted. For MCF-7 and SK-BR-3 cells, the maximum concentration of RLY-2608 and STX-478 used was 625 μmol/L (with 0.625% DMSO). Following 48 h exposure, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan, Cat# CK04) was carefully added to each well to avoid bubble formation. After 2 h incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, VT, USA). The blank control consisted of culture medium only, while the vehicle control contained corresponding gradients of DMSO (Sigma–Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) with differences expressed as mean ± SEM.

Lipid kinase activity

Lipid kinase activity was measured using the HTRF Kinase Assay (Merck, Cat# 33-017) as reported previously21. In brief, purified PI3Kα H1047R mutant complex was titrated and used at a concentration equivalent to its EC70 value (0.25 nmol/L). Compounds RLY-2608 and STX-478 were added separately at 0.5 μL per well, with RLY-2608 at a starting concentration of 100 μmol/L in 10-fold serial dilutions and STX-478 at 50 μmol/L in 10-fold serial dilutions. Following mixing with the H1047R mutant and PIP2 substrate (14.5 μL), RLY-2608 was incubated for 2 h, whereas STX-478 was incubated for 30 min. Subsequently, the ATP working buffer (5 μL) provided in the kit was added. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min at room temperature before termination with stop buffer and addition of detection mixture. The plate was then incubated in the dark for 2 h and read using an EnVision multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA, RRID: SCR_012163). The same procedure was simultaneously applied to the other mutants. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (RRID: SCR_002798). Results are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

MD simulations were performed by Gromacs 2021.428. The MD simulation of the RLY-2608–WT PI3Kα complex was built based on the X-ray structure of RLY-2608-bound WT PI3Kα (PDB ID: 8TSD)18 and prepared by the Protein Preparation Wizard (Schrödinger 2024-1). The protein chain termini were capped with acetyl and methylamide. All titratable residues were left in their dominant state at pH 7.0. To build MD simulation systems, the complexes were solvated with 0.15 mol/L NaCl in explicit TIP3P water molecules using CHARMM-GUI Solution Builder29. The CHARMM36m force field30 was adopted for the protein and salt ions. The parameters of RLY-2608 were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF)31. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to treat all electrostatic interactions beyond a cut-off of 10 Å, and the bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm32. The complex system was first relaxed using the steepest descent energy minimization, followed by slow heating of the system to 310.15 K with restraints. The restraints were reduced gradually over 10 ns. Finally, a restraint-free production run was carried out for each simulation, with a time step of 2 fs in the NPT ensemble at 310.15 K and 1 bar using the V-rescale thermostat33 and the isotropic Parrinello-Rahman barostat34, respectively. The interface area was calculated using the program FreeSASA 2.0, using the Shrake-Rupley algorithm with a probe radius of 1.4 Å35. The free energy landscape was plotted using the Deeptime 0.4.4 software package36. Similar simulation procedure and analysis were adopted for the MD simulations of RLY-2608–E542K PI3Kα, RLY-2608–E545K PI3Kα, RLY-2608–H1047R PI3Kα, STX-478–WT PI3Kα, STX-478–E542K PI3Kα, STX-478–E545K PI3Kα, and STX-478–H1047R PI3Kα complexes.

Results

Structural determination

To elucidate the binding modes of RLY-2608 and STX-478 (Fig. 1B), we determined the cryo-EM structures of H1047R, E545K, and E542K bound by them. The Bac-to-Bac expression system was used to prepare baculovirus carrying p110α and p85α, followed by infecting HighFive insect cells for protein production. During purification, each compound was added to ensure its incorporation into the mutant complex, with final concentrations of 250 nmol/L (RLY-2608) and 150 nmol/L (STX-478), respectively. A 6×His tag was introduced at the N terminus of p110α to facilitate purification. The PI3Kα mutant complexes were purified as previously described21 without the presence of BYL-719, and the purities were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Supporting Information Figs. S1 and S2). The RLY-2608-bound PI3Kα mutants were prepared by incubating the protein (1 mg/mL) with RLY-2608 at a final concentration of 500 μmol/L at 4 °C overnight. The homogeneity and dispersion of the particles were assessed by negative staining (Figs. S1 and S2). Cryo-EM imaging of the vitrified complexes was performed on a Titan Krios equipped with a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector (Table S1). Subsequent data analysis yielded high-resolution cryo-EM maps, enabling us to determine the structures of the RLY-2608-bound H1047R, E545K, and E542K mutants at overall resolutions of 2.84, 3.01, and 2.89 Å, respectively (Fig. 1, Fig. S1, and Table S1). Local resolution analysis of the three maps indicated high-resolution features within the catalytic core of p110α, whereas flexible regions such as the ABD and parts of p85α exhibited lower resolution (Fig. S1). In the RLY-2608-bound H1047R map, the density quality allowed for unambiguous model building of all p110α domains, the nSH2/iSH2 domains of p85α, and the bound RLY-2608. Regarding E545K and E542K, while all p110α domains could be modeled, the density of p85α was partial and insufficient for complete modeling (Fig. 1, Fig. S1, and Table S1). Owing to inherent structural flexibility, the SH3, BH, and cSH2 domains of p85α were not well resolved, consistent with our previous cryo-EM results10,21.

The same approach was employed to determine the cryo-EM structures of STX-478-bound H1047R, E545K and E542K (Fig. 1, Fig. S2, and Table S1). Each complex was prepared by incubating the PI3Kα mutant (1 mg/mL) with STX-478 at a final concentration of 300 μmol/L for 30 min at 4 °C prior to vitrification. Data collection parameters were consistent with those used for the RLY-2608-bound complexes. The resulting cryo-EM maps for STX-478-bound H1047R, E545K and E542K displayed resolutions of 3.31, 3.01, and 2.86 Å, respectively (Fig. S2). Structural modelling of them revealed a two-component architecture comprising all p110α domains along with the nSH2 and iSH2 domains of p85α (Fig. 1). In contrast, only the p110α was resolved in the E545K and E542K maps. Such domain visibility is consistent with that observed in RLY-2608-bound complexes (Fig. 1).

Binding of RLY-2608

RLY-2608 demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against three cancer cell lines, SK-BR-3 (PIK3CAWT), MCF-7 (PIK3CAE545K), and T-47D (PIK3CAH1047R). It exhibited an enhanced potency against T-47D breast cancer cells containing H1047R compared to other variants (Fig. 2A) and suppressed PI3K signalling by significantly reducing phosphorylated AKT levels at Ser473 (Fig. 2B).

In the RLY-2608-bound H1047R structure, RLY-2608 occupies an atypical shallow pocket formed by key regulatory elements: the catalytic loop (residues 909–920), the activation loop (residues 933–958), kα9 (residues 996–1009), and kα10 (residues 1016–1025)37, distinct from the orthosteric site commonly occupied by pan- or isoform-specific inhibitors (Fig. 2C). This unique binding site is fully occluded in both apo (PDB ID: 7MYN) and inhibitor-bound WT PI3Kα (PDB ID: 7MYO) and H1047R mutant (PDB ID: 8GUB), where the N-terminal segment of the activation loop (residues 933–941) adopts an inward-folded conformation stabilized by multiple polar and hydrophobic contacts with the catalytic loop (Fig. 2C and Supporting Information Figs. S3 and S4). In contrast, RLY-2608 binding induces a substantial outward displacement (7.6 Å at the Cα atom of F937) of the N-terminal region of the activation loop compared to the 7MYN structure, thereby expanding the allosteric pocket and enabling deep insertion of the inhibitor (Fig. S3B). Despite this large conformational change, the DFG motif maintains the canonical “DFG-in” conformation, similar to that observed in complexes with orthosteric inhibitors such as BYL-719 (Fig. S3B).

RLY-2608 adopts a rigid three-dimensional architecture centred on an aminoisoxindole core, flanked by three aromatic moieties: 2-chloro-5-fluorophenyl, [1,2,4]triazolo[1,5-a]pyridine-5-carbonitrile, and 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl)benzene. Structural analysis reveals that the 2-chloro-5-fluorophenyl ring penetrates deeply into a cleft between kα9 and kα10, stabilized by π-stacking with F1002 and hydrophobic interactions with I1019 and I1022. Additionally, its terminal chlorine and fluorine atoms form halogen bonds with the backbone carbonyl of E1012 and the side-chain nitrogen of K942. The 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl)benzene ring is clasped by the N-terminal segment of the activation loop and kα10, further stabilized by stacking with K942 and polar interactions with T813 and D939. The [1,2,4]triazolo[1,5-a]pyridine-5-carbonitrile group is oriented toward the solvent and encircled by the activation loop, though part of this loop remains unresolved due to limited local resolution (Fig. 2D). Key residues including R916 (catalytic loop), R951 (activation loop), and Y1021 (kα10) are likely to contribute additional stabilizing contacts. The central aminoisoxindole core is sandwiched by K942 and Y1021, engaging in cation–π and π–π interactions, respectively (Fig. 2D). Functional validation shows that mutations of key pocket residues (K942, E1012 and F1002) impaired the ability of RLY-2608 to suppress pAKT(S473) in the H1047R background (Fig. 2E). To further validate these findings, we performed in vitro kinase assays using purified PI3Kα proteins bearing the H1047R mutation alone or in combination with additional binding-site mutations. RLY-2608 potently inhibited H1047R single-mutant PI3Kα with an IC50 of 12.0 nmol/L, whereas introduction of K942E or E1012A mutations to the H1047R background substantially attenuated its inhibitory activity, confirming that these residues are critical for allosteric inhibitor action (Fig. S3F). Structure–activity relationship studies demonstrate that the high affinity of RLY-2608 is achieved through optimal shape and interaction complementarity within the cryptic allosteric pocket. Replacing the terminal [1,2,4]triazolo[1,5-a]pyridine-5-carbonitrile with a smaller methylamide significantly weakens van der Waals packing and enhances inhibitor dynamics, substantially reducing the potency. An approximate 20-fold loss of efficacy was observed by replacing the central aminoisoxindole with a bisaminopyridine, which reorganizes the core scaffold and disrupts essential π-stacking and polar networks critical for high-affinity binding18 (Fig. S3C).

Comparative structural analysis reveals that RLY-2608 binds E545K and E542K in a manner similar to that of H1047R, with key structural elements such as the catalytic loop, kα9 and kα10 well-aligned (Fig. 2F and G). However, mutation-specific conformational differences are evident in the activation loop, involving altered side-chain orientations of residues such as D939 and E950, as well as displacements of H940 and R951 (Fig. S3D). Notably, the cation–π stacking interaction between RLY-2608 and R949 is less pronounced in E545K than in H1047R, as the electron density of R949 in E545K is relatively poor, precluding reliable assessment of its side-chain rotamer. Other essential stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions are conserved between the two. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations confirm the stability of this conserved binding mode, demonstrating that RLY-2608 remains steadily bound within the allosteric pocket across three PI3Kα mutants (H1047R, E542K and E545K) throughout the simulations, with its pose closely resembling the corresponding cryo-EM structures (Supporting Information Figs. S5 and S6).

In the RLY-2608-bound WT PI3Kα (PDB ID: 8TSD), the N-terminal segment of the activation loop (residues 933–941) adopts a helical conformation rather than the outward-oriented feature seen in H1047R (Fig. S3E). This structural difference results in a more confined binding site that forces the 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl) benzene moiety of RLY-2608 to a flipped orientation closer to kα9. In addition, the side-chain of K942 shifts downward in the WT structure, displacing its charged nitrogen atom by approximately 3.8 Å away from its position in the H1047R (Fig. S3E). As a result, the stacking interaction between K942 (WT) and RLY-2608 is completely abolished, and the bottom of the binding pocket becomes loosened. Furthermore, the C-terminal region of the activation loop (residues 949–951) is largely unstructured in WT and the central moiety of RLY-2608 is displaced outward by about 1.0 Å. Collectively, the selectivity of RLY-2608 for mutant over WT is mediated by substrate recognition, in which residues K942 and R949 play critical roles38.

Mutant-specific inhibition by RLY-2608

Although binding of RLY-2608 to H1047R induces only minor conformational changes compared to BYL-719 (PDB ID: 8GUB), with an overall Cα RMSD of 0.82 Å (Fig. 3A), significant local structural rearrangements occur in regions critical for allosteric intervention by RLY-2608. The affected residues reside in the allosteric site, the activation loop, the phosphate-binding loop (P-loop), and several membrane-binding loops within p110α, as well as the N- and C-terminal regions of the iSH2 domain in p85α (Fig. 3A–E).

A key feature of allosteric binding by RLY-2608 is remodeling the activation loop, which folds inward to form a cryptic pocket accommodating the inhibitor. Specifically, the well-resolved C-terminal segment (residues 943–949) in the 8GUB structure becomes disordered upon RLY-2608 binding, while the adjacent region (residues 952–955) undergoes conformational shifts, with Cα displacements of 1.7 Å at V952, 1.0 Å at P953, and 1.5 Å at V955 (Fig. 3B). The catalytic DRH motif (residues 915–917), essential for kinase activity39, undergoes substantial structural rearrangement, with R916 exhibiting a pronounced 5.8 Å Cα displacement (Fig. 3B). These structural changes extend to functional regions involved in ATP and membrane binding40. The P-loop (residues 771–777) shifts away from the catalytic site upon RLY-2608 binding, leading to a 4.1 Å Cα displacement of A775, suggesting that RLY-2608, as opposed to BYL-719, differentially regulates coordination of lipid substrates and ATP, potentially affecting the phosphotransfer reaction between PIP2 and ATP39,41 (Fig. 3B). Similarly, all three membrane-binding loops exhibit conformational adjustments: membrane-binding loop 1 (residues 721–727) shifts away from the active site (1.9 Å at D725), while loops 2 (residues 863–873) and 3 (residues 966–974) display Cα displacements of 3.0 Å at F872 and 2.8 Å at T972, respectively (Fig. 3C). Similar conformational differences were observed when comparing unliganded (PDB ID: 7MYN) and BYL-719-bound WT complexes (PDB ID: 7MYO) (Supporting Information Fig. S4A–S4C).

RLY-2608 binding also triggers significant reorganization of the p110α–p85α interface in H1047R. Compared to the 8GUB structure, RLY-2608 binding enhances interactions between the ABD of p110α and p85α and increases contacts between the nSH2 of p85α and p110α, thereby stabilizing the autoinhibited state and preventing nSH2 release. In contrast, interactions between iSH2 and p110α are weakened, resulting from allosteric decoupling of iSH2 from C2 and kinase domains of p110α (Fig. 3E)42. RLY-2608 disrupts allosteric modulation that propagates signals from the C2 domain to iSH2, reducing iSH2 dynamics and its regulatory role in p110α autoinhibition43. Collectively, these rearrangements reflect an allosteric mechanism through which RLY-2608 suppresses H1047R activity.

The conformational changes induced by RLY-2608 remain conserved between E545K and E542K. Global structural alignment with their respective BYL-719-bound counterparts (PDB IDs: 8GUD and 8GUA) reveals close similarity, with Cα RMSD values of 1.0 Å for E545K and 1.2 Å for E542K, respectively (Fig. S4D). Notably, these structural models also lack both p85α and ABD domains. Beyond the expected changes in the allosteric pocket and activation loop, substantial conformational shifts occur in key functional elements, including the DRH motif, P-loop, and membrane binding loops (Fig. S4E, S4F, and S4H). Besides, in the RLY-2608-bound E542K structure, helices surrounding the allosteric pocket, such as kα2 (residues 807–827), kα8 (residues 974–995), and kα11 (residues 1032–1047), are displaced relative to the 8GUA structure (Fig. S4H). Although weak electron density was observed for portions of the ABD and iSH2 domains at higher contour levels, it remains insufficient for accurate modeling (Fig. S4G). The preservation of these conformational changes across H1047R, E545K and E542K underscores a common inhibitory mechanism, demonstrating that RLY-2608 exerts its effects not only by stabilizing the cryptic pocket but also by propagating structural changes that perturb the catalytic core and strengthen autoinhibition of p85α on p110α.

Comparative analysis of RLY-2608-bound PI3Kα mutant structures uncovers a highly conserved global architecture, with Cα RMSD values of 0.6 Å (H1047R vs. E545K) and 0.8 Å (H1047R vs. E542K) (Fig. 3F). Notable conformational differences are primarily localized to the activation loop: the side-chain of F937 is displaced by 1.1 Å (Fig. 3G), and D939 adopts distinct rotamer conformations in different mutant contexts. In H1047R, its side-chain orients outward, forming a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl of E1012 on kα10, whereas in the E545K mutant, D939 rotates inward, disrupting this interaction and forming a new hydrogen bond with the backbone amide of H940. Substantial structural divergence is also evident in the P-loop, where the side-chain of A775 adopts distinctly different orientations between mutants, separated by a Cα distance of 4.5 Å (Fig. 3G). Beyond the kinase domain, structural variations extend to the C-lobe, where loops 411–417 and 345–353 are well-defined in H1047R but poorly resolved in E545K, suggesting differences in local flexibility that may correlate with altered p85α regulatory interactions (Fig. 3G). Thus, although RLY-2608 maintains a conserved allosteric binding mode, it elicits mutation-dependent local conformational changes to execute its inhibition on oncogenic mutants. MD simulations provide dynamic corroboration of these mutation-specific differences. Structural superposition reveals that, while RLY-2608-bound WT PI3Kα maintains a compact and highly converged ensemble of the activation and catalytic loops, all three oncogenic mutants (H1047R, E542K, E545K) sample broader and more heterogeneous conformational distributions, yet remain largely aligned with the cryo-EM reference structures (Supporting Information Fig. S7A). Quantitative analysis using the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of both loops as collective variables further delineates these differences. The RLY-2608–WT system populates a narrow, low-SASA conformational basin (catalytic loop ∼370 Å2, activation loop ∼1500 Å2), reflecting restricted loop dynamics. The H1047R mutant maintains comparable SASA values for the activation loop but exhibits increased variability in catalytic loop SASA, indicating enhanced local flexibility in this region. Notably, the helical domain mutants (E542K and E545K)—which lack resolved density for the regulatory subunit p85α in the cryo-EM structures and are therefore inherently released from its autoinhibitory constraint—also sample conformational states with notably elevated SASA values, while remaining generally consistent with the cryo-EM reference conformations (Fig. S7B). These results indicate that RLY-2608 stabilizes mutation-associated states, locking the loops into a conserved global conformation that accommodates distinct, mutation-specific dynamic signatures.

Binding of STX-478

STX-478 displays potent anti-proliferative activity against three cancer cell lines, SK-BR-3 (PIK3CAWT), MCF-7 (PIK3CAE545K) and T-47D (PIK3CAH1047R), as determined by IC50 values, with T-47D showing more pronounced inhibitory responses (Fig. 4A). Like RLY-2608, STX-478 also effectively suppressed PI3K signaling, as indicated by a marked reduction in phosphorylated AKT levels at Ser473 (Fig. 4B).

To elucidate the structural basis of inhibition, we determined the high-resolution cryo-EM structure of STX-478-bound H1047R, showing that STX-478 binds to a cryptic allosteric pocket that is highly congruent with that of RLY-2608, sharing 14 common kinase residues within a 5 Å radius (Fig. 4C). However, the chemical topology of STX-478—featuring a linear molecule centred on a urea linker and lacking the 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl)benzene group—results in a distinct interaction profile. The terminal pyrimidine ring of STX-478 is enclosed between activation and catalytic loops (Fig. 4C), with its amine group forming two hydrogen bonds with the side-chains of D915 and E950. The central urea linker acts as a pivotal bridge, filling the gap between these loops and engaging in a polar network with the backbone atoms of G912 (catalytic loop) as well as F937, D939, and H940 (activation loop). Adjacent to the urea, the trifluoromethyl group extends toward kα10, making predominantly van der Waals contacts with Y1021. It follows that the benzofuran moiety penetrates into a hydrophobic cleft framed by kα9, kα10, and the catalytic loop, stabilized by π-stacking interactions with F937 and F1002, and extensive hydrophobic contacts with L938, I1019, and I1022 (Fig. 4D). Single-point mutation of key residues (K942, H940, and F1002) attenuated STX-478-mediated suppression of pAKT (S473). Consistently, in vitro kinase assays using purified PI3Kα proteins revealed that K942E and H940F mutations in the H1047R background completely abolished the dose-dependent inhibition of STX-478, which potently inhibited H1047R single-mutant PI3Kα with an IC50 of 2.2 nmol/L (Supporting Information Fig. S8I). These residues are therefore critically involved in inhibitor binding and signal transduction (Fig. 4E).

The binding pose of STX-478 is displaced outward in the cryo-EM structure relative to its position in the crystal structure of H1047R bound by the orthosteric inhibitor inavolisib (PDB ID: 8TGD) (Fig. 4C). The most pronounced conformational change involves the benzofuran moiety, which inserts into a subpocket formed by kα2 and kα9 in the crystal structure but withdraws toward kα9 and kα10 in that of cryo-EM. This movement is quantified by an increase in the minimum distance between the fluorine atom of the benzofuran ring and the Cα atom of Q809 (on kα2) from 3.6 Å in the X-ray structure to 9.3 Å in that of cryo-EM (Supporting Information Figs. S8 and S9). This binding pose difference primarily stems from distinct activation loop conformations, possibly induced by inavolisib occupancy in the crystal structure and methodological differences between cryo-EM and X-ray crystallography.

A comparative analysis of STX-478 binding among H1047R, E542K, and E545K shows both conserved and divergent interaction patterns (Fig. 4F). The benzofuran moiety of STX-478 occupies a fully buried allosteric pocket in all three mutants, involving conserved interactions like hydrogen bonds with G912, stacking interactions with F1002 and Y1021, and hydrophobic contacts with L911, F937 and L938 (Fig. 4F and G). However, the orientation of the outer pyrimidine ring, which is clasped by the activation and catalytic loops, differs significantly among the mutants. A key divergence concerns the side-chain orientation of D915. In H1047R, D915 rotates toward the positively charged amine group of the STX-478 pyrimidine ring and K941, forming a strong salt bridge network that stabilizes the inhibitor. In contrast, an outward rotation of D915 eliminates this interaction in both E542K and E545K (Fig. S8B). High conformational flexibility of the activation loop in these mutants, as evidenced by poor cryo-EM densities, destabilizes STX-478 binding. Consequently, in the E545K structure, the pyrimidine amine engages only in weaker, alternative interactions with R951 and the catalytic loop backbone, resulting in a more dynamic binding mode for STX-478 (Fig. S8C). These structural observations demonstrate that the binding pose of STX-478 diverges between H1047R and E542K/E545K, with mutant-specific interactions occurring at the activation loop. This conclusion is consistent with SPR analysis, showing that STX-478 binds to H1047R with a much lower KD (indicating higher affinity) compared to the WT or E545K mutant20. MD simulations align with these structural insights (Supporting Information Fig. S10). The binding pose of STX-478 remains generally stable in WT, E542K, and E545K PI3Kα throughout the simulations, albeit with slight orientational adjustments of the benzofuran moiety in the mutants.

Mutant-specific inhibition by STX-478

To understand the molecular basis of the superior inhibition of H1047R over WT and E542K/E545K by STX-478, comparative analysis of the local and global conformational difference across PI3Kα mutant structures was performed. It shows that while STX-478 engages a conserved allosteric pocket, its binding elicits mutant-specific conformational changes beyond the immediate binding site.

The overall structure of STX-478-bound H1047R is similar to that bound by BYL-719 (PDB ID: 8GUB), with a Cα RMSD of 1.3 Å (Fig. 5A). However, several local rearrangements were observed in key functional regions, including the activation loop, P-loop, and membrane-binding loops of p110α as well as notable shifts in p85α, particularly within the iSH2 domain (Fig. 5B–E). First, STX-478 binding directly remodels the conformation of the activation loop. The N-terminal segment (residues 933–945), which forms a helix in the BYL-719-bound structure, undergoes a substantial outward displacement (Cα shifts of 5.3 Å for D939 and 8.2 Å for K944) to adopt an extended loop conformation (Fig. 5B). This rearrangement opens the allosteric pocket while simultaneously altering the adjacent DRH motif (residues 915–917). Second, the DRH motif in the catalytic loop of H1047R retains a conformation similar to that in the BYL-719-bound structure upon STX-478 binding. In contrast, RLY-2608 induces a major rearrangement, including a mirror-image flip of R916 and a 5.7 Å Cα displacement of D915 (Fig. 5C). It appears that each allosteric inhibitor uniquely reshapes the activation loop, forging distinct interactions with the catalytic loop, a conformational pattern previously observed in the unliganded (PDB ID: 7MYN) and BYL-719-bound PI3Kα (PDB ID: 7MYO) (Fig. S8D–S8G). Third, STX-478 binding induces concerted shifts in kα8, kα11 (Fig. 5B), P-loop, and membrane-binding loops (Fig. 5B and D). Furthermore, substantial conformational changes in p85α upon STX-478 binding significantly strengthen the polar interaction network at p110α–p85α interface via enhancing contacts between ABD–iSH2, C2–iSH2, helical–nSH2 and kinase–nSH2 domains (Fig. 5E). For instance, the side-chain oxygen of E601, located in the C-terminal tail of iSH2, forms salt bridges with the positively charged nitrogen atoms of K941 and K942 in the kinase domain of p110α—key interactions absent in both unliganded and BYL-719-bound H1047R structures (Fig. 5E). Notably, the helical architecture of iSH2 near the ABD undergoes partial distortion, driven by the side-chain reorientation of E520, which shifts by 5.0 Å at the Cα position. This rearrangement disrupts the original salt bridge between E520 and R514, enabling the formation of a new salt bridge with K513 and conferring enhanced dynamic flexibility to this region (Fig. S8H). Collectively, these observations suggest that STX-478 employs a dual inhibitory mechanism against H1047R: it not only remodels the conformation of catalytically critical regions, such as the activation loop and membrane-binding sites, via allosteric pocket occupancy, but also reinforces the autoinhibitory interface between p110α and p85α subunits.

Structural comparison of STX-478-bound E545K and E542K with that bound by BYL-719 (PDB IDs: 8GUD and 8GUA) reveals conserved global folds, with Cα RMSD values of 1.7 Å and 1.3 Å, respectively (Fig. S9A and S9C), indicating that local conformational adjustments occur within a conserved framework. Notably, continuous electron density between iSH2 and ABD domains is more evident in the STX-478-bound than BYL-719-bound structures, where the ABD density is only weakly visible (Fig. S9B and S9F). This suggests that STX-478 binding helps stabilize the iSH2–ABD interface. However, the autoinhibitory contribution from p85α is not fully restored to the level observed in the wild-type, and the local resolution in these regions remains insufficient for detailed modeling. Regarding local conformational changes, STX-478 binding induces significant structural rearrangements in the E545K mutant compared to the BYL-719-bound form, including a 6.2 Å Cα shift at D939 in the activation loop and ordering of a previously disordered C-terminal segment (Fig. S9C). Repositioning of the P-loop and membrane-binding loop is also observed (Fig. S9D), along with increased flexibility in the RBD, in both E542K and E545K mutants. The Rα2 loop (residues 270–280) undergoes substantial reorganization, with L279 exhibiting a 3.1 Å Cα displacement (Fig. S9C). Furthermore, STX-478 binding in E542K triggers rearrangements of kα2, kα8, kα9 and kα11 helices (Fig. S9G). Together, these findings support a model in which STX-478 adopts a conserved mechanism by remodeling key functional regions while partially reinforcing inhibitory contacts at the p110α–p85α interface.

Comparative analysis of STX-478 bound by H1047R, E545K, and E542K demonstrates a conserved global architecture, with pairwise Cα RMSD values of 1.2 Å (H1047R vs. E545K) and 0.9 Å (H1047R vs. E542K) (Fig. 5F). Despite this structural conservation, each mutant exhibits distinct local variations that correlate with the differential inhibitory potency of STX-478. A key determinant of this mutant-selective profile is the divergent conformational response of the activation loop. Specifically, in H1047R, a unique salt bridge between D915 and K941 stabilizes STX-478 binding. In contrast, the activation loop of E545K displays an enhanced structural disorder, with H936 rotating outward by 2.5 Å compared to its position in H1047R (Fig. 5G). This conformational divergence is further evidenced by the inherent flexibility of the central activation loop segment and a 4.6 Å Cα displacement at V952 in both E545K and E542K, highlighting mutation-dependent plasticity of this region. Such mutation-specific instabilities extend to other functional regions. In E545K, the P-loop shows a 3.0 Å Cα shift at S773, while the RBD undergoes substantial rearrangement (Cα RMSD of 2.5 Å relative to H1047R) (Fig. 5G). MD simulations provide additional dynamic support for these mutant-specific conformational responses (Supporting Information Figs. S10 and S11). In WT, E542K, and E545K, STX-478 maintains stable binding throughout the simulations, with key residues such as F937 consistently contributing to inhibitor engagement (Fig. S10). As observed for RLY-2608, the helical domain mutants (E542K and E545K) exhibit elevated activation loop SASA values compared to WT—reflecting the absence of resolved p85α density and the consequent release from autoinhibitory constraints—yet their overall conformational ensembles remain well defined and stably bound (Fig. S11). In contrast, STX-478 binding to H1047R exhibits more pronounced dynamic variation, coupled with a marked increase in catalytic loop flexibility (SASA ∼540 Å2 vs. ∼390 Å2 in WT). Notably, however, the activation loop in H1047R remains relatively constrained, with SASA values comparable to those in WT and markedly lower than those observed in the E542K and E545K, indicating a compensatory stabilization. This trade-off—whereby the highly dynamic catalytic loop is accommodated while the activation loop is effectively restrained—may underlie the paradoxical observation that STX-478 achieves its most potent inhibition in H1047R despite displaying the greatest binding plasticity. Thus, STX-478 exploits mutation-specific conformational landscapes: it restricts loop dynamics in less flexible contexts (WT, E542K, and E545K) but adapts to and harnesses the intrinsic flexibility of H1047R, stabilizing the catalytic core to enforce inhibition. Together, these structural alterations compromise the shape and dynamics of the allosteric pocket, explaining the suboptimal binding mode of STX-478 in E542K and E545K compared to its stabilized configuration in H1047R.

Obviously, this mechanistic picture contrasts sharply with that of RLY-2608. Although both inhibitors bind the same allosteric pocket, RLY-2608 engages a broader set of hydrophobic and stacking interactions that are less dependent on the precise geometry of the activation loop. Its bulkier 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl) benzene moiety and branched architecture enable sustained high-affinity binding across H1047R, E542K, and E545K, even in the context of variable loop geometries. In contrast to the relatively conserved activation loop conformation observed with RLY-2608 across different mutants, STX-478 exhibits a pronounced conformational heterogeneity among the three mutants. This structural divergence manifests in both ordering and spatial orientation of the activation loop, particularly evident in the distinct conformations of key residues including H936, K942, and R949. Collectively, our observations support a model in which the selectivity of STX-478 arises from its high dependence on a well-ordered activation loop—a feature distinctively maintained in H1047R due to its stable interaction with p85α. Conversely, the relatively linear topology and specific polar engagement of STX-478 render it more sensitive to the structural plasticity of E542K and E545K, while the wider interaction profile of RLY-2608 confers a broader mutant coverage at the expense of selectivity.

Discussion

In this study, we present high-resolution cryo-EM structures of two first-in-class allosteric PI3Kα inhibitors, RLY-2608 and STX-478, bound by three hotspot mutants (H1047R, E545K and E542K). Our structural analysis not only identifies a shared allosteric binding site formed through a marked outward displacement of the N-terminal segment in the activation loop, but also reveals how the inhibitors exploit distinct binding dynamics and mutant-specific conformational changes to achieve potent and selective effects.

Our structural analyses show that RLY-2608 and STX-478 exert allostery by modulating key regulatory interfaces: the activation loop housing the PIP2 recognition site39,44, the phosphate-binding loop, and the interdomain communication between the iSH2 and nSH2 domains of p85α45,46 (Fig. 6A). The coordinated conformational changes not only disrupt catalytic competence but also alter membrane proximity and orientation, thereby providing a structural basis of linking allosteric inhibition with membrane-associated signaling47. Despite occupying the same cryptic allosteric site, RLY-2608 and STX-478 employ distinct mechanisms to suppress PI3Kα activity (Fig. 6). RLY-2608 binding induces large-scale conformational rearrangements within the activation loop, facilitating the formation of the allosteric pocket and enabling deep insertion of the inhibitor. This remodeling is coupled with substantial restructuring of other functional regions, including P-loop, catalytic DRH motif, and membrane-binding loops. In contrast, STX-478, constrained by its linear scaffold, is less effective in remodeling the activation loop and instead exerts suppression primarily by reinforcing the autoinhibitory interface between p110α and p85α. Our cryo-EM structures reveal that STX-478 triggers significant conformational changes within p85α, particularly in the iSH2, enhancing the interaction network at the p110α–p85α interface and effectively sequestering the enzyme in an autoinhibited conformation. The improved density for p85α in STX-478-bound complexes further supports a reduction in conformational flexibility and stabilization of the regulatory interface.

(B) Mutations in the kinase domain (H1047R, orange dot) and helical domain (E545K/E542K, cyan dot) activate PI3Kα via distinct mechanisms, leading to conformational rearrangements in major regulatory regions (dashed outlines). Known inhibitors fall into two classes: orthosteric ATP-binding pocket inhibitors, exemplified by the approved drug alpelisib (BYL-719), and allosteric modulators that bind a shallow pocket formed by key regulatory elements (e.g., RLY-2608 and STX-478)18,20. While BYL-719 inhibits WT and mutant almost equally, allosteric inhibitors stabilize mutant-specific conformations, conferring mutant selectivity. Allosteric binding of RLY-2608 or STX-478 induces concerted changes across the activation loop, P-loop, membrane-binding loops and adjacent helices; in the E545K/E542K variants, this is accompanied by partial ordering of previously unresolved electron density for p85α and ABD. Differences in inhibitor geometry help explain their selectivity profiles: RLY-2608, with a relatively rigid and compact three-dimensional scaffold, tolerates the enhanced mobility of the activation loop and the rearranged regulatory surfaces in E545K/E542K, preserving potency against these mutants (H1047R and E545K: >12-fold vs. WT; E542K: >8-fold vs. WT). In contrast, the more linear/extended STX-478 appears more sensitive to conformational variability in the activation-associated regions, likely restricting its potent inhibition to H1047R (>14-fold vs. WT) while producing similar potency against WT, E545K, and E542K. These structural and biophysical distinctions explain how allosteric modulators achieve mutant selectivity—a key advantage over classical ATP-site inhibitors such as BYL-719.

Comparative analysis demonstrates that the allosteric pocket is structurally conserved across H1047R, E545K and E542K, but mutation-specific conformational plasticity determines ligand selectivity (Fig. 6B). Despite mutation-specific variations9, such as distinct conformational landscapes of E545K and E542K that obscure iSH2–nSH2 interaction21, both inhibitors consistently induce structural perturbation in other functional domains. Notably, conserved alterations are observed in the membrane-associated C2 domain43,48,49 and regions within the kinase domain46,50 across different mutants. These findings underscore the ability of allosteric inhibitors to exert broad structural effects beyond immediate binding sites, highlighting their potential to achieve unified inhibition in spite of mutational heterogeneity. The STX-478-bound E545K, for instance, displays altered side-chain rotamers of key residues like H936 and increased flexibility in the central activation loop51, creating a more dynamic pocket that may modulate inhibitor affinity and binding kinetics. These structural adaptations indicate that oncogenic mutations not only enhance PI3Kα signaling but also reshape conformational changes, generating mutation-specific microenvironments that influence ligand efficacy. The ability of RLY-2608 and STX-478 to maintain potency across mutants while sparing the WT enzyme stems from their capability of interacting with a conformational state uniquely accessible in the activated mutants.

Beyond providing these detailed mechanistic insights, our structural findings establish a rational framework with direct translational implications for the development of next-generation PI3Kα-targeted therapeutics.

First, the structurally conserved allosteric pocket shared by both inhibitors across the three hotspot mutants (H1047R, E542K, and E545K) defines a versatile platform for mutation-selective PI3Kα inhibition. This conservation strongly suggests that the pocket itself represents a fundamental, mutation-tolerant architectural feature of activated PI3Kα, rather than a mutant-specific artifact. Given that these three mutants represent the two major classes of oncogenic PI3Kα activation mechanisms—helical domain and kinase domain mutations—it is reasonable to propose that this pocket remains targetable across a wide spectrum of additional cancer-associated mutations52. Indeed, STX-478 has been reported to potently and selectively inhibit multiple PI3Kα variants found in cancer, including H1047R/L/Y/A, M1043I/L/Y/A, and G1049R, with over ten-fold selectivity relative to WT PI3Kα20. The pocket remains structurally intact across high-frequency hotspot mutations, while its mutation-specific conformational plasticity provides a structural basis for precision drug design. The distinct chemical scaffolds of RLY-2608 and STX-478, now visualized at atomic resolution, offer complementary and optimized starting points for structure-based campaigns aimed at developing broad-spectrum mutant-selective inhibitors.

Second, the spatial and functional independence of the allosteric pocket from the orthosteric ATP-binding site provides a mechanistic solution to acquired resistance observed with orthosteric PI3Kα inhibitors such as alpelisib and inavolisib. Our structural comparisons demonstrate that the allosteric pocket remains accessible in the context of drug-resistant secondary mutations within the ATP pocket (e.g., W780R, Q859R), as the activation loop rearrangements required for pocket formation remain unperturbed. This supports the rationale for developing allosteric inhibitors as second-line therapies, either as monotherapy for patients resistant to orthosteric inhibitors or in combination with orthosteric agents to prevent resistance emergence. Notably, recent studies have identified emergent secondary resistance mutations in PIK3CA that alter the orthosteric inhibitor binding pocket11. Although some of these mutations exert differential effects on PI3Kα-selective versus pan-PI3K inhibitors, resistance induced by all such mutations could be overcome by RLY-2608. Our structural studies reveal that the allosteric pocket remains accessible despite these resistance-conferring mutations, providing a direct mechanistic rationale for these observations.

Third, our work reveals the central mechanistic role of the activation loop and catalytic loop as the core allosteric transmission apparatus. These loops are not merely passive structural elements; they are the dynamic linchpins that directly form the inhibitor-binding pocket and transduce the binding signal to the catalytic site and membrane-interacting regions. The specific conformational states of these loops, which differ between mutants and are differentially stabilized by various inhibitors, can serve as highly informative molecular fingerprints for future drug discovery. This establishes a novel structure-based paradigm: instead of screening for direct binding, virtual and experimental screens can be designed to identify small molecules that selectively stabilize mutant-specific, inactive conformations of these key regulatory loops. From this paradigm, two concrete and feasible design strategies emerge directly from our structural comparisons: (1) the region clasped by the N-terminal segment of the activation loop and kα10 accommodates the 3-fluoro-5-(trifluoromethyl)benzene moiety of RLY-2608, creating a subpocket that remains unexplored in the STX-478 scaffold; (2) both inhibitors possess solvent-exposed regions amenable to strategic modifications, providing avenues for optimizing pharmacokinetic properties without disrupting core interactions with the allosteric pocket. These insights are poised to guide targeted drug discovery against a wide array of PI3Kα mutations and drug-induced mutations, ultimately aiming to deliver more durable and personalized therapies for cancer patients.

The development of RLY-2608 and STX-478 represents a transformative advancement in treating hotspot mutation-driven cancers. By targeting a cryptic allosteric pocket, these inhibitors achieve exceptional mutant selectivity with the potential of reducing dose-limiting toxicities associated with WT PI3Kα inhibition38,53, 54, 55. Our work thus provides a mechanistic framework for understanding allosteric inhibition of PI3Kα and validates a new direction of targeting oncogenic mutants with precision.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides detailed structural insights into the allosteric inhibition mechanism of PI3Kα hotspot mutations. These findings not only advance our understanding of PI3Kα allostery but also lay the foundation for selectively targeting these oncogenic mutants.
