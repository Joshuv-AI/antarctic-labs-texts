Long-term impact of scleral contact lens use on endothelial cell density in patients with moderate to advanced keratoconus
Authors: Cezmi Doğan, Oğuzhan Kılıçarslan, Fatma Burcu Özdemir, Doğukan Atakan, Burak Mergen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe investigated the effect of long-term use of scleral contact lens (SL) on corneal endothelial parameters in patients with keratoconus (KC).MethodsSpecular microscopy (Konan Cell Check SL, Hyogo, Japan) imaging was performed on healthy subjects (control group) and patients with KC (KC group) who were long-term SL wearers using the center method. Specular microscopy measurements were taken for both the groups before and after SL use.ResultsThe mean duration of SL use in the KC group was 5 ± 2 years. No significant differences in specular microscopy parameters were noted between the first and last examinations of the control and KC groups.ConclusionCorneal endothelial parameters were not affected in long-term SL wearers when the recommended fitting and usage practices were followed, suggesting that SL use is well tolerated by the corneal endothelium. However, further research involving larger cohorts is essential to confirm the long-term safety of SL use.Keywords: Scleral contact lens, Keratoconus, Corneal endothelium, Specular microscopy, Endothelial cell density

Introduction

Keratoconus (KC) is the most common corneal ectatic disorder, particularly among teenagers and young adults [1]. Irregular corneal astigmatism with paracentral corneal thinning is a major manifestation of this disease. Progressive corneal thinning and astigmatism generally begin at an early age and continue to worsen over time. While corneal cross-linking is used to prevent progression, correction of refractive errors remains necessary [2]. Contact lenses are an indispensable component of the visual rehabilitation of patients with KC. Rigid gas permeable contact lenses (RGP) are the classic first-line choice for correcting irregular astigmatism. However, scleral contact lenses (SLs) are used in cases of RGP intolerance and primary applications [3].

SLs have been used in recent decades as an alternative to contact lens options for visual rehabilitation and therapeutic management. The extensive indications of SLs can be attributed primarily to their large structure, which extends over the entire corneal surface, limbus, and parts of the bulbar conjunctiva. Extended corneal coverage may provide enhanced ocular surface protection. SLs have been effectively used in the management of ocular surface diseases, such as dry eye, exposure keratopathy, cicatrizing conjunctivitis, neurotrophic keratopathy, and Stevens–Johnson syndrome [4]. Concurrently, SLs can be used to improve visual status, especially in patients with irregular corneal astigmatism. KC and other corneal ectasias, as well as post-LASIK ectasia and post-penetrating keratoplasty, are major indications for SL use [5]. However, SLs have some adverse effects. Difficulty when applying SLs, halos, haziness, discomfort, and pain have been reported [6]. Complications due to SL use include corneal abrasions, corneal edema, elevated intraocular pressure, corneal infiltrates, and microbial keratitis [7]. Corneal hypoxia is the primary cause of corneal endothelial dysfunction, clinical or subclinical corneal edema, and vascularization.

Patients with moderate to advanced KC or KC with more peripheral cones are good candidates for SL fitting. Visual rehabilitation can be initiated directly with SLs. If the lenses fit well, they can be used for a long time. This study aimed to investigate whether long-term SL use causes significant changes in corneal endothelial parameters in patients with KC by comparing measurements before and after SL use and to evaluate changes of corneal endothelium parameters in healthy control group in the same time period.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

The present study included patients with KC who had been using SL for more than three years (range: 5 ± 2 years) and were followed both in the Cornea and Contact Lens Department of İstanbul University Cerrahpasa-Cerrahpasa Medical Faculty and healthy controls. Specular microscopy imaging of patients in the KC group before SL use was obtained. The healthy controls and the patients with KC were evaluated over a similar follow-up period, and a within-group comparison of the results of the first and last examinations was conducted. Patients with a steepest corneal curvature greater than 48.5 D and without corneal scarring were included; therefore, the study population consisted only of patients with moderate or advanced KC, based on Lu et al. [8]. Patients in the control group did not use any type of contact lens. The KC group consisted of patients who experienced RGP intolerance or demonstrated inadequate visual improvement with RGP use. Although RGPs were initially tried, they were not prescribed to any of the KC patients because of intolerance or inadequate fitting during the examination.

KC was suspected in patients with reduced spectacle-corrected visual acuity, high myopia and astigmatism (mostly asymmetric), and scissoring of the red reflex on retinoscopy. Slit lamp biomicroscopy was used to detect typical signs of KC, and corneal tomography was performed using a rotating Scheimpflug imaging system (Pentacam HR, Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). KC was diagnosed based on the Rabinowitz criteria—K value greater than 47.2 D, inferior-superior corneal diopter asymmetry exceeding 1.2 D, skew of radial axis more than 21°, and SimK astigmatism over 1.5 D. Exclusion criteria included patients with forme fruste or subclinical KC, KC with central stromal scars due to hydrops sequelae, or KC with intrastromal corneal ring segments, and ocular diseases that could affect specular microscopy results, such as uveitis, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, and herpes keratitis, or systemic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus, that could affect specular microscopy results, patients who had undergone any intraocular surgery, patients with any history of ocular trauma or nystagmus, and patients who exhibited a lack of cooperation for specular microscopy. One eye of each patient and healthy control was evaluated in this study. All patients in the KC group demonstrated asymmetrical involvement, and the more severely affected eye treated with SL was evaluated. The other eye had milder KC, and SL was not applied. The right eye of each healthy control was assessed.

Mini Misa SLs (Microlens Contact Lens Technology, Arnhem, The Netherlands) were fitted in patients in the KC group. The lenses were available in base curves of 7.80, 8.30, and 8.80 mm. The diameter of the lens was 16.50–17 mm. The sagittal vault was 325–400 μm. The contact lens material was fluorosilicone acrylate with hydrophilic monomers (Dk: 122). The trial selection of SL was initially based on keratometry values. Trial lenses were applied to the patient’s eye with a reservoir fluid of unpreserved saline solution and sodium fluorescein. Optimum central fit was defined as the absence of contact with the cornea under cobalt blue light and a central corneal vault of approximately 200 μm. Central fit was adjusted by trialing steeper or flatter SL base curves. Peripheral fit was evaluated after adjusting for central fit. The ideal peripheral fit was defined as no compression or blanching of the conjunctival vessels or any area other than the limbal area. Patients who experienced irritation and conjunctival hyperemia after SL trials were reevaluated for better fitting. Assessment with a slit-lamp examination was performed by a single experienced contact lens practitioner (C.D.) after 30 min of wearing the SL.

An unpreserved saline solution was used as the reservoir fluid. The reservoir fluid was replenished every 4–6 h. Patient compliance was carefully questioned in every examination. The duration of SL use per day was almost 12–14 h for all patients. The same type of contact lens care system was used overnight for each patient. None of the patients wore their lenses overnight. The care regimen for scleral lenses included daily and weekly cleaning protocols. For daily care, the lenses were removed every evening and cleaned for approximately 20 s by rubbing them using hands to remove accumulated debris and deposits on the surface. Subsequently, they were cleaned with an RGP care solution (Wöhlk Aufbewahrung, Wöhlk GmbH, Germany). After cleaning, the lenses were left in this solution overnight to ensure disinfection. Before application in the morning, the lenses were thoroughly rinsed with preservative-free saline solution and then filled with preservative-free saline before being applied to the eye. In the weekly cleaning protocol, a surface-active cleaner specifically designed for RGPs (Wöhlk Reinigung, Wöhlk GmbH, Germany) was used to effectively remove lipid and protein deposits. The front and back surfaces of the lens were cleaned by manual rubbing. After cleaning, the lenses were rinsed with an RGP care solution (Wöhlk Aufbewahrung, Wöhlk GmbH, Germany) and stored in this solution until their next use.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Ethical approval was obtained from the Istanbul University Cerrahpasa-Cerrahpasa Medical Faculty Clinical Research Ethics Committee.

Data collection and image analysis

Before specular microscopy analysis, all patients underwent a detailed ophthalmic examination, including slit lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination, and noncontact tonometry. Specular microscopy (Konan Cell Check SL, Hyogo, Japan) was performed on all patients without any ocular medication. The center method was used for all measurements.

Three consecutive images were acquired. The best-quality images with clear cell borders were selected for each patient. Endothelial cell density (ECD), coefficient of variability (CV), hexagonal cell percentage (HEX), and central corneal thickness (CCT) were evaluated. More than 100 cells with clear borders were manually selected. Automatic software was used to calculate ECD, CV, HEX, and CCT. Images with motion artifacts, uncertain cell borders, and a low number of contiguous cells were excluded from the study. Measurements were taken at the same time of day for all patients. Specular microscopy was performed 1 h after removing SL.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included mean and standard deviation (SD) values. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and frequencies. The data were tested for normality. The results indicated that all data were normally distributed (p > 0.05). Within-group comparisons between the first and last examinations were performed using paired sample t-tests for both the KC and control groups. The analyses were conducted for four dependent variables: ECD, CV, HEX, and CCT. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS version 22.0 was used for the statistical analyses.

Results

In this study, 25 eyes of 25 patients diagnosed with KC who had been using SLs for more than three years and 35 eyes of 35 healthy individuals serving as the control group were included. The mean age of the KC group was 41.45 years; 10 patients (40%) were male, and 15 patients (60%) were female. In the control group, the mean age was 45.07 years; 14 patients (40%) were male, and 21 patients (60%) were female. The mean duration of SL use in the KC group and the mean follow-up time of the control group were 5 ± 2 years. None of the specular microscopy parameters differed between the first and last examinations in the KC group. Discussion

Endothelial cell abnormalities are considered a major limiting factor of SL use [9]. Corneal endothelial cell–related complications can sometimes emerge due to long-term SL use; however, there is a lack of data in the literature regarding corneal endothelial cell alterations after long-term SL use in patients with KC. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated potential endothelial cell alterations in patients with KC who were long-term SL wearers. The results showed no significant differences in terms of corneal endothelial parameters before and after long-term SL use in patients with KC. The same measurements were performed for healthy individuals during a similar time period to determine the natural course of the change in specular microscopy parameters. No differences were noted between the results of the first and last examinations of healthy individuals.

Accumulation of lactic acid secondary to anaerobic metabolism due to limited oxygen transmission causes clinically significant or subclinical corneal edema during SL use [10]. Corneal edema is more severe under closed-eye conditions; therefore, overnight use of SLs is generally not recommended. However, typical post-lens tear thicknesses and contemporary SLs have been shown to induce less than 2% corneal edema under open-eye conditions [10]. Different factors have been investigated regarding the induction of corneal edema. Iqbal et al. found similar percentages of corneal edema in central and peripheral corneas. They noted that corneal thickness tended to increase with medium and high reservoir thicknesses [11]. Fenestration was found to be ineffective in treating corneal edema in another study [12]. Corneal edema associated with SLs has been reported to increase with the thickness of the fluid reservoir [13, 14]. SL-induced corneal edema has also been noted after surgical procedures, such as radial keratotomy [15] and penetrating keratoplasty [16]. Increased SL thickness has been found to be associated with increased corneal edema under short-term open-eye lens usage conditions [17]. However, this relationship has not been reported under closed-eye conditions [18]. None of our patients exhibited clinical corneal edema, and all used SLs with similar thicknesses and identical Dk values. The SL employed in this study demonstrated ideal values in terms of both lens thickness and oxygen permeability.

Oxygen transfer to the deeper tissues of the cornea is reduced to varying degrees in contact lens applications. Consequently, issues related to hypoxia may arise with the use of various types of contact lenses. One of the variables that can affect oxygen transmission is the oxygen permeability of the contact lens material, which is expressed as Dk. The main features of the material and design, such as the fenestrated models, can significantly impact oxygen transfer through the SL. Over the years, the materials used for rigid gas-permeable SLs have advanced [19]. Modern high-Dk SLs appear to overcome this issue better than traditional polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) SLs [7]. Hypoxia-related complications are reduced by the use of new-generation SLs [20]. However, the oxygen permeability of contact lenses is not the sole factor contributing to corneal hypoxia. Dhallu et al. showed that SL use causes corneal swelling regardless of the oxygen permeability of the lens material and is primarily dependent on the depth of the fluid reservoir [21].

Another important factor is the post-lens tear thickness. Every type of contact lens creates a post-lens tear reservoir between the front and back surfaces of the lens. The post-lens tear reservoir acts as a storage space for oxygen and other substances necessary for corneal endothelial cells. However, its clearance is lower than that of natural tears under physiologic conditions. SLs create a thicker post-lens fluid reservoir than soft contact lenses and RGPs because of their structural properties and shape [22]. Therefore, oxygen transfer through the contact lens and reservoir fluid may be a potential problem for SL wearers. Increased thickness of both the contact lens and the post-lens fluid reservoir may increase the risk of hypoxia-related complications in SL wearers. No change was observed in the corneal endothelial parameters in our patients after long-term SL use. An appropriate contact lens material with an ideal thickness and optimum tear reservoir thickness does not induce corneal endothelial alterations with long-term SL use.

Tear exchange is another critical factor in oxygen transfer. Tear exchange refers to the movement of tears beneath the contact lens. This movement contributes to the renewal of the tear film, removal of debris and metabolic waste, and adequate oxygen supply to the cornea [23]. Tear exchange is more active and faster in smaller corneal rigid lenses, while this process is considerably slower in SLs. This is because the fluid reservoir is sealed as the lens contacts the sclera, thereby restricting tear flow [24]. Although SLs are made from highly oxygen-permeable materials, the static tear reservoir may restrict oxygen transmission, and insufficient tear exchange can lead to corneal edema. Recent advances have allowed the quantification of tear exchange using novel imaging techniques. Corneoscleral profilometry combined with fluorescein dynamics has shown that SLs have a considerably slower tear turnover rate than corneal rigid lenses, with the half-life of fluorescent decay being approximately four times longer under SLs [24]. Additionally, reduced tear exchange and decreased clearance of inflammatory cytokines beneath the lens can lead to subclinical inflammation. Ballet et al. observed no significant changes in tear inflammatory markers during the short-term use of well-fitted SLs [25], indicating that the proper fitting of SLs and adherence to lens care are critically important factors. In our study, the lens reservoir fluid was renewed every 4–6 h, and compliance with lens care guidelines may have prevented endothelial involvement.

There are only a few studies in the current literature on ECD alterations due to SL use. Most studies used anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) or other pachymetry methods to measure corneal edema rather than ECD, whereas studies that examined endothelial density used different devices or algorithms. One such study used in vivo confocal microscopy to evaluate the corneal endothelium [26]. Alipour et al. analyzed confocal microscopy data collected during the initial and 6-month visits of patients using soft toric lenses, RGPs, and SLs [26]. The SL used in this study was the MSD Select made of Hexafocon A (Dk 120), with a diameter of 15.8–18 mm. The authors did not report any significant reduction in ECD in the SL group after 6 months of SL use. However, the SL group was quite heterogeneous in this study, including patients with post-traumatic, post-keratoplasty, and KC. They noted that SLs were tried by patients who were unfit for RGPs, similar to the present study. Another study did not report any significant changes in terms of endothelial morphology or pachymetry in patients with KC who used SLs. In contrast to the previous study, specular microscopy was used for corneal endothelial assessment [27]. They reported that the daily use of SLs by patients with KC showed no adverse effects on the corneal endothelium over a 90-day period [27]. However, the duration of SL use considered in this study was relatively short. Both studies reported no endothelial effect in the short term, consistent with our findings that confirmed the safety of long-term SL use. CCT was also unaffected by long-term SL use in our results. Another study reported that CCT remained unchanged after one year of SL use [28]. Different causes of an irregular cornea were present in this study (not only in patients with KC), and anterior segment OCT was used to assess the corneal thickness. Other studies in the literature on SL use and corneal endothelium have been conducted in different clinical groups or for different contact lenses. The duration of SL use in these studies was shorter than in our study. However, most studies have noted that corneal endothelial parameters were not disrupted due to SL use, similar to our results.

The present study has some major limitations. The sample size was relatively small, making it difficult to draw a definitive conclusion about the effect of SL use on the corneal endothelium. The outcomes included only one type of SL. A comparison of the effects of different types of SLs on the cornea might be more useful. Specular microscopy data were not collected during the intermediate visits. This could limit the ability to track changes over time or to identify transient effects.

In conclusion, no statistically significant changes in corneal endothelial parameters were observed over the long term in SL wearers when ideal fitting and usage practices were followed. This suggests that SLs may not negatively impact the corneal endothelium in the long term; however, further research with larger cohorts is warranted to definitively confirm long-term safety.
