Hemi-Commando with aortic homograft and atrioventricular septal defect repair for recurrent triple valve endocarditis

Superior trans-septal approach for redo endocarditis involving the aortomitral curtain.

Central Message

We present a complex case of recurrent, triple valve endocarditis in which we highlight the advantages and optimal technique when utilizing an aortic homograft in a Hemi-Commando operation.

Case

A 42-year-old woman was transferred to our institution with chest pain, recurrent fevers, and hypotension. She had undergone bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement 7 months prior at her local hospital for aortic valve endocarditis. A computed tomography scan revealed a dilated aortic root to 5.2 cm and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe mitral regurgitation, severe tricuspid regurgitation, severe aortic regurgitation with dehiscence and significant movement of the entire prosthetic valve within the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) during each ejection, and significant biventricular dysfunction. While undergoing workup, she decompensated with conversion to complete heart block and significant inotrope requirements. She underwent urgent reoperation (Video 1).

A redo sternotomy was performed, and the patient was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) via central aortic and bicaval cannulation with a retrograde cardioplegia catheter and left ventricle vent placed in the right superior pulmonary vein. After crossclamping and arresting the heart, the aneurysmal aorta was first excised and the coronary buttons mobilized. The aortic root was then inspected, revealing a grossly infected and circumferentially dehisced prosthetic aortic valve that had migrated deep into the LVOT with an associated large pseudoaneurysm. The prosthetic aortic valve was first removed along with an associated central vegetation, revealing a large perforation from the LVOT into the right atrium and right ventricle forming an atrioventricular septal defect. The aortomitral curtain was highly involved, requiring extensive debridement and resection, including a basilar portion of the anterior mitral valve leaflet. The remaining anterior leaflet of the mitral valve was preserved. A superior transseptal incision across the right atrium and into the left atrial dome was performed to afford maximal exposure to all 3 valves and the aortomitral curtain.

The atrioventricular septal defect was repaired first using a pericardial patch and running 4-0 Prolene with reattachment of the tricuspid valve to the patch. Next, posterior mitral annular repair sutures were placed using a #28 Medtronic CG annuloplasty ring. This ring was selected due to its ability to provide anteropostero reduction with its rigid posterior section, along with its soft anterior band which minimizes disruption to the aortomitral junction. Before placing the anterior mitral annular sutures, a #28 cryopreserved homograft was brought onto the field and anastomosed first at the fibrous trigones. Next, the remnant anterior leaflet of the mitral valve on the homograft was anastomosed to the patient's native residual mitral valve anterior leaflet with a running 4-0 Prolene suture. The anterior mitral annuloplasty sutures were then placed on the atrial side of the homograft mitral valve anterior annulus and through the mitral ring's anterior soft band. This resulted in excellent geometric appearance of the reconstructed and repaired mitral valve. The homograft was then anastomosed along the remaining approximately 240° of its circumference, first with a homograft muscle-to-ventricle running layer and second with a homograft fibrous material-to-native aortic annular fibrous material running layer to ensure maximal seal. Next, mitral valve competency was saline pressure tested and found to be excellently repaired. Then, the left atrial tissue portion of the homograft was anastomosed to the dome of the native left atrium to begin the closure of the superior trans-septal incision. The right atriotomy was left open for later tricuspid valve repair. The coronary buttons were reimplanted on the homograft, followed by truncation of the homograft, inspection of the aortic valve leaflets, and completion of the distal aortic anastomosis. A tricuspid valve annuloplasty was then performed using a #28 Edwards MC3 Tricuspid Partial Ring. During tying of the annuloplasty sutures the heart was de-aired and the crossclamp removed. The right atriotomy was then closed. Permanent epicardial pacing leads were placed on the right ventricle given the development of complete heart block preoperatively and extensive debridement around the atrioventricular node. An intra-aortic balloon pump was placed via the right femoral artery. After a period of reperfusion, the patient was weaned from CPB with excellent hemodynamics, baseline biventricular function, and absence of any significant valvular regurgitation or stenosis on transesophageal echocardiogram. The mean gradient across the mitral valve was 4 mm Hg. The patient was decannulated, hemostasis was achieved, and the chest was closed. The total crossclamp and CPB times were 198 minutes and 268 minutes, respectively.

Postoperatively, she made a remarkable recovery. Her intra-aortic balloon pump was removed on postoperative day three and she was weaned off all inotropes by postoperative day seven. A permanent pacemaker generator was implanted on postoperative day nine. She was discharged home with intravenous antibiotics on postoperative day 15. A transthoracic echocardiogram at discharge demonstrated no aortic regurgitation, trace tricuspid regurgitation, trace mitral regurgitation, normal right ventricle function, and left ventricular ejection fraction of 50%. She was seen in the clinic one month after discharge, at which point her symptoms and physical activity tolerance continued to improve.

Discussion

Redo infective endocarditis operations involving the aortomitral curtain are among the most challenging cases for even highly experienced cardiac surgeons.1 Although a variety of techniques exist,2,3 evidence suggests that homografts and conventional prostheses offer equivalent long-term outcomes.4 In this particular case, we demonstrate how to best utilize the advantages of an aortic homograft—specifically using the excess homograft tissue to buttress the neoaortomitral curtain as well as the fundamental weak point of the Commando procedure at the fibrous trigones. In this case, the homograft also allowed for an excellent mitral valve reconstruction that would have otherwise been difficult to achieve. We also demonstrate how a superior transseptal approach can be used to afford the best possible exposure for triple valve endocarditis with an AV septal defect, and again, utilizing a component of the homograft tissue, the left atrial dome, to aid in closure. This case underscores how even the most complex of infective endocarditis cases can be approached safely with proper surgical planning to achieve outstanding results.
