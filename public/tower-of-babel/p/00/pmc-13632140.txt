Potential benefit of mirabilite external dressing combined with abdominal drainage in the early stage of severe acute pancreatitis
Authors: Jing-xian Zhang, Han-shu Jin, Shuang-shuang Lu, Yu-hui Sun, Kuang-jing Wang, Jin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo evaluate the potential benefit of external mirabilite dressing combined with abdominal drainage for treating severe acute pancreatitis (SAP).MethodsThis retrospective study included SAP patients from two centres who were divided into three groups: combined therapy (A, n = 24), mirabilite alone (B, n = 31), and drainage alone (C, n = 25). The outcomes included symptom recovery, laboratory parameters, intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), imaging findings, complications, and costs. Quantile regression adjusted for the Ranson score was performed.ResultsDespite the higher baseline Ranson score in Group A (4.00 vs. 3.00 in B and 4.00 in C; p = 0.026), Group A had a faster time to abdominal pain relief (7.50 vs. 11.00 and 9.00 days; P = 0.016), a faster time to flatus (2.00 vs. 3.00 and 2.00 days; P = 0.042), and faster white blood cell count normalization (3.00 vs. 6.00 and 5.00 days; P = 0.049). The IAP at discharge was lowest in Group A (7.35 vs. 8.46 and 8.82 mmHg, P = 0.032). Exudate area reduction (741.06 vs. 164.03 and 494.20 mm², P = 0.001) and pseudocyst area (323.94 vs. 484.30 and 453.38 mm², P = 0.038) were also both better in Group A. No differences were observed in the incidence of complications or health economic indicators (all p > 0.05).ConclusionIn this retrospective, non-randomized study, combining external mirabilite dressing with abdominal drainage in early-stage SAP patients was associated with short-term clinical benefits without increasing total hospitalization costs. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation.Keywords: Severe acute pancreatitis, Mirabilite dressing, Abdominal drainage, Inflammatory response reduction, Combination therapy

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis is a common inflammatory disorder of the exocrine pancreas, whose aetiology includes gallstones, hypertriglyceridaemia, alcohol use, and other less common factors [1]. Gallstone disease is the most common aetiology. Some studies have suggested that the small, sharp projections of spiculated pigment stones predispose them to migrate and become impacted at the sphincter of Oddi, which can increase pressure in the common bile duct, leading to reflux into the pancreatic duct and subsequent activation of pancreatic enzymes [2]. While most cases are self‑limiting, a subset of patients progress to a severe form.

Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), the most critical form of acute pancreatitis, is characterized by an aggressive disease course, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and frequent progression to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Despite advances in critical care, SAP mortality remains high, ranging from 20% to 40% [3, 4]. Therapeutic strategies that directly modify early disease progression remain a critical clinical need.

A pathophysiological hallmark of SAP is the rapid accumulation of protease- and cytokine-rich ascitic fluid within the peritoneal cavity. This pancreatitis-associated ascitic fluid (PAAF) acts as a potent driver of intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH), gut barrier disruption, and secondary systemic infections [5]. Although peritoneal drainage is routinely employed to evacuate PAAF and reduce intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), its efficacy as a monotherapy is often inconsistent and may be associated with risks such as haemorrhage or incomplete fluid clearance [6]. Conversely, conservative management alone is often insufficient to control rapid systemic inflammation. Thus, neither approach alone is fully satisfactory, and a combined strategy warrants investigation.

Mirabilite (Glauber’s salt, Na₂SO₄·10 H₂O) is a naturally occurring hydrated form of sodium sulfate. It has been used in traditional East Asian medicine for centuries to treat abdominal distension and inflammatory conditions [7]. From a modern biomedical perspective, mirabilite acts as a hyperosmotic and hygroscopic agent. When applied externally, it creates an osmotic gradient that promotes interstitial fluid redistribution. Preliminary clinical observations suggest that mirabilite may also reduce systemic inflammatory markers and lower the IAP in patients with acute pancreatitis [8, 9].

Given the complementary mechanisms of peritoneal drainage and mirabilite, a combined approach could provide potential synergistic benefits. However, few studies have directly compared combined therapy with monotherapy in patients with SAP. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effects of the combined treatment versus either intervention alone.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two medical centres: Changzhou Second Peopleʼs Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and Ma’anshan People’s Hospital. We reviewed the medical records of patients with a primary diagnosis of SAP who were admitted between January 2020 and January 2025. Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of SAP according to the revised Atlanta classification; (2) age between 18 and 70 years; (3) admission within 72 h of symptom onset; and (4) sonographic evidence of intra-abdominal fluid collection (defined as a fluid depth ≥ 1 cm or an estimated volume ≥ 50 mL). Patients were excluded if any of the following conditions were present: (1) preexisting severe chronic diseases affecting other major organ systems, such as heart, lung or kidney failure; (2) known contraindications to abdominal paracentesis, such as severe coagulopathy; (3) surgical intervention for SAP, such as laparoscopic or open surgery, within the first 7 days of admission; or (4) allergy to mirabilite or its components (Fig. 1).

Treatment allocation and interventions

Treatment allocation was nonrandomized and based on the clinical judgement of the attending physician, including imaging findings (extent of peripancreatic fluid collection, presence of infected collections), Ranson score at admission, and patient preference.

Group assignment

Patients were divided into three groups on the basis of the treatment they received:

Group A (combined therapy, n = 24): external mirabilite dressing plus peritoneal drainage

Group A (combined therapy, n = 24): external mirabilite dressing plus peritoneal drainage

Group B (mirabilite alone, n = 31): external mirabilite dressing only

Group B (mirabilite alone, n = 31): external mirabilite dressing only

Group C (drainage alone, n = 25): peritoneal drainage only

Group C (drainage alone, n = 25): peritoneal drainage only

Standard medical treatment

All enrolled patients received standard medical treatment upon admission, including fluid resuscitation, fasting, proton pump inhibitors for acid suppression, octreotide for the inhibition of pancreatic secretion, prophylactic or therapeutic antibiotics as indicated, and comprehensive nutritional support.

External application of mirabilite

Mirabilite (hydrated sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄·10 H₂O; also known as Glauber’s salt) was applied as an external dressing. A cotton bag containing 1000 g of crystalline mirabilite powder (40 cm × 20 cm) was placed over the epigastric and periumbilical area, covering the pancreatic region. The dressing was replaced every 6 h. Treatment continued until the patient achieved any two of the following: (1) resolution of abdominal pain, (2) passage of flatus or stool, and (3) reduction in the IAP to < 12 mmHg.

Peritoneal drainage

Peritoneal drainage was performed when patients met any of the following criteria: (1) suspected infected peripancreatic fluid collection (fever > 38.5 °C, procalcitonin > 2.0 ng/mL, or positive Gram stain on diagnostic aspirate); (2) intra-abdominal hypertension refractory to medical management (IAP > 20 mmHg despite nasogastric decompression and prokinetics); or (3) symptomatic large fluid collection (> 5 cm) causing respiratory compromise or persistent pain.

The procedure was performed under ultrasound guidance using an 8–12 Fr pigtail catheter inserted in the periumbilical region or bilateral flanks connected to a closed drainage bag. Drainage was removed when the output was < 50 mL/day for two consecutive days and when the patient was clinically stable.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the time to abdominal pain relief. All other outcomes were defined as secondary.

Clinical scores

Ranson and APACHE-II scores were both calculated on the basis of vital signs and laboratory test results at admission, with the APACHE-II score additionally assessed at discharge.

Symptom recovery

The time to flatus (days) and duration of abdominal pain (days) were recorded.

Laboratory parameters

The time to significant reduction (to two-thirds of peak values) and time to normalization were recorded for white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP) level, serum calcium level, and serum amylase (AMS) level.

IAP

IAP was measured at admission and at discharge using a bladder catheter technique.

Imaging findings

Computed tomography severity index (CTSI) scores were calculated on the basis of abdominal CT scans performed at admission and discharge. The area of the peripancreatic exudate (mm²) was measured, and the reduction in the exudate area was calculated as the admission value minus the discharge value. The pancreatic pseudocyst area (mm²) was also documented.

Complications

The following complications were recorded: intra-abdominal infection, pulmonary infection, septic shock, single or multiple organ failure, acidosis, pancreatic pseudocysts, pancreatic abscess, and abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS). Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) duration was recorded on the basis of the following established criteria: (1) temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C; (2) heart rate > 90 bpm; (3) respiratory rate > 20 breaths/min or PaCO₂ <4.3 kPa; and (4) WBC > 12 × 10⁹/L or < 4 × 10⁹/L or band neutrophils > 10%.

Health economic indicators

The length of hospital stay (days), length of ICU stay (days), and total hospitalization cost (CNY) were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables are presented as the median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables are presented as n (%). Comparisons among the three groups were performed using the Kruskal‒Wallis test. When the overall test results were significant (p < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction, and the results were analysed using nonparametric tests. Categorical data were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To adjust for baseline confounding (notably the higher Ranson score in Group A), outcomes (time to abdominal pain relief, WBC normalization, IAP at discharge, exudate area reduction, pseudocyst area, SIRS duration) were analysed using quantile regression (median, τ = 0.5) adjusted for the admission Ranson score. Group A served as the reference category. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of SAP patients, compared with either intervention alone, combining external mirabilite dressing with abdominal drainage was associated with faster symptom recovery, lower discharge IAP, and a greater reduction in peripancreatic exudate (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10). However, no significant differences were observed in major complications, hospital stay, ICU stay, or total costs (Tables 11 and 16). These findings suggest that combination therapy may provide short-term benefits, especially for controlling symptoms and reducing inflammation, but its effect on overall outcomes is still unknown and needs confirmation in future studies.

Our findings are largely consistent with those of multiple independent studies investigating combination strategies involving mirabilite. The use of mirabilite (Glauber’s salt, hydrous sodium sulfate) as a topical hyperosmotic agent has been documented in the gastroenterology and surgery literature. Prospective studies have demonstrated that the external application of Glauber’s salt significantly decreases the IAP, improves the APACHE II score, and restores intestinal motility by promoting fluid redistribution [9]. Furthermore, recent surgical evidence has indicated that topical mirabilite directly accelerates the absorption of abdominal fluid collections by creating a strong transdermal osmotic gradient [10, 11]. These biophysical properties strongly support our observation of a significantly greater reduction in peripancreatic exudate in the combined group.

Zeng et al. conducted a randomized trial of 234 paediatric patients who underwent ERCP and reported that external mirabilite application significantly reduced the incidence of post-ERCP pancreatitis and alleviated postprocedural pain. Notably, compared with the blank group, the mirabilite group had significantly lower TNF-α expression and significantly higher IL-10 expression at 24 h post-procedure. Although this study focused on PEP prophylaxis rather than SAP treatment, it provides robust evidence from a large multicentre sample that external mirabilite application effectively downregulates proinflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α) while upregulating anti‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑10) [12]. This mechanistic profile directly supports our observation of accelerated normalization of inflammatory markers (Table 5).

More directly relevant to our study, a prospective study by Li et al. evaluated early ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage in patients with SAP accompanied by acute fluid collection. Compared with the conventional group, the observation group demonstrated a significantly shorter duration for the resolution of abdominal pain and faster improvement in clinical symptoms [13]. Although this study revealed these short-term clinical benefits, it aligns with our findings that surgical interventions in the early phase primarily improve physiological parameters and symptom control without necessarily altering extended clinical endpoints, thus reinforcing the value of combined adjunctive strategies in the early management of SAP.

The observed pattern of improvement is consistent with that of complementary mechanisms, although formal synergy was not tested. As a hyperosmotic agent composed of hydrated sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄·10 H₂O), an external mirabilite dressing reduces tissue oedema by drawing fluid from inflamed tissues, which improves splanchnic microcirculation and downregulates inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 [14, 15]. Wu et al. demonstrated that mirabilite significantly reduced the serum levels of diamine oxidase and D-lactate, markers of intestinal permeability, after 7 days of treatment. The treatment also lowered IAP. These findings suggest that the primary benefit of mirabilite may come from preserving gut barrier function rather than from direct systemic anti-inflammatory effects [16].

Moreover, peritoneal drainage directly removes the PAAF. This fluid is a potent driver of IAH and systemic inflammation. It is rich in cytokines and activated pancreatic enzymes [17–20]. By reducing the IAP, drainage improves organ perfusion and prevents abdominal compartment syndrome [21]. Our finding that drainage alone was sufficient to lower the IAP (Table 7) but not to shorten the duration of SIRS (Table 14) supports this mechanistic distinction: drainage addresses the mechanical consequences of fluid accumulation, whereas mirabilite modulates local tissue inflammation and gut barrier function.

In general, the combination strategy removes the biochemical trigger (PAAF) and relieves IAH via drainage, whereas mirabilite reduces residual tissue oedema and protects gut barrier function. This two-pronged mechanism may explain why the combined treatment group outperformed both monotherapies across multiple short-term outcomes, including exudate reduction and abdominal pain relief (Table 3).

The optimal timing of percutaneous drainage in SAP remains debated. Current guidelines generally recommend deferring intervention until ≥ 4 weeks after onset to allow the encapsulation of necrotic collections, a strategy supported by evidence that delayed drainage is associated with lower rates of adverse events. However, some patients with suspected infected pancreatic necrosis and worsening organ dysfunction despite antibiotic therapy may benefit from early drainage [22, 23]. Our study involved early drainage in patients with substantial ascitic fluid, and our findings suggest that the combination of early drainage with mirabilite may offer clinical benefits in patients.

Beyond the interpretation of individual outcomes, our study has several potential strengths. First, the combined therapy showed consistent benefits across multiple domains, suggesting a potential multidimensional effect. Second, these advantages remained observable after adjustment for baseline severity, supporting the robustness of our findings despite the retrospective design. Third, the integration of external mirabilite with drainage offers a pragmatic strategy that warrants investigation in larger prospective studies.

A notable observation across our study and the literature is the consistent dissociation between improvements in short-term surrogate outcomes and the absence of demonstrable benefits in hard clinical endpoints. In the prospective cohort study by Huang et al., despite significant improvements in multiple short-term clinical indicators, no mortality benefit was demonstrated, as the study did not report differences in mortality between groups [6]. Similarly, a multicentre RCT by Ke et al. involving 120 patients with necrotizing pancreatitis and early persistent organ failure revealed that early catheter drainage for acute necrotic collections did not improve the composite primary outcome of major complications and death compared with standard delayed care. We hypothesize that several factors may explain this difference. First, our study (n = 80) and most existing studies are underpowered to detect differences in relatively rare events such as mortality or major organ failure. Second, the pathophysiology of SAP-related organ failure involves complex, multifactorial mechanisms beyond IAH and local inflammation, including microcirculatory dysfunction, mitochondrial injury, and immune paralysis. Third, the short-term nature of our follow-up may lead to overlooking later differences in complication rates or quality of life outcomes.

Other limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective, nonrandomized study, which may introduce selection bias despite our efforts to adjust for baseline differences using quantile regression. Specifically, we adjusted only for the Ranson score, the only variable with a significant baseline imbalance (P = 0.026). Other baseline variables, including the APACHE-II score (P = 0.841), BMI (P = 0.099), sex (P = 0.080), and aetiology (P = 0.178), did not significantly differ. The baseline exudate area was numerically greater in Group A than in Group B (P = 0.056) and comparable between Group A and Group C (P = 0.372). These non-significant differences may reflect limited statistical power rather than true equivalence. Given our modest sample size (n = 80), including multiple covariates would risk overfitting. Although we adjusted for the Ranson score using quantile regression, residual confounding and confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, although we applied the Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons, the analysis of multiple secondary outcomes carries a potential risk of type I error inflation.

In conclusion, compared with either intervention alone, the combination of external mirabilite dressing and abdominal drainage in early-stage SAP was associated with short-term clinical benefits without increasing total hospitalization costs. However, no significant differences were observed in terms of major complications. Given the retrospective design and modest sample size, these findings are hypothesis-generating and require validation in prospective, multicentre randomized controlled trials.
