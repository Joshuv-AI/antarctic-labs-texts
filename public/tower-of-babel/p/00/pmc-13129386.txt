From 0D to 3D: Carbon-based materials and their electrocatalytic CO2 reduction performance
Authors: Xu Wang, Xiao-jing Liu, Di Wang, Rui-tang Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis review summarizes recent advances in carbon-based materials for the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), focusing on the core innovation of structure-performance relationships in zero-to-three-dimensional carbon architectures and their regulatory mechanisms. Heteroatom doping and defect engineering are identified as key strategies to modulate local electronic environments, optimize intermediate binding, and enhance catalytic activity/selectivity. Reaction pathways for C1 and C2+ products, especially C–C coupling mechanisms, are highlighted. The core challenges lie in insufficient active-site density, inefficient C2+ formation, and scalable application, while future research should prioritize precise multidimensional carbon structural design and reactor-level optimization for practical CO2 electroreduction.Subject areas: Catalysis, Energy application, Materials science

Introduction

Under the difficult and escalating situation of global climate change and greenhouse effects, the concentration of atmospheric CO2 is rising steadily, showing a marked and continuous upward trend.1,2,3 Prior to the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric CO2 concentrations hovered around 280 ppm with little variation. However, since the mid-18th century, extensive exploitation and consumption of fossil fuels by human activities have led to a dramatic and consistent rise in CO2 levels. To date, monitoring data indicate that the atmospheric CO2 concentration has exceeded 410 ppm and continues to climb at an accelerating rate.4 This trend highlights the urgency of developing CO2 reduction and reuse technologies, making carbon management a core challenge for 21st-century sustainable development.5

In the current global energy mix, fossil fuels remain dominant, driving substantial anthropogenic CO2 emissions. To address this, researchers have explored diverse technologies for converting CO2 into high-value products, with electrocatalytic CO2 reduction emerging as a standout after its early 21st-century breakthroughs.6 It enables efficient conversion under mild conditions, compatible with intermittent renewable energy and scalable—offering an innovative solution to mitigate emissions while realizing renewable energy storage and conversion.

CO2 is a thermodynamically stable linear molecule characterized by a high C=O bond dissociation energy of approximately 750 kJ·mol−1. The activation and dissociation of CO2 typically require it to undergo a single-electron reduction process, forming an anion radical intermediate (i.e., CO2 + e− → CO2·-, with a standard redox potential E0 = −1.9 V vs. SHE). This activated CO2·- species is highly reactive and can readily undergo subsequent reaction with water molecules to form key intermediates such as COOH (carboxyl radical) or OCHO (formate radical). These intermediates then serve as precursors for a variety of reduced products. Depending on the carbon count in the synthesized molecules, the products of CO2 reduction can be categorized into C1 products (such as carbon monoxide (CO) and formic acid (HCOOH)), C2 products (including ethylene (C2H4) and ethanol (C2H5OH)), and even C3+ products (including propane (C3H6), propanol (C3H7OH), and longer-chain hydrocarbons or oxygenates).7 Compared to C1 products, C2 products such as ethylene (C2H4) offer higher energy storage density.8 This is primarily because the molecular structures of C2 products are more complex and contain a greater number of chemical bonds, enabling them to release more energy during combustion or subsequent chemical reactions. Consequently, the production of C2 products has attracted significant research interest. However, the formation of C2 products involves multi-step electron transfers (typically requiring 12 or more electrons per molecule) and protonation steps, making the reaction pathway considerably more complex and challenging to control. In contemporary research, efforts are focused on two main aspects: firstly, the development of catalytic materials with higher Faradaic efficiency (FE) to accelerate the conversion rate of CO2 to C2 products; and secondly, the design of catalysts that can effectively suppress competing reactions, especially the suppression of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), commonly referred to as HER and the formation of undesired C1 products, thereby enhancing the selectivity and overall yield of C2 products.

The current challenge in electrocatalytic CO2 reduction lies primarily in the development of robust, high-performance, and discriminating catalysts. Various classes of materials have been investigated, including noble metals, transition metals, metal alloys, transition metal oxides, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs).9,10,11,12 However, most of these materials exhibit limitations such as poor catalytic activity, low product selectivity, or insufficient stability under operating conditions. Among the materials studied so far, copper (Cu) remains unique in its ability to produce significant yields of hydrocarbons and alcohols, including multi-carbon products, via electrochemical CO2 reduction. Cu possesses favorable d-band electronic properties, suitable adsorption strengths for key C-C coupling intermediates, and high electrical conductivity, making it an excellent electrocatalyst for CO2 reduction.13,14 Nevertheless, most advanced Cu-based catalysts still face challenges related to product selectivity, and further improvements are needed to minimize the competing HER and the formation of C1 products, thereby maximizing the FE toward valuable C2 products. Moreover, surface modification strategies for Cu-based catalysts offer additional avenues to tune their catalytic properties, improve selectivity for C2+ products, and lower the overpotential that is necessary for the reduction of CO2.

Building on the foundation of metal catalyst research, recent studies have highlighted the promising potential of carbon-based materials as efficient catalysts for CO2 reduction. Carbon-based materials exhibit a wide range of tunable catalytic properties and have demonstrated the ability to effectively catalyze the CO2RR. For example, emerging carbon nanomaterials such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon nanofibers, and graphene are regarded as promising metal-free electrocatalysts due to their abundant natural resources, highly customizable porous structures, excellent resistance to acids and bases, high thermal stability, and environmental friendliness. These materials can facilitate the electrochemical reduction of CO2 by promoting reaction kinetics, enhancing charge transfer, and improving overall reaction efficiency.15,16,17,18,19 The active sites present in carbon-based materials play a critical role in determining their catalytic performance.

The active sites within carbon-based materials are key factors determining their catalytic performance. The nature and density of these active sites influence the physicochemical and electrochemical properties of the catalyst, thereby altering the reaction pathways and efficiency of CO2 electro-reduction. Research has revealed that modifications to the structure and composition of carbon materials can alter the electronic structure and chemical environment of the catalyst, which in turn influences the adsorption behavior and activation process of CO2 molecules, as well as the stability and transformation of key reaction intermediates. The detailed reaction mechanism encompasses several steps, including CO2 adsorption, proton-coupled electron transfer processes, and the formation and decomposition of reaction intermediates, all of which can be efficiently mediated by carbon-based catalysts.

Carbon-based materials possess abundant structural diversity, enabling them to exhibit a wide spectrum of properties tailored for CO2RR applications. These materials encompass zero-dimensional fullerenes, one-dimensional CNTs, two-dimensional graphene, and graphene oxide (GO), as well as graphene quantum dots (GQDs) and various other nanostructured forms, each with distinct sizes, morphologies, and structural characteristics. These structural features directly determine critical material characteristics, including specific surface area, pore structure, electrical conductance, and surface chemical properties. which collectively influence the adsorption capacity for CO2, the efficiency of electron transport, and the overall catalytic activity. In particular, the pore structure of carbon-based materials has been identified as a key factor affecting CO2RR performance. A well-designed porous architecture can enhance the mass transport and diffusion of CO2 to active sites, facilitate the release of products, and provide confined environments that favor specific reaction pathways.

In catalytic systems, carbon materials are far from being inert catalyst supports. Recent studies reveal that beyond anchoring active sites, carbon supports play at least three critical roles that profoundly influence the ultimate performance of CO2RR: (1) The primary support stabilizing and dispersing single-atom catalysts (SACs); (2) The electron-conducting support determining reaction current density and FE; (3) An active regulator that modulates the electronic structure and geometric configuration of active sites through intrinsic physicochemical properties (e.g., curvature). (1) As the primary support for SACs, carbon materials, with their high specific surface area and abundant defect sites, serve as ideal hosts for metal single-atom dispersion. Such SACs are typically anchored onto nitrogen (N)-doped carbon frameworks via strategies like high-temperature pyrolysis. Han et al. successfully constructed atomically dispersed CuN2C2 sites on graphene and CNTs using a confined self-initiated dispersion strategy,20 maximizing atomic utilization while providing an ideal platform for structure-activity relationship studies. (2) As an electron conduction carrier: Molecular catalysts such as phthalocyanines (Pc) exhibit poor intrinsic conductivity. Carbon carriers not only provide physical support but also serve as efficient electron transport pathways. Multiple studies confirm that loading CoPc molecules onto carbon materials significantly enhances CO2RR performance. Zhang et al. uniformly anchored CoPc onto CNTs via π-π interactions, forming CoPc/CNT hybrid materials that exhibited far superior current density, CO selectivity (Faraday efficiency >95%), and stability compared to either CoPc or CNTs alone.21 This stems from CNTs' high conductivity and strong electron interactions with CoPc, facilitating rapid electron transfer from the electrode to the catalytic center. Further investigations reveal that the microstructure of carbon supports (e.g., specific surface area and pore size distribution) significantly influences electron conduction and reactant mass transfer. Yang et al. compared CoPc catalysts loaded on five different carbon materials (mesoporous carbon (MC), CNTs, carbon fibers, graphene, and carbon black) loaded CoPc catalysts, revealing that MC with a unique nanostructure and pore size distribution delivered the best performance. It achieved 95% FE and 28 mA cm−2 current density at −0.8 V vs. RHE, demonstrating that optimized pore structure facilitates CO2 adsorption and transport.22 Furthermore, carbon supports can engineer specialized interfacial microenvironments to counteract HER competition in acidic electrolytes. Zhang et al. employed a covalent molecular design strategy to graft cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc) with long-chain quaternary ammonium groups onto CNTs.23 Song et al. further constructed layered structures combining ionic, hydrophobic, and gas-affinity properties. In these designs, CNTs serve as conductive scaffolds, synergistically regulating H3O+ distribution and local CO concentration at the electrode/electrolyte interface.24 In these designs, CNTs serve as conductive scaffolds that synergistically regulate H3O+ distribution and localized CO concentration at the electrode/electrolyte interface with organic functional layers. This enables highly efficient multi-electron reduction to methanol in strong acids (pH ∼1), achieving current densities and selectivities surpassing traditional neutral or alkaline systems. (3) As an active site-tuning carrier: The intrinsic physical structure of carbon carriers (particularly curvature) can finely modulate the catalytic activity of anchored active sites via a “strain effect.” Han et al. compared the ORR performance of CuN2C2 single-atom sites on carbon substrates with different curvatures (graphene vs. CNTs with varying diameters). They found that the curved surface of CNTs induces geometric distortion in the CuN2C2 sites, which is further amplified during the reaction.20 Compared to planar graphene sites, Cu sites on 8 nm-diameter CNTs exhibited optimized distortion during interaction with oxygen-containing intermediates—enabling strong bonding with oxygen species to promote electron transfer while maintaining moderate bonding with substrate N/C atoms, boosting ORR activity 6-fold. Conversely, 4 nm-diameter CNTs exhibit excessive strain, causing overstretched Cu-N bonds that weaken substrate coordination and consequently reduce activity. This curvature-induced strain effect similarly applies to molecular catalysts for CO2RR. Xin et al. compared the behavior of CoPc and FePc on different carbon substrates.25 It was discovered that when CoTAPc molecules adsorb onto small-diameter single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) via strong π-π interactions, they undergo bending deformation. This adjusts the adsorption energy of CO intermediates from “too weak” to “moderate,” facilitating further hydrogenation and boosting methanol Faraday efficiency (FE) to 52%. In contrast, FePc exhibits excessively strong CO adsorption (prone to poisoning), with the strain effect exacerbating this tendency. This leads to ∗CO struggling to desorb or react, resulting in a TOF reduction exceeding 50%. This demonstrates that the strain effect’s efficacy depends on the intrinsic properties of the active site. Appropriate strain can optimize the catalytic cycle, while excessive strain or application to unsuitable catalytic systems may produce adverse effects.

However, the practical application of carbon-based materials in CO2 reduction catalysis still faces several core challenges and developmental bottlenecks: First, carbon-based materials generally exhibit low active site density, and the catalytic selectivity of individual active sites is insufficient. This often leads to complex CO2 reduction products (such as the simultaneous formation of CO, CH4, HCOOH, and so forth), making it difficult to achieve high-selective production of target products. Second, although carbon-based materials exhibit superior conductivity compared to most non-metallic materials, they still lag behind metallic catalysts. Insufficient charge transfer efficiency limits reaction rates and hinders further enhancement of catalytic activity. Third, during prolonged electrochemical reduction reactions, the porous structure of carbon-based materials is prone to collapse, and active sites can become covered or lost due to reaction intermediates. This results in poor catalyst stability, making it difficult to meet the demands of long-term industrial operation. Furthermore, the preparation processes for carbon-based materials are often complex, leading to issues such as poor performance uniformity and high costs during large-scale production, which further restricts their industrial application.

The origin of the catalytic activity of carbon-based materials has long been the focus of intense debate and extensive discussion within the international academic community (e.g., American Chemical Society Annual Meeting, European Congress on Catalysis and International Carbon Materials Conference). The core of the controversy lies in whether the CO2RR performance of these materials originates from the intrinsic active sites engineered on the carbon skeleton itself (e.g., heteroatom-doped sites, topological defects, and edge sites) or is dominated by trace metal impurities (e.g., Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) introduced during synthesis (e.g., precursor raw materials and catalytic reactors) and post-treatment processes.

Researchers holding the “impurity-dominated” viewpoint have provided compelling evidence that transition metal impurities at ppb or even ppm levels in carbon materials can form highly active single-atom sites (e.g., M-Nx). The CO2RR activity (FE and current density) of such materials shows a significant positive correlation with the content of metal impurities. Furthermore, the catalytic selectivity of metal-free carbon materials is highly consistent with the catalytic characteristics of the corresponding metal impurities (e.g., Fe predominantly produces CO and Cu predominantly produces C2+), and the CO2RR activity is almost completely lost after removing trace metals by multi-step acid washing.

In contrast, the “intrinsic site-dominated” viewpoint is supported by ultra-high-purity experimental results: carbon materials synthesized using ultra-high-purity precursors (99.999% grade), metal-free reaction systems, and high-purity electrolytes exhibit significant CO2RR activity even when the metal impurity content is below the detection limit of ICP-MS (<0.1 ppb). Moreover, the density of intrinsic active sites shows a positive correlation with catalytic performance. More compellingly, some carbon-based metal-free materials (e.g., F-doped carbon and graphdiyne) maintain stable CO2RR activity in impurity-containing CO2 atmospheres, while metal-based catalysts are prone to poisoning and deactivation, indirectly proving the contribution of intrinsic active sites.

Currently, a preliminary consensus has been reached within the academic community regarding this controversy: To accurately identify the active origin of carbon-based metal-free electrocatalysts, strict impurity control and systematic characterization have become the basic research paradigm in this field. This includes multi-stage purification of precursors and reaction systems, high-sensitivity detection of metal impurities (ICP-MS, HAADF-STEM), control experiments involving impurity removal/addition, and the use of in-situ characterization techniques to track the dynamic evolution of carbon skeleton active sites during the CO2RR process. The origin of the activity is verified by comparing the activity before and after acid washing. For carbon materials with a low density of intrinsic active sites, the activity is mainly dominated by metal impurities. For carbon materials engineered to possess a high density of intrinsic active sites with strictly controlled metal impurities (<0.1 ppb), the intrinsic active sites (heteroatom doping, defects) are the core source of catalytic performance. Additionally, low-content metal impurities (0.1–1 ppb) can form a “metal impurity-carbon skeleton” interface with intrinsic active sites, which optimizes the adsorption energy of reaction intermediates and further enhances CO2RR activity.

In contrast to existing reviews that primarily summarize material categories and catalytic performances, this Review provides a structure- and mechanism-oriented perspective by systematically correlating carbon material dimensionality, active-site coordination environments, axial chemical modulation, and defect/pore engineering with electronic structure regulation, reaction pathways, and product selectivity in CO2 electroreduction.

In this review, we focus on recent advances in carbon-based materials for the electrocatalytic carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR), with particular emphasis on the structure-performance relationships of catalysts with different dimensional architectures. Carbon materials ranging from zero-dimensional carbon dots, one-dimensional CNTs, two-dimensional graphene/graphdiyne, to three-dimensional porous carbons are systematically discussed, highlighting how dimensionality, defect structures, and heteroatom doping regulate electronic properties, active-site characteristics, and mass/charge transport behavior. This Review summarizes key reaction pathways and performance evaluation metrics for CO2RR, with a specific focus on C1 and C2+ product formation and C-C coupling mechanisms. Studies primarily centered on metal or metal-oxide catalysts without carbon frameworks, as well as photocatalytic and thermochemical CO2 conversion processes, are beyond the scope of this Review and therefore not discussed in detail. Finally, the remaining challenges and future perspectives for the rational design and scalable application of 0D-3D carbon-based electrocatalysts are outlined to guide further research in this field.

In a typical electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), CO2 molecules dissolved in the electrolyte must first overcome significant mass transfer resistance, diffuse through the solution, and reach the surface of the electrode. Upon arrival at the electrode, the CO2 molecules adsorb onto the active sites of the catalyst material. This adsorption step is critical as it marks the point where the catalytically active sites begin to facilitate the subsequent chemical transformations. The activation and conversion of adsorbed CO2 can proceed through two primary mechanistic pathways. On one hand, after CO2 is chemisorbed on the active sites, it can directly form a carboxyl intermediate (COOH·) via a concerted proton-electron transfer (CPET) mechanism, wherein a proton and an electron are simultaneously acquired by the CO2 molecule. On the other hand, the adsorbed CO2 molecule may first undergo a one-electron reduction to form a metastable CO2·- anion radical intermediate.28 This radical intermediate is highly reactive and rapidly combines with a proton from the solution to likewise yield the COOH· intermediate.29,30

Subsequently, the COOH· intermediate undergoes further proton-electron transfer steps to form a chemisorbed CO intermediate (∗CO). The ∗CO intermediate then desorbs from the active sites of the catalyst—a process referring to the phenomenon where molecules, atoms, or ions that are adhered to the surface of a solid material detach from that surface and enter the surrounding medium. This desorption step allows the active sites to be regenerated and available for further catalytic cycles, while the released CO molecules enter the reaction system, thereby completing the overall conversion of CO2 to CO.31,32

Following the desorption of ∗CO, the reaction may branch into multiple possible pathways, and the direction of these pathways is strongly influenced by various factors, including the inherent properties of the catalyst (such as composition, morphology, and electronic structure) and the operating reaction parameters (such as applied potential, pH, and electrolyte composition). If the reaction system is oriented toward the production of HCOOH, the COOH· intermediate will acquire an additional proton and electron, overcome the associated energy barrier, and be converted into HCOOH, which subsequently desorbs from the catalyst surface.

When the target products are multi-carbon compounds (C2+), the reaction mechanism becomes considerably more complex, and the formation of carbon-carbon (C-C) bonds serves as the core step determining the generation of such products. In electrocatalytic CO2RR, the formation of C-C bonds is the core step for producing polycarbon products (C2+, such as C2H4, C2H5OH, CH3COOH, and so forth). The reaction pathways exhibit diversity, with significant differences in reaction conditions, intermediate types, and catalytic requirements across different pathways. The mainstream pathways are primarily categorized into the following four types, each with published research papers confirming their reaction mechanisms: (1) ∗CO-∗CO Coupling Pathway: This is the most extensively studied and fundamental C-C bond formation pathway in CO2RR, also known as the ∗CO dimerization pathway mentioned earlier. During the reaction, CO2 undergoes proton-coupled electron transfer reduction to form the ∗CO intermediate. Two adjacent ∗CO intermediates adsorbed on the catalyst active site then undergo a coupling reaction to form the ∗CO-CO intermediate. Subsequent steps, including further protonation and dehydrogenation, ultimately yield various C2+ products. This pathway operates under mild conditions (ambient temperature and pressure, low overpotential) and proceeds efficiently on both carbon-based and Cu-based catalysts. It represents the most readily achievable C-C coupling pathway in current laboratory and fundamental research. Its core principle involves regulating the adsorption strength and local coverage of the ∗CO intermediate to lower the coupling reaction energy barrier. (2) ∗CO-∗CHO Coupling Pathway: This cross-coupling pathway differs from conventional ∗CO dimerization. During CO2 reduction, two distinct key intermediates—∗CO and ∗CHO—simultaneously form. These intermediates undergo cross-coupling at the catalyst active site, generating key intermediates like ∗OCCHO, which subsequently convert into C2+ products. The advantage of this pathway is that it does not require simultaneous adsorption of two ∗CO intermediates, thereby reducing the demand for local ∗CO coverage. It maintains high C-C coupling activity even under low CO2 partial pressure conditions. Particularly in heteroatom-doped carbon-based catalysts (e.g., N-doped graphene and N-S co-doped carbon materials) and catalysts with heterogeneous interfaces (e.g., Cu0/Cu2+ interfaces), the reaction kinetics of this pathway are significantly enhanced. (3) ∗COOH-∗CO coupling pathway (low-energy barrier pathway): This pathway centers on ∗COOH and ∗CO intermediates, where the ∗COOH intermediate generated from CO2 reduction couples with the ∗CO intermediate at the catalyst active site. Its primary advantage lies in its low reaction energy barrier, enabling operation across a broad potential range. This pathway exhibits outstanding reactivity on bimetallic carbon-based catalysts (e.g., Cu-Ni/CNTs and Cu-Fe/porous carbon) while effectively suppressing competitive side reactions like HER. It significantly enhances FE for C2+ products, offering a novel direction for designing highly selective CO2RR catalysts. (4) Fischer-Tropsch-type chain growth pathway (high-temperature, high-pressure pathway): This pathway overcomes the limitations of traditional coupling pathways and primarily occurs under high-temperature, high-pressure reaction conditions (typically above 125°C). In this pathway, ∗CO intermediates first undergo hydrogenation to form hydrogen-containing intermediates like ∗CH2 and ∗CH3. Subsequently, chain-growth reactions between these hydrogen-containing intermediates progressively form C-C bonds, enabling continuous growth of polycarbon chains (C2+). This pathway is particularly suited for enhancing the selectivity of long-chain polycarbon products (e.g., C3+). The core of this pathway lies in regulating reaction temperature, pressure, and the hydrogenation capacity of the catalyst’s active sites. Significant research breakthroughs have been achieved using Cu-based catalysts and carbon-supported bimetallic catalysts. Other less common C-C coupling pathways, such as OCH2-OCH2 coupling and CHx-CHx coupling, are not elaborated upon here.33,34,35,36

It is important to emphasize that during the CO2 reduction process, each individual reaction step competes with others for limited resources such as active sites, protons, and electrons. For example, the evolution of the CO2·- intermediate and the COOH· intermediate compete for the same CO2 adsorption sites on the catalyst surface, as well as for the available protons and electrons within the reaction system. Furthermore, the differences in activation energy barriers among the various product formation paths mean that reaction conditions exert a profound influence on product selectivity. Increasing the reaction temperature generally accelerates the overall reaction rate by providing more thermal energy to overcome activation barriers; however, it may also alter the relative rates of parallel reactions, leading to shifts in the distribution of products. Similarly, increasing the electrode potential (making it more negative) can supply a greater driving force for the reduction reactions, promoting the formation of more deeply reduced products that require multiple electron transfers.

A well-designed catalyst plays a multifaceted role: it not only lowers the activation energy of the desired reaction, thereby accelerating the reaction rate, but also selectively stabilizes specific key intermediates through its unique active site geometry and electronic structure. This selective stabilization can effectively suppress competing side reactions, such as the HER, and enhance the FE and selectivity toward the target products. Therefore, the targeted design of high-performance electrocatalysts necessitates an in-depth, atomic-scale insight into the underlying reaction pathways. In the following section, several key performance parameters that are widely employed for the quantitative evaluation of electrocatalytic CO2RR systems will be introduced and discussed in detail.

Significant evaluation parameters in the electrocatalytic reduction of CO2

The rational evaluation of electrocatalysts for the CO2RR requires a comprehensive set of performance descriptors, as no single parameter can fully reflect catalytic activity, selectivity, energy efficiency, and intrinsic kinetics. In particular, CO2RR involves multi-electron/proton transfer pathways and competitive side reactions (especially the HER), making multidimensional performance assessment essential. Commonly used evaluation parameters include FE, onset potential, overpotential, current density, Tafel slope, electrochemical active surface area (ECSA), electrochemical impedance, and intrinsic activity descriptors such as turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF). These parameters collectively provide insights into reaction selectivity, energy consumption, kinetics, and active-site utilization.

A higher FE indicates that the reaction is more inclined to generate the target product, reducing energy waste and the occurrence of side reactions. For example, in the electrocatalytic reduction of CO2 to CO, a higher FE indicates better selectivity for CO, with the formation of by-products such as H2 effectively suppressed.

Onset potential

The onset potential refers to the electrode potential at which the electrocatalytic CO2RR begins to occur significantly. When the electrode potential reaches the onset potential, the thermodynamic energy barrier of the CO2RR is overcome, and reaction kinetics begin to dominate. The closer the onset potential is to the theoretical potential (e.g., the theoretical reduction potential of CO2/CO is −0.11 V vs. RHE), the more the catalyst can drive the reaction at a lower overpotential, resulting in higher energy efficiency. The onset potential is a key parameter for evaluating catalyst activity, and a low onset potential usually corresponds to excellent catalytic activity.

A lower overpotential indicates higher energy efficiency and reduced energy consumption. For example, if a catalyst reduces the overpotential for CO2 electroreduction to CO from 0.5 V to 0.3 V, the additional energy required for the reaction decreases by 40%, significantly improving economic viability.

Current density (j)

Current density is defined as the current passing through a unit geometric area of the electrode (unit: mA·cm−2), and it is a core indicator for measuring the reaction rate. At a fixed potential, a higher current density indicates a faster reaction rate. It is closely related to the active site density of the catalyst and mass transfer efficiency. For example, increasing the population of active sites through nanostructure design, or optimizing the electrode pore structure to improve CO2 diffusion, can significantly enhance the current density.

For instance, the ECSA of porous carbon-supported SACs can be more than 10 times that of traditional bulk materials.

Electrochemical impedance (Z)

Electrochemical impedance (Z) quantifies the overall opposition to charge transfer at the electrode/electrolyte interface. Via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), charge transfer resistance Rct and mass transfer resistance can be investigated. Impedance in the low-frequency regime mirrors mass transfer restrictions, whereas the high-frequency regime aligns with the charge transfer process. For instance, tailoring the catalyst surface can lower Rct, thus boosting reaction kinetics.

Turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF)

The TON refers to the number of times each active site catalyzes the CO2RR within a given time, while the TOF represents the number of catalytic reactions per active site per unit time, i.e., TOF=ReactionRateActiveSiteDensity。TON and TOF can directly reflect the intrinsic catalytic activity of the active sites in electrocatalysts, eliminating interference from factors such as catalyst loading and electrode area. They are important microscopic indicators for evaluating electrocatalyst performance. Higher TON and TOF values indicate that the catalyst has higher catalytic activity and efficiency.

In summary, a reliable assessment of CO2RR electrocatalysts requires the combined consideration of selectivity (FE), energy efficiency (onset potential and overpotential), productivity (current density), reaction kinetics (Tafel slope and impedance), and intrinsic activity (TOF and TON). Caution should be exercised when comparing data across different studies, as experimental conditions, reactor configurations, and product quantification methods can significantly influence the reported metrics.

CO2 reduction products

The electrochemical CO2 reduction process involves multiple proton and electron transfer steps, yielding various reduction products. Due to differences in the reaction pathways of different products, understanding the specific mechanism of CO2 reduction remains challenging, particularly as the formation pathways for certain products are still unclear. Current research on CO2 reduction mechanisms primarily relies on density functional theory (DFT) calculations, combined with in situ/operando characterization techniques to detect potential CO2-related intermediates.37,38

The electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2) to produce specific high-value-added products is a complex and multi-step process that typically relies on a series of hydrogenation reactions involving various CO2-derived intermediates adsorbed on the surface of the electrocatalyst. The nature, stability, and transformation pathways of these adsorbed species are fundamental to determining the overall reaction selectivity and efficiency. Therefore, accurate identification and characterization of these key adsorption intermediates are crucial for the rational design and development of highly selective, efficient catalysts tailored for specific reduction products.

Among the diverse array of possible intermediates, adsorbed carbon monoxide (∗CO) is widely regarded as a central and pivotal intermediate that plays a decisive role in steering the selectivity of CO2RRs. The subsequent transformation pathways of ∗CO are multifaceted and primarily include the following routes, as illustrated schematically in Figure 1: (1) Directly desorbed from the catalyst’s surface to form gaseous CO products; (2) progressive hydrogenation to yield various C1 hydrocarbons (such as methane, CH4) or oxygen-containing C1 compounds (such as methanol, CH3OH); and (3) Covalent coupling reactions, such as dimerization or insertion reactions with other co-adsorbed intermediates, to form polycarbonated (C2+) hydrocarbons or oxygen-containing compounds. The selectivity among these pathways is predominantly governed by a combination of the catalyst’s intrinsic properties (composition and electronic structure) and its extrinsic conditions (morphology and local reaction environment).39,40

Taking ethanol (C2H5OH) as a representative example—a common and valuable C2 oxygenated product—its formation necessitates the critical step of C-C bond formation. This process typically originates from the dimerization of two adjacent ∗CO species adsorbed on the catalyst surface, resulting in the formation of a C2O2− dimeric species. This dimer subsequently undergoes protonation to form relatively stable intermediates such as ∗OCCOH or ∗COCHO, which are mutant isomers that can interconvert under reaction conditions.41,42 The ∗OCCOH intermediate can undergo further sequential hydrogenation steps to form ∗OCCH2OH, which may ultimately be reduced to either ethylene (C2H4) or ethanol (C2H5OH), depending on the subsequent reaction pathway. In a parallel route, the ∗COCHO intermediate can be converted into the ∗CH2CHO (acetaldehyde) intermediate through a series of hydrogenation and dehydration steps. The ∗CH2CHO intermediate is widely recognized as a key branching point that determines the ultimate selectivity between C2 products. Its further hydrogenation yields ∗CH3CHO (ethanol precursor), which is then ultimately converted to C2H5OH. Recent studies indicate that enhancing the adsorption energy of ∗CH3CHO on catalyst surfaces—for instance, by constructing coordination-unsaturated sites or engineering defects to modulate the geometric and electronic structures of surface active sites can effectively improve the selectivity toward ethanol production by stabilizing this critical intermediate and facilitating its further reduction.

Another common oxygen-containing C2 product is acetic acid (CH3COOH). Its formation mechanism, particularly the C-C bond formation step, differs significantly from that of ethanol. One proposed pathway involves a methyl-CO2 coupling mechanism: CO is first deeply reduced to a methyl group (CH3), which then combines with the adsorbed CO2− radical anion to form an acetate species (CH3COO−).43 Another pathway involves the direct dimerization of two CO2− radical species, forming a stable ∗OOC-COO intermediate.

This specific intermediate has been experimentally detected and identified using in situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and is considered a key precursor for acetic acid formation.44 Therefore, catalyst designs that favor the stabilization of the CO2− species and promote its dimerization over its protonation would be expected to enhance the formation of the ∗OOC-COO intermediate, thereby increasing the selectivity toward acetic acid production.

Currently, mechanistic studies focusing on the formation pathways of C3 oxygen-containing compounds remain relatively scarce and less understood compared to C1 and C2 products. In most reported research works, C3 oxygenated products are typically detected only in trace amounts as minor byproducts in the CO2RR. To date, only a limited number of studies have successfully reported propanol (C3H7OH) and acetone (CH3COCH3) as major C3 products, achieving notable Faradaic efficiencies of up to approximately 30% under optimized conditions. Based on the existing but limited research, the formation of C3 compounds is generally believed to originate from the insertion reaction of an adsorbed C2 intermediate (such as ∗C2H5 or ∗CH2CHO) with another ∗CO molecule.27 The pathway for forming the first C-C bond in C3 products shares considerable similarities with the initial steps in the ethanol (C2H5OH) formation pathway, as both can initiate from the dimerization of two ∗CO molecules to generate the common C2O2− species. This C2 species can be further reduced to ∗OCOCH or ∗COCHO intermediates. Among these, the ∗OCCOH intermediate is particularly noteworthy as it plays a pivotal and dual role in the formation of both C2 and C3 products: it can be reduced to a ∗COC intermediate via cleavage of the C-O(H) bond, which is then converted to ∗COCH3 through a hydrogenation step. The ∗COCH3 intermediate can subsequently participate in coupling or insertion reactions with additional carbon-containing fragments to eventually form C3 products.

Summary of the performance of some carbon-based catalysts that convert CO2 into useful products

A notable advantage of carbon materials in electrocatalytic CO2 reduction is their relatively higher onset potential for the HER, which is a common competitive reaction in aqueous electrolytes. This inherent property provides carbon-based catalysts with a distinct advantage in suppressing HER and improving FE for CO2 reduction products.39 For instance, the introduction of N atoms, N-doped carbon nanotubes (NCNTs), exhibits unique electronic and geometric properties. The existence of graphitic N and pyridinic N species within the carbon lattice significantly modifies the local electron density, reduces the overpotential required for CO2 activation, and enhances the selectivity toward CO production.52 This tunability in electronic structure underscores the capability of carbon-based materials to steer reaction pathways and improve product selectivity.

However, pristine (undoped) carbon materials generally exhibit negligible catalytic activity toward CO2RR because the neutral carbon atoms in pure sp2-hybridized networks possess limited ability to adsorb and activate the stable CO2 molecules.39 To address this limitation, researchers have developed various heteroatom-doped carbon materials—incorporating elements such as N, phosphorus (P), boron (B), or sulfur (S)—to induce charge redistribution and create catalytically active sites. The introduction of these heteroatoms disrupts the electroneutrality of the carbon matrix, generates charged sites, and enhances the adsorption and activation of CO2 and reaction intermediates, thereby significantly improving the electrocatalytic performance.53,54,55

To date, carbon-based materials have been synthesized in diverse morphologies and hierarchical structures, each offering distinct advantages for electrocatalytic applications. For convenience and clarity, they are often categorized based on dimensionality: 0D carbon dots, 1D CNTs, 2D graphene, and 3D porous carbons.56 Each class of material possesses unique structural merits: zero-dimensional materials, with their ultra-compact size and high surface-to-volume ratio, promote ion conduction and improve interfacial contact between active materials and electrolytes; one-dimensional materials offer high specific surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and directional charge transport pathways, which collectively enhance electron transfer and mass transport; two-dimensional materials are characterized by their atomically thin layers, high specific surface area, excellent in-plane conductivity, and stackable laminated structures that facilitate the design of hybrid catalysts; three-dimensional materials, benefiting from their interconnected porous networks, high specific surface area, and continuous ion/electron transport pathways, provide abundant active sites, efficient reactant accessibility, and robust mechanical stability, thereby substantially improving overall electrochemical performance.57

0D

Since Xu and his colleagues discovered carbon quantum dots (CQDs) in 2004 while purifying SWCNTs,58 these particles have garnered extensive research interest due to their low toxicity, excellent biocompatibility, tunable fluorescence, and chemical inertness. They exhibit outstanding and multifaceted properties across physical, chemical, electrical, and optical domains. Various methods exist for synthesizing carbon dots, including large-scale production techniques like hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis, microwave-assisted synthesis, and magnetothermal microfluidic synthesis.59

Subsequent studies have categorized carbon dots into distinct types depending on their physical structures and chemical properties. However, different reports present varying classifications. The most prevalent and widely accepted classification scheme divides CDs into three primary types: GQDs with graphene-like structures; carbon nanodots (CNDs) typically featuring an amorphous carbon core; and CQDs characterized by a crystalline carbon core.60 Some scholars propose a fourth category: carbonized polymer dots (CPDs).61 A viewpoint gaining increasing recognition in recent years. The distinction lies in whether CPDs should be regarded as an independent category or as a subset within the broader classification of CNDs. Nevertheless, in the field of electrochemistry, despite their intriguing optical properties, CPDs have seen limited reports on their electrochemical applications due to their restricted carbonization degree and low electrical conductivity. Therefore, carbon dots used in the electrochemical field are restricted to GQDs, CQDs, and CNDs.62

The core of CQDs is usually composed of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms, exhibiting short-range ordered characteristics similar to nanocrystalline graphite. This is evidenced by lattice fringes of approximately 0.21 nm observed via high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), corresponding to the (100) plane of graphite. This quasi-crystalline core is enveloped by an amorphous carbon shell rich in heteroatoms such as oxygen and N. This shell contains numerous surface functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, carboxyl, carbonyl, and amino groups), which confer excellent water solubility and ease of modification on the CQDs. Notably, CQDs are typically smaller than 10 nm in size and exhibit a pronounced quantum confinement effect, making their optical properties (such as photoluminescence) strongly dependent on their size and surface state. Their photoluminescence mechanism is generally attributed to the synergistic effects of intrinsic state emission from the carbon core, surface defect state emission, and molecular state emission. Furthermore, doping with heteroatoms (e.g., N, S, P, and fluorine) can effectively tune the electronic structure, bandgap, and surface chemistry of CQDs. For example, N doping can introduce additional electron donor states, narrow the bandgap, enhance the fluorescence quantum yield, and alter the emission wavelength. In contrast, fluorine ion doping, leveraging its high electronegativity, can effectively passivate surface defects through the formation of C-F bonds and lower the Fermi level of the material, thereby improving charge separation efficiency at heterojunctions. This structural tunability enables CQDs to meet the requirements of diverse applications ranging from bioimaging to optoelectronic devices. In summary, CQDs are not simply amorphous carbon clusters but are quantum dot materials with a well-defined core-shell structure, tunable surface chemistry, and size-dependent properties. Their unique structural features—including the graphene-like crystalline core, the functional group-rich shell, and the fine-tuning of electronic structure achieved through doping—collectively form the physicochemical foundation for their multifunctional applications.63,64,65,66

CNDs are typically spherical nanoparticles with an amorphous or partially crystalline carbon core, often described as a “carbonized polymer dot” (CPD) model. This structural diversity arises from varied synthetic routes and precursor materials. While their surfaces remain rich in diverse functional groups (e.g., -COOH, -OH, and -NH2), advanced mass spectrometry (MS) techniques such as MALDI-TOF MS and ESI-Q-TOF MS/MS have revealed that many CNDs are not monolithic nanoparticles. Instead, they can exist as supramolecular aggregates or clusters of low-molecular-weight fluorophores or oligomers held together by non-covalent interactions. This challenges the traditional core-shell model and places CNDs on a structural spectrum from molecular aggregates to more condensed carbon nanoparticles. The defining features of CNDs include nanoscale dimensions (<10 nm), a carbon-based framework ranging from amorphous to small sp2 domains, and a densely functionalized surface. Their electronic structure is highly tunable through heteroatom doping and precise control over core conjugation and surface chemistry, enabling optimized reaction pathways, reduced overpotential in electrocatalysis, and tailored biological interactions. When incorporated into composite materials, CNDs act as structural additives, disrupting the stacking of other carriers and enhancing mass transfer due to their small size and functionalized surfaces.69,70,71,72,73,74

GQDs are essentially zero-dimensional (0D) nanomaterials derived from graphene, characterized by their nanoscale size in all three spatial dimensions. Although they inherit the in-plane structural motif of graphene, their extremely limited lateral dimensions (typically ranging from 2 to 20 nm) and atomically thin thickness (often less than 2 nm, corresponding to 1–4 graphene layers) classify them firmly as 0D systems, where quantum confinement effects dominate in all directions. Each carbon atom within the GQD core forms a hexagonal network with three neighboring carbon atoms, preserving a local graphene-like, sp2-hybridized honeycomb lattice structure. This well-defined crystalline core is central to their distinctive electronic properties. They possess a well-defined graphene lattice structure, often accompanied by various surface functional groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, or epoxy groups. Crucially, the high perimeter-to-area ratio of these 0D fragments results in a rich density of edge sites, which can adopt zigzag or armchair configurations. These edges, along with potential lattice defects, are highly chemically active and play a decisive role in modulating the electronic structure and optical properties. Due to their pronounced quantum confinement in all three dimensions, GQDs exhibit strong size-dependent and surface chemistry-dependent optical and electronic properties. This three-dimensional confinement opens a tunable bandgap, endowing the otherwise semi-metallic graphene with semiconductor-like behavior, including size-tunable photoluminescence. Their remarkable characteristics in electrochemical biosensing stem from excellent solubility in diverse solvents, superior electronic properties, strong chemical inertness, abundant functionalizable edge sites, low cost, and multifunctionality.63,76,77,78,79,80

Zhang et al. prepared GQD catalysts with surface-modified functional groups via an alkali-mediated hydrothermal method followed by oxidation/reduction treatment.81 These included pristine GQDs (p-GQDs), oxidized GQDs (o-GQDs), reduced GQDs (r-GQDs), and re-reduced GQDs (ro-GQDs). They further synthesized amino (-NH2) and sulfonic acid (-SO3) functionalized GQDs to regulate the selective conversion of CO2 electrochemical reduction (CO2RR) to CH4. As shown in Figure 5, the study revealed that electron-donating groups (EDGs, e.g., -OH, and -NH2) significantly enhance CH4 selectivity and yield, while electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs, e.g., -COOH and -SO3) suppress CO2RR and promote HER. Notably, -OH-modified ro-GQDs achieved a CH4 FE of 70.0% at −0.90 V (vs. RHE), with a CH4 partial current density (jCH4) of 200 mA cm−2; similarly, GQD-NH2-H with high -NH2 modification achieved 70.0% CH4 FE and 201 mA cm−2 with no significant decay in stability over 10 h. This performance difference stems from functional group regulation of active site electronic structure: EDGs maintain high charge density at adjacent C/N active sites while stabilizing key intermediates (∗COOH, ∗CH2O) via electronic interactions, whereas EWGs reduce active site charge density and weaken intermediate adsorption. Furthermore, 13CO2 isotope labeling experiments (detecting 13CH4, m/z = 17) and alternating CO2/Ar flow controls confirmed that CH4 products originate from CO2 reduction rather than catalyst decomposition; Inductively coupled plasma MS (ICP-MS) analysis revealed metal impurities (e.g., Cu) in the catalyst <0.5 μg g−1, ruling out metal interference in catalytic performance.

N-doped graphene quantum dots (NGQDs) constitute an important variant of GQDs.By deliberately introducing N atoms into the carbon lattice, entirely new electronic properties have been introduced. These nanostructures typically possess a slightly reduced thickness ranging from 0.7 to 1.8 nm (equivalent to 1–3 graphene layers) and a narrower lateral size distribution of 1–3 nm. They largely retain the hexagonal morphology and the fundamental honeycomb framework of pristine graphene. The substitutional doping of N atoms into the sp2 carbon matrix induces significant changes in the local electronic structure and surface chemical properties. This is primarily characterized by a greatly increased density of N-containing defect sites, particularly at the edges of the nanostructure. Consequently, the intensity ratio of the D band to the G band (ID/IG) in Raman spectroscopy, which is a common indicator of defect density, is markedly higher for NGQDs compared to their undoped counterparts. Wu and his team provided conclusive evidence of successful N doping through detailed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis and comparative surface characterization.17 The XPS survey spectra of NGQDs clearly revealed the presence of a distinct N1s peak, which is entirely absent in the spectra of pure GQDs. High-resolution deconvolution of this N1s peak allowed for the quantification of different N bonding configurations: pyridinic N was identified at a binding energy of 398.5 eV and constituted approximately 3.9 at% of the material; pyrrolic N was found at 400.0 eV and accounted for about 1.2 at%; and graphitic (or quaternary) N was detected at 401.2 eV, making up roughly 0.9 at%. The total N content was quantified to be approximately 6.0 at%.

Electrocatalytic evaluation of these NGQDs demonstrated their outstanding proficiency in CO2RRs. Across a broad window of cathodic operating potentials, the total FE for CO2 reduction products could reach values as high as 90%. Notably, the selectivity toward valuable multi-carbon (C2+) products was significant, with the combined selectivity for ethylene (C2H4) and ethanol (C2H5OH) reaching up to 45%. More specifically, at an applied potential of −0.75 V vs. RHE, ethylene emerged as the predominant hydrocarbon product, attaining a maximum FE of 31%. When the potential was slightly increased to −0.78 V vs. RHE, the total FE for multi-carbon oxygenates peaked at 26%, within which ethanol was the major component, contributing 16% of the total efficiency. To unequivocally establish that N doping is the fundamental source of the enhanced catalytic activity in these materials, subsequent studies referenced in reports employed complementary techniques, including XPS and rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) experiments. These analyses determined an electron transfer number close to 4 during the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), strongly suggesting a direct four-electron pathway. This data further corroborated the hypothesis that, as the primary active centers, carbon atoms are those adjacent to pyridinic N sites, facilitating the efficient activation and reduction of molecular oxygen.83,84

1D

One-dimensional carbon materials play a pivotal role in electrocatalytic applications, with their core advantages stemming from unique axial electron transport properties and high aspect ratio geometries. Substances like CNTs and CNFs can essentially be regarded as seamless tubular or fibrous structures formed by curled or stacked sp2-hybridized carbon atoms. This architecture endows them with exceptional mechanical strength, outstanding axial conductivity (enabling efficient one-dimensional electron migration along the tube walls), and rich surface chemical tunability. In electrocatalytic reactions, one-dimensional structures not only provide “electronic highways” for rapid charge transfer, significantly reducing transport resistance during reactions, but their vast specific surface area and open pore structures also facilitate reactant diffusion and product desorption. More importantly, their one-dimensional confinement effect provides an ideal site for anchoring metal active sites (such as single atoms). This effectively stabilizes metal species, prevents their agglomeration, and optimizes the adsorption energy of reaction intermediates through metal-support electron interactions. Consequently, it synergistically enhances the catalyst’s activity, selectivity, and stability.

CNTs, as a prominent class of carbon-based nanomaterials, are primarily divided into two distinct structural forms: SWNTs and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWNTs). The fundamental architectural principle underlying CNTs is based on the hexagonal carbon lattice characteristic of graphene, which serves as the foundational building unit. For SWCNTs, converting a planar graphene sheet into a cylindrical nanotube creates the structure, with carbon atoms in a perfectly regular hexagonal lattice pattern, analogous to a cylindrically wrapped graphene sheet without any seams or defects. The carbon atoms within this structure are interconnected via strong covalent bonds, resulting in an exceptionally stable and mechanically robust closed-loop tubular configuration. MWNTs, in contrast, comprise multiple concentric layers of these single-walled nanotubes nested within one another. Each individual layer maintains its own independent closed-loop graphene-derived structure, and the interaction between adjacent concentric layers is governed primarily by relatively weak van der Waals forces, which allow for interlayer sliding while maintaining structural coherence. Beyond this basic cylindrical geometry, the precise atomic arrangement—defined by the chiral vector (n, m)—dictates whether an SWNT exhibits metallic or semiconducting electronic behavior, a property critically important for nanoelectronics and optoelectronic applications. The diameter and chirality of CNTs directly influence their bandgap, electrical conductivity, and optical absorption characteristics. Moreover, structural defects such as pentagon-heptagon pairs (Stone-Wales defects), vacancies, or functional group attachments can significantly alter their mechanical strength, chemical reactivity, and electronic transport properties. In MWNTs, interlayer coupling, though weak, can induce electronic modulation and affect thermal transport, while the presence of interlayer bridging or cross-linking can enhance mechanical load transfer between shells. From a topological perspective, CNTs represent a seamless and continuous all-carbon architecture with sp2 hybridization dominating throughout the lattice, conferring remarkable axial stiffness and tensile strength—often exceeding that of steel—while maintaining exceptional flexibility and resilience. Their hollow interior and high aspect ratio also facilitate the encapsulation of molecules or serve as nanochannels for mass transport, making them suitable for catalytic, sensing, and filtration applications. Furthermore, the ability to chemically functionalize the inner or outer surfaces—through covalent bonding, π-π stacking, or non-covalent interactions—allows tailored integration into composite materials, biological systems, and energy storage devices without compromising their intrinsic structural integrity. In summary, the structural elegance of CNTs lies not only in their well-defined graphene-derived cylindrical form but also in the tunability of their chirality, defect engineering, and hierarchical multi-walled organization. These features collectively enable CNTs to exhibit outstanding mechanical, electrical, thermal, and chemical properties, underpinning their widespread utility in fields ranging from reinforced nanocomposites and field-effect transistors to thermal interface materials and electrochemical catalysts.85,86,87

In a significant study conducted by Shuang and collaborators,88 the research team successfully designed and constructed five different types of Ni-doped carbon-anchored SACs on nanotube substrates (NiPc/CNT, H2-Ni/CNT, H3-Ni/CNT, 2-Ni/CNT, 3-Ni/CNT), each with distinct local coordination environments around nickel active centers. As shown in Figure 7, charge density difference (CDD) analysis revealed obvious electron transfer from catalysts to CO2: H2-Ni/CNT exhibited strong CO2 adsorption (energy of −0.25 eV) with notable O-C-O bond angle variation (evidenced by intense yellow electron enrichment zones), indicating enhanced CO2 activation; 2-Ni/CNT showed the most prominent electron transfer, matching its superior electronic properties. Graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) are strip-like nanomaterials with a quasi-one-dimensional (quasi-1D) morphology, derived from the two-dimensional graphene lattice. They consist of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal honeycomb structure, typically terminated by either armchair or zigzag edges, which critically determine their electronic and magnetic properties. A common synthesis strategy involves the longitudinal unzipping or cleavage of MWNTs, resulting in structures that possess micron-scale lengths but confine their width to the nanoscale regime. This structural anisotropy enables GNRs to achieve a synergistic integration of the most desirable properties from both 2D graphene and 1D CNTs. Beyond conventional hexagonal rings, recent advances in bottom-up synthesis—such as on-surface coupling and cyclodehydrogenation—allow the precise incorporation of nonhexagonal carbon motifs (e.g., 5/7/5 ring units) into GNR backbones, which can significantly alter their aromatic character, induce localized electronic states, and modulate band structures. Similarly, substitutional doping with heteroatoms like N enables fine-tuning of GNRs' electronic properties, with distinct configurations (e.g., graphitic-N vs. pyridinic-N) imparting different charge distributions and gap states. These beneficial characteristics include excellent compatibility with various heteroatoms for doping, exceptional electrical conductivity, coupled with an ultra-high specific surface area, which is particularly advantageous for catalytic processes. It is particularly noteworthy that GNRs produced via chemical oxidation methods are endowed with a high density of oxygen-containing functional groups along their edges and basal planes, which endows them with a strong affinity for the adsorption of ions and polar molecules. Moreover, the edge topology and defect engineering—such as the introduction of pentagon-heptagon pairs or extended pyridine units—can further enhance their chemical reactivity and provide anchoring sites for catalytic metal centers, as demonstrated in recent on-surface synthesized systems. These structural nuances highlight the versatility of GNRs as designer materials whose physicochemical properties can be atomically tailored through precise synthetic control, paving the way for applications in nanoelectronics, sensing, and energy conversion.90,91,92,93,94,95

2D

Two-dimensional carbon materials are a star in the field of electrocatalysis, characterized most notably by their atomically thin thickness and nearly infinite lateral dimensions. This exposes an enormous specific surface area and a highly uniform in-plane sp2 carbon lattice. The most representative graphene and its derivatives (such as GO) possess extraordinary electronic properties (e.g., extremely high carrier mobility), outstanding mechanical flexibility, and surfaces amenable to chemical modification. Their two-dimensional planar structure provides an ideal platform for constructing highly efficient catalytic interfaces: on one hand, it offers a maximized substrate for uniform distribution of active sites, where nearly every carbon atom can potentially participate in surface reactions; on the other hand, their highly conjugated π-electron system not only facilitates rapid charge transfer at the interface but also engages in strong electronic interactions with supported metal active centers. This effectively modulates their electronic structure, thereby optimizing adsorption strength for reaction intermediates. Furthermore, through stacking or assembly, two-dimensional carbon materials can form three-dimensional structures with tunable interlayer spacing and abundant nanoscale channels. This approach not only addresses the agglomeration issues of monolayer materials but also creates favorable conditions for mass transport of reactants, demonstrating significant potential in electrocatalytic reactions requiring high current densities.

The defining structure of graphene is a single-atom-thick, two-dimensional layer of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb lattice, with a characteristic C-C bond length of about 0.142 nm. This unique structure endows it with a theoretical specific surface area as high as 2630 m2/g. Its remarkable electronic properties, such as an ambient electron mobility exceeding 15,000 cm2/(V·s), are primarily conferred by the extensive, continuous sp2-hybridized carbon network that constitutes its surface. Recent studies on GNRs further demonstrate how precise control over edge topology—such as the incorporation of nonalternant 5/7/5 ring motifs or N-doped edge extensions—can systematically tune electronic band structures and introduce localized gap states, illustrating the profound impact of atomic-level structural engineering on graphene-derived materials. The material’s electronic and structural characteristics can be finely tuned through controllable stacking facilitated by interlayer van der Waals forces, as well as through the intentional introduction of lattice defects and edge functional groups—such as carboxyl (-COOH) and epoxy groups—which significantly influence its electrical conductivity and chemical reactivity. Moreover, heteroatom doping, particularly N in graphitic or pyridinic configurations, not only modulates charge carrier density but also creates active sites for surface reactions, enhancing graphene’s functionality in catalytic and sensing applications. Furthermore, the ultra-thin, nearly two-dimensional nature of graphene facilitates exceptionally rapid in-plane charge transport and effectively suppresses volume expansion during electrochemical processes, making it an ideal support material for electrocatalysis. Additionally, the ability to construct well-defined one-dimensional GNRs via bottom-up synthesis—as exemplified by on-surface Wurtz coupling and cyclodehydrogenation—enables the integration of nonhexagonal rings and heteroatoms into the carbon lattice, thereby extending property tunability beyond conventional defect engineering. These advancements highlight how targeted structural modifications at the atomic scale—ranging from edge functionalization and topological defect insertion to precise heteroatom placement—can tailor graphene’s physicochemical properties for specific applications in nanoelectronics, energy storage, and electrocatalysis.97,98,99

Graphdiyne, another emerging carbon allotrope, possesses a unique two-dimensional planar conjugated framework featuring uniformly distributed hybrid carbon channels composed of sp- and sp2-hybridized carbon atoms, with a characteristic pore size of approximately 0.52 nm. It boasts a theoretical specific surface area of 1947 m2/g. Its structure incorporates alkyne bonds and benzene ring units, and it exhibits an exceptionally high electron mobility of up to 2 × 105 cm2/(V·s). The distinctive porous architecture of graphdiyne facilitates rapid ion transport, and the interlayer π-π interactions allow for tunable charge carrier migration, which collectively effectively suppresses the aggregation of catalytically active sites. Notably, graphdiyne’s sp-sp2 hybridized carbon network forms an extended π-conjugated system with 50% acetylenic linkages, distinguishing it from purely sp2-hybridized graphene and endowing it with a tunable direct bandgap ranging from 0.46 to 1.22 eV. This semiconducting property, combined with its high charge carrier mobility and intrinsic porosity, makes graphdiyne particularly suitable for photocatalytic and electrocatalytic applications. Furthermore, the presence of diacetylenic (-C≡C-C≡C-) linkages enhances π-electron delocalization, which reduces charge recombination rates and promotes efficient electron transport across the catalyst surface. In addition to its electronic and structural advantages, graphdiyne exhibits remarkable stability and confinement effects due to its well-ordered nanoporous framework. The uniformly distributed triangular macropores act as macrocyclic ligands, enabling strong interactions with metal atoms, clusters, or nanoparticles, thereby stabilizing metastable catalytic species and preventing their aggregation. This confinement effect, coupled with the material’s high acetylene bond density, allows graphdiyne to effectively anchor single atoms, nanoclusters, and nanoparticles, optimizing active site distribution and enhancing catalytic durability. The material’s high surface area—reaching up to 1150 m2 g−1 in practice—provides abundant sites for reactant adsorption and activation, which is crucial for processes such as CO2 reduction. Its layered yet porous morphology also facilitates mass diffusion and ion transport, making it an ideal matrix for energy storage and conversion devices. These structural and functional attributes collectively position graphdiyne as a versatile and robust platform for next-generation catalytic and energy-related applications.102,103,104,105,106,107,108

3D

Porous carbon networks represent a class of advanced carbon materials characterized by their three-dimensional, hierarchically interconnected framework that incorporates pores across multiple scales. These pores are classified as macropores (>50 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and micropores (<2 nm). Within this hierarchical system, macropores act as primary conduits for the rapid diffusion of reactants and products, while mesopores play a critical role in efficient mass transport and the exposure of embedded active sites. Moreover, the integrated multi-scale pore system further optimizes mass transfer efficiency, laying a solid foundation for rapid, effective, and efficient reactions in fields such as electrocatalysis. This sophisticated porosity endows the material with an exceptionally high specific surface area, reaching up to 164.3 m2/g, thereby providing abundant active sites for electrochemical reactions. From a structural perspective, this hierarchical pore architecture is not merely a simple superposition of pores but rather forms an interconnected and ordered mass transfer network. The macroporous framework constitutes macroscopic transport channels, the mesoporous network optimizes intermediate-scale diffusion, and the microporous system significantly increases the loading density of active sites through its vast internal surface area. Notably, the synergistic interaction among pores of different scales creates a three-tier transport system of “channels–buffer zones–reaction zones,” which markedly reduces mass transfer resistance and prevents the shielding of active sites. The surfaces of these carbon networks are often decorated with various oxygen-containing functional groups, such as hydroxyl (-OH) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups. These functional groups not only enhance the material’s hydrophilicity and interfacial properties but also contribute to excellent electronic conductivity by improving charge carrier mobility. Advanced characterization studies reveal that the distribution of these surface functional groups exhibits a gradient pattern: carboxyl groups dominate the inner walls of micropores, hydroxyl groups are more prevalent on mesopore surfaces, while macroporous regions show a sparser distribution of functional groups. This distribution ensures adequate wettability at reaction interfaces while avoiding the negative impact of excessive functionalization on conductivity. Further structural analysis indicates that the interconnected porous architecture also exhibits unique mechanical adaptability. During electrocatalytic processes, the material undergoes repeated volume changes, and its three-dimensional interconnected carbon skeleton can absorb stress through elastic deformation of the pore walls, thereby maintaining structural stability. This “rigid yet flexible” characteristic ensures that the material retains open pores and accessible active sites over long-term cycling. The interconnected porous structure serves multiple critical functions: it facilitates efficient mass transport and diffusion of reactant molecules to active sites, promotes effective product desorption and removal, and simultaneously suppresses the restacking of two-dimensional nanosheets, thereby preserving structural integrity and accessibility during prolonged operation. Recent advancements have further demonstrated that precise control over pore size distribution and surface chemistry can selectively promote specific reaction pathways under different potential windows, offering a new dimension for designing high-performance electrocatalysts.110,111,112

Chapter summary

Carbon-based catalysts are categorized into four-dimensional classes: 0D, 1D, 2D, and 3D. Each dimension leverages unique structural characteristics to exhibit distinct performance advantages in electrocatalytic CO2RR. These advantages are centered around complementary key metrics, including active site density, electron/ion transport efficiency, product selectivity, and structural stability. The specific features and advantages of each dimension are as follows:

Zero-dimensional carbon-based catalysts, centered on carbon quantum dots (CQDs, GQDs, CNDs), feature particle sizes below 10 nm. They combine quantum confinement effects with abundant surface functional groups/doping sites, serving as highly efficient active units or modifying additives in CO2RR. Structurally, they exhibit ultra-high specific surface area and surface-to-volume ratio, with surfaces rich in hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups, facilitating heteroatom (N, P, S, and so forth) doping and metal site loading. They enable precise regulation of electronic structures and reduction of CO2 activation energy barriers. The quantum confinement effect also endows them with unique optical and electronic properties, optimizing the adsorption/desorption behavior of reaction intermediates. Its core advantages include significantly enhancing the ECSA and increasing active site density in composite catalysts when used as a modifier. Surface functional groups and doped sites effectively suppress HER side reactions and improve CO2RR product selectivity. Size effects enable thorough electrolyte interface contact, accelerating ion conduction and charge transfer. It also serves as an anchoring site for metal nanoparticles, preventing metal agglomeration and enhancing catalyst stability. For instance, 1.5 wt % CQDs-Cu1Zn2 achieves a FECO of 92.5% at −1.3 V vs. RHE, maintaining over 80% FE within 24 h while N-MoS2@NCDs-180 exhibits a CO reduction onset overpotential of only 130 mV, achieving 90.2% CO FE at −0.9 V vs. RHE with a CO/H2 FE ratio of 12:1.

One-dimensional carbon-based catalysts, represented by CNTs and GNRs, feature tubular/ribbon-like structures composed of sp2-hybridized carbon. Their core advantages lie in directional electron transport and structural support from high aspect ratios. These structures exhibit excellent axial conductivity, forming “electron highways” that significantly reduce charge transport resistance. High aspect ratios yield large specific surface areas and open pore structures, facilitating CO2 diffusion and product desorption. The one-dimensional confinement effect also provides stable anchoring sites for single atoms/metal alloys and suppresses active site agglomeration. Core advantages include: Accelerated reaction kinetics and enhanced current density through directed electron transport - Functioning as a carrier to synergize with metal/metal alloys, modulating the electronic structure of active sites and enhancing CO2 adsorption and activation. They can form three-dimensional porous networks to further increase active site exposure. Their high mechanical strength maintains structural stability during prolonged electrolysis. For example, Ag85Cu15/CNT exhibits a CO FE of 98.6% at 0.7 V vs. RHE, retaining 86% initial FE after 18 h continuous electrolysis. Ni@GNRs achieved 96% CO FE at −1.1 V vs. RHE with a CO partial current density of 44 mA cm−2, maintaining over 90% FE across a wide potential range.

Two-dimensional carbon-based catalysts, centered on graphene, GO, and graphyne (GDY), feature atomically thin planar structures. Their core characteristics include ultra-large specific surface areas, high in-plane conductivity, and tunable interlayer/surface electron interactions. Structurally, single-atom layers/few-layer structures expose abundant surface atoms, with nearly all carbon atoms serving as potential active sites. The conjugated π electron system enables rapid in-plane charge transfer while facilitating strong electronic interactions with supported metal active centers, optimizing intermediate adsorption strength. Electronic structure and interlayer spacing can be precisely tuned through stacking, doping, and defect engineering to suppress material agglomeration. Core advantages include: ultra-high theoretical specific surface areas (graphene: 2630 m2/g, GDY: 1947 m2/g) providing abundant active sites - surface susceptibility to heteroatom doping (N, Fe, and so forth) and single-atom loading, forming efficient active centers such as M-N4 novel 2D materials such as GDY possess inherent channels and sp-sp2 hybridized structures, combining semiconductor properties with high electron mobility. This enables confined catalysis for CO2RR, lowers onset potential, and enhances product selectivity. Their layered structures can construct catalytic interfaces that suppress HER while promoting CO2RR intermediates. For instance, Fe/NG-750 achieves 80% CO2 reduction efficiency (CO FE) at −0.60 V vs. RHE with an onset overpotential of only 0.3 V, maintaining over 75% CO FE for 10 h. Graphityl-modified Cu (GDY/Cu) reduces the onset potential for CO2 reduction to CO by 0.16 V, effectively suppresses the HCOOH side reaction, and significantly enhances CO FE.

Three-dimensional carbon-based catalysts, exemplified by porous carbon networks and MC fiber/nanosheet composites, feature interconnected multi-scale pore networks within a 3D framework. They integrate the advantages of 0D/1D/2D carbon materials, characterized by efficient mass/electron transport and industrial-scale current density adaptability. Structurally, they possess macro/meso/micro multi-scale pore systems, with macropores serving as rapid transport channels for reactants/products. products, mesopores enhance active site exposure, and micropores increase specific surface area. The three-dimensionally interconnected carbon skeleton combines high electrical conductivity with excellent mechanical stability, effectively suppressing stacking of 2D nanosheets and maintaining reaction interface continuity. Surface functionalization or loading of single-atom/metal sites enables synergistic optimization of activity and selectivity. Core advantages include: - Multiporous channels drastically reduce mass transfer resistance, accommodating high current density demands for CO2RR and meeting industrial requirements - High specific surface area and abundant active site loading capacity enhance catalytic activity - Conductive framework accelerates electron transfer and lowers reaction overpotential exceptional mechanical stability that maintains structural integrity during prolonged, high-current electrolysis while resisting acidic/alkaline electrolyte environments, enabling high-density single-atom loading and improved atomic utilization. For instance, Ad-Ir-1.4 (single-atom Ir loaded on 3D porous carbon) maintains over 95% FECO (CO FE) at −1.07 to −1.68 V vs. RHE, with a CO partial current density of 572.4 mA cm−2 at −1.68 V. Ni-N/IPCF@CS achieves nearly 100% CO FE under acidic conditions (pH = 2), with a peak partial current density of 572.4 mA cm−2 and a single-pass carbon efficiency of 89.2%, meeting industrial-scale CO2RR requirements.

Modification strategies for 0D carbon-based materials

0D carbon-based materials exhibit pronounced size effects, large specific surface areas, and highly dispersed active sites. Their modification centers on the precise construction of single-atom active sites and microenvironmental regulation to maximize the site exposure advantages inherent in zero-dimensional structures.

Coordination environment modification

In 0D carbon-based SACs, the coordination environment—defined by the type and number of atoms surrounding the active metal center—is a core determinant of CO2RR performance, directly governing the catalyst’s electronic structure and surface reaction energy barriers. Precise tailoring of the coordination environment enables optimization of CO2 adsorption and binding strength of key intermediates (e.g., ∗COOH and ∗CO), thereby directing catalytic behavior. Primary strategies include reducing N coordination numbers and introducing diverse coordinating atoms.

N coordination number regulation: M-Nx (x = 2–4) sites form the core structural units of 0D carbon-based SACs. Strategically reducing the coordination number increases the metal’s vacant 3d orbitals, enhancing its electron deficiency. Wang et al. precisely engineered the coordination environment of Co SACs using MOFs as precursors, precisely controlled via pyrolysis temperature. Within the 800°C–1000°C range, they obtained zero-dimensional carbon-based SACs featuring Co-N4, Co-N3, and Co-N2 coordination structures. Among these, the Co-N2 structure exhibited a shifted d-band center, achieving a FECO of 95% at −0.9 V (vs. RHE) in 0.5 M KHCO3 electrolyte with a partial current density of 45.0 mA·cm−2120; Gong et al. synthesized 0D NiSA-Nx-C (x = 2,3,4) SACs with tunable Ni-N coordination numbers by introducing polypyrrole into MgNi-MOF-74 precursors and controlling pyrolysis. The CO-COOH formation at Ni-N2 sites exhibits a low free energy of 0.32 eV, achieving a CO FE of 98% at −0.7 V (vs. RHE) with a TOF of 1622 h−1. Axial chemical environment engineering modification

The axial chemical environment (atomic/group interactions perpendicular to the coordination plane) of metal active sites in 0D carbon-based materials is a key factor in regulating their electronic structure. By establishing axial coordination between metal atoms and zero-dimensional carbon carriers or introducing specific axial functional groups, the splitting energy and energy-level distribution of metal d orbitals can be altered, thereby optimizing the adsorption and conversion kinetics of reaction intermediates. Zhang et al. synthesized a 0D SACs featuring an Fe-N5 structure. Beyond coordinating with four in-plane N atoms, the Fe atom also coordinates with one axial pyrrole N. This axial pyrrole N maintains Fe’s +3 oxidation state, whose p orbitals form p-d coupling with Fe’s d orbitals. This coupling reduces the electron density in Fe’s 3d orbitals, weakens the binding energy between ∗CO and Fe, and prevents CO poisoning. At a low overpotential of −0.35 V (vs. RHE), it achieves 92% CO FE and a TOF of 580 h−1, significantly outperforming traditional Fe-N4 structures.128

Hu et al. synthesized 0D Fe-CNP SACs via SiO2-coated pyrolysis. Their hierarchical porous carbon support provided abundant active sites and efficient mass transfer pathways, achieving 98.8% CO FE at −0.8 V (vs. RHE) while maintaining over 95% selectivity during 30 h of continuous operation.131

Modification strategies for 1D carbon-based materials

1D carbon-based materials possess unique one-dimensional tubular/fibrous structures that combine high conductivity with oriented mass transfer pathways. The core of their modification lies in leveraging these one-dimensional structural advantages through coordination environment regulation, axial chemical environment engineering, and defect/pore structure optimization to further enhance active site utilization and catalytic kinetics.

Coordination environment modulation

By controlling the type and number of coordination atoms at active sites on 1D carbon supports, their electronic structure and intermediate adsorption energy can be precisely optimized, enhancing CO2RR activity and selectivity.

N coordination number modulation: Atomically dispersed metal-Nx sites serve as core active centers in 1D carbon-based materials, where coordination number regulation significantly impacts catalytic performance. Y. Kim et al. prepared a series of Ni-N/NCNT catalysts via dispersion methods. The Ni-N/NCNT catalyst loaded with 1.37 wt % Ni achieved a CO FE of 97.83% at −0.56 V (vs. RHE), while the 3.02 wt % Ni-loaded catalyst achieved 97.71% CO FE at −0.41 V (vs. RHE). Performance enhancement stemmed from increased Ni-Nx active sites and optimized electronic structure, accelerating CO2 adsorption and ∗COOH formation kinetics132; F. Pan et al. prepared the Fe-N/CNT@GNR-2 composite catalyst by oxidatively exfoliating CNTs with KMnO4. Fe-N4 sites anchored on both the CNT surface and the edges/base planes of GNRs achieved a CO FE of 95% at −0.76 V (vs. RHE) in 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte, with a partial current density of 22.6 mA cm−2. The synergistic effect of one-dimensional carbon supports optimized active site accessibility and mass transfer efficiency.133

Ligand atom type regulation: Introducing different types of heteroatom ligands can modulate the electronic properties of active sites. P. Sharma et al. synthesized the NCNTs-ACN-850 catalyst using acetonitrile as a carbon-N source via liquid chemical vapor deposition. Its surface is rich in pyrrole N and graphitic N-doped sites, achieving 80% CO FE at −0.7 V (vs. RHE) in 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte. The synergistic interaction between graphitic N and pyrrole N promotes proton-electron pair transfer and lowers the formation energy barrier for ∗COOH.52

Axial chemical environment engineering modification

Constructing axial functional groups or coordination structures on the surface of 1D carbon carriers can further regulate the microenvironment and electronic structure of active sites, thereby enhancing catalytic selectivity. Z. Ma et al. prepared the Au/Py-CNTs-O composite catalyst by axially grafting pyridine groups onto CNT surfaces via diazotization. The pyridine groups synergistically interact with Au nanoparticles, stabilizing the ∗ COOH intermediates via hydrogen bonding. In 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte, the CO FE remained stable at 93% within the −0.58 V to −0.98 V potential range, significantly outperforming the ungrafted Au/CNTs-O catalyst (89%).134

Carrier defects and pore structure modification

Defects and pore structures in 1D carbon-based materials can further increase the number of active sites, optimize mass transfer efficiency, and enhance CO2RR performance.

Defect engineering: Defect sites can serve as active centers or modulate electronic structures to enhance CO2RR activity. J. Wu et al. prepared NCNTs via liquid chemical vapor deposition, whose surfaces are rich in N defect sites. Rogers et al. employed a bottom-up synthesis to embed Au nanoparticles within a GNR matrix, forming a Mott-Schottky heterojunction interface. The low work function of GNRs facilitates charge transfer to Au nanoparticles, regulating the onset potential of catalytic reactions. This approach achieved a CO FE exceeding 90%, with catalytic activity 100 times higher than conventional amorphous carbon/Au composites.136

Pore Structure Engineering: Hierarchical pore structures optimize mass transfer pathways, enhancing diffusion efficiency between CO2 and intermediates. H. Macroporous structures serve as mass transfer channels, while micropores and mesopores provide abundant active sites. while N-doped pyrrole N-sites enhanced CO2 adsorption, achieving 81% FE for HCOOH in 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte.137 Q. 2D carbon-based materials possess ultra-large specific surface areas, atomic-scale thicknesses, and high electrical conductivity. Scaling up and challenges of electrochemical CO2 reduction technologies

As a key pathway toward carbon neutrality, electrochemical CO2 reduction technologies have achieved substantial progress in both laboratory research and industrial demonstrations in recent years.155,156 However, current technological developments still face multiple challenges for large-scale commercial application, requiring breakthroughs in reaction engineering, catalyst systems, and economic viability.

In reactor engineering, conventional H-type electrolysers typically operate at current densities below 100 mA/cm2 due to mass transfer limitations, failing to meet industrial requirements.157 Novel designs such as microfluidic reactors and membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) have now elevated current densities to 200–400 mA/cm2. Domestically, a system engineered by the Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, has demonstrated continuous and stable operation for over 1,000 h, providing a crucial reference for engineering scale-up. Internationally, an industrial-scale unit developed by Denmark’s Haldor Topsoe has reached a CO production capacity of 200 Nm3/h, demonstrating the feasibility of technological scaling.

The selection of catalyst systems directly impacts reaction efficiency and product distribution, while the large-scale preparation of catalysts is a core prerequisite for transitioning from laboratory to industrial applications. Although precious metal catalysts exhibit superior performance, non-precious metal catalysts hold greater industrial potential due to cost considerations. Cu-based catalysts exhibit unique advantages in synthesizing multi-carbon products. Meanwhile, carbon-based SACs such as nickel and iron demonstrate outstanding selectivity for CO due to maximized atomic utilization and tunable active sites. Breakthroughs in their large-scale preparation technology are crucial for advancing the industrialization of electrochemical CO2 reduction.

The scalable production of carbon-based SACs has attracted focused research efforts. A notable advance was reported by Yang et al.,158 who devised a universal method employing commercial carbon black as the support. This approach, involving the adsorption of metal-pyrrole complexes onto the carbon substrate and subsequent pyrolysis in Ar, enabled the mass production of over 1 kg of SACs with high metal loading. The resulting Ni-NCB) demonstrated a FECO of 98.9% at −1.2 V vs. RHE. This work confirms the feasibility of large-scale SACs manufacture without compromising performance, offering critical guidance for industrial translation, particularly in selecting low-cost supports and optimizing scalable synthesis protocols. Furthermore, the self-supporting electrode design offers new avenues for industrial catalyst applications. Yang et al.152 fabricated a flexible self-supporting nickel single-atom-modified porous carbon membrane (NiSA/PCFM membrane) that functions directly as a GDE without requiring polymer binders. This design not only avoids the masking of active sites by binders during conventional catalyst coating but also establishes a stable gas-liquid -solid three-phase interface. In a flow cell system, it achieved stable operation for 120 consecutive hours, significantly outperforming the 40-h performance decay cycle observed in conventional H-type electrolytic cells. The GDE and flow cell system are core components of industrial CO2 electrolysis equipment, and this design offers a feasible solution for integrating catalysts with industrial electrolysis systems.

Current research focuses on enhancing the activity and stability of these catalysts, with some systems achieving stable operation exceeding 500 h under industrial-scale current densities. Recent advances exemplify the development of high-performance SACs approaching industrial benchmarks. Rong et al.127 constructed a vacancy-defect Ni-N3-V site by selectively removing oxygen from a Ni-N3O precursor, which achieved a CO partial current density of 65 mA/cm2 and >90% FE at −0.9 V vs. RHE, maintaining stability for 14 h.121 In a separate approach, Wang et al.120 obtained a Co-N2 structure through precise pyrolysis temperature control, delivering a CO current density of 45 mA/cm2 with 95% efficiency at the same potential. These works collectively provide valuable references for selecting highly active catalysts suited for industrial-scale CO2RR applications.

From an economic perspective, electricity costs significantly impact technological commercialization. Domestic industrial electricity prices exhibit substantial regional variations, with more competitive rates available in regions abundant in renewable energy. CO2 feedstock costs also vary considerably depending on the source, with industrial waste gas capture being relatively low-cost. Notably, multiple demonstration projects have been launched domestically, such as Sinopec’s engineering practices in carbon capture and utilization, providing crucial data for techno-economic assessments.

Regarding system engineering, product separation and purification processes still require optimization. Developing tailored separation technologies for different product characteristics is essential for reducing overall energy consumption. Currently, numerous domestic and international demonstration projects are exploring optimal system integration solutions.

From an industrialization perspective, this technology is transitioning from laboratory to industrial scale. Internationally, companies have established production facilities capable of processing tens of thousands of tons annually, while domestic enterprises and research institutions are actively advancing technology transfer. Over the next 5–10 years, breakthroughs in key technologies and the maturation of the industrial chain are expected to enable the commercial application of electrochemical CO2 reduction in specific scenarios, providing technical support for carbon neutrality goals. This process requires sustained investment and collaborative innovation from industry, academia, and research institutions.

Summary and outlook

Summary of research content

This study systematically investigates the application of carbon-based materials in the electrocatalytic CO2RR, focusing on their structural characteristics, heteroatom doping strategies, and catalytic mechanisms. It provides theoretical foundations and material design directions for developing efficient CO2 conversion technologies. Key conclusions are as follows: CO2 molecules exhibit high stability, requiring single-electron transfer to form CO2− radicals during electrolytic reduction. These radicals undergo proton-coupled electron transfer to generate key intermediates (COOH or OCHO), ultimately converting into C1 or C2+ products. Notably, ∗CO adsorption strength critically determines product selectivity. Regarding performance evaluation, FEFE, overpotential, current density, and Tafel slope serve as core metrics for assessing catalytic performance. Carbon-based materials emerge as ideal catalysts for CO2RR due to their excellent conductivity, high stability, and low cost. Carbon structures across different dimensions exhibit unique catalytic behaviors: zero-dimensional materials (e.g., CQDs) enhance CO2 adsorption through surface functional groups and quantum effects; one-dimensional materials (e.g., CNTs) provide rapid electron transport pathways and edge-active sites; two-dimensional materials (e.g., graphene and GDY) feature high exposure of active sites and delocalized π-electron structures; while three-dimensional porous carbons alleviate mass transfer limitations and increase active site density. Heterogeneous atom doping serves as an effective strategy to enhance the CO2RR performance of carbon materials. Doping with N, B, P, S, and other elements modulates the electronic structure of the carbon framework, introduces defects, and expands interlayer spacing, thereby optimizing intermediate adsorption and lowering reaction energy barriers. Co-doping strategies (e.g., N-S and N-P) generate synergistic effects, further improving C2+ product selectivity and reaction kinetics. Regarding reaction pathways, in situ characterization and theoretical calculations confirm that COOH and OCHO are key intermediates for C1 product formation, while ∗CO dimerization is the critical step for C-C coupling to generate multi-carbon products. Suppressing HER side reactions is essential for enhancing CO2RR selectivity, achievable through increasing HER overpotential and introducing hydrophobic interfaces.

Research outlook

Despite the immense potential of carbon-based materials in CO2RR, several challenges remain. Over the next 3–5 years, future research is expected to focus on the following key directions. First, the active site density and intrinsic activity of current carbon-based catalysts require further enhancement. Precise synthesis methods—such as single-atom anchoring and M-N-C structural regulation—must be developed to finely tune the electronic structure and coordination environment of active sites at the atomic scale, while balancing catalytic activity and long-term stability. A specific technical pathway could adopt a “theory-driven, experiment-validated” innovation paradigm. Leveraging computational techniques like artificial intelligence (AI), DFT, and machine learning (ML), structural descriptors (e.g., φ′, σ, and φ) can be constructed. High-throughput screening methods can then rapidly identify promising catalyst candidates, replacing traditional trial-and-error approaches. This significantly reduces R&D costs while enhancing precision. In M-N-C structural regulation, the “BASED” theory can be introduced to integrate d-band centers, ICOHP, and molecular orbital parameters to precisely describe active site surface interactions. This enables precise anchoring of metal atoms like Fe and Co within the carbon framework. Concurrently, axial ligand modification weakens intermediate adsorption, further enhancing the catalyst’s intrinsic activity. This ensures low overpotentials and high durability in half-cell testing, leading to higher peak power densities in device applications.

Second, low selectivity for polycarbonate products (especially C2+) remains a critical bottleneck in CO2 reduction applications. Deeply elucidating the C-C coupling mechanism under operational conditions is paramount. This is achieved by combining strategies such as constructing dual active sites, regulating local ∗CO coverage, and leveraging confinement effects to enhance ∗CO dimerization efficiency while suppressing competing reactions. Innovative approaches and specific technical pathways include: First, overcoming limitations of traditional ∗CO dimerization mechanisms by utilizing high-temperature, high-pressure electrolytic cells to regulate reaction conditions. When temperatures exceed 125°C under high-pressure environments, the C-C coupling mechanism shifts from ∗CO dimerization to Fischer-Tropsch-type chain growth, significantly enhancing multi-carbon product selectivity. Second, establishing a synergistic “heteroatom co-doping-dual active site” system. For instance, N-S co-doped carbon materials loaded with Cu-Fe bimetallic sites regulate the electronic structure of the carbon framework through heteroatoms. Leveraging the synergistic effect of bimetallic sites optimizes local ∗CO coverage. while leveraging the confinement effect of three-dimensional porous carbon to trap ∗CO molecules near active sites, promoting ∗CO dimerization and suppressing competitive side reactions like HER. Third, designing MOF precursors enables the preparation of three-dimensional porous carbon-based catalysts with gradient pore size distributions, enhancing electrolyte permeability and ion transport efficiency to provide ample reactant supply and efficient mass transfer pathways for C-C coupling.

Third, the lack of in situ characterization techniques capable of directly correlating dynamic structural evolution with catalytic performance hinders rational catalyst design. Therefore, integrating advanced in situ/operando spectroscopic techniques with theoretical simulations is crucial for revealing true active sites and reaction pathways. Specific technical approaches include: First, employing high energy resolution fluorescence detection X-ray absorption near-edge structure (HERFD-XANES) technology developed at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, combined with Operando extended X-ray absorption fine structure(EXAFS) and synchrotron radiation infrared spectroscopy (SR-IRAS), to monitor real-time dynamic structural changes of active sites during CO2RR. Quantifying the influence of mass transfer conditions on active site evolution and deducing catalytic mechanisms from a reaction kinetics perspective; Second, we developed in situ characterization techniques adapted for high current density conditions, such as online in situ NMR methods compatible with industrial-scale current densities exceeding 100 mA cm−2. These techniques enable real-time quantification of electrolyte dynamics, product formation patterns, and water migration phenomena, overcoming the limitation of traditional in situ techniques that are only suitable for low current conditions and cannot reflect actual industrial reaction characteristics. Third, establish an integrated “in situ characterization-theoretical simulation” model. Combine dynamic structural data from in situ characterization with DFT calculations and ML models to precisely identify actual active sites and short-lived intermediates, providing direct experimental evidence for catalyst precision design.

In summary, carbon-based materials offer advantages such as structural tunability and abundant resources as CO2RR catalysts. Through material innovation and system optimization, they hold promise for advancing the practical application of CO2 electro-reduction technology, providing critical technical support for achieving carbon neutrality goals.
