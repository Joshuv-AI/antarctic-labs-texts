Aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads combined with hybridization chain reaction for detection of Bacillus cereus
Authors: Xiaoting Song, Changzheng Shi, Yunbin Lv, Xinmei Liu, Xiaomei Bie, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on the specific aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads and the hybrid chain reaction (HCR), a rapid detection method for Bacillus cereus was established. B. cereus competes with carboxylfluorescein-labeled cDNA (FAM-cDNA) to bind to biotin-labeled aptamer fixed on streptavidin magnetic beads. After magnetic separation, the released FAM-cDNA was used as an initiator to trigger HCR amplification, significantly enhancing the fluorescence signal. In the absence of B. cereus, the initiator binds to the aptamer, generating only a weak fluorescent signal. More importantly, graphene oxide (GO) can quench the fluorescence of unreacted FAM-cDNA, achieving a more accurate detection effect and thus enabling the detection of B. cereus at lower concentrations. This method has good specificity and can detect B. cereus as low as 5.4×101 CFU/mL. A linear response was observed across the concentration range of 5.4×105 CFU/mL-5.4×101 CFU/mL. The feasibility of this method was verified by detecting B. cereus in artificially contaminated milk samples. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Bacillus cereus, as a common foodborne pathogen, is widely present in various foods, such as rice, milk, and meat, etc1,2. When people accidentally consume contaminated food, due to the related toxins produced by B. cereus, it is very easy to cause serious food poisoning or foodborne diseases3,4. Due to its inherent characteristics, including facultative anaerobic conditions and the ability to produce endogenous spores and various toxins, B. cereus is considered to have serious impacts and cause corresponding hazards if not given sufficient attention during food processing5. Therefore, establishing an efficient detection strategy is of great significance for preventing and monitoring poisoning caused by the accidental ingestion of food contaminated with B. cereus.

There are various detection methods for B. cereus. Generally, the standard detection method is the bacterial culture method, which is regarded as the gold standard for identifying and differentiating bacteria6. However, this is a time-consuming, laborious and sensitive detection process, making it difficult to conduct on-site detection7. In recent years, in order to overcome the limitations of traditional detection methods in the detection and identification of pathogenic bacteria, a variety of different rapid methods with high sensitivity and specificity have been developed8. For instance, detection methods based on molecular biology, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and rolling cycle amplification (RCA), have been developed for the detection of B. cereus9,10. However, the reaction process requires complex primer design and expensive reagents or instruments, and complex cell disruption and nucleic acid extraction procedures need to be carried out in advance11. Moreover, incorrect results may be obtained due to contamination during the experiment12. Therefore, this method has certain limitations. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) based on antigen-antibody immune reactions has also achieved relatively rapid detection, but this method sometimes has low sensitivity, requires a long pretreatment time, and has a relatively high cost13. Therefore, in view of the limitations of current bacterial analysis strategies, there is an urgent need to explore a simple, low-cost and accurate detection method to monitor and control the spread of B. cereus in food and the environment.

Separation techniques utilizing magnetic nanoparticles have emerged as a highly promising sample pretreatment methodology in the field of bioanalysis. This technique uses magnetic particles functionalized with specific antibodies or aptamers to selectively isolate targets from complex mixture14,15. This method has the advantages of speed, efficiency, and minimal interference16. Consequently, various functionalized magnetic nanoparticle separation techniques have been employed for sample preparation, effectively enriching targets and removing interferents to facilitate detection17,18. For example, superparamagnetic nanoparticles were used to specifically capture Pseudomonas aeruginosa to achieve the determination of its sensitivity19 The specific binding and detection of Escherichia coli O157: H7 by using exonucase-assisted amplification and aptamer-functionalized magnetic bead-mediated auxiliary sensors20. The anti-Salmonella antibody fixed on the AG-magnetic beads can effectively concentrate Salmonella typhimurium, with a capture rate of 95%21.

Nucleic acid aptamers refer to specifically designed oligonucleotides produced via in vitro selection through the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) process, involving multiple rounds of screening and molecular evolution22. They can achieve specific binding with the target as ligands by forming complex three-dimensional structures such as convex rings, hairpins, or G-quadruplexes, showing high affinity, sometimes even exceeding the affinity between antigen and antibody23. Compared with antibodies, aptamers exhibit advantages such as small volume, low cost, high stability, easy synthesis, and labeling24. Furthermore, their remarkable versatility allows recognition of various targets, such as proteins25, nucleic acids26, cells27, and even bacteria28, making them increasingly attractive as antibody alternatives in detection technologies29. Moreover, the detection based on the aptamer will be combined with various signal output forms, such as fluorescence30, colorimetry31, and electrochemistry32. Among these, aptamer-based fluorescence detection has garnered particular attention due to its high sensitivity and low detection limit33. In such systems, enhancing the fluorescence signal while minimizing background interference is key to improving overall detection sensitivity.

To achieve higher detection sensitivity, a signal amplification process is usually required. HCR without enzyme participation and at constant temperature is suitable for application in further signal amplification due to its simplicity, stability, and excellent signal amplification capability34,35. HCR is a signal amplification technology, which utilizes cascaded hybridization events between nucleic acid probe molecules to drive an autonomous self-assembly process, ultimately generating elongated double-stranded DNA polymers36. In addition, the output signal during the HCR process depends on the long-chain DNA polymer generated during the reaction37. This reaction generates minimal byproducts, a characteristic that significantly reduces the possibility of non-specific reactions and false-positive signals38,39. This distinctive attribute endows the methodology with superior capability for analyzing complex samples while fulfilling stringent specificity demands in diagnostic applications. The process of HCR reaction is universal and can be combined with different signal output forms. HCR technology is mostly used to detect target nucleic acid chains. It can also be combined with aptamer technology to detect small biomolecular substances such as cell40, bacteria41, and mycotoxins42. Yu et al. developed a flow cytometry method combining immunomagnetic separation with HCR amplification for rapid detection of emetic B. cereus. The system employed probe-modified magnetic beads to capture cereulide-encoding gene ssDNA, triggering HCR-mediated signal amplification. This approach achieved 7.6 CFU/mL sensitivity in pure culture with excellent specificity43. Xu et al. used g-C3N4 nanosheets assisted upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) to detect Staphylococcus aureus. Based on the partial complementary sequence of specific aptamers, hairpin probes were designed to modify the surface of UCNPs. In the presence of the target bacteria, HCR reactions would occur. And it was far away from the g-C3N4 nanosheets to form a double helix structure, resulting in a decrease in its peroxidase activity, and the fluorescence recovery rate was proportional to the concentration of the target bacteria44. Furthermore, to enhance operational simplicity and integration, recent advances have focused on combining magnetic separation with microfluidic automation. For instance, Panphut et al. developed a superhydrophobic magnetic flow system utilizing aptamer-based technique for the semi-automatic electrochemical detection of Salmonella typhimurium in food samples, demonstrating a rapid integrated response45. Although the above research demonstrated that aptamers and HCR have a powerful synergistic effect in bacterial detection, they still face certain challenges, including complex separation steps, reliance on expensive instruments (such as flow cytometer), and the preparation of complex nanomaterials. These challenges have to some extent hindered the large-scale application of detection. Therefore, in order to solve these problems, there is an urgent need to develop a detection strategy that combines the high sensitivity of HCR with a simpler pretreatment mode. In addition, graphene oxide (GO), as a two-dimensional carbon nanomaterial, can adsorb oligonucleotides through π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding to achieve efficient quenching of labeled fluorescent groups46. Since double-stranded DNA is not stably adsorbed by it, it can maintain the double helix structure, show a high quenching efficiency, and achieve the reduction of background signals47,48. Based on this, this study aims to construct a novel integrated detection platform for B. cereus, which combines the capture ability of aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads, the signal amplification function of HCR, and the background signal suppression function of GO.

In conclusion, this study established a highly sensitive and selective detection method for B. cereus. First, it is necessary to prepare aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads, which can specifically bind B. cereus and the HCR reaction inducer FAM-cDNA. In the presence of the target, the supernatant obtained by magnetic separation will contain FAM-cDNA that has not bound to the aptamer, thereby inducing the HCR reaction. In the absence of a target, the supernatant obtained by magnetic separation contains a very small amount of FAM-cDNA, which hardly induces the HCR reaction. Ultimately, the system can be verified by measuring the fluorescence signal values generated after the reaction ends.

Results and discussion

Specificity verification of aptamers

According to relevant literature, the specific binding aptamers of B. cereus (Apt 13–18 and Apt B. c) were obtained49,50. The secondary structure of the aptamer was predicted using NUPACK online software (Fig. 1). The binding specificity between the aptamer and target bacteria was subsequently verified through an aptamer-functionalized gold nanoparticle (AuNPs) colorimetric assay. As shown in Fig. 2C, the colorimetric assay using Apt 13–18 demonstrated distinct visual differentiation: solutions containing positive strains exhibited a characteristic purple color shift, while negative strains maintained the original red coloration. This clear color contrast confirmed the specific binding affinity of the selected aptamer. The values of A630/A520 were measured by UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and there were significant differences between the positive strains and the negative strains (Fig. 2B). As shown in Fig. 2D, the experimental results verifying the binding specificity of Apt B. c demonstrated that following the reaction, the solution containing positive strains appeared purple in color, while those with no-target strains still maintained their original red color, showing a clear visual differentiation in the colorimetric assay. Furthermore, a statistically significant difference was observed between the A630/A520 absorbance ratios of positive and negative samples (Fig. 2B). This proves that both aptamers have good specificity for B. cereus. A comprehensive analysis of the reaction results of the two aptamers revealed that the specificity of Apt 13–18 was better than that of the Apt B. c (Fig. 2B).

Preparation of aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads and calculation of capture rate

The absorbance values at a wavelength of 260 nm (A260) of Bio-Apt before binding and supernatant separated after binding were compared by UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and the zeta potential changes of SA-MB before and after modification of Bio-Apt were measured by Zeta potentiometer. To verify whether Bio-Apt has been successfully modified onto the surface of SA-MB. As shown in Fig. 3A, A260 value were measured by UV-vis spectrophotometry before and after aptamer conjugation to SA-MB. Comparative analysis revealed a distinct maximum absorption peak at A260 for the free aptamer prior to conjugation. Following aptamer immobilization onto SA-MB, the supernatant obtained after magnetic separation exhibited a significant reduction at A260. This marked decrease at A260 clearly demonstrated successful conjugation between the aptamer and SA-MB. In addition, as shown in Fig. 3B, the Zeta potentials of the Bio-Apt, SA-MB, and MB-Apt were measured respectively. It was found that compared with SA-MB, the Zeta potential of MB-Apt shifted from –9.55 mV to –18.5 mV. Since Bio-Apt also showed a negative potential, binding Bio-Apt on the surface of SA-MB would further reduce its potential. Therefore, the result of Zeta potential further indicated that Bio-Apt had been successfully modified on the surface of SA-MB, laying the foundation for its subsequent application in the detection of B. cereus.

According to the prepared MB-Apt, it was incubated together with B. cereus. After incubation for a certain period of time, the supernatant obtained by magnetic separation was coated on a plate at an appropriate concentration, thereby calculating the capture rate of the functionalized magnetic bead for this bacterium. As shown in Fig. 3C, based on the capture rate, the capture rate of Apt 13–18 for this bacterium could reach 92.22%, and the capture rate of Apt B. c for this bacterium was 88.69%. In comparison under the same conditions, the capture efficiency of Apt 13–18 for this bacterium was higher than that of Apt B. c. A comprehensive analysis of the specificity and capture rate of the two aptamers once again proved that the effect of Apt 13–18 was better than that of Apt B. c. Subsequently, Apt 13–18 was selected as the specific aptamer of B. cereus for the experiment.

Verification of the HCR system

Based on the sequence of aptamer 13–18, complementary DNA (cDNA) and the hairpin probes (H1 and H2) required for HCR were synthesized as listed in Table 1. And again, the hairpin probes and HCR reaction were simulated using the NUPACK online software (Fig. 1C–E). It was observed that the HCR reaction could proceed effectively under the simulated conditions, generating extended double-stranded DNA sequences, thereby confirming the feasibility of the designed probe for the HCR implementation. HCR reactions were carried out using cDNA of different concentrations and the same amount of hairpin probes, as shown in Fig. 4A. HCR reactions did not occur when H1 and H2 were present alone or when H1 and H2 were present simultaneously. The HCR reaction occurred only when cDNA was also present, and as the concentration of cDNA increased, the length of the bands shown on the gel electrophoresis diagram also varied. As shown in Fig. 4B, based on the fluorescence spectrum, the optimal excitation wavelength and emission wavelength of carboxylfluorescin (FAM) labeled on the initiator cDNA were measured to be 495 nm and 520 nm, respectively, which were consistent with those in the literature51. Therefore, subsequent quantitative analysis was performed by recording the fluorescence emission spectra within the range of 510 nm-600 nm at the excitation wavelength of 495 nm. As shown in Fig. 4C, when the HCR reaction was carried out using FAM-cDNA of different concentrations, when the concentration of FAM-cDNA was 0, the fluorescence spectrum curve did not show the maximum absorption peak. With the increase of concentration, the maximum absorption peaks of the fluorescence spectrum curve all appeared, and the fluorescence signal value at 520 nm gradually increased. This proves the effectiveness of the designed probe and that the HCR reaction can only be carried out when cDNA and hairpin probes are present simultaneously.

Optimization of reaction conditions

A single-factor experiment was designed to optimize the key influencing factors and experimental conditions. The preparation of functionalized magnetic beads is the basis of subsequent experiments. Here, the optimal concentration of Bio-Apt was determined by comparing the A260 value of the supernatants obtained after magnetic separation of 0.1 mg SA-MB incubated with varying Bio-Apt concentration. As shown in the Fig. 5A, with the increase of Bio-Apt concentration, the A260 value in the supernatant gradually increased. At an aptamer concentration of 100 nM, the A260 value of the supernatant showed no statistically significant difference compared to the water as the blank control, making it impossible to determine whether all biotin-binding sites on SA-MB were fully occupied by Bio-Apt. When the concentration of the aptamer increased to 200 nM, there was a significant difference in the A260 value of the supernatant compared with that of the blank group. In order to reduce the waste of reagents, it was subsequently decided to select the concentration of Bio-Apt combined with 0.1 mg of SA-MB at 200 nM. Under different incubation times, by comparing the A260 values of the supernatant, there was no significant difference, that is, Bio-Apt and SA-MB could bind rapidly within a relatively short period of time. Based on these findings, an incubation time of 15 min was determined to be sufficient for achieving optimal conjugation efficiency between the two components (Fig. 5B). Different volumes of MB-Apt (1 mg/mL) were added to the reaction system, and the optimal dosage of MB-Apt was determined based on the capture efficiency.

Condition optimization.

A Optimization of bio-apt binding concentration; B optimization of incubation time between magnetic beads and aptamers; C optimization of the amount of MB-Apt; D optimization of incubation time between MB-Apt and bacteria.

The dosage of functionalized magnetic beads and the incubation time are important reasons affecting the binding efficiency of bacteria. As shown in the Fig. 5C, the capture rate was relatively low when the addition of MB-Apt was 40 μL. With the increase of the amount of MB-Apt, the capture rate gradually rose. When the addition volume of MB-Apt was 80–120 μL, there was no significant difference in capture rate. To reduce reagent consumption and save costs, MB-Apt was added to the subsequent experimental system with a volume of 80 μL, that is, 0.08 mg. As shown in the Fig. 5D, MB-Apt was incubated with the target bacteria for different durations. When the incubation time was less than 30 min, the capture rate was about 20%. When the incubation time was 75 min, the capture rate reached the maximum. However, there was no significant difference in the capture rates corresponding to the incubation time between 60 min and 120 min. Subsequently, the incubation time of MB-Apt with the bacteria was selected as 60 min.

Subsequently, parameters such as the addition concentration of FAM-cDNA, the incubation time of FAM-cDNA, the reaction time of HCR, and the addition concentration of GO in the reaction system were optimized. The optimal reaction conditions are as follows: the addition concentration of FAM-cDNA was 175 nM (Fig. 6A), the incubation time of FAM-cDNA was 35 min (Fig. 6B), the reaction time of HCR was 10 min (Fig. 6C), and the addition concentration of GO was 0.15 mg/mL (Fig. 6D).

Specificity evaluation of the reaction system

Given the diversity of bacteria responsible for foodborne illnesses, it is essential to evaluate the specificity of the proposed method. Based on the optimized experimental conditions, the specificity of this system was performed, including 7 strains of B cereus and 7 strains of non-B. cereus. The specific strain information was detailed in the Table 2. As shown in the Fig. 7A, B, it could be seen from the results of the specificity evaluation that there was a significant difference between the positive strains and the negative strains. The fluorescence signal value of the positive strains was much higher than that of the negative strains. The fluorescence intensity of the negative strains was close to that of the blank control group, while the fluorescence signal intensity corresponding to the positive strains was significantly higher than that of the blank control group. Therefore, it was proved that this system had good specificity for the detection of B. cereus, which was attributed to the good specificity of the aptamer for the target bacteria. Moreover, this aptamer has been successfully applied as an identification element in microfluidic chip biosensors to detect B. cereus50.

Sensitivity evaluation of the reaction system

Based on the high capture efficiency and excellent fluorescence characteristics of this method, we used this detection system to test the sensitivity of the pure bacterial liquid of B. cereus. The results showed that when the concentration of the added bacterial liquid was 5.4 × 106 CFU/mL-5.4 × 101 CFU/mL, the fluorescence spectrum curve showed a gradually decreasing trend (Fig. 7C), and the fluorescence signal value at 520 nm also gradually decreased (Fig. 7D). When the concentration of the bacterial liquid was within the range of 5.4×105 CFU/mL-5.4 × 101 CFU/mL, a standard curve was plotted with the fluorescence signal value at 520 nm as the vertical coordinate and the logarithm of the concentration of B. cereus bacterial liquid as the horizontal coordinate (Fig. 7D), and it was obtained that there was a certain linear relationship between the two factors, y = 9186x + 3706. At this time, the correlation coefficient was R2 = 0.9812 which confirmed the method’s excellent linear response for quantitative detection. Therefore, the proposed method showed a great potential for the detection of B. cereus within a wide concentration range and can sensitively detect B. cereus at concentrations as low as 5.4×101 CFU/mL.

The experimental results showed that the constructed system demonstrated excellent performance in the detection of B. cereus. Compared with the traditional culture method, the detection time required by this method is significantly shortened52. The specific aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads required in the detection system can be prepared in advance and stored in a 4 °C refrigerator, further reducing the time needed for the entire system. As a result, from sample processing to result output, it only takes about 2 hours, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the detection results. Compared with molecular biology methods such as PCR and LAMP, the established system does not require the extraction of target DNA and the design of specific primers, simplifying the complex experimental process and avoiding the problem of false positives caused by aerosol contamination53. The established system can achieve a detection limit of 5.4×101 CFU/mL in the detection of pure bacterial liquid of B. cereus. The sensitivity is consistent with that obtained by Zheng et al. in detecting B. cereus in milk based on upconversion fluorescence and magnetic separation techniques49. Compared with the method of catalytic hairpin assembly combined with GO for detecting vomiting-type B. cereus in milk established by Zhan et al., it can also achieve the same order of magnitude of sensitivity54. However, compared with the post-modified MOF and nucleic acid aptamer detection system for B. cereus established by Yan et al., the sensitivity is one order of magnitude lower2. Compared with biosensors based on the broad-spectrum recognition of vancomycin and the principle of electrochemical impedance, this study uses specific nucleic acid aptamers to identify B. cereus, avoiding the cross-reaction of vancomycin with all gram-positive bacteria, thereby achieving highly specific detection of the target bacteria55. Compared with the recently reported high-throughput electrochemical sensors based on AuNPs@Ti3C2Tz and sandwich peptides, the reaction system established in this study avoids the modification of complex electrodes and has a more concise process56. In addition, we also made a list comparison of the detection limits of the constructed method with those of other rapid detection methods for B. cereus (Table 3)57,58. From this, it can be seen that the detection sensitivity obtained by this method can reach the same level as that of similar detection methods and meet the needs of daily detection. Overall, this detection method achieves a good balance among performance, ease of operation and cost. It is expected that it can provide certain reference value for the detection of foodborne pathogenic bacteria based on the reaction of aptamer functionalized magnetic beads and HCR.

Detection of artificially contaminated milk samples

In order to verify the application of the proposed detection system in actual samples, artificially contaminated milk samples were tested. The results showed that when the concentration of the bacterial liquid in the artificially contaminated milk samples was 4.6 × 106 CFU/mL-4.6 × 101 CFU/mL, the fluorescence spectrum curve showed a gradually decreasing trend (Fig. 8A), and the fluorescence signal value at 520 nm also gradually decreased (Fig. 8B), both of which were higher than the fluorescence signal values of the blank control group. Therefore, this system has a good detection effect on artificially contaminated milk samples.

In this study, when the inducer FAM-cDNA for the reaction between the target bacteria and HCR was all present in the reaction system, it would competitively combine with the functionalized magnetic beads. This design is based on magnetic separation technology to achieve the output of fluorescence signals for different samples and stimulate the HCR reaction to achieve the signal amplification effect. Zhong et al. utilized magnetic nanoparticles modified by aptamers to selectively capture Pseudomonas aeruginosa and conduct sensitive fluorescence determination. They also obtained quantitative analysis of the concentration of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with a detection limit as low as 1 CFU/mL, verifying the application of this method in labeled food samples59. Wang et al. combined the hybridization chain reaction, the fluorescence synergy of FAM and SYBR Green Ⅰ, and the structural transformation aptamer to achieve the detection of tobramycin using the trigger signal amplification technique, and successfully reached a detection limit as low as 17.37 nM42. Moreover, GO plays a pivotal role in quenching the fluorescence of non-specifically adsorbed FAM-cDNA probes. By reducing the interference of the background signal, the signal-to-noise ratio is improved60. When the target bacteria are present, their specific binding to the aptamer can compete to release FAM-cDNA, which in turn triggers the HCR reaction to generate a rigid double-stranded DNA product with a large number of fluorescent groups and far from the GO surface. At this time, the fluorescence is completely retained and significantly enhanced. This differentiated treatment is the key to achieving highly specific detection. Tang et al. applied this characteristic in the method based on HCR reaction and GO for the sensitive detection of Staphylococcus aureus. In the absence of a target, the free FAM label would be adsorbed by GO through π-π, quenching the fluorescence signal. This method was successfully applied to the detection of Staphylococcus aureus in milk samples. The detection limit was 4×102 CFU/mL61. Yun et al. reported a fluorescence amplification strategy based on GO and catalytic hairpin assembly for ultrasensitive detection of Hg2+. The addition of GO greatly suppressed the background signal and improved the reliability of the method62.

It should be emphasized that the effectiveness of this system depends on the specific binding of the aptamer to the complete target on the surface of the target bacteria. Therefore, the advantage of this method, which requires no nucleic acid extraction, is strictly applicable to live bacteria with intact cellular structures. The detection efficiency for bacteria that have been completely inactivated due to high temperatures, intensification reagents or physical damage may be significantly reduced. This characteristic makes this method suitable for risk screening of active pathogenic bacteria in food safety This is the fundamental difference in application between this technology and the PCR method based on nucleic acid extraction. Additionally, this study has certain limitations. Although we have verified the feasibility of the method in a single representative food matrix, the diversity of food matrices may bring different interference challenges, and its wide universality in more food matrices still needs to be confirmed. In addition, to achieve truly on-site rapid detection, this technology also faces the challenge of portability. This is mainly reflected in the fact that the current detection process relies on laboratory-scale fluorescence spectrophotometers to read the final detection signals. The entire system has not yet been integrated into a closed, automated portable device. However, this study aims to provide reliable prototypes and performance benchmarks for the future development of integrated portable devices. Future research should focus on developing portable devices that integrate magnetic separation, isothermal reaction and miniaturization of fluorescence detection modules into one, in order to achieve the on-site detection goal of “sample in, result out”.

In conclusion, based on aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads and HCR reaction, this study designed a rapid, enzyme-free and effective method for the detection of B. cereus. The binding specificity of the aptamer to the bacteria was detected by using an aptamer-functionalized gold nanoparticle colorimetric assay. Through the specific affinity between biotin and streptavidin, MB-Apt were prepared, achieving the specific capture of B. cereus. This MB-Apt was applied to the established reaction system to enable the target bacteria to competitively bind with FAM-cDNA. Through magnetic separation technology, the output of fluorescence signals was achieved, and the HCR reaction was utilized to further amplify the fluorescence signals. This platform exhibits good specificity and enables quantitative detection for B. cereus with a limit detection (LOD) of 5.4×101 CFU/mL. Validation using artificially contaminated milk samples confirmed its applicability in complex food matrices. Furthermore, by replacing the corresponding aptamer sequence, this detection method can be readily adapted for detecting other foodborne pathogens.

Methods

Materials and apparatus

B. cereus was used as the target bacteria for detection, and other non-B. cereus strains were used for specificity detection. All of the standard strains were stored in the Enzyme Engineering Laboratory of Nanjing Agricultural University by glycerol tube storage. The isolates of B. cereus that appeared in this study were from Nanjing Institute of Food and Drug Administration and Inspection.

Streptavidin-modified magnetic beads (SA-MB, 10 mg/mL, with a diameter of 10 μm) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Single-layer Graphene oxide dispersion (GO, 0.5 mg/mL, 50–200 nm) was purchased from Xianfeng Nano Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). AuNPs was purchased from Shanghai Jieyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The buffers used in the experiment were as follows: TBS buffer (200 mM Tris, 1.37 M NaCl, pH 7.6), 2×Binding & washing buffer (2×B&W buffer: 10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl, 0.01%-0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.5), 1×Binding buffer (1×BB buffer: 50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM KCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4), 1×PBS buffer (135 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.4), HCR buffer (0.5 M NaCl, 50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.8). The aptamer of B. cereus and other DNA sequences (shown in Table 1) were synthesized by Genscript Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

The magnetic grate (BS-MGS-015-08) was purchased from Biosharp Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The PowerPac HC electrophoresis system was used for gel electrophoresis (Bio Rad, California, USA). The fluorescence spectra were collected by EnSight multifunctional enzyme marker (PerkinElmer, USA). The approximate concentration of bacteria and absorbance was obtained by the UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-Vis, UV-2600, Japan) and the NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The zeta potential analysis used the Zetasizer Nano-ZS nanoparticle size potentiometer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK). The HCR reaction was accomplished through a constant-temperature oscillating metal bath.

Bacterial culture

A single colony of B. cereus was selected and inoculated in fresh Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium and cultured overnight in a rotary shaker at 37 °C. The bacterial suspension used in the experiment was cultured to the logarithmic stage. The approximate concentration of bacteria was obtained by OD600. The accurate concentration of bacteria was calculated by gradient dilution plate coating method.

Synthesis of aptamers and design of probes

According to the references, the specific binding aptamer of B. cereus was obtained49,50, and the corresponding complementary DNA (cDNA) and hairpin probes were synthesized according to the specific aptamer sequence. Prior to synthesis, the sequences of the hairpin probes and the results of the HCR reaction were simulated using the NUPACK online software. After synthesis, the probes were diluted with sterilized water to a concentration of 10 μM. In preparation for the ensuring reactions, the diluted probes underwent a pre-denaturation step at a temperature of 95 °C for 5 min, then slowly cooled to room temperature, and stored in a 4 °C to maintain their stability for future experimental applications.

Specificity verification of aptamer and bacteria binding

The specificity of the aptamer binding to the target bacteria was confirmed using an aptamer-functionalized gold nanoparticle (AuNP) colorimetric assay. This approach is based on the principle that AuNPs undergo aggregation under high salt concentrations, resulting in a distinct color change from red to blue63,64. Due to the effect of electrostatic repulsion, the AuNPs can remain in a dispersed state, making the solution appear red. Once the high-concentration salt solution is added, the formed stability is disrupted, resulting in particle aggregation and the solution color changes accordingly, from red to blue-purple65. On the other hand, the free aptamer will adsorb onto the surface of the AuNPs under the influence of electrostatic force. Even in the case of high concentration of salt, the solution can remain red. In the presence of target bacteria, the aptamer binds specifically to the surface of bacteria, and the addition of a high concentration of salt will destroy the stability of AuNPs, causing the solution to turn blue and purple. The results were validated by visual observation and specific absorbance values to demonstrate the specificity of the aptamer to the target bacteria66. The detection principle is shown in the Fig. 2A.

Base on the above principles, 50 μL aptamer with a concentration of 0.5 μM was mixed with equal volume of bacterial suspension. After incubation for 10 min, 50 μL AuNPs was added to the mixture for equilibrium for 5 min, and then 10 μL 1 M NaCl solution was added for equilibrium for 5 min67. The color change was recorded, and the value of A630/A520 was determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometer, so as to determine the specificity of binding between the aptamer and bacteria. In addition, a blank control group was set up with equal volume of sterilized water instead of bacterial suspension.

Preparation of aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads

The strong affinity between biotin and streptavidin was utilized to immobilize biotin-labeled aptamer (Bio-Apt) onto SA-MB. Initially, Bio-Apt was heated at 95 °C for 5 min to ensure proper folding, followed by gradual cooling to room temperature and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use20. Subsequently, 50 μL of thoroughly resuspended SA-MB were washed three times with 1×TBS buffer. After washing, add 200 μL of Bio-Apt with an appropriate concentration to the SA-MB, blow and mix well, and react at 37 °C for a specified duration, so that Bio-Apt successfully modified on the SA-MB. Following the reaction, the solution was subjected to magnetic separation to remove any unbound Bio-Apt. Repeat washing above magnetic beads with 1×B&W buffer. Finally, aptamer-functionalized magnetic beads (MB-Apt) with a final concentration of 1 mg/mL were obtained by resuspension with 500 µL PBS and stored at 4 °C for later use.

Evaluation of aptamer-functionalized magnetic bead capture effect

Following overnight culture, bacterial suspension of a defined concentration was prepared and diluted to 104 CFU/mL. The bacterial suspension was co-incubated with MB-Apt at 37 °C to facilitate the efficient absorption of bacteria. After incubation for a specific period, the supernatant was separated, and the uncaptured bacteria were collected. By diluting the plate coating, the number of colonies contained in the supernatant was obtained. Calculated according to the formula of magnetic bead capture rate: Capture rate (CE) = (1-N1/N0)×100% (Where N0 is the total number of bacteria in the sample, and N1 is the number of bacteria unbound to the MB-Apt in the supernatant)68.

Verification of HCR system

In order to verify the hybridization chain reaction, varying concentrations of cDNA (0.2 μM、0.4 μM、0.6 μM、0.8 μM、1.0 μM) were introduced into the reaction system and incubated with H1 and H2 probes (1 μM each) at 37 °C for 1 hour. Upon completion of the reaction, the products were immediately analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis at 120 V for 30 min to visualize the resulting bands69. In addition, the HCR system was validated using FAM-cDNA, where varying concentration of FAM-cDNA (0, 0.2 μM、0.4 μM、0.6 μM、0.8 μM、1.0 μM) were reacted with an equal amount of hairpin probe H1/H2 (1 μM). The reactions were carried out under the following conditions: incubation at 37 °C in a metal bath for 1 h. Post reaction, 20 μL of GO (0.2 mg/mL) was added to each product mixture and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The final volume was adjusted to 200 μL by adding sterile water. Fluorescence spectra were recorded within the range of 510 nm to 600 nm using an excitation wavelength of 495 nm to analyze the HCR reaction products.

Construction and detection of reaction system

Prior to use, H1 and H2 should be heated at 95 °C for 5 min, respectively, and then gradually cooled to room temperature for 1 h, followed by storage at 4 °C for later use70. Take a certain amount of MB-Apt, after magnetic separation, remove the supernatant, and add different concentrations of B. cereus bacterial suspension to the precipitation. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for a specified duration to allow sufficient binding between the bacteria and the aptamers2. After incubation, magnetic separation was performed to remove unbound bacteria, and 200 μL of a specific concentration of FAM-cDNA was added to precipitate. After incubation for a period of time under identical conditions, the retained supernatant was retained as the initiator of HCR reaction. H1 and H2 with a final concentration of 1 μM were added to the supernatant. After the reaction, GO solution with appropriate concentration was added to the mixture, which was incubated at room temperature for 5 min41. The fluorescence spectra were recorded within the range of 510 nm-650 nm using an excitation wavelength of 495 nm. As shown in the graphical abstract, it is the schematic diagram of the detection principle of this reaction system.

Specificity evaluation of reaction system

According to the established reaction system, different target strains and non-target strains were used to verify the specificity of this strategy. In addition, sterilized water was used instead of bacterial suspension to set up a blank control. Different strains were incubated with MB-Apt, and other conditions remained unchanged. Finally, the specificity of the reaction system was determined by measuring the fluorescence spectra in the excitation wavelength of 495 nm and the emission wavelength range of 510 nm-650 nm.

Detection of B. cereus in artificially contaminated milk samples

First, preliminary treatment of the aseptic milk samples should be performed prior to detection. Specifically, 10 mL of milk samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. After removing the precipitate, the supernatant was retained, diluted tenfold with PBS buffer, and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use. Then, the cultured bacterial suspensions were diluted by 10 times gradient to obtain the bacterial solution at different concentrations, which were then mixed with the pretreated milk samples in a certain proportion to prepare artificially contaminated milk mixtures. Following thorough homogenization, the artificially contaminated sample was analyzed through the established reaction system.
