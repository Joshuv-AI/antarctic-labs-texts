Extracellular matrix architecture promotes immunosuppressive microenvironments in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Mackenzie K. Callaway, Brock J. Noonan, Kathryn L. Schwertfeger, Paolo P. Provenzano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) is an aggressive cancer with poor clinical outcomes, due in part to altered fibrotic environments and striking immune dysfunction. Physical properties within tumors, such as aligned extracellular matrix (ECM) fiber architectures, are fundamental to cancer progression and outcome. However, the influence of ECM alignment on immune cell localization and function within tumors, particularly PDA, remains largely unexplored. Here, analysis of mouse and human PDA reveal an inextricable link between collagen architecture and the distribution of immunosuppressive macrophages in both early preinvasive disease and invasive carcinomas. In vitro characterization of primary macrophages demonstrates alignment alone is sufficient to induce elongation, polarization, and immunosuppressive activity, including suppression of CD8+ T cell proliferation and motility. Analysis reveals differential focal adhesion kinase (FAK) activity in aligned macrophages, while FAK inhibition (FAKi) disrupts the immunosuppressive phenotype that emerges from encountering ECM alignment. Furthermore, FAKi in vivo significantly reduces the correlation between elongated immunosuppressive macrophages and aligned collagen, further highlighting the opportunity for FAKi to target stromal immunity. Importantly, the correlation between aligned collagen and immunosuppressive macrophages is also observed in human chronic pancreatitis, a known PDA risk factor that has recently been shown to prime stromal ECM alignments for early dissemination, suggesting that precursor disease is also likely to create stromal memory conducive to early immunosuppression. Taken together, these results support a model in which collagen architecture supports early establishment and maintenance of an immunosuppressive microenvironments and defines a role for targeting stromal matrices to “reprogram” patient immunity.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) is an extremely aggressive and highly lethal form of pancreatic cancer with a 5-year survival rate of ~13 % [1]. Poor patient outcomes have also been linked to the unique fibroinflammatory, or “desmoplastic,” stromal environment in PDA, a key driver of disease progression and resistance to therapeutic intervention [2–4]. The stroma is comprised of various non-transformed supportive cells, soluble factors, and a highly fibrotic extracellular matrix (ECM), all of which can support tumor progression, hinder therapeutic interventions, and promote immune evasion [3,5–7]. Included in this milieu are distinct extracellular matrix architectures, termed “Tumor-Associated Collagen Signatures” (TACS; [8]). Included in this panel are fibers aligned parallel to cell clusters or ductal boundary (e.g. TACS-2) and TACS-3 where fibers orient perpendicularly to the cell clusters or ductal boundary, or result in aligned collagen regions throughout the tumor mass in later stages [8,9]. These aligned fibers provide guidance for carcinoma cell invasion [10–12] and correlate with worse survival in human cancer patients [13]. Yet, while recent studies demonstrate the presence of TACS architectures in PDA [9,14,15] and the influence of aligned ECM on T cell migration [12,16], our understanding of the role of ECM architecture in immune cell regulation, and in particular myeloid cell behavior, in PDA is extremely limited. Here we sought to address this knowledge gap and hypothesized that aligned collagen fibers influence macrophage behavior in PDA.

In PDA, a remarkable influx of leukocytes begins very early in disease and persists through disease progression [6]. However, the majority of these immune cells are myeloid derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) that are harnessed to exacerbate disease and hinder productive host anti-tumor immune responses [6,17,18], in contrast to very low numbers of anti-tumor classically activated macrophages [19,20]. Long appreciated for their contributions to fibrosis, angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance [17,18,21–23], the pro-tumor macrophage is a key contributor to the severity of PDA, and their numbers correlate with poor prognosis in many solid tumors including those of the pancreas [24–28]. Indeed, even in early disease, macrophages localize to the periductal space around early pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (PanINs)[6,17] to establish and maintain tumor-permissive, immunosuppressive, environments that protect tumor cells by altering antigen presentation, abrogating T cell trafficking and killing, and regulating other stromal cells to support tumor progression [22,24–27,29–32]. Furthermore, along with robust ECM and complex architectures [12,16], macrophages are thought to play a key role not only in limited natural anti-tumor immune responses but also performance of immunotherapies [7,21,29]. As such therapeutic interventions to selectively target, deplete, or repolarize macrophages in PDA have resulted in increased immunotherapy efficacy and survival [21,23,33,34]. Thus, collective data suggests that macrophage phenotype is critical and may be dependent on inherent cues presented in local TMEs, yet the local physical factors influencing their behavior in the early and late stages of PDA are not well defined.

Chemical cues from cytokines are known regulators of macrophage polarization and function. Yet, an emerging body of evidence suggests mechanical and physical cues also play a role in dictating macrophage phenotype and function In vitro and may even supersede chemical cues in some cases [35–42]. For example, macrophages have been shown to polarize in response to both perceived mechanics, like surface roughness and stiffness, and applied mechanics, like pressure and stretching [38]. Of note, many of these biophysical cues are present in tumors and have been shown to directly influence the behavior of transformed and stromal cells such as carcinoma-associated fibroblasts [43–45]. These physical changes are largely perpetuated by the deposition, organization and mechanics of ECM components, such as collagen, and ultimately manifest as detrimental to patient survival [2,43,44]. Interestingly, aligned collagen signatures also appear early in PDA progression [9], and temporally coincide with the accumulation of periductal TAMs [6,21]. Therefore, given the evidence that macrophages are responsive to mechanical and architectural cues, and the early accumulation of both macrophages and aligned collagen, we hypothesized a relationship between collagen architecture and macrophage phenotype.

In this study, we show that immunosuppressive macrophages preferentially colocalize with aligned stromal collagen in pre-invasive and invasive PDA. Moreover, we demonstrate that alignment alone is sufficient to drive macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, increasing their ability to suppress T cell migration and proliferation. This suppressive phenotype can be attributed to differential FAK signaling dynamics, where the inhibition of FAK both In vitro and in vivo diminishes the prevalence of elongated immunosuppressive macrophages. Interestingly, these observations are conserved in human pancreatitis suggesting that precursor diseases may generate stromal memory and highlight the utility of targeting the matrix in both precursory pathology and established disease to alleviate immunosuppressive tumor-conducive boundaries in PDA.

Results

Macrophage location, morphology, and phenotype correlate with collagen density and fiber alignment

We first evaluated macrophage localization relative to collagen architecture in human normal pancreata and PDA samples using mulitphoton microscopy for multiphoton excitation (MPE) of fluorescence combined with second harmonic generation (SHG) to assess macrophage location, morphology, and polarization state. Perhaps surprisingly, analysis of normal human pancreata and tumor adjacent “normal” tissue showed that while macrophages were sparsely distributed, virtually all macrophages in normal tissues were observed interacting with collagen fibers and elongating in the direction of fiber orientation (Fig. 1A). Indeed, 97 % of the macrophages were associated with a collagen fiber (Fig. 1B). To examine this association further we next evaluated different stages of human pancreatic cancer and observed that the prevalence of macrophages increased with disease progression from normal pancreas to PanIN lesions to PDA (Figs 1C). However, multiphoton imaging of CD206+ TAMs, which are largely immunosuppressive macrophages in human PDA [20,46], demonstrates that TAMs are observed in association with collagen fibers and are enriched in regions with highly aligned collagen (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these results suggest that aligned stromal collagen may serve as a mechanical cue to direct macrophage shape and influence phenotype during normal tissue function and then through early to late stages of disease.

Given our observations in human PDA, we sought to further define macrophage morphology and distribution in PanIN lesions and frank PDA using murine pancreata isolated from the KrasG12D/+;p53R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mice. This model faithfully recapitulates the histological and clinical progression of human disease, and closely mimics the fibrotic and immunosuppressive TMEs characteristic of human PDA [5,6,47,48]. Of note, the deposition and remodeling of collagen in KPC mice gives rise to collagen architectures similar to those observed in human disease [9,15], allowing the interrogation of how aligned ECM influence disease dynamics. However, while it is clear that these architectures direct pancreatic carcinoma cell migration [9,15], their role in directing immune cells motility and phenotype is less defined. Therefore, using multiphoton microscopy we evaluated ECM architecture and macrophage phenotype in KPC mice. Consistent with previous reports [6,18,47], we observed immunosuppressive myeloid expansion early in disease progression, beginning around PanIN lesions in mice as young as 8 weeks and persisting throughout PDA (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, macrophages positive for F4/80 and CD206 or Arginase-1 (Arg-1), two common markers of a pro-tumor immunosuppressive polarization state for PDA TAMs [20,46,49], were strongly localized with dense regions of aligned collagen fibers (e.g. regions of aligned collagen fibers associated with both TACS-2 and -3) at all stages of disease examined (Fig. 2A and Supp Fig. 1), similar to finding in human PDA (Fig. 1). Indeed, macrophages associated with aligned collagen fibers displayed high elongation in the direction of fiber orientation (a maximum ratio roughly 8-times higher than the isotropic average (Fig. 2B), in stark contrast to the minority of TAMs that are not directly associated with aligned, collagen dense, regions and display a much more rounded phenotype and lack directionality (Fig. 2B–D). These observations raise interesting questions regarding the influence of shape on macrophage function as fundamental work by Mcwhorter and colleagues suggests a link between macrophage aspect ratio and macrophage polarization toward a more “alternatively activated” or M2 phenotype In vitro [37], and work by Vasse and colleagues showing that macrophage shape and polarization behavior can be resulted by collagen fiber structure [41]. Furthermore, in PDA that vast majority of TAMs are known to be immunosuppressive [19,20,49,50]. Thus, utilizing a reductionist perspective where macrophages display either an anti-tumor “classically activated” or “M1-like” phenotype or the pro-tumor “alternatively activated” phenotype [27,32], our data suggests that a more pro-tumor immunosuppressive phenotype in vivo may be influenced by aligned collagen in both pre-invasive and invasive disease.

Alignment influences macrophage morphology and phenotype In vitro

The evident link between collagen alignment and macrophage phenotype in fixed samples suggests the potential for a direct cause-and-effect relationship between aligned collagen fibers and macrophage phenotype and polarization. Therefore, to directly test the hypothesis that collagen architecture directly guides macrophage behavior, we first examined primary bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) In vitro. We note that these macrophages are capable of polarizing toward both M1 and M2 phenotypes in response to canonical stimulation In vitro, and exhibit low baseline expression of canonical polarization markers (Supp. Fig. 2A–C). Therefore, to first evaluate if macrophage polarization drives changes in cell shape we examined BMDM aspect ratio following cytokine exposure (Fig. 3A). Polarization resulted in distinct, significantly different, cell shapes with M1 macrophages (i.e. LPS+IFNγ induced) showing a more rounded epithelial-like morphology, while M2 polarization (i.e. IL4+IL13 induced) demonstrate an elongated morphology (Fig. 3A,B), consistent with a previous report [37], and demonstrating a relationship between macrophage shape and polarization state. Therefore, to test our hypothesis we sought to induce shape change (i.e. elongation) via an engineered environment. As the stroma in live tumor environments is quite complex, we sought to perform more reductionist experiments to test if ECM alignment is sufficient to drive macrophage behavior. To accomplish this we employed “2.5D” nanopatterned culture systems which mimic physiologically relevant ECM alignment, spacing, and texture and allow study of large numbers of cellular events in defined microenvironments that reasonably capture key 3D behaviors [15,51]. Thus, with this platform we can analyze large cell numbers in a biomimetic environment to directly define how physical cues impact macrophage phenotype. We seeded primary macrophages onto aligned or flat control topographies and observed that BMDMs on aligned topographies exhibited striking elongation in contrast to macrophages on flat surfaces which showed more rounded, randomly oriented morphologies (Fig. 3A,B). This behavior is consistent with findings of robust contact guidance of carcinoma cells along nanopatterns [12,15,51]. Thus, our results suggest aligned topographies alone are capable of regulating macrophage phenotype, even in the absence of specific chemical cues. Moreover, BMDMs on aligned topographies displayed significantly increased expression of the canonical M2 marker Arg-1 (Fig. 3C) and a decrease in expression of “M1” markers (Supp. Fig. 2D), consistent with results found by McWhorter where the elongated cell shape promotes a M2 state. Thus, our data suggests that matrix alignment is directly promoting a more M2 macrophage phenotype, which suggests that matrix alignment may influence immunosuppressive behavior.

Matrix alignment promotes an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype

In many solid tumors, myeloid cells have the potential to attenuate or abolish nearly every facet of CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, including their recruitment, trafficking, expansion, and survival. Known to be an immunologically “cold” tumors, PDA contains sparse CD8+ T cells, even at the earliest points of inception, and this stark absence can be largely attributed to stromal fibrosis and immunosuppressive macrophages [6,7,19,20]. We therefore sought to determine whether matrix alignment directly drives functional immunosuppressive macrophage behavior. To test the hypothesis that guidance of macrophages along aligned ECM impacts both CD8+ T cell proliferation and migration, activated primary CD8+ T cells were cocultured with BMDMs. Both populations were derived from the same mouse. Proliferation was assessed by directly coculturing activated CD8+ T cells on top of BMDMs on flat or aligned topographies. Indeed, T cells overlaid on macrophages in aligned conditions exhibited decreased proliferation compared to flat controls (Fig. 3D). This suggests macrophages on aligned substrates have a heightened ability to abrogate T cell expansion. Moreover, activated CD8+ T cells seeded into the upper chamber of collagen-coated transwell inserts exhibited decreased migration toward BMDMs on aligned topographies when compared to flat controls (Fig. 3E), suggesting alignment not only impedes expansion, but also T cell trafficking. Taken together, these data implicate alignment as a direct regulator of not only macrophage polarization, but also of macrophage function. Thus, it is evident that alignment skews macrophages toward a functionally immunosuppressive state.

Focal adhesions are enriched and striated in macrophages on aligned topographies

Cells utilize surface receptors such as integrins to sense and respond to their external environment via focal adhesion dynamics. In fact, anisotropic distribution of mature focal adhesions is known to govern spontaneous sensing of guidance cues by carcinoma cells [15]. Furthermore, recent studies from DeNardo and colleagues and our group have implicated FAK as a key driver of fibrosis, macrophage accumulation, and cancer cell dissemination, suggesting a link between FAK, collagen alignment, and disease severity [7,9]. Thus, we sought to determine the role of FAK as a regulator of the link between macrophage shape and function. Given the evidence that alternatively activated macrophages display more elongated morphologies (Fig. 3), we speculated that M2 polarization of macrophages via IL-4+IL-13 may also alter FAK dynamics. We first evaluated active FAK by examining FAK phosphorylation at tyrosine 925 (pY925), one of several sites phosphorylated downstream of FAK activation. As anticipated, IL-4 + IL-13 stimulated BMDMs and J774 macrophages exhibited significantly elevated FAK phosphorylation (Fig. 4A,B and Supp. Fig. 3A), with a significant increase in number of FAK-positive focal adhesions per cell (Fig. 4C), while the area of focal adhesions was not significantly different (Supp. Fig. 3B), further implicating active FAK as a key player in this elongation-polarization axis. To confirm our findings and gain a broader understanding of how chemical stimulation influences macrophage global FAK dynamics, BMDMs were cultured with either media alone, complete media supplemented with tumor conditioned media (TCM), or media with IL-4+IL-13. Consistent with the pFAK(Y925) patterns, M2 stimulation exhibited significantly elevated levels of phosphor-FAK(pY397), while TCM had little effect (Fig. 4D).

To inspect the level and localization of phosphorylated FAK in macrophages in response to ECM alignment, BMDMs were cultured on aligned nanopatterns. Compellingly, BMDMs on aligned topographies exhibited high levels of pFAK compared to BMDMs on flat control substrates (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the distribution of pFAK was strikingly striated along aligned substrates, forming long stretches of highly aligned focal adhesions along aligned nanopatterns (Fig. 4E,F), which is distinct from more localized focal adhesions at the cell edges in carcinoma cells responding to nanopatterns via contact guidance [12,15]. In contrast, on flat conditions, pFAK was largely dispersed diffusely within the cell with lower levels of pFAK observed at the membrane in small, punctate focal adhesions with no apparent pattern or orientation (Fig. 4E,F). To confirm our findings, we validated and characterized the aligned adhesion phenotype in immortalized J774 macrophages. Interestingly, J774 cells exhibited a similar increase in and organization of pFAK with long, highly aligned focal adhesions along the length of the cell (Supp Fig. 3C). Furthermore, to confirm pFAK is indeed associated with mature focal adhesions, we performed dual-immunofluorescence and evaluated colocalization of pFAK with vinculin. Consistent with FAK staining, striated vinculin was observed demonstrating that these are indeed highly aligned, mature, focal adhesions (Supp Fig. 3C,D), which we also confirmed through quantitative analysis showing that focal adhesions in aligned cultures are overwhelmingly oriented in the direction of alignment (Supp Fig. 3C,D). Collectively, this data prompted us to evaluate if cell shape is dependent on FAK signaling.

FAK signaling is necessary for cell shape induced macrophage polarization

To test whether FAK singling is necessary for the shape-associated immunosuppressive polarization behavior that we identified, we employed selective FAK inhibition. First, we analyzed samples from a previous study treating KPC mice with either FAK inhibitor VS-4718 or vehicle control [7] to determine if TAM elongation in vivo is FAK-dependent (Fig. 4G). Jiang and colleagues previously characterized these samples and noted that FAK treated samples exhibited drastically reduced numbers of CD206+ macrophages, consistent with our findings (Fig. 4H and Supp Fig. 4), but the localization and aspect ratios of these macrophages was not assessed. Consistent with earlier analysis in KPC mice (Fig. 2), vehicle treated KPC mice exhibit high aspect ratio TAMs in regions with collagen alignment (Fig. 4G), again consistent with our observations in human tumors (Fig. 1). However, tissues from age-matched KPC mice treated with FAK inhibitor VS-4718 exhibited significantly lower aspect ratios (Fig. 4I), suggesting that FAK is required for the elongated macrophage phenotype, which supports immunosuppressive function. However, FAKi also results in lower collagen levels and a more disorganized matrix in KPC tumors [7], which may alter the TAM elongation phenotype by reducing elongated fibers that influence TAM alignment and polarization. Therefore, we next sought to employ a more reductionist system to confirm that FAK signaling regulates TAM shape and polarization.

To further test the link between cell shape, FAK signaling, and the M2 phenotype, BMDMs were seeded into ultra-low adhesion plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h after which they were subjected to either 1uM/mL VS-4718 or DMSO control treatment. BMDMs were then collected and seeded onto aligned nanopatterned substrates and cultured for 24 h with continued FAKi or vehicle (DMSO) treatment and were then fixed and stained to assess cell morphology and Arg-1 expression. FAK inhibition In vitro diminished BMDM ability to elongate in response to alignment (Fig. 4J), suggesting that FAK plays a role in contact guidance sensing and the response to guidance cues. Indeed, BMDMs under FAKi exhibited a significant reduction in Arg-1 expression relative to vehicle controls (Fig. 4K). These results implicate FAK as a regulator of macrophage polarization in response to ECM alignment. Furthermore, these results support the utility of FAK inhibitors in combination with other stroma targeted or immune therapies to not only target cancer cells, but also to reengineer extracellular matrix architectures and stromal immunosuppression.

Myeloid-collagen landscape in chronic pancreatitis is similar to PDA

Pancreatitis is a well-known risk factor for PDA [52–56]. However, changes to ECM architecture and macrophage number and localization during pancreatitis that may precede transformation and development of robust, malignancy-associated, desmoplasia are not well defined. Along these lines, we recently described changes to the periductal space during pancreatitis that results in invasion-conducive architectures for carcinoma cells, thus priming the stroma for disease progression prior to frank malignant disease [9]. As such, we sought to define the myeloid-collagen landscape in pancreatitis to determine if architectures emerge which could provide a form of stromal priming or memory in the pancreas conducive to promoting early PDA. To address this question, we obtained human chronic pancreatitis (CP) samples with no histological signs of PDA, as determined by clinical pathologist evaluation, to determine if the relationship between collagen architecture and macrophage are similar to those present in PDA (Fig. 5A–D). Similar to PDA, pancreatitis is accompanied by a morphologically abnormal ductal structures, acinar drop out, and robust stromal regions. Notably, we observe similar collagen architecture in CP and PDA, with an accumulation of fibrillar, aligned collagen occurring in both the peri‑epithelial space and further into the distal stroma (Fig. 5E). Similar to human PDA, analysis in four independent CP patient samples shows a strong relationship between CD206+/CD68+ macrophages and aligned stromal collagen (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, the number of immunosuppressive macrophages was greatly increased in CP compared to normal patient biopsies (Fig. 6B) and the fraction of CD68+ cells expressing CD206 greatly increased when examining regions with increased collagen content (Fig. 6C). CD206+ cells also displayed significantly higher aspect ratios in regions with aligned collagen, with a maximum ratio >5X higher than the average ratio of cells in regions with low to no aligned collagen (Fig. 6D). These ratios, and their correlation with collagen alignment, are consistent with findings from macrophages in mouse and human PDA samples (Figs. 1 and 2), suggesting key stromal macrophage dynamics in CP have similarities to PDA and could help establish and robustly promote immunosuppressive microenvironment more conducive to early PDA progression.

Given the evident role of FAK in facilitating myeloid response to alignment, and the coinciding, colocalized accumulation of immunosuppressive macrophages and aligned collagen in CP, we sought to identify the presence of elongated, phospho-FAK+ macrophages in pancreatitis. Corroborating our PDA results, elongated CD68+ macrophages did indeed express pFAK at the cell membrane in all four samples examined (Fig. 6E). Moreover, these pFAK+/CD68+ cells were predominantly found in regions of highly aligned collagen. These data suggest that similar to PDA, FAK plays a novel role in the regulation of macrophage shape and function during CP, and the connectivity of extracellular matrix architecture and immune dysfunction in early PDA onset and precursory fibroinflammatory disease. Moreover, given the heightened risk of developing PDA during CP, and the rapid timeline in which PDA spreads after inception, this study collectively supports the notion that stromal architectures are priming the microenvironment to support immune suppression, evasion of anti-tumor immunity, and tumor cell dissemination from the earliest stages of pancreas cancer.

Methods

Mouse models

Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Minnesota. C57BL/B6 mice of 10–16 weeks of age were obtained from Jackson Labs for all primary immune cell isolation. Both male and female mice were utilized to account for any possible gender-based differences. For murine PDA, the KrasG12D/+; p53R172H/+;Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mouse model was employed. The KPC model are genetically engineered mice which develop spontaneous genetic aberrations specifically within pancreatic epithelial cells, giving rise to disease that is highly faithful to the human condition. Of particular importance, the KPC model closely mimics the establishment of early disease, recapitulating both the accumulation and reorganization of collagen into distinct architectures, as well as the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune infiltration and immunosuppression [5,6].

Human patient samples

Human normal, normal adjacent, chronic pancreatitis, and PDA samples were obtained from the University of Minnesota BioNet core facility, which conveys the full pathologist assessment, as either FFPE sections or as freshly resected tissues (which were processed immediate after receipt) that were obtained in compliance with approved Institutional Review Board protocols. Tissues were accompanied with pathological assessment of disease staging and other relevant information when available.

Cell isolation, lines, and culture

L929 fibroblasts were used to generate conditioned media for macrophage differentiation by culturing L929 cells in 100 mL complete DMEM in T-150 flasks. Conditioned media was collected in large batches 7–10 days post-confluence, combined, filter sterilized and stored at −20° J774 murine macrophages were cultured in T-75 flasks and passaged using cell scraping. Primary bone marrow derived macrophages were obtained by harvesting and resuspending bone marrow from wild type C57BL/B6 mice of at least 10 weeks of age. BMDMs were differentiated by plating bone marrow suspension on ultra-low adhesion plates (Corning, Cat# 29,443–030) and culturing with 20 % L929 conditioned media for seven days, except in the case of the paired T cell cocultures (see below). BMDMs were passaged using gentle pipetting. Primary CD8+ T cells were isolated from C57BL/B6 mice using the EasySep mouse CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit following manufacturer’s instructions (Stemcell technologies, Cat# 19,853). T cells were then activated and expanded in 96 well round bottom plates (Corning, Cat# 0720,095) for either 5 or 6 days using Dynabeads mouse T-activator CD3/CD28 (Life Technologies, Cat# 11452D). Half media changes were used to supplement 30 units IL-2 on days 3 and 4. On day 5, T cells were prepared differentially according to their future use described below. T cells were expanded in RPMI 1640 medium with l-glutamine (Fisher, Cat# mt10040cv), with 10 % FBS (Fisher, Cat# SH3007103), 1X Antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher, Cat#15,240,062), 25mg/mL plasmocin (Invivogen, Cat# ant-mpt). All other cell lines were cultured in complete media (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Media (media tech, Cat#MT10013CV), 10 % fetal bovine serum, 2 mM l-glutamine (Life Technologies, Cat# 25,030,081), 1X Antibiotic-Antimycotic, and 25mg/mL Plasmocin) unless otherwise specified. All culture reagents and conditioned media were filter sterilized prior to use.

Multiphoton laser-scanning microscopy

FFPE sections were imaged using a custom-built multiphoton laser scanning microscope equipped with 4 detectors for simultaneous viewing of multi-labeled samples. A Mai-Tai Ti:Sapphire laser (Spectra-Physics) was used in conjunction with filters for 440/20, 460/50, 525/50, 525/70, 595/50, 604/70, and 690/50 (Prairie Technologies / Bruker) to excite engineered fluorophores and dyes, fluorescent antibodies, and endogenous fluorescence, and also perform Second Harmonic Generation for collagen fibers. SHG signal was produced using an 890 nm excitation wavelength and captured using a blue bandpass filter with a 440–450 nm center wavelength. SHG was used to characterize collagen content and fiber orientation in tissues, described in detail in the Quantitative Analysis section. Live tumor slices were imaged while culturing in air-buffered Leibovitz’s l-15 media (Gibco, Cat# 11,415,064) using a heated stage for temperature control and monitored to keep sample temperature at 37°F. KPCT tissues were visualized by exciting tdTomato with a wavelength of 800 nm, and fluorescence emission was detected using a red 595 filter. Table S1 lists reagents and wavelengths for antibodies.

2.5D nanopatterned substrates for alignment studies

BMDMs were seeded onto control glass insets of 35 mm flat control dishes (MatTek, P35G-1.0–20-C) or nanopatterned “2.5D” dishes for ECM alignment (Curibio, ANFS-0001–10) [15] at 300,000 cells per dish in 600uL media for 6 h to allow adherence to inset. After 6 h, 1.4 mL complete media was overlayed, and cells were cultured for 24 h for assessment of arginase-1 and pFAK expression.

Macrophage stimulation protocol

Macrophages were stimulated with tumor conditioned media collected from primary tumor cells previously isolated from KPC tumors using methods previously described [19]. Tumor cells were grown 3 days passed confluence in T-75 flasks, media was collected, filtered, and mixed 1:1 with complete DMEM. Macrophages stimulated were stimulated with 20 ng/mL LPS (MilliporeSigma Cat# L3129) and 20 ng/mL IFNγ (Abcam Cat# ab9922) or 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-4 (MilliporeSigma, Cat# I1020–5ug) and 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-13 (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 413ML005) for 24 or 48 h depending on subsequent experiments.

Paired T cell and BMDM experiments

Spleens and matched bone marrow were isolated from C57BL/B6 mice. BMDMs were isolated as described above and cultured in 20 % L929 for 5 days after which they were harvested, plated at (200,000 cells in 600uL) into 24 well nanopatterned (Curibio, Cat# anfs-0024) or unpatterned (Cellvis, Cat# NC0397150) plates, and left to adhere overnight. T cells were isolated and expanded as described above for 5 days, after which they were differentially prepared for coculture experiments:

Transwell

On day 5, T cells were collected, magnetic beads were removed, and T cells were reseeded in RPMI with 1 % FBS overnight. On day 6, 8um 6.5 mm transwell inserts (Fisher Cat# 07–200–174) were prepared by coating with 100uL 50ug/mL high protein rat tail collagen I (Fisher, Cat# CB35249) suspended in sterile 1X PBS (Gibco, Cat# 10,010–049) for 1 hour at RT. Membranes were gently washed with PBS and placed above BMDMs. Prepared membranes were overlaid with 100uL 1 % RPMI containing 500,000 T cells and incubated at 37° for 6 h. Membranes were fixed with ethanol and stained using 0.2 % crystal violet (sigma, Cat# C0775–25 g) and imaged. FIJI was used to process images and quantify percent area positive for crystal violet in each field of view.

Proliferation

On day 5, T cells were supplemented with IL-2. T cells were harvested on day 6, magnetic beads were removed, and cells were stained in serum free RPMI using CellTrace™ CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen) at 1:1000 for 15 min in a 37° water bath. Cells were rinsed with complete RPMI, resuspended in complete DMEM at a density of 1.5 million T cells per 100 uL. 100 uL T cell suspension was added directly on top of BMDMs. After 3 days of coculture, T cells were harvested using gentle pipetting and stained 1:100 with PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD8a antibody (Biolegend, Clone 53–6.7) for 30 min on ice protected from light. Proliferation was quantified using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer by measuring fluorescence dilution by CD8+ cells.

Immunofluorescence

Formalin fixed paraffin embedded tissues were stained for CD206 (1:100), arginase-1 (1:50), F4/80 (1:100), CD68 (1:100), and phospho-FAK Y925 (1:100). Briefly, sections were baked, dewaxed with xylene, and rehydrated with ethanol. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed by boiling Trilogy buffer and slides for 20 min. Samples were permeabilized using 0.3 % Triton X-100 in 1X TBS, and blocked using 10 % goat serum and 1 % BSA for 1 hour at RT. Primary antibodies incubated overnight at 4° in 1 % serum + TBST. Secondary antibodies were incubated at 1:200 with Draq5 (Biolegend, Cat# 424,101) at 1:500 for 1 hour at RT. Slides were mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade Reagent (Fisher, Cat# P36934) and imaged using multiphoton laser scanning microscopy (MPLSM) (Table S1 lists reagents and wavelengths).

Slices were washed with PBS, fixed with 0.02 % PFA for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 in PBS for at least 30 min, and blocked overnight with 2 % fatty acid free BSA + 5 % goat serum at 4° Primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4° at 1:100, with consideration of the added volume of the tissue. Slices were washed for one hour in PBS-Triton, and were stained for 2 h using secondary antibody at 1:100. Sections were washed for at least 30 min before imaging using MPLSM.

Adherent cultures were fixed with either ice cold methanol or PFA for 10–15 min, permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 in 1X PBS, and blocked for 1 hour at RT with 1 % BSA, 10 % serum, 0.3 M glycine, and in PBS-Triton. Hoescht dye was used at 1:10,000 to visualize nuclei.

FAK inhibition In vitro and in vivo

For In vitro studies macrophages where subjected to either 1uM/mL VS-4718 or DMSO control treatment. For in vivo studies, the KPC mouse model was employed. Control and FAKi samples were obtained from Dr. David Denardo and have been previously described [7]. Briefly, 50mg/kg VS-4718 was administered by oral gavage twice a day in a formulation with 0.5 % carboxymethyl cellulose and 0.1 % Tween-80 (Sigma) in sterile inhibitor. Primary BMDMs were pretreated with 1 uM/mL VS-4718 FAK inhibitor or vehicle control (DMSO) for 24 h before plating on aligned nanopatterned substrates. Cells were grown for 24 h to allow adherence and response to alignment in the presence of inhibitor or vehicle.

Quantitative and statistical analysis

Collagen content was quantified using FIJI to quantify SHG signal. Cellular orientation and collagen fiber orientation were quantified using Curvelet Analysis and CT-FIRE and MatLab,as we have described in detail [9,15,57] and correlated using regression and Pearson’s correlation. Grading of regions with high, medium, and low collagen content or alignment was performed blindly. The distance of macrophages to the nearest collagen fiber was calculated by measuring the pixel distance between a cell and its nearest collagen fiber in pixels and converting pixels to microns. CT-FIRE was also used to characterize focal adhesion distribution by treating striated focal adhesions as “fibers” to characterize adhesion angle with respect to alignment of nanopatterned topographies (Fiber Angle). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) levels in macrophages was quantified using ImageJ. All statistical analyses were performed using Students t-test or Welch’s t-test between two samples, and one-way ANOVA with posthoc testing for comparison across data sets with greater than two conditions for comparison of one variable.

Discussion

Macrophages are one of the most abundant immune cells found in the tumor microenvironment of several different solid tumor types, including PDA, and their presence is correlated with poor clinical outcomes and metastatic disease [24–27,32,52]. This can be largely attributed to pro-tumor signaling and robust immune suppression that can effectively limit anti-tumor immune responses. Furthermore, their flexible functionality makes them attractive targets for therapeutic intervention, posing an interesting possibility to reprogram native patient immunity to kill cancer cells, rather than utilizing toxic chemotherapeutics. Emerging evidence also suggests that macrophages respond to mechanical stimuli, and thus highlights potential utility of targeting FAK signaling and/or disrupting ECM levels and aligned architectures to reduce or perhaps alleviate immunosuppression in pancreas cancer. However, much of the study of how local mechanics and mechanotransduction influence cancer progression has largely prioritized study of cancer cells, limiting our understanding of how immune cells perceive and respond to their physical environment from early inflammation to late-stage disease. Yet, defining this behavior will likely play key roles in our understanding of how to successfully tilt the balance to suppress tumor progression while enhancing anti-tumor immunity. As such, here, we establish a cause-and-effect relationship between stromal architecture and macrophage behavior. Interrogation of fixed pancreas samples and live cell experiment revealed extensive macrophage elongation at sites of dense, aligned collagen. Moreover, macrophage localization and morphology also correlated with increased expression of canonical pro-tumor immunosuppressive markers. Interestingly, our data suggests that while ECM architecture directly promotes TAM polarization and immunosuppressive behavior, it is also clear that polarization markers can be reduced by disrupting the TAM-ECM mechanosignaling interaction. This raises interesting questions regarding the plastic nature of polarization and in particular how levels of immunosuppressive behavior are modulated. Indeed, our observations that ECM alignment enhances immunosuppressive behavior of TAMs while disrupting the TAM-ECM interaction reduces this behavior, but does not completely ablate it, suggests that a spectrum of states and behaviors exists that can be modulated by the physical properties of TMEs, highlighting the need for deeper study into these dynamics.

Further, our functional studies reveal that aligned topographies increase macrophage-mediated suppression of cytotoxic T cell expansion and chemotaxis. This shift in macrophage behavior was at least partially attributable to increased expression and activity of focal adhesion kinase in response to alignment. Upregulated in many solid tumors, FAK has an established role in promoting processes like tumor cell invasion, migration, proliferation, and has a primary role in orchestrating cellular responses to mechanical stimuli which lead to increased invasiveness and correlate poor patient prognosis. In this study, consistent with other reports [7,9], in vivo data from mice suggests FAK inhibition decreases 1) collagen deposition and remodeling into TACS-2 and -3 signatures conducive to invasive disease and immunosuppression, and 2) myeloid numbers and polarization in aligned ECMs. This raises interesting questions regarding the spatiotemporal dynamics of the collagen-TAM interaction in vivo with regards to ECM-alignment regulation of TAM shape and polarization. Under FAKi, ECM levels and organization are decreased, which our data suggests would decrease immunosuppressive TAM behavior. However, we also demonstrate that FAKi directly disrupts adhesion signaling that regulates the TAM response to aligned ECM. As such, it is difficult to fully discern the order of TAM-regulating events, which may in fact be a dynamic, reciprocal, interaction over time or may be additive, particularly since the TAM polarization and immunosuppressive behavior are modulated by ECM but not fully suppressed in less permissive environments. That is, ECM alignment clearly enhances immunosuppressive TAM behavior, but in the absence of ECM alignment TAM behavior is not fully suppressed but instead at a lower level. While In vitro studies with highly aligned ECM provide great mechanistic insight, it is clear that our understanding of these physical mechanisms that regulate TAM behavior requires additional study, particularly in the in vivo setting.

This work highlights a novel relationship between collagen alignment and macrophage function in the pancreas, and is the only to our knowledge to establish a link between physical cues and myeloid cells not only in PDA, but also in chronic pancreatitis. While the connections between chronic pancreatitis and PDA are not fully defined, CP is a long-accepted risk factor of PDA and parallels PDA involution in many facets, including the appearance of ductal structures and PanIN lesions, acinar dropout, and extreme inter- and intralobular fibrogenesis [52–54,58,59]. Importantly, CP often exhibits a fibro-inflammatory reaction nearly indistinguishable from PDA, including high levels of collagen deposition and alignment, and a coinciding massive accumulation of immunosuppressive macrophages [56,60,61]. Therefore, CP samples provide an opportunity to understand how collagen architecture correlates with macrophage phenotype in the absence of an apparent transformation event. Here, we show an undeniable link between collagen architecture, macrophage distribution, and macrophage phenotype in human chronic pancreatitis biopsies which perfectly mirrors the correlation we observed in frank PDA in both mouse and human biopsies. However, we do note that our CP patient cohort size is limited and increasing sample numbers would further strengthen our ability to understand key CP architectures that influence disease, an endeavor that our data suggests is worth undertaking in order to gain critical insight into how CP shapes the pre-malignant pancreatic landscape. Indeed, the broader significance of this work indicates macrophage elongation and their resultant immunosuppressive phenotype is driven by stromal architectures, beginning early in preceding and parallel inflammatory disease states, irrespective of cancer onset. Alternatively stated, this work suggests the pancreatic microenvironment is primed to support tumorigenesis, immune evasion, and metastasis long before genetic transformation has occurred.

In further support of the notion of microenvironment priming and memory, a mounting body of literature suggests the pro-tumor pancreatic microenvironment may indeed precede the emergence of tumor cells, serving to support the early, rapid, and robust dissemination of cancer cells upon PDA inception. Given the heightened risk of developing PDA after chronic pancreatitis, and the rapid timeline in which PDA is established and massively disseminated, it is extremely likely these stromal architectures prime the microenvironment to support immune suppression, immune evasion, and tumor cell dissemination from the earliest stages of, or perhaps even prior to any detectable sign of, pancreas cancer. Intriguingly, some of the earliest studies interrogating the connection between pancreatitis and PDA discovered patients who underwent pancreatitis resection surgery could still develop PDA metastases, even in the absence of any histopathological evidence of primary PDA. Moreover, studies in mice have shown that induced pancreatitis is sufficient to invoke circulating epithelial cells and an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition expression profile reminiscent of metastasizing cancer cells, again in lieu of any primary pancreatic dysplasia. Further, it is widely accepted that inflammation precedes, exacerbates, and foreshadows cancer in several contexts, and while this work does not conclusively define a mechanistic connection between pancreatitis, myeloid cells, and dissemination, it does support a need to further study the connection between precursor inflammation and PDA with the intent of informing new treatment strategies for high-risk, pre-invasive fibrosis. There is a desperate need to explore proactive, preventative treatments in addition to the existing reactive, inadequate regimes. For example, is it possible to aggressively treat pancreatitis with antifibrotic therapeutics as a preventative strategy to abrogate or even slow PDA inception and spread? Identifying the ways in which mechanical cues influence patient immunity will be key to allowing us to tilt the scales back in favor of the patient.
