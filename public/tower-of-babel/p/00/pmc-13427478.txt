Metabolomic identification of antibacterial 5‑indanol from Amomum tsaoko fruits and the application of its stem‑leaf by‑products as feed additives for Partridge Shank chickens
Authors: Yinqiang Zi, Guohui Liao, Yuyi Zhang, Yuping Wu, Ping Li, Jian Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmomum tsaoko is a medicinal and edible Zingiberaceae herb, while its stems and leaves are largely discarded and underutilized compared with its fruits. This study aimed to characterize the antibacterial active components of A. tsaoko stem and leaf ethyl acetate extract, clarify the antibacterial mechanism of core metabolite 5-indanol, and evaluate the application value of A. tsaoko stem-leaf by-products as green feed additives for Partridge Shank chickens. In vitro antibacterial assays combined with GC-MS metabolomics were performed on four Zingiberaceae species (A. tsaoko, Alpinia officinarum, A. villosum, A. oxyphylla); broth microdilution, time-kill curve, biofilm and cell membrane leakage tests were conducted to verify the bactericidal mode of 5-indanol. For the animal feeding trial, 30-day-old Partridge Shank chickens were randomly divided into five groups: control group (CK, basal diet), 0.05% A. tsaoko stem-leaf group (SL0.05), 0.10% stem-leaf group (SL0.10), 0.15% stem-leaf group (SL0.15), and positive control group supplemented with 0.10% A. tsaoko fruit (F0.10). Growth performance, meat quality, small intestinal morphology and cecal microbiota were systematically detected. The in vitro results revealed that ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko stems and leaves exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with an MIC of 62.5 μg/mL against Staphylococcus aureus. Metabolomic screening identified 5-indanol as the dominant antibacterial marker compound, whose antibacterial efficacy was 20-fold higher than 2,3-dihydro-1H-indene-4-carbaldehyde. Mechanistically, 5-indanol disrupted bacterial membrane integrity, triggered leakage of intracellular biomacromolecules, suppressed biofilm formation and attenuated hemolytic virulence of S. aureus. In vivo data indicated that 0.10% dietary supplementation of A. tsaoko stems and leaves (SL0.10) achieved the optimal effect: Dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko significantly increased the average daily gain and reduced the feed conversion ratio of Partridge Shank chickens, with the most pronounced effect observed in the 0.10% stem-leaf group. The drip loss of breast and thigh muscle was decreased without adverse impacts on meat pH, color and tenderness. The villus height/crypt depth ratios of duodenum and ileum were elevated by 34.2% and 26.43%, respectively. Meanwhile, the relative abundances of beneficial phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes and Ascomycota in gut microbiota were significantly enriched, and the alpha diversity of bacterial and fungal communities was improved. Collectively, 5-indanol is the key antibacterial component in the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko. Waste stems and leaves of A. tsaoko can be developed as novel antibiotic-free phytogenic feed additives, which realizes full-plant high-value utilization of A. tsaoko agricultural by-products and provides theoretical support for sustainable green livestock breeding.Keywords: Amomum tsaoko, 5-Indanol, Antibacterial mechanism, Gut microbiota, Partridge Shank chickens

Introduction

Amomum tsaoko Crevost et Lemarié is a perennial herbaceous species in the family Zingiberaceae, mainly cultivated in Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi provinces of China, with additional distribution in parts of northern Vietnam and Laos (Liao et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022a, Li et al., 2023). The plant is widely used as a culinary spice and condiment, and in traditional Chinese medicine as an aromatic herb for resolving dampness, highlighting its significant economic and medicinal importance (Zhang et al., 2022b; Lee et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025).

The inappropriate use of antibiotics, particularly their routine addition to animal feed, has significantly contributed to the rise of bacterial resistance (Mashauri et al., 2025; Mdegela et al., 2021). Today, multidrug-resistant bacterial infections pose a serious threat to global public health, a crisis exacerbated by the declining development of new antibiotics (Pica, 2026). Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel antibiotics or alternative antimicrobial strategies (Malkawi et al., 2025; Elshobary et al., 2025).

A. tsaoko exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Its essential oil and extracts show inhibitory effects against strains such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhimurium, and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Peng et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2016). These findings indicate the potential of A. tsaoko as a source of natural antimicrobial agents.

Due to population growth and agricultural expansion, large amounts of agricultural waste are generated worldwide, often incinerated or left to accumulate in fields (Rao and Rathod, 2019). In traditional production systems, only the fruits or medicinal parts of plants are utilized, while stems and leaves are neglected. However, plant stem and leaf wastes contain substantial bioactive substances. For instance, discarded tea stems contain higher concentrations of polyphenols and caffeine than tea leaves (Zhang et al., 2022a), and tea waste derivatives can be used for environmental remediation (Debnath et al., 2022). Banana by-products, rich in nutrients and bioactive compounds, have potential as natural growth promoters and prebiotics in animal production (Saeed et al., 2025). Similarly, the stems and leaves of A. tsaoko contain volatile oils (0.2%–0.97%) rich in terpenoid compounds such as eucalyptol, α-pinene, and d-limonene (Yang et al., 2019), and their essential oil exhibits stronger Fe³⁺ reducing capacity than those from roots or flowers (Yang et al., 2020), suggesting that the stems and leaves possess potential pharmacological value.

Chinese medicinal herbs contain bioactive components such as flavonoids and polyphenols, which possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. These compounds are derived from a wide range of sources, exhibit low residue levels, and have a lower potential to induce resistance than antibiotics, making them promising alternatives as functional feed additives (Zhou et al., 2025; Abdelli et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2025). Studies have demonstrated that plant-derived feed additives can improve average daily gain, feed conversion efficiency, and immune function in broilers, while also modulating gut microbiota (Khan et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Future research should integrate multidisciplinary approaches to elucidate the synergistic mechanisms of active components in Chinese herbs and clarify dose–effect relationships, providing a scientific foundation for the development of antibiotic‑free feed additives.

Although A. tsaoko exhibits antibacterial activity, previous studies have primarily focused on its fruits, while its stems and leaves are often discarded or underutilized. Therefore, to further evaluate the resource utilization potential of A. tsaoko stems and leaves, the present study was conducted to: analyze the differential metabolites in the ethyl acetate extracts of A. tsaoko, A. officinarum, A. villosum, and A. oxyphylla; explore the antibacterial mechanism of 5-indanol, a key bioactive component in the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko; and investigate the effects of A. tsaoko‑based feed on the growth performance, meat quality, intestinal morphology, and cecal microbiota of Partridge Shank chickens. This study provides a theoretical basis for the subsequent application of A. tsaoko stems and leaves in feed.

Experimental materials and methods

Experimental materials

In this study, fruits of A. tsaoko, A. officinarum, A. villosum, and A. oxyphylla were purchased from Wanyi Pharmacy in Guangzhou, Guangdong, China. Stem and leaf samples of these plants were collected from seedlings propagated in our laboratory at the Ecological Farm of South China Agricultural University. The seedlings were regularly watered and fertilized, and the aboveground stems and leaves were harvested when the plants reached the vegetative stage. Five bacterial strains, including Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC-6538) and Escherichia coli (ATCC-25922), were purchased from Beijing Biobw Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Thirty-day-old F1 fast-growing Partridge Shank chickens (initial body weight approximately 0.5 kg) were obtained from Guangzhou Xiangxin Rare Poultry Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The basal diet ingredients (corn, soybean meal, etc.) for these chickens were purchased from Jiahui Feed Co., Ltd., and the premixes and mycotoxin binders were obtained from Chaoguo Animal Health Co., Ltd.

Study on the antibacterial effects of different plant-derived extracts on five pathogenic microorganisms

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) against common pathogenic bacteria were determined following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, using the broth microdilution method (Santos et al., 2020; Petersen et al., 2006). Briefly, 50 μL of sterile cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) was added to each well of a 96‑well plate. Twofold serial dilutions of the test solution were prepared in rows 1 to 10. Column 11 served as the penicillin control (positive antibiotic control), and column 12 served as the sterile CAMHB negative control (no bacteria). Subsequently, 50 μL of bacterial suspension (adjusted to approximately 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL) was added to all wells except the negative control wells.The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the test solution that resulted in no visible bacterial growth. For MBC determination, 10 μL of the broth from each well showing no visible growth (including the MIC well and higher concentrations) was subcultured onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates and incubated at 37 °C for another 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration that yielded no visible bacterial colonies on the TSA plates.

Analysis of differential metabolites of extracts from A. tsaoko, A. officinarum, A. villosum, and A. oxyphylla

Based on the antibacterial activity assay results described above, the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity against Staphylococcus aureus. Therefore, in the present study, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC‑MS) analysis was performed on the ethyl acetate extracts of A. tsaoko, A. officinarum, A. villosum, and A. oxyphylla. Differential metabolites among these four species were further analyzed to identify potential antibacterial components that may be responsible for the observed activity (Konappa et al., 2020).

Antibacterial activity testing and verification of 5-indanol and 2,3‑dihydro‑1H‑indene‑4‑carbaldehyde in A. tsaoko

Based on the aforementioned results, the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 5‑indanol and 2,3‑dihydro‑1H‑indene‑4‑carbaldehyde against S. aureus were determined using the broth microdilution method according to CLSI guidelines (Akilandeswari and Ruckmani, 2016). Subsequently, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC‑MS) analysis was performed, and the characteristic chromatographic peaks of 5‑indanol and 2,3‑dihydro‑1H‑indene‑4‑carbaldehyde were identified by comparing their retention times and mass‑to‑charge ratios (m/z) with those of authentic standards. In addition, time–kill curves were used to assess the bacterial viability of S. aureus treated with different concentrations of 5‑indanol, in order to determine its bactericidal kinetics and the duration of its antibacterial effect.

Biological characteristics related to staphylococcus aureus

Bacteria were cultured in TSB medium to form biofilms. Two experimental conditions were tested: (1) co‑incubation – the drug solution was added simultaneously with the bacterial suspension (to assess inhibition of biofilm formation); (2) disruption – the drug was added after biofilm had been established (to assess disruption of pre‑formed biofilms). After treatment, the biofilms were stained with crystal violet, dissolved in 95% ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The biofilm inhibition rate (or formation rate) was then calculated. To measure the contents of extracellular nucleic acids, glucose, total protein, and alkaline phosphatase (AKP), bacterial cultures were first incubated in LB medium, adjusted to a standardized concentration (e.g., 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL), and then incubated with varying concentrations of the drug solution. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected. Nucleic acids were quantified using a spectrophotometer, while glucose, total protein, and AKP were measured using commercial kits (glucose detection kit, total protein kit, and AKP kit, respectively). Bacterial suspensions were cultured in TSB medium and treated with the drug. The culture supernatant was collected and incubated with defibrinated rabbit blood. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 543 nm, and the hemolysis percentage was calculated. Appropriate controls were included in all experiments, and each sample was assayed in triplicate.

Feed formulation and feeding

The stems, leaves, and fruits of A. tsaoko were dried, ground, and sieved through a 40‑mesh sieve. The stems and leaves were mixed into the basal diet at the levels of 0.05%, 0.10%, and 0.15%, respectively, while the fruits were mixed at the level of 0.10%. Thirty‑day‑old Partridge Shank chicken were randomly divided into 5 groups (20 birds per group): the control group (CK, fed with basal diet), the low‑dose stem‑leaf group (SL0.05, fed with basal diet containing 0.05% stems and leaves), the medium‑dose stem‑leaf group (SL0.10, fed with basal diet containing 0.10% stems and leaves), the high‑dose stem‑leaf group (SL0.15, fed with basal diet containing 0.15% stems and leaves), and the fruit group (F0.10, fed with basal diet containing 0.10% fruits). All chickens were reared in fully enclosed pens, with free access to feed and drinking water.

The fruit supplementation level (0.10%) was selected based on our preliminary dose‑screening experiments in the same chicken model (unpublished data), in which this concentration showed optimal efficacy. Therefore, the fruit group was designed as a positive reference, whereas the stem and leaf groups were tested at graded levels to establish their dose‑response relationship.

Production performance measurement

Feed intake was recorded regularly. Animals were weighed every three days to calculate the weight gain. After slaughter, the carcass weight, half-gutted weight and other related weights were measured, and the feed-to-meat ratio was calculated (Ren et al., 2025).

Meat quality determination

The drip loss rate, shear force at different time points, pH value, and color (l*, a*, b*) of breast and thigh muscles were determined (Lipiński et al., 2019).

Intestinal tissue processing and observation

Intestinal Tissue Processing and Observation: Tissue samples such as the duodenum were fixed or rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, followed by paraffin section preparation and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Under a microscope, the villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the VH/CD ratio (V/C ratio) were measured (Liu et al., 2023).

Cecal microbiota analysis

The cecal chyme was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at -80°C, and then sequenced by the Meiji Bio platform. Subsequent steps included quality assessment, Alpha/Beta diversity analysis, screening of intergroup differential microbial communities, and visualization of the results.

Statistical analysis software

The data analysis and visualization in this paper were performed using the following software: Origin, GraphPad, SPSS, and the Meiji Bioinformatics Platform.

Results

Antibacterial activity test of extracts from different solvents

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) results are presented in Table S2. For each of the four Zingiberaceae plants, the methanol, ethanol, and ethyl acetate extracts of the fruits all exhibited antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus. Statistical analysis revealed that the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko showed significantly stronger activity than those of the other three plants, with an MIC of 62.5 μg/mL (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko also displayed antibacterial activity against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and E. faecalis, indicating a broad‑spectrum antibacterial activity. In contrast, only three of the extracts (specific ones not listed here) showed inhibitory effects against P. aeruginosa and E. faecalis, and the required concentrations were higher than those needed against S. aureus, which may be attributed to differences in bacterial cell wall composition or permeability.

Analysis of differential metabolites in ethyl acetate extracts of A. tsaoko, A. officinarum, A. villosum, and A. oxyphylla

Volatile compounds in the fruits of four Zingiberaceae medicinal plants were analyzed by GC-MS, and a total of 122 compounds were identified after screening (Fig. 1). PCA revealed that the first two principal components accounted for 85.80% of the total variance, with significant differences in volatile compositions among the four fruits, which could be clearly distinguished by GC-MS (Fig. 1a). Using OPLS‑DA, 31 characteristic compounds with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 (e.g., camphor, 5-indanol) were screened. A 200-time permutation test verified that the model was reliable and free of overfitting (Fig. 1b,c). Cluster heatmap analysis showed satisfactory clustering of samples. Relatively high contents of differential metabolites including 5-indanol and n-octanal were observed in A. tsaoko, which might be crucial factors responsible for its antibacterial activity (Fig. 1d).

Antibacterial activity testing and verification of 5-indanol and 2,3-Dihydro-1H-indene-4-carbaldehyde in A. tsaoko

Determination using the CLSI checkerboard method showed that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 5-indanol against Staphylococcus aureus was 62.5 μg/mL, and its antibacterial efficacy was 20 times that of 2,3-dihydro-1H-indene-4-carbaldehyde (MIC = 1250 μg/mL) (Table S3). The excellent antibacterial effect of 5-indanol against Staphylococcus aureus was further verified by the spot-on-plate assay (Fig. 2a). Comparison with reference standards confirmed that these two characteristic compounds were specifically detected only in the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko (Fig. 2b). Time-kill curve analysis revealed that 31.25 μg/mL 5-indanol had no antibacterial activity, while 62.5 μg/mL exhibited inhibitory effects. At concentrations of 125 μg/mL and 250 μg/mL, bactericidal effects were observed after 8 h and 4 h, respectively, with complete bacterial eradication ultimately achieved. In contrast, penicillin exerted bactericidal activity within 2 h (Fig. 2c).

Study on the mechanism of action of 5-indanol on staphylococcus aureus

The disruption of S. aureus biofilms by 5-indanol was concentration‑dependent, achieving a disruption rate of 79.76% at 4 × MIC, although this effect was weaker than that of penicillin at the same multiple of its MIC (Fig. 3a). Mechanistically, 5-indanol compromised the integrity of the bacterial cell membrane and cell wall. At concentrations ranging from 0.5 × to 4 × MIC, 5-indanol significantly inhibited hemolysin production in S. aureus, reducing the hemolysis rate to 24.72%–62.85% and thereby attenuating bacterial virulence (Fig. 3b). Extracellular alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was significantly elevated at 2–4 × MIC (Fig. 3c), and total extracellular protein content was markedly increased at 1–4 × MIC (Fig. 3d), indicating leakage of intracellular biomacromolecules. Furthermore, extracellular glucose content was significantly increased at 2–4 × MIC (Fig. 3e).

The effect of A. tsaoko feed on the growth performance and slaughter performance of Partridge Shank chickens

Dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko stem and leaf at 0.10% (SL0.10) and 0.15% (SL0.15), as well as A. tsaoko fruit at 0.10% (F0.10), significantly promoted the growth of indigenous chickens (P < 0.05; Fig. 4a). The SL0.10 group achieved the highest body weight gain. Moreover, all supplementation groups reduced the feed conversion ratio (FCR), with the 0.10% stem and leaf group exhibiting the most pronounced effect (Fig. 4b, c, d). The live weights of the SL0.10, SL0.15, and F0.10 groups were significantly higher than that of the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4b). Compared with the control group, the SL0.05, SL0.10, and F0.10 groups showed significantly higher eviscerated carcass weight, half‑eviscerated carcass weight, and breast muscle weight (P < 0.05). Additionally, thigh muscle weight tended to increase following A. tsaoko supplementation, with the highest value observed in the F0.10 group (Fig. 4f, g, h).

The effect of A. tsaoko feed on the meat quality of Partridge Shank chickens

Dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko reduced the drip loss of both breast and thigh muscles in indigenous chickens, thereby improving their water-holding capacity (Fig. 5a, b). A. tsaoko supplementation did not adversely affect meat tenderness, as no significant decrease in tenderness was observed (Fig. 5c, d). At 45 min post‑slaughter, the pH of all A. tsaoko‑supplemented groups exceeded 6.0, indicating no risk of pale, soft, exudative (PSE) meat. At 24 h post‑slaughter, the pH of breast muscle remained within the ideal range of 5.4–5.8, which is typical for normal chicken meat. Compared with the control group, the pH values of thigh and breast muscles in all A. tsaoko groups showed no significant changes at either 45 min or 24 h post‑slaughter, suggesting that A. tsaoko does not significantly affect post‑slaughter pH (Fig. 5e, f, g, h). At 45 min post‑slaughter, the l* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values of both breast and thigh muscles were within the normal range for chicken meat, with no significant differences among groups. At 24 h post‑slaughter, these color parameters also showed no significant changes compared with the control group, indicating that A. tsaoko does not affect post‑slaughter meat color (Fig. 5i, j, k, l).

The effect of A. tsaoko feed on the intestines of Partridge Shank chickens

The ratio of villus height to crypt depth (VH/CD) is a key indicator of intestinal health. Villus height (VH) is positively associated with nutrient absorptive capacity, whereas crypt depth (CD) reflects the renewal rate of intestinal epithelial cells. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that, compared with the control group (which exhibited shorter and loosely arranged villi), the duodenal, jejunal, and ileal villi of indigenous chickens in the A. tsaoko supplementation groups were longer, more densely arranged, and contained a greater number of goblet cells (Fig. 6a).The VH/CD values in the duodenum and jejunum increased in a concentration‑dependent manner. In the ileum, the most marked improvement was observed: the 0.05% and 0.10% A. tsaoko stem‑and‑leaf groups increased VH/CD by 20.91% and 26.43%, respectively, compared with the control group. The 0.10% A. tsaoko fruit group also showed an increase in ileal VH/CD (Fig. 6b).Collectively, these results indicate that dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko enhances the digestive and absorptive capacity of indigenous chickens by improving intestinal morphology.

The effects of dietary A. tsaoko supplementation on the alpha diversity of gut microbiota in indigenous chickens were analyzed using the two‑sample Wilcoxon rank‑sum test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Fig. 7). Bacterial community richness (Chao1 index) increased in a concentration‑dependent manner with increasing levels of A. tsaoko stem and leaf supplementation. The 0.15% stem‑and‑leaf group showed a significantly higher Chao1 index than the control group (P < 0.05). For fungal community richness, no significant differences were observed among groups; however, a numerical upward trend was noted with increasing stem‑and‑leaf levels. Overall bacterial diversity exhibited a concentration‑dependent but non‑monotonic pattern: it decreased at 0.05% stem‑and‑leaf supplementation, whereas it increased significantly at 0.15% supplementation. Fungal diversity showed a clear upward trend across treatment groups. Both the 0.15% A. tsaoko stem‑and‑leaf group and the 0.10% A. tsaoko fruit group significantly increased fungal community diversity (P < 0.05). In summary, dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko can effectively enhance both the richness and diversity of the gut microbiota in indigenous chickens.

In summary, dietary A. tsaoko supplementation significantly alters the community structure and relative abundance of gut microbiota in indigenous chickens at the phylum level.

Discussion

This study found that the organic extracts of the fruits of four Zingiberaceae plants all exhibited antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, among which the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko showed the strongest activity (MIC = 62.5 μg/mL). This extract was also effective against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and E. faecalis, indicating broad‑spectrum antibacterial properties. This result is consistent with the findings of Liu et al., who reported the potent antibacterial effect of the lipophilic extract of A. tsaoko (Liu et al., 2018). Notably, P. aeruginosa and E. faecalis were relatively less sensitive to the extracts. This reduced sensitivity is closely related to the structure of bacterial cell walls: the outer membrane barrier of Gram‑negative bacteria (e.g., P. aeruginosa) and the thick peptidoglycan layer of E. faecalis can hinder the penetration of active components. These observations are consistent with the findings of Zhang et al., who reported that membrane structure influences the activity of Zingiberaceae extracts (Zhang et al., 2022c).

Based on the above antibacterial findings, a total of 122 compounds were identified from the fruits of four Zingiberaceae plants using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC‑MS). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‑DA) effectively distinguished the compositional differences among the four species. The clustering heatmap revealed that compounds such as 5‑indanol were relatively abundant in A. tsaoko, suggesting a potential association with the superior antibacterial activity of this plant. This hypothesis is consistent with the findings of Li et al., who confirmed that volatile components in A. tsaoko essential oil exert strong inhibitory effects against Staphylococcus aureus (Li et al., 2022b). To validate this hypothesis, the antibacterial activity of 5‑indanol was assessed using the CLSI broth microdilution method. The results showed that its MIC against S. aureus was 62.5 μg/mL, exhibiting 20‑fold higher activity than 2,3‑dihydro‑1H‑indene‑4‑carbaldehyde. Notably, both compounds were exclusively detected in the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko. Time‑kill curve analysis demonstrated that the bactericidal activity of 5‑indanol was concentration‑dependent. Although its bactericidal rate was slower than that of penicillin at the same concentration (4 × MIC), it still exhibited considerable bactericidal potential, consistent with the general characteristics of natural antimicrobial agents reported by Tebeje et al. (Tebeje et al., 2025). Further mechanistic investigation revealed that 5‑indanol disrupted S. aureus biofilms in a concentration‑dependent manner, achieving a disruption rate of 79.76% at 4 × MIC. The underlying mechanism involved compromised bacterial membrane integrity, as evidenced by inhibited hemolysin production and increased leakage of intracellular biomacromolecules and glucose into the extracellular medium. This mode of action is consistent with the findings of Yao et al., who demonstrated that natural compounds exert anti‑biofilm effects by compromising bacterial membrane integrity (Yao et al., 2024).

Based on the aforementioned in vitro antibacterial activity and compositional analysis, we further investigated the in vivo effects of A. tsaoko supplementation. The results showed that both the stems/leaves and fruits of A. tsaoko significantly improved the growth and slaughter performance of Partridge Shank chickens. The SL0.10 and SL0.15 groups, as well as the F0.10 group, significantly promoted growth (P < 0.05). Among these, the SL0.10 group achieved the highest weight gain and the lowest feed conversion ratio (FCR). Multiple A. tsaoko‑supplemented groups showed significantly higher live weight, eviscerated carcass weight, half‑eviscerated carcass weight, and breast muscle weight compared with the control group (P < 0.05). Additionally, thigh muscle weight exhibited an increasing trend. These findings are consistent with Wen et al. (Wen et al., 2020), who reported that extracts of Zingiberaceae plants can improve growth performance and muscle development in poultry. This suggests that the active components in A. tsaoko exert growth‑promoting effects by modulating digestion, absorption, or metabolism.

Further analysis revealed that dietary A. tsaoko significantly reduced the drip loss rate of the breast and thigh muscles in Partridge Shank chickens, suggesting an improvement in muscle water‑holding capacity. This effect may be attributed to the bioactive components abundant in the stems and leaves of A. tsaoko. Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2019) reported that the essential oil from the stems and leaves is rich in terpenoid compounds such as eucalyptol, α‑pinene, and d‑limonene, all of which possess potent antioxidant activity. Furthermore, Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2020) confirmed that the essential oil from the stems and leaves exhibited an Fe³⁺ reducing power of 77.91 ± 0.38 μmol Trolox/g, which may reduce drip loss by mitigating muscle lipid oxidation and preserving cell membrane integrity. In a study on Xichou black‑bone chickens, Huang et al. (Huang et al., 2024) also found that dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko stems and leaves significantly increased muscle protein and fat content, thereby improving muscle nutritional composition. Regarding pH and meat color, no significant differences were observed between the A. tsaoko‑supplemented groups and the control group in l, a, and b* values at 45 min and 24 h post‑slaughter, all of which remained within the normal range for chicken meat. These results indicate that A. tsaoko does not interfere with normal post‑slaughter glycolysis and has no adverse effects on chicken meat quality.

The aforementioned improvements in growth performance and meat quality may be closely related to the regulatory effects of A. tsaoko on intestinal health. This study found that A. tsaoko significantly improved the intestinal structure of Partridge Shank chickens, as evidenced by longer and more densely arranged villi in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, along with a concentration‑dependent increase in the villus height to crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio. The most pronounced improvement was observed in the ileum, where the SL0.10 group exhibited a 26.43% increase in the VH/CD ratio. These findings are consistent with those of Fu et al. (Fu et al., 2024), who reported that dietary supplementation with Chinese herbal preparations significantly increased small intestinal villus height and the VH/CD ratio while reducing crypt depth in Arbor Acres (AA) broilers, thereby improving intestinal morphology. Similarly, Peng et al. (Peng et al., 2024) observed that A. tsaoko extract markedly enhanced intestinal villus length and morphology in fish, leading to improved digestive function. Collectively, these studies suggest that A. tsaoko enhances nutrient absorption by promoting intestinal development—an effect that appears to be conserved across species.

Further gut microbiota analysis revealed that A. tsaoko effectively modulated the intestinal microecology of Partridge Shank chickens. The richness of bacterial communities increased in a concentration‑dependent manner with the supplementation level, with the Chao1 index being significantly elevated in the SL0.15 group; fungal diversity was also markedly enhanced. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant bacterial taxa, consistent with the typical gut microbiota composition of poultry. At the genus level, A. tsaoko significantly affected the relative abundance of several bacterial genera, including Bacteroides and Alistipes. Notably, Shen et al. (Shen et al., 2026) reported that in white‑feathered broilers, A. tsaoko extract significantly increased the relative abundance of Bacteroides in the cecum, which was associated with elevated levels of umami‑related amino acids (threonine, glutamate, and aspartate) in the muscle. As Bacteroides are major producers of short‑chain fatty acids, butyrate (a key metabolite) can provide energy to intestinal epithelial cells and promote villus development—a mechanism that may explain the improved intestinal morphology observed here. In turn, this optimized intestinal structure provides an enhanced structural foundation for nutrient digestion and absorption, thereby contributing to improved growth performance and meat quality. This cascade of active components–gut microbiota–intestinal morphology–production performance elucidates a potential pathway through which A. tsaoko exerts its beneficial effects on meat quality.

This study systematically elucidated the multiple biological functions of A. tsaoko through integrated approaches encompassing in vitro antibacterial evaluation, active component identification, and in vivo animal experiments. Characteristic components such as 5-indanol, identified in the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko, exerted broad-spectrum antibacterial activity by disrupting bacterial membrane integrity. As a plant-derived feed additive, A. tsaoko significantly enhanced both growth performance and slaughter performance in Partridge Shank chickens, reduced muscle drip loss rate, and improved water-holding capacity. Its mechanism of action is closely associated with the regulation of gut microbiota (increasing the abundance of Bacteroides) and the promotion of intestinal villus development (elevating the VH/CD ratio), thereby enhancing nutrient absorption efficiency and indirectly improving meat quality. This study provides a scientific basis for the resource utilization of A. tsaoko stems and leaves and offers valuable insights for the development of novel plant-derived feed additives.

Conclusion

This study integrated metabolomic analysis with in vitro antibacterial assays to elucidate the antibacterial mechanism of 5‑indanol against S. aureus, while also investigating the effects of underutilized A. tsaoko stems and leaves, used as a plant‑derived feed supplement, on the growth, development, and intestinal health of Partridge Shank chickens. In vitro antibacterial experiments demonstrated that the ethyl acetate extract of A. tsaoko possessed broad‑spectrum antibacterial activity, inhibiting both Gram‑positive and Gram‑negative bacteria. Specifically, 5‑indanol exhibited a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 62.5 μg/mL against S. aureus and showed concentration‑dependent bactericidal effects at 2-4 × MIC. Its antibacterial mechanism involves membrane damage: it disrupts the integrity of the bacterial cell membrane and cell wall, leading to the leakage of intracellular macromolecules such as alkaline phosphatase, glucose, total proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately resulting in bacterial death. Furthermore, 5‑indanol was found to disrupt bacterial biofilms and inhibit hemolysin production, suggesting its potential as an anti‑infective agent.

Dietary supplementation with A. tsaoko stems, leaves, or fruits significantly enhanced the average daily gain and reduced the feed conversion ratio (FCR) of Partridge Shank chickens, while also improving meat quality traits, including drip loss, tenderness, pH, and meat color. These findings suggest that utilizing underutilized A. tsaoko stems and leaves as a feed additive can not only increase the economic returns of Partridge Shank chickens farming but also help mitigate issues associated with agricultural waste. Furthermore, dietary A. tsaoko positively modulated intestinal morphology and microbial community structure, significantly increasing the villus height to crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio in the duodenum and ileum. It also improved the balance of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes) to potentially harmful microorganisms (e.g., Basidiomycota) in the gut. Collectively, these changes enhanced the digestive and absorptive capacity as well as immune function of the chickens, ultimately promoting their growth and development.
