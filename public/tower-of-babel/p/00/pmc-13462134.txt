NLRP3 inflammasome promotes PAD2/PAD4 release and protein citrullination in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Kristen J Zaranski, Mingxin Yang, Lacie R Scaletta, Erika Darrah, Tatiana Ort, Gary P Sims, Teneema Kuriakose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs), a hallmark of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), arise from citrullinated proteins presenting immunogenic neoepitopes that break self-tolerance. Citrullination is a post-translational modification catalyzed by peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs). Among five isoforms, PAD2 and PAD4 expressed in immune cells are most closely associated with RA pathogenesis. Although typically intracellular, PAD2 and PAD4 are detectable extracellularly in RA, yet the mechanisms governing their release remain unclear. Here, we show that NLRP3 inflammasome activation triggers PAD release from human neutrophils via an NLRP3-caspase-1-gasdermin D axis, independent of cell death. Inflammasome activation increases citrullination of intracellular proteins, while released PADs retain enzymatic activity and citrullinate extracellular substrates. In RA serum, PAD concentrations and PAD activity correlate with IL-1β, suggesting an analogous inflammasome-driven mechanism for extracellular PAD release in vivo. Collectively, these findings indicate that the NLRP3 inflammasome may promote autoimmunity in RA by amplifying citrullinated neoepitope generation that fuels ACPA production. Subject terms: Autoimmunity, NOD-like receptors

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by inflammation of synovial joints that leads to progressive joint damage, functional impairment, and persistent pain1,2. Diagnosis of RA incorporates clinical classification criteria for joint involvement together with inflammatory markers, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive- protein (CRP), as well as rheumatoid factor (RF). A key serologic marker is the presence of anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs), which are highly specific for a diagnosis of RA, present in 60-70% of patients, and frequently associated with worse prognosis, greater disease severity, and radiographic progression3.

ACPAs are autoantibodies directed against citrullinated proteins and are induced by breaches of immune tolerance in RA4. Citrullination is a post-translational modification catalyzed by a family of calcium-dependent enzymes, the peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs), which convert peptidylarginine to peptidyl-citrulline5,6. This modification can alter antigen processing and presentation, generating immunogenic neoepitopes7. ACPAs often emerge years before the clinical onset of RA and exhibit progressive epitope spreading across diverse intracellular and structural proteins, suggesting that sustained citrullination contributes to maturation of the humoral response3,4. Of the five PAD isoforms, PAD2 and PAD4 are expressed in immune cells, particularly neutrophils and monocytes, and are most strongly implicated in RA pathogenesis6,8,9. Although PAD enzymes are primarily localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus where they regulate multiple physiological processes, dysregulated PAD activity and aberrant citrullination have been implicated in the pathology of several inflammatory conditions5. ACPAs recognize citrullinated epitopes on both intracellular and extracellular proteins, targeting a broad repertoire of antigens including histones, vimentin, fibrinogen, α-enolase, collagen, and laminin thereby promoting immune complex formation, complement activation, and tissue inflammation3. In-depth chemoproteomic analyses of the RA citrullinome using serum, synovial fluid, and synovial tissue have identified over 150 citrullinated proteins, underscoring a prominent role for extracellular citrullination in RA pathophysiology10. While plasma membrane disruption or other stimuli that facilitates Ca2+ influx is required for heightened intracellular citrullination11,12, the mechanisms that promote extracellular citrullination by catalytically active PADs remain incompletely defined.

PAD2 and PAD4 are detectable in the serum and synovial fluid of patients with RA, and higher concentrations of soluble PAD enzymes in the joint correlate with greater cellular infiltration and synovial inflammation13–15. PADs are generally thought to reach the extracellular space following cell death, particularly during neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation (NETosis)16,17, although whether this reflects a regulated export pathway or passive release after membrane rupture remains unclear. Moreover, whether other cell death programs or stimuli that activate intracellular PADs also modulate extracellular PAD release has not been systematically examined. Accordingly, we investigated whether cellular pathways governing PAD biology regulate extracellular release of PAD2 and PAD4 from primary human neutrophils. Our findings suggest that the NLRP3 inflammasome promotes PAD release and protein citrullination, potentially amplifying autoimmunity in RA.

Results

TLR ligands and proinflammatory cytokines did not increase PADI2/PADI4 expression in neutrophils

PAD2 and PAD4 are expressed in many cell types, including neutrophils8,9,17. Gene expression analysis in primary human neutrophils showed relatively higher PADI4 than PADI2 expression (Sup. Fig. S1a). To assess transcriptional regulation of PADs by activating stimuli, we measured PADI2 and PADI4 after treatment with toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands and proinflammatory cytokines. LPS (TLR4 ligand), R848 (TLR7 ligand), CpG-B (TLR9 ligand), TNF, IL-1β, or IFNα did not increase PADI2 or PADI4 expression (Sup. Fig. S1b), whereas TNF was induced by several stimuli, confirming responsiveness to agonists (Sup. Fig. S1c). These results suggest that common inflammatory stimuli do not transcriptionally induce PADs in primary neutrophils.

Nigericin treatment, but not hypercitrullination and PMA-induced NETosis, promoted PAD2 and PAD4 release from neutrophils

Influx of Ca2+ via ion transport (e.g. ionomycin) or membrane disruption (e.g. Membrane Attack Complex [MAC]), nigericin) induce PAD-mediated intracellular protein citrullination9,11,12. To investigate whether the induction of protein citrullination triggers release of PADs, we treated neutrophils with agents that increase calcium influx or induce membrane damage. As expected, treatment with the Ca2+ ionophore, ionomycin, led to hypercitrullination in neutrophils (Fig. 1a). Addition of the calcium chelator EDTA decreased citrullination, confirming calcium dependence. PMA did not induce protein citrullination, whereas the potassium ionophore nigericin led to a modest, detectable increase in citrullination in neutrophils (Fig. 1a). To assess whether hypercitrullination can trigger release of PAD2 and PAD4 from neutrophils, we measured the concentrations of these enzymes in supernatants collected from the citrullination assay. Consistent with our previous observations17, PAD2 was detected in supernatants from untreated neutrophils. However, ionomycin-induced hypercitrullination did not increase PAD2 or PAD4 concentrations (Fig. 1b). Notably, nigericin induced release of both PAD2 and PAD4 from neutrophils (Fig. 1b).

PAD release from neutrophils is generally considered to be associated with cell death, particularly NETosis16. To assess whether NETosis facilitates PAD release, we treated neutrophils with PMA, a trigger of NADPH oxidase-dependent NETosis. PMA triggered NETosis in neutrophils in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1c, d); however, this was not associated with an increase in PAD release. PMA treatment reduced PAD2 and PAD4 in neutrophil supernatants (Fig.1e). In contrast, nigericin, which induced a morphologically similar cell death to classical NETosis (referred to as NADPH-independent NETosis), increased the release of both PAD2 and PAD4 from neutrophils (Fig. 1f, g, h). Collectively, these data indicate that while neither hypercitrullination nor PMA-induced NETosis promotes the release of PADs from neutrophils, nigericin treatment does trigger the extracellular release of PAD2 and PAD4.

Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers PAD2 and PAD4 release from neutrophils

Regulated cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and NETosis, each rely on unique molecular machinery and serve distinct biological processes18. Among these, pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of cell death driven by inflammasome activation18. Because nigericin, a standard stimulus for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, increased PAD2 and PAD4 release, we investigated the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in PAD release. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome proceeds via priming and activation: priming via pattern recognition receptor/cytokine signaling increases NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β; activation assembles the inflammasome, forming membrane pores and driving IL-1β/IL-18 release and pyroptosis19.

To assess whether the NLRP3 inflammasome promotes PAD release in neutrophils, we induced inflammasome activation with LPS followed by nigericin and measured PAD2 and PAD4 in neutrophil supernatants. The expression of both NLRP3 and IL1B was increased in neutrophils treated with LPS for 4 h, confirming inflammasome priming (Fig. 2a, b). Treatment with LPS followed by nigericin increased the release of both PAD2 and PAD4 and importantly, this response was decreased in neutrophils treated with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (Fig. 2c). The reduction in PAD release was generally consistent across donors; however, the concentrations of released PAD2 and PAD4 and inflammasome inhibition varied between individuals. PAD2 and PAD4 release occurred after LPS treatment in some donors, and inhibition of NLRP3 decreased PAD release under these conditions as well (Fig. 2c). Inflammasome activation resulted in NLRP3-dependent IL-1β release, while TNF secretion, which is not regulated by the inflammasome, was unchanged by MCC950 treatment (Fig.2d).

To determine whether reduced PAD release with NLRP3 inhibition reflected reduced cell death, we performed real time live cell imaging over a time course. Minimal cell death occurred in untreated and LPS-treated cells, indicated by low staining with SYTOX green, a cell impermeant DNA dye that binds to extracellular DNA or DNA in membrane-compromised (dead) cells (Fig. 2e, f). Nigericin triggered cell death in LPS-primed neutrophils, and MCC950 did not reduce this effect, indicating NLRP3-independent cell death (Fig. 2e, f). The lack of cell death in LPS-treated cells and NLRP3-independent cell death following LPS plus nigericin treatment was further confirmed by LDH release assays (Sup. Fig. S2). Overall, NLRP3 inflammasome activation drives PAD2 and PAD4 release from primary human neutrophils independent of cell death.

Release of PAD2 and PAD4 from neutrophils following inflammasome activation depends on caspase-1 and gasdermin D

A cascade of molecular events follows inflammasome assembly and activation, including caspase-1 cleavage, processing of IL-1β and IL-18, gasdermin D (GSDMD) membrane pore formation and pyroptosis20–22. To validate inflammasome-dependent PAD release, we assessed the involvement of caspase-1 and GSDMD. Inhibiting caspase-1 (VX765) or GSDMD (disulfiram) decreased LPS plus nigericin-induced PAD2 and PAD4 release (Fig. 3a, b). Caspase-1 and GSDMD inhibitors also reduced IL-1β release confirming inflammasome inhibition (Fig. 3a, b). These observations mirrored the data with the NLRP3 inhibitor (Fig. 2c, d). After LPS+nigericin treatment, total cell death was comparable with DMSO, NLRP3 inhibitor, or caspase-1 inhibitor, whereas GSDMD inhibition modestly but consistently reduced SYTOX Green staining, indicating less plasma membrane rupture (Fig. 3c, d). Overall, these data demonstrate that both caspase-1 and GSDMD contribute to PAD release, further confirming that the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers PAD release from neutrophils in a manner uncoupled from cell death.

NLRP3 inflammasome activation with LPS and ATP leads to PAD2 and PAD4 release from neutrophils in the absence of cell death

To evaluate whether PAD release occurs in response to other classical NLRP3 activating stimuli, we quantified extracellular PADs from neutrophils treated with LPS followed by ATP. As previously reported23, LPS plus ATP did not induce cell death/pyroptosis in neutrophils (Fig. 4a, b). Notably, LPS plus ATP triggered release of both PAD2 and PAD4, which was reduced by inhibitors of NLRP3, caspase-1, or GSDMD (Fig. 4c–e). Inflammasome activation was confirmed by decreased IL-1β with these inhibitors (Fig. 4f–h). Overall, these data support an inflammasome-dependent, cell death-independent export mechanism for PAD2 and PAD4 in human neutrophils.

Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome drives citrullination of intracellular proteins and, via PAD release, extracellular substrates

Inflammasome activation can raise intracellular Ca2+ via P2X7R-mediated influx, membrane damage, or release from intracellular stores, enhancing PAD activity. Because NLRP3 inflammasome activation alters intracellular Ca2+ concentrations24,25, we assessed whether this also promotes protein citrullination. Histone H3, a well-known substrate of PADs, was citrullinated after LPS plus nigericin treatment (Fig. 5a). However, this was not entirely NLRP3-dependent, consistent with NLRP3-independent plasma membrane rupture and cell death under these conditions. In contrast, when ATP was used as ‘signal 2’ to trigger inflammasome activation, H3 citrullination increased in an NLRP3-dependent manner (Fig. 5a, b). Consistent with the observed reduction in plasma membrane rupture, GSDMD inhibitor disulfiram tended to decrease histone H3 citrullination in response to LPS plus nigericin, but not with LPS plus ATP (Fig. 5c, d). Caspase-1 inhibition with VX-765, which does not impact inflammasome assembly or plasma membrane rupture, had no consistent effects on histone H3 citrullination under either condition (Supp. Fig.S3a, b).

Many extracellular proteins are PAD substrates and sources of citrullinated neo-epitopes in autoimmune diseases10. Citrullinated fibrinogen is a bona fide ACPA target in RA26–28 and fibrinogen can be citrullinated by recombinant PAD enzymes and PAD4 expressed on the surface of monocytes and neutrophils17,26–30. To determine whether PADs released upon inflammasome activation retain their enzymatic activity on extracellular substrates, we assessed fibrinogen citrullination by neutrophil supernatants. Citrullinated fibrinogen was detected when the protein was incubated with supernatants from neutrophils (Fig. 5e). Fibrinogen citrullination was enhanced when incubated with supernatants from neutrophils treated with LPS and either nigericin or ATP, paralleling increased PAD release under these conditions (Fig. 5e, f). Together, these data demonstrate that NLRP3 inflammasome activation promotes citrullination of intracellular and extracellular substrates.

PAD2/PAD4 concentrations and total PAD activity correlate with IL-1β in serum samples from patients with RA

Previous reports have shown increased concentration and activity of PAD enzymes in serum and synovial fluid samples from RA patients13,15,16. Our in vitro data shows that PADs are released upon inflammasome activation, raising the possibility that inflammasome-activating triggers in patients could promote extracellular PAD release. Notably, increased IL-1β has been reported in patients with RA, and blockade of IL-1β or IL-1R signaling has been evaluated as a therapeutic approach in RA31,32. To assess whether systemic PAD enzymes in RA correlate with IL-1β, we quantified serum IL-1β in RA patient samples previously shown to have elevated PAD2/PAD4 concentrations and PAD activity compared with healthy controls33. IL-1β and inflammasome-independent TNF were significantly elevated in these patients compared with healthy donors (Fig. 6a, b) and were positively correlated (r = 0.4280; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6c). In addition, IL-1β showed a modest positive correlation with concentrations of PAD2 (r = 0.2578; p = 0.0142), PAD4 (r = 0.2989; p = 0.004) and total PAD activity (r = 0.2782; p = 0.0095) in the RA patient samples (Fig. 6d). Although TNF concentrations were also elevated in patient samples, neither PAD concentrations nor total PAD activity showed a significant correlation with TNF (PAD2 - r = 0.02332, p = 0.8302; PAD4 - r = 0.1518, p = 0.1580; PAD activity - r = 0.1413; p = 0.1916) (Fig. 6e). Overall, these findings indicate an association between extracellular PADs with bioactive IL-1β production, suggesting an inflammasome-dependent axis that may amplify RA-related autoimmunity.

Discussion

ACPAs, a defining feature of autoimmunity in RA, arise from immunogenic citrullinated proteins that break self-tolerance. Although extracellular PADs are linked to epitope spreading and ACPA production, how PADs are released has remained unclear. In this study, we identify cellular responses downstream of NLRP3 inflammasome activation as a trigger for PAD release from neutrophils. PAD release requires NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD, yet is independent of plasma membrane rupture and cell death. Inflammasome activation induced citrullination of the intracellular histone H3, while catalytically active released PADs citrullinated the extracellular substrate fibrinogen, revealing a dual process that could drive neo-epitope generation and autoantibody responses. In serum from patients with RA, PAD concentrations and total PAD activity correlated with IL-1β, suggesting that inflammasome-driven processes may contribute to extracellular PAD release in vivo.

Neutrophil death, especially NETosis, has been proposed to drive extracellular PAD release. In our assays, however, PMA-induced NETosis, an NADPH oxidase-dependent process, did not increase PAD release. This contrasts with a previous report that detected extracellular PADs, especially PAD2, in supernatants of PMA-stimulated neutrophils, although a fraction of PADs remained associated with extruded DNA following NET formation16. PAD4 activity is required for histone citrullination and chromatin decondensation during NETosis in response to some stimuli34,35. PAD4 colocalizes with NADPH oxidase subunits, interactions critical for assembly of the active enzyme and NADPH-dependent neutrophil functions36. Activation of PAD4 disrupts these interactions, impairing reactive oxygen species generation and killing of phagocytosed bacteria, whereas pharmacologic inhibition of NADPH oxidase in PMA-stimulated leukocytes enhances PAD activity, as evidenced by increased fibrinogen citrullination37. Collectively, these findings link PADs to the neutrophil machinery governing NADPH-dependent effector responses and suggest that NADPH oxidase-related pathways may counter-regulate PAD release during PMA-induced NETosis.

Disruption of plasma membrane integrity during pyroptosis induces intracellular citrullination in neutrophils, but the signal is markedly weaker than the leukotoxic hypercitrullination triggered by ionomycin and polyperforin9,12. Consistent with this, nigericin elicited limited citrullination and, moreover, hypercitrullination was not associated with PAD release. However, nigericin induced cell death and the release of PAD2/ PAD4, implicating involvement of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Using selective stimuli and inhibitors, we show that PAD release is regulated by NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD, and can occur without neutrophil death. Multiple studies have reported a lack of pyroptosis induction and non-lytic release of IL-1β in neutrophils following inflammasome activation23,38–41. Although our study lacks direct mechanistic evidence for PAD export from neutrophils following inflammasome activation, our data align with PADs using a similar exit route to IL-1β. Inflammasome-dependent cytokine release proceeds differently in neutrophils than in macrophages, potentially due to the unique subcellular organization within neutrophils, which includes specialized granules and secretory vesicles42. Notably, neutrophils can secrete IL-1β through unconventional vesicular pathways (exosomes, microvesicles) without undergoing lysis40, raising the possibility that PAD enzymes may employ similar nonlytic export routes following inflammasome activation. Supporting this concept, PAD4 has been detected in secretory pathway organelles and secretory vesicles in monocytes43. Enrichment of PADs in secretory vesicles together with citrullination-dependent regulation of vesicular trafficking suggests that PADs may utilize conventional secretory pathways for exocytosis.

Citrullinated autoantigens generated by PADs are widely implicated in the initiation and progression of RA3. Our data link inflammasome activation to extracellular PAD availability and enhanced citrullination. In neutrophils, NLRP3 inflammasome activation boosts intracellular citrullination and releases active PADs that citrullinate extracellular substrates. The increase in intracellular citrullination is likely driven by Ca2+ influx during inflammasome assembly or after a breach in plasma membrane integrity. Thus, inflammasome signaling indirectly amplifies citrullination, potentially fueling ACPA production and inflammation. Although extracellular PADs can arise from multiple sources including cell death and vesicular secretion, our results indicate an additional NLRP3-linked mechanism in neutrophils.

Multiple endogenous triggers relevant to RA can engage the NLRP3 inflammasome and contribute to PAD release. Extracellular ATP present in plasma or within the synovial cavity can serve as a second signal to activate NLRP3 and propagate inflammation44,45. Calcium-containing crystals from bone erosion can also activate NLRP3 in inflamed joints46. Emerging evidence further implicates citrullinated histone H3 and ACPAs in inflammasome activation47–49, suggesting a feed-forward loop in which NLRP3 activation promotes the generation of citrullinated autoantigens and ACPAs, which in turn further activate the inflammasome. Given neutrophil abundance in inflamed joints and the biochemical milieu of inflamed synovia (elevated extracellular calcium associated with bone erosions, and abundant substrates, such as fibrin and collagen), local conditions may further sustain PAD activity and expand the repertoire of extracellularly citrullinated proteins. Of note, extracellular Ca2+ functions as a danger-associated signal that activates the NLRP3 inflammasome50, further supporting the view that synovial joints in patients with RA provide an environment conducive to sustained PAD release and citrullination.

While our study focuses on NLRP3 in neutrophils, other inflammasomes, such as AIM2 and NLRP1, which are activated by DNA and proteotoxic stress, respectively, may also influence PAD biology in RA51. Monocytes and macrophages in blood and synovium could contribute to extracellular PAD release and protein citrullination during inflammasome activation and pyroptosis52–54. GSDMD pores in these cells may permit Ca2+ influx to drive intracellular citrullination, yet their size likely precludes efficient export of larger proteins like PAD2/PAD4. Thus, contributions of monocytes and macrophages to inflammasome-dependent PAD responses warrant further study. Beyond the joint, the relevance of inflammasome activation in RA-associated comorbidities also merits investigation. The inflammasome is pathogenic in periodontitis, a common RA comorbidity55. Porphyromonas gingivalis, an oral pathobiont linked to periodontitis, activates the inflammasome, suggesting recurrent infection could amplify extracellular PAD activity and citrullination, contributing to RA pathogenesis56–59.

Multiple lines of evidence implicate inflammasome activity in RA. Transcriptomics analysis and synovial histopathology show enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome pathway activity in patients with RA60. Consistent with this, the inflammasome-dependent cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 are elevated in synovial fluid and serum in RA patients, correlating with disease activity61. Moreover, calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)-driven NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been shown to amplify inflammatory responses in RA46. A recent prospective, longitudinal study reports higher plasma IL-1β in ACPA-positive at-risk individuals and early RA patients compared with healthy controls62. In line with these observations, we find increased serum IL-1β in RA and a modest positive correlation between circulating IL-1β and PAD enzymes, supporting a link between inflammasome activity and elevated extracellular PADs. Assessment of PADs and inflammasome activity in more selective cohorts (e.g. ACPA+ RA patients) may yield better insights into the interplay between these two pathways in the pathogenesis of RA.

Taken together, our data indicate that the NLRP3 inflammasome and downstream effectors orchestrate PAD release and protein citrullination, potentially exacerbating autoimmunity in RA. The export mechanism for PADs from neutrophils remains undefined; current evidence favors inflammasome-linked export over passive lytic release, but the precise trafficking routes are unknown. How inflammasome responses shape extracellular PAD activity, ACPA titers, and epitope spreading also warrants study. Translational relevance should be tested by evaluating whether pharmacologic PAD inhibition and/or targeted modulation of the inflammasome pathway attenuates extracellular citrullination and alters ACPA dynamics in vivo. Overall, we propose that inflammasome activation indirectly broadens the antigenic landscape of RA via extracellular PAD release and enhanced citrullination, highlighting testable opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Isolation and stimulation of primary human neutrophils

Blood from healthy volunteers was obtained with informed consent under AstraZeneca’s blood donation program, and studies using human cells were performed in accordance with the Institutional Review Board guidelines. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Neutrophils were isolated using EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (StemCell, 19666) and cell viability and cell numbers were assessed using Vicell Blu cell viability analyzer (Beckman Colter, C19196). Cell viability was above 95% in all experiments.

Neutrophil purity was assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, 250,000 cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue (Invitrogen, L34962; 1:2000 dilution in PBS; 10 min incubation at room temperature), washed with PBS, then incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with anti-CD45–BV650 (BioLegend, 304044), anti-CD15–BV510 (BioLegend, 323028), and anti-CD66b–PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, 305116) at 1:100 dilution in FACS staining buffer (BD, 554656). Cells were washed, resuspended in FACS buffer, and acquired immediately on a BD FACS Symphony SE A5. Data were analyzed using FlowJo. Purity exceeded 96% in all donors tested (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b).

For gene expression analysis and inflammasome activation, 5.0 ×106 neutrophils were seeded per well in a 24-well plate in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 61870-036) media. Cells were left untreated or treated with Ultrapure LPS, E. coli 0111:B4 (100 ng/mL), R848 (1 μg/mL), CpG ODN Class B (10 μg/mL), rhTNFα (100 ng/mL), rhIL-1β (100 ng/mL) or rhIFNα2 (100 ng/mL) for 4 h. All TLR ligands were purchased from InVivogen (tlrl-3pelps, tlrl-r848, tlrl-2006, respectively) and recombinant proteins were purchased from Sigma (H8916-10UG, GF331, 4595-100UG, respectively).

Inflammasome activation was achieved by treatment with LPS from E. Coli 0111: B4 (1 μg/mL; InVivogen, tlrl-3pelps) for 3 h followed by nigericin (10 μM; InVivogen, tlrl-nig) or ATP (5 mM; InVivogen, tlrl-atpl) treatment for 45 min. MCC950 (5 μM; InVivogen, inh-mcc), VX-765 (50 μM; InVivogen, inh-vx765i-1) and disulfiram (10 μM; SelleckChem, S1680) were used as inhibitors of NLRP3, Caspase-1 and Gasdermin D, respectively. DMSO (Sigma, D2438-10mL) was used as vehicle control. Neutrophils were treated with DMSO or inhibitors at the time of LPS addition.

For total citrullination experiments, 5.0 ×106 neutrophils per well were seeded in 500 µL HBSS without calcium or magnesium (Gibco, 14175-095) in 24 well plates. Cells were either left untreated or treated with 10 mM calcium chloride (Sigma, C-34006) along with ionomycin (1 μM; Invivogen, inh-ion), ionomycin (1 μM) + EDTA (1 mM; Invitrogen, 15575-038), PMA (100 ng/mL; Sigma, P1585-1MG) or nigericin (10 μM; InVivogen, tlrl-nig) for 1 h.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR analysis

The plates were centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at the end of treatment. After removing the supernatants, cells were lysed in buffer RLT (Qiagen, 79216) containing beta-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, M3148). RNA lysates were homogenized utilizing the QiaShredder (Qiagen, 79656) and RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104) following manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentrations were assessed by Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ND-8000-GL). Isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio Rad, 1708891) after normalizing the RNA concentrations. RT-PCR reactions were performed with Taqman primers and Fast advanced master mix (Invitrogen, 4444557) on the QuantStudio7 Flex System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4484643). Primers used were: PADI2: Hs01042505_m1, PADI4: Hs01057483_m1, IL1β: Hs01555410_m1, TNF: Hs00174128_m1, NLRP3: Hs00918082_m1, GAPDH: Hs02786624_g1. Relative gene expression was calculated by normalizing the gene of interest to GAPDH and untreated control samples using the ΔΔCt method.

Immunoblotting analysis

The plates were centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at the end of treatment. After collecting the supernatants, cells were lysed in lysis buffer containing RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89900) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Millipore Sigma, 04693159001 and 04906837001 respectively), benzonase nuclease (25 units/mL; EMD Millipore, 70746-3), and MgCl2 (10 mM; Invitrogen, AM9530G). For histone H3 immunoblotting, 2.0 ×106 cells in RPMI 1640 or DMEM media (Gibco, 61870-036 and 31053028 respectively) were seeded in a 96-well plate, and cells along with supernatants were lysed in the lysis buffer at the end of treatment. For evaluation of fibrinogen citrullination, plasminogen-depleted fibrinogen (0.7 μM; Millipore sigma, 341578) was incubated with 100 µL cell free supernatants from treated neutrophils in the presence of 1.5 mM Ca2+. Lysis buffer was added to the wells at the end of incubation. Protein lysates were immediately boiled for 5 min at 95 °C after adding 4X Loading buffer (LI-COR, 928-40004) containing beta-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, M3148) per manufacturer’s instructions.

For Western blotting, lysates were loaded into Nupage 4-12% Bis-Tris Gels (Invitrogen, NP0335BOX). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using the XCell Sure Lock Electrophoresis System (Thermo Fisher, 121201-1276 and Bio-Rad, 041BR91174) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Invitrogen, IB24001) using iBlot2 gel transfer system (InVitrogen, IB21001). After transfer, the membranes were blocked in Intercept® (TBS) Blocking Buffer (LI-COR, 927-60001) for 1 h at room temperature followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Anti-peptidyl citrulline clone F95 (EMD Millipore/Merck, MABN328), anti-citrullinated histone H3 (Abcam, ab5103), human anti-citrullinated fibrinogen (MQR2.101; ImmunoPrecise Antibodies, MQ13.102-100), anti-histone H3 (CST, 4499S), GAPDH (CST, 2118S), and β-actin (CST, 3700S and 4970S) were used as primary antibodies. This was followed by incubation with IRDye 680LT Goat anti-mouse IgM, IRDye 800CW Donkey anti-mouse IgG or IRDye 680LT Goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (LI-COR, 925-68080, 926-32212, and 926-68021 respectively). Images were acquired using the LI-COR Odyssey CLX imaging system (LI-COR, DLX-3145) and protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software. Bands were normalized to housekeeping protein (either GAPDH or β-actin) using a lane normalization factor. Lane normalization factor was defined as the observed signal of housekeeping protein for each lane divided by the highest observed signal of housekeeping protein on the blot. Then, the normalized experimental signal was determined by dividing the observed experimental signal of the lane divided by that lane’s normalization factor. All uncropped Western blots are available as Supplementary Fig. S5a–e.

ELISA

Protein concentrations in cell culture supernatants and serum were quantified by ELISA. Neutrophil supernatants collected at the end of treatment were stored at −80 °C and thawed once on the day of the assay. PAD2 and PAD4 (Cayman Chemical, kits 501450 and 501460), IL-1β (R&D Systems, kit DLB50), and TNF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, kit KHC3011) were measured according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Optical Density was measured on SpectraMax M5 (Molecular Devices, M5).

Cell death analysis

Neutrophil cell death was assessed following the protocol for IncuCyte NETosis assay (Sartorius; https://www.sartorius.com/download/878848/incucyte-netosis-assay-protocol-en-l-8000-0610-a00-sartorius-1--data.pdf). 96-well plates were coated with 0.1 μg/mL of Fibronectin (Sigma, F1141) in PBS without Mg2+ or Ca2+ (Gibco, 14190-144) for 1 hour. 20,000 neutrophils per well were seeded in RPMI 1640 media and treated accordingly with inhibitors and/or stimulants. Cell death was visualized by the IncuCyte SX5 live cell imaging system (Sartorius, 4816) using SYTOX Green Nucleic Acid Stain (250 nM; Invitrogen, S7020). Image analysis was performed using IncuCyte Live cell analysis software (2023 A Rev2) using area filters and appropriate processing definitions. SYTOX-positive events for NETosis or total DNA staining categories were assessed based on image processing definitions validated against PMA-induced NET morphology. The confluence mask was the same for both NETosis and total DNA staining as they both used ‘AI Confluence’ for segmentation, and no manual adjustments needed to be made. For NETosis, the green mask utilized ‘Surface Fit’ for segmentation set at a minimum area of 543 µm2 to only include the signal intensity of extruded DNA material whereas total DNA utilized ‘Surface Fit’ for segmentation with no minimum area in order to include all signal intensity.

LDH release was measured using LDH-Glo Cytotoxicity assay (Promega, J2380) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Serum samples

Sera from RA patients previously diagnosed according to ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria (n = 87) were obtained from Sanguine Bio. Healthy donor sera were acquired via AstraZeneca’s internal blood donor program. Sample procurement and handling was done in accordance with AstraZeneca Institutional Review Board guidelines. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

PAD activity assay

Half-area, 96-well high-binding plates (Corning, 3690) were coated with 1 μg/mL human histone H3 (Cayman, 10263) in HBSS with Ca2+ (Sigma, H8264) overnight at 4°C. Fresh citrullination buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 15 mM CaCl2, 1 mM DTT) was prepared prior to assay. Plates were washed 3x in 1x MSD TBS-T wash buffer (MSD, R61TX-1) and blocked in 1% BSA (MSD, R93BA-4) in TBS-T buffer for 1 h at RT. Serum samples diluted in citrullination buffer were added to the assay plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. After washing the plates with MSD TBS-T wash buffer, Rabbit anti-human histone H3 (citrulline R8) antibody (Abcam, ab219406) diluted in 1% BSA/TBS-T buffer (1:2000) was added and incubated for 1 h at RT. Plates were washed and incubated with HRP-labeled Goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Invitrogen, A24357) in 1% BSA/TBS-T buffer (1:2000) for 1 hour at RT. After washing the plates in MSD TBS-T wash buffer, room temperature equilibrated ultra-sensitive TMB (Sigma, ES022) was added and incubated at RT in the dark for 4–8 min. Reaction was quenched with 0.3 M sulfuric acid, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm on Envision 2105 Multi-mode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer, SCR_018038).

Statistics and Reproducibility

All experiments were performed with a minimum of n ≥ 3 independent biological samples unless stated otherwise. Here, n refers to the number of independent human donors; each donor constitutes one biological replicate. For cell-based assays, technical replicates were averaged to yield one value per donor for statistical analysis. Exact tests used for each figure are specified in the corresponding legends. Reproducibility was confirmed by repeating key experiments in at least three independent donors. All statistical analyses were performed on GraphPad Prism. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.
