Working memory and language development have a bi-directional longitudinal relationship in autistic preschoolers
Authors: Aanya Sehgal, Gina Schnur, Viviane Valdes, Susan Faja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong neurotypical children working memory and language development are closely related, but the longitudinal relationship between these factors is unknown in autism. This study had the goal of evaluating potential bidirectional relationships between these domains.MethodsA longitudinal design included language and visuospatial working memory assessments across three time points with 160 autistic preschoolers. Time points were one year apart. A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) using Full-Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) was used to evaluate longitudinal associations between performance on a working memory task (i.e., the Hide and Seek Task) and language scores (i.e., verbal development quotient).ResultsCross-lagged associations were significant for paths from working memory at the first time point to language at the second time point (β = .31, p = .01) and from language at first time point to working memory at the second time point (β = .40, p < .001). Cross-lagged paths from the second to third time point were not statistically significant in either direction.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence of a bidirectional relationship between working memory and language in autistic toddlers and preschoolers among younger autistic preschoolers. These results highlight working memory as a potential early assessment and intervention domain for improving language outcomes in autism as well as the role of language delays as a factor related to reduced working memory skills later in development.

Introduction

Understanding the early neurocognitive development of autistic children can clarify etiology and inform intervention. Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind while updating it to problem-solve [1]. Language includes speech, gesture, sign, writing, and enables communication, social interaction, and engagement with cultural and environmental contexts [2]. Both working memory and language underly foundational aspects of child development, including the acquisition of new knowledge, social communication, and adaptive functioning across everyday contexts. Difficulties in either domain can therefore have cascading effects on learning and developmental outcomes. In autism, working memory and language abilities are frequently characterized by persistent delays or differences relative to same-age peers, though the extent and quality of these difficulties varies considerably across individual children and is sensitive to modality and task demands [3–7].

There are several potential theoretical relationships between working memory and language development. Diamond [8] suggested that working memory supports language development by allowing children to link speech or speech sounds they hear with other information that is not well-synchronized such as gestures or labeled objects. Conversely, language skills including inner speech may allow children to develop cognitive skills including working memory. Specifically, language supports the development of self-regulation and of reflective and abstract thinking [9–13]. Language may also enable children to represent things that are not currently present in their memory. Of course, it is possible that a third factor could underlie the development of both language and working memory, or that the relationship between working memory is bi-directional with mutual influence in early development [14]. Indeed, there is some evidence for a bi-directional longitudinal relationship between language and executive function batteries that include working memory tasks in young neurotypical children [15–17].

Evidence suggests that working memory is an early developing component of cognitive function [18–21]. For neurotypical children, the capacity to actively hold information in mind typically emerges between 9 to 12 months of age [20], and working memory abilities increase in their complexity to include manipulating and updating information around 15 months [18]. Working memory capacity continues to increase during the preschool years [19, 21], a period that also coincides with rapid language development. Neurotypical children are also acquiring receptive language within their first year, and expressive language expands through toddlerhood and into their preschool years.

Numerous studies have examined the relationship between language and working memory across varying developmental levels and age groups. Among young neurotypical children, early working memory predicts later language skills, particularly vocabulary [22–26]. For example, verbal working memory ability at age four predicted vocabulary a year later [25], working memory at 30 months predicted vocabulary size at age three [22], and verbal working memory at age two predicted both receptive and expressive language at age four [26]. Conversely, early language ability predicts working memory and other aspects of executive functioning among neurotypical children [27–30]. For instance, verbal ability at age three predicted executive function performance on a battery that included working memory tasks at age four [30], and significant correlations between earlier language and later executive function as well as earlier executive function and later language were reported across two-, three-, and four-year assessments [27]. However, a second study examining these relationships over the same time period found evidence of a direct relationship for language on later executive function but not for executive function on later language [16]. These studies document the potential influence of language on later working memory and of working memory on later language early in neurotypical development, but most investigations examined executive function generally rather than isolating working memory and not all studies tested potential bidirectional relationships. No research has specifically investigated the longitudinal relationship between working memory and language in autistic children during the preschool period.

Several studies have explored the concurrent relationship (i.e., at cross-sectional time points) between working memory and language in autism. Among school-aged children, working memory and language were not related in an autistic group despite a relation in a neurotypical group [31, 32]. In contrast, working memory relates to both structural and pragmatic language in autistic groups [33, 34], and a group of autistic children with language impairment had lower verbal working memory ability than a group of autistic children without language impairment [35]. School-aged autistic children may be less likely than neurotypical children to use verbal strategies to support their working memory performance [31, 32]. More recent work found autistic children with structural language impairment were less likely to use verbal mediation during a planning task that involved working memory compared to autistic children with normal language and neurotypical peers suggesting the relation between working memory and language may differ within autism depending on a child’s language profile [36]. Among preschoolers between the ages of 2–4 years, verbal ability related to working memory performance and this relationship was stronger among autistic children than neurotypical children [37]. These authors used lab-based tasks to create a working memory composite and measured language using a composite of receptive and expressive language. Similar results were reported for 2- to 8-year-olds using parent report of real-world working memory ability and parent reported communication, pragmatic, and receptive-expressive social communication skills [38].

Investigating the potential longitudinal relationship between working memory and language is particularly important due its clinical implications. A deeper understanding of how these domains influence each other during the preschool period could help to inform early interventions, promote school readiness, and support better outcomes across a variety of domains that involve language and working memory. The current study aims to identify whether a bi-directional relationship exists between working memory and language development in autistic preschool-aged children. Specifically, we tested the bi-directional longitudinal relationship between visuospatial working memory and language in a combined sample of autistic 2- and 4-year-olds at three time points that were one year apart. To our knowledge, this was the first longitudinal evaluation of these relationships in autistic preschoolers. Due to the mixed results in neurotypical preschoolers, we did not have a priori hypotheses about the directionality of the relationship between these domains in autism.

Methods

Study design

Autistic children were enrolled at age 2 or 4 years and participated in a baseline (T1), 1-year (T2), and 2-year (T3) follow-up. Cohorts were combined to provide adequate power for a cross-lagged longitudinal model. At each time point, children completed language assessments and an executive function battery that included working memory assessment. At baseline, children completed a clinical evaluation to determine eligibility after written consent was obtained from each child’s parent or legal guardian. The protocol for the study was reviewed and approved by the Boston Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board in accordance with the Belmont Report, federal, and state regulations guiding human subjects research.

Participants

The sample included 160 autistic children who were followed for 2 years. At baseline, there were 94 2-year-olds and 66 4-year-olds in the sample; 37 of the children were girls (23%). Children were recruited as part of a larger study examining the development and trajectory of executive function and social development in autistic and non-autistic children. Children were eligible for the study if they were 2–2;11 or 4–4;11 years at enrollment and able to participate in English. The mean age of the sample was 40.42 months (SD = 12.60, 24–60).

All participants completed an assessment of global cognitive functioning using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL; [39], at their initial visit. Toddlers who were 30 months or younger were required to have a Visual Reception age equivalent of at least 12 months to participate in the study. Toddlers and preschoolers who were 31 months or older were required to have a Visual Reception age equivalent of at least 18 months. These age equivalents ensured the valid use of autism diagnostic measures; minimum age equivalents for the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2; [40] are 12 months for the Toddler Module (used with children 30 months and younger) and 18 months for Module 1 (used with children 31 months and older). Minimum age equivalents also supported the valid use of experimental measures of executive function but may exclude children with more significant cognitive delays, limiting sample representativeness and the generalizability of findings to the broader population of children with autism.

The mean score at baseline for the Mullen Early Learning Composite was 68.22 (SD = 19.71, range = 49–122). The Early Learning Composite is a standard score with a normative mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15, and scores between 85 and 115 are considered within the average range. We also computed a developmental quotient [(mean mental age/chronological age) × 100] for the nonverbal (Visual Reception, Fine Motor) and verbal (Expressive, Receptive) subtests. Developmental quotients for children who are developing at the expected rate would fall at 100, with higher scores reflecting more advanced development relative to age expectations and scores below 100 reflecting a developmental level below age expectations. Means were 74.18 (SD = 18.92, range = 25.00–116.98) for the nonverbal developmental quotient and 62.11 (SD = 27.64, range = 8.00–128.70) for the verbal developmental quotient.

Autism diagnosis was also required for eligibility. Children were eligible if they met DSM-5 [41] criteria for autism spectrum disorder based on the ADOS-2 [40] and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R, [42, 43]). Diagnosis was determined by licensed psychologists who were research reliable on the ADOS-2 and ADI-R. Because electroencephalography was collected as part of the larger study to examine neural and behavioral development of cognition, exclusionary criteria focused on factors that would limit the validity and comparability of neural and behavioral assessments. These criteria included parent report of a history of seizures or seizure medication, medical disorders or medications impacting the central nervous system, prolonged prenatal substance exposure, and significant sensory, motor, or physical conditions that limited participation in the planned tasks. Parent report of demographic characteristics including child sex, race, ethnicity, caregiver educational level, and household income was collected via a study-specific background questionnaire.

Language assessment

At each time point participants completed a standardized assessment of their receptive and expressive communication. Children under 5 years old completed the Receptive Language and Expressive Language subtests of the MSEL [39]. Children 5 years or older completed the Verbal Comprehension and Naming Vocabulary subtests of the Differential Ability Scales, Second Edition (DAS-II, [44]) unless they could not obtain a valid score for either verbal subtest. For children with a raw score lower than 3 points for either subtest or with an age equivalent below the floor for both subtests, the MSEL verbal subtests were administered instead of the DAS-II verbal subtests. The MSEL provides a valid and reliable assessment of language from 0–68 months [39] and the DAS-II Early Years provides a valid and reliable assessment of language from 30–83 months [44]. Each test includes one receptive and one expressive subtest. As described above, a verbal developmental quotient was derived from the mean age equivalent of the receptive and expressive subtests divided by the chronological age and used as the language dependent variable at each time point.

Working memory assessment

Participants were assessed annually using a battery of executive functioning tasks. One working memory test–the Preschool Executive function Battery (PEFB) Hide and Seek Task [45]–was administered at all time points. The Hide and Seek Task required children to remember the locations of hidden animals. Before starting, the experimenter administered practice trials to demonstrate how the game is played with minimal verbal instructions and task demands. If they passed the practice trials, the experimenter hid a small animal in one of nine locations while the child watched. After a 10-s delay, the child was prompted to identify where the animal was hidden. Initially, basic working memory was required to hold one location in mind. On subsequent trials, the complexity of the working memory demands increased as the number of animals hidden increased. As multiple animals were hidden in different locations, children were required to update representations of previous locations and hold several locations in mind. The task has been used with neurotypical children from 18–67 months [45, 46]. Simple and complex trials were combined into one score, and the primary dependent variable was the proportion of correct searches pro-rated for the total number of trials attempted.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was carried out using Stata SE Version 17 [47]. Descriptive statistics for sociodemographic variables and variables of interest were calculated to characterize the sample. Effects with a p-value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed) were considered statistically significant. To address the proposed aims, a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) approach was used. Specifically, we built a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) with maximum likelihood estimation with missing values (MLMV), which implements Full-Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) to estimate parameters for paths and covariances. Longitudinal associations between child performance on a working memory task (i.e., the Hide and Seek Task) and their language scores (i.e., verbal development quotient) were modeled in the cross-lagged panel model. Hide and Seek scores represent the proportion of correct searches pro-rated for the total number of trials attempted (with a possible range from 0 to 1) in the task. Values can be interpreted as percent correct. For instance, a mean value of 0.329 represents 32.9% correct. Language scores represent verbal developmental quotients (DQ) on the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL). DQ scores closer to 100 are interpreted as language scores that correspond closely to chronological age, while lower scores are below age expectations and higher scores are above age expectations. The CLPM had three waves of data collection (i.e., T1, T2, and T3) across two cohorts of autistic children. Data from the two cohorts were pooled into a single model and all parameters were estimated across the combined sample to maximize power in the model given the modest sample size relative to the number of paths being estimated. The model included both autoregressive paths between time points of each measure (e.g., from working memory at T1 to working memory at T2 to working memory at T3) and cross-lagged paths across each measure (e.g., working memory at T1 to language at T2; language at T1 to working memory at T2). Covariances between constructs were also estimated in the CLPM within each time point of assessment (e.g., between working memory at T1 and language at T1). The CLPM included freely estimated autoregressive paths (e.g., working memory at T1 → working memory at T2 → working memory at T3; language at T1 → language at T2 → language at T3) and cross-lagged paths between constructs at adjacent time points (e.g., working memory at T1 → language at T2 and language T1 → working memory at T2). No additional constraints were imposed. Covariances were also estimated as shown in the model. No additional covariates were included in the current model, although language scores did rely on age-equivalent metrics to account for developmental differences. Missing data were handled in the SEM models using FIML, which allows all available data to contribute to parameter estimation. Model fit indices were evaluated using multiple SEM fit indices.

Cross-lagged panel model

A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was fit to examine longitudinal associations between working memory and language scores across three time points using FIML, with an effective sample size of N = 159 (Fig. 1, Table 3). This approach allows for all available data to contribute to parameter estimation, as opposed to listwise deletion methods where data can be lost across cases due to missingness. The model demonstrated good fit, with a non-significant likelihood ratio test, χ2(4) = 6.53, p = 0.163. Additional indices supported acceptable to good fit, RMSEA = 0.063, 90% CI [0.000, 0.147], PCLOSE = 0.325; CFI = 0.988; TLI = 0.956. Information criteria were AIC = 2311.96 and BIC = 2382.54.

Cross-lagged panel model parameter estimates for longitudinal associations between working memory and language

Both constructs assessed were stable across waves of data collection. Working memory scores during T1 significantly predicted subsequent scores at T2 (β = 0.30, p = 0.02), and scores at T2 also predicted scores at T3 (β = 0.91, p < 0.001). Similarly, language scores at T1 significantly predicted scores at T2 (β = 0.76, p < 0.001), and scores at T2 predicted scores at T3 (β = 0.87, p < 0.001).

Cross-lagged associations were also modeled and were significant for paths from working memory at T1 to language at T2 (β = 0.31, p = 0.01) and from language at T1 to working memory at T2 (β = 0.40, p < 0.001). Cross-lagged paths from T2 to T3 were not statistically significant in either direction. That is, bi-directional longitudinal associations were present between working memory and language at earlier, but not later, time points.

The cross-lagged model also assessed concurrent covariances between the residuals of each construct within each respective time point. There was shared covariance at T1 (r = 0.39), T2 (r = 0.62), and T3 (r = 0.49) between working memory and language scores, even after accounting for autoregressive and cross-lagged effects. This covariance indicates the presence of concurrent relationships between working memory and language at each time point.

Discussion

This study detected bi-directional longitudinal associations between working memory and language in autistic toddlers and preschoolers. Better language abilities at Time 1 predicted better visuospatial working memory performance measured with the Hide and Seek Task at Time 2, and working memory at Time 1 similarly predicted later language at Time 2. However, these reciprocal associations were not observed from Time 2 to Time 3. To our knowledge, this is the first study to document such a relationship longitudinally in young autistic children as young as two years at study entry. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as cross-lagged panel models conflate between-person differences with within-person processes and may not necessarily reflect effects at the individual level [52].

Our model extends work with young neurotypical children that examines longitudinal associations between working memory and language. Our findings are consistent with studies that have demonstrated a bi-directional, rather than unidirectional, relation between these domains––at least early in development [15–17]. The shared covariance between concurrent working memory and language scores at each time point after accounting for autoregressive and cross-lagged effects is also consistent with previous cross-sectional analyses in autistic children as young as age two [37, 38] that found concurrent verbal abilities are closely related to working memory performance in autistic toddlers and preschoolers across parent report and lab-based measures.

Of note, our working memory task indexed visuospatial working memory and not verbal working memory. The Hide and Seek Task required children to hold information in mind about the spatial locations of each animal in a grid with nine possible hiding locations. Visuospatial working memory appears to be relatively more disrupted in autism than verbal working memory [7, 53] and working memory is reduced relative to same age peers regardless of age suggesting that these differences in working memory are somewhat stable throughout development once children reach elementary school [7].

The bidirectional association observed between language and visuospatial working memory from the first to second time point and the absence of relationships between the second and third time point warrants closer consideration. It is possible that our findings reflect different developmental phases such that the earlier preschool period reflects stronger co-development of emerging skills related to language and visuospatial working memory. At earlier time points, visuospatial working memory is rapidly developing and we speculate it may be particularly important for processing prelinguistic input such as gestures, spatial referencing, and joint attention cues, which scaffold early language acquisition [54]. In autism, reduced ability to hold and update visuospatial information during infancy and toddlerhood may limit the information gained from prelinguistic inputs and may disrupt subsequent executive function development by limiting the ability to manipulate and reflect on ideas [8, 12]. Conversely, emerging language may support initial working memory development by providing labels and strategies that aid in holding and manipulating information. The absence of a relationship between visuospatial working memory and language at later time points in our sample is not entirely surprising. As children progress through the preschool years, other cognitive abilities may be more related to increasingly complex language development. For example, developing inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility may relate to the ability to parse the meaning of more complex or ambiguous utterances and narrative language [55, 56]. Our later timepoints may reflect a developmental transition wherein language processing becomes more phonologically and syntactically complex, thereby reducing reliance on holding visuospatial information in mind.

The bi-directional relationship between visuospatial working memory and language was present when 58.8% of the sample was 2–3-years-old and 41.2% was 4–5-years-old, suggesting that the early development of language and working memory are more closely related among toddlers and younger autistic preschoolers than they are during the later preschool period as children approach school entry. Our sample size and the number of paths being estimated limited our ability to examine these cohorts separately. At age two, autistic children who often exhibit delays in development are in the early stages of acquiring both language and working memory skills. Language and working memory may develop together as these foundational abilities are emerging. As children grow older, both language and working memory become more established and specialized, reducing the strength of observable bi-directional effects. Our findings align with previous literature documenting the bi-directional development of these processes in neurotypical children [15–17, 57]––and inconsistencies in this literature including no bi-directional relationships [58] or partial relationships [59]. Longitudinal studies did not find bi-directional relationships with language and working memory among older children who were at least age four at study entry [58]. By this age, neurotypical preschoolers have acquired more advanced language skills, have much larger vocabularies, and use language more automatically. Likewise, they may be better able to engage with more complex working-memory task demands and rely less on language to support their performance. This may help explain why children with lower language abilities continue to struggle with working memory tasks as they age [60]. Future research should further examine this longitudinal relationship in school-aged autistic children with a range of language profiles to determine whether these developmental patterns persist.

An alternative account for the early association between working memory and language tasks may be the presence of a domain general factor that contributes to performance in both—particularly earlier in development. For example, attention, social features of autism, or general developmental level may influence the ability of younger children to engage with both the Mullen language subtests and the Hide and Seek Task. Our selection of Hide and Seek as a measure of working memory in young autistic children prioritized sensitivity to a range of developmental levels, minimal language and sustained attention demands, and materials that are engaging. Data collection protocols also emphasized flexibility to provide breaks and re-engage children between trials if their attention waned. Nonetheless, it is possible that one or more of these factors contributed to differences in performance. Replication with other tasks and larger samples to test covariates will help clarify the domain specificity of the observed associations.

Our data provide critical clinical and theoretical information about the importance of visuospatial memory early in the developmental course of autism—proximal to the initial diagnosis. Whereas assessment and early intervention with autistic children have historically targeted language and communication development directly, our data suggest early visuospatial working memory skills are longitudinally associated with later language acquisition in young autistic children and may represent a potential intervention target for some children. Our battery emphasized a variety of receptive and expressive vocabulary skills such as labeling pictures and objects and following instructions that required comprehension of specific words and highlighted differences in these language related outcomes for children who entered the study with different levels of visuospatial working memory. Together with data from neurotypical children [22–26], our data are consistent with the theory that working memory is associated with vocabulary development. It is possible that children who can hold information in mind and update their representations as they parse the language stream and map relevant visual input, such as gestures or objects and actions that are being labeled, may show stronger vocabulary outcomes. Conversely, the bi-directional associations in our dataset underscore the potential importance of improving early language functioning in autistic preschoolers to support later working memory development [61].

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, we relied on a single laboratory-based visuospatial working memory task (Hide and Seek). And second, we combined 2- and 4-year-old cohorts. Future studies should incorporate additional working memory tasks and recruit larger samples to increase statistical power and determine whether these patterns hold within narrower cohorts. Furthermore, future work should explore how this relationship manifests in real-world contexts, including through parent-report measures.

Additionally, we used a cross-lagged panel model, which has faced growing critique over the past decade. Hamaker et al. [52] highlighted a key limitation: cross-lagged effects conflate between-person differences with within-person processes, potentially leading to inaccurate conclusions about the presence, strength, or direction of causal influences. In other words, while the findings in the current study suggest that there may be bi-directional associations between working memory and language at earlier developmental time points, this pattern may not necessarily reflect effects at the individual level. Furthermore, cross-lagged models were fit with FIML which tends to have tolerance for missing data across time points, but systematic attrition at T2 and T3 may have biased findings. Future research with larger sample sizes will be needed to determine whether the current pattern of effects replicates, and to adjust for other potential covariates like domain-general differences in cognitive ability, autism severity, and socioeconomic status.

The language measures used in this study also represent a limitation. The MSEL and DAS-II provide useful information about expressive and receptive language abilities, although the measures we used did not capture the full range of children’s language skills. In particular, working memory has been linked to phonological processing [62], vocabulary acquisition [63], and new word learning [64], which were not specifically measured with our assessments. Future research would benefit from incorporating more domain-specific language assessments to better reflect the complex relationships between working memory and language abilities.

Conclusions

This study highlights the early bi-directional association between visuospatial working memory and language which could inform future intervention efforts aimed at supporting both areas. Although these associations were detected at earlier time points in our longitudinal model, we did not detect associations between language or working memory skills at later time points. These longitudinal relationships and their timing provide important theoretical and clinical information about neurocognitive development in autistic children, who often experience challenges in these areas.
