Localized sample-based quantum diagonalization for strongly correlated chemistry
Authors: Qiaohong Wang, Kevin J. Sung, Ruhee D’Cunha, Matthew R. Hermes, Tanvi Gujarati, Yukio Kawashima, Yu-ya Ohnishi, Gavin O. Jones, Mario Motta, Laura Gagliardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceStrongly correlated systems, such as transition-metal complexes, are central to catalysis and energy science, and remain challenging for classical algorithms because their ground states are not qualitatively approximated by a single electron configuration. Quantum computers, used with classical computers, offer solutions to the Schrödinger equation with a more favorable relationship between accuracy and computational cost. We combine physically motivated, fragment-based multireference wave function theory with sample-based quantum diagonalization in a hybrid workflow, LASSQD. By focusing multireference calculations on spatially localized regions and solving the Schrödinger equation with accurate and scalable approximations, LASSQD is immediately compatible with near-term quantum devices, systematically improvable as larger fragment sizes become accessible, and provides a routine method on current quantum hardware.

Exact diagonalization or full configuration interaction (FCI) provides numerically exact solutions to the nonrelativistic, time-independent Schrödinger equation under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, for a given finite orbital basis. However, the computational cost of FCI scales combinatorially with the number of electrons and orbitals, limiting its practicality for large systems. To date, the largest demonstration reported involves 26 electrons in 23 orbitals (1). To solve larger systems, especially molecules with multiple transition metals, methods such as the complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) (2) restrict FCI to a subset of chemically relevant orbitals. However, the computational cost of CASSCF still scales combinatorially with the size of the active space (AS), which severely limits its practicality (3).

One direction to reduce the cost of CASSCF involves the reduction of the active space based on chemical intuition, for example, the generalized active space self-consistent field (GASSCF) (4–7), restricted active space self-consistent field (RASSCF) (4, 8), and localized active space self-consistent field (LASSCF) method (9, 10), also known as the cluster mean-field (cMF) method (11). LASSCF approximates the active-space wave function as a single antisymmetrized product of smaller active-space wave functions, with the assumption that such active spaces are relatively weakly interacting. Previously, LASSCF has been shown to accurately describe the dissociation of two double bonds in bisdiazene (10), to generate more consistent orbital evolutions throughout the potential energy surface of sulfonium salts compared to CASSCF (12), and to predict spin-state energy gaps close to those of CASSCF for bimetallic compounds (13). However, the computational cost of LASSCF within the fragments still scales exponentially, thus limiting the size of the fragments and the resulting active spaces that can be studied.

Another direction to reduce the cost of CASSCF involves approximately solving the active-space Schrödinger equation. Efforts to approximate CASSCF active space FCI solvers include density matrix renormalization group (14–16), multiple flavors of selected configuration interaction (SCI) followed by perturbation theory (17–20), variational two-electron reduced-density-matrix (v2RDM) (21) and semistochastic/stochastic methods like Full CI quantum Monte Carlo (FCIQMC) (22–24), among others. These approaches aim to accurately approximate the exact solution of the active-space (or fragment) Schrödinger equation with a significantly lower computational cost than exact diagonalization.

Recent advances in quantum computation have produced ground-state approximation methods that can be applied within a limited computational budget on today’s hardware. Examples include the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) (27–30), quantum selected configuration interaction (QSCI) (31, 32), and sample-based quantum diagonalization (SQD) (33). SQD has drastically increased the possibility of running quantum chemical algorithms on near-term devices, as well as the number of qubits used in quantum chemical calculations, with the largest demonstration to date, of seventy-seven qubits for a single point energy calculation of the [Fe4S4] cluster (33). SQD has been integrated with density matrix embedding theory (DMET) (34) and wave-function-based embedding (EWF) to enable applications in protein systems (35). SQD has also been employed to study intermolecular interactions (36), dissociation of methylene (37), hydrogen abstraction (38), among other chemical phenomena.

In this work, we introduce LASSQD, a method that uses SQD as the fragment solver to approximate LASSCF. An approximate solver is used in the fragments, which allows us to assess the impact of approximations in the fragment active space solution and solve fragment sizes intractable by FCI in the LASSCF framework. We find that LASSQD efficiently approximates the FCI space while allowing for meaningful orbital optimization of both bimetallic compound [Fe(H2O)4]2bpym4+ and trimetallic compound [FeIIIFeIIIFeII(μ3-O)-(HCOO)6]. In addition, we demonstrate LASSQD calculations on iron-porphyrin, which has one fragment size intractable for classical FCI. We test the limits of current hardware and of quantum methods for efficiently solving fragments of such sizes.

By leveraging SQD, we can probe how near-term algorithms on current quantum devices may serve as scalable active space solvers for multireference quantum chemistry. LASSQD provides a framework for assessing the current limitations and future potential of quantum hardware and software, enabling systematic progress toward solving challenging multireference systems on quantum devices.

Results

SQD Equivalent to FCI in Small LAS Fragments.

To understand the accuracy of our method, we compare LASSQD results against classical LASSCF, for which FCI is the fragment solver and therefore is exact in the fragments. For sufficiently small fragments, SQD reproduces FCI exactly, and thus we expect LASSQD to reproduce LASSCF exactly. We perform LASSQD hardware experiments (Fig. 1B) for [Fe(H2O)4]2bpym4+ (Fig. 1A). We use 15 batches (K=15), 6 iterations (Niter=6), samples per batch to 10 (d=10). Fragment 1 contains 4 α and 2 β electrons, while fragment 2 has 2 α and 4 β electrons. Each fragment is of size (6e,5o), and the bitstrings sampled from the circuits contain all configurations, and thus SQD diagonalizes the Hamiltonian in the full Hilbert space. Our results in Fig. 1C and Table 1 show that LASSQD achieves near-exact agreement with LASSCF, with an energy difference of just 5.3·10−7 Eh. The residual difference between LASSQD and LASSCF in Fig. 1D arises from finite numerical tolerances rather than from the SQD approximation itself. In this benchmark, the sampled bitstrings span the full fragment configuration space, so the SQD is formally equivalent to the FCI fragment solver used in LASSCF. We further compare the pair-density functional theory (PDFT) energies from the LASSCF wave function and LASSQD-PDFT. As Table 1 shows, the two values are again in agreement to 1·10−6 Eh. In the standard SQD solver, the Davidson algorithm searches for a single root with a default residual norm tolerance (defaulted at 10−9), which can cause convergence to a low-lying excited state rather than the true ground state. This is particularly problematic in our case of the intermediate spin configuration, where the triplet configurations have nearly degenerate eigenstates with each other. The SQD solver can converge when the residual vector norm becomes sufficiently small, even if the variational subspace does not yet contain the true ground state. To address this, we make improvements to the original SQD solver by allowing multiple root searches in a single calculation and lowering the tolerance to 10−16. This allows us to successfully find the exact ground state in the fragments and yield the same energy as LASSCF as reported in Fig. 1 and Table 1. See SI Appendix, section 2 for more details. To assess the approximation introduced by the LAS partitioning, we also report the CASCI energy evaluated with the LASSCF-optimized orbitals. As expected, the CASCI energy is lower than the LASSCF result, reflecting the missing interfragment entanglement in the LAS wave function, which could be directly measured by the energy difference between CASCI and LASSCF.

SQD Approximates FCI in LAS Fragments.

The full Hilbert space grows exponentially with system size, making exact diagonalization classically intractable for most systems of interest. The core of SQD lies in the selection of a compact, physically relevant subspace of determinants in which the Hamiltonian is diagonalized, following the philosophy of SCI methods. By restricting the diagonalization to a judiciously chosen subset of determinants, SQD may provide an efficient approximation to FCI that could be well-suited for a fragment solver in LASSCF. To demonstrate that LASSQD provides an efficient approximation to FCI in the fragments while enabling meaningful orbital optimizations, we enlarge the fragment active space for [Fe(H2O)4]2bpym4+ to 10 orbitals, including 5 3d orbitals and 5 4d orbitals, and 6 electrons in each fragment in a triplet configuration (Fig. 1A). This specific fragment AS size is still classically tractable, which allows a comparison between LASSQD performance to LASSCF when the LASSQD fragment solution is no longer equal to FCI. In addition, we use 1 kcal/mol as an energy error reference. This threshold reflects the typical scale of chemically meaningful energy differences, and also serves as a practical indicator of LASSQD’s approximations to LASSCF.

Conventional SQD for LASSQD.

We first present results from the conventional SQD implementation of LASSQD for [Fe(H2O)4]2bpym4+ with three different QASM simulations (LASSQD QASM 1, QASM 2, QASM 3). Heuristically, we use 15 batches (K=15), 6 iterations (Niter=6), samples per batch to 170 (d=170), which is around 80% of the maximum α(β) configurations. QASM 1 uses 1·105 shots and fully optimized LUCJ circuit parameters with the linear method, with nroot=10 and tolerance set to 1·10−16 for the Davidson algorithm. QASM 2 has 8·104 shots and a maximum optimization iteration to 20, with nroot=3 and tolerance set to 1·10−16. QASM 3 is carried out with 8·104 shots, maximum optimization iteration of 40, nroot=5, and tolerance set to 1·10−16. The energy differences to reference LASSCF (converged LASSCF energy) from the above three simulations are plotted in Fig. 2A. As Fig. 2A shows, all three simulations exhibit stochastic behavior, arising from the new sampling at every macro iteration. LASSQD is not purely stochastic: while the sampling step introduces stochasticity, the orbital optimization and subspace diagonalization are deterministic. The observed oscillations therefore arise from variability in the sampled subspace at each macro iteration, rather than from the optimization procedure itself.

To evaluate whether these oscillations fall within an acceptable energy range, we discard the first 20 macro iterations as a burn-in period, corresponding to the initial phase before the orbitals approach a stable minimum. We then calculate the mean and the SE of the mean (SEM) for the remaining ensemble and plot the energy differences with error bars in Fig. 2B. Although some LASSQD QASM1 points reach 1 to 3 kcal/mol accuracy, the post-burn-in mean exceeds 3 kcal/mol, indicating that conventional SQD is not suitable for stable SCF optimization. The consistent oscillations across simulations point to a fundamental limitation of SQD, motivating the development of carryover SQD.

Carryover SQD for LASSQD.

While both challenges are intrinsic to SQD, we focus here on addressing the first. To this end, we use a retention step, in which important bitstrings are saved and carried over from one iteration to the next. This ensures that the diagonalization consistently incorporates the most relevant contributions.

With carryover SQD, where key bitstrings are retained from macro cycle n to n+1, the algorithm exhibits improved behavior. Using the qubit configuration in Fig. 3B, hardware calculations (Fig. 3C, red line) reach sub–1 kcal/mol accuracy after nine macro cycles and converge smoothly with orbital optimization. The setup employs K=15 batches, Niter=6, and d=170 samples per batch, covering 80% of the maximum α(β) configurations. Third, to analyze SQD subspace efficiency, we perform LASSCI calculations as comparison.

From Fig. 3C, we first observe that when the carryover cutoff (ϵ) is small (e.g., 1·10−1, 1·10−2), the absolute energies appear stochastic, which is similar to when there are no bitstrings retained from one macro cycle to the next (Fig. 2). We also find that the hardware LASSQD calculation and the rest of the simulations reach convergence, which we define using the criterion ΔE<1·10−5 Eh for two consecutive energy values, where ΔE is the difference in LASSQD energy from macro n to n+1 cycle. This threshold is consistent with standard practices in multireference SCF methods and is chosen to ensure that both the energy and the orbital parameters have reached a stable minimum. In our calculations, we observe that this criterion serves as a meaningful stopping point for comparison of absolute energies and SQD dimensions in the following analysis. QASM energy values beyond convergence are plotted with dotted lines in Fig. 3C.

We further take the LASSQD orbitals at the end of the calculations and use them to perform subsequent LASCI calculations. The LASCI energy difference from LASSQD should only include the difference between FCI and the SQD approximation. In other words, by examining how close LASCI energies are to LASSCF, we can understand if the LASSQD orbitals are in a similar minimum as LASSCF. From Table 2, we see a near-exact agreement (to 1·10−7 Eh) among the LASCI calculations and the LASSCF calculation. It means that the various LASSQD calculations, though differing in final energies, carryover cutoffs, and therefore the SQD dimensions, all converge to the same set of orbitals and are in agreement with LASSCF. It affirms that LASSQD orbitals, despite SQD approximating FCI in the fragments, are of the same set of orbitals as LASSCF. It shows that the SQD approximation to FCI still allows for meaningful orbital optimization.

To confirm that this is not an isolated case with the [Fe(H2O)4]2bpym4+ compound, we tested LASSQD before and after carryover on a more challenging case with three strongly correlated fragments. As shown in four QASM simulations B of Fig. 4, the conventional implementation of SQD exhibits strong oscillations in macroiteration energies, with errors remaining above 1 kcal/mol relative to LASSCF. In contrast, when using carryover LASSQD, the stochastic fluctuations are substantially reduced: The method surpasses 1 kcal/mol accuracy within 10 macro cycles, and the overall energy profile converges smoothly. With the carryover strategy, LASSQD consistently achieves sub–1 kcal/mol deviations from LASSCF within only a few macro cycles, while simultaneously optimizing orbitals and mitigating sampling noise.

SQD and SCI as fragment solvers.

Next, we compare the SQD dimensions from LASSQD QASM simulations in Fig. 3 to LASSCI calculations to see if SQD generates compact wave functions that are competitive to a classical variant of SCI. We carry out independent LASSQD QASM simulations and LASSCI with various cutoffs. Based on results from Fig. 3D, we use ΔE<1·10−5 Eh for two consecutive energy values as the energy and dimension termination points for LASSQD simulations. The subspace size d is taken as the maximum size used in both algorithms.

The comparison between LASSQD and LASSCI is made at comparable diagonalization subspace size, rather than equal wall-clock time or total solver effort. This comparison is intended to isolate the efficiency of the selected determinant space: LASSQD constructs its sparse subspace from quantum-sampled configurations, whereas LASSCI constructs its subspace using a classical SCI criterion. The LASSCI cutoffs were chosen to span a representative range of subspace dimensions. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as evidence that quantum-sampled subspaces can be competitive with classical selected subspaces at similar diagonalization dimensions, rather than as a definitive runtime comparison between the two approaches.

As Fig. 5 shows, the performance of LASSQD is comparable to LASSCI and within 1 kcal/mol agreement to LASSCF with about 50% of the FCI space. LASSQD simulations also generate lower energies compared to LASSCI when the subspace dimension d is between 25% to 70%. The energy values and subspace usage data can be found in SI Appendix, section 4. This illustrates that LASSQD is competitive against LASSCI and that with carryover, SQD is able to retain useful bitstrings efficiently. This feature is especially important when the FCI space becomes intractable and we have to rely on the sampling and retaining of the SQD algorithm.

LASSQD Solves Beyond Classical LASSCF Fragment Sizes.

From the previous results, we have established encouraging examples where SQD with carryover can approximate FCI efficiently while allowing meaningful optimization. We next demonstrate that LASSQD can address systems beyond the reach of LASSCF, yielding results comparable to LASSCI in regimes where LASSCF can no longer be employed (Fig. 6).

Predicting the spin-state energetics of metalloporphyrins with near-degenerate spin states remains a major challenge for computational and theoretical methods. A prominent example is Iron(II) porphyrin (FeP, C20H14N4Fe). Density functional theory calculations (39–41), along with some experimental studies on porphyrin derivatives (42–45), predict a triplet ground state. However, the combination of the extensive π-electron system of the porphyrin and the strongly correlated 3d electrons of the iron center demands prohibitively large active spaces for multireference wave function methods. Limited active-space treatments have incorrectly assigned the quintet as the ground state (46–49), whereas more recent studies show that expanding the active space and/or including dynamic correlation stabilizes the experimentally observed triplet (50–52). Our preliminary demonstration aims to test the limit of current hardware capabilities by comparing the LASSQD spin gap between the lowest spin (S=0) and highest spin (S=2) with LASSCI. We use the same total active space (32e, 29o) as in ref. 24, and fragment it into the iron atom (6e,5o) and porphyrin ring (26e,24o). We perform independent LASSCI and LASSQD hardware calculations with different SCI thresholds and carryover cutoffs for both high-spin state and low-spin state. We then carry out energy-variance extrapolation (53) (detailed in SI Appendix, section III) for LASSCI and LASSQD. The results are reported in Table 3.

First, both methods consistently predict the lower energy state to be the high-spin quintet, instead of the singlet low-spin configuration. Second, the two spin-gap predictions are agreeable within their respective uncertainties. The relatively large uncertainties (17 mHa, 10.7 kcal/mol for LASSCI and 65 mHa, 40.7 kcal/mol for LASSQD), however, need to be addressed.

Uncertainties are computed as σhs2+σls2 and reflect the quality of the energy-variance extrapolations. For classical LASSCI, reducing uncertainty requires larger subspaces and higher cost; here, 12 HS and 11 LS calculations are sufficient to establish a linear trend, though more points would improve precision.

For LASSQD, quantum resource constraints limit sampling; we performed 5 calculations per spin state (10 total), varying carryover thresholds and subspace sizes.

In addition, finite sampling in SQD leads to statistical noise in the estimated energies, which propagates into the energy-variance extrapolation. Second, hardware noise affects the quality of the sampled bitstrings, introducing additional variance and potentially biasing the sampled subspace. While increasing the number of LASSQD data points would certainly improve the extrapolation and narrow the uncertainty, the present results already demonstrate that today’s quantum hardware can execute sufficiently deep circuits and return signals stable enough for reliable classical postprocessing, in agreement with classical alternatives. These results should be interpreted as a feasibility demonstration of applying LASSQD in a regime beyond classical LASSCF, where exact fragment diagonalization is no longer tractable. While the extrapolated LASSCI and LASSQD spin gaps are consistent within uncertainty, the current error bars, particularly for LASSQD, remain relatively large and do not yet support predictive superiority. With improved hardware quality, better code partitioning to leverage more HPC resources, and algorithmic improvement, we are confident that LASSQD will deliver increasingly accurate and precise energetic predictions moving forward.

Conclusion

We introduced LASSQD, a hybrid quantum–classical framework that integrates SQD solvers into LASSCF fragments. This approach represents the demonstration of using approximate solvers within fragment-based multireference methods, enabling treatment of beyond-FCI–sized fragments while retaining meaningful orbital optimizations. A key innovation is adapting the carryover strategy to the SCF formulation, which mitigates sampling stochasticity, leading to smoother convergence and stable orbital optimization. Applications to bimetallic and trimetallic complexes show that LASSQD efficiently approximates FCI, achieves agreement within 1 kcal/mol of LASSCF using only a fraction of the Hilbert space, and produces results competitive with LASSCI for fragment sizes inaccessible to LASSCF, like the iron-porphyrin case with preliminary hardware demonstration. A particularly important validation is obtained by performing subsequent LASCI calculations using the LASSQD-optimized orbitals. As shown in Table 2, the resulting energies agree with LASSCF to near numerical precision, which provides strong evidence that the orbital optimization remains well-defined and converges to the correct minimum despite the use of an approximate fragment solver.

An important aspect of the parallel execution framework of LASSQD is that fragment sizes can be scaled up to the qubit capacity of a given quantum backend, since fragment circuits are not constrained to be executed on the same chip. In this work, this capability is not explicitly executed to showcase multibackend calculations. However, the underlying infrastructure can support such extensions. Independent fragment circuits can be run on distinct quantum backends, which provides a clear pathway for extending LASSQD to larger fragment sizes.

For chemically relevant applications, such as reaction energetics, it is important that the method yields smooth and consistent potential energy surfaces along a reaction coordinate. In localized active space approaches, this requires that the partitioning of orbitals into fragments remains physically meaningful as the electronic structure evolves. While this cannot be guaranteed in general, prior studies of LASSCF have shown that due to fragmentation, the orbital evolutions are better controlled and stable compared to CASSCF (12). Since LASSQD retains the same orbital optimization procedure, it inherits this behavior.

A central approximation underlying LASSQD (and LASSCF) is the treatment of interfragment correlation at the mean-field level, arising from the factorized form of the wave function as an antisymmetrized product of fragment states. While this approximation enables a substantial reduction in computational cost and is well justified when fragments are weakly to moderately coupled, it becomes less accurate in regimes of strong interfragment entanglement. Efforts to recover the interfragment correlations include classical CI-like expansions, namely LASSI (54) and LASSIS (55); and hybrid algorithm VQE with CC-like ansatz (56, 57) and hardware-efficient ansatz (58).

Looking ahead, improvements to the SQD solver, such as generalized eigenvalue strategies for larger subspaces or semistochastic approaches that leverage sampling probabilities, will further increase scalability. In addition, extending LASSQD to recover interfragment correlations beyond the mean-field level will be essential for treating strongly coupled systems. This can be achieved, for example, by enlarging fragment sizes or by incorporating interfragment correlation through additional operators or circuit ansatz, such as LUCJ, within the LASSQD framework. This direction is currently under active development. Together, these developments position LASSQD as a powerful and improvable pathway for applying near-term quantum algorithms to large, strongly correlated systems.

Materials and Methods

At a high level, the LASSQD workflow partitions the system into localized active-space fragments, samples compact configuration subspaces with quantum circuits, and classically diagonalizes the projected Hamiltonian to obtain fragment wavefunctions. The resulting reduced density information updates the orbitals within an iterative SCF procedure. Carryover further improves stability and convergence by retaining important configurations between iterations.

In the LASSCF framework, the total energy is obtained by variationally minimizing the expectation value of the second quantized molecular Hamiltonian,[4]H^=hqpa^p†a^q+14hqspra^p†a^r†a^sa^q

with respect to the fragment active space configuration coefficients xa~i, and orbital rotations for the full set of molecular orbitals xrq,[5]ELASSCF=minxrq,xa~i⟨ΨLASSCF|H^|ΨLASSCF⟩,

where hqp and hqspr are the one-electron and two-electron Hamiltonian matrix elements, a^p†(a^p) creates (annihilates) an electron in spin–orbital p. The q,r index individual spin–orbitals in two different subspaces, and a→i is a determinant or configuration state function. The ansatz for the wave function is expressed as[6]ΨLASSCF=∏KU^CI,KU^orb|(⋀KΨAK)∧ΦD⟩,

where ΨAK represents a correlated wave function localized on an active space labeled by an index K, and henceforth ΨAK is denoted as “fragment” wave function. And ΦD denotes a single determinant spanning the inactive or core space. The wedge symbol ⋀ indicates that the wave function is constructed as an antisymmetrized product of the fragment-local wave functions and the core determinant. In practice, the choice of fragments and their active spaces is made to capture the short-range static correlation within subunits of the system. Within the standard LASSCF procedure, each fragment’s correlated wave function ΨAK is obtained by solving an FCI problem in that fragment’s active orbitals, with the mean-field effect of the remaining fragments included self-consistently. The specific LASSCF implementation used in this work is described in later sections.

The LAS ansatz shares conceptual similarities with multilevel DMRG (ML-DMRG) approaches, which also partition orbitals into chemically motivated subspaces to improve computational efficiency (59). However, they differ in the treatment of electron correlation in that ML-DMRG uses matrix product states with different subspaces treated at varying levels of accuracy to generate a single global wave function, whereas the LAS ansatz has mean-field treatment for interfragment correlation.

Quantum Algorithms.

QSCI and SQD.

QSCI diagonalizes an effective Hamiltonian to obtain an approximate eigenvalue (i.e., ground-state energy) in a subspace of electronic configurations sampled by a quantum computer. The QSCI framework has been further extended with numerous improvements to be compatible with near-term hardware limitations, including features of noise-resilient and shot-budget friendly, as sample-based quantum diagonalization (SQD) (33). Here we present a short review of the algorithm.

SQD relies on measurements of a quantum state Ψ in the computational basis, which yield bitstrings x∈0,12NMO, where NMO is the number of molecular orbitals. Under the Jordan–Wigner (JW) (60) encoding, each bitstring represents an electronic configuration, with |0⟩ and |1⟩ denoting empty and occupied spin orbitals, respectively (60). Repeated measurements generate a set of outcomes:[9]X~=x∣x∼P~Ψ(x),

where the P~Ψ(x) dictates the probability of sampling that particular bitstring with the presence of noise. To parallelize the computational cost of the classical postprocessing, the raw output space X~ is then subsampled into K batches of configurations S(k),k=1,...,K according to the same probability P~Ψ (i.e. empirical frequencies of each x in X~), of a fixed batch size d (the dimension of the subspace). The many-body Hamiltonian can then be projected into this subspace as[10]H^S(k)=P^S(k)H^P^S(k), with P^S(k)=∑x∈S(k)|x⟩⟨x|,

where we diagonalize the projected Hamiltonian[11]H^S(k)ψ(k)=E(k)ψ(k)

by solving the eigenvalue equation with a spin-constrained eigenstate solver, where S^2 is the total spin operator:[12]S^S(k)2=P^S(k)S^2P^S(k),[13]H^S(k)+λS^S(k)2−s(s+1)2|ψ(k)⟩=E(k)|ψ(k)⟩.

Here, λ is the Lagrange multiplier, which is set to a default value of λ=0.2, consistent with standard implementations (e.g., PySCF), which is sufficient to enforce the desired spin symmetry.

In the first iteration, bitstrings in X~ with incorrect particle number or z-component of spin are temporarily excluded. The remaining configurations are used to construct batches S(k), onto which the Hamiltonian is projected and diagonalized as in Eq. 10. The resulting spin–orbital occupations are averaged across batches,[14]npσ=1K∑k=1K⟨ψ(k)|a^pσ†a^pσ|ψ(k)⟩,

where p=0,…,NMO−1 and σ∈α,β.

In subsequent iterations, these occupations guide the recovery of bitstrings with incorrect particle number or spin projection. For each invalid bitstring x, |Nx−N| bits are flipped from the occupied or empty spin–orbital set, with selection probabilities weighted by |xpσ−npσ| from the previous iteration. The recovered configurations XR are then batched, projected, and diagonalized as before. The lowest-energy eigenstate among the batches is selected, and the updated occupations are used in the next recovery step. The SQD procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

SQD with carryover.

To improve convergence, we use a revised SQD procedure with a carryover mechanism (61), in which important bitstrings from one iteration are retained and reintroduced in subsequent subspaces. By preserving configurations with significant correlation contributions, carryover guides the iterative LASSQD procedure toward physically relevant electronic configurations. The carryover SQD procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.

After the first iteration, the CI-coefficient tensor of the lowest-energy solution is saved. Determinants are sorted by the absolute values of their amplitudes, and those exceeding a user-defined threshold ϵ are selected and mapped back to their corresponding α and β strings, denoted as carryover determinants. In subsequent iterations, unique carryover bitstrings are appended to each batch,[15]S(k)∪C∗α⊗C∗β→S~(k).

The enlarged batches S~(k) are then used for diagonalization. Thus, the subspace size |S~(k)| depends on both the batch size d and the carryover cutoff ϵ. After all batches are diagonalized, the lowest-energy eigenstate is selected, and the carryover set is updated for the next iteration.

In practice, we recommend choosing the carryover parameter through internal convergence diagnostics rather than comparison to a reference energy. Specifically, users can monitor: 1) stability of the LASSQD energy across macroiterations, 2) convergence of the final energy as a carryover threshold is tightened, and 3) saturation of the retained subspace size.

Local unitary cluster jastrow (LUCJ) ansatz.

SQD uses a quantum circuit to sample configurations. In this work, we choose the local-unitary cluster Jastrow (LUCJ) ansatz (62). LUCJ is derived from the unitary cluster Jastrow (UCJ) ansatz (63), which is the unitary variant of the cluster Jastrow (CJ) ansatz (64). The UCJ ansatz is a product of L layers of exponentials:[16]|Ψ⟩=∏μ=1LeK^μeiJ^μe−K^μΦ0,

where Φ0 is a reference state, which here is the Hartree–Fock state. Here,[17]K^μ=∑pq,σKpqμa^pσ†a^qσ,J^μ=∑pq,στJpσ,qτμn^pσn^qτ.

is an orbital rotation operator, and each J^μ is a diagonal Coulomb operator. p,q=0,...,NMO−1, and σ,τ label spin polarizations.

LUCJ introduces a “local” approximation of the UCJ ansatz (65) that allows opposite spin interactions at the same orbital and linear interactions of the same spin from the adjacent orbital:[18]∑pqJpα,qβn^pαn^qβ→∑p∈SJpα,pβn^pαn^pβ∑pqJpσ,qσn^pσn^qσ→∑pq∈S′Jpσ,qσn^pσn^qσ.

Here, σ∈α,β; S,S′ are sets of qubits that encode the occupation of spin-up and spin-down orbitals. On IBM’s heavy-hex processor, for instance, S={4k,k=0,...,⌊NMO−14⌋}, S′={(p,p+1),p=0,...,NMO−2}. The implementation of eiJ^μ is of constant depth (O(1)). The implementation of e±K^μ uses a Bogolyubov circuit (66, 67), which scales as O(Nq) in depth, and Nq is the number of qubits.

LASSQD.

RDM-based LASSCF.

The LASSCF implementation in the package mrh (68) allows substitution of the local fragment FCI wave function solver with any approximate wave function solver, provided the corresponding 1- and 2-RDMs are also available to drive the orbital optimization. In conventional LASSCF, FCI is performed for the Kth fragment in the presence of an external potential defined by the RDMs of the other fragments in the microcycle, and this potential is updated every time FCI is performed. In this work, we lightly modify this implementation: The external potentials are precomputed before the loop over fragments, enabling independent fragment-local CI calculations and thus allowing parallel computation of the fragment wave functions on quantum hardware. Orbital rotations in the full molecular orbital (MO) space are optimized during the macro cycles, except for orbital rotations within fragments, which become nonredundant for approximate wave functions, and are not considered in the LASSCF orbital optimization. Specifically, the microcycles correspond to solving each fragment’s electronic problem using the SQD solver, where the U^CI,K are updated while keeping the molecular orbitals fixed. Once all fragment solvers converge, their 1- and 2-RDMs are assembled. The macro cycle then updates the molecular orbitals U^orb using these RDMs to minimize the total energy, analogous to the orbital-optimization step in LASSCF.

LASSQD.

In this work, we use the SQD algorithm to return fragment 1- and 2-RDMs to the LASSCF solver for orbital optimization. Specifically, a macroiteration consists of: i) generating all fragment circuits, ii) hardware execution and bitstring retrieval, and iii) classical postprocessing with SQD to process hardware results and update orbitals. Repeating this loop over several macroiterations yields fragment solutions and orbital optimization consistent with the LASSCF framework. To enable parallel execution of fragment circuits on quantum hardware, we decouple circuit generation and classical postprocessing. In each macro iteration, fragment circuits are generated and executed on hardware in parallel, with SQD postprocessing performed sequentially to obtain fragment energies and RDMs. See SI Appendix, section I for more details. To integrate the carryover workflow, we save the MO coefficient matrix for each fragment and perform the standard carryover SQD procedure. At its completion, we obtain both the carryover bitstrings and the MO coefficient matrices for subsequent iterations. At the next iteration, the previously saved MO coefficients are loaded, and the overlap matrix between the current and prior MO bases is computed for each fragment. The saved α and β strings are then permuted according to the optimal overlap mapping and used as input configurations in the next SQD cycle.

LASSCI.

To compare the accuracy of LASSQD results when solving for fragment sizes intractable to LASSCF, we use the classical SCI solver implemented in PySCF v2.10 (the pyscf.fci.selected_ci module) for the fragments, namely LASSCI. Specifically, LASSCI constructs a solver object for each fragment in sequence, applies a spin constraint, and then diagonalizes with the Davidson algorithm to obtain the lowest-energy CI eigenstates and coefficients. From the leading root, it constructs spin-resolved 1- and 2-RDMs, as well as the expected spin-squared value for verification. The only difference between classical LASSCI and LASSQD consists in the way in which the SCI space is built.

LASSQD-PDFT.

Active-space methods capture the essential static correlation in the multireference systems but inherently miss dynamical correlation, regardless of how accurate the active-space treatment is. Here, we retrieve it through classical postprocessing based on densities exclusively, namely the multiconfiguration pair-density functional theory (MC-PDFT). MC-PDFT is a post-SCF treatment that uses the kinetic energy, electron density, and on-top pair density of a multiconfigurational wave function. MC-PDFT expresses the total energy for a single state as (69–72):[19]EMC−PDFT[ρ,Π]=VNN+TMC+∫ρ(r)νNe(r)dr+12∫∫ρr1ρr2r12dr1dr2+Eot[ρ,Π],

where VNN is the nuclear repulsion energy, TMC the kinetic energy of the multiconfigurational reference wave function, ⟨ΨMC|T^|ΨMC⟩, ∫ρ(r)νNe(r)dr the nuclear-electron attraction term and 12∫∫ρr1ρr2r12dr1dr2 is the Coulomb repulsion calculated from the wave function density ρ. These four terms represent the classical energy. The last term, Eot[ρ,Π], called the on-top energy, is the nonclassical energy. It is a functional of ρ and the on-top pair density Π of the reference wave function. In this work, the multireference wave function is ΨLASSQD.

In our implementation, each fragment’s 1- and 2-RDMs are stored during the LASSQD calculation for post-LASSQD analysis. The ρ and Π term depend on the 1- and 2-RDMs of the whole molecule, which are obtained by assembling the 1- and 2-RDMs of the fragments in a straightforward way.

In this work, we assess the accuracy of LASSQD based on total electronic energies (in Eh) rather than relative energy differences (in mEh or kcal/mol). This choice allows a more direct evaluation of the variational quality of LASSQD and LASSCI compared to LASSCF, and all the methods mentioned above obey the variational principle.

The number of shots needed in LASSQD is determined by the need to adequately sample the dominant configurations contributing to the wave function. While the Hilbert space grows exponentially with fragment size, the number of effective configurations is often much smaller due to sparsity, and the sampling effort is expected to scale with this effective dimension rather than the full space. Although a precise scaling law remains an open question, these observations suggest that shot requirements are governed more by wave function structure and ansatz quality than by the formal size of the Hilbert space.
