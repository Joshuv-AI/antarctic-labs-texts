Fibrocartilaginous Emboli in a 15‐Year‐Old Pony Mare
Authors: Elizabeth Kyle‐LaBell, Amelia Andersson, Brittany Baughman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFibrocartilaginous embolic myelopathy (FCE) was diagnosed in a 15‐year‐old pony mare by histopathologic examination of the spinal cord. The horse was euthanized and submitted for necropsy following focal seizures and lateral recumbency for approximately 12 h. Histopathology of spinal cord segments C5–C7 revealed severe ischemic myelomalacia with intravascular amorphous material that stained positive with Alcian Blue, consistent with cartilaginous matrix. These features in addition to the acute onset of clinical signs are characteristic for FCE. This case report describes the clinical and histopathological findings of a horse with an FCE with confirmation via histochemical staining.Keywords: degenerative disc disease, disc lesions, FCE, horse

Introduction

Fibrocartilaginous emboli of the spinal cord have been described in several mammalian species including humans, dogs, cats, calves and swine, but are rarely reported in horses (Tosi et al. 1996; Bartholomew et al. 2016; Okada and Kagawa 2019; Landolfi et al. 2004; Vidaña et al. 2020). FCE in large animals is poorly documented and the current literature in these species is outdated. The diagnosis is often made by histopathologic evaluation of the spinal cord where evaluation of cartilaginous material within vessels of the central nervous system is considered confirmatory (Cauzinille and Kornegay 1996). However, this is rarely performed because it is common to have spontaneous neurologic recovery in dogs, thus few cases end up being submitted for postmortem evaluation (Gandini et al. 2003). The pathogenesis of fibrocartilaginous emboli is poorly understood but there have been several proposed mechanisms that involve the vessels within the spinal cord, leptomeninge or both (Sebastian and Giles 2004). One theory describes lateral rupture of the intervertebral disc causing damage to the adjacent artery, which allows for entry of the disc material into arterial circulation. Another theory is high axial loading resulting in increased pressure against an unruptured intervertebral disc that allows material from the nucleus pulposus to seep into the nearby arteries (Sebastian and Giles 2004). From a clinical point of view, it can be difficult to differentiate between fibrocartilaginous embolic myelopathy (FCE), intradural/intramedullary intervertebral disk extrusion (IIVDE) and acute non‐compressive nucleus pulposus extrusion (ANNPE). Histologic evidence of disc material within a vessel at a distant site is confirmatory for FCE and the mode of injury can occasionally be elucidated on postmortem examination. However, in some cases, the cause isn't as definitive. In small animal medicine, advanced imaging such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be helpful in diagnosing disc disease or disc rupture; however, the utility of MRI in large animals is far less common. One case report in a horse with a disc herniation utilized postmortem examination and postmortem MRI for diagnosis of intervertebral disease. The limitation of MRI in large animals is the degree of superimposition making visualization of the spinal cord and associated disc spaces difficult, particularly with standing radiography. The article states that ‘standing radiography provides a baseline screening tool […] but cannot be used to confirm the diagnosis of IVD herniation’ (Mól et al. 2024). This supports the clinical difficulty of diagnosing FCE in horses and the need to correlate clinical signs with ancillary diagnostics. To date, there are no sex, breed or age predilections reported in the literature for horses.

Case History

A 15‐year‐old pony mare was presented to Mississippi State University Equine Emergency Services for possible colic on an evening in June 2023 after being found laterally recumbent in the pasture despite being normal that morning per the owner. The horse was reportedly up to date on core vaccines including Eastern and Western equine encephalitis, West Nile virus and tetanus. In addition, the horse had also been vaccinated for influenza and rhinotracheitis per the owner's history. The mare presented to the equine hospital laterally recumbent in her trailer and was minimally responsive.

Clinical Findings

Upon presentation, the mare remained minimally responsive and was dehydrated with pale, dry mucous membranes and a capillary refill time of less than 4 s. Vital parameters consisted of a temperature of 95.9°F, a heart rate of 40 beats/min and a respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min. There were no signs of abdominal pain or colic. The mare's neurologic status was non‐ambulatory tetraparetic. Per the owner, the mare had been ambulatory in the pasture earlier that morning. During hospitalization, the mare displayed focal seizures, which were described as shaking and teeth chattering. She also remained unable to stand or stay in sternal recumbency without assistance. A chemistry panel and complete blood count were performed which found a severely elevated creatinine kinase (CK) of 1147 U/L (Taylor et al. 1977), mildly elevated globulins of 5.8 g/dL (2.4–4.7), moderately elevated total protein of 8.9 g/dL (5.6–7.9) and mild hypocalcaemia of 8.9 g/dL (10.4–12.9). She also had mild leukocytosis characterized by neutrophilia with a left shift.

Treatment/Outcome

After initial presentation, the mare was hospitalized for 24 h and treatment was initiated, consisting of administered flunixin meglumine 1.1 mg/kg IV q12h, dexamethasone 0.05 mg/kg IV q24h, IV fluids 50 mL/kg/day and 100 mL DMSO added to intravenous fluids once daily. Seizure activity was noted three times throughout her hospitalization; however, no medications were administered. Throughout her hospitalization, she had an adequate appetite totalling 2 ½ pounds of senior mash and ½ flake of alfalfa hay per day and her bowel movements were within normal limits. She remained depressed, tachycardic and laterally recumbent. Due to a lack of improvement after 24 h of hospitalization and a poor prognosis with a low likelihood of return to function, the owners elected humane euthanasia with submission of the body for postmortem examination.

Postmortem Findings

Postmortem evaluation was unremarkable with no appreciable gross abnormalities of the brain or spinal cord. Major organs including the brain and spinal cord collected during postmortem examination, were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h. The samples were paraffin‐embedded and cut to 5 mm thickness then stained with haematoxylin and eosin. The most striking histologic change was marked myelomalacia and haemorrhage throughout the cervical spinal cord in the C5–C7 segments. Perivascular haemorrhage was present around multifocal vessels with occasional vessels containing intraluminal material consistent with cartilage (Figure 1A) that stained positive with Alician Blue (Figure 1A inset). Additional microscopic findings included multifocal neuronal necrosis and dilated myelin sheaths containing swollen axons (spheroids) (Figure 1B).

Discussion

FCE is an uncommon neurologic condition that has been reported in several mammalian species including humans, dogs, cats and swine. In a cohort study reported in dogs, the most common clinical presentation is acute onset of paraparesis that is non‐painful and non‐progressive in nature (Bartholomew et al. 2016). In our case, the only neurologic deficit observed was non‐ambulatory tetraparesis. Other case reports regarding FCE in horses have also identified cervical spinal cord lesions similar to the current case (Fuentealba et al. 1991; Sebastian and Giles 2004; Taylor et al. 1977). To date, there are no antemortem diagnostic tests to definitively diagnose FCE, thus it is considered a diagnosis of exclusion (Sebastian and Giles 2004). Other differential diagnoses include trauma, discospondylitis, Wobbler syndrome and equine degenerative myelopathy. Less common differentials include viral encephalitis and herpesvirus. The pathogenesis of fibrocartilaginous emboli is poorly understood, however, there are several proposed hypotheses for the pathogenesis of FCE in small animals. These include: (1) direct penetration of nucleus pulposus fragments into the spinal cord or vertebral vessels; (2) chronic inflammatory neovascularization of the degenerated intervertebral disc; (3) presence of embryonic remnant vessels within the nucleus pulposus; and (4) mechanical herniation of the nucleus pulposus into the vertebral bone marrow sinusoidal venous channels with subsequent retrograde entrance into the basivertebral vein and internal vertebral venous plexus (De Risio and Platt 2010). More research is necessary to find the mechanisms by which these occur to implement an effective treatment option.

Definitive diagnosis of FCE is made via postmortem examination of the spinal cord. Histologically, emboli noted in this case had a similar appearance to the nucleus pulposus, which may suggest this as an alternative tissue of origin in lieu of cartilage (Sebastian and Giles 2004). Both cartilage and material from the nucleus pulposus will positively stain with Alcian Blue. There is often necrosis and/or malacia in the nervous tissue, which is often associated with focal haemorrhages, spongiosis and axonal swelling (Fuentealba et al. 1991). These histologic findings can explain the neurologic deficits noted in these horses during clinical evaluation. Three cases of fibrocartilaginous embolism have been previously described in horses, which were associated with trauma, vigorous exercise or intervertebral disc degeneration (Fuentealba et al. 1991; Sebastian and Giles 2004; Taylor et al. 1977). In our case, there were no obvious spinal lesions apparent at the time of gross necropsy and no known trauma.

Treatment in small animals involves physical rehabilitation and supportive care. In acute stages of disease, it is important to prevent secondary spinal cord injury by administration of neuroprotective agents, such as methylprednisolone sodium succinate or polyethylene glycol and maintaining spinal cord perfusion (De Risio and Platt 2010). Prognosis in small animals depends on the severity and extent of ischemic injury (De Risio and Platt 2010). In contrast to small animals, there are physical barriers to keeping a recumbent horse alive for prolonged periods of time. These challenges include the inability to easily turn a horse to prevent decubital ulcer formation and atelectasis of the dependent lung field. As a result, horses have a poor to grave prognosis of returning to normal function.

Taylor et al. (1977) and Whitwell (1980) demonstrated an association between degenerative disc disease and fibrocartilaginous emboli in horses; however, the present case was not associated with degenerative disc disease. There are currently two other case reports of FCE in horses that are not related to degenerative disc disease, which further highlights the different proposed etiologies for FCE in horses (Fuentealba et al. 1991; Taylor et al. 1977).

Based on the disc material appreciated within vessels on histopathology, the present case likely had a disc lesion, however, the source was not appreciated at the time of necropsy. Disc rupture leading to FCE was suspected, however, narrow disc spaces were not appreciated at the time of necropsy and neither antemortem nor postmortem imaging was performed. This is a clinical case where the cause of the fibrocartilaginous emboli is unknown and emphasizes the need for further research on identifying the mechanism by which FCEs occur in large animals.
