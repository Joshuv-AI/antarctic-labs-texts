Transformations in nursing education: a qualitative study of student experiences in Türkiye during the pandemic and post-pandemic
Authors: Füsun Uzgör, Berrak Mızrak Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe education sector is among the sectors most affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. Nursing students experienced different education models during the pandemic and the normalization process.ObjectivesThis qualitative study explored the experiences of nursing students in Türkiye regarding online, hybrid, and face-to-face teaching models during the COVID-19 pandemic and the normalization process, aiming to identify the challenges, opportunities, and implications for nursing education practice and policy.MethodsData were collected through semi-structured interviews with 13 third- and fourth-year nursing students and analysed using Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis approach within a qualitative descriptive design. Nursing students aged 18 years or older who had been enrolled in the third and fourth grades, had both traditional face-to-face and online courses, and could understand and speak Turkish were eligible to participate in the study. Snowball sampling was employed to reach the target population.ResultsThe main themes identified were (1) challenges of online learning (social isolation, technical difficulties, task overload), (2) opportunities for online learning (flexibility, self-management, access to resources), and (3) adaptation to the new normal (valuing face-to-face teaching, integrating technologies, regaining confidence in clinical learning). Each theme is discussed separately in this study.ConclusionThe findings of this study emphasize the need to integrate the best practices of online, hybrid, and face-to-face teaching to create a more adaptable and student-centered learning environment. Nursing education institutions should invest in technological infrastructure, teacher training, and student support strategies to optimize the learning experience in all teaching models.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05033-9.Keywords: COVID-19, Nursing education, Online learning, Hybrid teaching, Student experiences, Türkiye, Qualitative descriptive study, Clinical learning

Introductıon

The World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19, first identified in Wuhan, China, as an “international public health emergency” on January 7, 2020, and later as a global pandemic on March 11, 2020 [1, 2]. The pandemic disrupted various sectors worldwide, particularly education [3]. Universities worldwide suspended in-classroom teaching and rapidly switched to online education to protect students’ health and prevent the spread of COVID-19 [4]. This transition significantly affects students’ academic, social, emotional, and financial well-being [5].

The transition to distance learning has changed the academic education structure. For nursing educators and students, there were some modifications in technical and administrative issues, such as the design and presentation of courses, interactions, counseling, registration services, learner support systems, and assessment-evaluation systems [6]. During the pandemic, nursing students received their clinical education through distance learning with activities such as simulation training, projects, and case analyses, and the exams assessing students’ success were web-based.

Clinical education was delivered remotely through simulations, projects, and case analyses, using web-based evaluations. However, nursing education, which emphasizes hands-on clinical practice, is not fully compatible with distance learning. According to Türkiye’s National Nursing Core Curriculum, nursing programs must last for at least four years and include 4600 h, half of which must be in clinical practice [7, 8]. Clinical training is vital for applying theoretical knowledge, developing a professional identity, and gaining real-world experience [9, 10]. Therefore, it is obvious that nursing education is not fully suitable for distance education [11, 12]. Owing to the pandemic, nursing education has undergone radical changes in many countries, significantly affecting students’ learning opportunities due to the suspension of clinical practices and the shift from face-to-face education to online learning. Furthermore, preclinical activities, such as laboratory- and simulation-based teaching, were impacted by social and organizational restrictions aimed at limiting unnecessary access and contact with others [13].

In line with the Council of Higher Education (CoHE) decisions in Türkiye, universities implemented various education models during the pandemic and the normalization process. Distance education has continued through the 2019–2021 academic years. For 2021–2022, CoHE recommended face-to-face or hybrid models depending on the course of the pandemic. Accordingly, the nursing programs conducted practical courses in person, while theoretical courses were offered online or in grouped face-to-face formats. While there are studies on the impact of the pandemic on education in general [14], there is a scarcity of qualitative research that explores the specific experiences of nursing students in Türkiye regarding the different teaching models implemented during the pandemic and the normalization process [15, 16]. Understanding the experiences of nursing students is crucial to inform the development of more effective teaching strategies and ensure that future nurses are prepared to face the challenges of professional practice in a constantly changing world. Our study is important because it provides practice-oriented insights into how nursing students experienced abrupt transitions between online, hybrid, and face-to-face education in a practice-based discipline. Furthermore, it may inform curriculum planning, institutional decision-making, and the design of flexible, and high-quality nursing education strategies that can respond to future educational disruptions.

In light of this information, the objectives of this study were (1) to explore the experiences of nursing students in Türkiye with different teaching models (online, hybrid, and face-to-face) during the pandemic and the normalization process; (2) to identify the challenges and opportunities perceived by students in each model; and (3) to understand students’ perceptions of how these experiences affected their clinical learning, confidence, and adaptation to changing educational conditions.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore nursing students’ subjective experiences in real-world educational contexts. This design was adopted to remain closely aligned with participants’ accounts and to provide a comprehensive and practice-oriented understanding of nursing students’ experiences as they transitioned through online, hybrid, and face-to-face education during the pandemic and subsequent normalization phase. This methodological approach is widely used in healthcare and nursing research, as it aims to provide a comprehensive and pragmatic summary of participants’ perspectives [17, 18]. The choice of purposive sampling and qualitative content analysis as the primary analytic strategy was consistent with this descriptive approach. The study design and reporting were guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist. Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant University Non-Interventional Clinical Trials Ethics Committee.

Setting

In Türkiye, universities are affiliated with CoHE, and nursing education lasts for four years, comprising eight semesters. The curriculum follows the National Core Curriculum for Nursing and includes both mandatory and elective courses. Core courses such as the Fundamentals of Nursing, Internal Medicine, Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynecology, Pediatrics, Psychiatry, Public Health Nursing, and Nursing Management include compulsory in-hospital internships for skill development. To provide a visual overview of the study context, a detailed timeline of the educational transitions and data collection periods is presented in Fig. 1. Following the first confirmed COVID-19 case in Türkiye on March 11, 2020, all higher education institutions suspended face-to-face education on March 16 and transitioned to online learning [19]. On April 9, 2020, the CoHE mandated that both theoretical and clinical components of nursing education be conducted remotely [20]. This distance education model continued throughout the 2019–2020 spring semester and the entire 2020–2021 academic year. During the 2021–2022 academic year, with the normalization process, CoHE allowed a return to face-to-face learning while recommending hybrid models depending on the progression of the pandemic [21]. Participants included in this study were third- and fourth-year nursing students who had experienced all three instructional formats (online, hybrid, and face-to-face) across this period. Data were collected through semi-structured online interviews conducted after the normalization phase, allowing participants to reflect retrospectively on their experiences across these different instructional modalities.

Sampling

Using purposive sampling, third- and fourth-grade nursing students of undergraduate nursing programs in Turkey were included in the study. These students were purposively selected because, unlike first- and second-year students who only had online education during the pandemic, they had experienced both face-to-face and online instruction, which allowed them to provide richer and more comprehensive insights aligned with the study objectives. The study included nursing students aged 18 or older, enrolled in the third or fourth year, who had experienced both face-to-face and online education. First- and second-year students were excluded as they had only online education. Sampling was conducted using the snowball method, where eligible participants referred others, and researchers also utilized their social networks to initiate the process [22]. A research announcement, including the study’s title and purpose, was shared via social media accounts of the nursing student association. Volunteers were asked to contact the researchers directly, and their contact details were provided. The first participant was unaware of the researcher. Each participant was then asked, ‘Who would you suggest we discuss this with?’ to reach others [23]. Students who were willing to participate in the study reached the researchers, and no students dropped out of the study. The sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation, a common standard in qualitative research. In qualitative studies, “saturation” is an important guide for calculating the required sample size [24]. Data saturation was achieved through an iterative process of concurrent data collection and analysis. Initial interviews generated the core themes of the study, including challenges of online learning, opportunities, and adaptation to the new normal. Repetition in codes and themes became evident in later interviews. By approximately the 11th interview, no new codes or themes were identified, indicating that code saturation had been reached. Subsequent interviews (n = 2) provided additional depth and nuanced variations within existing themes, including concerns about the “1.5-year clinical gap” and anxiety related to professional social cues, such as hesitation in simple interactions like knocking on a patient’s door. These findings further confirmed the consistency and richness of the data. Data collection was concluded after 13 interviews, as no new meaningful insights emerged.

Data collection

Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire with open-ended questions developed in line with the study’s objectives [25, 26]. The questions addressed the transition to distance learning, its advantages and disadvantages, educational practices during normalization, and the overall impact on students. (see Appendix 1). The interview guide was refined through preliminary discussions with two nursing students, who were not included in the study. It began with demographic questions (e.g., age, gender, academic year) and included 12 open-ended items grouped under four main titles: experience of transitioning to distance education (e.g., “Can you tell us about your experience of transitioning from face-to-face education to distance education?”); evaluation of the distance education process (e.g., “In your opinion, what are the positive and negative aspects of distance education?”), educational practices during the pandemic and normalization period (e.g., “How were your practical courses conducted during the pandemic and the normalization process?”); views on the continuation of distance education (e.g., “In your opinion, should distance education continue when the pandemic is completely over?”). To ensure rich and in-depth data collection, interviews were conducted using a flexible, participant-centered approach. In addition to the main interview questions, probing and follow-up techniques were used to explore participants’ experiences in greater depth. These included general prompts such as “How did that make you feel?” and “Can you give an example?”. For instance, to move beyond surface-level answers, specific prompts like ‘Can you elaborate on your clinical anxiety?’ were utilized. Clarification questions were also used when needed to ensure an accurate understanding of participants’ responses. This iterative and responsive interviewing style allowed participants to express their experiences in detail and supported the generation of rich qualitative data.

Online interviews were conducted via teleconferencing in a quiet, comfortable setting to ensure focus and privacy. Before each session, participants were informed about the study and their rights, and verbal consent was obtained prior to audio recording. Each interview lasted about 30 min, involving one participant and the two authors (FU and BMŞ), who are trained female researchers with expertise in qualitative methodology. Recordings were transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and securely stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team. Participants were assured of confidentiality, and no repeated interviews were conducted.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis approach [27]. In this approach, meaning units, condensed meaning units, codes, categories, and themes represent different levels of abstraction within the analytic process [28]. Qualitative content analysis was considered appropriate for the qualitative descriptive design because it allowed a structured and transparent description of participants’ experiences while remaining close to the data [17, 18]. All interviews were conducted by the two authors (FU and BMŞ). After each interview, the recordings were carefully listened to and transcribed verbatim. Both authors (FU and BMŞ) independently reviewed the transcripts to identify meaning units related to the research aim. Each author coded the data separately to support analytic consistency and credibility. They then compared and discussed their initial codes to resolve any discrepancies through consensus, supporting the credibility and consistency of the coding process. No additional researchers or external analysts were involved in the data analysis. Disagreements were discussed thoroughly until mutual agreement was reached, reinforcing the consistency and trustworthiness of the coding process. This iterative coding and discussion process was repeated across all transcripts until data saturation was achieved, indicating that no new codes emerged. To ensure the robustness of the final themes, negative case analysis was integrated into the process. The researchers actively sought and analyzed ‘disconfirming evidence’—accounts that diverged from the dominant patterns (e.g., participants who did not experience the typical ‘task overload’ or social isolation). This process of searching for deviant cases helped to refine the boundaries between subthemes and themes and ensured that the coding structure reflected the diversity of student experiences. To ensure analytic transparency, the coding process followed a structured and stepwise manner. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into sub-themes, which were further organized into main themes that reflected the overarching patterns in the data. The resulting hierarchical structure, illustrating the transparent transition from raw codes to final themes, is detailed in Table 2. For example, codes such as ‘feeling lonely’ and ‘lack of interaction’ were grouped under the sub-theme ‘social isolation’, which contributed to the broader theme “Challenges of Online Learning”. Additionally, codes related to recorded lectures and flexible scheduling were grouped under “Opportunities of Online Learning,” while experiences concerning the return to clinical practice and hybrid education models formed the theme “Adaptation to the New Normal”. To protect participant confidentiality, unique identifiers were assigned to each student (S1-S13). The coding criteria focused on relevance, clarity, and consistency to accurately represent participants’ experiences.

No member checking was conducted; however, trustworthiness was supported through iterative analysis, maintaining an audit trail, reflexivity, providing rich descriptions, and ensuring COREQ compliance. Transcripts were not provided to participants for comment and/or correction; instead, the credibility of the findings was strengthened by comparing coding decisions between researchers and maintaining a close alignment between the findings and participants’ statements.

Trustworthiness of the research

Following Graneheim and Lundman’s (2004) qualitative content analysis, credibility, dependability, and transferability were addressed to ensure trustworthiness. Credibility was supported through iterative analysis, constant comparison, and negative case analysis, which involved challenging emerging interpretations by searching for contradictory data. Dependability was ensured by maintaining a detailed audit trail of analytic decisions. Transferability was enhanced by providing rich descriptions of participants’ experiences and the study context, including challenges faced by nursing students in Türkiye during the pandemic. Adherence to the COREQ checklist further strengthened transparency, reliability, and validity.

Reflexivity

The research team consisted of two female nursing faculty members (FU and BMŞ) with expertise in qualitative research and nursing education. As educators working in Turkish nursing departments during the COVID-19 pandemic, the researchers were familiar with the study context and experienced the transition between face-to-face, online, and hybrid education alongside their students. This “insider” positionality facilitated rapport with participants but also introduced the potential for interpretive bias and power imbalances, particularly with students within their institutional networks. To address this, participants were informed that participation was voluntary and would not affect their academic standing. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study. The researchers used reflexive journals and field notes to document assumptions and responses during data collection. Data analysis involved independent coding and consensus discussions to ensure that themes were grounded in participants’ narratives rather than researchers’ preconceptions.

Results

The qualitative content analysis revealed three main themes describing nursing students’ experiences during the pandemic and normalization process: (1) Challenges of Online Learning, including social isolation, technological barriers, increased academic workload, and the absence of hands-on clinical training; (2) Opportunities of Online Learning, including flexible scheduling, enhanced access to digital resources, and the development of self-directed learning and time management skills; and (3) Adaptation to the New Normal, marked by a gradual return to in-person instruction, the integration of hybrid education models, and regaining confidence in clinical learning.

Three emerging themes

Three main themes and their related subthemes were identified in the content analysis of the interviews (Table 2). The identified themes were as follows: (1) Challenges of Online Learning, (2) Opportunities of Online Learning, and (3) Adaptation to the New Normal. Each theme is discussed below:

Theme 1: Challenges of online learning

Students experience loneliness and reduced motivation due to limited social interaction and loss of campus life. Poor Internet access and unfamiliarity with online platforms have hindered learning, compounded by device sharing at home. Heavy assignments and unequal group work increase stress and exhaustion. Lack of clinical practice causes skill gaps and low confidence, with case studies and videos proving inadequate substitutes.

Social isolation

One of the most prominent challenges was social isolation, which undermined not only students’ emotional well-being, but also their sense of academic belonging and professional development. The absence of campus life and peer interaction has led to a loss of identity among university students.

Living with my family, you feel like you are going to high school. It’s as if I never really was a university student. (S13)

This disconnection hindered individuation and social learning processes, which the participants viewed as central to their university experience. Moreover, the students described feeling psychologically withdrawn and demotivated.

I turned in upon myself. Socially, I was introverted like most students. I think this affected my performance a bit. (S6)

The limitation of communication with instructors further deepened this sense of detachment. The lack of real-time interaction and classroom dynamics diminished engagement.

Since we were not face-to-face with our lecturers, I turn on the computer; my mind inevitably drifts elsewhere… I get bored in front of the computer. (S9)

Technological challenges

The rapid and unprepared transition to online education during the pandemic caused numerous technical difficulties. Participants reported significant deficiencies in both infrastructure and digital competencies.

Frankly, we were very unprepared at that time because no one expected the closure. Education was suddenly suspended and we were not ready for the online education process; there was no system we had used before. (S6)

Internet connectivity has emerged as a primary obstacle in uninterrupted learning. Participants noted that connection drops, low bandwidth, and system disconnections reduce the effectiveness of online courses.

Sometimes I couldn’t attend the course; the number of participants was too high. Sometimes, since the instructor was inadequate, he was kicked out of the system, and we couldn’t have an efficient course. (S3)

A clear geographical digital divide was also evident in the data. Students from rural or underserved areas are disproportionately affected by connectivity issues, often to the extent of being excluded from the learning process.

I live in the city center, but many of my classmates are from Eastern Anatolia. Their provinces are rural, and they had many disconnection problems. I even had friends living in villages who couldn’t follow online courses because they didn’t have internet access. (S4)

Moreover, intra-household device sharing has emerged as another layer of difficulty, particularly in families where multiple members are simultaneously engaged in remote education. Limited access to digital devices and competing demands within households have led to fragmented learning experiences. “I have two siblings in secondary school. One was using the computer, so I had to connect via my mobile phone. I constantly had connection problems.” (S7).

Task overload

The predominance of assignment- and project-based evaluations in online education significantly increased students’ workload. Participants emphasized that both individual and group assignments were time-consuming and often conflicted with other responsibilities.

Doing assignments was very difficult because it took so much time. I could only finish one assignment per week and it was very challenging. (S10)

Group assignments intended to foster collaboration often led to additional stress due to poor coordination and unequal participation.

We met for group assignments, but meetings often turned into chatting, and some members did not participate. While I worked hard, others contributed little. (S1)

Additionally, the cumulative effect of academic demands and long screen time has also resulted in negative health outcomes for students. Prolonged screen time causes concentration problems and health issues such as eye strain and musculoskeletal pain.

I had difficulty looking at the screen; the brightness tired me out, and I was unmotivated. (S6)

I had leg cramps and was diagnosed with a herniated disc due to prolonged sitting. (S10)

Another layer of burden emerged from the blurring of academic and domestic spheres. Studying from home also meant managing household chores during breaks and reducing opportunities for rest.

During online classes, breaks were spent doing housework like laundry or washing dishes instead of getting fresh air. (S10)

Most critically, the overload of theoretical assignments could not compensate for the absence of clinical practice, which constituted a major gap in nursing education. Despite continuing theoretical lessons online, the students felt unprepared in real clinical settings.

We received lessons efficiently but remained inexperienced without clinical practice. (S5)

I didn’t know how to communicate with patients during my first internship. (S3)

Attempts to replace clinical experience with case studies and videos have been seen as insufficient, thus undermining confidence.

We wrote care plans for imaginary cases but didn’t know if we could apply them to real patients, which was a major drawback. (S5)

Theme 2: Opportunities of online learning

Online learning offers several advantages to nursing students. Recorded lectures provide flexibility, allowing students to review content at their own pace without missing important information. It also helped those who struggled with taking notes during face-to-face classes. Students improved their time management skills by creating their own study schedules, and had more time for additional courses and hobbies.

Flexibility

While much of the discourse surrounding online education during the pandemic focused on its limitations, an important counternarrative emerged among participants who emphasized the flexibility and convenience afforded by online learning. Most of the nursing participants looked positively at their learning experiences, particularly with the use of pre-recorded online courses. They reported that such recordings provided the ease and flexibility to review lectures at any time, place, and speed without worrying about missing content.

…I can say that lecture recordings were taken and we had a chance to listen to them later. (S2)

Beyond academic flexibility, the online format enabled participants to integrate education more harmoniously with personal life, reducing the tensions often experienced in traditional settings.

…I spent more time exercising and with my family. Apart from that, I was able to deal with different hobbies. (S2)

Some students also highlighted the convenience of accessing education from home, especially those who had previously faced logistical or climatic challenges while commuting.

Sometimes the conditions prevent it. Because the weather can be very harsh sometimes; we can’t go; we can’t do anything. But online education was more comfortable. (S8)

Self-management

Another prominent outcome of the transition to online learning was the development of self-management skills among students. The absence of face-to-face supervision and structured timetables initially posed a challenge; however, over time, many participants adapted by establishing personalized routines and cultivating greater academic autonomy.

At first, my whole routine had changed, but then I started to be organized. I was planning my day: ‘I will get this lecture at this time; I will eat at this time, I will repeat the lecture at this time; if I am going to work on a case, I will deal with this assignment at this time.’ (S10)

This enhanced sense of control over academic progress was further reinforced by the flexibility of the online format, which allowed students to set their own pace and learning schedules. In some cases, this leads to increased motivation and ambition, with students proactively seeking additional academic challenges.

Myself, I also take courses with higher grades. When online, there is the opportunity to these higher-gradecourses. (S1)

Access to resources

The shift to online education has notably enhanced students’ access to academic resources, addressing the long-standing limitations observed in traditional learning environments. A recurrent benefit identified by participants was the availability of recorded lectures, which enabled repeated access to instructional content, an advantage particularly valued by students who previously struggled to obtain lecture materials.

We were able to find the lecture notes because some of our lecturers did not give us the lecture notes. (S4)

In addition to pedagogical materials, financial accessibility has also emerged as a significant advantage of the online format. Several students highlighted a reduction in living costs, particularly those related to accommodation and transportation.

Because dormitory and house fees were problematic. It had a positive impact in terms of finances. (S4)

Theme 3: Adaptation to the new normal

Students valued face-to-face education for its direct interaction with instructors and peers, which enhanced learning effectiveness, practical skill development, and a sense of belonging, as reflected in their regained confidence in clinical practice after the pandemic-related gaps. They recognized the benefits of integrating online technologies, especially for theoretical and elective courses, and supported hybrid models that combine in-person and online learning. Students also described remedial laboratory courses and renewed clinical exposure as helpful in addressing learning gaps and regaining confidence in clinical practice.

Valuing face-to-face teaching

The transition back to face-to-face education was highly valued by the participants, who highlighted the unique benefits of in-person interaction for both learning effectiveness and emotional engagement. Students emphasized that being physically present in the classroom facilitated clearer communication through non-verbal cues, such as gestures and facial expressions, which enhanced their understanding and motivation.

Seeing the lecturer face-to-face, his hand gestures, facial expressions, and lecture style were more effective for me. (S4)

Moreover, the return to in-person education provided opportunities to recover critical gaps in clinical training that emerged during the remote learning period. Participants described how face-to-face instruction enabled hands-on learning and remediation, particularly through laboratory sessions added to compensate for missed practical experiences.

In the third grade, they added a laboratory course. We couldn’t improve our basic skills in the first grade, just then the schools were closed, so they added a remedial laboratory course. (S13)

The consequences of disrupted clinical education were particularly evident in students’ emotional responses during their early internships. Many have reported feelings of inadequacy and anxiety stemming from the absence of real-world exposure.

…When I went to my first internship, I couldn’t even knock on the door and enter the patient’s room because I didn’t know how to communicate. (S3)

Now, we have just gotten used to it. We are in the hospital, we can do everything, and we have self-confidence. We are in more contact with patients. (S11)

Beyond the recovery of clinical skills, students highlighted that the prolonged absence from clinical settings created a sense of hesitation and anxiety regarding basic professional and social cues. Participant S13 shared a poignant example of this uncertainty when returning to the hospital environment:

When I first went to the clinic, I had to enter a patient’s room, and I didn’t know how to do it because we were so inexperienced. I was so hesitant—should I knock on the door, or should I say “pardon” and ask for permission? I was very indecisive, but eventually, we got used to it. (S13)

Integration of technologies

The pandemic-driven shift to online education has led students to reassess the role of technology in their academic experiences. While numerous challenges emerged, the participants acknowledged the potential of technological integration, particularly for the delivery of theoretical and elective courses.

…For elective courses that we can understand when we listen online, I think we don’t have to go to school for a two-hour lecture. But the theoretical sections of applied courses should definitely be face-to-face. (S2)

The idea of using hybrid education models, in which technology is used as a complement to face-to-face education in applied fields such as nursing, students also emphasized.

… We received hybrid education. In other words, while one group received face-to-face education, another group received online education. (S13)

…when the pandemic was completely over, online learning could be used together with standard face-to-face education. (S2)

Regaining confidence in clinical learning

Participants described the return to face-to-face and clinical education as an important step in addressing the practical learning gaps that had emerged during the pandemic. In particular, the suspension of clinical practice and limited opportunities for hands-on skill development had created uncertainty about their readiness for clinical settings. Remedial laboratory sessions and renewed exposure to clinical environments were perceived as helpful in supporting students’ confidence.

We couldn’t improve our basic skills in the first grade, just then the schools were closed, so they added a remedial laboratory course. (S13)

The return to clinical practice was also described as a moment of relief and motivation after a prolonged period away from hospital settings.

…after 1.5 years. I missed the hospital. I missed the practices. I was very happy that way because I was going to the hospital. (S4)

As students re-engaged with patient care and clinical routines, some reported that they gradually became more comfortable and confident in the hospital environment. These accounts suggest that students perceived face-to-face clinical exposure as important for rebuilding confidence after pandemic-related disruptions.

Discussion

This study identified three main themes in nursing students’ experiences during the pandemic: the challenges of online learning, emerging opportunities, and adaptation to the new normal. Students described facing social isolation, technical issues, and heavy workloads, which were perceived to negatively affect their engagement and confidence. Conversely, they have also identified gains in flexibility, self-management, and access to digital resources. A key finding was that the return to face-to-face education was perceived as important for rebuilding confidence and addressing clinical skill gaps. Students emphasized the potential of hybrid models that integrate online technologies as a complement to face-to-face teaching, describing a process of regaining confidence as they re-engaged with clinical practice routines.

Discussion of the findings of the main theme of “challenges of online learning”

Online (distance) education is defined as remotely conducted learning without face-to-face interaction using various communication tools [29]. Similar to formal education, it offers both opportunities and challenges. One of the primary challenges identified in this study is social isolation, which hindered students’ internalization of a university identity, motivation, and professional development, particularly in people-centered fields like nursing.This finding aligns with the literature, which indicates that limited interaction in online learning can affect students’ experiences, professional development [30], and motivation [31]. Moreover, this study contributes to existing knowledge by revealing that social isolation weakens sense of belonging to nursing education among nursing students [32]. Although learning processes can occur through social group interactions even in online settings, studies have shown that increasing opportunities for social interaction [33, 34] and encouraging student participation in classrooms [34] can positively impact academic success. However, in this study, online group assignments were often ineffective because of poor coordination and unequal participation, increasing stress rather than fostering collaboration.

Passive learning without interaction between the lecturer and the students makes it difficult for students to maintain their concentration for a long time [35]. The students reported that the absence of face-to-face communication led to distraction and boredom. Park and Seo noted that prolonged screen time causes fatigue and reduced focus, whereas access to recorded lectures encourages procrastination and distraction [36]. Participants similarly emphasized that long hours in front of the computer reduced concentration and contributed to demotivation. In contrast, seeing the instructor’s face during online education creates a sense of one-on-one instruction, enhancing engagement [37]. Therefore, using synchronous platforms or visible video recordings, along with short videos and in-class quizzes, can boost student motivation [36].

The sudden and unprepared transition to online education has left students and faculty members with significant technological challenges. These barriers, including unstable Internet, device sharing, the need for multiple household members to use the Internet simultaneously, and inadequate infrastructure in rural areas, widened the digital divide. Our findings are consistent with the literature reporting Internet disconnections [38], hardware/software deficiencies [40], access issues in rural areas [39, 40], and the need for multiple household members to use the Internet concurrently [40]. Lecturers’ struggles to adapt to new platforms also impacted course delivery. These challenges highlight the need to invest in digital infrastructure, educator training, and inclusive policies to promote long-term digital equity and institutional preparedness [41]. The pandemic exposed gaps in infrastructure and strategic planning for digital education.

Task overload, driven by increased assignments and projects, heightened stress, and blurred the boundaries between academic and personal life due to the lack of a quiet study space at home and conflicts between household responsibilities and student roles. The literature supports the idea that excessive workloads cause stress [26], reduce participation [38], and negatively affect learning [36]. While Ghaedi-Heidari et al. [42] suggested that students can manage heavy workloads and follow courses effectively, our study found that students struggled to balance academic demands with personal and household responsibilities. The literature also emphasizes that the absence of a quiet study space, conflicts between household duties and the student role, and digital access issues limit online participation [38, 22, 42]. Thus, clear instructions, realistic deadlines, and effective feedback are critical for managing workloads.

In nursing education, the lack of clinical training, with theoretical assignments failing to substitute for hands-on practice, left students unprepared and lacking confidence. This aligns with the literature, which indicates that the absence of practical experience during the pandemic increased stress and reduced self-confidence [43, 44], underscoring the limitations of online learning in practice-based disciplines.

Discussion of the findings of the main theme of “advantages of online learning”

The findings of this study revealed that online education has limitations and meaningful opportunities. As demonstrated by [42, 45], flexibility has emerged as a highly appreciated facet, enabling students to transcend temporal and spatial limitations for a more tailored and adaptive learning experience. Students reported enhanced engagement as the online format mitigated distractions associated with in-class note-taking, allowing for a deeper focus and repeated review of recorded lectures. Furthermore, this modality fostered self-regulation, time management, and academic autonomy, thereby bolstering motivation and personal responsibility, aligning with evidence that online learning promotes self-directed approaches [25, 46], increases motivation [25], and improves responsibility skills [26]. According to Bdair, self-management of online education and listening to recordings whenever and wherever students wanted increased their academic success [25]. Nonetheless, this flexibility may not uniformly benefit all learners; those with underdeveloped planning skills are at risk of procrastination and diminished performance. As stated by Broadbent and Poon, success in an online learning environment depends on the students’ self-management skills [47].

Financial advantages also featured prominently, with students citing reduced expenditures on transportation, accommodation, and daily necessities, rendering online education more economically viable. Similarly, previous studies identified the key advantages of online education as the lack of need for transportation to the education center, convenience, and cost savings (accommodation, stationery, nutrition, and transportation costs) [38, 45]. Concurrently, the format facilitated greater access to resources, including lecture notes and recordings, which supported sustained learning and addressed prior barriers in traditional settings, consistent with the findings of Stecula and Wolniak’s findings [48]. However, these benefits must be tempered by the recognition of potential inequities, as unequal access to technology could exacerbate the disadvantages for underresourced students.

Discussion of the findings of the main theme of “adaptation to the new normal”

The transition to online education during the pandemic has underscored the importance of hybrid or blended approaches as more effective learning models for the future [35, 49]. Students emphasized that face-to-face education was indispensable for motivation, comprehension, and the development of clinical skills. Similarly, Salmani et al. reported that students perceived online education as inadequate and preferred to return to in-person learning [38]. Following prolonged online education, students experienced a lack of confidence in clinical practice, particularly in communication and skill performance. Following prolonged online education, students described a lack of confidence in clinical practice, particularly in communication and skill performance. Renewed face-to-face and clinical education was perceived as important for gradually regaining confidence in clinical learning [9]. Students also described the return to in-person education as supportive of their emotional adjustment. At the same time, students did not completely reject online tools, acknowledging their value, particularly in theoretical and elective courses. This finding is consistent with Shahalizade and Musavi [50], who highlighted the increasing role of e-learning tools in health education, and with the systematic review by Mazzotta et al. [45]. Similarly, Tartavulea et al. found that the transition from fully online to blended education has a positive impact on students and may represent a sustainable model for the future [51]. The success of such hybrid models, however, depends not only on the course format, but also on instructors’ digital competence, content adaptation, and the use of interactive methods.

Another important finding was students’ reported adaptation during the return to face-to-face and clinical education. Initial anxiety related to limited clinical experience appeared to decrease through remedial practices and renewed exposure to clinical settings. Participants described engagement in patient care, teamwork, and repeated clinical experiences as important for regaining confidence and a sense of belonging in the clinical environment. These findings are consistent with studies reporting students’ insecurity and difficulties upon returning to clinical practice [52, 53], and with findings showing that participation in teamwork, patient care, and gaining new experiences gradually rebuilt their confidence and sense of belonging [53, 54]. Nevertheless, differences in students’ attitudes may be shaped by contextual factors such as the intensity of the pandemic in their region, the status of health services, and personal experiences.

Practical ımplications for nursing education

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that nursing curricula should include flexible and multimodal learning pathways while ensuring that online education complements, rather than replaces, clinical learning. The transition back to face-to-face instruction highlighted the importance of hands-on exposure in supporting students’ confidence and perceived preparedness for clinical learning. Furthermore, educational institutions should invest in robust digital infrastructures to ensure equitable access for all students. Educators also need targeted support and training to strengthen their digital pedagogical competence and to design interactive, student-centered learning strategies that can respond future educational disruptions.

Contributions and limitations

This study offers valuable insights into nursing education practice by showing that, although online learning provides flexibility and access to resources, it cannot fully replace the practical and interactive elements essential to nursing education. The findings support a balanced approach in which online theoretical learning is combined with hands-on clinical experience to develop more adaptable education models for future disruptions, such as pandemics.

This study has several limitations. The sample was limited to third- and fourth-year nursing students, and the use of retrospective interviews may have introduced recall bias. Although participants were recruited from different geographical regions, the sample size and qualitative descriptive design did not allow systematic comparison of experiences according to student year, gender, socioeconomic position, clinical exposure, or access to technology. In addition, conducting interviews via teleconferencing may have reduced interaction quality and limited the observation of non-verbal communication. The absence of quantitative data on participants’ academic performance is another limitation, as academic achievement may influence how students experience and evaluate different educational modalities. Because this was a qualitative descriptive study based on retrospective interviews, the findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating causal, temporal, or longitudinal processes. The study did not include follow-up interviews; therefore, it cannot determine whether students’ reported adaptation or regained confidence persisted over time or influenced their later professional practice.

Future research should include more diverse student populations, adopt longitudinal designs, and incorporate face-to-face or hybrid interview methods to enhance the depth and transferability of findings. Such approaches may contribute to a better understanding of the long-term impacts of crisis-driven changes in nursing education.

Conclusion

This study revealed the multifaceted experiences of nursing students during the transition between different educational models during the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent normalization process. The findings indicate that nursing students recognized several advantages of online education, including flexibility, time efficiency, and easier access to learning materials. However, they predominantly valued face-to-face education, particularly for clinical learning, interaction with instructors and peers, and regaining confidence in clinical practice. Online education was perceived as useful when used to complement, rather than replace, face-to-face and clinical education.

These findings underscore the need for a flexible and student-centered approach to nursing education that integrates the strengths of online, hybrid, and face-to-face modalities. Nursing education institutions should strengthen digital infrastructure, support educators’ online pedagogical competence, and ensure that clinical learning opportunities remain central to nursing curricula. Simulation-based learning, virtual laboratories, and hybrid education models may support theoretical learning and preparedness for educational disruptions, but they should be used alongside hands-on clinical experience.

Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to investigate the long-term clinical and academic outcomes of students who experienced different educational models. Comparative studies including both students’ and instructor’ experiences may also provide useful evidence for developing more responsive and sustainable nursing education policies.
