Analysis of optic neuritis risk in patients with allergic diseases
Authors: Jaeryung Kim, Kyungdo Han, Jin-hyung Jung, Sei Yeul Oh, Kyung-Ah Park, Ju-Hong Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association between allergic diseases and optic neuritis (ON) remains insufficiently characterized. This nationwide cohort study investigated the relationship between major allergic diseases (atopic dermatitis, asthma, and allergic rhinitis) and the risk of ON development. We performed a population-based cohort study utilizing data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service database. The study included 4,052,827 individuals aged 20 years or older who participated in health screenings in 2009. All participants were monitored until December 2018 or until ON diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for ON, adjusting for relevant confounders. Over a median follow-up duration of 8.2 years, 3,640 individuals (0.09%) were diagnosed with ON. After adjusting for confounders, individuals with allergic diseases exhibited a statistically significant increased ON risk compared to those without allergic diseases (adjusted HR 1.317, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.216–1.426, p < 0.0001). Among the specific allergic diseases, allergic rhinitis showed the highest association (adjusted HR 1.335, 95% CI 1.229–1.450, p < 0.0001), whereas asthma and atopic dermatitis did not demonstrate statistically significant associations after adjustment. These results highlight potential common pathogenic mechanisms and underscore the importance of future studies to clarify the biological pathways connecting allergic diseases and ON.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18088-6.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Asthma, Allergic rhinitis, Allergic diseases, Optic neuritisSubject terms: Optic nerve diseases, Demyelinating diseases, Risk factors, Allergy, Asthma, Atopic dermatitis

Introduction

Optic neuritis (ON) is recognized as an autoimmune-mediated inflammatory demyelinating disorder of the optic nerve that is typically marked by acute or subacute vision loss, ocular pain, and decreased color discrimination, with an annual incidence of 3.7 cases per 100,000 person-years1. Although this condition is frequently observed in association with autoimmune demyelinating diseases, such as multiple sclerosis2the precise cause of ON remains uncertain and continues to be the subject of ongoing research.

Allergic diseases, which include atopic dermatitis (AD), asthma, and allergic rhinitis (AR), are common chronic conditions triggered by an exaggerated immune response to generally harmless environmental antigens. Of the various allergic conditions, these three disorders are the most prevalent and extensively studied, together comprising the ‘allergic triad’ in clinical contexts3,4. A significant proportion of the global population is affected by these disorders, with the prevalence of AR ranging between 10% and 30%5, and these rates have shown a notable increase over the past decades. These conditions demonstrate shared pathophysiological pathways involving Type 2 immune responses and are consistently linked to systemic inflammation that affects areas beyond the primary site of involvement6. Emerging evidence suggests there may be an association between allergic diseases and a variety of neurological conditions7–9however, the relationship between allergic diseases and ON has yet to be clearly defined.

The potential association between allergic diseases and ON is noteworthy because of possibly shared inflammatory and immunological mechanisms10which may affect the optic nerve either directly or through secondary effects. In addition, allergic diseases are capable of inducing systemic inflammation and modifying immune responses11which could influence the central nervous system12. Elucidating this relationship may have important implications for clinical care, such as optimizing screening protocols, surveillance, and therapeutic strategies for ON among patients with allergic diseases. To investigate this possible link, we performed a comprehensive, nationwide population-based cohort study drawing on data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database, evaluating the association between allergic diseases (AD, asthma, and AR) and subsequent development of ON.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study cohort included 4,052,827 individuals, of whom 636,779 (15.7%) were diagnosed with at least one allergic disease at baseline. Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and clinical features of the cohort. Individuals who developed ON tended to be older (mean age 53.12 ± 13.30 years vs. 47.10 ± 14.09 years) and more frequently female (49.23% vs. 44.95%) compared to those without ON. Moreover, the ON group exhibited greater rates of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus (17.09% vs. 8.75%), hypertension (37.75% vs. 26.98%), and dyslipidemia (26.24% vs. 18.18%).

The risk of ON according to the presence of allergic diseases

During the follow-up period, 3,640 new cases of ON were identified. The incidence rate was found to be significantly higher among individuals with AD, asthma, or AR, as shown in Table 2. In Model 1, which did not adjust for confounders, individuals with allergic diseases had a significantly increased risk of ON relative to those without (hazard ratio [HR] 1.503, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.389–1.626, p < 0.0001). This elevated risk persisted after adjustment for age and sex in Model 2 (HR 1.328, 95% CI 1.227–1.438, p < 0.0001) and after comprehensive adjustment for additional confounding factors in Model 3 (HR 1.317, 95% CI 1.216–1.426, p < 0.0001). Within individual allergic diseases, AR showed the strongest correlation with ON risk in Model 3 (HR 1.335, 95% CI 1.229–1.450, p < 0.0001). The associations observed for asthma (Model 3; HR 1.022, 95% CI 0.864–1.210, p = 0.7981) and AD (Model 3; HR 1.446, 95% CI 0.968–2.160, p = 0.0721) did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the smaller case numbers in these subgroups. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to compare ON disease-free survival rates between patients with and without allergic diseases (Fig. 1). No significant additive interactions between allergic diseases and either age or sex were detected in the HRs for ON (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study, we identified a significant association between allergic diseases and an increased risk of ON. The observed association persisted even after adjusting for numerous potential confounders, including demographic details, socioeconomic factors, lifestyle variables, and comorbid health conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and body mass index (BMI). Our findings indicate that individuals with allergic diseases, especially those with AR, are at an increased risk for developing ON compared to those without allergic conditions.

ON is a multifaceted disorder marked by an aberrant immune response against the optic nerve, leading to inflammation and myelin destruction, which impairs visual function. It is commonly linked with several autoimmune neurological conditions, including multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and myelin-oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), each distinguished by specific pathogenic pathways and antigenic targets. NMOSD, for example, is characterized by autoantibodies that selectively bind the Aquaporin-4 water channel expressed on astrocyte endfeet13. In MS, while the specific target antigen has not been conclusively identified, studies suggest that patient-derived autoantibodies are capable of triggering complement-mediated demyelination14. Distinct immune targets likely contribute to the variability in ON presentation, therapeutic responsiveness, and clinical outcome across these disorders, emphasizing the importance of distinguishing these conditions. Our investigation focused on cases coded as H46 within the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, primarily representing idiopathic and inflammatory demyelinating ON, while excluding cases related to infection or drug exposure. Although approximately 30–50% of ON cases continue to be categorized as idiopathic2longitudinal analyses indicate that a significant subset of those initially diagnosed with idiopathic ON later fulfill criteria for clinically definite MS or other demyelinating disorders15,16indicating that the idiopathic designation may often reflect an early or undiagnosed demyelinating process. In light of this possible overlap with demyelinating disorders, it should be considered that our observed associations might be influenced by patients who progress to MS. However, current evidence indicates that allergic diseases are not significantly associated with increased MS risk, and some research even points to a potential protective effect. Recent large-scale investigations have identified no meaningful relationship between AR, eczema, or asthma and the risk of developing MS17and comprehensive meta-analyses demonstrate an absence of clear links between allergic diseases and MS risk, which implies that allergic diseases are unlikely to constitute a prodromal stage of MS18. Additionally, some studies indicate a decreased prevalence of allergic diseases among individuals with MS, and increased IL-12 production has been proposed as a potential explanation for this inverse association19. These data support the notion that the observed connection between allergic diseases and ON is unlikely to be chiefly attributable to patients who later receive a diagnosis of MS. For other demyelinating disorders, such as NMOSD and MOGAD, current research investigating their association with allergic diseases remains sparse. The limited prevalence of these conditions and their recent differentiation as distinct clinical entities have precluded large-scale epidemiological studies concerning their link with allergic diseases—a key topic that warrants attention in future research. This underscores the need for further investigation to clarify the mechanisms involved in ON pathogenesis.

The interplay between autoimmunity and allergic diseases is multifaceted, encompassing overlapping genetic, immunological, and environmental determinants. Although these conditions were historically regarded as separate entities, recent investigations have uncovered notable intersections in their pathogenesis, such as convergent genetic loci identified by genome-wide association studies, which indicate the presence of unified molecular pathways for both conditions20,21. Both autoimmunity and allergy are marked by aberrant immune regulation, commonly resulting from disruptions in T-helper (Th) cell subset activities. Allergic disease is primarily linked to a Th2-skewed immune profile, contrasting with the Th1 bias observed in autoimmune disorders22. Nevertheless, accumulating research demonstrates that Th1 and Th2 responses may occur simultaneously within individuals, thereby questioning the validity of the conventional Th1/Th2 dichotomy23. Disease-specific interactions, including the observed linkage among AR, asthma, and systemic lupus erythematosus17,24the increased incidence of autoimmune disorders among patients with AD25and higher rates of respiratory and food allergies seen in those with autoimmune thyroid diseases26collectively imply that allergic disorders could play a contributory role in the onset of autoimmune conditions. Interestingly, within the field of autoimmune-related demyelinating neurological disorders, certain studies have demonstrated that immune responses characteristic of atopic allergies and asthma may exert a protective role against MS, with a history of atopic allergies being associated with a reduced risk of MS and a milder disease progression among patients with concomitant allergic respiratory disease27,28. In contrast, other studies have found no significant correlation between allergic conditions, including AR and asthma, and the likelihood of developing MS29.

In our nationwide study investigating the association between the three major allergic diseases and ON risk, we identified AR as an independent risk factor for ON; in contrast, asthma and AD did not exhibit statistically significant associations following comprehensive adjustment. We selected AD, asthma, and AR for analysis due to their high prevalence, established diagnostic criteria, and prior validation in studies using administrative databases4,30. The fully adjusted HR of 1.317 (95% CI 1.216–1.426) indicates that individuals with AR have an approximately 32% higher risk of developing ON than those without AR. This finding, based on a nationwide cohort with extended follow-up and rigorous adjustment for potential confounders, offers robust evidence for this association. Although our joint analysis of allergic diseases revealed a significant link with ON risk, this overall association is predominantly attributable to AR, which presented the strongest individual effect. The lack of statistically significant associations for asthma and AD after thorough adjustment implies that the observed combined effect is mainly due to the prominent contribution of AR, rather than a synergistic effect among all allergic conditions. This trend may be indicative of the specific anatomical and pathophysiological connection between AR and ON. Nevertheless, the combined analysis is still clinically meaningful because it mirrors real-world situations in which patients frequently have multiple allergic disorders, and it adds important perspective for understanding the overall relationship between allergic inflammation and ON risk.

Our results indicate that the link between allergic diseases and ON is likely mediated by broader inflammatory mechanisms, rather than mechanisms limited to demyelination. This interpretation is further supported by the strong association observed with AR, suggesting that both systemic and localized inflammatory pathways play a central role in elevating ON risk. Systemic inflammation resulting from allergic diseases may extend beyond the initial allergic reaction sites, potentially impacting the optic nerve by promoting oxidative stress and subsequent tissue injury31. Immune dysregulation accompanying allergic conditions may render individuals more susceptible to the development of autoimmune responses targeting self-antigens; additionally, disruptions in neuroimmune interactions could increase the likelihood of neural tissue damage, including the optic nerve, from inflammatory processes32. Moreover, vascular alterations associated with allergic reactions may undermine the integrity of the blood-optic nerve barrier33,34thereby increasing the optic nerve’s susceptibility to inflammatory injury. Additionally, shared environmental factors could modulate immune responses and thereby contribute to the risk of both conditions by engaging common pathophysiological mechanisms35. In particular, the observed correlation between AR and ON is most plausibly attributable to a combination of anatomical and immunological determinants. The anatomical closeness of the nasal cavities to the optic nerve may facilitate direct local inflammatory effects, such as the liberation of inflammatory mediators and corresponding alterations in local vascular dynamics that have the potential to disrupt the blood-optic nerve barrier36. On a systemic scale, both AR and inflammatory demyelinating ON are characterized by perturbations in Type 2 immune responses, involving helper T cell activation and elevated synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines with potential effects on the central nervous system37,38. Disruption of the blood-brain barrier occurring during allergic inflammation may promote interactions between peripheral allergic responses and inflammation within the central nervous system39,40. Furthermore, mechanisms such as molecular mimicry and immune dysregulation in allergic states may induce cross-reactive immune responses, thereby increasing susceptibility to autoimmune damage of neural tissues41,42.

Our study demonstrates several strengths that bolster the validity and generalizability of our findings. By employing a large, nationwide cohort, we ensured considerable statistical power and reduced the likelihood of selection bias. The extended follow-up period permitted evaluation of long-term outcomes, thereby mitigating the potential for reverse causality. The comprehensive NHIS database enabled adjustment for an extensive array of potential confounders, such as socioeconomic status, health-related behaviors, and comorbid conditions. Given these methodological strengths, it is essential to interpret the clinical relevance of our findings within the framework of population health. Although the observed HRs indicate relatively modest effect sizes (approximately 1.3 for AR), these results may still hold clinical significance at the population level43,44as even modestly increased relative risks in large epidemiological studies can lead to a considerable absolute burden when exposures, such as allergic diseases, are highly prevalent45. The distinction between statistical and clinical significance is especially relevant in population-based research, where even small associations may translate to important public health consequences when extended to a large population46,47. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The identification of allergic diseases and ON was based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes, which, despite the use of validated criteria and multiple clinical encounters for specificity, introduce a risk of diagnostic misclassification. Furthermore, the absence of detailed information on the severity of allergic conditions and specific therapeutic interventions limited our ability to assess their impact on the observed relationships. Additionally, although the NHIS database provided information on corticosteroid prescriptions, critical details including medication dosages, duration, and steroid potency were not available, which restricted our capacity to perform fully medication-adjusted analyses. Given these limitations in the granularity of medication data, we made a methodological decision to exclude steroid usage as a covariate, reasoning that incomplete medication information could introduce bias instead of strengthening the analysis. Although we adjusted for multiple confounders, there remains the possibility of residual confounding from variables that were either unmeasured or unavailable in our dataset, as several potential confounding factors were not included in our analysis design or could not be accessed. Family history of autoimmune diseases, which may affect both allergic disease risk and ON susceptibility, is not systematically documented in administrative health datasets. In addition, other autoimmune-related conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis were excluded as covariates, since our study was designed to focus specifically on allergic diseases and their inflammatory pathways with respect to ON. Including additional autoimmune conditions could have introduced methodological challenges, such as overadjustment and diminished statistical power for our primary outcome. Further investigations that include a wider range of autoimmune disorders and their associations with ON would help elucidate the full spectrum of immune-mediated risk factors. Additionally, the study cohort was selected from NHSP records, which encompass approximately 70–80% of eligible individuals48; this recruitment strategy may have introduced selection bias, as participants involved in the program could be more health-conscious. As a result, this sampling approach might restrict the generalizability of the study population. Lastly, even though the detected association between allergic diseases and ON risk points to a possible relationship, the observational nature of this study does not allow for causal inference; thus, the results may reflect shared risk factors or unaccounted confounding instead of a direct causal link.

In conclusion, our large population-based cohort study identifies a significant association between allergic diseases, particularly AR, and an elevated risk of ON, suggesting that allergic conditions and autoimmune-related optic nerve inflammation may involve overlapping pathogenic pathways. These results are highly relevant for clinical management and public health, highlighting the importance of clinicians being aware of the higher ON risk in patients with allergic conditions to facilitate prompt detection and intervention. Effective management of allergic symptoms and inflammatory processes may potentially lower the risk of ON; however, definitive evidence is needed to establish whether improved control can alter ON risk. Future research should aim to clarify the pathophysiological mechanisms linking allergic diseases to ON by examining inflammatory biomarkers, immune cell profiles, and genetic predispositions. Longitudinal studies with comprehensive clinical assessments, as well as investigations into how treatments for allergic diseases affect ON risk and studies validating these findings in different geographic and ethnic populations, are necessary to confirm these relationships in varied settings. Furthermore, research exploring associations between allergic conditions and additional neurological diseases, and longitudinal follow-up studies comparing ON outcomes in individuals with and without allergies, would enhance understanding of patient prognosis and preventive approaches.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data derived from the Korean NHIS database. The NHIS, which functions as a compulsory universal health insurance program, encompasses about 97% of the Korean population. This extensive database contains a broad range of health-related data, such as demographic information, clinical treatments, medical procedures, and diagnostic codes classified by ICD-10 codes. Prior to 2020, the Korean NHIS implemented a biennial National Health Screening Program (NHSP) for all primary household income earners and employees, regardless of age, as well as for all adults aged 40 and older.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline, the institutional review board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (IRB no. SMC 2023-05-098) granted approval for this study. Due to the retrospective study design and analysis of anonymized public data consistent with confidentiality regulations, the IRB determined that it was appropriate to waive the requirement for individual patient consent.

Study population

We identified 4,234,339 individuals aged 20 years or older who participated in the NHSP screening program in 2009, designating this year as the baseline to secure an adequate duration for follow-up. Exclusion criteria were applied to remove participants lacking essential data on key variables (n = 165,856), those with a pre-existing history of ON prior to the index date (n = 4,561), and those who developed ON within one year after enrollment (n = 11,095) to minimize reverse causality. After these exclusions, 4,052,827 participants were included in the final analysis.

Definitions of exposure, outcome, follow-up, and censoring

Our analysis concentrated on three primary allergic diseases: AD, asthma, and AR, all defined according to their respective ICD-10 codesAD (L20), asthma (J45-46), and AR (J30) and selected based on their high prevalence in the general population as well as prior validation studies using the Korean NHIS database that demonstrated high specificity in case ascertainment4,30. Other allergic diseases, such as food allergies, drug allergies, or contact dermatitis, were excluded due to concerns regarding misclassification bias and the variability of coding in administrative datasets. To improve the precision of disease classification and prevent inclusion of transient or misclassified cases, we only included participants with a minimum of three outpatient visits within a year carrying the relevant diagnosis code prior to the index date. Previous studies utilizing the Korean NHIS database have validated this approach8,49,50.

The primary outcome was the new diagnosis of ON, ascertained using the ICD-10 code H46 (ON). Infectious ON (coded as H48.1) and toxic optic neuropathy (coded as H46.3) were excluded from the analysis. To enhance case definition specificity and minimize the misclassification of potential rule-out diagnoses, we required at least one hospital admission or two outpatient visits associated with this code to confirm ON. The initial date of diagnosis was recorded as the event date for analytical purposes.

Participants were followed from the index date (the date of health screening in 2009) until the earliest of ON diagnosis, death, emigration, or study end date (December 31, 2018). This methodology permitted a maximum follow-up of approximately 10 years.

Covariates

A variety of potential confounding factors was evaluated based on prior literature and their clinical importance. Demographic information included age and sex, and socioeconomic status was determined by dividing income levels into quartiles according to insurance premium contributions. Health-related behaviors were assessed via self-reported health screening questionnaires, with smoking status classified as never, former, or current. Alcohol consumption was specified as none, mild (< 30 g/day), or heavy (≥ 30 g/day). Physical activity criteria included moderate-intensity exercise for at least 30 min on ≥ 5 days per week or vigorous-intensity exercise for at least 20 min on ≥ 3 days per week. BMI was calculated and categorized using the World Health Organization Asia-Pacific guidelines: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m²), overweight (23–24.9 kg/m²), obese (25–29.9 kg/m²), and severely obese (≥ 30 kg/m²). Comorbidities were ascertained through ICD-10 codes and prescription information from the NHIS database. Diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were included as additional confounders. Comorbid conditions were determined based on past medical history, supporting ICD-10 codes, or prescription data relevant to diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia30.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants with and without ON were compared by presenting percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Kaplan-Meier curves were utilized to illustrate the cumulative incidence of ON over time, with group differences evaluated using the log-rank test. The risk of ON associated with allergic diseases was estimated using Cox proportional hazards models to calculate HRs and 95% CIs. Three models were constructed: an unadjusted model, a model adjusted for age and sex, and a fully adjusted model that included all aforementioned covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the separate effects of AD, asthma, and AR on ON risk. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.
