Satellite monitoring recalibrates unilateral battle reports of the US and Iran
Authors: Dongjie Fu, Kunlong Zhang, Fenzhen Su, Yinxia Cao, Leyan Shi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

On February 28, 2026, US-Israeli airstrikes triggered retaliatory strikes by Iran against US bases in the Middle East. On April 4, the United States invoked “shutter control” over commercial satellite companies such as Planet Labs, suspending the distribution of high-resolution imagery of the conflict zones.1 This move established a “data-denial environment” in flashpoints, aiming to secure wartime information dominance.2 This data embargo restricts the objective assessment of battlefield realities by global open-source intelligence.3,4 Under this data vacuum, the US emphasized effective defense, while Iran claimed to have inflicted heavy losses on critical hubs. This information asymmetry makes it difficult for the international community to accurately assess the true physical scale and humanitarian impact of the conflict.

To strip away the selective embellishments of narratives, we leveraged the open-source satellite data to achieve a rapid battlefield evaluation. Specifically, we utilized 10-m-resolution Sentinel-2 Level-2A imagery to monitor all US military bases reported to be attacked across the Middle East from February 28 to April 9, 2026, encompassing both targets with confirmed damage (red triangles, Figure 1A) and those with no obvious destruction at this resolution (orange circles, Figure 1A). Bi-temporal image pairs (pre- and post-strike) were aligned using cubic convolution resampling, and relative radiometric normalization was applied within valid overlapping regions to mitigate illumination discrepancies.5

To identify “damage fingerprints,” we executed a feature optimization protocol. Initially, prior damage and safe background samples at the Ali Al Salem Air Base were manually delineated using the post-strike image acquired on March 5, 2026. These samples were then extracted to calculate the two-sample separability across a comprehensive feature library,6 which included single-band relative changes, spectral angle mapper (SAM), change vector analysis (CVA), and other indices (normalized difference vegetation index and modified normalized difference water index). The analysis revealed that the relative changes in the red (0.71) and near-infrared (0.69) bands, alongside the multi-dimensional CVA magnitude (0.68), exhibited the highest discriminative power. Based on the separability rankings, we evaluated eight multi-dimensional damage identification strategies (detailed in Figure 1D1). Based on prior samples, by employing a sliding threshold approach across the continuous anomaly score matrices produced by each strategy, we generated empirical receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (Figure 1D2). The “spatial-spectral hybrid” mechanism emerged as the optimal strategy, achieving an area under the curve of 0.98. This model calculates the product of SAM and CVA,7,8,9 scaled by the local standard deviation within a 3 × 3 pixel neighborhood.10

The generated anomaly scores were binarized using a dynamic threshold defined as the valid mean plus three standard deviations (μ+3σ), with a lower bound of 0.00015 set (above the 99th percentile of Sentinel-2’s systematic noise in unattacked areas) to suppress extreme, low-variance background noise. Spatial sensitivity analysis (Figure 1D3) confirmed that the μ+3σ threshold balances noise suppression and effectiveness of damage extraction. The target boundaries were then refined via mathematical morphology—a 2 × 2 pixel connectivity dilation followed by a 3 × 3 pixel closing. An area filter was ultimately applied to eliminate isolated noise artifacts smaller than 4 pixels. Finally, to translate pixel-level detections into tactical intelligence, a multi-dimensional geometric analysis was conducted on the isolated damage polygons, defined as the contiguous spatial boundaries of structural ruins. We mathematically quantified the absolute physical destruction area, estimated the physical yield radius (the effective blast radius estimated from the destruction area), and calculated the morphological circularity and major-axis azimuth.

Applying the established framework, a damage assessment of the US military bases in the Middle East was conducted. As detailed in Figure 1B, satellite imagery identified 64 independent damage points (the point representations of the damage polygons) across 10 strategic hubs, yielding a cumulative 10 m detectable damage area of approximately 21.79 × 104 m2. Spatial analysis of these impacted zones revealed an average damage area of ∼0.34 × 104 m2 per strike point, with an overall standard deviation of ∼0.35 × 104 m2. As illustrated in the remote sensing identification in Figure 1B, the high variance indicates significant differences in the physical scale of the strikes across various targets, ranging from large-scale areal destruction to localized damage contained within 500 m2.

20 strike points were identified at the Ali Al Salem Air Base, with a cumulative damaged area exceeding 5.43 × 104 m2—the largest single target affected in this assessment. At Camp Arifjan, the assessment identified the smallest localized damage point (456.76 m2) alongside damaged zones exceeding 1.13 × 104 m2. The Al Dhafra Air Base in the UAE recorded a single damage area of 1.94 × 104 m2; hangars used for housing MQ-9 Reaper drones and U-2 high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft were destroyed in the attack. The US Naval Forces Central Command/5th Fleet Headquarters in Bahrain saw an average single-point damage area of over 0.46 × 104 m2, corresponding to the destruction of integrated building complexes. Crucially, these extracted spatial damages provide a quantitative reference that diverges from the narratives of both sides. The observed scale of destruction indicates physical impacts that exceed the characterizations of “minimal damage” or “successful defense” commonly presented in US briefings. Concurrently, it also suggests that Iranian claims of “massive strikes” may be overstated. In a highly complex information environment, remote sensing observations serve as an independent gauge to clarify the physical scale of the conflict, addressing the information asymmetry caused by selective reporting.

Beyond absolute area metrics, the morphological and geometric characteristics of the damage polygons decode tactical capabilities (Figures 1C1–1C4). The analysis of destructive yield (Figure 1C1) reveals a unimodal but right-skewed distribution, with a median damage radius of 26 m (mean: 30 m, 95% confidence interval: 26.2–33.3 m). However, the distribution’s long tail signifies a subset of strikes achieving significantly greater destructive effects, reaching a maximum single-point destruction radius of 79 m. Bivariate density analysis (Figure 1C2) correlates impact area with shape circularity, revealing that the highest data density clusters at a circularity index > 0.6 (mean: 0.64 ± 0.11). The polar plot (Figure 1C4) highlights clustered strike azimuths, with 70% of attack vectors originating at 90°–135° and 25% at 0°–45°. Combined, these metrics may indicate the use of near-vertical, high-explosive contact fuzes and the maintenance of consistent terminal ballistic trajectories.

Furthermore, the radial axis of the polar plot demonstrates that strikes were executed across distances ranging from 78.4 to 644.2 km (Figure 1C4). Notably, there is no observable degradation in payload effectiveness with range; high-yield, large-area strikes appear at both short and long distances. Spatiotemporally, the temporal density curve (Figure 1C3) exposes a “two-wave collapse” in regional air defense resilience. The initial attack caused a single-day destruction area of 5.86 × 104 m2. After nearly 2 weeks of low-intensity confrontation, the second wave of main attacks around March 15 still inflicted nearly 5 × 104 m2 of daily damage, indicating that air defense resilience failed to recover in the short term. In this damage-and-repair game cycle, 5 bases were recorded enduring multi-temporal strikes, with an average “kill-repair-restrike” interval window of 15 days.

From a military geography perspective, the empirical data demonstrate the escalating spatial vulnerability of fixed forward-deployed military bases. The observed “two-wave” concentrated damage footprint provides reasonable grounds to infer that these static hubs may be highly susceptible to defense saturation. The results indicate that fixed bases and their anti-missile networks exhibit large-scale vulnerability for the first time under mixed drone and missile attacks, which may prompt a re-evaluation of future forward base defense and deployment strategies. Concurrently, from the perspective of information and communication, this research highlights the potential and rapid response capability of open-source satellite networks in overcoming data-denial environments.

In conclusion, to address the information asymmetry caused by wartime commercial satellite blackouts, this study adapted an automated spatial-spectral hybrid extraction framework using Sentinel-2 imagery to assess actual physical destruction. The analysis confirmed 64 distinct damage points accumulating to approximately 21.79 × 104 m2. However, this study is subject to certain limitations. Methodologically, the 10 m spatial resolution of medium-resolution optical sensors inherently restricts the detection of sub-pixel structural alterations or small damage. Visual evidence from reported strikes at the Muwaffaq Salti Air Base reveals damage marks measuring only a few meters across. The spectral signatures of such micro-scale damages are severely diluted by the sub-pixel mixing effect, rendering them undetectable at a 10 m resolution. Consequently, while the extracted damage polygons provide a quantitative foundation—despite local boundary expansions inherent to morphological processing—they represent a conservative baseline of the total physical impact due to the omission of sub-pixel macroscopic events, indicating that the actual damage severity is likely higher than this 10 m detectable damage.
