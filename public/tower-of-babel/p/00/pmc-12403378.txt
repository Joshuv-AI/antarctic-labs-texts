Medical interns and senior medical students’ perceptions toward clinical teaching
Authors: Bader Khawaji, Raghad Ayman Alorabi, Rahaf Attia Alzahrani, Batool Adeeb Alhnaidi, Reem Saeed Alzahrani, Ramah Mohammed Althobaiti, Hebah Ghandora, Hussain Alsaffar, Alaa Althubaiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeveloping teaching skills is essential for medical students, as it enhances communication, reinforces clinical knowledge, and prepares them to educate peers and patients effectively. Engaging in teaching activities has also been linked to a deeper understanding of core medical concepts. This study aims to explore the perceptions of medical interns and senior medical students toward clinical teaching and medical education.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between 2023 and 2024 at a health sciences university in Saudi Arabia using an online survey. The survey consisted of 17 structured questions addressing various domains, including demographic characteristics, self-assessed confidence in teaching abilities, prior teaching experiences, and future career aspirations. The survey also explored participants’ views on the inclusion of medical education electives as a potential option in the curriculum.ResultsA total of 227 responses were collected. Over half (64.3%) had no prior teaching experience. Most of the participants (n=173, 76.2%) plan to work in an academic hospital. While 75.8% expressed interest in pursuing clinical teaching after residency, only 39.2% were interested in a medical education elective. The majority chose the ideal duration for a medical education elective being one month course (n=80, 35.2%). A significant association was found between interest in a medical education elective and plans to pursue clinical teaching (p < 0.001). Interest in medical education was also associated with higher confidence in bedside teaching (p = 0.012) and presenting at a journal club (p = 0.019). Similarly, participants interested in clinical teaching reported significantly higher confidence in these and other teaching-related areas. No significant associations were observed with gender or academic level.ConclusionThis study highlights a gap between students' long-term interest in clinical teaching and their engagement with formal training opportunities during medical school. Strengthening the integration and appeal of teaching-focused activities, particularly in key areas such as bedside teaching, may enhance students’ preparedness for future educational roles in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s12909-025-07864-7.

Background

Choosing a medical residency specialty for medical students is a complex and multifactorial decision, that may change as students progress through medical school, as it is influenced by numerous factors, such as the increased exposure to various specialties during later phases in their studies [1, 2]. Regardless of the clinical specialty, there are fundamental skills that should be developed professionally to prepare for a successful medical career. It is essential for medical students to gain knowledge and ability to teach clinical skills formally since becoming an educator for colleagues or someone with less experience in the future is certain [3].

Peer teaching for medical students plays a major role in their careers. Learning how to teach allows medical students to communicate more effectively, thereby improving their ability to communicate with their patients [4]. In addition, students who can teach effectively are more likely to gain a deeper understanding of the fundamental concepts followed in their daily practice. This will help them to enhance their learning by applying these principles in their own education [4]. Additionally, the opportunity to develop leadership, presentation, and organizational skills are crucial components of clinical practice, which will be developed by allowing students the opportunity to educate others [5].

In 2017, a Canadian study conducted by Hughes et al. demonstrated that a considerable number of senior medical students are interested in improving their clinical teaching skills and are willing to take clinical teaching training as an option in their elective selections [6]. The factors that motivate medical students to be interested in teaching are unclear; however, there are some factors that motivate healthcare practitioners to contribute to teaching medical students. For instance, intrinsic aspects such as personal satisfaction and joyfulness are shown to be associated with teaching commitments, and this was found to be superior to extrinsic factors such as career advancement of opportunities, recognition, and reward. In addition, Faculty-specific skills such as leadership and curriculum organization can also have a significant role in driving them to pursue teaching [7].

Although many medical students appear to be interested in medical education and teaching, they receive limited formal training in practical teaching. Generally, there are no adequate approved qualification programs to prepare medical students for teaching [8]. However, several medical academies in the United Kingdom and the United States offer formal courses designed to help medical students develop clinical teaching skills [9]. According to a study of 130 medical programs in the United States, 44% offer practical formal training in teaching [10]. In Saudi Arabia, it appears that there are no current medical programs that provide student-as-teacher (SAT) programs.

Developing medical education and teaching skills is crucial for training medical students and junior doctors, as well as for ongoing professional development [11]. However, in reviewing the literature, there are a shortage of studies that examine elective courses in clinical teaching, and the attitude of medical students toward them, especially in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, the current study aims to explore the perception of medical interns and senior medical students in Saudi Arabia regarding clinical teaching and the potential inclusion of elective courses in medical education.

Methods

The current study is a descriptive cross-sectional study, which allows data from a large sample to be collected and analyzed at a specific point in time [12]. It was conducted at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS), College of Medicine, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The college offers an undergraduate medical program leading to the Bachelor of Medicine and Surgery (MBBS) degree. This program is available to both high school graduates (stream I students) and bachelor’s degree holders in scientific fields (stream II students).

In Saudi Arabia, medical education is offered as a six-year undergraduate program, followed by a one-year internship. The curriculum typically includes two to three pre-clinical years, followed by clinical rotations in major specialties such as internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, and obstetrics and gynecology. Several Saudi medical schools, including KSAU-HS, include problem-based learning (PBL) that incorporates small-group discussions, case-based learning, and research tutorials. These formats offer students opportunities to develop communication and leadership skills and, in some cases, to informally engage in peer teaching or case presentations during clinical rotations and group activities. While some institutions may offer elective courses or workshops in medical education, such opportunities are not consistently emphasized or available across all programs.

All final-year medical students and medical interns in Saudi Arabia during the academic year of 2023–2024 were invited to participate in an online survey, with no exclusion criteria applied. A convenience sampling technique was used. The survey was distributed via email and social media applications by the investigators, and hard copies were also shared to allow broader participation. Although the authors did not track the exact number of students contacted, they estimated that approximately 400 eligible students were enrolled at the institution. Based on the efforts of the study team to reach and communicate with their peers at other Saudi universities, it was estimated that additional approximate of 1,000 students may have been reached. A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the use of convenience sampling and the exploratory nature of the study [13]. Instead, the data was collected from as many eligible participants as possible during the study period. The response rate will be reported based on the estimated number of students invited to participate. No specific measures were implemented to prevent duplicate responses; however, participants were instructed to respond only once, and the dataset was reviewed for potential duplications, with no evidence of repeated entries identified.

The data collection instrument was a survey designed to explore the perceptions of medical interns and senior medical students toward clinical teaching. The survey was validated in prior studies conducted in Canada and Iraq [6, 14], and a written permission to use the same instrument was obtained. To adapt the survey for the local context, an additional face and content validity assessments with a small group of medical students and interns were conducted, who reviewed the questionnaire for clarity and relevance. Furthermore, internal consistency of the 7-item confidence scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.907, indicating excellent reliability. The questionnaire was administered in English only. Since the medical curriculum is delivered entirely in English and all participants were senior medical students or interns fluent in English, translation into other languages was not required.

The survey consists of 17 questions, three of the questions are concerned with the demographic data of the respondents, including their gender, university, and level of education. In addition, the survey includes a question regarding the intended residency choice, dichotomous (yes/no) questions regarding teaching or research experience and future plans, and another group of questions exploring the level of confidence in teaching abilities, which can be answered by rating on a 5-point Likert scale (‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’). Lastly, the survey concludes with an open-ended narrative question inviting participants to provide additional feedback on medical education electives or clinical teaching.

Data analysis

Descriptive measures (frequencies and percentages) were used to describe the categorical variables, including demographic characteristics of the participants, responses to confidence in teaching abilities scale, and the answers to questions regarding teaching experiences and future career goals (e.g., presence prior teaching experience). Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between key variables such as interest in medical education electives and interest in clinical teaching. Additional comparisons were made between gender, academic level, and confidence in teaching tasks. For further analysis, participants’ responses to confidence items were grouped into three categories: high confidence (those who answered “agree” or “strongly agree”), neutral (those who answered “neutral”), and low confidence (those who answered “disagree” or “strongly disagree”). Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between confidence levels and other variables such as, interest in medical education electives, and plans to pursue clinical teaching after residency. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (Jon’s Macintosh Project), version 10.0 (SAS Institute In., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical consideration

The study, SP23J/010/02, was conducted after receiving the medical research ethical approval from the institutional review board (IRB) of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) following the declaration of Helsinki standard. Participants’ privacy and confidentiality were ensured, and no identifiers were collected. Consent from the participants was also obtained prior to their participation.

Results

A total of 229 responses were initially collected from senior medical students and medical interns. After excluding responses from universities outside Saudi Arabia, the final sample included 227 respondents from Saudi universities. Based on an estimated total of 1,400 students reached, the approximate response rate was 16.2%. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. About half of the respondents were females (n = 120, 52.9%) and the majority were 6th year senior medical students (n = 144, 63.4%).

As indicated in Tables 1, 146 (64.3%) of respondents did not have any previous teaching experience. On the other hand, the students who experienced teaching before were peer tutors (n = 51, 22.4%), lecturers (n = 43, 18.9%), or assistant teachers (n = 13, 5.8%). Additionally, more than half of the students (n = 133, 58.6%) did not participate in medical education research. Most of the surveyed individuals (n = 173, 76.2%) plan to work in an academic hospital. There were three preferred future residency specialties received the highest selection by the students, which included internal medicine (n = 41, 18.1%), pediatric (n = 26, 11.5%), and surgery or one of its branches (n = 23, 10.1%). Most respondents (n = 159, 70.0%) had an elective period in their final year of medical school, while the remaining 30% (n = 68) did not.

Discussion

As a future physician, particularly during residency, doctors will have responsibilities for teaching their team, interns, and medical students. Formal training to learn tutoring and clinical teaching is increasingly recognized as an essential component of medical education. Student-as-teacher (SAT) programs will offer valuable opportunities for students to gain teaching experience and prepare for these responsibilities [15].

The findings of this study reveal a key paradox: while the majority of respondents (n = 172, 75.8%) expressed interest in pursuing clinical teaching after residency, most (60.8%) were not interested in taking a medical education elective during their senior year. This discrepancy suggests that, although students acknowledge the importance of teaching in their future careers, they may not prioritize developing these skills during undergraduate training. Possible reasons may include a preference for clinically oriented electives that are perceived to strengthen residency applications, limited awareness of the content or value of teaching electives, or a lack of confidence in their readiness to take teaching responsibilities. Time constraints and lack of structured opportunities may also be contributing factors.

Despite this, a notable proportion (n = 89, 39.2%) expressed interest in participating in a medical education elective, indicating potential for targeted development. Given the importance of teaching as a core competency for physicians, medical curricula should offer formal opportunities for students to develop clinical teaching skills through elective programs, workshops, or modules. Raising awareness about the relevance and value of teaching may help bridge the gap between long-term interest and short-term choices.

Several medical schools have recognized this need and developed structured clinical teaching electives. For example, the University of Ottawa established the first Canadian Clinical Teaching Elective (CTE) [6]. Vanderbilt University School of Medicine (VUSM) offers a year-long student SAT elective for post-clerkship medical students [14], while the Uniformed Services University (USU) and Avalon University School of Medicine (AUSOM) also provide medical education electives [16, 17]. At AUSOM, students who completed the elective reported increased interest in academic medicine alongside clinical practice [17].

Similarly, programs like the “Teaching on the Run” (TOR) in Australia have demonstrated positive outcomes in preparing students to plan, deliver, and evaluate teaching activities [18]. Harvard Medical School also offers a 9-month medical education elective course [19]. Internationally, the interest in such training is evident. A Canadian study by Hughes et al. reported that 75% of senior students would participate in a clinical teaching elective if available [6]. Likewise, a study in Iraq by Alsaffar et al. found that over 1,505 students (n= 2562) were interested in medical education electives [20].

The current study found no significant differences in interest in medical education or teaching confidence based on gender or academic level, suggesting that these demographic factors do not influence perceived readiness to teach. However, students who expressed interest in medical education electives or planned to engage in clinical teaching after residency reported significantly higher confidence in teaching-related skills across multiple domains. This indicates that those inclined toward teaching roles also feel more capable of performing them effectively. Identifying and supporting these students early in their training could help nurture future clinical educators. Providing structured teaching experiences may further strengthen their skills and preparedness to contribute meaningfully to medical education both during residency and in their future careers.

Although 64.3% of participants reported no formal prior teaching experience, many still expressed high confidence in specific teaching tasks, such as facilitating small group discussions (73.1%) and giving verbal feedback (64.8%). This apparent gap between experience and perceived competence may be explained by the structure of the problem-based learning curriculum, which emphasizes active participation in group discussions, research tutorials, and peer-led activities. These engagements may enhance communication and teaching-related skills, even if students do not formally recognize them as teaching experiences.

The study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling and voluntary participation may introduce selection bias, as students with a greater interest in medical education might have been more likely to respond. According to this study’s estimates, the response rate was approximately 16.2%, which is low but consistent with response rates typically observed in surveys involving medical students and physicians [21]. This may limit the generalizability of the findings. Nonetheless, despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into medical students’ perceptions of clinical teaching and their interest in medical education electives.

Conclusion

The findings of the study indicated that 39.2% of the surveyed medical students and interns are interested in pursuing a medical education elective. While a larger proportion (75.8%) expressed interest in pursuing clinical teaching after residency, most respondents agreed that a one-month duration would be appropriate for such an elective. Bedside teaching was highlighted as one of the most important skills to be included in the elective curriculum.

Since clinical teaching is an important part of a physician’s future role, students should be supported in learning these skills early. While some teaching opportunities may already exist in Saudi medical school curricula, more can be done to make them more structured, and more relevant to students. This could include offering formal electives for those interested in teaching, helping students understand the long-term value of clinical education, and focusing on practical skills. These should emphasize key areas identified by respondents, including bedside teaching, to ensure the training is aligned with students’ needs and future roles. Such steps can help close the gap between students’ future teaching goals and the choices they make during medical school.
