Penile ulcers in a 35-year-old man
Authors: Jiang-Wei Cheng, Jiu-Hong Li
Published: 2025

A 35-year-old man presented to the dermatology department with penile ulcers. Two weeks previously, the initial ulcer had begun as a papule, progressed to a painful ulcer, and then spread. Soon after onset, he had been treated for genital herpes at a local clinic, with no improvement. We saw several discrete, tender, superficial ulcers (1 to 2 cm in diameter), with indurated and rolled borders, on the penis (Figure 1). Compression of the ulcers elicited serous exudate, and bilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy was present. The patient had no systemic symptoms, and we saw no other cutaneous lesions. He reported no history of sexually transmitted infections but did report multiple episodes of unprotected heterosexual sexual contact 3 to 5 weeks before symptom onset. A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test was positive (titer 1:16). Results from a particle agglutination test and direct fluorescent antibody staining were positive for Treponema pallidum (Figure 2). Bacterial culture, screening for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, screening for HIV antibody and antigen, and serology for herpes simplex 1 and 2 (although the latter was limited in ability to exclude recent infection because of possible window-period negativity) were negative. We diagnosed primary syphilis, prescribed a single intramuscular dose of benzathine penicillin G (2.4 million units), and provided counselling and support for timely partner notification, evaluation, and treatment. The lesions completely resolved within 1 month. Three months after treatment, the RPR titre decreased to 1:2; at 6 months, it was negative and was still negative at 1 year.

Syphilis is a global public health concern. In Canada, 12 135 cases were recorded in 2023 — a 77% increase from 2018.1 Primary syphilis typically presents with a chancre — a solitary, painless, and indurated ulcer with a clean base and rolled edge — that usually develops around 3 weeks after exposure.2 However, chancres can be painful, accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy,2 and are occasionally deep,2,3 particularly among men who have sex with men; sometimes more than 1 chancre will develop.3 Syphilitic chancres are never blistering in appearance.2 The differential diagnosis for genital ulcers includes genital herpes, Behçet disease, chancroid, chancriform pyoderma, Mpox, and lymphogranuloma venereum.
