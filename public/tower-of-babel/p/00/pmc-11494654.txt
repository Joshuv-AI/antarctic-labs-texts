Optimization of Exon-Skipping Riboswitches and Their Applications to Control Mammalian Cell Fate
Authors: Yoko Nomura, Narae Kim, Bochen Zhu, Muhammad Hamzah, Haifeng Zhang, Yohei Yokobayashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mammalian riboswitches that can regulate transgene expression via RNA–small molecule interaction have promising applications in medicine and biotechnology, as they involve no protein factors that can induce immunogenic reactions and are not dependent on specially engineered promoters. However, the lack of cell-permeable and low-toxicity small molecules and cognate aptamers that can be exploited as riboswitches and the modest switching performance of mammalian riboswitches have limited their applications. In this study, we systematically optimized the design of a riboswitch that regulates exon skipping via an RNA aptamer that binds ASP2905. We examined two design strategies to modulate the stability of the aptamer base stem that blocks the 5′ splice site to fine-tune the riboswitch characteristics. Furthermore, an optimized riboswitch was used to generate a mouse embryonic stem cell line that can be chemically induced to differentiate into myogenic cells by activating Myod1 expression and a human embryonic kidney cell line that can be induced to trigger apoptosis by activating BAX expression. The results demonstrate the tight chemical regulation of transgenes in mammalian cells to control their phenotype without exogenous protein factors.

Introduction

Inducible transgene expression by a small molecule in mammalian cells1,2 allows the study of gene functions, efficient production of proteins, and regulation of therapeutic genes in gene/cell therapies. It also allows the control of synthetic genetic circuits constructed in mammalian cells.3,4

Conventional small-molecule-regulated gene switches based on engineered transcription factors such as Tet-ON and Tet-OFF systems5,6 can be immunogenic, require engineered promoters to drive expression of the gene of interest, and leave a large genetic footprint to encode the switch components. On the other hand, riboswitches regulate mammalian gene expression based on a small molecule that specifically interacts with an RNA aptamer.7,8 Riboswitches do not contain exogenous protein factors and typically require genetic footprints much smaller than those of protein-based switches. Unlike bacteria, there are no known natural riboswitches in mammals. However, researchers have devised various mechanisms to regulate gene expression in mammalian cells through RNA–small molecule interaction.7,8 For example, by coupling an RNA aptamer with a self-cleaving ribozyme (aptazyme), one can regulate the stability of an mRNA encoding the target gene by controlling self-cleavage within the 3′ untranslated region (UTR).9−15

Alternatively, an [intron]-[suicide exon]-[intron] cassette can be inserted into the coding region of the target gene. Inclusion of the suicide exon results in the loss of target gene expression due to the presence of a premature stop codon or frame shift. However, an RNA aptamer can be inserted near or within the suicide exon to chemically induce suicide exon skipping to restore the target gene expression. Finke et al.16 and Vogel et al.17 demonstrated this strategy using a tetracycline aptamer18 within the suicide exon to control the accessibility of the 3′ splice site (upstream of the suicide exon) or the 5′ splice site (downstream of the suicide exon), respectively. However, the switches showed relatively high basal expression levels with ON/OFF ratios of up to 5.7 in the presence of 200 μM tetracycline16 or 16.9 in the presence of 50 μM tetracycline.17 More recently, our group implemented a design19 in which an RNA aptamer binds ASP2905,20−22 a cell-permeable drug candidate that inhibits the potassium channel Kv12.2, immediately downstream of a suicide exon, which was originally disclosed in a patent.23 The riboswitches showed excellent characteristics, with low basal expression levels (<1% of the positive control) and high ON/OFF ratios exceeding 100 in the presence of 5 μM ASP2905. However, the riboswitch design was not systematically optimized, the riboswitch was used to regulate only a reporter gene (EGFP), and detailed riboswitch characterization was not performed.

This report provides an in-depth characterization of the exon-skipping riboswitches and improvements in the riboswitch performance for more practical applications. We also showed that a circularly permuted variant of the original AC17-4 aptamer is functional in vitro and in mammalian cells, expanding the practical options for the aptamer applications. Furthermore, we demonstrated plug-and-play control of functional transgenes by fusing them to the riboswitch-controlled EGFP to control the cell fate. Overall, our new designs significantly enhance the applicability of mammalian riboswitches.

Results

Modulation of Aptamer-Base Stem Stability by Introducing Wobble Base Pairs

The mechanism of our exon-skipping riboswitches is illustrated in Figure 1. A suicide exon (Alt-exon) contains a stop codon in frame with the EGFP coding sequence. Alt-exon is inserted into the middle of the truncated intron from the second β-globin intron (IVS2). The AC17-4 aptamer is inserted immediately downstream of Alt-exon such that the base stem (P1) masks the 5′ splicing site (AG|GUAAUGU, the vertical line denotes the splice junction) upon binding ASP2905, resulting in exon skipping. Therefore, stabilization of the P1 stem was expected to favor exon skipping (higher EGFP expression), whereas destabilization was expected to enhance exon inclusion (lower EGFP expression). Consistent with this expectation, as the number of base pairs in P1 increased from 7 (ex169-AC17-4-a7) to 8 (ex169-AC17-4-a8) and to 9 (ex169-AC17-4-a9), the EGFP expression level progressively increased both in the absence and presence (10 μM) of the small molecule (Figure 2A, B).

While ex169-AC17-4-a8 showed an excellent ON/OFF ratio of 132, the ON level was less than 50% of that of the positive control without an intron/riboswitch (Empty). The ON level reached ∼100% in ex169-AC17-4-a9, but the basal expression was also high, resulting in a poor ON/OFF ratio of 3.7 (Figure 2B). We sought to improve the performance of ex169-AC17-4-a9 by modest destabilization of the P1 stem. We designed a series of P1 variants by introducing a G-U wobble base pair within ex169-AC17-4-a9. Although the results varied, all P1 stem destabilization attempts resulted in lower EGFP levels and improved ON/OFF ratios up to 99.2. The introduction of a wobble base pair closer to the aptamer (ex169-AC17-4-a9u8, ex169-AC17-4-a9u9), however, resulted in significantly lower ON levels (Figure 2A,B).

Modulation of Aptamer-Base Stem Stability by Introducing an Antistem Hairpin

As an alternative approach to modulating the P1 stem stability, we introduced a hairpin structure downstream of the aptamer that was designed to compete with the P1 stem structure (Figure 3A). By competing with the P1 stem structure, we sought to expose the 5′ splice site sequence, thereby lowering the basal expression level of ex169-AC17-4-a9, which showed a high basal expression level with an ON/OFF ratio of 3.4 (Figure 3B). A 3-bp competing hairpin (ex-AC17-4-a9hp3) showed a modest reduction in the basal expression level (ON/OFF = 5.3). ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4, ex169-AC17-4-a9hp6, and ex169-AC17-4-a9hp7 showed markedly lower basal expression levels, resulting in ON/OFF ratios from 21.3 to 213. Unexpectedly, ex169-AC17-4-a9hp5 showed a high basal expression level with an ON/OFF ratio of 2.8 (Figure 3B). Nevertheless, varying the size of the competing hairpin allowed for fine-tuning of the riboswitch characteristics.

We next determined the dose dependence of the ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4, which exhibited an ON/OFF ratio exceeding 200 while reaching >60% expression level of the positive control (Empty) in the presence of 10 μM ASP2905 (Figure 3C). The switch showed a dose-dependent response with an EC50 of approximately 2.8 μM.

Circularly Permuted Aptamer Binds ASP2095 In Vitro and Functions in Mammalian Cells

Circular permutation of RNA aptamers24 and ribozymes25 can add flexibility to RNA device design or improve performance. To expand the sequence options of the ASP2905 riboswitch, we studied a circularly permuted variant of the original AC17-4 aptamer. Mutational analysis of AC17-4 in our previous work19 suggested that cpAC17-4 (Figure 4A) should be capable of binding ASP2905. This was confirmed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and cpAC17-4-bound ASP2905 with a KD of 21.2 nM at 25 °C, which is comparable to that of AC17-4 (Figure 4B).

cpAC17-4 was substituted for AC17-4 in the competing hairpin riboswitch designs and tested in HEK293 cells (Figure 4C). Of the four variants tested, ex169-cpAC17-4-a9hp7 showed the highest ON/OFF ratio of 35.3 with a notably high ON level (Figure 4C). We next determined the dose-dependence of ex169-cpAC17-4-a9hp7 (Figure 4C). The switch exhibited a dose-dependent response with an EC50 value of approximately 1.8 μM, with the induced expression level of approximately 80% of the positive control (Figure 4D). Although we did not perform further optimization for cpAC17-4, we confirmed that the aptamer is functional in mammalian cells.

Regulation of Myogenic Differentiation of Mouse Embryonic Stem Cells

To demonstrate the utility of the exon-skipping riboswitches with a low basal expression level and a high ON/OFF ratio, we sought to control the expression of a differentiation factor that triggers stem cell differentiation. Forced expression of a transgene is often a powerful means of controlling stem cell differentiation. However, such transgene expression must be tightly regulated to prevent premature differentiation. Myod1 is a transcription factor that can induce robust differentiation into myogenic cells.26 We sought to use our riboswitch to construct a mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) line in which Myod1 expression can be induced by ASP2905.

To achieve this goal, we fused the Myod1 gene to EGFP containing a riboswitch with an optimized antihairpin stem (ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4) through a P2A “self-cleaving” peptide linker that induces ribosomal skipping to produce two independent proteins (Figure 5A).27 A similar design was reported by Mol et al. for their TetR-regulated splicing switch.28 The fusion gene was placed under the control of the CAG promoter in a piggyBac vector containing a drug-selectable marker. The vector was transfected into mESCs along with a piggyBac transposase expression plasmid to stably integrate the riboswitch-controlled Myod1 expression cassette into the mESC genome. After the selection of stably integrated cells with hygromycin, several clonal cell lines were established.

Two of the clonal cell lines (clones 12 and 13), which responded to the addition of ASP2905 with intense EGFP expression, were used for the following experiments. To maximize the transgene induction level in mESCs, the ASP2905 concentration in the medium was initially varied (Figure S1). The EGFP level in both clones reached a plateau at around 50 μM ASP2905, although the number of living cells also decreased significantly (Figure S1). Therefore, we decided to use 50 μM ASP2905 for the induction of Myod1 expression in these cell lines. The difference in the dose-dependent responses of the riboswitch in different cell types may be due to the differences in cell permeability of the small molecule or the intracellular environment (e.g., concentrations of the splicing factors). The extent of variations of the riboswitch performance among different cell types requires further investigation.

The established mESC clones were stably maintained in the pluripotent stem cell medium, and the protein expression of the pluripotency marker genes OCT4 and NANOG was confirmed (Figures S2 and S3). Therefore, it is likely that the basal expression levels of Myod1 in these cell lines are low enough to maintain the pluripotency.

The cells were then transferred to a differentiation induction medium without 2i (MEK inhibitor and GSK3β inhibitor) and LIF (leukemia inhibitory factor), which are critical for maintenance of the robust naïve pluripotency.29 After the addition of ASP2905 (d0), the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium with or without ASP2905 daily for 4 days (Figure 5). The cells cultured in the presence of ASP2905 showed spindle-like morphologies in a few days accompanied by strong and uniform EGFP expression, whereas the cells cultured in the same medium without ASP2905 maintained a round morphology and no detectable fluorescence (Figures S1 and S2).

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed expression of myogenic differentiation markers (MHC: myosin heavy chain, α-SMA: alpha smooth muscle actin) on day 4 (d 4) in the cells cultured in the presence of ASP2905, whereas visible signals were not observed in the cells cultured without ASP2905 (Figure 5B). The mRNA levels measured by quantitative PCR (qPCR) to detect the muscle cell specific genes showed significant upregulation of Myogenin after 3 days of culture with ASP2905 compared with the cells cultured without ASP2905. However, the Myf5 expression level remained low under all conditions (Figure S3). Overall, these results indicate that the cells treated with ASP2905 have committed to the myogenic cell lineage.

Regulation of Apoptosis in HEK293 Cells

To further demonstrate the superior performance of the riboswitch, we sought to regulate BAX (human Bcl-2-associated X protein), which forms a heterodimer with BCL2 to activate apoptosis. Such a switch can function as a “kill switch” in engineered cells that can be used to eliminate them upon completion of intended tasks or in case of undesired side effects.30,31 It is also a challenging target gene for gene switches because high basal expression of a pro-apoptotic gene would not be tolerated. The same riboswitch with an optimized antihairpin stem (ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4) was used as described for the regulation of Myod1 above.

BAX was inserted into the piggyBac vector as described for Myod1 above. The expression cassette was stably integrated into HEK293 cells, and clonal cell lines were isolated. We selected two clones (clones 1 and 5) for further analysis. One day after passaging the cells in the culture medium, 0, 5, 10, or 20 μM ASP2905 was added to the culture medium (Figure 5C). The cells were analyzed 2 days later (d 2) by staining the live cells with a fluorescent dye and observing EGFP expression. Clone 1 showed significant cell death at 5 μM ASP2905, with very few surviving cells at higher concentrations. Clone 5, on the other hand, showed a clearly reduced number of living cells at 20 μM ASP2905, but some EGFP-expressing cells were still detectable. The different responses to ASP2905 probably reflect the different copy numbers or expression levels of the transgene (BAX) in these cell lines. In fact, we observed that the cell growth of clone 1 was slower than that of clone 5 and the unmodified cells in the absence of ASP2905, suggesting that the basal expression level (and the induced level) is higher (Figure S4). Nevertheless, the results show that our riboswitch can regulate a proapoptotic gene to function as a kill switch.

Discussion

Chemical regulation of gene expression using mammalian riboswitches7,8 has emerged as an attractive technology for potential biomedical applications such as gene and cell therapies and regenerative medicine.32−35 However, there are still several challenges for practical applications of mammalian riboswitches. We have recently reported a new RNA aptamer (AC17-4) and a small molecule (ASP2095) that function in mammalian cells as riboswitches at lower concentrations (<10 μM) compared to the conventional aptamer-small molecule pairs.19 ASP2905 also showed no effect on cell (HEK 293) viability up to 10 μM.19 This was a notable advance for riboswitch technology because such aptamer–small molecule pairs that function in mammalian cells have long been limited, mostly to guanine, theophylline, and tetracycline (and their aptamers). Another advance was the introduction of a riboswitch mechanism that controls exon skipping, which yielded low basal expression and high induced expression levels (high ON/OFF ratios). Vogel et al. controlled exon skipping by placing a tetracycline aptamer in the suicide exon that blocks the 3′ splice site upon ligand binding.17 Alternatively, Finke et al. designed exon-skipping riboswitches with a tetracycline aptamer controlling the accessibility of the 5′ splice site.16 However, the ON/OFF ratios and basal expression levels of these riboswitches were moderate. The new configuration of placing an aptamer immediately downstream of the suicide exon to block the 5′ splice site upon ligand binding, which was originally disclosed in a patent,23 yielded superior riboswitches with ON/OFF ratios exceeding 100 with minimal basal expression in our previous report.19

This study builds on these preliminary observations to further optimize the exon-skipping riboswitches that respond to ASP2905. Recognizing that the stability of the P1 stem that blocks the 5′ splice site could be the main factor that determines the extent of exon skipping, we tested two approaches to modulate stem stability. First, we introduced a G-U wobble base pair in lieu of the A-U or G-C pair in P1 in ex169-AC17-4-a9. ex169-AC17-4-a9 showed high basal and induced expression levels, suggesting that the P1 stem is too strong and enhances exon skipping, even in the absence of ASP2905. Therefore, lowering the P1 stability was expected to improve riboswitch characteristics. Only the 3′ side of the P1 stem was mutated because the 5′ side of the stem constitutes the splice site sequence that affects the intrinsic strength of the splice site. The introduction of a mismatch within P1 was found to be too destructive, yielding no or low levels of EGFP expression with or without ASP2905 (data not shown). Indeed, we observed a lower basal expression in all constructs with one G-U pair within P1. The ON and OFF levels were dependent on the position of the G-U pair. The presence of the G-U pair closer to the aptamer was found to be more detrimental. ex169-AC17-4-a9g7 showed an ON/OFF ratio of 39.2.

Next, we introduced a short hairpin downstream of the P1 stem in ex169-AC17-4-a9 that competes with the P1 stem, thereby lowering P1 stability. These hairpins generally decreased the basal expression levels, resulting in an ON/OFF ratio of up to 213 (ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4). However, the effects were not entirely rationalizable. For example, ex169-AC17-4-a9hp5 showed a higher basal expression level and a lower ON/OFF ratio compared to the parent riboswitch. ex169-AC17-4-a9hp6 showed a slightly higher basal expression level compared to ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4, whose hairpin was designed to be less stable (thus more stable P1 and higher basal expression). The irregularity may be due to inadvertent introduction or elimination of intronic splicing enhancer (ISE) or silencer (ISS) elements or secondary structures that expose/hide such elements.36,37 It could also be the result of cryptic splice sites that were introduced or removed inadvertently. Nevertheless, the introduction of a competing hairpin is another useful strategy for tuning the riboswitch performance. Design and evaluation of additional variants based on both strategies (wobble base pair and antistem hairpin) are necessary to determine the best optimization strategy for exon-skipping riboswitches.

Among the variants evaluated, those with high ON/OFF ratios or low basal expression tend to exhibit somewhat lower induced expression (ON) levels (∼60% of positive control ex169-AC17-4-a9hp4). Applications that require higher ON levels could use riboswitches such as ex169-AC17-4-a9u1, -a9g2, or a9hp6 if their moderate ON/OFF ratios (16.7–30.0) and basal expression levels are acceptable.

By fusing a gene of interest downstream of the riboswitch-regulated EGFP via the ribosome skipping P2A peptide sequence, we can control the expression of a desired gene by ASP2095. This strategy comes with the added benefit of coexpressing a fluorescent reporter gene (EGFP) for facile detection. However, if the coexpression of EGFP is not desired, then the exon-skipping riboswitch module (approximately 600 bp) can be inserted into the coding region of the desired transgene. The potential insertion site is not highly restrictive. In general, AGG|G (the vertical line denotes the position to insert the riboswitch module) based on the native flanking sequence of the β-globin intron is an appropriate site for riboswitch incorporation. Considering the possibility of introducing silent mutations, multiple insertion sites can be found in most genes of interest.

The superior performance of the newly developed riboswitch was exploited to control cell fate by regulating two genes. By regulating Myod1 expression, we could chemically induce myogenic differentiation in mESCs that were stably integrated with the riboswitch-controlled Myod1 expression cassette (Figure 5B). We also demonstrated the chemical induction of apoptosis by regulating hBAX expression in HEK 293 cells (Figure 5C). Both of these examples require tight and robust regulation of transgene expression because high basal expression would not be tolerated. We plan to further improve the riboswitch performance (i.e., lower basal and higher induced expression) to enable more demanding riboswitch applications.

Recently, several new mammalian riboswitch mechanisms that involve alternative splicing have been reported to exhibit an excellent gene regulatory performance. Monteys and colleagues engineered a synthetic [intron]–[exon]–[intron] cassette termed Xon that allows inducible inclusion of the synthetic exon upon administration of LMI070, a small-molecule drug candidate designed to treat spinal muscular atrophy.38 LMI070 functions by stabilizing a splice site complex; thus, it is not a bona fide riboswitch that is based on RNA–small molecule interaction. Therefore, LMI070-based regulation is likely limited to those that involve alternative splicing. Luo et al. combined the regulation of polyA signal sequence embedded in the 5′ UTR coupled with alternative 3′ splice site selection to engineer tetracycline-responsive riboswitches with an excellent ON/OFF ratio (∼900) and EC50 (∼1.1 μM).39 However, the switch design is highly complex, with a polyA signal embedded within three nested tetracycline aptamers that also indirectly control 3′ splice site selection. It remains to be seen how extensible this strategy is to other aptamers and small molecules. On the other hand, the suicide exon strategy adapted in this work has been demonstrated with other aptamers, including tetracycline, guanine, and theophylline.16,17,23 ON/OFF ratios >1000 have been reported in the patent description.23 Therefore, combined with our work, this design strategy seems to be adaptable to different aptamers and their ligands.

In summary, we examined two design strategies to optimize the performance of the exon-skipping riboswitch that we reported previously and confirmed that a circularly permuted AC17-4 aptamer is functional in mammalian cells. An optimized riboswitch was used to regulate functional genes (Myod1 and hBAX) to control the cell fate, demonstrating their potential for biological applications.

Methods

Plasmid Design and Construction

Riboswitch plasmids controlling EGFP (ex-AC17-4-xx) were designed and constructed as reported previously.19 The piggyBac plasmids (Myod1 and BAX) were constructed by cloning the expression cassettes into pPB-PGK-hKLF4 (Addgene #60435). Sequence information is provided in Supporting Information (Table S2 and Appendices S1, S2, and S3).

Riboswitch Assay in HEK293 Cells

HEK293 cells were cultured in complete medium: “DMEM, high glucose, GlutaMAX Supplement” (Gibco 10566016) mixed with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco 10437028) and 100 units/mL of penicillin-streptomycin solution ×100 (Fujifilm 168-23191). An ASP2095 (TargetMol T9070) stock solution (20 mM) was prepared in cell culture grade DMSO (Fujifilm 031-24051). HEK293 cells were grown in a 25 cm2 flask in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 3–4 days to reach approximately 80% confluency. Approximately 20 h prior to transfection, the cells were trypsinized and washed with a complete medium. The cells were diluted with complete medium to 3.5 × 105 cells/mL and 100 μL per well was seeded onto a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The cells were counted before dilution using a Countess II FL (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

A riboswitch-controlled or empty EGFP plasmid (100 ng) and pmCherry-BsaI-Amp plasmid (20 ng, for transfection control) were cotransfected into the cells using 0.3 μL of TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent (Mirus) per well according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Five hours after transfection, the medium was partially removed to leave 50 μL in each well. Next, 50 μL of fresh complete medium containing a 2 × concentration of ASP2905 was added to each well. The final concentrations of ASP2905 ranged from 0 to 10 μM (0.05% DMSO). Forty hours after the addition of ASP2905, the medium in each well was carefully aspirated, and the cells were washed with 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice. Finally, PBS (100 μL) was added to each well before the fluorescence measurement.

Fluorescence intensity was measured at 484 nm excitation and 510 nm emission (5 nm bandwidth) for EGFP and at 587 nm excitation and 610 nm emission (5 nm bandwidth) for mCherry using an Infinite M1000 PRO microplate reader (Tecan). The fluorescence intensity of the untransfected cells was subtracted from the EGFP and mCherry fluorescence values of the transfected cells. Then, the EGFP fluorescence was normalized by mCherry fluorescence to correct for variations in transfection efficiency and cell density (EGFP/mCherry). The EGFP/mCherry values were further normalized by the EGFP/mCherry value of the empty vector (Empty) without ASP2905 on the same plate. Measurements were repeated at least twice to ensure reproducibility.

SPR Measurement of cpAC17-4

The dsDNA templates for transcription of the cpAC17-4 were prepared by primer extension of two partially complementary oligo DNAs using a OneTaq DNA Polymerase (NEB).

(cpAC17-4_Forward: 5′-CCTAATACGACTCACTATAGGTGTCCGCGAATTCACGCTGCTTGTTCGCAAGTGAG-3′; cpAC17-4_Reverse: 5′-TTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTGGTGTCTCTCTCACTTGCGAACAAGCAGCG-3′)

The cpAC17-4 RNA aptamer was synthesized by in vitro transcription using ScriptMAX Thermo T7 Transcription Kit (TOYOBO) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcription products were treated with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C to remove DNA templates. The final products were purified using an RNA Clean & Concentrator-25 Kit (Zymo Research).

The cpAC17-4 RNA aptamer sample was denatured at 80 °C for 3 min in water and cooled on ice. Then, the RNA solutions were diluted with an equal volume of high-salt buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5) to a final concentration of 1.8 μM.

ASP2905 was dissolved in cell culture grade DMSO (Fujifilm) to prepare a 20 mM stock solution. The stock solution was diluted to 640 μM, 320 μM, 160 μM, 80 μM, 40 μM, 20 μM, 10 μM, and 5 μM with SPR running buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.05% Tween-20, 2% DMSO, pH 7.4).

SPR measurements were performed on a Biacore T200 (Cytiva). Immobilization of a 5′ amine-modified (5′ amino modifier C6, IDT) capture DNA (5′ H2N-TTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTT) on a Series S Sensor Chip CM5 (Cytiva) was performed as previously reported.19 The RNA aptamer was injected at a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 30 s, followed by an injection of the SPR running buffer for 20 s to capture the RNA aptamer on the sensor chip. Different concentrations of ASP2905 solutions were injected at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 120 s to monitor association, and then, the SPR running buffer was injected for 180 s to monitor the dissociation kinetics. The sensor surface was regenerated by injecting 25 mM NaOH at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 20 s, followed by an injection of the SPR running buffer for 30 s to remove the RNA aptamer and ASP2905.

Data processing and analysis were performed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software version 1.0 (GE Healthcare). Background signal from the reference flow cell and a blank injection of the running buffer signal were subtracted from the sample signal. The reference data were fit to the 1:1 Langmuir interaction model, and the dissociation constants (KD) were determined from the measured association and dissociation rate constants (KD = koff/kon). The reported kon, koff, and KD values are the averages of three independent experiments.

mESC Culture

mESCs (BRC6: RIKEN BRC, AES0010) were cultured as described.12 The mESCs were maintained in the GKF ESM: Glasgow Minimum Essential Medium (Fujifilm 078-05525), 15% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific 10828-028), 0.3% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific 10437-028), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific 25030-081), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11360-070), 1% MEM nonessential amino acid solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11140-050), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific 21985-023), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15140-122). For pluripotency maintenance, 1 μM MEK inhibitor (PD0325901) (Fujifilm 162-25291), 3 μM GSK3β inhibitor (CHIR99021) (Sigma-Aldrich SML1046), and laboratory-prepared human LIF (1000× dilution: Produced from HEK293T transfected with pCAGGS-LIF (RIKEN BRC, RDB09182). The titer was confirmed in BRC6. mESCs were added to GKF ESM (2iL) as described previously.12 The medium was replaced daily. The mESCs were passaged every 3 days at 5 × 104 cells per well in a 6-well plate (Corning 3516) on mitomycin C-treated SNL feeder cells (Cell Biolabs CBA-316) (at a density of 1 × 106 cells per 10 cm dish or 6-well plate). mESCs were passaged every 3 days by dissociation with TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific 12604-013). For immunofluorescence staining of mESCs under pluripotency maintenance condition shown in Figure S2, mESCs and the clones were passaged on gelatin-coated μ-Plate 24 well black (ibidi 82406) at 2 × 104 cells per well in 2iL-supplemented GKF ESM with ASP2095 (50 μM) or 0.25% DMSO (d0). The medium was replaced with fresh medium daily for cell maintenance.

Generation of mESC Clones with Stably Integrated Riboswitch-Myod1

mESCs that stably maintain the riboswitch-regulated Myod1 expression cassette (Figure 5A) were established using the piggyBac transposon system. One microgram each of the piggyBac vector (riboswitch-Myod1) and pCMV-hyPBase (provided by the Wellcome Sanger Institute, Cambridge, U.K.), which were purified using a Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN 12163), were mixed in 400 μL of Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific 31985-062) in a 1.5 mL tube. Next, 2 μL of PLUS reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific 10964-021) was mixed with the solution and incubated for 5 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of 16 μL of Lipofectamine LTX (Thermo Fisher Scientific 94756). After 25 min incubation at room temperature, the plasmid-containing solution was directly added to the culture of mESCs in 2iL-supplemented GKF ESM without penicillin-streptomycin passaged the day before transfection at 1 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2, the medium was changed to a fresh 2iL-supplemented GKF ESM. The next day, hygromycin selection was initiated by replacing the medium with a fresh medium containing hygromycin (200 μg/mL) (Fujifilm 084-07681). On day 10 (d 10), or after 3 passages post-transfection, the cells were dissociated with TrypLE Express and passaged onto the SNL feeder layer in a 96-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 167008) while diluting the dissociated cells to single cells per well. A few days later, when the cells multiplied, each clone was split into two wells, one of which contained ASP2095 (10 μM). Clones that showed strong EGFP fluorescence in the presence of ASP2905 were selected for further analysis.

Induced Differentiation of mESCs Using ASP2905

mESC clonal cell lines with the stably integrated riboswitch-Myod1 expression cassette were maintained in GKF ESM with 2iL as described above. To induce myogenic differentiation, the cells were dissociated using TrypLE Express and washed three times by centrifugation with GKF ESM. The cells were then passaged on a gelatin-coated μ-Plate 24 well black (ibidi 82406) at 5 × 104 cells per well in GKF ESM with ASP2095 (50 μM) or 0.25% DMSO (d0). The medium was replaced daily until day 4 (Figure 5B).

Immunofluorescence Staining

The adherent cells from d4 in Figures 5B and S2 were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 50-980-495) in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and washed with PBS. PBS containing 5% goat serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific 31872) and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich T8787) was then added to the cells and incubated for 45 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS three times, PBS containing 1% goat serum and primary antibody was added to each well and incubated at 4 °C overnight at following dilutions: 1:100 of Oct3/4 (C-10) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-5279), 1:300 of Anti-Nanog (Abcam, ab80892), 1:400 of Anti-Myosin Heavy Chain, Mouse-Mono (MF20) (MHC) (R&D Systems, MAB4470), and 1:200 of Antialpha smooth muscle Actin antibody (1A4) (α-SMA) (Abcam, ab7817). Next, the primary antibody containing PBS in each well was washed three times with PBS (first wash: rinsed immediately, second/third wash: 10 min). The secondary antibody was prepared in PBS: 1:500 dilution of Alexa 594 goat antimouse IgG (H+L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11032) for Oct3/4, MHC, and α-SMA; 1:500 dilution of Alexa 594 goat antirabbit IgG (H+L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11012) for Nanog. The cells were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark and washed with PBS three times as described above. For nuclei staining, Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, H1399) diluted in PBS (1 μg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. The cells were then washed with PBS three times (first wash: rinsed immediately, second/third wash: 5 min) and observed under a microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2, Nikon). The pictures were taken by a Nikon 7500.

Quantitative PCR

To extract total RNAs from the cells during the myogenic differentiation induction protocol shown in Figure S3, RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN 74104) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNAs were generated using the Hi Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific 4368814) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNAs were then used to estimate the RNA levels by real-time qPCR on StepOnePlus (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using gene-specific primers (Table S1) and a PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A25742). Three replicate reactions were prepared for each gene in one plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 4346907). The data were analyzed using the StepOnePlus software.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

mESCs cultured in various ASP2095 concentrations (Figure S1) were dissociated using TrypLE Express and suspended in PBS for flow cytometry analysis (BD Aria III). Cellular EGFP fluorescence was detected by the Alexa 488 channel.

Generation of HEK293 Clones with Stably Integrated Riboswitch-BAX

HEK293 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin as described above and were passaged using TrypLE Express. The day before transfection, about 1 × 106 cells were seeded into a well in a 6-well plate. The next day, 1 μg of the piggyBac vector (riboswitch-BAX) and 1 μg of pCMV-hyPBase, which were purified using the Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN), were mixed with Opti-MEM (250 μL). TransIT-293 (6 μL) was mixed with the plasmid mixture and incubated for 25 min at room temperature. The solution was added directly to the culture of HEK293 cells, and the medium was changed after 6 h of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The next day, hygromycin (200 ng/mL) treatment was initiated by changing the medium. The cells were maintained for 9 days (3 passages) with hygromycin selection, followed by limited dilution on a 96-well plate to isolate single-cell clones.

Induced Apoptosis of HEK293 Cells with ASP2905

HEK293 cells and the clones dissociated by TrypLE Express were passaged on a 96-well plate at 2 × 104 per well in DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin. A day after the passage, ASP2095 (5, 10, and 20 μM) was added to each well. The same amount of DMSO as 20 μM ASP2095 (0.1%) was added for the negative control. For 2 days of culture after the addition of ASP2095, Cell Viability Assay Kit (Abcam, ab176748) was applied to the cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were then observed using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2, Nikon). The photographs were taken using a Nikon 7500.
