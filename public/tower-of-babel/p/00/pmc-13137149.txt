Seipin-defined ER sites undergo distinct enrichment of factors for the biogenesis of sterol ester rich lipid droplets
Authors: Amit Khatri, Surabhi Yadav, Umair Hashmi, Atul Kumar Dandriyal, Vineet Choudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLipid droplets (LDs) store triacylglycerol (TAG) and sterol ester (SE) and play a crucial role in cellular homeostasis. Seipin (Sei1 in yeast)-complex defines ER sites at which TAG-rich LDs originate. However, the mechanisms of biogenesis of SE-rich LDs are largely unknown. Here, we report that the biogenesis of de novo SE-rich LDs occurs from the Sei1-complex. We found that the deletion of Nem1, Yft2, or Pex30, factors crucial for TAG-rich LD formation, is dispensable for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. Neither Pah1, the mammalian lipin homolog, nor diacylglycerol (DAG) accumulated at Sei1 sites upon the induction of SEs. Moreover, human seipin complemented sei1Δ mutants in organizing ER sites for SE-rich LD formation. Remarkably, Are1 and Are2, yeast enzymes catalyzing SEs, become enriched at Sei1 sites, suggesting localized SE production. Collectively, we propose a model in which SE-rich LDs originate from the Sei1-complex, at which distinct classes of factors and/or lipids get recruited.Subject areas: Lipid, Cell biology

Introduction

Lipid droplets (LDs) are organelles dedicated to the storage of neutral lipids (NLs)/fats such as triacylglycerol (TAG) and sterol (cholesterol in mammals and ergosterol in yeast) esters (SE). LDs play a crucial role in cellular homeostasis, and defects in LD metabolism manifest in various pathological conditions, such as lipodystrophy, obesity, type-2 diabetes, insulin resistance, and cancer.1,2,3 Hence, understanding LD biogenesis and its turnover is central to deciphering the etiology of its associated diseases. LD biogenesis occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where enzymes catalyzing the production of NL localize.4,5 LDs are born de novo upon the accumulation of NLs within the ER bilayer membrane that coalesce into lens-like structures that grow into nascent LDs emerging toward the cytoplasm while still remaining connected with the outer leaflet of the ER.6,7 This ensures the unique architecture of an LD having a phospholipid monolayer that shields the LD core,8 but at the same time it remains interconnected with the ER bilayer via a membrane bridge to facilitate the exchange of lipids and proteins between the two compartments.4,9 LDs mature and become functional within the cytoplasm by acquiring class I and class II LD-resident proteins.10,11,12 However, LDs can also completely detach from the ER during lipophagy or when they are being secreted as milk globules by epithelial cells of the mammary gland.13

Growing evidence supports the model in which seipin (Sei1/Fld1 in yeast), an ER transmembrane protein, defines discrete ER sites that undergo ordered recruitment of LD biogenesis factors for the assembly of LDs.14,15 At these domains, seipin forms a complex with partner proteins such as Ldb16 and Ldo16/45 in yeast and LD assembly factor (LDAF1)/Promethin in humans,2,14,16,17,18,19 and recruits various LD biogenesis factors: the lipin complex (Nem1/Spo7/Pah1 in yeast),20,21 fat-storage-inducing transmembrane (FITM; Yft2 and Scs3 in yeast) protein,22 ACSL3, a key acyltransferase that activates fatty acids,23 Pex30/MCTP2, an ER tubulating protein,24,25 and members of the perilipin (PLIN) family of LD scaffolding proteins (Pet10 in yeast).26,27 Seipin deletion results in aberrant LD morphology, including many tiny LDs and/or a few large LDs, with the accumulation of NLs within the ER membrane.11,14,28,29,30 Mutations in BSCL2-encoded human seipin (hereafter called hSeipin) manifest in a severe form of lipodystrophy, along with upper, lower, and peripheral motor neuronal disorders and encephalopathy, collectively known as seipinopathies.31,32 Recently resolved cryo-electron microscopy structures of human, fly, and yeast seipin revealed that it forms a large membrane-embedded ring-shaped oligomeric structure composed of 11, 12, and 10 subunits, respectively.18,33,34 Seipin oligomerization is crucial for its functioning as a point mutant (A212P) associated with lipodystrophy disease, forming relatively smaller oligomers and failing to complement seipin-associated LD biogenesis defects.11 The structure revealed that the luminal domain of seipin adopts an eight-stranded beta-sandwich fold, characteristic of lipid-binding proteins such as the sterol-binding Niemann Pick C2 protein (NPC2).33,34 Interestingly, mammalian and fly seipin contain a hydrophobic helix (HH) within the luminal domain that binds to NLs.34 However, yeast Sei1 lacks this HH, and its function is provided by Ldb16, a yeast-specific seipin partner protein.18 Lack of either Sei1 or Ldb16 results in a similar droplet morphology defect that can be rescued by the expression of hSeipin.35,36 Recently, it has been demonstrated that the luminal domain region (LR) of Sei1 or hSeipin is dispensable as a truncated seipin, Sei1-ΔLR, in yeast, and the human equivalent hSeipin-ΔLR can functionally complement the defects of yeast sei1Δ mutants by creating ER sites similar to wild-type Sei1 permissive for the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs.37

Although the role of seipin in the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs is well established, more recently the role of seipin in the formation of other classes of NLs, such as SE or retinyl esters (RE) has been investigated. Surprisingly, biogenesis of RE-rich LDs is independent of seipin or TAG.38 However, seipin and its partner Ldb16 are required for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs, and expression of hSeipin complements the LD morphology defect of yeast mutants lacking these proteins.39 It has been shown that carboxyl esters present in NLs interact with the hydroxyl residues in hSeipin or Ldb16, and this interaction is important for the biogenesis of SE/TAG-containing LDs.39 More recently, in an in vitro study using model membranes and cultured cells, it has been demonstrated that the formation of SE-rich LDs occurs at ER sites defined by seipin, and the presence of TAG facilitates its nucleation.40 Strikingly, the absence of seipin in steroidogenic tissues of mice, particularly in the ovary, testis, and adrenal glands, resulted in the reduced accumulation of SE-rich LDs, thus impairing the production of steroid hormone.41 This indicates seipin plays a vital role in SE-rich LD formation. However, it is not known if SE-synthesizing enzymes get recruited at LD biogenesis sites.

In this study, we investigated the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs in vivo. We demonstrate that, similar to TAG-rich LDs, the Sei1-complex defines sites in the ER at which de novo SE-rich LDs begin to form. However, these ER sites recruit a distinct class of factors and/or lipid intermediates during the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs compared to TAG-rich LDs. Interestingly, we found Nem1, a component of the lipin complex, to localize at Sei1 sites. However, the deletion of Nem1 did not perturb the biogenesis of SE-rich LD from the Sei1-complex. We show that neither Pah1, the mammalian lipin homolog, nor diacylglycerol (DAG) becomes enriched at Sei1 sites during assembly of SE-rich LDs. Moreover, factors indispensable for the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs, such as yeast FITM2 protein Yft2 and Pex30, failed to concentrate at Sei1 sites upon the stimulation of SE production. Interestingly, the deletion of Yft2 or Pex30 did not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex. We demonstrate that Are1 and Are2, yeast SE acyltransferases, either endogenous or upon overexpression, become enriched at Sei1 sites, indicating localized SE production and droplet assembly. We show that Ldb16, a Sei1 partner protein, is required for efficient sequestration of SEs in the Sei1-complex. We found that mutating a conserved catalytic site histidine residue to alanine (H547A) in Are1 abolished the SE-synthase activity and resulted in the impaired localization of Are1 at Sei1 sites. We further show that loss of Sei1 is complemented by the expression of hSeipin, suggesting a conserved role of seipin in the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. Taken together, we propose a model in which the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs occurs at the Sei1-complex that selectively recruits factors and/or lipid intermediates distinct from the packaging of TAG-rich LDs. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of LD biogenesis.

Results

Sei1 demarcates sites at which de novo SE-rich LDs begin to form

To investigate the origin of SE-rich LDs, we generated conditional yeast mutants in which SE-rich LDs can be produced de novo. NL production in yeast is under the control of four acyltransferases: Dga1 and Lro1 synthesize TAG, whereas Are1 and Are2 produce SE. Yeast mutants lacking all four enzymes are1Δ are2Δ dga1Δ lro1Δ (called 4ΔKO) are devoid of NL synthesis and hence contain no detectable LDs.5 Therefore, we constructed yeast mutants capable of synthesizing either exclusively TAG-rich LDs, are1Δ are2Δ dga1Δ GAL-LRO1 (called 3Δ GAL-LRO1) or SE-rich LDs, dga1Δ lro1Δ are2Δ GAL-ARE1 (called 3Δ GAL-ARE1)/dga1Δ lro1Δ are1Δ GAL-ARE2 (called 3Δ GAL-ARE2) and expressed Sei1-mCherry from the endogenous locus (Figures 1, S1A, and S1B). These mutants lack NL synthesis when grown in glucose medium. However, the production of TAG/SE begins when cells are shifted to galactose-containing media (Figures S1A and S1B). NL analysis by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) revealed the absence of both TAG and SE in 4ΔKO cells compared to WT cells. However, 3Δ GAL-LRO1 mutants lacked any detectable SEs as reported earlier,4 a finding we confirm (Figures S1C–S1E), and 3Δ GAL-ARE1/3Δ GAL-ARE2 mutants lacked any detectable TAGs (Figures S1C–S1E). Thus, we followed a time course of induction of TAG-rich/SE-rich LD formation. Previously, we have reported that de novo synthesis of TAG results in the formation of LDs at Sei1-defined ER sites,14 a finding we confirm (Figures 1A–1C). At 0 h, Sei1 localized into puncta in 3Δ GAL-LRO1 mutants; however, no LDs were detected as revealed by BODIPY staining, a NL dye (Figure 1A). The induction of LRO1 resulted in BODIPY-stained LDs that colocalized with Sei1-mCherry in a time-dependent manner (Figure 1A; 45 min, 90 min). Quantification revealed ∼75% colocalization between Sei1 and TAG-rich LDs by 90 min (Figure 1B), indicating that TAG-rich LDs originate at Sei1-defined ER sites (Figure 1C).

We next visualized the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs in a time-dependent manner. At time 0 h, Sei1 localized into distinct puncta in both 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 1D) and 3Δ GAL-ARE2 (Figure S1F) mutants, lacking any detectable BODIPY-stained LDs. This indicates that the localization of Sei1 to discrete ER subdomains is independent of SE synthesis or the presence of LDs. However, galactose induction of either ARE1 or ARE2 resulted in the very first BODIPY-marked LDs to form in a time-dependent manner that colocalized with Sei1 (Figures 1D and S1F; 45 min, 90 min). Quantitative analysis revealed ∼65% colocalization of LDs with Sei1 by 90 min in 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 1E), and ∼70% in 3Δ GAL-ARE2 (Figure S1G), indicating that SE-rich LDs originate at Sei1-marked ER sites (Figure 1F). Upon quantification, cells containing TAG-rich LDs had twice the number of droplets formed per cell when compared with SE-rich LDs (Figure S1H). In agreement with this, previously it has been reported that the presence of TAG facilitates the nucleation of SE-rich LDs.40 In the absence of TAG, the number of SE-containing LDs remains low, indicating that TAG helps in the nucleation and growth of LDs. We found that these LDs remained associated with Sei1 foci upon the prolonged induction of SE synthesis until 16 h (Figures S1I and S1J; 16 h). However, both types of LDs, TAG-rich and SE-rich, showed ∼100% colocalization with Erg6-mCherry, an LD marker (Figures S2A–S2C). This indicates that LD coat protein are properly targeted to both TAG-rich and SE-rich LDs. Taken together, this indicates that Sei1 demarcates ER sites for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs, and these droplets remain associated with the ER throughout their life cycle.

Absence of Ldb16, a seipin partner protein, results in the accumulation of SEs within the ER

We wondered how the removal of Ldb16, a Sei1 partner protein, affects the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. Therefore, we created 3Δ ldb16Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants that expressed Sei1-mCherry endogenously. At 0 h, Sei1 showed punctate distribution, suggesting it localizes independently of Ldb16; however, these cells lacked any LDs (Figure S3A). Remarkably, the induction of SE synthesis for 2 h resulted in BODIPY-stained ER with a few isolated puncta that failed to colocalize with Sei1 (Figure S3A). Upon prolonged induction until 5 h, the pattern of ectopic LD formation or the ER staining did not change (Figure S3A). Quantitatively, we found no difference in the number of SE-rich LDs formed in the presence or absence of Ldb16 (Figure S3B). However, these ectopic droplets showed significantly reduced colocalization with Sei1 when cells lacked Ldb16 (Figure S3C). To test if Ldb16 deletion results in an ER stress response upon the stimulation of SE production, we spotted 3Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants having or lacking Ldb16 onto plates containing tunicamycin, a drug known to induce ER stress. Interestingly, unlike ireΔ, a crucial factor that senses unfolded proteins in the ER lumen,42 3Δ ldb16Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants did not show growth sensitivity, suggesting deletion of Ldb16 does not perturb the ER stress response, and these cells can respond to mitigate ER stress similar to wild-type cells (Figure S3D). Taken together, these results indicate that Ldb16 is required for efficient sequestration of SEs at Sei1 sites and to prevent them from being deposited ectopically within the ER membrane.

Deletion of Nem1, a regulator of DAG production, does not impair SE-rich LD formation

We further asked if Sei1 demarcates ER subdomains for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs, and which factors might facilitate this process. Previously, we have shown that Nem1, a regulator of DAG production, is required together with Sei1 to create functional ER sites for the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs.14 Therefore, we determined the localization of Nem1 upon the induction of SE-rich LDs. Hence, Nem1 fused to GFP was expressed on a plasmid driven by the ADH promoter in 3Δ GAL-LRO1 (Figure 2A) and 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 2C) yeast cells expressing Sei1-mCherry endogenously. At 0 h, cells lacked any detectable LDs as evidenced by MDH staining, a NL staining dye (Figures 2A and 2C). However, few Nem1 foci showed colocalization with Sei1 (Figures 2A and 2C). The induction of TAG-rich LD formation upon the expression of LRO1 showed MDH-stained LDs at Sei1 puncta that colocalized with Nem1-GFP foci (Figure 2A; 2-, 5 h). Similarly, the induction of SE production upon the expression of ARE1 resulted in MDH-stained LDs at Sei1-mCherry sites that colocalized with Nem1-GFP puncta (Figure 2C; 2-, 5 h). Quantification revealed ∼60% colocalization between Nem1 and Sei1 by 5 h of induction of either LRO1 or ARE1 (Figure 2B). Moreover, we did not find any significant difference in the number of Nem1 puncta formed per cell when cells were stimulated to produce either TAG-rich or SE-rich LDs until 5 h (Figure 2D). This indicates that Nem1 localizes with the Sei1-complex during the induction of TAG/SE-containing droplets.

We further asked whether the deletion of Nem1 would impair the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex. Therefore, we generated 3Δ nem1Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants expressing Sei1-mCherry from the genomic locus. Surprisingly, the induction of SE synthesis in 3Δ nem1Δ GAL-ARE1 resulted in the formation of BODIPY-stained LDs that colocalized with Sei1 puncta in a time-dependent manner (Figure 2E; 2-, 5-h). Quantitatively, we found no difference in the number of SE-rich LDs formed or the colocalization between LDs and Sei1 puncta in the presence or absence of Nem1 (Figures 2F and 2G). Taken together, these results suggest that the lack of Nem1 does not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex.

PA-phosphatase Pah1 fails to get recruited at Sei1 sites upon the induction of SE synthesis

We wondered whether the localization of Nem1 at Sei1 sites during SE-rich LD formation would activate the lipin complex at these ER sites. Lipin (Pah1 in yeast) is a class of lipid phosphatases catalyzing the dephosphorylation of phosphatidic acid (PA) to DAG.21 Therefore, Lipin/Pah1 controls the cellular flux between either phospholipid synthesis and membrane expansion or synthesis of storage lipid TAG from precursor DAG43 (Figure 3A). Lipin activity is controlled by an ER membrane-embedded phosphatase complex composed of the catalytic subunit Nem1 and the regulatory subunit Spo7.21 DAG produced at these sites is utilized by TAG-synthase Lro1/Dga1. Therefore, the recruitment of Pah1 is crucial for the biogenesis of TAG-rich LD.14 Hence, we compared the localization of Pah1 when cells are generating either TAG/SE-containing LDs. Therefore, Pah1 was fused to GFP and expressed on a plasmid driven by the ADH promoter in 3Δ GAL-LRO1, 3Δ GAL-ARE1, and 3Δ GAL-ARE2 yeast cells expressing Sei1-mCherry endogenously. At 0 h, Pah1 showed diffused localization in the cytoplasm of 3Δ GAL-LRO1 (Figure 3B), 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 3D), and 3Δ GAL-ARE2 cells (Figure S4A). The induction of TAG synthesis resulted in Pah1 puncta that colocalized with Sei1 and MDH-stained LDs (Figure 3B; 2-, 5h). Surprisingly, unlike TAG production, the stimulation of SE synthesis in 3Δ GAL-ARE1/3Δ GAL-ARE2 failed to recruit Pah1 at Sei1 sites, and it remained mostly diffused within the cytoplasm with a few isolated puncta until 5 h; however, cells showed the formation of SE-rich MDH-stained LDs (Figures 3D and S4A; 2-, 5h). Quantitative analyses revealed ∼50% colocalization between Pah1 and Sei1 puncta upon the induction of LRO1; however, it significantly reduced to ∼10% colocalization upon the induction of ARE1 until 5 h (Figure 3C). Similarly, we found a significantly reduced number of Pah1 puncta formed per cell upon the induction of ARE1 in comparison to the induction of LRO1 until 5 h (Figure 3E). Taken together, these results suggest that Pah1 gets recruited to Sei1 sites when cells are producing TAG-rich LDs (Figure 3B), perhaps to generate DAG locally at these ER sites, a substrate for TAG-synthase. However, during the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs, Pah1 failed to concentrate at Sei1 sites (Figure 3D), indicating the likely dispensable role of Pah1 in the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs.

Biogenesis of SE-rich LDs does not drive elevated DAG levels at Sei1 sites

We further assessed whether the recruitment of Pah1 at Sei1 sites results in the localized production of DAG when cells are engaged in de novo LD biogenesis. Using a GFP-tagged ER-DAG sensor, we had previously demonstrated that DAG accumulates at LD biogenesis sites.14,44 Thus, we expressed ER-DAG sensor-GFP on a plasmid in 3Δ GAL-LRO1 (Figure 4A), 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 4C), and 3Δ GAL-ARE2 (Figure S4B) cells that co-expressed Sei1-mCherry endogenously. Upon the induction of TAG synthesis in 3Δ GAL-LRO1 for 2-5h, the ER-DAG sensor-GFP displayed punctate distribution that colocalized with Sei1 foci or MDH-stained LDs (Figure 4A). Interestingly, the ER-DAG sensor-GFP failed to concentrate into puncta when cells were stimulated to produce SE-rich LDs in either 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 4C; 2-, 5h) or 3Δ GAL-ARE2 (Figure S4B; 2-, 5h). Quantitative analyses revealed ∼70% colocalization between ER-DAG sensor-GFP and Sei1 puncta upon the induction of LRO1; however, significantly reduced colocalization upon the induction of ARE1 until 5 h (Figure 4B). We found a significantly reduced number of ER-DAG sensor-GFP puncta formed per cell upon the induction of ARE1 in comparison to the induction of LRO1 until 5 h (Figure 4D). This suggests that levels of DAG, a substrate for TAG-synthase, are elevated at Sei1 sites during the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs (Figure 4E). However, DAG levels remain markedly low at Sei1 sites when cells produced SE-rich LDs (Figure 4F). This is consistent with the failure of Pah1 to get recruited at Sei1 sites upon the induction of SE-synthase (Figure 3D) in comparison to TAG-synthase (Figure 3B).

LD emergence factor Yft2 fails to get recruited upon the induction of SE-rich LDs

We investigated if the yeast FITM2 protein, Yft2, becomes enriched at Sei1 sites during the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. We have previously demonstrated that Yft2 gets recruited at Sei1-defined ER sites during the stimulation of TAG-rich LD formation.14,44 FITM2 proteins play an important role in the proper emergence of LDs from the ER membrane toward the cytoplasm; the lack of which results in LDs being embedded within the ER.6 Thus, 3Δ GAL-LRO1 and 3Δ GAL-ARE1/3Δ GAL-ARE2 cells expressing Sei1-mCherry and co-expressing Yft2-superfold-GFP were induced to produce either TAG- or SE-rich LDs, respectively, and stained with MDH dye. At 0 h, cells lacked any LDs, and Yft2 showed uniform ER localization (Figures 5A, 5C, and S5A). However, upon the induction of TAG synthesis in 3Δ GAL-LRO1, Yft2 enriched at Sei1 foci that colocalized with MDH-stained LDs (Figure 5A; 2 h). This indicates that locally enriched DAG at Sei1 sites during TAG-rich LD formation likely drives Yft2 at these ER sites, consistent with previous reports that FITM2 proteins bind to DAG/TAG and might regulate DAG levels at LD biogenesis sites.2,44,45 Quantitative analysis revealed ∼75% of Yft2-GFP puncta colocalized with MDH-stained TAG-rich LDs upon LRO1 induction (Figure 5B). However, the induction of ARE1 resulted in the uniform ER distribution of Yft2-GFP in most of the cells, with a few cells showing isolated puncta that colocalized with Sei1, but these sites lacked any MDH-stained LDs (Figures 5B and 5C; 2-, 5h). We found a ∼2-fold reduction in the puncta formation of Yft2 upon the induction of SE production in 3Δ GAL-ARE1 cells, with the majority of cells displaying uniform ER localization (Figures 5C and 5D; 2h). A similar pattern of Yft2 localization was found upon the induction of ARE2 (Figure S5A; 2-, 5h). Further, we tested if the Yft2 function is dispensable in the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. Thus, we generated 3Δ yft2Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants expressing Sei1-mCherry from the genomic locus. Intriguingly, the induction of SE synthesis in 3Δ yft2Δ GAL-ARE1 resulted in the formation of BODIPY-stained LDs that colocalized with Sei1 puncta in a time-dependent manner (Figure 5E; 2-, 5-h). Quantitatively, we found no difference in the number of SE-rich LDs formed or the colocalization between LDs and Sei1 puncta in the presence or absence of Yft2 by 5 h of SE production (Figures 5F and 5G). Taken together, these results suggest that the lack of Yft2 does not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex.

ER shaping protein Pex30 does not localize at Sei1 sites during SE-rich LD formation

We next determined whether Pex30, an ER shaping protein, would concentrate at LD biogenesis sites upon the stimulation of SE production. It has been shown previously that Pex30 colocalizes with Sei1-containing ER subdomains, and in cells missing Sei1, Pex30 mislocalizes to a single punctum.24,25 Pex30 localizes to the tubular ER region and plays an important role in the ordered assembly of factors to establish functional ER sites for the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs.14 Hence, we expressed Pex30-GFP on a plasmid under native promoter in 3Δ GAL-LRO1, 3Δ GAL-ARE1 and 3Δ GAL-ARE2 cells expressing Sei1-mCherry genomically. Upon the induction of 3Δ GAL-LRO1 to generate TAG-rich LDs, Pex30 colocalized with Sei1 and MDH labeled LDs (Figure 6A; 2-, 5 h). In contrast, Pex30 displayed faint and diffused ER localization in most of the cells with few puncta upon the induction of de novo SE production until 5 h in either 3Δ GAL-ARE1 (Figure 6C; 2-, 5 h) or 3Δ GAL-ARE2 (Figure S5B). Interestingly, few cells showed Pex30 puncta that colocalized with Sei1; however, these sites lacked MDH-stained LDs (Figure 6C; 2-, 5 h). Quantification revealed significantly reduced colocalization of Pex30 foci with Sei1 upon the expression of ARE1 in comparison to LRO1 (Figure 6B), together with a significantly reduced number of Pex30 foci formed per cell upon the induction of ARE1 with respect to LRO1 (Figure 6D). Moreover, the induction of LRO1 until 5 h resulted in ∼40% colocalization of Pex30 foci with MDH-stained LDs; however, this dropped significantly to ∼5% when cells expressed ARE1 (Figure 6E). This suggests that Pex30 is distinctly recruited at Sei1 sites when cells are producing either TAG- or SE-rich LDs. Therefore, we tested whether the Pex30 function is dispensable in the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs. Thus, we generated 3Δ pex30Δ GAL-ARE1 mutants expressing Sei1-mCherry from the endogenous locus. Remarkably, the induction of SE synthesis resulted in the formation of BODIPY-stained LDs at Sei1 sites in a time-dependent manner (Figure 6F; 2-, 5-h). Quantitatively, we found no difference in the number of SE-rich LDs formed or the colocalization between LDs and Sei1 puncta in the presence or absence of Pex30 (Figures 6G and 6H). Taken together, these results suggest that the deletion of Pex30 does not abrogate biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex.

Are1 and Are2 become enriched at LD biogenesis sites upon the stimulation of SE-rich LD formation

We next asked if SE-rich LDs are born at Sei1-marked ER sites, then would the seipin scaffold recruit SE acyltransferases, Are1 or Are2 for localized SE synthesis at these ER sites, and hence facilitate droplet assembly. Are1 and Are2 are polytopic ER membrane proteins. Previously, we have shown that TAG-synthase Lro1/Dga1 gets recruited at Sei1 sites for localized TAG synthesis and droplet biogenesis, and thus Sei1 acts to sequester TAG at these ER subdomains.14 Consistent with this, molecular dynamic simulations (MDS) and mutation analyses have shown that the hydrophobic helices (HHs) within the seipin ring complex can sequester nanoscale TAG19,46 and SE39 molecules. Interestingly, the occurrence of NL nucleation remained low in seipin deletion, thus suggesting direct interaction of various NLs with seipin.39 Therefore, to visualize Are1 and Are2 localization, we generated endogenous GFP fusion at the N-terminus of Are1 and Are2 in lro1Δ dga1Δ are2Δ GFP-ARE1 (3Δ GFP-ARE1)/lro1Δ dga1Δ are1Δ GFP-ARE2 (3Δ GFP-ARE2) mutants co-expressing Sei1-mCherry endogenously. First, we determined if the endogenous GFP fusion with ARE1 and ARE2 is functional. Hence, we performed steady-state NL analysis by TLC. TLC analysis revealed that 3Δ GFP-ARE1 and 3Δ GFP-ARE2 mutants produced SEs to the same extent as untagged Are1/Are2 (Figures 7A and 7B). This indicates that both genomic fusions, GFP-ARE1 and GFP-ARE2, are functional in vivo. Later, we visualized the localization of GFP-ARE1 and GFP-ARE2. Both Are1 and Are2 showed enrichment in puncta at Sei1 foci that colocalized with MDH-stained SE-rich LDs (Figures 7C and 7D).

Mutating the catalytic histidine residue impairs the recruitment of Are1 at Sei1 sites

To investigate the possible mechanism of Are1 recruitment at Sei1 sites, we performed multiple sequence alignment with human sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1) to identify putative catalytic residues in Are1. It has been previously shown that hSOAT1 contains a critical catalytic residue, histidine 460 (H460),47 a site targeted by inhibitors such as CI-976.48 We identified H547 of yeast Are1 that aligned with H460 of hSOAT1 (Figure S6). Therefore, we mutated the conserved histidine to alanine (H547A) to generate a point mutant of Are1 and expressed it as a GFP fusion on a plasmid driven by the ADH promoter in 4ΔKO Sei1-mCherry mutants. GFP-Are1H547A showed uniform ER distribution but failed to get recruited at Sei1-marked ER sites (Figure 7I). MDH staining revealed the lack of presence of any detectable LDs (Figure 7I), indicating abolished catalytic activity. Consistent with this, TLC analysis showed the absence of SEs in the presence of Are1H547A (Figures 7J and 7K). Taken together, these results indicate that the localization of Are1 at the Sei1 site requires functional catalytic residue H547, the mutation of which impairs the recruitment and SE synthase activity of Are1.

Ldb16 plays a crucial role in the efficient sequestration of SEs at Sei1-complex

We further tested if the deletion of Ldb16 perturbs the recruitment of GFP-ARE1/GFP-ARE2 at the Sei1-complex. Hence, we expressed pGFP-ARE1 and pGFP-ARE2 in 4ΔKO ldb16Δ mutants co-expressing Sei1-mCherry genomically. Interestingly, both pGFP-ARE1 and pGFP-ARE2 showed punctate ER distribution that colocalized with Sei1 foci (Figure 8A). Time-lapse imaging revealed a stable association of both GFP-ARE1 and GFP-ARE2 puncta with Sei1 foci (Videos S3 and S4). However, most of these foci lacked any detectable LD, with a few that occasionally colocalized. MDH staining revealed diffused ER staining, indicating ectopic SE deposition within the ER (Figure 8A). Quantitative analyses revealed a reduced number of LDs formed per cell in the absence of Ldb16 in cells containing GFP-ARE2 (Figure 8B). Similarly, we found that the colocalization of Sei1 foci with LDs was significantly reduced in the absence of Ldb16, albeit to a greater extent in GFP-ARE2 containing cells compared with GFP-ARE1 (Figure 8C). This indicates that the recruitment of GFP-ARE1 and GFP-ARE2 at the Sei1-complex is independent of the presence of Ldb16. However, the absence of Ldb16 results in failure of SE-rich LDs to be formed at Sei1 sites, thereby resulting in ectopic SE deposition within the ER.

Human seipin complements the lack of yeast seipin in restoring the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs

We next determined if the process of SE-rich LD formation is functionally conserved. Therefore, we asked if hSeipin would complement the loss of yeast Sei1 in demarcating ER subdomains for the biogenesis of TAG/SE-rich LD formation. Hence, hSeipin-mCherry was expressed on a plasmid driven by the ADH promoter in 3Δ sei1Δ GAL-LRO1 and 3Δ sei1Δ GAL-ARE1 cells. Before galactose induction at time 0 h, hSeipin localized into puncta similar to yeast Sei1; however, LDs were absent, indicating hSeipin localizes independently of the presence of LDs (Figures 9A and 9B). By 2 h of either TAG or SE synthesis, BODIPY-stained LDs showed colocalization with hSeipin foci and remained associated until 5 h of NL synthesis (Figures 9A and 9B). Quantitative analysis revealed ∼80% colocalization for TAG-rich LDs and ∼70% colocalization for SE-rich LDs with hSeipin puncta until the 5 h time point (Figures 9C and 9E). To determine if hSeipin rescued the sei1Δ mutant to the same extent as yeast Sei1, we compared the number of TAG/SE-containing LDs formed when yeast mutants expressed either hSeipin or WT Sei1. Quantitatively, LDs filled with either TAG/SE were formed to the same extent when cells expressed either Sei1 or hSeipin until 5 h (Figures 9D and 9F). Moreover, using mean fluorescence intensity, we found no significant difference in the expression of hSeipin-mCherry in comparison with Sei1-mCherry in 3Δ sei1Δ GAL-ARE1 cells until 2 h of induction (Figure 9G). This suggests that hSeipin can functionally restore yeast Sei1 function by establishing functional ER sites permissive for the biogenesis of both TAG-rich and SE-rich LDs.

Discussion

Growing evidence suggests that seipin establishes functional ER sites of LD biogenesis. Although the role of seipin oligomeric complex in the assembly of TAG-rich LDs has been well studied, whether seipin orchestrates the biogenesis of other classes of NLs, such as SEs remains largely unknown. It has been reported that loss of yeast seipin, Sei1 or its partner Ldb16 results in defects in SE-rich LDs at non permissive temperature, 37°C.39 Similarly, seipin-independent assembly of RE-rich LDs has also been proposed.38 Under normal growth conditions, LDs are amorphous, consisting of a mixture of TAG and SE. Recent cryoEM studies have revealed that LDs undergo phase transition when TAG:SE ratio decreases, where liquid crystalline lattice is formed in LDs under acute glucose restriction,49 mitotic arrest and starvation,50 or when the cell is supplemented with cholesterol.51 This results in LD proteome remodeling affecting the cellular metabolism and physiology.49

Whether factors that are important during assembly of TAG-rich LDs at Sei1 sites are also essential for the assembly of SE-rich droplets has not been investigated. In this study, we demonstrate that the Sei1-complex defines ER sites for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs similar to TAG-rich LDs. Hence, seipin creates a universal platform for the biogenesis of TAG/SE-containing LDs. However, these ER sites recruit distinct classes of factors during the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs compared to TAG-rich LDs. We provide evidence that several key factors that localize at Sei1 sites during the biogenesis of TAG-rich LDs fail to do so when cells are engaged in the production and assembly of SE-rich LDs. Surprisingly, we found that Nem1, a lipin complex protein, localizes with Sei1; however, the deletion of Nem1 did not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex (Figure 2). Unlike the biogenesis of TAG-containing droplets, Pah1 failed to enrich upon the induction of SE formation (Figure 3). As a result, DAG levels remained markedly low as revealed by ER-DAG-sensor-GFP, a probe to determine the distribution of DAG (Figure 4). Similarly, yeast FITM2 protein, Yft2 and membrane shaping protein Pex30, factors serving an indispensable role in TAG-rich LD formation, were excluded from Sei1 sites during assembly of SE-rich LDs (Figures 5 and 6). However, the deletion of either Yft2 or Pex30 did not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex. This indicates that the previously observed phenotype of indistinguishable localization of Nem1, Yft2, and Pex30 when cells make either TAG-rich or SE-rich LDs is likely indirect.39 It is plausible that the localization of some of these factors at Sei1 sites must be driven by lipid-mediated cues or interaction with other factors; however, the fact that their deletion does not impair the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex indicates their dispensable role. Studies have shown that mammalian FITM2 protein interacts with membrane-shaping proteins Rtn4 and REEP5, and the interaction is crucial for the function of FITM2.52 The interaction of Yft2 with Pex30 in cells having TAG-rich LDs or SE-rich LDs awaits future investigation.

The fact that the absence of Ldb16 resulted in SEs being deposited within the ER membrane suggests that Ldb16, together with Sei1, plays a crucial role in the sequestration of SEs to facilitate LD formation. We also demonstrate that GFP-tagged Are1 and Are2, ER proteins catalyzing the formation of SEs are recruited at Sei1 sites (Figure 7), independently of the presence of Ldb16 (Figure 8), suggesting localized SE production and droplet assembly (Figure 7). Moreover, mutating histidine 547, a conserved catalytic site residue in Are1, abolishes SE-synthase activity and impairs the localization of Are1 at Sei1 sites (Figure 7). We further show that the deletion of Ldb16 results in failure of SE-rich LDs to be formed at Sei1 sites, thereby resulting in ectopic SE deposition within the ER (Figures 8 and S3). We demonstrate that hSeipin complements sei1Δ mutants, suggesting a conserved role of seipin in the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs (Figure 9). We propose that Sei1-complex recruits factors and/or lipids distinctly when cells are engaged in the production of either TAG- or SE-rich LDs (Figure 9H).

How does Sei1-complex at LD biogenesis sites distinctly recruit factors? We do not yet know the complete mechanisms for this, which should be pursued in future studies. However, our data suggest that DAG, a substrate for TAG-rich LD formation, does not accumulate at Sei1 sites when cells are actively producing SE-rich LDs (Figure 4). Moreover, failure of Lipin/Pah1 to get recruited at Sei1 sites suggests that the lipin complex is inactivated during the biogenesis of SE-rich LD; thereby, the flux of lipid synthesis shifts toward phospholipid synthesis and membrane biogenesis rather than storage lipid or TAG synthesis. In that case, PA would not accumulate and would be channeled toward phospholipid synthesis.21,53 This indicates a crucial branch point of lipid synthesis regulated by the lipin complex. Consistent with this, during SE-rich LD formation, perhaps relatively low PA levels are maintained at Sei1 sites, as most of it would be actively consumed for phospholipid synthesis. Recently, it has been reported that the distribution of Pex30 in the ER is driven by the availability of PA at LD biogenesis sites.54 Consistent with this, we found Pex30 failed to localize at Sei1 sites when SE-rich LDs are induced, likely due to low PA levels at these ER sites (Figure 6). Pex30 has a reticulon homology domain, which is crucial for inducing membrane curvature, and it has been shown that the presence of free TAG is energetically unfavorable in curved membranes or ER tubules, leading to outflow of TAG into ER sheets, a process controlled by the presence of Seipin in ER tubules to prevent ectopic TAG nucleation.55 However, free SE is favored in tubules, which, in high concentrations, inhibits the membrane curvature-induced nucleation of TAG-rich LDs.55 Therefore, the deletion of Pex30 did not impair the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs, suggesting a dispensable role of Pex30 in SE-rich LD formation (Figure 6). Previously we have reported that Yft2 co-enriches with DAG at LD biogenesis sites,14,44 hence the lack of presence of DAG at Sei1 sites during SE-rich LD formation, might result in failure of Yft2 to get recruited at Sei1 sites (Figure 5). However, the deletion of Yft2 did not abrogate the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs from the Sei1-complex, suggesting a dispensable role of Yft2 in SE-rich LD formation (Figure 5). Since intrinsic curvature of ER phospholipids regulates architecture of LDs,44 it is plausible that other tubule forming proteins such as Rtn4, and REEP5 implicated in LD biogenesis may regulate the differential recruitment of factors at LD biogenesis sites.52 Taken together, this indicates that the availability of PA and DAG at LD biogenesis sites, together with membrane curvature, drives the selective recruitment of factors during the biogenesis of TAG-rich or SE-rich LDs.

The fact that yeast SE acyltransferases, Are1 and Are2, show co-enrichment with Sei1 during the stimulation of SE production (Figure 7) indicates that SE synthesis occurs locally, a likely prerequisite for efficient sequestration of SEs and droplet assembly. Recently, it has been shown that mammalian SOAT1 is distributed throughout the ER network, and it concentrates in the juxtanuclear region upon stimulation by pro-inflammatory agents such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) for SE-rich nuclear LD formation.56 It remains to be determined if mammalian SE-acyltransferases SOAT1/SOAT2 would also localize at seipin sites for the synthesis of SE-rich LDs. How does the Sei1-complex concentrate SEs? Mammalian and fly Seipin contain HH within the luminal domain that binds to NLs; however, yeast Sei1 lacks HH.18 Recently, it has been reported that conserved hydroxyl-containing residues of mammalian seipin and equivalent residues in yeast Ldb16, Sei1 partner protein, concentrate NLs likely via interactions between the H-bonds and carboxyl ester group.39 In agreement with this, we find that the absence of Ldb16 from the yeast Sei1-complex in ldb16Δ mutants resulted in a lack of SEs being sequestered, and they accumulated in the ER membrane (Figure S2). Hence, Sei1and Ldb16 are functionally coupled to facilitate SE-rich LD biogenesis. Recently, we have demonstrated that the LR of Sei1 or hSeipin is dispensable, as a truncated Sei1 devoid of LR, Sei1-ΔLR, in yeast, and the human equivalent hSeipin-ΔLR can functionally complement the defects associated with yeast sei1Δ mutants by creating functional LD biogenesis sites similar to wild-type Sei1.37 Moreover, Sei1-ΔLR can rescue the LD morphology defect and terbinafine drug sensitivity of sei1Δ ldb16Δ double mutants such as hSeipin.37 Future studies should focus on whether Sei1-ΔLR marked ER sites would also act as platforms for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs.

Previous study using model bio-mimicking membranes and cultured cells demonstrated that the formation of SE-containing LDs occurs at seipin-enriched ER sites when a critical ratio of SE to phospholipids is attained in membranes, and this nucleation is facilitated by the presence of TAG.40 Remarkably, seipin deletion in steroidogenic tissues in mice, in particular, the testis, ovary, and adrenal glands that specialize in storing SE-rich LDs, resulted in a significant decrease in LD formation and reduced accumulation of SE-rich LDs, thereby impairing steroid hormone synthesis.41 Exogenous addition of lipoprotein cholesterol did not reverse the defect of hormone production in seipin-deleted mice.41 This indicates the important role of seipin in SE-rich LD formation. It remains to be determined how SE packaging is coordinated with TAG filling into LDs.

In conclusion, we propose a model in which the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs occurs at the Sei1-complex that selectively recruits factors and/or lipid intermediates distinctly from the assembly of TAG-rich LDs. These intriguing findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of LD biogenesis. Future studies should investigate what factors are enriched at Sei1 sites during the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs and how seipin might function in the assembly of diverse classes of NLs.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides insight into the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs in budding yeast, further studies employing cultured mammalian cells would be valuable for broadening the perspective and demonstrating the conserved nature of SE-rich droplet formation. Moreover, factors that are critical for the biogenesis of SE-rich LDs remain to be determined. Therefore, a comprehensive mass spectrometry-based approach will unravel the role of unidentified proteins that get recruited at Sei1-complex upon the stimulation of SE-rich LD formation. Another limitation of the study is the use of a fluorescent-based imaging approach to demarcate the origin of SE-rich droplets. Further studies should focus on ultrastructural analysis using electron microscopy (EM) and immuno-EM to define ER subdomains from which SE-rich droplets emanate and to determine the architecture of ER-LD contact sites when cells contain LDs exclusively filled with SEs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Yeast strains

The yeast strains used in this study are derivatives of the Saccharomyces cerevisiae wild-type strains BY4741 (MATa his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 met15Δ0 ura3Δ0) and BY4742 (MATα his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 lys2Δ0 ura3Δ0)58 and are described in the Table S1.

Cell lines

HEK293T cells were a kind gift from Dr. Kalpana Luthra, Head, Department of Biochemistry, AIIMS, New Delhi. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic and were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were used for RNA isolation as described in method details. HEK293T is not a commonly misidentified cell line as suggested by the International Cell Line Authentication Committee (https://iclac.org/databases/cross-contaminations/). Therefore, it has not been authenticated. The cell line was not tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Generation of yeast strains and plasmids

Yeast knockout strains were generated by PCR-based targeted homologous recombination by replacing the ORF of the gene of interest with a gene knock-out cassette followed by a marker rescue strategy.59,60 Chromosomally C-terminal tagged Sei1-mCherry and Erg6-mCherry fusion protein was constructed using PCR-based homologous recombination.59,61 N-terminal endogenous tagging of GFP-ARE1 and GFP-ARE2 were generated using protocol as previously described.62 Plasmid constructs pNem1-GFP and pPah1-GFP used in the study were PCR amplified from S. cerevisiae genomic DNA and inserted into pRS415 with C-terminus GFP. PEX30-GFP under endogenous promoter was cloned in SacI and HindIII digested plasmid pRS415 with promoter region (450bp upstream of PEX30 start codon) amplified from yeast genomic DNA. Plasmids pGFP-ARE1 and pGFP-ARE2 were generated by PCR amplification of ARE1 and ARE2 from genomic DNA of yeast strain, and cloned into plasmid pGREG575-GFP by replacing GAL1 promoter to ADH using Sac1 and Spe1. PCR mediated site directed mutagenesis was performed to generate ARE1H547A and cloned into PGREG575-GFP under ADH promoter. The plasmids encoding Yft2-sf-GFP, and the ER-DAG sensor (pADH1-ER-DAG sensor) have been described previously.14 For cloning human Seipin into pADH1-hSeipin-mCherry, mRNA was isolated from HEK293T cells using TRIzol reagent as per manufacturer’s information (T9424; Sigma-Aldrich). cDNA was synthesised using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1621; Thermo Fisher Scientific). 50–100 ng of cDNA was used as a template to amplify isoform 2 of hSeipin having BamH1 and Xho1 restriction sites in the forward and reverse primers respectively and cloned in lab generated plasmid pADH-mCherry-575.63 All constructs were verified by sequencing.

Media and growth conditions

WT and mutant yeast cells were grown at 30°C in rich YPD medium (1% Bacto yeast extract, 2% Bacto Peptone, 2% glucose) unless otherwise indicated. Cells bearing plasmids were grown in synthetic complete (SC) media containing 6.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base without amino acids (Sigma Aldrich), an amino acid mix (Sigma Aldrich), containing either 2% glucose, galactose or raffinose. For induction experiments cells were grown in SC-raffinose media overnight at 30°C 200 rpm and transferred to SC-galactose. Samples were aliquoted for imaging at indicated time points.

Fluorescence microscopy

Live cell imaging was performed using inverted fluorescence microscope, Zeiss Axio Observer 7 (Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a sCMOS pco.edge 4.2 camera and Nikon Eclipse Ti-2E inverted widefield fluorescence microscope equipped with Prime BSI Scientific sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics). Images were captured using a plan apochromatic 100×, 1.42 NA oil immersion objective and a DAPI, FITC or TXRED filter set, taken as single slice at approximately mid-plain (unless indicted in the figure legends), deconvolved and processed using Zen software (version 3.8) at regularised inverse filter settings or Nikon NIS elements (version 5.41.00) at Richardson Lucy deconvolution settings. Images being directly compared were obtained using identical microscope settings. z stack images (Figures 1 and 9) were acquired by taking 3 Z sections with a step size of 0.45 μm, 3D deconvolved using Richardson Lucy deconvolution settings (Nikon NIS elements version 5.41.00) and maximum intensity projected using Fiji (NIH).57 Time lapse images were acquired for 15 s with 3 s interval having 500 ms exposure for TX-RED channel, 200 ms exposure for FITC channel, and deconvolved. Brightness and contrast were adjusted using Adobe Photoshop 2024 (Adobe Systems). Colocalization in acquired images was scored manually, values were recorded in Microsoft Excel and analyzed in Prism (Version 10.2.1) (GraphPad Software) (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Flow cytometric analysis of mean fluorescence intensity

Cells of the genotype 3Δ sei1Δ GAL-ARE1 + pADH-Sei1-mCherry and 3Δ sei1Δ GAL-ARE1 + pADH-mCherry-hSeipin were cultured in SC-LEU-Raffinose media and induced using SC-LEU-Galactose media for 2h to stimulate LD production. Aliquots were drawn at both the timepoints (0h-raffinose, 2h-galactose), washed with 1× PBS, finally resuspended in 750 μL 1× PBS and transferred in flow cytometry tubes (BD, USA). Data acquisition was performed via flow cytometry using BD LSRFortessa with BD FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences, USA). 5000 cells per sample were analyzed and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) calculation was performed using FlowJo (Version 10, BD Biosciences, USA).

Staining to visualize lipid droplets in yeast

To visualize LDs in yeast, cells were grown in YPD/SC media to mid-log phase and stained with either 0.5 μg/mL BODIPY 493/503 (D3922; Invitrogen) or 0.1 mM MDH AutoDOT (SM1000a; Abcepta) and incubated for 5 min at RT. Cells were collected at 2000 rpm for 2 min and washed twice with 1× PBS. Cells were resuspended in 50 μL of 1× PBS and visualised using FITC (for BODIPY) and DAPI (for MDH) excitation filters of Nikon Eclipse Ti-2E inverted widefield fluorescence microscope or Zeiss Axio Observer 7.

Colocalization analysis

Acquired images were analyzed for colocalization using Pearson’s correlation coefficient using the BIOP JACoP plugin in ImageJ/Fiji (Figures 7G and 7H). Briefly, merged images were fed into Fiji and BIOP JACoP plugin was used at Costes Auto-Threshold for channels 1, 2 and 3. Pearson’s coefficient was recorded for ≥30 cells, where obtained values indicate the association between fluorescence signals (0 = no correlation, 1 = max correlation). Colocalization analysis was also done by manually scoring the colocalizing puncta over total puncta.

Yeast LD number quantification

LD number quantification was scored manually using Fiji (NIH)57 by counting the number of LDs in 25 cells in quadruplets and analyzed in GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.1) (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Neutral lipid analysis by thin-layer chromatography

Cells were grown in SC media with shaking at 30°C for 16 h until early stationary growth phase. 100 OD600 units of cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed in MQ water, and stored at −80°C until further processing. Cell pellet was resuspended in 600 μL MQ water and lysed in the presence of glass beads using a Precellys Evolution Homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Germany). The homogenate was transferred to a fresh tube, and total protein was estimated using the BCA method. Volume equivalent to normalised protein concentration was subjected to lipid extraction using modified version of Folch extraction method.64 Briefly, 600 μL of cell lysate was added to 600 μL of chloroform: methanol (2:1) and mixed by vertexing for 5 min. Then, 300 μL of 0.9% NaCl was added to induce phase separation, vortexed, and centrifuged at 8500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The lower organic phase was transferred to a new tube. The aqueous phase was re-extracted with 600 μL of chloroform: methanol (2:1), organic phases were pooled followed by washing with 2M KCl. Lipid extracts were dried under the stream of N2 gas and dissolved in chloroform: methanol (1:1). The lipids were spotted onto silica gel 60 thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), along with standards. Neutral lipids were separated using hexane/diethyl ether/acetic acid (70/30/2, vol/vol/vol) as the eluent, and developed until ∼3/5 of the plate.65 Plates were dried and re-eluted using hexane/chloroform (9/1, vol/vol) and developed further until ∼4/5 of the plate, to separate steryl esters. The TLC plate was air dried and sprayed with 10% Cupric acetate (wt/vol) in 8% phosphoric acid, dried, and charred in an oven at 145°C for 1 h to visualize the lipid spots.49 Migration front of lipids was compared to that of standards, and spots were quantified using Fiji (NIH).57 Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.1) (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Drug sensitivity assay

Cells were grown in YPD media at 30°C until early stationary growth phase. 6-fold serial dilutions were made using a 96 well microtiter plate and stamped onto YP-Galactose plates with and without Tunicamycin (0.5 μg/ml and 1 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich), using a replica plater. The plates were incubated at 30°C for 3–5 days and imaged using FluorChem E (Protein Simple). Brightness and contrast were adjusted using Adobe Photoshop 2022 (Adobe Systems).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, data were obtained from at least three independent experiments. The images presented are representative of the results obtained. Quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed with unpaired, two-tailed Students’ t test or ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test applied, using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.1) (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). For both t test and ANOVA, p-values <0.05 were considered significant. Sample size and statistical analysis details of experiments can be found in the respective figure legends.
