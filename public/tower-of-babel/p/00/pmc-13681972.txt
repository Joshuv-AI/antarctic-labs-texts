A 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid biosensor for lignin valorization based on a newly identified transcriptional regulatory system
Authors: Kenta Tanatani, Hyo Suzuki, Masaya Fujita, Takuma Araki, Yuzo Suzuki, Tsuyoshi Michinobu, Yuichiro Otsuka, Masaya Nakamura, Eiji Masai, Naofumi Kamimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLignin is the most abundant renewable aromatic resource on Earth, yet its efficient conversion into value-added chemicals through biological funneling remains a key challenge. Sphingobium lignivorans SYK-6 exhibits broad metabolic capacity for lignin-derived aromatic compounds, ranging from monomers to dimers, making it a suitable host for biological funneling. Production systems for the polymer building block 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid (PDC) from lignin-derived aromatic compounds have been constructed using SYK-6. In this study, we developed a PDC-responsive biosensor to facilitate high-throughput screening of improved PDC-producing strains.ResultsPDC-assimilating bacteria were isolated from environmental samples, and their genomes were analyzed to obtain candidate PDC-responsive transcriptional regulatory systems. Among these, the LysR-type transcriptional regulator PdcR and its target promoter PPDC from Cupriavidus sp. 8B were identified and characterized as a PDC-responsive system, in which PdcR is suggested to act as both a repressor and an activator. A reporter plasmid pCup1 carrying these components was introduced into a PDC hydrolase gene-deficient strain (ΔligI) of SYK-6 to construct the PDC biosensor ΔligI(pCup1). The biosensor was specific for PDC, with no response observed for its upstream metabolites. When vanillic acid or protocatechuic acid was supplied as a substrate, concentration-dependent fluorescence responses were observed over a range of 0.1–5 mM, demonstrating that PDC production from lignin-derived aromatic compounds can be monitored. Fluorescence increased synchronously with PDC accumulation, suggesting real-time monitoring of PDC production. Furthermore, the biosensor responded to PDC produced from aromatic compounds in softwood black liquor extract.ConclusionsΔligI(pCup1) enables real-time, specific detection of PDC produced from lignin-derived aromatic compounds, including those in black liquor extract derived from lignocellulosic biomass, demonstrating applicability to screening for lignin valorization. Integration of this biosensor with diverse strain engineering approaches, including random mutagenesis, adaptive laboratory evolution, and metagenomics-based enzyme discovery, would provide a platform for efficient selection of SYK-6 strains with enhanced PDC production capability.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02797-9.

Introduction

Lignin, comprising 15–30% of plant biomass, is the most abundant renewable aromatic polymer on Earth and a promising feedstock for the production of value-added chemicals [1, 2]. Approximately 50 million tons of lignin are generated annually as a byproduct of the pulp and paper industry [2]; however, the vast majority is combusted for energy recovery rather than valorized as a chemical feedstock. Converting lignin into value-added chemicals is therefore a central objective of lignocellulosic biorefinery strategies.

To address this challenge, strategies combining chemical depolymerization of lignin with biological funneling into a single target compound have attracted considerable attention [3, 4]. One such target is 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid (PDC), a compound with promising applications as a building block for bioplastics and adhesives, and as a metal chelator [5–7]. Several PDC production systems exploiting metabolic pathways for lignin-derived aromatic compounds have been reported [8–14]. However, when lignocellulosic biomass-derived lignin is used as a feedstock, PDC production efficiency remains well below economically competitive levels. This limitation is partly attributed to the limited capacity of many host bacteria to efficiently convert heterogeneous lignin-derived aromatic compounds, including dimers and oligomers. In this context, Sphingobium lignivorans SYK-6, which catabolizes not only monomeric but also dimeric lignin-derived aromatic compounds, represents a promising production platform (Fig. 1A) [15]. Engineered SYK-6 strains lacking the PDC hydrolase gene ligI have achieved molar yields of nearly 100% from individual lignin-derived model dimers [16–18]. More recently, PDC was produced at a molar yield of 135% from softwood-derived black liquor [14]. The yield exceeding 100% suggests conversion of unidentified oligomeric components, highlighting the strong potential of SYK-6 as a PDC production host. Achieving economically competitive PDC production will therefore require further improvement of aromatic conversion efficiency in SYK-6. Transcriptional regulator-based biosensors, constructed by combining a transcriptional regulator, its cognate promoter, and a reporter gene, generate fluorescence signals in response to target compound concentration [19, 20]. Such biosensors have attracted increasing attention as high-throughput alternatives to conventional chromatographic analyses, and their application to screening of large mutant libraries for improved production strains has expanded considerably (Fig. 1B) [21–25]. To apply this approach to the development of improved PDC-producing SYK-6 strains, a biosensor based on a transcriptional regulatory system specifically responsive to PDC is essential. The promoter of the pmd operon from Comamonas sp. E6 is induced in the presence of PDC [26]. However, this promoter lacks specificity, as it responds to both PDC and protocatechuic acid (PCA), and the corresponding transcriptional regulator has not been identified [26]. To date, no PDC-specific transcriptional regulatory system has been reported, and its identification represents a key challenge in constructing a screening platform for improved SYK-6 strains.

In this study, we developed a PDC-responsive biosensor using S. lignivorans SYK-6 as the host to enable screening for improved PDC producers (Fig. 1C). Through genome analysis of PDC-assimilating bacteria isolated from environmental samples, we identified a PDC-specific transcriptional regulatory system from Cupriavidus sp. 8B and constructed the biosensor strain ΔligI(pCup1). This biosensor exhibited concentration-dependent fluorescence responses specific to PDC and detected PDC produced from softwood black liquor extract.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, culture conditions, and chemicals

The strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Additional file 1: Tables S1 and S2. Strains were cultured in Wx minimal medium [27] or in Lysogeny Broth (LB) as the rich medium. Wx medium was supplemented with SEMP (10 mM sucrose, 10 mM glutamate, 0.13 mM methionine, and 10 mM proline) [27]. For reporter assays, Wx-SEMP medium was further supplemented with PDC, PCA, vanillic acid, or black liquor extract as indicated. For solid media, agar was added to a final concentration of 1.5% (w/v). Strains were cultured aerobically at 37 °C for E. coli and at 30 °C for all other strains. When required, nalidixic acid (12.5 mg/L) and kanamycin (50 mg/L) were added to SYK-6 cultures, whereas kanamycin (25 mg/L) was added to E. coli cultures. PCA and vanillic acid were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). PDC was prepared as described previously [28]. Black liquor extract was prepared from black liquor obtained by oxygen-soda anthraquinone pulping of Japanese cedar [29]. The black liquor was acidified to approximately pH 2.0 with 12 N HCl and extracted three times with an equal volume of ethyl acetate. The organic phase was evaporated under reduced pressure, and the residue was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide at half the volume of the original black liquor, yielding a twofold concentrated extract.

Isolation of PDC-assimilating bacteria

A total of 26 environmental samples, including decaying wood and forest soil, were collected in Niigata and Ibaraki Prefectures, Japan. Each sample was suspended in Wx buffer [12.5 mM KH2PO4, 27.4 mM Na2HPO4, and 7.6 mM (NH4)2SO4, pH 7.0] and spread onto Wx agar plates containing 5 mM PDC as the sole carbon and energy source. Colonies with distinct morphological characteristics were selected from each sample, yielding two to four isolates per sample and a total of 82 PDC-assimilating strains.

Evaluation of inducible PDC conversion activity

The isolated PDC-assimilating strains and Comamonas sp. E6 were cultured for 24 h in LB medium (non-inducing conditions) or LB medium containing 10 mM PDC (inducing conditions). Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed twice with Wx buffer, and resuspended in the same buffer to prepare resting cells. Resting cells were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 or 0.2 and incubated with 500 μM PDC in a 96-well plate at 30 °C with shaking at 567 cycles per minute (cpm) using an EPOCH2 microplate reader (Agilent BioTek Inc.). The absorbance at 310 nm, corresponding to the absorption maximum of PDC, was monitored over time. The time required for the absorbance at 310 nm to reach a stable minimum, reflecting complete PDC consumption, was defined as the complete PDC conversion time. Inducibility was assessed by comparing PDC conversion times under non-inducing and inducing conditions.

For strains showing inducibility, resting cells prepared after cultivation in LB with or without 10 mM PDC were adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 and incubated with 500 µM PDC at 30 °C. PDC concentrations at the start of the reaction and after 1 h were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and the PDC conversion rate (µM PDC·h−1·OD600−1) was calculated.

HPLC analysis

All HPLC analyses were performed on an ACQUITY iClass UPLC system (Waters). Samples were filtered through a 0.2 µm PTFE membrane filter (Captiva Econofilter, Agilent), and separated on a TSKgel ODS-140HPT column (2.3 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Tosoh) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. For routine analyses, the mobile phase consisted of a solvent A (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (water containing 0.1% formic acid), with the following gradient: 0–3.0 min, 12–60% A; 3.0–3.1 min, 60–12% A; 3.1–4.0 min, 12% A. For the analysis of PDC produced from black liquor extract, solvent A (acetonitrile containing 0.5% phosphoric acid) and solvent B (water containing 0.5% phosphoric acid) were used with the following gradient: 0–4.0 min, 1–10% A; 4.0–5.0 min, 10–100% A; 5.0–6.0 min, 100% A; 6.0–6.1 min, 100–1% A; 6.1–7.0 min, 1% A. PDC and vanillic acid were detected at 315 nm and 260 nm, respectively. The aromatic components in the black liquor extract were analyzed as previously described [14].

16S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers 533f and 1525r with total DNA extracted from each strain as the template. The amplified products were sequenced by Eurofins Genomics (Japan). Homology searches of the 16S rRNA gene sequences were performed using NCBI BLASTn against the 16S ribosomal RNA sequences (Bacteria and Archaea) database [30]. Pairwise alignments were carried out using EMBOSS Needle [31], and phylogenetic trees were constructed by the maximum likelihood method using MEGA11 [32].

Whole-genome sequencing and identification of putative PDC hydrolase genes

Whole-genome sequencing of total DNA extracted from each strain was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Novogene (China). The obtained reads were assembled using VelvetOptimiser v2.2.6 [33] and annotated using DFAST [34]. The draft genome sequences were deposited in GenBank under the following accession numbers: Cupriavidus sp. 8B, NZ_BAAILO000000000.1; Burkholderia sp. 3C, NZ_BAAILN000000000.1; Paraburkholderia sp. 2C, NZ_BAAILL000000000.1; Pseudomonas sp. 3A, NZ_BAAILM000000000.1; and Novosphingobium sp. 11B, NZ_BAAILP000000000.1. Putative PDC hydrolase genes were identified by BLASTp searches of the predicted protein sequences from each genome using the amino acid sequences of known PDC hydrolases listed in Table S3.

Construction of reporter plasmids

Primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S2, and the sequences of synthetic genes are provided in Table S4. A synthetic gene consisting of a Shine–Dalgarno (SD) sequence followed by an mNeonGreen coding sequence codon-optimized for SYK-6 was cloned into the SmaI site of pSEVA231 using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs) to generate pPNS. Promoter regions (400–420 bp upstream of putative PDC hydrolase genes) were amplified by PCR using total DNA from each strain as the template. Transcriptional regulator genes were codon-optimized for SYK-6 and synthesized as gene fragments preceded by the bla promoter (Pamp) and an SD sequence. The promoter regions and transcriptional regulator genes were then cloned into the EcoRV site of pPNS to construct candidate biosensor plasmids. Gene synthesis was outsourced to Twist Bioscience (USA).

Construction of reporter strains and fluorescence assays

Each reporter plasmid was introduced into the SYK-6 ΔligI or ΔligAB strains by triparental mating to generate reporter strains. ΔligI was used as the host for all reporter strains, whereas ΔligAB was used to evaluate the specificity of PdcR. Each reporter strain was precultured in Wx-SEMP medium for 24 h, harvested by centrifugation at 4800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed twice with Wx buffer, and resuspended in the same buffer.

To evaluate substrate responsiveness, washed cells were inoculated into 200 µL of Wx-SEMP medium containing 0–50 mM PDC, 0–5 mM vanillic acid, or PCA, or 0.25–1.00% (v/v) black liquor extract in 96-well plates to an OD600 of 0.2. The cultures were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 567 cpm in a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent BioTek Inc.), and OD600 and mNeonGreen fluorescence (excitation, 506 nm; emission, 547 nm) were measured over time. Fluorescence intensity was expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU) and normalized to cell density (OD600), yielding RFU/OD600.

For evaluation of the time lag between PDC production and fluorescence, washed cells of ΔligI(pCup1) were inoculated into 10 mL of Wx-SEMP medium containing 1 or 5 mM vanillic acid in L-shaped tubes to an OD600 of 0.2 and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 160 rpm. At multiple time points up to 40 h, OD600 and fluorescence (RFU) were measured using a microplate reader, and culture supernatants were collected in parallel for determination of vanillic acid and PDC concentrations by HPLC.

To evaluate the response to black liquor extract, washed cells of ΔligI(pCup1) were inoculated into 200 µL of Wx-SEMP medium containing 0.25–1.00% (v/v) black liquor extract in 96-well plates to an OD600 of 0.2 and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 567 cpm in a microplate reader. Because the color of the black liquor extract precluded accurate OD600 measurements, fluorescence (RFU) alone was used as the indicator of PDC production. Culture supernatants were collected when RFU reached a plateau, and PDC concentrations were quantified by HPLC. The composition of major lignin-derived aromatic compounds in the original black liquor before extraction is shown in Table S5.

Results

Isolation of PDC-assimilating bacteria with inducible PDC-converting activity

To identify PDC-responsive transcriptional regulatory systems, we hypothesized that bacteria capable of assimilating PDC as a sole carbon source would harbor such regulatory systems and accordingly screened environmental samples for such bacteria. A total of 82 PDC-assimilating strains were isolated from environmental samples collected at 26 locations, including decayed wood and forest soils. Among these isolates, 16 strains exhibited induction of PDC-converting activity upon cultivation in the presence of PDC (Fig. S1). Six strains showed particularly strong induction, with 4–81-fold increases in PDC conversion rates under inducing conditions (Fig. 2), and were selected as candidates harboring PDC-responsive transcriptional regulatory systems. Based on 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis, these strains were classified into the genera Cupriavidus, Burkholderia, and Paraburkholderia (β-Proteobacteria); Pseudomonas (γ-Proteobacteria); Novosphingobium (α-Proteobacteria); and Pseudarthrobacter (Gram-positive bacteria) (Fig. S2, Table S6). For subsequent analyses, we focused on the Gram-negative strains: Cupriavidus sp. 8B, Burkholderia sp. 3C, Paraburkholderia sp. 2C, Pseudomonas sp. 3A, and Novosphingobium sp. 11B, considering that the transcriptional regulatory systems would be evaluated in the α-proteobacterial host strain SYK-6.Fig. 2Screening of bacteria exhibiting inducible PDC conversion activity. Identification of PDC-responsive transcriptional regulatory systems through genome analysis

The genomes of the five selected strains were sequenced and analyzed (Table S7). To identify promoters potentially inducible by PDC, we searched each genome for genes homologous to known PDC hydrolases. One or two putative PDC hydrolase genes with more than 40% amino acid sequence identity to known PDC hydrolases were found in each genome (Table S3). Genes encoding enzymes of the PCA 4,5-cleavage pathway were found near the putative PDC hydrolase genes in Cupriavidus sp. 8B and Novosphingobium sp. 11B, whereas genes encoding vanillic acid O-demethylase were located near the corresponding genes in Paraburkholderia sp. 2C and Pseudomonas sp. 3A (Fig. 3, Table S8). In contrast, no genes encoding metabolic enzymes potentially involved in PDC metabolism were found in the vicinity of the putative PDC hydrolase gene in Burkholderia sp. 3C. Candidate PDC-responsive transcriptional regulator genes and their cognate promoter regions were then selected from regions flanking the putative PDC hydrolase genes. In Cupriavidus sp. 8B (CS8_000090), Burkholderia sp. 3C, Paraburkholderia sp. 2C, and Pseudomonas sp. 3A, the putative PDC hydrolase genes were preceded by genes encoding putative LysR-type transcriptional regulators (LTTRs). Accordingly, the upstream regions of these PDC hydrolase genes were selected as candidate promoter regions (Fig. 3, Table S4). For CS8_000160 in Cupriavidus sp. 8B and NRB_04770 in Novosphingobium sp. 11B, genes predicted to form operons were located upstream of the PDC hydrolase genes; therefore, the upstream regions of CS8_000120 and NRB_04780 were selected as the candidate promoter regions. LTTR genes located near each putative PDC hydrolase gene were selected as candidate regulatory genes (Fig. 3).

Evaluation of the PDC responsiveness of candidate transcriptional regulatory systems

Each selected LTTR gene and its cognate promoter region were cloned into the pPNS vector carrying mNeonGreen as a reporter gene to construct reporter plasmids (Table S4). Each reporter plasmid was introduced into ΔligI, a ligI-deficient PDC-accumulating mutant of SYK-6, to generate candidate biosensor strains (Fig. 4A). When cultivated in Wx-SEMP medium under non-inducing conditions or in the presence of 0.5 or 5 mM PDC, ΔligI strains harboring the reporter plasmids pCup1 or pCup2 from Cupriavidus sp. 8B or pBur from Burkholderia sp. 3C exhibited marked, PDC concentration-dependent increases in fluorescence intensity (Figs. 4B, S3). The PDC concentration ranges eliciting fluorescence responses were then examined for ΔligI(pCup1), ΔligI(pCup2), and ΔligI(pBur) (Fig. 5). For all three biosensors, the fluorescence response increased in a PDC concentration-dependent manner and reached its maximum or a plateau around 10 mM PDC. Defining the dynamic range as the concentration range over which the fluorescence response increased in a concentration-dependent manner, the dynamic ranges of ΔligI(pCup1), ΔligI(pCup2), and ΔligI(pBur) were 0.2–10 mM, 0.5–10 mM, and 0.5–10 mM PDC, respectively. Among these, ΔligI(pCup1) exhibited the widest dynamic range for PDC detection under the assay conditions used in this study. Response range and specificity of the PDC biosensor

The response of ΔligI(pCup1) was evaluated using vanillic acid and PCA as substrates, both of which are metabolized to PDC in SYK-6 (Fig. 6A). For both substrates, a concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed over the range of 0.1–5 mM added substrate, indicating that ΔligI(pCup1) responds to PDC produced from lignin-derived aromatic compounds (Fig. 6B). To exclude the possibility that endogenous regulators of SYK-6 act on PPDC, a control plasmid (pCup0) containing only PPDC without pdcR was constructed and introduced into ΔligI. No fluorescence induction was observed in ΔligI(pCup0) even in the presence of PDC (Fig. S4), confirming that the PDC response in ΔligI(pCup1) depends on PdcR. Notably, ΔligI(pCup0) exhibited higher basal fluorescence in the absence of PDC but lower fluorescence in its presence compared with ΔligI(pCup1) (Figs. 4, S4), suggesting that PdcR functions as both a repressor and an activator of transcription from PPDC. To further evaluate the specificity of PdcR, ΔligAB(pCup1), a PCA catabolism-deficient mutant carrying pCup1, was cultivated in the presence of vanillic acid or PCA. Real-time detection and responsiveness to biomass-derived mixtures

One potential application of the PDC biosensor is the screening for SYK-6 mutant strains with improved conversion efficiency of lignin-derived aromatic compounds to PDC. For this application, a fluorescence response with minimal lag relative to PDC production is desirable. To assess this, the correlation between PDC production from vanillic acid and the fluorescence responses (RFU and RFU/OD600) was evaluated using ΔligI(pCup1). The RFU increased synchronously with PDC concentration and reached a plateau when PDC concentration became maximal (Fig. S5). In contrast, RFU/OD600 tended to plateau earlier in the presence of 5 mM vanillic acid despite continued PDC production (Fig. S5). These results suggest that, in this time-course experiment, RFU may serve as an indicator for real-time evaluation of PDC production from lignin-derived aromatic compounds.

To simulate PDC production from lignocellulosic biomass, ΔligI(pCup1) was cultivated in Wx-SEMP medium containing 0.25–1.00% (v/v) black liquor extract prepared from softwood by oxygen-soda anthraquinone pulping, a complex mixture of lignin-derived aromatic compounds (Fig. 7A). The major aromatic components of the extract were vanillin, vanillic acid, acetovanillone, and 5-carboxyvanillin, with concentrations of 40.1, 11.4, 3.91, and 3.17 mM, respectively. Because the coloration of the extract interfered with accurate OD600 measurement, RFU rather than RFU/OD600 was used as the indicator of the fluorescence response. Both the maximum RFU (Fig. 7B) and the PDC concentration at the RFU plateau (Fig. 7C) increased with extract concentration, indicating that ΔligI(pCup1) responds to PDC produced from lignin-derived aromatic mixture in the extract and that RFU can be used to estimate PDC production under these conditions.Fig. 7Responsiveness of ΔligI(pCup1) to softwood-derived black liquor extract. Discussion

The PDC biosensor ΔligI(pCup1) constructed in this study responded not only to exogenously supplied PDC but also to PDC generated intracellularly through the conversion of lignin-derived aromatic compounds (Figs. 5, 6). When vanillic acid or PCA was used as a substrate, a concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed over the range of 0.1–5 mM, indicating that PDC production from these substrates can be quantitatively monitored (Fig. 6). No fluorescence induction was observed in response to the upstream metabolites of PDC, suggesting that PdcR responds specifically to PDC rather than to its upstream metabolites. Monitoring RFU enabled real-time evaluation of PDC production from lignin-derived aromatic compounds. Notably, a concentration-dependent fluorescence response was also observed for PDC produced from softwood black liquor extract (Fig. 7). The two additional PDC-responsive systems identified in this study, pCup2 and pBur, could expand the available repertoire of PDC-responsive biosensors (Fig. 5). Their detailed characterization in future studies may reveal distinct properties suitable for specific screening applications.

ΔligI(pCup1) has practical significance not only as a detection tool but also as a screening platform for strains with improved PDC production. To date, evaluation of PDC production from lignin-derived aromatic compounds has largely relied on HPLC analysis [8–14, 16–18], making large-scale screening of mutant or evolved strains impractical. In contrast, fluorescence-based biosensors enable simultaneous evaluation of multiple samples using a microplate reader or a droplet microfluidics platform, thereby overcoming throughput limitations [21–23]. Furthermore, the use of SYK-6 as the host integrates the detection system and production host into a single strain, simplifying implementation of the screening platform [14–18]. The fluorescence response associated with PDC production from lignocellulosic biomass-derived black liquor extract further demonstrates that this biosensor functions effectively for lignin valorization. Beyond the oxygen-soda anthraquinone pulping in this study, vanillin, vanillic acid, and other guaiacyl-type aromatic compounds are commonly generated during various oxidative depolymerization processes of softwood lignin, such as tetrabutylammonium hydroxide-mediated aerobic oxidation [35, 36] and membrane reactor-based oxidative depolymerization [37]. In addition, metabolically engineered SYK-6 can efficiently convert syringyl-type compounds, such as syringic acid, which are abundant in oxidative depolymerization products of hardwood lignin, into PDC [14]. Therefore, aromatic compounds released from different types of lignin by oxidative depolymerization can serve as substrates for PDC production in SYK-6-based strains. Taken together, engineered SYK-6-based PDC biosensors are broadly applicable to screening for PDC production from oxidative depolymerization products of various lignin sources. Furthermore, the PdcR–PPDC system identified here is expected to be applicable beyond SYK-6: introduction into previously engineered PDC-producing strains of Pseudomonas putida [8–11] and Novosphingobium aromaticivorans [12, 13] would provide a direct route toward accelerated strain improvement in these chassis.

The PDC biosensor can be combined with diverse strain improvement strategies, including random mutagenesis, adaptive laboratory evolution, and metagenomics-based enzyme discovery [38–43]. For example, combining adaptive laboratory evolution with a product-responsive biosensor has been shown to simultaneously improve host tolerance and production titer, as demonstrated in a PCA production system [42]. Applied to lignin valorization, screening with the PDC biosensor would enable selection of strains with improved PDC production rates based on real-time fluorescence monitoring. In addition, combining the PDC biosensor with metagenomic libraries represents a particularly promising application [44, 45]. Even in SYK-6, which catabolizes a wide range of aromatic compounds, the enzymes responsible for converting aromatic oligomers, which constitute a major fraction of depolymerized lignin [46], remain unidentified. The genomes of uncultured microorganisms from diverse environments are expected to harbor novel enzymes involved in the degradation of these oligomers. By using ΔligI(pCup1) harboring a metagenomic library, fluorescence signals would be generated only when metagenome-derived enzymes convert aromatic oligomers into compounds that can be catabolized through the SYK-6 pathway to PDC, enabling direct identification of novel enzyme genes involved in PDC production from aromatic oligomers. In practice, this biosensor can support at least two screening criteria. For selecting strains that produce PDC at higher concentrations, the maximum fluorescence reflects the amount of PDC produced, since a higher concentration of PDC generated from upstream substrates was accompanied by a higher fluorescence at the RFU plateau (Figs. 7, S5). For selecting strains that produce PDC more rapidly, the time course of fluorescence reflects the rate of PDC production, since the fluorescence increased in synchrony with the concentration of PDC generated from vanillic acid in the time-course assay (Fig. S5). The biosensor thus provides a practical readout for ranking strains by either the amount or the rate of PDC production from lignin-derived aromatic compounds. Several considerations nevertheless remain. Unlike such relative comparisons, estimating the absolute concentration of PDC produced from the fluorescence values requires caution. The process leading to the fluorescence response differs between exogenously supplied PDC and PDC generated intracellularly from upstream substrates. Although the two responses are unlikely to differ markedly, the relationship between fluorescence level and PDC concentration established with exogenous PDC cannot necessarily be applied directly to the quantification of PDC produced from upstream substrates. Quantitative estimation of the produced PDC concentration would therefore require, for each substrate, constructing a calibration curve that relates the fluorescence output to the PDC concentration based on time-course measurements during substrate conversion. Furthermore, signal normalization remains an important challenge for practical screening using colored lignin-derived substrates such as black liquor extract. In the experiments with black liquor extract in this study, the coloration of the extract precluded accurate cell density measurement, and the fluorescence signal could not be normalized to OD600. For future applications, normalization independent of OD600, using a constitutively expressed fluorescent protein with an emission wavelength distinct from that of the reporter (such as mCherry) to correct the reporter fluorescence, would provide an effective solution.

Transcriptional regulator-based biosensors are versatile tools for measuring target ligand concentrations, with applications spanning metabolic engineering, environmental analysis, and medical diagnostics [47–49]. However, their construction relies on the availability of transcriptional regulators responsive to target compounds, and expanding the repertoire of regulators that recognize new ligands remains a key challenge [50]. Engineering approaches such as computational design and directed evolution have been developed to address this challenge [51]; however, these methods are constrained by the structures of existing regulators and thus limited in the range of ligands that the engineered variants can recognize. Although biosensors for several lignin-derived compounds such as vanillin [44, 52], vanillic acid [53], and muconic acid [54] have been developed using existing transcriptional regulators from well-characterized aromatic degradation pathways [24], no PDC-responsive regulatory system had been identified prior to this study. The wet-lab approach described here therefore provides a practical route to construct biosensors targeting compounds for which no natural regulatory system is known. Here, we employed a traditional wet-lab approach to identify a PDC-responsive transcriptional regulator: screening of PDC-assimilating bacteria, selection of strains exhibiting inducible PDC-converting activity, genome-based identification of regulator genes and promoters located proximal to conversion enzyme genes, and reporter-based evaluation of PDC responsiveness. This approach is effective for identifying novel natural transcriptional regulatory systems responsive to specific compounds without relying on sequence information from known regulators.

Genome analysis of PDC-assimilating bacteria revealed that genes predicted to encode dicarboxylate transporters were conserved near putative PDC hydrolase genes (Table S8). Homologs of these transporter genes were also identified in SYK-6, with amino acid sequence identities of 45.0–80.8% (Table S9), suggesting that these transporters are involved in PDC uptake by both PDC-assimilating bacteria and the biosensor host SYK-6.

Compared with ΔligI(pCup1), which retains pdcR, ΔligI(pCup0), lacking pdcR, exhibited higher basal fluorescence in the absence of PDC but lower fluorescence in its presence (Figs. 4, S4), suggesting that PdcR functions as both a repressor and an activator of transcription from PPDC. This dual regulatory mode is consistent with the sliding dimer mechanism characteristic of LTTRs, to which PdcR belongs [55, 56]. Characterization of the PdcR binding sequence and its positional relationship with PPDC would provide a foundation for sequence optimization to improve biosensor sensitivity and dynamic range. In the assays using exogenously supplied PDC, the biosensors reached the upper limit of their response around 10 mM (Fig. 5). If a broader response range is required for future applications, the dynamic range and sensitivity of the PdcR-based PDC biosensor could potentially be tuned by sensor engineering. Indeed, in LTTR-based biosensors, improvements in dynamic range and sensitivity have been achieved through engineering of transcriptional regulator variants and of operator and promoter sequences [49, 57, 58].

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a PDC-responsive biosensor using S. lignivorans SYK-6 as the host to enable high-throughput screening for strains with improved PDC production. Through combining the screening of PDC-assimilating bacteria followed by genome analysis, we identified the PdcR–PPDC transcriptional regulatory system from Cupriavidus sp. 8B and constructed the PDC-responsive biosensor ΔligI(pCup1). This biosensor would enable real-time fluorescence-based detection of PDC production from lignocellulosic biomass-derived black liquor extract and is applicable to large-scale screening under a wide range of lignin valorization conditions. By integrating this biosensor with diverse strain engineering approaches, including random mutagenesis, adaptive laboratory evolution, and metagenomics-based enzyme discovery, the ΔligI(pCup1) system would provide a platform for efficient selection of SYK-6 strains with enhanced PDC production capability.
