Dissecting the functional and spatial roles of peroxiredoxins as H2O2 scavengers and redox transducers
Authors: Laura de Cubas, Adrian Konopko, Susanna Boronat, José Ayté, Elena Hidalgo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since their discovery in the late 1980s as peroxidases that eliminate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), peroxiredoxins (Prxs) have been recognized not only for detoxification but also for roles in redox signaling. In fission yeast, the main Prx, Tpx1, maintains steady-state H2O2 below toxic levels through its scavenging activity. Under excess peroxides, Tpx1 switches to a signaling role, transmitting oxidative signals to the transcription factor Pap1. Dissecting the relative contributions of Tpx1’s peroxidase and sensor functions is challenging. To address this, we used a synthetic biology strategy to separate these dual roles. Tpx1 or catalase (Ctt1) was expressed in distinct subcellular compartments, and their capacity to remove H2O2 and activate Pap1 was assessed. Both scavengers can complement the growth defects of cells lacking Tpx1 in high oxygen. Combined with H2O2 biosensors, our results show that Tpx1 efficiently controls H2O2 levels from any compartment, whereas Ctt1 does not. However, cells expressing mitochondrial- or nuclear-targeted Tpx1 display reduced oxidative stress tolerance because Pap1 activation requires cytosolic Tpx1. Overall, Tpx1 is a highly effective peroxidase throughout the cell, but its subcellular localization is essential for proper redox signaling regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06356-1.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced in all oxygen-consuming organisms. The main site of production of ROS is the mitochondria [1]. One of these species, H2O2, can damage biomolecules but also participates in signaling events; it can cross membranes to higher or lower extent and therefore antioxidant activities are localized in different subcellular compartments to contribute to the final peroxide steady-state levels and to H2O2 signaling fluxes [2–4].

The main families of H2O2 scavengers are catalases, thioredoxin peroxidases (peroxiredoxins; Prxs) and glutathione peroxidases. While catalases use a heme group to catalyze the dismutation of two molecules of H2O2 into oxygen and H2O [5], the active sites of Prxs and glutathione peroxidases feature a redox-active cysteine (Cys) residue (the peroxidatic Cys), which undergoes oxidation to a sulfenic acid by H2O2; this group then reacts with another Cys residue, named the resolving Cys to form a disulfide that is subsequently reduced by an appropriate electron donor to complete a catalytic cycle [6, 7]. Eukaryotic organisms express a different collection of H2O2 scavengers, and members of each of these families are expressed from the same or different genes to give rise to differentially localized isoforms.

We and others have performed a genetic characterization of the repertoire of H2O2 scavenging activities in the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe. The main Prx in S. pombe, Tpx1, has high sensitivity for its substrate, H2O2, and it is expressed at very high number of molecules per cell [8] as to control the low levels of peroxides produced during aerobic growth: cells lacking this protein are only able to grow on solid plates under low-oxygen conditions [9]. Catalase is the next barrier of detoxification when the steady-state levels of peroxides are increased in Δtpx1 cells [10]. Furthermore, cells lacking Ctt1 are very sensitive to extracellularly added peroxides, and over-expression of Ctt1 is sufficient to suppress the sensitivity to oxidative stress of cells lacking the antioxidant transcription factors Pap1 or Atf1 [10]. S. pombe also expresses other two Prxs, Pmp20 and Bcp1, of unclear participation in H2O2 homeostasis [10]. Gpx1, the only glutathione peroxidase encoded by the S. pombe genome, only has a minor role, if any, when extracellular peroxides are added [10] or during the stationary phase [11].

While in other eukaryotes different Prx isoforms are expressed at different subcellular compartments, Tpx1 seems to display only cytosolic localization. By using the H2O2 biosensor HyPer7 expressed at either the cytosol or the matrix we have recently proposed that the steady-state levels of peroxides at the mitochondrial matrix, are significantly higher than those at the cytosol [12]. This steep gradient between the main site of ROS production and the cytosol is caused by Tpx1, which very efficiently consumes most peroxides diffusing out of the mitochondria. Indeed, cells lacking Tpx1 display high levels of peroxides in both compartments [12].

Regarding the role of Tpx1 in cell signaling, this Prx of cytosolic localization has been demonstrated to be essential for activation of the transcription factor Pap1 (pombe AP-1) [13, 14]. As shown before for other transcription factors, Pap1 is blind to peroxides in cells lacking the upstream component of this redox signaling cascade, Tpx1. Two models have been described for H2O2-dependent signal transduction process: a sulfenic acid-to thiol redox relay (this is the case of the Gpx3-Yap1 in budding yeast [15]) or a disulfide-to-thiol transfer [16]. The genetic and biochemical characterization of the Tpx1-Pap1 redox relay suggests that oxidized Prx, once the cellular reducing system becomes transiently overcome, can transfer the redox-signal to Pap1 through a disulfide-to-thiol relay, causing the formation of two intra-molecular disulfide bonds in Pap1 which promote the nuclear accumulation of the transcription factor and the activation of its antioxidant gene expression program [17, 18]. Tpx1 has also been proposed to positively regulate the activation of the mitogen activated protein (MAP) kinase Sty1, although in this case the effect is only partial (cells lacking Tpx1 can still activate the MAP kinase to a lower extent that in wild-type cells) [19].

As explained above, the lack of Tpx1 causes significant growth defects under aerobic conditions, due to the lack of H2O2 scavenging capacity; under this situation, it is not easy to decipher the contribution of Tpx1 as a sensor of antioxidant cascades in the cell’s tolerance to oxidants. Here, we have taken a synthetic biology approach to construct strains expressing Tpx1 exclusively in the cytosol or in the matrix of fission yeast cells, and we have analyzed the effects on: (i) basal H2O2 levels within each compartment; (ii) phenotypic consequences on aerobic growth; (iii) scavenging capacity upon H2O2 treatments added at the extracellular milieu; and (iv) tolerance to H2O2 on plates. Our study concludes that cells expressing Tpx1 at either the cytosol or the mitochondria can accomplish wild-type levels of H2O2 under basal or treated conditions. Nevertheless, physical separation of the two-component redox system, Tpx1-Pap1, fully inactivates the relay and yields cells sensitive to oxidative stress.

H2O2 survival assays

Assays were performed as previously described [31]. Briefly, strains were grown at 30 °C in YE5S or MM until they reached logarithmic phase, at an OD600~0.5. The same number of cells (105 and 1/10 serial dilutions) in 3 µl was spotted on YE5S or MM agar plates, as indicated, with or without the indicated concentration of H2O2. The spots were allowed to dry and the plates were incubated at 30 °C for 2–4 days under aerobic conditions or inside an anaerobic chamber, with low oxygen tension, as described before.

Growth of strains expressing HyPer7 or MTS-HyPer7 for fluorescence determination

For wild-type backgrounds, standard MM-based early stationary phase pre-cultures were diluted in filtered MM to reach an OD600 of 1 after 4–5 duplications. The fluorescence of 190 µl of these cultures, at an OD600 of 1, was directly monitored in 96-well plates in a FLUOstar OMEGA (BMG Labtech) as described below.

In vivo measurement of basal and induced oxidation of HyPer and roGFP-derivatives

The oxidation of genetically encoded reporters was monitored using a fluorescence plate reader FLUOstar OMEGA (BMG Labtech). We used excitation filters of 400 − 10 and 485BP12, combined with emission filter EM520. The experiments were performed as previously described [12]. The graphs depict the probe oxidation degree (OxD) before treatment (OxD0) or after treatment over time; 0 corresponds to treatment with 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 1 corresponds to treatment with 1 mM H2O2. All experiments were performed in biological triplicates using cells obtained from three independent cell cultures.

TCA extracts and immunoblot analysis

TCA extracts with alkylating agents followed by non-reducing polyacrylamide electrophoresis was obtained as described before [13]. Briefly, yeast cells were grown in MM until an OD600 of 0.5 was reached. Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to a final concentration of 10%. Cells were pelleted and washed in 20% TCA. The pellets were resuspended in 100 µl of 12.5% TCA and lysed by vortexing in the presence of glass beads. Cell lysates were pelleted, washed in acetone, and dried. Pellets were resuspended in alkylating buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, 75 mM iodoacetamide), loading buffer with (standard SDS-PAGE) or without DTT (non-reducing SDS-PAGE) was added and samples were boiled for 5 min at 100 °C. Samples were detected by immunoblotting with monoclonal anti-GFP antibody (Takara) or polyclonal anti-Tpx1 antibody [9], as indicated.

Statistics

For in vivo fluorescence probe oxidation quantification, OxD, one independent culture of the strain of interest was grown for each replicate. In all figure panels, values of mean of n = 3 are shown, with error bars representing standard deviation (S.D.). When indicated statistics were performed with a Student t-test.

Results

Enhancing cellular H2O2 scavenging capacity in either the cytosol or the mitochondrial matrix suppresses the aerobic growth defects of Δtpx1

As described in the Introduction, Tpx1 scavenges H2O2 generated during basal aerobic metabolism, likely originating mainly from mitochondria. During aerobic growth, most of the abundant Tpx1 exists as non-covalent dimers, despite being continuously reducing peroxides to maintain physiological H2O2 levels (Fig. 1A, left panel). When extracellular peroxide concentrations rise, Tpx1 predominantly forms disulfide-linked dimers because oxidation exceeds reduction. Under these conditions, the electron donor capacity may become transiently exhausted, and oxidized Tpx1 shifts toward its signaling role, activating Pap1 (Fig. 1A, right panel). As shown in Fig. 1B, the scavenging function of Tpx1 is readily demonstrated: cells lacking this peroxiredoxin fail to grow aerobically on solid media. To assess Tpx1’s role in Pap1 activation, cells must be plated on media containing added peroxides (Fig. 1C). To further investigate the contribution of the main H2O2 scavengers, Tpx1 and Ctt1, in the cell’s physiology, and to separate the scavenging and signaling roles of Tpx1, we design four strains expressing the scavengers in different sub-cellular locations. Thus, we expressed in strain Δtpx1 Ctt1 or Tpx1 from episomal plasmids and under the control of the constitutive sty1 promoter, either at the cytosol or at the mitochondrial matrix (using the mitochondrial targeting signal (MTS) from Aco1, a matrix resident protein [12]) (Fig. 1D). Of note, the MTS tag is capable of dragging Tpx1 and Ctt1 to the mitochondrial matrix, as previously demonstrated with fluorescence microscopy and subcellular fractionation [26]. The concentrations of the Tpx1 isoforms expressed from these episomal or integrative plasmids were very high (100,000–250,000 copies per cell), based on comparison with endogenous wild-type Tpx1 levels [~ 400,000 copies per cell [8], as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1AB using polyclonal anti-Tpx1 antibodies. Because antibodies against Ctt1 were not available, we used tagged versions of Tpx1 and Ctt1 to compare the levels of these scavengers. This also allowed us to confirm that Ctt1 expression levels were comparable to those of Tpx1, as both proteins were expressed under the control of the same sty1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Also importantly, we have previously demonstrated that the matrix electron donor network is sufficiently robust to support the scavenging activity of MTS-Tpx1 [32]. As shown in Fig. 1E, the four isoforms of these scavengers could suppress the phenotypic defects on aerobic solid plates of cells lacking Tpx1.

Ctt1 and Tpx1 have different effects on basal steady-state levels of peroxides

To analyze the molecular bases of the suppression of Δtpx1 growth defects by these scavengers, we used the H2O2 biosensor HyPer7, expressed either at the cytosol or the matrix in these different strain backgrounds. We have shown before that HyPer7 and MTS-HyPer7 expressed in wild-type cells display different basal oxidation levels, OxD0, of ∼0 and 0.4 respectively, mainly due to higher relative peroxide levels in the matrix compared to the cytosolic levels [12, 32]. When expressed in strain Δtpx1, HyPer7 and MTS-HyPer7 display OxD0 values of ∼0.6 in both compartments, indicating that the peroxide gradient between the matrix and the cytosol is caused by the powerful scavenging at the cytosol exerted by the Prx, and that the H2O2 toxic levels corresponding to an OxD0 of ∼0.6 cause growth defects [12].

We first tested the effect of expressing Tpx1 or Ctt1 in the cytosol of wild-type or Δtpx1 cells (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B (left panel), the cytosolic OxD0 values were almost (with Ctt1) or fully (with Tpx1) restored to wild-type levels, justifying their suppressor capacity. When the scavengers were expressed at the matrix (Fig. 2C), the high mitochondrial peroxide levels of cells lacking Tpx1 were significantly (with MTS-Ctt1) or totally (with MTS-Tpx1) diminished, as determined with the OxD0 values (Fig. 2D, left panel). On the contrary, MTS-Ctt1 could not restore wild-type levels of H2O2 in the cytosol, while the OxD0 of cytosolic HyPer7 went down to 0 by expression of MTS-Tpx1 (Fig. 2D, right panel). These results demonstrate that augmenting the cellular H2O2 scavenging capacity with either Ctt1 or Tpx1 can totally alleviate the aerobic growth defects of Δtpx1 cells by decreasing peroxides under toxic levels. Nevertheless, each one of the four cell types displayed different levels of H2O2 at each compartment.

After adding peroxides to the media, Tpx1 can scavenge intracellular H2O2 from any compartment, whereas catalase cannot

We then tested the behavior of each of these four new strains in response to extracellularly added peroxides. Time-dependent probe oxidation following peroxide addition to the growth medium allowed us to assess the extent of initial scavenging in each strain background. We tested the phenotypic consequences of localized expression of scavengers by spotting our four strains (together with the wild-type and Δtpx1 strains as controls) on aerobic plates containing or not increasing concentrations of H2O2. Cells lacking Tpx1 were as sensitive to oxidative stress when carrying an empty plasmid or when transformed with expression plasmids for Ctt1 or MTS-Ctt1 (Fig. 4B). As expected, over-expression of cytosolic Tpx1 yielded cells with wild-type tolerance to peroxides: this genetically-engineered strain is very similar to an original wild-type background, although with slightly lower levels of Tpx1 (even though the transgene is located in an episomal plasmid with ~ 7–8 copies per cell [33], it is expressed from the sty1 promoter instead of from the endogenous tpx1 promoter, which is ~ 30 times stronger based on proteomic data [8]). However, even though MTS-Tpx1-expressing Δtpx1 cells were capable of detoxifying extracellular peroxides to similar extent as a wild-type background (see Fig. 3), they were almost as sensitive to peroxides as cells carrying the empty plasmid (Fig. 4B).

We then monitored the ability of MTS-Tpx1 to transduce the redox signal to Pap1 upon peroxide addition. As shown by western blot, only cells expressing Tpx1 in the cytosol were capable of triggering Pap1 disulfide formation and activation (Fig. 4C). Our results indicate that the role of Tpx1 as a redox transducer to Pap1— which cannot occur if the two proteins are physically separated— is essential for tolerance to oxidative stress.

In the same line, we constitutively expressed Tpx1 in the nucleus, by tagging it with a nuclear localization signal. The NLS-Tpx1 chimera suppressed the aerobic growth defects of strain Δtpx1 (data not shown), but could not trigger activation of a Pap1 isoform lacking its NLS signal and with constitutive cytosolic localization [34] (Fig. 4D, lower panel). We conclude that while Tpx1 is equally able to scavenge low levels of peroxides from any subcellular localization, the presence of Pap1 in the cytosol explains why S. pombe cells express their main active Prx in this compartment (Fig. 4E).

We then compared the H2O2 detoxifying activity of Tpx1 expressed from an episomal plasmid (left panel of Fig. 5C) or from an integrative plasmid (right panel of Fig. 5C), using again the HyPer7 biosensor. We determined that Δtpx1 cells expressing cytosolic Tpx1 from the integrative plasmid could largely reduce the steady-state levels of H2O2, decreasing the HyPer7 OxD0 from 60% of a of the Δtpx1 background to 20%. However, the low levels of Tpx1 arising from this integrative plasmid provided very limited peroxide scavenging activity upon H2O2 addition, and the slopes of HyPer7 reduction/recycling resembled those of cells lacking Tpx1 (see Fig. 3). This result suggest that Tpx1 is a sensitive but not very efficient enzyme regarding peroxide detoxification, and that explains why this peroxiredoxin is one of the proteins with higher copy number per cell in fission yeast [8].

Discussion

Eukaryotic cells express a complex battery of peroxide scavengers, which vary in their composition, the concentration of the different enzymes and the sub-cellular localization. We have used here fission yeast, with a limited collection of H2O2 scavengers, to test the consequences of expressing catalases or Prxs in the cytosol or in the main site of ROS production, the mitochondrial matrix. Two of these engineered strains, expressing Ctt1 in either the cytosol or the matrix, display abnormal responses to extracellular peroxides, demonstrating the importance of the powerful Prx-dependent scavenging in wild-type tolerance to oxidative stress. The other two strains, expressing Tpx1 in the cytosol or the matrix, have excellent responses to extracellularly added H2O2, suggesting that this Prx is a potent H2O2 scavenger which can control peroxide levels from compartments different from its natural localization. However, its physical separation from downstream components of a signaling cascade can jeopardize wild-type tolerance to peroxides: MTS-Tpx1 cannot activate the transcription factor Pap1 (Fig. 5D) and therefore cells are sensitive to H2O2.

While all four engineered strains, expressing scavengers in a Tpx1 depleted background, could suppress the aerobic growth defects of strain Δtpx1, their responses to extracellular peroxides were dramatically different. Ctt1 and MTS-Ctt1 could slightly improve H2O2 scavenging of cells lacking Tpx1 in the respective location where they were expressed, and this effect was limited to such compartment. This result suggest that Ctt1 can scavenge peroxide concentrations over a certain threshold, and it is blind to concentrations below; thus, low H2O2 levels that are not detected by Ctt1 can diffuse and reach other sub-cellular locations. In some sense, Ctt1 would work as a floodgate, which only starts working when the concentration of its substrate is sufficiently high. While it remains unclear why cytosolic Ctt1 influences mitochondrial steady-state H2O2 levels whereas mitochondrial Ctt1 does not similarly affect the cytosolic H2O2, we speculate that MTS-Ctt1, despite being expressed at a concentration comparable to that of MTS-Tpx1, reduces matrix H2O2 generated by the ETC but fails to prevent H2O2 leakage from the intermembrane space into the cytosol. Consequently, overexpression of cytosolic Ctt1 in Δtpx1 cells, H2O2 accumulates in the cytosol because this compartment lacks an efficient scavenging system. In contrast, overexpression of cytosolic Ctt1 in Δtpx1 cells, which exhibit elevated H2O2 levels in both compartments, allows cytosolic Ctt1 to function as a sink, thereby reducing the overall intracellular H2O2 concentration.

The scavenging capacity of Prxs is radically different: Tpx1 from any location can control the concentration of H2O2 in other sub-cellular compartments. Prxs consume H2O2 at the site of production with great efficiency. Therefore, in order to allow peroxide fluxes and redox communication inter-compartments, our experiments suggest that active Prxs should not be located at the source of H2O2 production; otherwise, peroxide fluxes would be fully inhibited. In our synthetic biology study, we have designed a strain expressing MTS-Tpx1 at the matrix, close to the main site of H2O2 production; this strain can control matrix and cytosolic H2O2 levels even better than wild-type cells. Contrary to the expectations, its physical separation from downstream components of signaling cascades, such as Pap1, renders its function useless as a signal transducer, and this strain is very sensitive to extracellular peroxides. Similarly, expression of nuclear-localized Tpx1 in Δtpx1 is unable to activate Pap1 upon peroxide addition.

Another important issue regarding Tpx1 expression concerns the protein levels required for efficient peroxide scavenging and redox transduction. Tpx1 is a highly abundant protein, expressed at approximately 440,000 copies per cell [8], and expression of Tpx1 from an episomal plasmid yields sufficient protein levels as to provide wild-type tolerance to peroxides. However, reducing Tpx1 levels by approximately tenfold, achieved through expression from an integrative plasmid, renders cells capable of growing on high oxygen conditions, but sensitive to H2O2. In conclusion, the only configuration that yields wild-type tolerance to peroxides in S. pombe is the expression of a highly abundant peroxiredoxin, Tpx1, in the cytosol of fission yeast cells, where it can effectively perform both scavenging and signaling functions.
