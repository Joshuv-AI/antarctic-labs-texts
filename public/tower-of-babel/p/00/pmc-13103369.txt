A deployable digital twin framework for bolt-torque specification compression in EV chassis assembly
Authors: Haijun Wang, Zhijie Huang, Zhengxin Lan, Tie Xu, Hao Chen, Deyang Luo, Danhua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBolted-joint tightening affects both structural integrity and line performance in EV chassis assembly, yet production torque tables are often conservative to accommodate variability in joint mechanics and shop-floor conditions. This study uses a deployable digital-twin workflow to reduce torque variants while jointly considering mechanics constraints, AGV-related logistics, and cost/OEE objectives. The workflow couples (i) a torque–preload joint model with vibration and fatigue checks, (ii) an uncertainty-calibrated, value-aware LSTM for quality-risk prediction from torque–angle signatures and context, and (iii) a multi-objective PSO variant for mixed discrete–continuous search with feasibility-first handling. Candidate torque tables are admitted only after verification-twin re-evaluation, which serves as a release gate. In an industrial study covering 5,524 vehicles (Feb 2024–Jan 2025), torque specifications are reduced from 23 variants to 8 (− 65.2%) while keeping the observed reject rate within a ≤ 0.05% cap and meeting Goodman safety ≥ 1.5. Standardization reduces end-effector changeovers by 31% and AGV idle time by 14% at a 42 s takt without increasing fleet size. Cross-platform transfer is evaluated on three EV platforms, and ablations are reported with statistical tests and effect sizes alongside V&V metrics. Introduction

Threaded fasteners are central to new-energy-vehicle (NEV) chassis assembly because they combine load-bearing integrity, serviceability, and low-cost joining under mixed-material structures and tight takt time1. Compared with internal-combustion platforms, electric-drive harmonics, regenerative-braking reversals, and tri-axial road spectra impose persistent transverse and torsional excitation. Such excitation can widen preload scatter, promote micro-slip, and accelerate fretting at thread and bearing interfaces2,3. Torque set-point compliance therefore does not guarantee clamp retention when friction and process dispersion drift across tools, operators, and batches4,5. To hedge against these drifts, torque tables are often conservative and split into many bolt-family variants. In practice, more variants mean more tool programs and end-effector changeovers, harder line balancing, and tighter coupling with AGV dispatch and station scheduling under a fixed takt6. The engineering task is system-level: standardize torque specifications while keeping mechanics margins and quality risk within limits, without adding throughput penalties.

Mechanics models relate torque to preload and check separation/fatigue using contact assumptions and stiffness sharing7,8. On the shop floor, tightening signatures (e.g., torque–angle/process curves) are widely used for monitoring, and machine-learning approaches have been shown to flag fastening anomalies from these curves9. Sequence models such as LSTM provide a practical way to learn the temporal structure of signatures, while probabilistic formulations can report decision-relevant uncertainty near acceptance boundaries10,11. Open datasets for torque–angle curves have also started to appear, enabling more reproducible comparisons12. For system-level decisions, multi-objective optimization is widely used to balance quality, cycle time, and cost under discrete tool-program choices. NSGA-II remains a common baseline13. Multi-objective PSO variants and dynamic multi-swarm strategies are often used to improve convergence and spread, and many-objective extensions become useful when objectives proliferate14–17. Digital-twin-driven multi-objective design has also been reported in other engineering contexts18. Digital-twin studies in manufacturing emphasize deployment maturity, integration bottlenecks, and the need for explicit verification and validation (V&V) when moving from pilot analyses to shop-floor release19,20. Even so, an end-to-end loop that links prediction, Pareto search, and a DT-based V&V gate for torque-table standardization is still rarely specified and seldom validated at production scale.

This paper presents i-LS-AMOPSO, a closed-loop digital-twin workflow that implements a predict → optimize → verify → deploy process for EV chassis bolt-torque governance. It couples a mechanics twin (torque–preload and durability constraints), a quality twin (value-aware LSTM risk prediction), and a logistics twin (AGV congestion and changeover effects) into a unified multi-objective program. Pareto search uses an adaptive chaotic multi-swarm MOPSO with feasibility-first selection and a ± 2 N·m minimum-norm projection for near-feasible solutions. Elite candidates are periodically re-checked by a higher-fidelity verification twin with uncertainty propagation before shop-floor release and monitoring updates.

The workflow is evaluated via cross-platform transfer across three EV platforms and a one-year industrial deployment (5,524 vehicles, Feb 2024–Jan 2025). The manuscript reports V&V criteria and transparent statistical reporting (n, confidence intervals, effect sizes), with extended benchmarks and full parameter/ablation tables in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note S1; Supplementary Tables S1–S6).Sects. 2–4 describe the formulation, learning/optimization, and V&V with computational and field results; Sect. 5 discusses limitations and concludes.

Parameter identification, calibration, and operational measurement

Mechanical parameters are calibrated with a tiered protocol to improve generalizability beyond a single case. (i) Torque coefficient : weekly torque–tension audits on a stratified bolt-family sample (lubrication state, washer, surface finish) measure preload using an instrumented bolt or ultrasonic clamp-length gauge; is obtained by least-squares fitting of Eq. (1) and tracked as a distribution (mean ± SD) rather than a point value. (ii) Wear/reuse term : controlled reuse cycles on representative joints quantify seating/embedment and friction drift; in production, is updated online by recursive least squares using the residual between audit-preload and Eq. (1) predictions conditioned on reuse count and torque–angle features. (iii) Vibration coupling : tri-axial accelerometers mounted near the joint provide PSD; is fitted by matching measured clamp-loss envelopes from transverse-vibration tests (Junker-type) and verified by road/bench excitation segments in the digital twin. (iv) Load-transfer : computed from stiffness sharing with validated by local compression tests or FEA where available. These measurements follow standard shop-floor audit practices and can be replicated on other platforms by re-estimating the same physically interpretable coefficients. The manuscript reports parameter bounds and update rules to support transfer, while noting that extreme changes in joint design or lubrication would require re-auditing.

Operational AGV and changeover metrics are computed from time-stamped event logs. AGV idle-time ratio uses with decomposed into travel, pickup/drop, and queue/service components from log states. To associate operational improvements with torque standardization, a controlled before/after design is used: two matched production windows (≥ 4 weeks each) with the same takt (42 s), shift pattern, AGV fleet size, and product-mix constraints; abnormal downtime days are excluded by predefined criteria. Uncertainty is quantified by bootstrap confidence intervals over daily aggregates and by run charts/SPC summaries; residual confounding cannot be fully excluded and is discussed as a limitation.

Digital twin verification and validation (V&V) protocol

The deployed digital twin couples three layers: (i) a mechanics layer mapping torque to preload and checking separation/fatigue constraints via the closed-form joint model with calibrated stiffness-sharing and vibration-coupling parameters; (ii) a quality layer estimating reject probability from fastening signatures and context using the value-aware LSTM; and (iii) a logistics layer translating station load, AGV congestion, and changeovers into takt/OEE objectives. The workflow runs in a unified MATLAB/Python pipeline.

Ground truth and uncertainty. In this study, “ground truth” is the verification-twin output used in the V&V gate. Mechanics constraints are checked against torque–tension audits (plus local compression tests/FEA when available), quality risk against observed reject counts, and logistics metrics against time-stamped AGV/changeover logs. Uncertainty bands are obtained by Monte-Carlo sampling of calibrated parameter distributions (e.g., torque coefficient, wear/reuse drift, vibration coupling) and reporting feasibility at the 95th percentile.

Elite verification and rejection. Candidates are first screened by the surrogate stack under a fixed budget. Every 10 generations, the top-K = 20 archive candidates are re-evaluated by the verification twin with 1000 Monte-Carlo samples. A candidate is rejected if any Manufacturing Red Line is violated (reject cap > 0.05%, Goodman safety < 1.5, parameter bounds) or if the 95th-percentile check fails. Near-feasible cases may be adjusted by a ± 2 N·m minimum-norm projection and must pass re-checking before archiving23,24.

Integrated co-optimization framework

The proposed i-LS-AMOPSO framework integrates the enhanced predictive digital twin (i-LS-VA) and the chaotic multi-swarm optimizer (EAC-MOPSO) into a unified co-optimization pipeline. This hybrid framework enables simultaneous optimization of mechanical reliability, production efficiency, and cost objectives under 3D dynamic constraints. The framework operates in three key phases:

(i)Predictive Phase: The i-LS-VA model receives real-time sensor data and value features from the digital twin to predict torque outcomes and quality metrics. Computational validation and performance analysis

Digital twin architecture and cross-platform validation setup

The digital twin validation25 across three vehicle platforms (E50, E260, and E50MCE) demonstrates the generalization capability of i-LS-AMOPSO. A production digital twin architectures26 is instantiated on E50 and transferred to E260 (+ 85 kg, + 100 mm wheelbase) and E50MCE (− 43 kg, 0 mm) under a common co-optimization loop. To ensure a fair comparison across platforms, the computational environment (hardware, software versions), the data preprocessing pipeline (including filtering, feature scaling, and segmentation), and the core hyperparameters of the i-LS-AMOPSO framework were held constant. This controlled setup ensures that any performance differences can be more confidently attributed to the platform variations themselves rather than to inconsistencies in the experimental conditions. Validation spans 16 road/bench conditions per wheel with tri-axial forces and roll torque; runs terminate by hypervolume stall or a 200-generation budget.

Figure 4 shows that the E50 reference front projects onto E260 and E50MCE with small, near-parallel displacements (Fig. 4a,b), while the knee locations remain co-located (Fig. 4c). This pattern implies that mass and geometry differences primarily scale the load envelope without altering the trade-off curvature. Consequently, the front shape and the knee position are preserved across platforms, enabling low-touch transfer of operating setpoints.

Optimization algorithm performance and convergence analysis

The performance of the integrated i-LS-AMOPSO optimizer is benchmarked against NSGA-II and a standard MOPSO algorithm. Convergence quality is assessed using the Inverted Generational Distance (IGD↓) and Hypervolume (HV↑) metrics over generations, while final front quality is measured by Spacing (SP↓) and IGD, with wall-clock time recording computational cost.

As shown in Fig. 10a, the IGD decreases monotonically on all three platforms. i-LS-AMOPSO and MOPSO achieve comparable, favorable convergence in the mid-to-late stages and consistently surpass NSGA-II in proximity to the true Pareto front. Collectively, these convergence profiles indicate that the predictor-in-the-loop design does not compromise asymptotic front quality, even across platforms with differing mass and geometry.

Field validation and shop-floor deployment results

This section validates the deployability of the proposed i-LS-AMOPSO in production and under operational driving conditions. The evaluation covers (i) torque-variant standardization and bolt-family selection on the E50 platform and (ii) end-to-end assembly plus road-test outcomes over a year-long quality dataset. Standardization reduced torque variants from 23 to 8 (− 65.2%), decreased end-effector changeovers by 31%, and lowered AGV idle time by 14% at a 42 s takt. To support causal attribution, these operational outcomes are computed using a controlled before/after design with matched production windows and are reported with daily-aggregate bootstrap confidence intervals and run-chart summaries (Supplementary Data D1). Fleet size was unchanged, with mean utilization 0.78.

Data inclusion. Field results include only vehicles meeting the exposure gate at data freeze (≥ 6 months or ≥ 5,000 km). Units below the threshold were excluded from point estimates. We removed cases with crash/major repair, abnormal payload campaigns, or early re-work. Sensitivity gates (3 months/2,500 km and 9 months/10,000 km) preserved the same torque-group ranking, with absolute-rate changes ≤ 0.007 pp.

Compliance gate. Deployment follows the Manufacturing Red Lines: candidates breaching the reject cap (> 0.05%) are blocked28. Near-misses are adjusted by a ± 2 N·m minimum-norm projection and re-verified before release. All Sect. 4.3 shop-floor metrics are reported post-gate.

Torque unification. Using the optimized torque set inferred by i-LS-AMOPSO for the E50 chassis, bolt-level recommendations are first generated at part granularity in Fig. 14. To reduce changeovers and tooling complexity, torques within a ± 5 N·m band are consolidated to a common specification (Fig. 15). This standardization compresses the catalog from 23 variants to 8, a 65.2% reduction, directly supporting AGV end-effector commonality, near-feasible candidates were projected within ± 2 N·m as specified in Sect. 2, after which all samples passed the gate.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study treats EV chassis torque-table standardization as a closed predict–optimize–verify workflow. Under the same primary evaluation budget, i-LS-AMOPSO produces Pareto fronts with lower IGD and tighter spread than the baselines on both ZDT1/2/3 and the industrial co-optimization cases, while maintaining comparable hypervolume. Early progress is stronger, as reflected by the HV-AUC over the first 100 generations. In the plant deployment, the torque table is reduced from 23 to 8 variants (− 65.2%) while staying within the reject cap (≈ 0.01–0.05%) and meeting the safety constraint (Goodman ≥ 1.5). Cross-platform transfer across three EV platforms shows small front shifts and a stable knee, with cross-platform knee-cost dispersion below 0.4%. Ablations indicate that value/uncertainty weighting mainly improves learning in risk-sensitive regions, and that tent-map perturbation with dynamic sub-swarms helps avoid loss of diversity under feasibility-first selection. Field outcomes after the ≥ 6-month/≥5,000-km exposure gate are consistent with the simulation-based thresholds.

Limitations and implementation. The main long-term risk is calibration drift when road spectra, lubrication, or friction conditions move outside the audited range. In practice, the predictor and the calibrated mechanics/logistics coefficients should be re-audited on a fixed cadence (e.g., per maintenance cycle) or when monitoring signals indicate drift. The added compute time comes from periodic verification-twin checks with uncertainty propagation; this is an offline governance step and can be handled by batching, pruning, and asynchronous scheduling. Transfer to platforms with substantially different joint design or surface treatment will likely require additional audits and a larger few-shot set.

Operational implications. After standardization, end-effector changeovers decrease by 31% and AGV idle time decreases by 14% at a 42 s takt. These effects are reported using a matched before/after design; remaining confounding is discussed as a limitation. Compared with off-line optimization that reports only surrogate results, the workflow enforces a release gate: elite solutions are re-evaluated by the verification twin (with uncertainty checks) before shop-floor use, which reduces the risk of deploying infeasible torque tables.

Future work. Priorities include modeling and monitoring friction/road-spectrum drift, improving online or federated updates for faster transfer, and extending the objective set to longer-horizon value terms (e.g., escape cost and maintenance windows) for cross-cell optimization.

Conclusion

The framework has been piloted at SAIC-GM-Wuling plants, demonstrating scalability across multiple vehicle platforms. The proposed i-LS-AMOPSO provides a unified, auditable path to co-optimize reliability, takt efficiency, and cost under 3D feasibility constraints. Across vehicle variants, the Pareto front remains well-shaped with a stable knee; torque-spec compression (23→8) reduces changeovers and AGV-scheduling complexity; IGD and Spread decrease while HV is preserved or improved; and field rejection remains within internal control limits (≈ 0.0–0.05.0.05%). Representative outcomes include up to ≈ 96% IGD reduction on Model-E50 and ≈ 94% on Model-E260 relative to the best ablation, with faster early HV gain. The looped integration of value-aware prediction, chaotic multi-swarm search, and digital-twin validation offers a reusable blueprint for deploying multi-objective optimization in EV-chassis assembly, with clear trade-off transparency and low-touch transferability.
