Molecular mechanisms and translational implications in apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and cuproptosis of spermatogonial stem cells
Authors: Feng Deng, Jianghong Xiang, Zuping He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) are essential for male fertility because they form the cellular foundation for normal spermatogenesis. Here we address the regulatory mechanisms governing cell deaths of SSCs, e.g., apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and cuproptosis, including transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, RNA-binding proteins, and epigenetic modifications (e.g., non-coding RNAs). We systematically elucidate testicular microenvironment and signaling pathways in controlling SSC deaths, including mitochondrial signaling, death receptor signaling, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and WNT/β-catenin pathways. We also discuss the translational applications of targeting key pathways or remodeling the microenvironment to intervene in SSC deaths. We highlight the prospects and requirements to develop the advanced technologies, e.g., the long-term in vitro human primary SSC culture systems, single cell multi-omics, novel gene editing approaches with high safety and efficiency, and translating efficacy from basic research to clinical applications. The present review aims to provide new and overall insights into better understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying cell deaths of SSCs and the pathogenesis of non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA), which could offer novel strategies for precise treatment of male infertility.

Introduction

Spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) are the adult stem cells capable of lifelong self-renewal, and they form the cellular basis of spermatogenesis. These cells are located at the basement membrane of the seminiferous tubules, and they can self-renew, differentiate, and undergo cell death to maintain the normal spermatogenesis. Precise regulation of SSC fate decisions is critical for retaining male reproduction. Upon receiving signals from the microenvironment or niche, SSCs must maintain a dynamic balance among self-renewal, differentiation, and cell death (Fig. 1). Excessive self-renewal of SSCs can lead to the accumulation of undifferentiated spermatogonia and, in some cases, germ cell tumors, while differentiation or cell death exceeds the capacity for self-renewal compensation of SSCs and thus leads to irreversible depletion of the stem cell pool. The maintenance of this balance is facilitated by the synergistic interaction of intrinsic genetic programs and extrinsic microenvironmental factors. The precise regulation of cell death pathways is essential for maintaining homeostasis of SSCs for stem cell pool. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms that govern SSC cell death is imperative for elucidating the fundamental principles of spermatogenesis and for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting spermatogenesis disorders [1–5].

Cell death is a pivotal mechanism for maintaining homeostasis in the body, and it usually includes apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis. Apoptosis is known as the programmed cell death, while pyroptosis is another cell death resulted from inflammation. The process of apoptosis is initiated by a series of protease reactions within the cascades of the caspase family, which results in the condensation of the cytoplasm and nuclei to maintain the integrity of the plasma membrane. Pyroptosis is initiated by the activation of inflammatory caspases, which cleaves proteins such as Gasdermin D (GSDMD). The N-terminal domain of GSDMD is responsible for the formation of pores in the plasma membrane, resulting in the release of pro-inflammatory factors, including mature IL-1β, and the subsequent occurrence of cell lysis. Ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, which is arisen from the failure of antioxidant defence mechanisms, e.g., XCT and GPX4 [6]. Cuproptosis is a newly discovered cell death where copper ions accumulate in mitochondria, triggering proteotoxic stress and leading to cellular collapse [7].

In this review, we systematically discuss the molecular mechanisms underlying the cell deaths of SSCs, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, and we integrate the regulatory networks of microenvironmental signals and systemic factors. Molecular mechanisms underlying various kinds of cell deaths in SSCs

Apoptosis of SSCs

Apoptosis is mediated by the mitochondrial pathway and the death receptor pathway. The mitochondrial pathway is regulated by the BCL-2 protein family, which comprises three functional subgroups, including BH3-only proteins (e.g., BIM, BID, and PUMA), anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BCL-2, BCL-xL, and MCL-1), and pro-apoptotic effector proteins (e.g., BAX and BAK). When cells encounter DNA damage, growth factor/cytokine deprivation, or oncogene activation, BH3-only proteins are activated. Inhibiting anti-apoptotic proteins or directly activating pro-apoptotic effector proteins BAX/BAK causes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, which leads to the release of cytochrome c and SMAC and activates Caspase-9 and downstream effector caspases. In the death receptor pathway (e.g., Fas, TNFR1), ligand binding triggers the recruitment and activation of Caspase-8 via adaptor proteins such as FADD. Both pathways ultimately converge on effector caspases, which cleaves numerous protein substrates to execute cell death [6, 8, 9]. Multi-level molecular regulatory network of SSC apoptosis

Transcription factor networks play pivotal roles in the regulation of SSC apoptosis. As a key sensor of the DNA damage response, p53 is phosphorylated at Ser15 and translocates into cellular nuclei by ionizing radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSB). This results in the transcriptional activation of pro-apoptotic target genes, including Puma, Noxa, and Bax, thereby driving the activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway [10]. It has been reported that PUMA is a key factor in maintaining the genomic integrity of mouse SSC precursors and its deficiency significantly reduces germ cell apoptosis caused by radiation [10]. E2F1 has been observed to inhibit apoptosis by maintaining the SSC pool [46]. Furthermore, Nkapl acts as Notch signaling inhibitor to form a co-repressive complex with CIR, HDAC3, and CSL and suppress the transcription of Notch target genes, e.g., Hes1 and Hes5 [11]. Nkapl deficiency leads to abnormal activation of Notch signaling, disrupting the balance between SSC maintenance and differentiation, and triggering apoptosis [11]. PLZF has been shown to maintain the quiescent state of undifferentiated spermatogonia, including SSCs, by recruiting histone deacetylases and nuclear co-repressors [47]. We and peers have demonstrated that the forkhead box transcription factor family FOXP3 and FOXP4 maintain SSC homeostasis by regulating proliferation-related genes and apoptosis pathways [48, 49]. FOXP3 variants have been shown by us to be closely associated with NOA [48]. Transcription factor TCF3 stimulates human SSC proliferation and inhibits apoptosis by directly binding to the promoters of PODXL [50], while its expression is significantly downregulated in patients with NOA [50]. Under hypoxic conditions, the transcription factor FOXA2 activates the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway to regulate the transcription of antioxidant genes, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and inhibiting spermatogonial apoptosis [51]. Furthermore, transcription factor E4F1 regulates energy metabolism by binding to the promoters of mitochondrial respiratory chain genes, whereas its absence leads to cell cycle arrest and the increased apoptosis [52]. In addition, transcription coactivator CITED2 has been shown to regulate HSPA6 expression by interacting with EP300, thereby activating the MAPK pathway to maintain cell survival [12]. Notably, we have recently reported that YAP1 forms a complex by interacting with RAD21, binds to the NEDD4 gene promoter to regulate its transcription, thereby inhibiting apoptosis of human SSCs [53]. Silencing of YAP1 or its S227L mutation leads to downregulation of NEDD4 expression, which results in the enhanced apoptosis and the reduced DNA synthesis [53]. Furthermore, YAP1 protein levels are significantly reduced in testicular tissues from patients with NOA [53]. Finally, GPx3 stimulates human SSC proliferation by regulating CXCL10 transcription, and its knockdown impairs DNA synthesis and enhances early apoptosis of these cells [54]. These studies from peers and us demonstrate pivotal roles of transcription factor networks in regulating human SSC apoptosis, since they precisely coordinate decisions regarding cell survival and deaths of SSCs through multi-level transcriptional programs.

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have been identified as critical regulators involved in the fine-tuning of SSC apoptosis. We have discussed the roles of miRNAs in controlling SSC fate decisions [55]. Here we focus on the fine-tuning mechanisms of SSC apoptosis regulation mediated by non-coding RNAs. At the level of microRNAs, miR-10b has been shown to inhibit SSC apoptosis by targeting Kruppel-like factor 4 [56]. Similarly, miR-21 functions as an anti-apoptotic agent to retain the SSC population by affecting transcription factor ETV5 [57]. Furthermore, miR-202 absence has been shown to lead to accelerated cell cycle progression accompanied by a significant increase in apoptosis of SSCs [58], while miR-224 targets DMRT1 to regulate self-renewal of SSCs by the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway [59]. In human SSCs, we have reported that the miR-1908-3p inhibits their apoptosis by targeting KLF2 [60]. With regard to the mechanism of apoptosis inhibition, we have demonstrated that miR-663a targets NFIX and upregulates cell cyclins, thereby suppressing the apoptosis of SSCs [61]. Conversely, miR-31-5p enhances early and late apoptosis by downregulating Cyclin A2 through targeting JAZF1 [62]. In addition, the inhibition of early apoptosis of SSCs is facilitated by the targeting of CBL by miR-122-5p, a process that is subject to competitive regulation by the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) CASC7 [63]. Interestingly, lncRNA ACVR2B-as1 has been observed by us to interact with the glycolytic enzyme ALDOA, thereby inhibiting apoptosis of SSCs by enhancing glycolytic activity [64]. In a similar manner, the lncRNA HOTAIR has been demonstrated to inhibit apoptosis by upregulating NANOS2 expression [65].

Post-transcriptional regulation, facilitated by RNA-binding proteins, is critical for cell fate decisions. Specifically, the DND1 protein has been shown to bind to the UU(A/U) trinucleotide motif in the 3’UTR of target mRNAs [66]. By recruiting the CCR4-NOT deadenylation complex, DND1 protein induces the degradation of these target mRNAs, thereby suppressing the expression of pro-apoptotic and inflammatory factors in male germline stem cells [66]. In contrast, ELAVL2 has been reported to promote the translation of target mRNAs in spermatogonia, including Id4, Taf4b, and Plzf, thereby further activating the ERK and AKT pathways to inhibit apoptosis [67]. Interestingly, ELAVL2 expression is downregulated in patients with NOA [67]. Furthermore, we have recently demonstrated that IGF2BP1 enhances the stability of HMOX1 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner, thereby activating System Xc⁻to suppress the apoptosis of human SSCs [68].

Post-translational modifications of proteins are involved in controlling SSC apoptosis. In particular, the ubiquitin-proteasome system precisely regulates cell fate determinations by controlling the stability of key proteins. Specifically, the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 mediates the degradation of the RNA-binding protein NANOS2 via the endosomal-lysosomal pathway through ubiquitination, thereby lifting NANOS2’s influence on stem cell proliferation and differentiation. Under heat stress conditions, NEDD4 regulates the rapid clearance of stress granules, preventing apoptosis of SSCs caused by their pathological accumulation [69]. Meanwhile, the E3 ubiquitin ligase ASB9 degrades HIF1AN via ubiquitination, ultimately suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis of human SSCs [70]. On the other hand, we have found that the deubiquitinating enzyme USP11 interacts with the transcription factor HOXC5 to maintain its protein stability, thereby activating the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway to inhibit apoptosis of human SSCs [71]. Notably, the REGγ-proteasome system maintains SSC homeostasis by promoting p53 degradation, whereas REGγ deficiency leads to p53 accumulation, which inhibits PLZF transcription and ultimately triggers SSC apoptosis [72].

Phosphorylation plays a pivotal role in mediating the process of apoptosis. Specifically, CDK2 is subject to precise regulation by phosphorylation at Tyr15, and the absence of this phosphorylation results in increased kinase activity, which in turn blocks the nuclear translocation of the FOXO1 to induce the clearance of SSCs with aberrant proliferation via apoptosis [13]. In a similar manner, MKK7 has been demonstrated to maintain SSC survival by phosphorylating JNKs. The knockdown or inhibition of JNK phosphorylation results in a significant increase in apoptosis [73]. Furthermore, MAP4K4 has been demonstrated by us to regulate the balance between proliferation and apoptosis of human SSCs by phosphorylating JNK [74].

As a central mechanism of epigenetic regulation, histone modifications influence the transcriptional accessibility of apoptosis-related genes by altering chromatin structure. Specifically, the histone methyltransferase PRMT5 maintains the expression of H3K9me2 and H3K27me2 demethylases by catalyzing symmetric H3R2 dimethylation. PRMT5 deficiency leads to the accumulation of repressive histone modifications in the Plzf gene promoter region, resulting in a significant increase in apoptosis of SSCs and G0/G1 phase arrest [75]. Similarly, SETDB1, as an H3K9 methyltransferase, suppresses E2F1 and Nox4 expression through H3K9me3 modification, thereby maintaining ROS homeostasis. Its loss leads to excessive ROS production by NADPH oxidase 4, activating the JNK and p38 MAPK pathways to induce apoptosis of SSCs [76, 77]. On the other hand, histone demethylase KDM1A/LSD1 suppresses abnormal Oct4 expression by removing H3K4me2 modifications from the Oct4 gene promoter, while its loss results in the apoptosis [78]. Similarly, KDM2A/FBXL11 relieves inhibition of PRC2 by removing H3K36me2 modifications from CpG island promoter regions, thereby promoting gene silencing. Its loss leads to the sustained expression of development-related genes to trigger germ cell apoptosis [79]. Furthermore, ESET suppresses Cox4i2 expression through H3K9me3 modification and DNA methylation, and its knockdown activates Caspase 9 and Caspase 3 to induce SSC apoptosis [80].

Protein interactions and receptor-mediated signal transduction regulate SSC apoptosis. Specifically, we have reported that RNF144B regulates membrane receptor signaling through its scaffolding function [81]. RNF144B interacts with FCER2, which in turn binds to the intracellular domain of NOTCH2 (N2ICD) [81]. RNF144B knockdown decreases expression levels of NOTCH2, FCER2, HES1, and HEY1, which results in human SSC apoptosis [81]. Additionally, Leydig cells communicate with SSCs via PTN and its receptor SDC2, activating the HIF-1 signaling pathway and maintaining GFRA1 expression. SDC2 silencing leads to inhibition of the HIF-1 pathway and increases in apoptosis of human SSCs [82]. Similarly, RGS14 interacts with the G protein α-subunit GNAI3 to regulate MAPK signaling activation and PLPP2 expression. RGS14 knockdown results in downregulation of GNAI3 and PLPP2 and the increased apoptosis of human SSCs [83].

During spermatogenesis, three post-translational modifications, namely, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and histone modification, jointly regulate SSC apoptosis by modulating signaling pathways, controlling protein stability, and influencing gene expression, thereby maintaining stem cell homeostasis.

Key signaling pathways involved in the apoptosis of SSCs

The central mechanism underlying SSC apoptosis is the mitochondria-mediated intrinsic apoptosis pathway. Within this pathway, the apoptosis of the cells is determined by Bcl-2 family proteins, which regulates the permeability of the outer mitochondrial membrane. Specifically, the anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL maintain mitochondrial membrane integrity, while the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, Bak, Bim and Puma enhance the release of cytochrome c. It is worth noting that apoptosis of SSCs induced by radiation, chemotherapeutic agents or environmental toxins involve the activation of the mitochondrial pathway. This is manifested by the upregulation of Bax and the downregulation of Bcl-2, resulting in the release of cytochrome c from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm [10, 14, 15, 84–87]. This subsequently activates the Caspase-9 and Caspase-3 cascade reactions [10, 14, 15, 84–87]. On the other hand, astaxanthin inhibits bleomycin-induced oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis by activating the NRF-2/HO-1 pathway to reduce Bax and Caspase-3 expression and increase Bcl-2 [84], while simultaneously modulating ferroptosis-related factors [88]. Ultimately, the activation of the caspase cascades, e.g., the cleaved Caspase-3 as an effector caspase, marks the entry of apoptosis into an irreversible phase [10, 89–91].

Death receptor-mediated extrinsic apoptotic pathways are involved in the clearance of SSCs under specific stress conditions. Upon TLR3 activation, for example, interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) is phosphorylated and forms a TLR3/pIRF3 complex with TLR3 [16]. This complex binds to the FasL promoter, inducing its transcriptional upregulation and thereby promoting SSC apoptosis via the Fas/FasL pathway [16]. Similarly, constitutive activation of NOTCH1 triggers apoptosis in spermatogonia by upregulating the expression of the pro-apoptotic genes Trp53 and Trp63 [17].

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway integrates extracellular signals to regulate SSC apoptosis. Specifically, as a key pro-survival pathway, its activation is regulated by various upstream factors. For instance, SCF/c-Kit binding activates PI3-kinase at the Tyr719 site, thereby activating AKT to maintain SSC survival. Notably, the Y719F mutation reduces AKT activation by 90%, triggering serious apoptosis of SSCs [92]. Concurrently, nonylphenol induces SSC apoptosis by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [85]. The MAPK signaling pathway regulates cell fate via multistep kinase cascades. Specifically, PAK1 is activated via the EGF-EGFR signaling axis. We have demonstrated that PAK1 interacts with PDK1 to activate the ERK1/2 and AKT pathways to inhibit human SSC apoptosis [93]. Moderate activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway is essential for SSC survival, whereas abnormal activation triggers apoptosis. Constitutive activation of WNT/CTNNB1, for example, leads to the abnormal proliferation of SSCs and the depletion of spermatocytes via apoptosis [94]. This is manifested by the CTNNB1-TCF/LEF complex upregulating target genes, thereby inducing apoptosis [94]. Conversely, USP11 suppresses apoptosis of human SSCs by activating the canonical WNT/β-catenin pathway via HOXC5 [71]. Furthermore, miR-322 relieves repression of the WNT/β-catenin pathway by targeting Rassf8, thereby promoting the nuclear translocation of β-catenin and enhancement of downstream target genes to maintain survival [95]. The ER stress pathway activates the apoptotic program when protein homeostasis is disrupted. Bisphenol A induces apoptosis by activating the Atf4/Ddit3-mediated ER stress pathway. Although no difference in Bax/Bcl-2 transcription is detected, Atf4/Ddit3 initiates apoptosis by inducing the expression of pro-apoptotic genes and inhibiting Bcl-2 protein synthesis [14]. Furthermore, palmitic acid induces ROS production through the accumulation of lipids and β-oxidation, triggering ER stress, which leads to DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction [96].

Cell cycle-DNA damage response mechanisms protect SSC genomic integrity. For example, CDKN1C maintains ploidy by regulating the spindle assembly checkpoint, and its loss leads to centromere amplification and premature chromosome separation, ultimately resulting in the elimination of abnormal cells via TRP53-dependent apoptosis [18]. Additionally, Rb1 protects SSCs from DSB damage by regulating E2F1 activity, whereas its deficiency causes excessive E2F1 activation and triggers DNA damage and p53-dependent apoptosis [97]. Similarly, loss of function of Xrcc1, a key scaffold protein in base excision repair, results in the accumulation of DNA damage, subsequently activating the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [15]. In addition, we have reported that OIP5 regulates the levels of cyclins and cell cycle progression, and abnormality in this process leads to G1 phase arrest and induce apoptosis of human SSCs [98].

The interaction between apoptosis and autophagy in SSCs exhibits bidirectional regulation. It can manifest as cell-protective autophagy that inhibits apoptosis, or as cytotoxic autophagy that exacerbates apoptotic damage. Under cryopreservation stress, p53 nuclear translocation simultaneously activates apoptosis and autophagy, whereas the use of the p53 inhibitor pifithrin-µ or the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3MA) to reduce autophagy flux significantly improves the survival rate of SSCs after thawing [99]. This finding indicates that a moderate baseline level of autophagy is critical for the survival of SSCs after thawing, rather than excessive activation or complete inhibition. Consistent with this, Melatonin exerts a protective effect in cryopreservation models through a dual mechanism: on the one hand, it inhibits excessive autophagy by reducing the expression of LC3-I/II, Beclin1, and ATG7, and increasing p62 levels; on the other hand, it blocks the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway by decreasing Bax expression, increasing Bcl-2/Bcl-XL expression, and inhibiting cytochrome c release and Caspase-3 activation, thereby simultaneously regulating both cell fate determination programs [100]. Conversely, under conditions of toxin exposure, autophagy can act as a cytotoxic mechanism that exacerbates apoptosis. Acrylamide induces oxidative stress via the Sp2/HSPH1 signaling pathway, thereby simultaneously activating both autophagy and apoptosis [101]. Furthermore, inhibiting autophagy significantly reduces the extent of apoptosis, reflecting that autophagy exerts a cytotoxic effect under these conditions. Similarly, exposure to HT-2 toxin induces autophagy by activating the AMPK-mTOR-ULK1 pathway, while simultaneously inducing apoptosis via ROS accumulation and mitochondrial damage pathways [90]. The parallel activation of these two processes jointly leads to a decline in cell viability. In summary, the interplay between autophagy and apoptosis in SSCs is context-dependent, and the balance between them determines the cells’ ultimate fates.

Microenvironmental regulation and mechanism integration in apoptosis of SSCs

The aforementioned regulatory network reveals the intrinsic complexity of SSC apoptosis regulation that involves precise coordination at multiple levels, including transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational mechanisms. However, SSC fate determinations are strongly influenced by the testicular microenvironment, or niche. Components of the testicular microenvironment, including Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, peritubular myoid cells (PMCs), growth factors and cytokines, influence SSC apoptosis [19, 69, 82, 84, 102–108]. The testicular microenvironment forms complex paracrine regulatory networks through growth factors, hormones [86, 87, 96, 109], and physicochemical factors [110] secreted by somatic cells, e.g., Sertoli cells and Leydig cells, which determine the fates of SSCs for self-renewal, differentiation, and apoptosis.

The associations between abnormal SSC apoptosis and male reproductive disorders

NOA is the most common outcome of abnormal apoptosis regulation in SSCs. Transcription factors (e.g., YAP1 [53], FOXP3/4 [48, 49], and TCF3 [50]) and transcriptional co-activators (EEF1B2-TAF4B [111]) suppress the apoptosis of SSCs, while signaling molecules (e.g., PAK1 [93], MKK7 [73], RGS14 [83], and USP11 [71]) transmit survival signals through kinase cascades or scaffold protein functions. RNA-binding proteins (ELAVL2 [67], IGF2BP1 [68]) and epigenetic regulators (e.g., MAGEB2 [112], EZH2 [113], and MALAT1 [114]) stabilize target mRNAs or regulate chromatin states. Metabolic and oxidative stress-related molecules (GPx3 [54], ASB9 [70], SPOCD1 [115], NEDD4 [53, 69]) maintain energy homeostasis and protein quality control. Mutations or downregulation of these survival-promoting genes impair the capacity for self-renewal and increase the susceptibility to apoptosis in SSCs. This progressive depletion of the stem cell pool ultimately contributes to NOA.

Chemotherapy-induced spermatogenic dysfunction involves the activation of multiple apoptotic pathways. Busulfan induces an increase in ROS, leading to ATM phosphorylation and p53 activation, which in turn upregulates the transcripts of p16, p19, p21, and p27, while simultaneously downregulating Bcl-2 and upregulating Bax, thereby inducing apoptosis of spermatogonia [87]. Interestingly, melatonin ameliorates busulfan-induced apoptosis by upregulating the expression of MnSOD and SIRT1 to reduce ROS and p53 levels [86]. Furthermore, melatonin reduces the levels of the busulfan-induced DNA damage marker γH2AX [87]. Obesity-associated oligozoospermia is closely linked to insufficient IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling. In mice fed with a high-fat diet, there is decreases in PCNA and Ki-67 expression and increase in Caspase-3 activation and apoptosis [116]. There are other male reproduction disorders, such as c-Kit haploinsufficiency, which causes severe impairment of spermatogenesis, as manifested by the reduced testicular weight, decreased seminiferous tubule diameter, an increased proportion of azoospermic tubules, and decreased male fertility [19]. Loss-of-function in Poc1a causes centrosome dysfunction, which induces cell apoptosis [117]. Xrcc1 deficiency leads to DNA repair defects and activates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, which is associated with male infertility and idiopathic azoospermia [15]. Abnormalities in these factors are associated with germ cell tumors [46, 59, 66].

Ferroptosis of SSCs

Ferroptosis mechanisms in SSCs

Ferroptosis is another kind of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and it was discovered by Dixon et al. in 2012 [28]. The molecular networks underlying ferroptosis spans three biological domains, including metabolic regulation, ROS response, and iron homeostasis. At the level of lipid metabolism, free polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) is catalyzed by acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) to be esterified and incorporated into membrane phospholipids, and thus they acquire their lethal potential [29–31]. Phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) with arachidonic acid (20:4) or adrenic acid (22:4) acyl tails are most closely associated with ferroptosis sensitivity. Regarding amino acid metabolism, cystine is transported into the cells via the System Xc⁻ (SLC7A11/SLC3A2) heterodimer and reduced to cysteine, and then it participates in glutathione (GSH) synthesis [29–31]. GSH acts as a reducing agent for glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and converts phospholipid hydroperoxides into inert alcohols. The antioxidant defense system comprises multiple parallel pathways. In addition to the classic GPX4-GSH axis, FSP1 (ferroptosis suppressor protein 1) utilizes NAD(P)H to reduce CoQ10 (coenzyme Q10) to ubiquinol, which functions as a lipid radical scavenger in extracellular membrane structures, e.g., the plasma membrane. DHODH (dihydroorotate dehydrogenase) and GCH1/BH4 (GTP cyclohydrolase 1/tetrahydrobiopterin) pathways also inhibit ferroptosis through similar mechanisms [29–31]. In the context of iron metabolism, the endocytosis mediated by transferrin (Tf) and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) as well as the degradation of divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) and Ferritin (ferritinophagy) collectively regulate the labile iron pool (LIP) [29–31]. Fe(II) generates hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction to initiate the lipid peroxidation cascade. ER serves as a central hub for lipid peroxidation, and mitochondria participate in the regulation of this process through electron transport chain leakage and the DHODH/CoQ10 axis. Furthermore, ether lipid synthesis in peroxisomes, iron storage in lysosomes, and stress response in the Golgi apparatus can influence the ferroptosis process. In terms of therapeutic interventions, stimuli include System Xc⁻ inhibitors, direct GPX4 inhibitors, GPX4 degradation inducers, and endoperoxides, whereas inhibitors comprise iron chelators, free radical-scavenging antioxidants, and lipoxygenase (LOX) inhibitors [29–31].

As an iron-dependent and lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death (RCD), ferroptosis has been shown to be involved in the onset and progression of various male reproductive disorders, including oligoasthenozoospermia, azoospermia, and testicular damage caused by environmental factors, e.g., cadmium (Cd), busulfan, smoking, and PM2.5. Current research primarily focuses on three core mechanisms, including abnormal iron metabolism characterized by downregulation of ferroportin and iron overload, dysfunction of the cysteine/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant axis, and lipid peroxidation-mediated membrane damage of testicular cells. Mechanisms of ferroptosis in SSCs

The regulatory mechanism of ferroptosis in SSCs primarily involves the NUPR1-MYC-GPX4 axis at the transcriptional level [32]. In patients with NOA, transcription factor MYC binds to the NUPR1 promoter to activate its transcription, thereby maintaining GPX4 expression and the uptake of cystine via xCT/SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 to suppress ferroptosis [32]. NUPR1 knockdown accelerates the degradation of GPX4, NRF2, and KEAP1, leading to elevated ROS level, lipid peroxidation, and Fe²⁺ accumulation, ultimately inducing ferroptosis in SSCs [32].

In spermatogonia including SSCs, ferroptosis is regulated by multiple mechanisms. Firstly, under busulfan-induced testicular injury, iron uptake is enhanced by upregulating TFR, ZIP14/SLC39A14, and DMT1/SLC11A2. It simultaneously activates HO-1 to degrade heme and release free iron, which leads to iron overload, GSH depletion, GPX4 downregulation, the accumulation of lipid peroxides, and upregulated xCT/SLC7A11 expression [33]. Notably, ACTRT2 deficiency increases sensitivity to the labile iron pool by upregulating ACSL4, ALOX15, and IREB2 and downregulating GPX4 [34]. Furthermore, the herbicide diquat induces ferroptosis in spermatogonia via HO-1-mediated oxidative stress and mitochondrial morphological changes, while ZnPP (zinc protoporphyrin IX) blocks this process and Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) restores elevated HO-1 level and prevents spermatogonial death [35].

Pyroptosis of SSCs

Pyroptosis is newly discovered form of programmed cell death mediated by inflammatory caspases, inflammasome formation, and the Gasdermin protein family, as characterized by cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The molecular mechanisms underlying pyroptosis include the canonical pathway and non-canonical pathway. Sensors, e.g., NLRP1/3, NLRC4, AIM2, or pyrin recognize PAMPs/DAMPs, assemble into inflammasomes with ASC and pro-caspase-1, and the activated caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to form membrane pores and release IL-1β/IL-18 [38, 39]. In the non-canonical pathway, LPS binds to the CARD domain of Caspase-4/5/11, which cleaves GSDMD, induces K+ efflux, and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome [38, 39]. In the Caspase-3/8-mediated pathway, chemotherapeutic agents activate Caspase-3 to cleave GSDME, or under hypoxic conditions, nuclear translocation of PD-L1 synergizes with phos-Stat3 to promote GSDMC transcription, followed by TNF-α-activated Caspase-8 cleavage to induce pyroptosis [38, 39]. In the granzyme-mediated pathway, GzmA cleaves GSDMB, while GzmB cleaves GSDME or triggers pyroptosis via the Caspase-3/GSDME axis [38, 39].

Pyroptosis mechanisms have been uncovered in germ cell damage under pathological conditions, such as testicular inflammation, varicocele (VC), and exposure to environmental toxins. NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis has been associated with male infertility [40]. Levels of pyroptosis markers, including NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 are significantly elevated in the semen of patients with varicocele [40]. Furthermore, a decrease in these markers following varicocele surgery is accompanied by the improved semen quality, reflecting that pyroptosis contributes to the formation of the local inflammatory microenvironment in the testis [40]. Pyroptosis causes the release of IL-1β/IL-18 through GSDMD-mediated pore formation to exacerbate male germ cell loss and blood-testis barrier (BTB) disruption in testicular damage induced by environmental pollutants (e.g., cadmium, Cd), microplastics, and other environmental pollutant [41]. Furthermore, there is association between NLRP3 inflammasome activation and male subfertility with elevated levels of IL-1β, ASC, and NLRP3 in the semen of patients with varicocele [42]. Notably, we have recently demonstrated that LPS induces pyroptosis of human SSCs [43]. Upon LPS stimulation, GBP4 oligomerizes and interacts with NLRP3 to promote NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, which in turn activates Caspase-1 (CASP1) to cleave GSDMD and release its N-terminal domain (N-GSDMD), which leads to membrane perforation, cell swelling and rupture, and the release of intracellular contents of human SSCs [43]. Simultaneously, activated CASP1 cleaves the pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 precursors into their mature forms to trigger an inflammatory cascades [43]. Silencing GBP4 significantly suppresses NLRP3 expression of human SSCs, reduces ROS production, decreases IL-1β and IL-18 secretion, and attenuates GSDMD- and CASP1-mediated pyroptosis, thereby maintaining human SSC homeostasis [43]. Our study is the first to reveal a critical role of the GBP4/NLRP3 axis in regulating human SSC pyroptosis, providing new insights into the etiology underlying male infertility caused by genital tract infections [43].

Cuproptosis of SSCs

Cuproptosis is a copper-dependent form of regulated cell death that differs from other cell deaths mentioned above. Copper ionophores (e.g., elesclomol or disulfiram) transport Cu²⁺ into the cells, and Cu2⁺ binds to lipoylated mitochondrial enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle), particularly the E2 subunit DLAT (dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase) of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, leading to DLAT oligomerization and protein aggregation. Simultaneously, FDX1, as an upstream regulator of the lipoic acid pathway, contributes to cell death through the loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins that it mediates. This process is not blocked by inhibitors of apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, or oxidative stress, whereas copper chelators can inhibit copper-mediated cell death.

Cuproptosis is a key mechanism by which Cu overload induces male reproductive damage. Clinical studies have shown that copper concentrations in the serum, seminal plasma, and urine of infertile men are significantly higher than those in fertile controls, which are associated with the reduced sperm count and motility [41, 44]. Exposure to copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) and copper sulfate (CuSO₄) can lead to reduced sperm quality, hormonal imbalances, and testicular dysfunction, as characterized by mitochondrial ATP depletion, increased oxidative stress, and germ cell death [41, 44]. Furthermore, copper toxicity can cause transgenerational astenospermia through abnormal immune-inflammatory response and epigenetic modifications [41, 44]. Copper overload can also disrupt the BTB, inhibit the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG axis), and alter the expression profile of non-coding RNAs [41, 44]. Bioinformatics analyses have identified cuproptosis-related genes with diagnostic potential for male infertility [41, 44], which provides a theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for this disease.

Interactions among different types of cell deaths in SSCs

Apoptosis and ferroptosis in SSCs

Apoptosis and ferroptosis in SSCs are interconnected through a common redox signaling pathway. The NRF-2/HO-1 antioxidant axis constitutes a central intersection between these two pathways. Astaxanthin activates this pathway to reduce Bax and Caspase-3 expression while increasing Bcl-2 in the apoptotic cascade [84], and it simultaneously decreases ACSL3, MDA, and iron levels while enhancing GPX4 and FADS2 expression to counteract ferroptosis [88]. This demonstrates that a single antioxidant intervention can concurrently suppress both mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and lipid peroxidation. The cystine-glutathione-GPX4 axis further links these two modalities. We have revealed that IGF2BP1 enhances HMOX1 mRNA stability of human SSCs in an m6A-dependent manner to activate System Xc⁻ and promote glutathione synthesis [68], whereas NUPR1 maintains GPX4 expression and cystine uptake via SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 [32]. Notably, NUPR1 knockdown accelerates GPX4 degradation and elevates ROS and lipid peroxidation to induce ferroptosis [32]. In parallel, busulfan simultaneously activates both death programs through distinct and convergent mechanisms. It increases ROS and upregulates Bax and cytochrome c to induce apoptosis of spermatogonia [87]. Meanwhile, busulfan enhances TFR, ZIP14/SLC39A14, and DMT1/SLC11A2 to promote iron influx, activates HO-1 to degrade heme and release free iron, and leads to iron overload, GSH depletion, GPX4 downregulation, and the accumulation of lipid peroxides, thereby inducing ferroptosis [33]. These observations highlight that busulfan targets the SSC pool through dual vulnerability. Similarly, bisphenol A induces the apoptosis through the ATF4/DDIT3-mediated ER stress pathway. Although no difference in Bax/Bcl-2 transcription is detected, ATF4/DDIT3 initiates apoptosis by inducing the expression of pro-apoptotic genes and inhibiting Bcl-2 protein synthesis [14]. Palmitic acid similarly enhances ROS production through lipid accumulation and β-oxidation, leading to ER stress and apoptosis [96]. The ROS generated through this process coincides with the oxidative requirements for lipid peroxidation in ferroptosis. Collectively, these findings indicate that ROS generation, antioxidant depletion, and shared dependence on mitochondrial and ER integrity function as the integrated signal nodes where apoptotic and ferroptotic cascades converge in SSCs.

Apoptosis and pyroptosis in SSCs

Apoptosis and pyroptosis are not independent programs in SSCs. Instead, they share executioner caspases that can direct cells toward either membrane blebbing or inflammatory lysis. Cleaved caspase-3 functions as an effector caspase that drives apoptosis into its irreversible phase [89]. However, Caspase-3 can also cleave GSDME to form membrane pores and shift the death program toward pyroptosis [38, 39]. Similarly, Caspase-8 propagates the extrinsic apoptotic cascade, while it can alternatively cleave GSDMD or GSDMC under inflammatory conditions to induce pyroptotic rupture [38, 39]. At the post-transcriptional level, DND1 suppresses both pro-apoptotic and inflammatory factors by recruiting the CCR4-NOT deadenylation complex to degrade target mRNAs [66]. This positions DND1 as a dual negative regulator of both death programs. Inflammatory stimulus LPS simultaneously engages in these pathways. In SSCs, LPS upregulates the pro-apoptotic factor caspase-3 and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome through the TLR4 receptor [118]. Simultaneously, we have found that LPS induces pyroptosis in human SSCs via GBP4 oligomerization and CASP1-dependent GSDMD cleavage [43]. Furthermore, LPS stimulates Sertoli cells to upregulate miR-187 and increase FASLG expression [119], which can engage in the Fas/FasL death receptor pathway to promote apoptosis in SSCs [16]. These convergent observations demonstrate that inflammatory stimuli and shared caspase machineries can simultaneously activate apoptotic and pyroptotic cascades in SSCs, and that the death receptor signaling amplified by the inflammatory microenvironment serves as a bridge between the two modalities.

Regulation of SSC deaths by the microenvironment

Currently significant progress has been made in research on the SSC microenvironment or niche, with a primary focus on its components, e.g., Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, peritubular myoid cells, macrophages, and vascular endothelial cells. The niche of testes regulates SSC self-renewal and differentiation through paracrine factors, e.g., GDNF, FGF2, and SCF, as well as intercellular contacts [120]. Nevertheless, existing studies lack a systematic and the in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms by which the testicular niche regulates SSC deaths. Here we addressed how microenvironmental signals precisely regulate SSC apoptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis, and we discussed the association between microenvironmental dysregulation and male infertility, thereby providing a new theoretical framework for fertility preservation. Regulation of SSC deaths by Sertoli cells and their paracrine factors

Sertoli cells regulate SSC apoptosis via paracrine factors, with WNT4 and SCF constituting pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic pathways, respectively. Specifically, activation of CTNNB1 in Sertoli cells induces ectopic expression of WNT4 which acts on SSCs to trigger apoptosis via the non-canonical WNT pathway [121]. On the other hand, β-estradiol binds to the ERβ receptor on Sertoli cells to upregulate SCF expression, which is accompanied by a reduced apoptosis rate in human SSCs [122]. Furthermore, disruption of the microenvironment leads to secondary apoptosis. For example, CXCL12 and CCL9 expression is significantly reduced in Sertoli cells of Gata4 conditional knockout (cKO) mice, and SSCs lose microenvironmental support and undergo apoptosis [123]. It is important to note that this apoptosis is a secondary consequence of microenvironmental disruption rather than an apoptosis signal actively induced by Sertoli cells [123]. Concurrently, LPS induces immature porcine Sertoli cells to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulate miR-187, thereby increasing FASLG expression [119]. It is worth noting that co-culture of porcine spermatogonia with Sertoli cells reduces the apoptosis rate [136], reflecting that Sertoli cells as an important component of the niche for SSCs are essential for their anti-apoptosis.

GDNF deficiency leads to SSC depletion primarily due to the impaired self-renewal or increased apoptosis [137]. Specifically, GDNF derived from Sertoli cells regulates SSC self-renewal through two downstream pathways, including the PI3K/Akt pathway and the Src family of kinases. Inhibition of Akt signaling results in a significant increase in the proportion of SSCs undergoing apoptosis, whereas inhibition of SFK does not increase their apoptosis [124]. Furthermore, GDNF upregulates POU3F1 expression via the PI3K/Akt pathway to maintain SSC survival, while Pou3f1 silencing increases the apoptosis rate of these cells [107]. In contrast, FGF2 deficiency depletes the SSC pool via a “self-renewal to differentiation” shift, which is independent of apoptosis regulation. For example, there is no significant difference in apoptosis rates in cryptorchidism patients [138]. Similarly, Mast4-knockout mice exhibit failure of SSC self-renewal mediated by the FGF2/ERM pathway, and increased apoptosis is a secondary consequence of SSC depletion [139]. Furthermore, MEHP specifically inhibits the GDNF/ERK1/2/FOS signaling pathway to reduce cell proliferation and induce cell death of SSCs [125].

Hormones on SSC deaths

Testosterone and the SSC microenvironment on SSC apoptosis

Current evidence suggests that testosterone regulates the fate decisions of SSCs through indirect mechanisms since SSCs do not express AR. In a mouse model of adolescent DEHP exposure, a dose-dependent decrease in plasma testosterone levels has been found to be closely associated with the impaired SSC self-renewal [140]. This mechanism involves the inhibition of DEHP in the expression of steroidogenic genes in Leydig cells, such as Star and Cyp17a1, leading to reduced testosterone synthesis [140]. Subsequent testosterone deficiency impairs the function of Sertoli cells and peritubular myoid cells (PMCs) to disrupt the homeostasis of the SSC microenvironment, as evidenced by significant downregulation of both GDNF-dependent (Bcl6b, Etv5) and non-GDNF-dependent (Plzf, Taf4b) SSC self-renewal genes [140]. Busulfan can reduce serum testosterone levels and induce Leydig cell apoptosis [141]. Interestingly, this study indicates that vitamin B12 reduces the number of TUNEL-positive germ cells and stimulates spermatogonial proliferation [141], suggesting that vitamin B12 may influence SSC apoptosis by improving testis microenvironment.

Melatonin effect on SSC apoptosis

Melatonin has been shown to effectively protect SSCs from deaths through multiple signaling pathways by inhibiting ER stress, counteracting oxidative stress, and maintaining mitochondrial functions. LPS enhances ES stress in SSCs via the TLR4 receptor to upregulate ERS markers (GRP78, CHOP) and the pro-apoptotic factor Caspase-3 and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome to release inflammatory factors, such as IL-6 and NF-κB [118]. Melatonin intervention significantly suppresses the expression of the ERS-related proteins and inflammatory factors [118]. Melatonin exerts a protective effect on cell apoptosis via the MT1/MT2 receptor-SIRT1-p53 axis [86]. Upon binding to its receptors, melatonin upregulates SIRT1 expression, promotes p53 deacetylation, and enhances its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby reducing p53 protein levels. Simultaneously, melatonin upregulates MnSOD to scavenge excess ROS, blocks the ROS-p38-p53 apoptotic pathway, and protects mitochondrial membrane potential [86]. Regarding the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy) and apoptosis, melatonin protects SSCs by inhibiting ROS/PINK1-Parkin-mediated excessive mitochondrial autophagy in cryopreservation models [126]. Cryopreservation induces excessive ROS production and PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitochondrial autophagy activation, which is accompanied by the loss of SSC markers (PLZF, UCHL1) and increased apoptosis [126]. Melatonin maintains tissue structure, inhibits ROS accumulation, restores redox balance, and reduces cell apoptosis by inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway [126]. In a goat SSC cryopreservation model, melatonin inhibits the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway through multi-level antioxidant defenses [100]. Melatonin significantly improves the viability of freeze-thawed SSCs, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and mitochondrial membrane potential, while reducing ROS and MDA levels [100]. Melatonin upregulates the activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px), inhibits the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, increases the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2/Bcl-XL, reduces mitochondrial damage, and decreases cytochrome c release and Caspase-3 activation [100].

Estrogen effect on SSC apoptosis

The mechanism by which estrogen signaling regulates SSC apoptosis has not yet been fully elucidated, and it likely acts primarily on Sertoli cells. It has been shown that DEHP exposure can increase the expression levels of the estrogen receptor ERβ and the nuclear receptors Nr0b1/Nr0b2, while simultaneously suppressing the expression of the steroidogenic inducer Nr5a2 [140]. This suggests that estrogens may influence microenvironmental function by disrupting receptor signaling networks, thereby indirectly affecting the deaths of SSCs.

Hormones and the microenvironmental scaffold in SSC deaths

It has been reported that the combined use of melatonin and three-dimensional decellularized testicular matrix can synergistically promote SSC survival and differentiation [142]. In this study, treatment with either melatonin or ECM alone reduces germ cell apoptosis, while the combined treatment leads to the higher SSC viability. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this synergistic effect on SSC deaths require further investigation.

Physical and chemical stress in SSC apoptosis

Radiation specifically causes SSC apoptosis via the Trp53-Trp53inp1-Tnfrsf10b pathway. Radiation activates p53 which subsequently upregulates Trp53inp1 expression, ultimately increasing the expression of the death receptor Tnfrsf10b (DR5) for sensitizing SSCs to the TRAIL-mediated extrinsic apoptosis pathway [127]. Although radiation simultaneously activates the endogenous apoptosis pathway mediated by BBC3 (PUMA), this pathway only protects spermatogonial progenitor cells and fails to prevent SSC apoptosis, revealing a unique DNA damage response mechanism in SSCs [127].

Environmental toxins regulate the fate determinations of SSCs through various mechanisms. Among them, hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] triggers the oxidative stress and mitochondrial apoptosis pathway where it generates excessive ROS, leading to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, which in turn promotes the release of cytochrome c and activates the caspase cascade [128]. This process depends on P53 phosphorylation, and SSCs are more sensitive to Cr(VI) than somatic cells [128]. In contrast, bisphenol A induces ER stress-related apoptosis by upregulating ATF4/DDIT3 (CHOP) [14]. On the other hand, silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) block GDNF downstream PI3K/Akt-N-Myc by inhibiting the Fyn kinase, and they inhibit proliferation independently of apoptosis at low concentrations [143].

Chemoprotective agents can effectively antagonize apoptosis. For example, lovastatin significantly downregulates pro-apoptotic genes Bax/Fadd, upregulates the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, and inhibits Caspase-3 activation by blocking the synthesis of downstream products of the mevalonate pathway [104]. In contrast, resveratrol exhibits concentration-dependent bidirectional regulation in SSC apoptosis. At low concentrations (2 µM), resveratrol maintains mitochondrial integrity and upregulates the anti-apoptotic BCL2 via the SIRT1-FOXO1 axis [129]. Conversely, it induces DNA breaks and promotes apoptosis of SSCs at high concentrations (200 µM) [129].

Metabolites and redox state on SSC deaths

ROS exerts bidirectional regulation on the fate decisions of SSCs. Moderate levels of ROS maintain SSC self-renewal, whereas excessive ROS trigger the apoptotic program. Moderate hypoxia maintains proper ROS levels by upregulating ABCG2 expression, thereby promoting SSC proliferation [110]. Conversely, inhibition of ABCG2 leads to ROS accumulation, which in turn upregulates pro-apoptotic genes (Bax, p53) and downregulates the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 to increase the risk of apoptosis [110]. Complementarily, Gln supports SSC self-renewal by maintaining NOX1-derived ROS. Gln deficiency causes elevated total ROS level and reduced GSH synthesis, ultimately activating the TP53-dependent apoptotic pathway [130]. Existing evidence indicates that NOX1 knockout significantly attenuates apoptosis of SSCs, implicating that NOX1-ROS is an important mediator of apoptosis induction [130]. Conversely, Asn supplementation rescues apoptosis of SSCs by supporting GSH synthesis [130]. Furthermore, ascorbic acid influences the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to reduce ROS and apoptosis rates by downregulating Bax and upregulating Bcl-2 gene expression [20]. MiR-30a-5p reduces ROS and MDA levels in freeze-thawed SSCs, upregulates SOD activity, and regulates Bax/Bcl-2 expression to inhibit apoptosis [131].

Imbalances in amino acid metabolism are linked to the regulation of apoptosis. In addition to Gln, alanine deficiency depletes pyruvate, thereby activating Trp53-dependent apoptosis via an energy crisis, and conversely, pyruvate supplementation can sustain long-term culture and reproductive function [132]. Additionally, in testicular ischemia-reperfusion injury, EGR1 alleviates oxidative stress, maintains the integrity of the BTB, and inhibits SSC apoptosis by increasing the expression of its target gene Nudt16L1 [133].

Cell-cell contact and the ECM effect on SSC deaths

In a 3D culture system and co-culture with Sertoli cells, the addition of laminin significantly reduces apoptosis in human SSCs. Following the addition of laminin and growth factors, the expression of hallmarks for undifferentiated spermatogonia, including PLZF, and α6-integrin, as well as the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, is increased, whereas the level of the pro-apoptotic gene Bax and the differentiation marker c-Kit is decreased [134]. This suggests that laminin and SSCs-Sertoli cells contact exert a protective effect on SSC apoptosis through its receptors [134]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms of apoptosis inhibition remain to be elucidated. Complementing these findings, an alginate hydrogel 3D system combined with Sertoli cell co-culture reduces pro-apoptotic gene expression and increases stemness [144]. The 3D-cultured SSCs exhibit a higher colonization efficiency after transplantation into recipient mice without male germ cells [144]. Furthermore, SSCs are dependent on continuous physical contact with Sertoli cells. When Sertoli cells are forced to contract, SSCs exhibit characteristics of apoptotic sequence. Following vigorous oscillatory movements, SSCs release apoptotic bodies and cease activity. Notably, Fas-activating antibodies can induce the same apoptotic phenotype [135], suggesting that morphological changes in Sertoli cells may trigger apoptosis of SSCs by activating the Fas signaling pathway. Apoptotic cells also exhibit disruption of the actin cytoskeleton, indicating that cytoskeletal integrity is a structural foundation for SSC survival [135].

Comparative perspectives on cell death mechanisms among SSCs, embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)

Although SSCs, ESCs, and iPSCs share core cell death machinery, distinct mechanistic differences reflect their unique developmental contexts and microenvironmental dependencies. For apoptosis, human ESCs constitutively maintain active Bax in its pre-activated conformation at the Golgi apparatus, and DNA damage triggers p53-dependent translocation of this active Bax to the mitochondria, thereby priming human ESCs for rapid mutation elimination during early embryonic development [145]. Upon differentiation, Bax is no longer active, and ESCs lose this acute sensitivity to DNA damage [145]. Mouse ESCs employ a distinct SIRT1-p53 axis as evidenced by the fact that SIRT1 functions as a p53 deacetylase and blocks nuclear translocation of cytoplasmic p53 in response to endogenous ROS [146]. In wild-type mouse ESCs, ROS triggers mitochondrial translocation of p53 and BAX to induce mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis, whereas in SIRT1-deficient mouse ESCs, p53 translocates to the nuclei and suppresses Nanog expression [146]. Furthermore, p53 and p73 are required for DNA damage-induced apoptosis in mouse ESCs [147]. Notably, the generation of iPSCs from somatic cells involves reprogramming factor-induced oxidative stress and p53-dependent apoptosis, a process that is not relevant to SSCs [148]. In contrast, SSC apoptosis is initiated by stress-induced upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins and downregulation of Bcl-2, which is coupled with the cues from the testicular niche, including GDNF/PI3K/AKT signaling and death receptor activation.

With regard to ferroptosis, human ESCs are highly prone to dissociation-induced ferroptosis, which is mediated by iron accumulation, a decrease of GPX4, and p53-mediated repression of SLC7A11 [149]. Nrf2 inhibits ferroptosis by controlling iron metabolism, antioxidant defense, and mitochondrial function via downstream targets including SLC7A11 and GPX4 [150]. In dissociated human ESCs, SLC7A11 and GPX4 are downregulated by Nrf2, and their ferroptosis is suppressed by ferrostatin-1 and deferoxamine [149]. In contrast, SSC ferroptosis is triggered by environmental toxicants, e.g., busulfan and cadmium, through the NUPR1-MYC-GPX4 axis, whereas mechanical dissociation does not induce ferroptosis in SSCs.

Additionally, human ESCs undergo dissociation-induced apoptosis via ROCK-dependent hyperactivation of actomyosin, which is triggered by loss of E-cadherin-dependent intercellular contact and regulated by the upstream factor Abr [151]. This cell death is efficiently suppressed by blebbistatin and Y-27,632, which is not observed in mouse ESCs [151]. SSCs are tissue stem cells that do not undergo enzymatic dissociation in vivo, and thus this culture-specific vulnerability is irrelevant to their biology. Collectively, these comparisons highlight that the initiating triggers, upstream regulatory mechanisms, and microenvironmental dependencies of cell deaths in stem cells are highly cell-type-specific although central death pathways are conserved across different stem cell types.

Prospects for targeted intervention strategies and translational research on SSC deaths

Targeting key signaling pathways or remodeling the testicular microenvironment offers important strategic directions for intervening in SSC depletion due to excessive deaths and preserving male fertility. At the intervention level, precise regulation of core pathways, e.g., mitochondrial apoptosis, death receptor signaling, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and WNT/β-catenin, is expected to restore the dynamic balance between SSC self-renewal and differentiation and inhibit the activation of pathological apoptosis. Importantly, several strategies have already demonstrated preclinical efficacy in animal models. Pharmacological targeting of mitochondrial oxidative stress has proven protective in busulfan-induced apoptosis models. Astaxanthin relieves busulfan-induced SSC apoptosis by activating the Nrf-2/HO-1 pathway. It reduces ROS and MDA levels, downregulates pro-apoptotic Bax, and restores Bcl-2 expression [84]. Melatonin ameliorates busulfan-induced cell apoptosis through the MT1/MT2-SIRT1-p53 axis and MnSOD-mediated ROS scavenging [86]. It also reduces busulfan-induced DNA damage, as evidenced by the decrease in γH2AX level [87]. Beyond antioxidant intervention, metabolic pathway modulation represents another effective approach. Lovastatin, a clinically approved statin, promotes SSC self-renewal and suppresses cell apoptosis in busulfan-treated mice [104]. It inhibits the mevalonate pathway and reduces downstream pro-apoptotic signals, including Bax, Fadd, and cleaved Caspase-3 [104]. This approach offers a higher safety than growth factor-based therapies. In models of obesity-associated oligozoospermia, exogenous IGF-1 enhances PCNA and Ki-67 expression and reduces Caspase-3 activation [116]. These findings demonstrate that the proposed pathways can be pharmacologically modulated to protect SSCs against cell death in vivo. However, the optimal dosing, route of administration, and long-term safety remain to be established in non-human primates and human clinical trials.

Regarding microenvironment remodeling, optimizing the supportive function of Sertoli cells, regulating the paracrine factor networks of Leydig cells, improving the homeostasis of the immune microenvironment, and intervening in stress-inducing cell deaths, e.g., oxidative stress and ER stress, provide diverse strategies for establishing a niche conducive to SSC survival and anti-deaths. The 3D culture platforms have proven effective for microenvironment reconstruction. Co-culture of SSCs with Sertoli cells in alginate hydrogels significantly reduces apoptosis and enhances stemness marker expression, including Bcl2, integrin α6, Nanog, Plzf, and UCHL1. It also reduces pro-apoptotic proteins P53, Fas, and Bax [17]. Human testicular decellularized matrix combined with melatonin synergistically decreases male germ cell apoptosis and promotes SSC differentiation [142]. In addition, laminin supplementation in 3D co-culture systems increases Bcl2 and reduces Bax through α6-integrin signaling [134]. Under immune-inflammatory conditions, melatonin suppresses LPS-induced ER stress and inflammation in SSCs [118]. We have recently demonstrated that GBP4 silencing attenuates LPS-induced pyroptosis by blocking NLRP3 assembly and reducing IL-1β and IL-18 release [43]. These microenvironment-focused strategies are particularly attractive because they do not require direct genetic manipulation of SSCs. Instead, they create a protective extracellular environment. These approaches may be more feasible for clinical fertility preservation, such as testicular tissue transplantation or in vitro maturation systems for prepubertal cancer patients.

On the other hand, current translational research still faces numerous technical bottlenecks that hinder the advancement for translating our findings into clinical practice. Firstly, the stability and functionality of long-term in vitro human primary SSC culture systems remain a major obstacle. Human SSCs account for only a small fraction of male germ cells and somatic cells within the testis. Their survival and self-renewal abilities depend on feeder cell requirement and cytokine cocktails. While 3D systems improve SSC viability, the long-term maintenance of genetic and epigenetic fidelity in cultured human SSCs has not been established. This raises concerns about whether expanded cells have normal spermatogenesis after transplantation. Secondly, the safety and efficiency of precision gene editing technologies raise serious concerns. Our observations such as YAP1/RAD21/NEDD4 [53] and IGF2BP1/HMOX1 [68] offer attractive gene therapy targets for NOA patients due to gene mutations causing SSC apoptosis or ferroptosis. However, off-target gene editing in germline cells could introduce heritable mutations with unpredictable consequences for offspring. Moreover, excessive activation of self-renewal pathways, such as GDNF overexpression, carries the risk of germ cell tumor formation. This necessitates stringent preclinical safety profiling. The regulatory frameworks globally impose strict restrictions on germline genome editing. Clinical gene therapy for SSCs therefore remains a prospect with safety evaluation. Thirdly, allogeneic SSC transplantation confronts immune rejection and ethical barriers. The testis possesses immune privilege that permits xenogeneic colonization in experimental settings. However, allogeneic transplantation in humans would likely require prolonged immunosuppression. This carries attendant risk of infection and malignancy. Autologous transplantation using cryopreserved prepubertal testicular tissues is a promising alternative. Nevertheless, it is unavailable for patients with congenital SSC defects or prior testicular damage. Fourthly, the translational gap from animal models to human patients remains to be determined. Murine and human spermatogenesis differ significantly in SSC kinetics, differentiation patterns, and microenvironment composition. The majority of protective strategies have been validated primarily in rodents. Primate data and human clinical evidence are virtually rare. Standardized preclinical evaluation pipelines, including non-human primate validation and human tissue xenografts, are urgently required. Finally, cryopreservation and tissue engineering face practical hurdles. Cryopreservation of testicular tissues triggers oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive mitophagy. This leads to substantial SSC loss. Although antioxidants improve post-thaw viability, long-term spermatogenic recovery rates after transplantation remain to be assessed. Graft fibrosis further compromises functional outcomes of spermatogenesis [126].

In the future, it will be necessary to integrate multi-omics technologies to deepen mechanistic understanding of cell deaths, employ modern approaches, such as testis organoids, establish the standardized preclinical evaluation systems, and promote interdisciplinary collaboration, with the ultimate goal to close the gap between basic research and clinical applications. Single-cell multi-omics have already begun to transform SSC research. They resolve cellular heterogeneity at high resolution. Interestingly, CITED2 [12], EEF1B2 [111], and the NUPR1-MYC ferroptosis axis [32] has been identified by scRNA-seq. Future integration of scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, single-cell proteomics, and metabolomics will enable the construction of dynamic regulatory maps of cell deaths in SSCs. These maps can chart SSC vulnerability to each mode of cell death. Spatial transcriptomics will add the dimension of tissue architecture. It allows researchers to map paracrine signaling, including GDNF, FGF2, and SCF, onto histological testis sections, and it can also identify regional differences in SSC death susceptibility across the seminiferous tubules. Testicular organoids provide a good platform for modeling SSC and somatic cell interactions, screening therapeutics, and assessing toxicity without using animal models. Achieving complete human meiosis and sperm formation in vitro, however, remains a major challenge. At the computational level, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and multi-omics data can accelerate biomarker discovery of cell deaths in SSCs, patient stratification, and new drug development for NOA. Ultimately, the convergence of organoid technology, single-cell and spatial multi-omics, RNA-targeted therapeutics, and computational analytics would support the transition from mechanistic research to precision medicine for male infertility. This can translate the accumulated knowledge of SSC cell death mechanisms into effective clinical strategies for patients with spermatogenic failure.

Conclusions

In summary, we have systematically elucidated the multidimensional mechanisms governing SSC deaths and their dependence on the microenvironment. Regarding the modes of cell death, apoptosis serves as the central regulatory mechanism, mediated by the mitochondrial and death receptor pathways, and it is precisely regulated by multi-level mechanisms including transcription factor networks, non-coding RNAs, RNA-binding proteins, and post-translational modifications. Research on other forms of cell death in SSCs, including ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and copper-mediated cell death, is in progress, and their physiological and pathological significance remains to be further explored. At the microenvironmental regulation level, Sertoli cells and their paracrine factors, hormonal signaling, physio-chemical stress, metabolites and redox status, cell-cell contacts, and the ECM collectively constitute regulatory networks that determine SSC fate decisions, which maintains the homeostasis and deaths of SSCs by integrating endogenous and exogenous signals. Under pathological conditions, disruptions in these mechanisms are closely associated with NOA, cryptorchidism, chemotherapy-induced damage, and metabolic diseases. In the future, targeting key pathways or reshaping the testis microenvironment or niche may offer new strategies for fertility preservation. Meanwhile, technical advancements in areas, e.g., long-term culture system of human primary SSCs, novel gene editing approaches, and multi-omics, need to be developed further for translating the basic research on SSC deaths into clinical applications in reproductive and regenerative medicine.
