Postpartum contraception receipt among Oregon Medicaid-funded births with stimulant and substance use disorder
Authors: Stephan R Lindner, Menolly Kaufman, Ann Martinez Acevedo, Deborah J Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare postpartum contraceptive receipt among (i) Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related substance use disorder (SUD) to those without SUD; and (ii) Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUD to those without SUD.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of birthing events using Oregon’s linked Medicaid claims and vital statistics data 2012–2019 (N = 141,479). We described demographic characteristics, birth outcomes, and substances used, and compared rates of contraception receipt (i) immediately postpartum (IPP), (ii) within 60 days postpartum excluding IPP, and (iii) within 60 days postpartum including IPP.ResultsPrevalence of stimulant-related and other SUD diagnosis was 3.8% and 9.4%, respectively. After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors, contraception receipt within 60 days postpartum including IPP and contraception receipt IPP were greater for birth events with a stimulant-related SUD compared to birth events without SUD (adjusted relative risk [aRR], 60-days postpartum including IPP: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.17; aRR, IPP: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.24–1.40). Adjusted rates of contraception receipt were also elevated for birth events with other SUD compared to birth events with no SUD (aRR, 60-days postpartum including IPP: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.09–1.15; aRR, IPP: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.16–1.26; aRR, 60-days postpartum excluding IPP: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.06–1.13).ConclusionsBirthing people enrolled in Medicaid who misused stimulants were more likely than those without SUD, controlling for relevant factors, to receive postpartum contraception within 60 days due to elevated contraception receipt during the IPP period. Further research may examine whether these findings can be replicated outside of Oregon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02420-y.Keywords: Stimulant use disorder, Substance use disorder, Postpartum, LARC, Contraception, Medicaid

Introduction

Stimulant-related substance use disorder (SUD), which encompasses misuse of prescription drugs and illicit drugs such as cocaine, methamphetamine, and other amphetamines, is a growing concern in the United States (U.S.) [1]. The rise in the misuse of stimulants, particularly the misuse of methamphetamines, has doubled among pregnant people, from 1.2 per 1,000 hospital deliveries in 2008–2009 to 2.4 per 1,000 hospital deliveries in 2014–2015, according to the most recent national estimate [2]. More recent evidence from California further suggests a continued increase between 2012 and 2020 [3]. However, we lack evidence of the association between maternal stimulant use and reproductive health outcomes. This is especially a concern for birthing people enrolled in Medicaid, who tend to be younger and have elevated rates of stimulant-related and other SUD [4, 5].

Stimulant use during pregnancy has been linked to a number of risks to maternal health such as gestational hypertension, placental abruption, and preeclampsia [6]. Further, birthing people with stimulant use disorder are at higher risk of mistimed or unintended pregnancies [7, 8]. In turn, unintended pregnancies are associated with short interpregnancy intervals (< 18 months between a birth and subsequent pregnancy) [9], which increases the risk of adverse birth outcomes [10]. Timely uptake of contraception by the birthing parent, especially during the period immediately following birth, is critical to reducing short interpregnancy intervals, [11, 12] which benefits all birthing people, but particularly those at higher risk for complications, including birthing people who misuse stimulants [7].

Birthing people who misuse stimulants face numerous barriers to timely and consistent prenatal care due to perceived judgment from clinicians and fear of child protective service involvement [13, 14]. This may lead to fewer opportunities to discuss contraceptive choices and preferences and thus lead to lower rates of postpartum contraception use [15].

Although there is a growing body of evidence examining the receipt of postpartum contraception among birthing people with SUD [16, 17], little is known about postpartum contraceptive use among those that misuse stimulants specifically. Our objective was to compare postpartum contraceptive receipt among Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUD to those without SUD. In parallel, we also compared contraception receipt among Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUD to those without SUD. By assessing disparities in contraception receipt across these populations, our study provides guidance whether public health efforts to increase postpartum contraception use should specifically focus on birthing parents with stimulant-related SUD.

Methods

Data

We designed a retrospective cohort study using linked administrative datasets with Oregon Medicaid claims and vital records from 2012 to 2019. We included live hospital births in Medicaid claims data with a matching birth record in the vital statistics dataset. We identified hospital birth events using a previously published algorithm and Current Procedure Terminology (CPT) codes published by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [18, 19]. Medicaid claims and vital records were linked by the Oregon Health Authority’s Integrated Client Services. The Oregon Health & Science University Institutional Review Board approved the study.

Study population

Our analytic sample included individuals with live births aged 15–44 years and births with gestational ages between 24 and 44 weeks [20–23]. We excluded births among recipients who were dual-eligible for Medicaid and Medicare due to unavailability of Medicare claims. We also excluded those with missing maternal county of residence. Additionally, we restricted the sample to live births between January 2013 and October 2019 to allow a one-year pre-period to identify substance use diagnoses in Medicaid claims and 60 days of follow-up postpartum (Supplemental Table 1).

Variables

Our primary exposure was SUD category, which we operationalized into three mutually exclusive groups: (1) stimulant-related SUD; (2) other SUDs; and (3) no evidence of SUD. We did not consider exclusive tobacco use as evidence of SUD but kept it as a covariate given the known impacts of tobacco on birth outcomes [24]. We identified SUD diagnoses using International Classification Diagnosis (ICD) (versions 9 and 10) published by the Center for Medicaid and Medicare Services (Supplemental Table 2) [25]. We identified evidence of substance use as at least one SUD diagnosis from 12 months prior to birth through the childbirth hospitalization. The stimulant group included those misusing cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamines, prescription stimulants, and other stimulants. The ‘other SUD’ group consisted of people misusing alcohol, opioids, sedatives, cannabis, and substances classified as ‘other’ (e.g., hypnotics, inhalants, hallucinogens, sedatives, caffeine, or unspecified/unknown). We included those who had a both a stimulant-related SUD and other types of SUD in the stimulant-related SUD group.

Our outcomes were: (i) contraception receipt immediately postpartum (IPP), defined as receiving contraception during the childbirth hospitalization stay; (ii) contraception receipt after discharge from the childbirth hospitalization through 60 days postpartum (i.e., within 60 days postpartum excluding IPP); and (iii) contraception receipt within 60 days postpartum including IPP.

We allowed for 60 days of follow-up because Oregon’s Medicaid program provide 60 days of postpartum coverage for people that qualify for Medicaid benefits because of pregnancy for the duration of our study period [4]. We identified outpatient postpartum visits in medical claims data following the birth hospitalization discharge using ICD 9 and 10 diagnosis and procedure codes, and CPT codes (Supplemental Table 3). Our definition of contraception included effective long-acting reversible contraceptive methods (LARC; implant or intrauterine device (IUD)), short-acting reversible methods (SARM; pill, patch, ring, injection), and receipt of a sterilization procedure. We excluded methods such as condoms and diaphragms because they cannot be reliably captured in claims data [26]. We identified LARC and sterilization procedures using CPT codes in medical claims and SARM using National Drug Codes (NDC) in pharmacy claims (Supplemental Table 3) [27]. We imputed contraception receipt date using either the date of service in medical claims or the date dispensed in pharmacy claims. We calculated the time between the date of childbirth and the contraception receipt date to categorize each contraception outcome measure by timing (IPP or within 60 days from the childbirth hospitalization discharge date). Secondary outcomes included contraception type (LARC, SARM, or sterilization).

We included a number of demographic, clinical, and birth related factors in our analysis. From vital statistics records, we included the following demographic characteristics: maternal age in years (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, Unknown) maternal residential location (urban, rural, missing), measured using the Rural Urban Commuting Areas (RUCA) codes [28], self-reported maternal race and ethnicity (American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Black, Hawaiian/Pacific-Islander, Hispanic/Latina, White, or Other/Unknown), and maternal education (less than high school, high school graduate or GED, some college, college graduate or more, and unknown). We further included the following clinical and birth related factors, also from vital statistics: number of prenatal visits (0, ≤ 7, >7), parity (multiparous vs. nulliparous), cesarean delivery, infant sex (female, male, or unknown), birthweight (in grams; <1,500, 1,500-2,499, 2,500–3999, and > 4,000), gestational age (in weeks; <34 (early pre-term), 34–36 (late pre-term), and ≥ 37 (term)), NICU admission, pre-pregnancy diabetes, gestational diabetes, pre-pregnancy hypertension, gestational hypertension, and maternal tobacco use (yes, no, missing). We used the cutoff of seven prenatal visits based on recommendations from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [29].

Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis was the birth event. We first conducted descriptive analyses of demographic, clinical, and birth related variables by our exposure variable, SUD category. We tested for differences between Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUD, Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUD, and Medicaid beneficiaries without SUD using Pearson’s Chi-square. We quantified the proportion of births with stimulant-related SUD that used multiple substances during the pre-conception and prenatal periods and reported each substance used. Next, we examined unadjusted rates of postpartum visit attendance and contraception receipt across SUD categories and tested for differences across the three groups using Pearson’s Chi-square.

To examine differences in contraception receipt across SUD categories, we used multivariable robust Poisson regression and reported adjusted relative risks (aRR) [30]. In these models, we compared (i) Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUDs to Medicaid beneficiaries with no SUD and (ii) Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUDs to Medicaid beneficiaries with no SUD. Those without an SUD diagnosis thus served as a reference group in both regressions. For contraception outcome measures IPP or within 60 days (including IPP), we estimated regressions on the complete study population. For contraception received after the childbirth hospitalization discharge and within 60 days, we excluded those that received contraception IPP.

We selected confounders using a causal model approach, where confounders were based on prior research and a priori assumptions on temporality [31, 32]. In the final models, we adjusted for maternal age, parity, number of prenatal visits, rurality, and year of birth. We included year fixed effects to account for trends in contraception receipt during our study period. Standard errors were clustered at the birthing parent level to account for correlated outcomes of children with the same birthing parent.

We conducted a number of sensitivity analyses. First, our definition of stimulant use included a broad range of stimulants, including cocaine. Prior literature has noted population differences in cocaine versus methamphetamine use [33]. Therefore, we re-ran our model after removing cocaine use from our definition of stimulant use to examine if the associations meaningfully changed. Second, because we did not impose a continuous Medicaid enrollment criterion for inclusion into the study population, we assessed if imposing three-, six-, and nine-month continuous Medicaid enrollment criteria in the prenatal period changed associations. Third, we compared those with stimulant-use disorders to those with other SUD.

All tests of significance were two-sided with an accepted alpha of 0.05. We used t-tests to assess the significance of regressions estimates. We conducted all analyses using R statistical software (4.10).

Results

Our analytic sample included 141,479 births among 109,542 Medicaid beneficiaries with live birth (Fig. 1). Within our analytic sample, 3.8% (N = 5,445) of births had evidence of a stimulant-related SUD, 9.4% (N = 13,275) had evidence of other SUDs, and the remaining 86.8% (N = 122,759) did not have evidence of stimulant–related or other SUDs (Table 1). Compared to those without SUD, people with births that had evidence of a stimulant-related SUD were less likely to live in an urban area (66.4% vs. 75.2%, p < 0.001), have less than a high school education (34.9% vs. 23.5%, p < 0.001), be multiparous (75.7% vs. 65.9%, p < 0.001), and receive no prenatal care (7.9% vs. 0.6%, p < 0.001). People with births with evidence of other SUD were more likely to be 15–24 years of age (40.4% vs. 36.8%, p < 0.001), receive 7 or less prenatal care visits (16.8% vs. 10.8%, p < 0.001), and less likely to be multiparous (61.9% vs. 65.9%, p < 0.001) compared to births without SUD.

Births with evidence of stimulant-related SUD received contraception within 60 days (including IPP) at similar rates as births with no evidence of SUD (44.7% vs. 43.0% respectively; Table 3), while births with evidence of other SUD had the highest rate of contraception receipt (47.5%). However, the type of contraception initiated varied across each group. Of note, sterilization within 60 days postpartum including IPP was the most common among births with stimulant-related SUD (20.9%), followed by births with other SUD (19.6%) and births without SUD (18.1%; p < 0.001). Similarly, receipt of LARC was more common among births with stimulant-related or other SUD compared to births without evidence of SUD. By contrast, SARM within 60 days postpartum including IPP was the least common among births with stimulant-related SUD (8.1%), followed by births without SUD (10.6%) and births with other SUD (12.2%; p < 0.001).

After adjusting for covariates, the likelihood of any contraception receipt within 60 days postpartum including IPP was 12% greater (aRR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.17; Table 4) for births with evidence of stimulant-related SUD compared to those with no evidence of SUD. We observed a similar rate of contraception receipt between births with evidence of stimulant-related SUD and births with no evidence of SUD during the period following the immediate postpartum period. Among births with evidence of other SUD, contraception receipt was 12% greater overall (aRR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.09–1.15), 21% greater during the immediate postpartum period (aRR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.16–1.26), and 9% greater within 60 days post hospital discharge excluding the immediate postpartum period (aRR: 1.09, 95% CI:1.06–1.13).

When we excluded births with evidence of cocaine misuse (N = 244), the associations between SUD category and contraception initiation did not substantively change (Supplemental Table 5). Separately, after imposing multiple prenatal continuous enrollment criteria, the association between stimulant-related SUD and IPP contraception was modestly attenuated towards the null but did not change our overall conclusions (Supplemental Table 6). We observed that the associations between stimulant use disorder and contraception initiation within 60 days postpartum excluding IPP were not statistically significant regardless of prenatal Medicaid enrollment criteria. Contraception use during the 60-days postpartum period including the IPP was similar between those with stimulant-related SUD and other SUD (Supplemental Table 7). IPP contraception use was slightly elevated among those with stimulant-related SUD compared to those with other SUDs.

Discussion

Our study found that unadjusted rates of contraception receipt within 60 days postpartum were similar among those with stimulant-related SUD compared to those without SUD. Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUDs had a somewhat higher rate of contraception receipt compared to those without SUD. Though overall unadjusted rates were similar, the timing and type of contraception initiated differed between groups, with sterilization being the most common and SARC the least common among Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUD. After adjusting for key characteristics, those with stimulant-related SUD were more likely to receive contraception immediately postpartum and within 60 days postpartum compared to those without SUD. For births with other SUDs, rates of contraception receipt within 60 days postpartum, immediately postpartum, and within 60 days postpartum excluding IPP were also greater compared to births with no indication of an SUD.

While literature on postpartum health outcomes among those that misuse stimulants is sparse, prior studies on postpartum outcomes among those that misuse opioids or with SUD more generally found lower rates of postpartum contraception receipt [34, 35]. The higher rates of contraception receipt among those with stimulant-related SUD and other SUD may reflect birthing providers focusing on contraception counseling during the childbirth hospitalization. However, greater receipt of contraception could also reflect providers’ perspectives regarding contraception needs for those with stimulant-related or other SUDs [36]. Future research should investigate patient, provider and healthcare related factors explaining these results for birthing people with stimulant-related SUD and other SUD, and investigate whether it is specific to Oregon’s context.

Over 60% of the study population did not receive contraception within 60 days postpartum. Our findings are consistent with prior literature that indicate rates of postpartum contraception among Medicaid beneficiaries are well below the Health People 2020 and 2030 goals [37, 38]. Increasing postpartum contraception receipt among all Medicaid enrollees, to the extent that it reflects patients’ preferences, should be a priority of state Medicaid programs.

Our analysis also documented variation in contraception type across population groups. Most notably, those with stimulant-related SUD had the lowest rate of SARM and the highest rate of sterilization. These differences may reflect patient preferences or their characteristics. It may also be the case that providers recommended different forms of contraceptives to birthing parents with stimulant-related SUD based on their beliefs regarding this population [36]. Overall, differences were fairly small but warrant further investigation.

While our was to compare receipt of contraception among Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUD to Medicaid beneficiaries without SUD and contraception receipt for those with other SUD to those without SUD, we also compared in supplemental analysis contraception receipt among Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-related SUD and Medicaid beneficiaries with other SUDs. When adjusting for demographic characteristics, contraception receipt was similar between these two groups during the full postpartum period but slightly elevated among those with stimulant-related SUD compared to those with other SUD during the immediate postpartum period. Future research could further investigate differences between individuals with stimulant-related SUD and specific other types of SUD, such as opioid use disorder.

Our study suggested some implications for policy. Notably, greater receipt of LARC and sterilization among birthing parents with stimulant-related or other SUDs as found in our study may reflect patients’ preferences but may also be influenced by providers’ perceptions and beliefs. Education and training activities regarding reproductive health and substance use for providers may be useful to ensure that contraception use is based on patient-centered conversations and shared-decision making that respects a patient’s preferences. Policymakers could also consider implementing team-based care models that bring together medical care, substance use treatment and other forms of care that foster a supportive, multi-disciplinary and patient-centered approach to reproductive choices for birthing parents with stimulant-related or other SUDs [39, 40].

Our study had a number of strengths. Most notably, we used Medicaid claims data linked with vital records, which allowed us to include a number of important variables associated with postpartum contraception from the vital statistics data not otherwise available in administrative claims. A few of these variables included gestational age, birth weight, maternal race and ethnicity, and maternal education. However, our study was not without limitations. We relied on administrative claims data to identify stimulant use disorder and other SUDs prenatally and pre-conception. Our use of administrative claims data limited us to those with a health care encounter and a clinician appropriately documenting the SUD. This may result in an undercount of those with stimulant disorder and other SUDs. Further, claims data did not document the length of time one misused substances or at what frequency one misused substances before or during pregnancy. We may have grouped new and chronic substance misuse, which may have affected postpartum contraception receipt. We also used administrative claims data to measure postpartum contraceptive receipt as a proxy for contraception initiation. In addition, due to claims data limitations we did not have access to birthing people’s choice and preferences as it related to contraception, or whether they received contraception not routinely captured in claims data. Further, we used a cross-sectional instead of longitudinal study design to obtain a sufficiently large sample for statistical inference. Future research could expand on our study by assessing trends in postpartum contraception receipt. Furthermore, we did not have information about delivery hospitals or perinatal providers. This could be a confounder if Medicaid beneficiaries visited hospitals or providers with different practice patterns. Moreover, we observed that most Medicaid beneficiaries with stimulant-use disorder also had another SUD. Thus, results may reflect broader substance use issues rather than stimulants specifically. Finally, we were only reporting results from one state, which may impact the generalizability of our findings.

Our study found that those with stimulant-related SUD and other SUD had similar or greater rates of postpartum contraception receipt than those with no SUD during the 60-days postpartum period including the immediate postpartum period. These findings were in contrast to other related studies and suggest that the policy environment may influence disparities in contraception receipt by SUD status. Replicating this study for other states and examining policies and other Medicaid-related factors contributing to differences in postpartum contraception use among birthing people with stimulant-related and other SUD across states may be important venues for future research.
