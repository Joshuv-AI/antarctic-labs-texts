Independent and synergistic effects of OGTT-based hyperglycemia phenotypes and gestational weight gain on feto-maternal outcomes in GDM
Authors: Yayuan Zhou, Linling Zhu, Hongmin An, Haiyan Wen, Peipei Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational weight gain (GWG) and hyperglycemia identified via the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) are critical factors influencing adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, the applicability of universal management standards for these factors across specific patient populations remains a subject of critical clinical inquiry. The objective of this study is to examine the independent and synergistic effects of different OGTT-based hyperglycemia phenotypes and GWG on adverse feto-maternal outcomes in women with GDM.MethodsFrom a total cohort of 52,942 parturients, this study included 5,593 cases of GDM after applying exclusion criteria. Participants were classified based on 75-g OGTT results and GWG categories. We systematically performed univariate analysis, multivariate logistic regression analysis, and interaction analysis to explore the associations between OGTT-based hyperglycemia phenotypes, GWG groups, and the incidence of adverse feto-maternal outcomes.ResultsThe incidence of adverse outcomes differed significantly by hyperglycemia phenotypes and GWG categories. Importantly, interaction analysis revealed that the impact of GWG on adverse outcomes varied significantly across different hyperglycemia phenotypes. The risks of preeclampsia, hyperlipidemia, polyhydramnios, large for gestational age infants, and cesarean section were highest in the combined hyperglycemia phenotype.ConclusionsOur findings indicate phenotype-specific epidemiological associations with feto-maternal outcomes. Given the retrospective nature of this study, these observational data are strictly hypothesis-generating. They raise the hypothesis that exploring refined GWG targets—particularly for the isolated fasting hyperglycemia phenotype—warrants future prospective investigation. Furthermore, inadequate GWG was associated with a higher risk of preterm birth exclusively in the isolated post-load hyperglycemia phenotype, indicating a specific correlational trend that merits further exploratory research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09264-1.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is abnormal glucose tolerance of varying severity, first identified during pregnancy. GDM is a common obstetric complication, which is present in 10.9% of pregnancies in Europe, 17.5% in China, and 12.8% worldwide [1, 2]. Research suggests that GDM primarily arises from insufficient insulin production by pancreatic β-cells. As pregnancy progresses, reduced insulin sensitivity raises resting glucose levels, requiring increased insulin secretion to maintain regulated normative glucose control. GDM occurs when the body cannot adapt these changes, and pancreatic β-cells in the endocrine system fail to produce sufficient insulin [3]. Other factors contributing to the development of GDM include gluconeogenesis, placental hormones, oxidative stress, low-grade chronic inflammation, and adipose expandability [4, 5].

In recent decades, the prevalence of GDM has risen due to an increasing incidence of maternal risk factors, such as advanced age and overweight [6–8], leading to health consequences for mothers and offspring during the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. Many studies have confirmed that GDM is positively correlated with an increased risk of adverse feto-maternal outcomes, including large for gestational age (LGA) infant, cesarean delivery, preeclampsia, preterm birth, fetal and neonatal mortality, neonatal hypoglycemia, shoulder dystocia, birth injuries, and hyperinsulinemia [9–12]. Moreover, GDM also impacts long-term outcomes, increasing the mother’s risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and the child’s risk of obesity, diabetes, and neurological disorders [13].

Based on the International Association of the Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria, GDM was categorized into three phenotypes: Isolated Fasting Hyperglycemia (IFH) (FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L only), Isolated Post-load Hyperglycemia (IPH) (1-h ≥ 10.0 or 2-h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L only), and Combined Hyperglycemia (CH) (elevated fasting and post-load glucose), representing distinct pathophysiological profiles [14]. Evidence suggests that IFH is predominantly driven by hepatic insulin resistance and a specific defect in early-phase insulin secretion, leading to excessive basal hepatic glucose production [15]. In contrast, IPH is primarily characterized by peripheral (muscle) insulin resistance and a decline in late-phase insulin sensitivity [15, 16]. CH represents a more advanced stage where both hepatic and peripheral defects coexist, resulting in global glucose dysregulation. Distinguishing these phenotypes is therefore crucial for understanding the underlying pathophysiology and tailoring targeted interventions.

The 2009 Institute of Medicine (IOM) Pregnancy Weight Guidelines is the most common applied recommendations for optimal gestational weight gain (GWG) [17–19]. However, the current IOM guidelines offer a generic recommendation based solely on pre-pregnancy BMI, largely derived from the general obstetric population. This “one-size-fits-all” approach overlooks the metabolic heterogeneity of GDM and may fail to address the distinct pathophysiological demands of IFH versus IPH [20, 21]. Furthermore, while recent literature in comparable populations suggests that more restrictive GWG targets may be necessary for women with GDM to optimize outcomes [21, 22], it remains unknown how these tighter targets should be adapted across different glycemic phenotypes. Consequently, the applicability of standard GWG targets to these specific phenotypes remains uncertain, indicating a critical re-evaluation of stratified weight management strategies is crucial. It is well established that both insufficient and excessive GWG are associated with adverse outcomes [23–25]. Substantial evidence indicates that excessive GWG is linked to adverse feto-maternal outcomes, maternal postpartum weight retention, and an increased risk of obesity in offspring [26, 27]. Moreover, increased GWG contributes to greater maternal fat accumulation, which may exacerbate impaired insulin sensitivity, making the link between GWG and GDM biologically plausible. Therefore, GWG represents a modifiable risk factor that requires precise management. Despite the existing IOM guidelines, the optimal weight gain range remains uncertain and needs further evaluation, especially for high-risk GDM phenotypes such as IFH, IPH, and CH.

Women diagnosed with GDM are at elevated risks of experiencing adverse feto-maternal outcomes, and excessive GWG appears to compound these risks. Research on the associations between hyperglycemia phenotypes during oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and adverse outcomes is limited. Only a few studies have investigated the impact of different hyperglycemia phenotypes on adverse outcomes [28–31]. Especially, the impact of varying GWG on fetal-maternal outcomes across different GDM phenotypes has been rarely investigated. To address this gap in the literature, it is essential to explore these synergistic effects in order to propose a precise and personalized stratified weight management strategy for GDM treatment.

In the present study, we investigate the associations between adverse feto-maternal outcomes and categories of hyperglycemia in women diagnosed with GDM to determine whether the level of risk is similar for each GDM phenotype or each GWG subgroup. Moreover, we perform the interaction analysis to determine the impact of two factors, i.e., the fasting, post-load and combined hyperglycemia groups or inadequate, sufficient and excessive GWG groups, on adverse feto-maternal outcomes. Given the observational nature of this cohort, our primary aim is to generate hypotheses regarding the potential need for phenotype-specific weight management. We seek to provide epidemiological evidence that can inform future prospective studies and interventional trials on personalized maternal care.

Methods

Study population

Between January 2019 and September 2024, a total of 52,942 women were hospitalized for childbirth at Hangzhou Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang Province, China. Among these, 6887 were diagnosed with GDM. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Fig. 1), a final cohort of 5593 patients was selected for analysis. The sample comprised women over 18 years old (range 18–53 years; median/mean 31/31.1 years) with age distribution as 18–25 years (4.9%), 26–30 years (42.7%), 31–35 years (39.5%), 36–45 years (12.8%), and 46–53 years (0.02%). BMI distribution is underweight (10.3%), normal weight (74.0%), overweight (13.7%), and obese (2.0%), while GWG distribution is divided into below IOM guidelines (47.5%), within IOM-recommended range (37.5%), and above IOM guidelines (15%). All participants underwent a 2-hour 75-gram mid-term OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation without prior 50-gram glucose challenge tests and delivered live singleton births at ≥ 28 weeks’ gestation. Women with placenta previa, uterine anomalies, preconception diabetes, chronic hypertension, liver and kidney dysfunction, thyroid disorders, and other endocrine or autoimmune diseases were excluded, respectively. All the clinical data were collected from electronic medical records system used in our hospital and digitized into a database, capturing demographic, obstetric, and delivery information, including age, height, preconception weight, preconception BMI, GWG, gravidity, parity, gestation age, OGTT values, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), birth weight, newborn gender, delivery mode (cesarean section, forceps extraction or vaginal delivery), feto-maternal outcomes. The methods in this work were carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Women’s Hospital (Approval No. [2024] Medical Ethics Audit A-149), with a waiver of informed consent. Full details are provided in the Declarations section.

Categorization of OGTT values, preconception BMI and GWG results

GDM was diagnosed and classified as IFG, IPH and CH using IADPSG thresholds [14], as mentioned above. BMI categories were defined as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Preconception body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight/height² (kg/m²) using preconception weight and height measured in hospital. GWG was determined by subtracting preconception weight from delivery weight. The 2009 IOM guidelines recommend GWG of 12.5–18.0 kg for underweight, 11.5–16.0 kg for normal weight, 7.0–11.5 kg for overweight, and 5.0–9.0 kg for obese women [32]. Thus, GWG below, within, or above the IOM-recommended range was categorized as GWG-B, GWG-W and GWG-A respectively.

Feto-maternal outcomes

All feto-maternal outcomes were diagnosed based on the Chinese guidelines [33–35]. Gestational hypertension was defined as a blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Preeclampsia was defined by the presence of proteinuria along with maternal blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg. The diagnosis of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) was based on the classic symptoms of pruritus, along with elevated bile acids (TBA ≥ 10 µmol/L) and liver enzymes. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a total serum triglyceride level ≥ 220 mg/dL (2.49 mmol/L) or total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL (6.24 mmol/L). Oligohydramnios was defined as an amniotic fluid index (AFI) < 5 cm. Fetal distress was characterized by comprehensive life-threatening symptoms, including abnormal fetal heart rate caused by fetal hypoxia in the third trimester of pregnancy. Cord entanglement referred to the umbilical cord encircling the fetal neck, typically due to a long cord or a small fetus. Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count of < 150 × 109/L. Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) referred to the spontaneous rupture of the amniotic sac before the onset of labor. Placentae abruption was defined as the premature separation of the placenta before childbirth [36, 37]. Postpartum hemorrhage is defined as an estimated blood loss of ≥ 500 mL after vaginal delivery or ≥ 1000 mL after a cesarean section. Gestational age was categorized into three groups: early preterm (28 ≤ gestational age < 34 weeks), late preterm (34 ≤ gestational age < 37 weeks), normal/post-term birth (gestational age ≥ 37 weeks). Large for gestational age (LGA) and small for gestational age (SGA) were defined as birth weights of neonate above the 90th percentile or below the 10th percentile, respectively, based on gestational age.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. Given the large sample size, the normality of continuous variables was evaluated via visual inspection of data distributions (e.g., histograms, Q-Q plots) and assessment of skewness and kurtosis. Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variances (Table S1, Supporting Information). Continuous variables that met parametric assumptions were assessed via one-way ANOVA. Variables that violated these assumptions (e.g., highly skewed distribution or substantial heteroscedasticity) were evaluated using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. Continuous data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) as appropriate. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson chi-square tests (frequencies, percentages). A statistical significance level was established with a p < 0.05. After identifying statistically significant differences in pregnancy outcomes among the three groups (hyperglycemia phenotypes or GWG groups) through univariate analysis, we used multivariate logistic regression to perform pairwise comparisons. Multivariate logistic regression analyzed categorical variables, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) at P < 0.05. ORs were calculated for adverse feto-maternal outcomes by hyperglycemia phenotypes (CH, IPH vs. IFH) and GWG category (GWG-A, GWG-B vs. GWG-W). The multivariate logistic regression models and interaction analyses were adjusted for the following potential confounders: maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and parity [38, 39]. For interaction analysis, ORs (CIs) were established in the multivariate logistic regression, determined with respect to vaginal delivery (for mode of delivery), and to normal and post-term birth (for gestational age type), respectively. Given the binary nature of the outcomes, the interaction between hyperglycemia phenotypes and GWG categories was assessed on a multiplicative scale using multivariable logistic regression models, with significance evaluated by the P-value of the interaction terms. Specifically, we incorporated the interaction term of ‘hyperglycemia phenotypes × GWG categories’ into the models to systematically evaluate whether the association between GWG and adverse feto-maternal outcomes varied across different hyperglycemia phenotypes.

Results

General characteristics

Among the 52,942 parturients, 6887 cases of GDM were identified, representing an incidence rate of 13%. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final cohort comprised 5593 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM (Fig. 1). These patients were categorized into three OGTT-based hyperglycemia phenotypes: IFH (n = 1,038, 18.6%), IPH (n = 3,727, 66.6%), and CH (n = 828, 14.8%). The cohort was further stratified into three GWG groups: GWG-B (n = 2656; 47.5%), GWG-W (n = 2097; 37.5%), and GWG-A (n = 840; 15.0%). As summarized in Table 1, statistically significant differences (all p < 0.05) were observed among the three phenotypes for maternal age, height, preconception weight, BMI, GWG, gravidity, parity, fasting blood glucose, OGTT 1 h/2 h values, BUN and Cr. UA was the only expection (p > 0.05). Detailed P values were provided in Supplementary Table S2. Similarly, all variables exhibited significant differences across the GWG groups, except for parity and Cr (Table 1). Notably, infants in the IPH group had significantly lower birth weights compared to the IFH and CH groups (3244 g vs. 3329 g and 3378 g, p < 0.0001), while newborn gender distribution remained consistent across all phenotypes (P = 0.28).

Univariate analysis revealed distinct clinical profiles among the phenotypes. Compared to the IFH and IPH groups, CH group was generally older, shorter, and heavier with a higher preconception BMI. They also exhibited greater gravidity, lower GWG, higher UA levels, shorter gestational ages, and higher infant birth weights. Conversely, IFH group was typically younger, taller, and had the greatest GWG, alongside the highest Cr and lowest BUN levels. Significant demographic variations were also observed across the GWG categories. GWG-B group was typically older, shorter, and lighter, with a lower preconception BMI and gravidity compared to the other groups. They also presented with higher BUN levels, lower UA levels, shorter gestational ages, and the lowest birth weights. In contrast, GWG-A group was the youngest and tallest, exhibiting the highest preconception BMI, gravidity, UA levels, and infant birth weights.

Univariate analysis

As shown in Table 2, multiple adverse feto-maternal outcomes were significantly associated with the hyperglycemia phenotypes (all P < 0.05). These included preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, hyperlipidemia, polyhydramnios, history of uterine scar, mode of delivery, LGA, and SGA. All categorical variables were assessed using Pearson chi-square tests. Detailed P values for these adverse feto-maternal outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table S3. No significant differences were found among the phenotypes regarding ICP, thrombocytopenia, oligohydramnios, postpartum hemorrhage, uterine inertia, PROM, fetal distress, cord entanglement, or gestational age type. Table 2 also summarizes feto-maternal outcomes across the GWG groups. Significant associations (all P < 0.05) were found between GWG categories and preeclampsia, history of uterine scar, mode of delivery, gestational age type, LGA, and SGA. However, no significant differences were observed for the remaining feto-maternal complications. Additional pairwise comparisons using the Mann-Whitney test are detailed in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5.

Interaction analysis

To systematically evaluate the synergistic effects of OGTT groups and GWG on feto-maternal outcomes, we performed a comprehensive interaction analysis. Associations between OGTT based hyperglycemia phenotypes (or GWG group) and adverse outcomes were analyzed and the related results with statistically significant differences were presented in Fig. 3. In the IFH category, GWG-A was associated with reduced odds of SGA (OR = 0.28) and LGA (OR = 0.60) compared to the GWG-W group. Excessive GWG was a risk factor (OR = 1.42) for the cesarean section, in comparison to vaginal delivery. In the IPH category, inadequate GWG was associated with decreased odds of preeclampsia (OR = 0.35), LGA (OR = 0.52), and cesarean section (OR = 0.84), but increased odds of SGA (OR = 1.94), early preterm (OR = 2.82), and late preterm (OR = 1.80). Excessive GWG was a risk factor for LGA (OR = 2.20) and cesarean section (OR = 1.49). For CH, GWG-B was associated with decreased odds of LGA (OR = 0.60) and cesarean section (OR = 0.66), but increased odds of SGA (OR = 5.38). Whereas, GWG-A was a risk factor for LGA (OR = 1.69) and cesarean section (OR = 1.55). These findings suggest that different hyperglycemia phenotypes during OGTT exhibit varying associations between GWG and adverse feto-maternal outcomes. Discussion

The overall prevalence of GDM in this cohort was 13%, a rate that is significantly lower than the 27% reported in India [40], yet higher than the 10.9% documented in Europe [41], and closely aligned with the global estimate of 12.8% [2]. The incidence of GDM is influenced by multiple determinants, including race/ethnicity, maternal age, and the specific diagnostic criteria applied [4, 42–44]. In our cohort, the distribution of OGTT hyperglycemia phenotypes was as follows: 18.6% were classified as IFH, 66.6% as IPH, and 14.8% as CH. This distribution differs from that reported in a previous Western cohort using the IADPSG criteria, which documented prevalences of 21.7% for IFH, 48.4% for IPH, and 30% for CH, respectively [28].

Compared to the other two groups, the CH group demonstrated a higher prevalence of established GDM risk factors, including advanced maternal age and elevated pre-pregnancy BMI, which is consistent with previous reports [45, 46]. The analysis further revealed that the CH group exhibited the shortest stature, highest parity, lowest mean GWG, and shortest gestational age among the groups. In contrast, women with IFH were generally younger and taller, and demonstrated the highest mean GWG. Both IFH and CH groups had significantly higher pre-conceptional BMI and mean birth weight compared to the IPH group.

Multivariate analyses of our cohort revealed that the risks of LGA were the highest in the CH group. In contrast, IPH group were 72% as likely to have LGA infants than those with IFH. That is, GDM women with abnormal fasting blood glucose values, i.e., IFH and CH groups using our classification, had higher rate of LGA than IPH group. These findings are consistent with the trends reported in the HAPO study, in which GDM patients with elevated fasting glucose showed a higher prevalence of LGA neonates (19.5%) than those with normal fasting glucose levels (12.6%) [47].On the other hand, in our work, CH group had lower risk to have SGA than IPH, with a likelihood of 42%. These findings may be explained by the mechanism of fasting insulin resistance. During the fasting state, plasma glucose is primarily derived from hepatic glucose production, which is regulated by insulin [48]. When hepatocytes become resistant to insulin, they continue producing glucose, leading to fasting hyperglycemia, which requires additional insulin to overcome hepatic insulin resistance [49]. Studies have shown that individuals with abnormal fasting blood glucose exhibit higher fasting insulin levels compared to those with post-load elevated glucose. Elevated maternal insulin levels may contribute to greater birth weight and an increased incidence of LGA newborns, as insulin acts as an anabolic factor regulating fetal body size [50].

Moreover, it was revealed that CH group was associated with the highest risk of preeclampsia among all hyperglycemia phenotypes. The elevated incidence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy among CH group women may be attributed to the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms specific to this population. Specifically, abnormal fasting plasma glucose levels are indicative of hepatic insulin resistance, whereas abnormal post-load plasma glucose levels primarily reflect insulin resistance in muscle and adipose tissues. Thus, CH group may present with hepatic, muscle and adipose tissues insulin resistance [51, 52]. The CH group exhibit a higher degree of insulin resistance compared to IFH and IPH phenotypes. Insulin resistance contributes to the development of hypertensive disorders through multiple mechanisms including diminished NO production and the upregulation of harmful molecules like PAI-1, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and E-selectin [51, 53]. These alterations result in endothelial dysfunction which induce hypertensive disorders. A large study conducted in the general population, which investigated the associations between various stages of impaired glucose metabolism and hypertension, demonstrated that individuals with both impaired fasting plasma glucose and impaired glucose tolerance exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension compared to those with isolated impaired fasting plasma glucose or isolated impaired glucose tolerance [54].

Furthermore, the CH phenotypes demonstrated the highest risks of hyperlipidemia, polyhydramnios, uterine scar history, and cesarean delivery among all hyperglycemia phenotypes. This trend is consistent with observations from the HAPO study [47], which demonstrated that increases in each of the three OGTT values are associated with a proportional rise in the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Noteworthy, Troìa et al. reported that severe hyperglycemia requiring insulin therapy is associated with significantly reduced umbilical artery pulsatility index (UA-PI) in the third trimester, indicating a direct alteration of fetoplacental hemodynamics [55]. This hemodynamic adaptation may represent a distinct physiological pathway through which specific hyperglycemia phenotypes influence feto-maternal outcomes.

Appropriate GWG represents a cornerstone for optimizing feto-maternal outcomes [23]. In our study, 47.5% of women had GWG below the IOM recommendation, while 15.0% exceeded it. A previous report found that 29.6% of women had insufficient weight gain, and 31.8% had excessive weight gain [56]. The observed differences in proportions may result from the use of nutritional therapy and exercise interventions, which are the primary strategies for blood glucose control following a GDM diagnosis in our hospital. These interventions likely led to a reduced incidence of excessive GWG (15.0% vs. 31.8%) and an increased rate of appropriate GWG (47.5% vs. 29.6%).

Perinatal death in SGA infants is four times higher than in infants appropriate for gestational age [57]. SGA is linked to severe complications such as decreased oxygen level, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, polycythemia and developmental delay, whereas LGA outcomes may related to adverse obstetric outcomes such as shoulder dystocia, chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage and longer hospital stay [58]. Multivariate analysis of our cohort revealed that women with GWG-A were 2.14 times as likely to have LGA neonates and had a 50% reduced likelihood of having SGA neonates compared to GWG-W women. While GWG-B were 56% as likely to have LGA neonates and 1.82 times as likely to have SGA neonates as GWG-W. These findings are consistent with previous reports. For instance, Gou et al. reported that excessive GWG was associated with a higher incidence of LGA neonates and a lower incidence of SGA neonates, while insufficient GWG was linked to a lower incidence of LGA neonates [56]. Furthermore, a study by Kim et al. indicated that GWG was the primary factor associated with LGA outcomes, whereas GDM played the least role [59].

Additionally, our analysis indicated that women with excessive gestational weight gain had a 20% lower likelihood of cesarean delivery and a 3% lower likelihood of instrumental delivery (forceps extraction) compared to GWG-W women. Regarding the mode of delivery, women with insufficient gestational weight gain had 1.49-fold higher odds of cesarean section and 19% lower odds of instrumental delivery (forceps extraction) compared to those with GWG within the recommended range. These findings are consistent with the report by Cheng et al., which analyzed data from 31,000 women with GDM, found that those with GWG exceeding IOM guidelines had a higher incidence of preterm birth, cesarean delivery [60]. Our study provided more precise results. Women with GWG-A were 2.33 times and 1.67 times as likely to have early preterm and late preterm, respectively. Interestingly, women with GWG-B are also 18% more likely to develop late preterm than those with GWG-W. Several studies have reported that excessive GWG is significantly associated with an increased risk of cesarean section and a reduced likelihood of preterm birth, whereas inadequate GWG is linked to an increased risk of preterm birth [61, 62]. GWG-A were 26% more likely to develop history of uterine scar, compared to GWG-W. GWG-B were 39.3%, 154%, 116% as likely to develop preeclampsia, ICP, PROM, respectively. The increased risk of adverse feto-maternal outcomes associated with excessive GWG may be attributed to elevated glycated hemoglobin levels [63].

Feto-maternal outcomes are strongly associated with hyperglycemia level during OGTT and GWG [27, 64]. In the interaction analysis of GWG groups and hyperglycemia phenotypes, we found that the association between GWG and adverse feto-maternal outcomes varied significantly across different hyperglycemia phenotypes, indicating an interaction between these factors in influencing feto-maternal outcomes.

In the IPH and CH phenotypes, inadequate GWG was associated with a higher risk of SGA (ORs = 1.94 and 5.38, respectively), while excessive GWG was linked to an increased likelihood of LGA (ORs = 2.20 and 1.69, respectively), compared to GWG-W. These findings suggest that for these phenotypes, maintaining GWG within the recommended range is correlated with a reduced incidence of both SGA and LGA. However, for IFH phenotypes, excessive GWG was associated with a reduced risk of SGA, while insufficient GWG was linked to a lower likelihood of LGA. These findings indicate that for IFH phenotype, identification of an elevated risk for LGA during pregnancy warrants a reduction in the recommended GWG to mitigate the risk of delivering a LGA neonate. Conversely, identification of an elevated risk for SGA during pregnancy warrants an upward adjustment of the recommended GWG to mitigate the likelihood of delivering a SGA neonate in this population. The current IOM guidelines for GWG may require adjustment for IFH phenotype, with recommendations tailored to specific pregnancy risks: a lower GWG target when there is elevated risk of LGA, and a higher target when the primary concern is prevention of SGA. However, the current lack of GWG guidelines specific to different hyperglycemia phenotypes during OGTT—particularly for Asian or Chinese populations—poses a challenge in validating these findings across diverse cohorts.

In the three groups, excessive GWG was associated with a higher rate of cesarean section, with ORs of 1.42, 1.49, and 1.55 for IFH, IPH, and CH, respectively. In contrast, inadequate GWG was associated with a reduced risk of cesarean section, except in the IFH.

For preeclampsia, only IFH with inadequate GWG showed statistically significant differences, leading to a lower rate (OR = 0.35). A previous study on a combined cohort of GDM and non-GDM women showed that both excessive and inadequate GWG were associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia [65]. Another previous studies focused on the impact of excessive GWG on preeclampsia, without analysis of inadequate GWG as a influencing factor [66].

Notably, it was found that inadequate GWG is associated with a higher risk of early (OR = 2.82) or late preterm (OR = 1.80) birth exclusively in the IPH, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed for the other two hyperglycemia phenotypes. Thus, to reduce the risk of preterm birth, IPH phenotype should carefully manage their GWG to be within the IOM recommended range. These results we found are pivotal in advancing personalized obstetric care by providing evidence-based weight management strategies for various hyperglycemia phenotypes during OGTT. It highlights the role of weight gain in GDM-related complications, contributing to improved maternal and neonatal outcomes while informing clinical practice and public health policies.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the research was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants might differ from those in other regions or countries, potentially affecting the applicability of the results to diverse populations. Secondly, the study relied on retrospective data collection from electronic medical records, which might introduce biases related to data accuracy and completeness. Although efforts were made to ensure the integrity of the data, the possibility of missing or misclassified information could not be entirely ruled out. Additionally, the categorization of GWG based on the IOM guidelines might not fully capture the complexities of weight gain patterns in different populations. The IOM guidelines are based on Western populations, and their applicability to Asian populations, particularly in the context of GDM, may require further validation. Finally, the study’s cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish causal relationships between hyperglycemia phenotypes during OGTT, GWG, and adverse feto-maternal outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the temporal dynamics and causal pathways involved in these associations. Furthermore, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. It is important to note that all GDM cases in our study were uniformly diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, ensuring a consistent timeline for intervention. Subsequently, all patients were prescribed a standardized clinical management protocol encompassing dietary guidance, exercise recommendations, and blood glucose monitoring. Importantly, patients requiring insulin therapy were excluded from our study cohort, which mitigates potential confounding related to varying intensities of pharmacological interventions. However, detailed data regarding socioeconomic status and individual patient compliance with these post-diagnosis lifestyle interventions, such as specific dietary adherence and actual physical activity levels, were not available in our database. Variations in individual execution of weight management and glycemic control strategies could still influence feto-maternal outcomes, serving as unmeasured confounders in the observed associations. To definitively address these unmeasured confounders, future prospective cohort studies incorporating precise monitoring of real-time glycemic control (e.g., via continuous glucose monitoring) and objective tracking of physical activity and dietary adherence are warranted. Finally, given the observational nature of our study, we must acknowledge the potential for reverse causality. While we analyzed GWG as a predictor of adverse outcomes, it is plausible that certain developing complications influenced weight gain trajectories. For instance, fluid retention associated with preeclampsia could manifest as excessive GWG, or subclinical placental dysfunction leading to SGA might result in inadequate maternal weight gain. Thus, the associations observed should be interpreted as correlational rather than strictly causal. In particular, regarding the outcome of preterm birth, we acknowledge the potential bias introduced by including early preterm births (< 34 weeks) in our analysis of total GWG. The association between inadequate GWG and preterm birth is susceptible to time-dependent bias, as a shorter gestational duration inherently limits the opportunity for maternal weight accumulation. Consequently, lower total GWG in these cases may be a consequence of the shortened gestation rather than a causative factor. Furthermore, early preterm births are often driven by distinct pathological mechanisms (e.g., infection, cervical insufficiency) compared to late preterm births, which may not be directly related to metabolic factors or GWG. Therefore, the observed associations between GWG categories and preterm birth should be interpreted with caution, considering these inherent physiological constraints. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that we evaluated multiple feto-maternal outcomes without applying formal statistical corrections for multiple comparisons. Consequently, the potential for Type I error inflation exists, and the marginal statistical associations observed in our secondary outcomes should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

This study highlights the independent and synergistic associations of OGTT-based hyperglycemia phenotypes and GWG groups with adverse feto-maternal outcomes. Among 52,942 parturients, the incidence of GDM was approximately 13%. Our data indicated that the CH phenotype had the highest risks of preeclampsia, hyperlipidemia, polyhydramnios, history of uterine scar, LGA, and cesarean delivery. Compared to the IFH phenotype, the IPH phenotype was associated with a lower likelihood of developing gestational hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Adherence to IOM-recommended GWG was associated with a reduced incidence of both SGA and LGA neonates among IPH and CH phenotypes. However, for the IFH phenotype, our findings indicate a correlational trend where a lower GWG is associated with a reduced risk of LGA, whereas a higher GWG is associated with a lower risk of SGA. These observations provide a rationale for future studies to investigate the potential value of refining the current IOM guidelines for GWG. Inadequate GWG was specifically associated with an elevated risk of preterm birth among the IPH phenotype, highlighting an epidemiological link that warrants further investigation. In conclusion, both hyperglycemia phenotypes and GWG classifications appear to be significant factors associated with feto-maternal outcomes. Given the retrospective and observational design of this study, these conclusions are strictly hypothesis-generating. The hypothesis that integrating hyperglycemia phenotypes into GWG recommendations could optimize pregnancy outcomes remains preliminary and requires robust validation in future prospective cohorts and interventional studies.
