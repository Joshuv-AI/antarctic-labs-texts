Effect of Transthoracic Echocardiography on Prognosis Outcomes in Diabetic Patients With Various Cardiovascular Diseases: A Retrospective Cohort Study Based on the MIMIC‐IV Database
Authors: Ying‐er Feng, Cun‐jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and prognosis in diabetic patients without complications who present with seven types of cardiovascular diseases.MethodsData were sourced from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)‐IV database (N = 5553). The included participants were divided into TTE examination (n = 2263) and non‐TTE examination (n = 3290) groups. Clinical endpoints included survival time, initial Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission, and hospital readmission. Logistic and Cox regression analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between TTE examination and these outcomes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated the predictive exploratory prognosis utility of TTE examination. The Kaplan–Meier (KM) and restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses were used to compare the survival difference between the TTE examination and non‐TTE examination groups.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in the distribution of the other three outcomes between the TTE examination and non‐TTE examination groups (all p < 0.05). However, only the association between TTE and survival time was influenced by whether the patient had cardiovascular diseases (observed only in patients with cardiovascular diseases, all p < 0.05). KM and RMST analyses further showed that TTE examination was associated with dual benefits in longer survival time and extended event‐free survival time compared with the non‐TTE examination group (all p < 0.05). Cox regression analysis indicated a negative correlation in patients specifically with coronary heart disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or hypertension (all p < 0.05), but observed an independent association only in subgroups with heart failure (p = 0.012) or cardiomyopathy (p = 0.035). Exploratory ROC analysis demonstrated the clinical significance of TTE, particularly in predicting long‐term survival (exceeding the 75th percentile) among diabetic patients with heart failure (all DeLong test p < 0.05).ConclusionTTE was independently associated with survival time in diabetic patients with heart failure or cardiomyopathy, and exploratory analyses suggested more favorable performance in predicting the longer survival duration among those with heart failure.

Introduction

Diabetes is an endocrine disease characterized by persistent hyperglycemia and high urinary sugar levels, which can lead to chronic damage or dysfunction of organs such as the eyes, kidneys, heart, blood vessels, and nervous system (Pappachan et al. 2019; Cole and Florez 2020). With the development of society and the improvement of people's living standards, the incidence of diabetes worldwide has been increasing annually. Moreover, diabetes acts as a significant cause of death. According to the reported research, by 2030, it will become the seventh leading cause of death globally (Fedeli et al. 2022; Mathers and Loncar 2006). Notably, cardiovascular disease such as cardiomyopathy and heart failure played an important role in the increase of the incidence rate and mortality of diabetes (Kaur et al. 2002). This is mainly because a large amount of glycated proteins deposits infiltrate into the myocardium in diabetic patients, leading to diffuse myocardial fibrosis and myocardial hypertrophy, resulting in left ventricular dysfunction (Ng et al. 2018; Tadic et al. 2015). Additionally, this can further lead to severe unfavorable conditions such as coronary atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, which are more serious than isolated diabetes or heart disease alone, with poor treatment outcomes. Therefore, it is of great significance to assess cardiac function and initiate treatment for diabetic and cardiovascular patients to improve prognosis (Enomoto et al. 2015; Tadic et al. 2018).

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is a noninvasive examination that provides sectional views of the patient's four cardiac chambers and outflow tracts, allowing for the visualization of cardiac parameters. It is commonly used as a means to assess cardiac function and is widely applicable in the examination of cardiovascular diseases (Mor‐Avi et al. 2011; Chamsi‐Pasha et al. 2017). For patients with severe complications of diabetes, doctors often perform TTE as one of the indicators to diagnose the severity of the condition.

In recent years, although the positive association between diabetes and cardiovascular disease has been well established through multiple large‐scale cohort studies (Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration et al. 2010; Visseren et al. 2021), current clinical guidelines primarily emphasize cardiac screening for patients with established cardiovascular disease or diabetes‐related complications such as proteinuria or retinopathy (Cosentino et al. 2020). In addition, subclinical cardiac dysfunction or stage B heart failure, mediated by the integrated effects of diabetes (including asymptomatic diabetes) on cardiac structure and function, serves as the primary contributor to the increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality (Epstein 1997; Yancy et al. 2017). Such early structural and functional abnormalities are often undetected by conventional biomarker assessments (Salik et al. 2021). Although the European Society of Cardiology has issued expert consensus statements recommending the inclusion of TTE in the annual evaluation of patients with diabetes (Valensi et al. 2021), there remains a significant implementation gap in clinical practice, potentially due to a lack of awareness regarding the importance of TTE assessment in this population.

Therefore, we speculate that receipt of TTE may be associated with prognostic assessment in diabetic patients without complications but with concurrent cardiovascular diseases. However, no studies have been reported regarding this matter. Therefore, in this study, we explored the relationship between TTE application and the different prognosis outcomes of diabetic patients without complications (in the following referred to as the patient) but with various kinds of cardiovascular diseases to verify our hypothesis.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

Demographic data, physiological data, and disease data used in this study were collected from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care‐IV (MIMIC‐IV). The database was established in 2003 by the Computational Physiology Laboratory of Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) of Harvard Medical School, and Philips Medical Corporation with funding from the National Institutes of Health. MIMIC‐IV comprises de‐identified electronic health records from patients admitted to BIDMC, a tertiary care center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, between 2008 and 2019, and includes both ICD‐9‐CM and ICD‐10‐CM diagnostic codes depending on the admission date. TTE exposure was identified using the MIMIC‐IV‐ECHO dataset, a dedicated echocardiographic subset linked to the MIMIC‐IV Clinical Database via subject_id. The test type field in MIMIC‐IV‐ECHO was used to identify patients who underwent TTE. Patients in the MIMIC‐IV‐ECHO dataset with a test type of “TTE” were classified as the TTE group, while patients meeting the inclusion criteria but without a corresponding record in MIMIC‐IV‐ECHO were classified as the non‐TTE group.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus without complications (ICD‐10‐CM codes E099, E109, E119; and corresponding ICD‐9‐CM codes 250.00, 250.01, 250.02); and (3) length of hospital stay ≥ 2 days. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with gestational diabetes and (2) patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus with complications.

Eligible patients were then divided into two groups based on whether they underwent TTE during their hospitalization: the TTE group (patients with a documented TTE record) and the non‐TTE group (patients without a documented TTE record).

Covariables and Outcomes

In this study, the following data were extracted from the MIMIC‐IV database: age (years), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), the length of hospital stay (days), sex (n, %), married status (n, %), Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), and cardiovascular related comorbidities. Comorbidities included coronary heart disease, heart failure, arrhythmia, hypertension, cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, and atherosclerosis. The comorbidities were diagnosed according to the ICD code. The specific ICD code of each comorbidity is presented in Appendix S1.

In this study, clinical outcomes included the primary outcome: survival time (days) and survival status (living vs. death), and the secondary outcome: intensive care unit (ICU) stay requirement (yes vs. no) and hospital readmission (yes vs. no). Mortality information for discharged patients was acquired from the US Social Security Death Index. The follow‐up period was set at 5 years. Follow‐up time was measured from the date of hospital admission for each patient. TTE exposure was classified based on whether TTE was performed during the index hospitalization. Since the inclusion criteria required a minimum hospital stay of ≥ 2 days and the observed median length of hospital stay was approximately 6 days, the potential immortal time, the interval between admission and TTE examination, was confined to a short hospitalization window. Although the exact timing of TTE within the hospitalization was not available in the dataset, the brief duration of hospitalization relative to the 5‐year follow‐up period suggests that the magnitude of any immortal time bias is limited. The study defined hospital readmission as any subsequent hospital admission for any reason following the current discharge, without specifying a time interval.

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States) and R software (version 4.1.2, R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. First, baseline characteristics were compared between the TTE examination group and the non‐TTE examination group. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared using the independent samples t‐test and reported as mean ± standard deviation; nonnormally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test and reported as frequencies (percentages). Characteristics (age, CCI, sex, and married status) with significant differences between groups were included as covariates in subsequent regression analyses. Then, logistic regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between TTE examination and first ICU admission or hospital readmission, with adjustment for covariates. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between TTE examination and survival time across disease subgroups, with adjustment for covariates. Survival analysis included Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by disease subgroups, with between‐group differences tested using the log‐rank test. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was calculated with a restriction time of τ = 60 months (5 years), estimated directly from the Kaplan–Meier survival curves, to estimate the 5‐year mean survival difference between TTE and non‐TTE groups in each subgroup. The RMST represents the area under the survival curve up to the specified time point, and the difference in RMST between groups quantifies the average survival time gained or lost. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the predictive performance of variables for survival probability, and the DeLong test was used to compare ROC curves. It should be noted that the ROC analyses were intended as exploratory discrimination analyses rather than as clinically actionable predictive tools.

In addition, to quantitatively evaluate the potential influence of immortal time bias on the survival analysis results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a simulated landmark approach. Since follow‐up was measured from hospital admission while TTE was performed during the hospitalization, patients in the TTE group necessarily survived until the time of examination, introducing a potential immortal time window. Because the exact timing of TTE during hospitalization was not available in the dataset, we conservatively assumed that TTE was performed at 2, 4, 6, or 7 days after admission. For each assumed delay, the follow‐up duration of TTE group patients was shortened by the corresponding number of days to simulate a landmark time point, and patients whose adjusted follow‐up time became ≤ 0 days were excluded. Both crude and adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were then re‐estimated for each disease subgroup to assess the stability of the observed associations under varying immortal time assumptions.

The above results indicated that TTE examination was associated with patients' survival time in subgroups. Next, we used the Kaplan–Meier analysis and log‐rank test to evaluate the survival difference between TTE and non‐TTE groups among subgroups with different cardiovascular diseases. The results showed that the survival time had significant differences between the two groups in all subgroups, and receiving TTE examination was associated with the longer survival time in the 5‐year follow‐up period (Figure 1A–D, all p < 0.05). At the same time, RMST results also showed that the event‐free survival time of the patients in the TTE examination group was longer than that of patients in the non‐TTE examination group within 5 years in all subgroups. In patients with coronary heart disease, TTE examination was associated with an average survival extension of 8.813 months, while in those with heart failure, it corresponded to an average increase of 9.949 months. For patients with hypertension or cardiomyopathy, TTE examination was associated with survival extension of 7.284 and 11.445 months, respectively (Figure S1, all p < 0.05). However, the number of patients remaining at risk near the end of the follow‐up period was limited, and therefore the RMST estimates in the tail of the survival curves should be interpreted with caution.

The sensitivity analysis using simulated landmark delays further confirmed the robustness of the above survival associations against potential immortal time bias (Table S1 and Figure 3). In the heart failure subgroup, the adjusted hazard ratio remained statistically significant across all assumed delay scenarios, ranging from 0.494 (95% CI: 0.285–0.854, p = 0.012) in the original analysis to 0.375 (95% CI: 0.206–0.681, p = 0.001) under a 7‐day delay assumption. In the cardiomyopathy subgroup, the adjusted HR similarly remained significant, ranging from 0.328 (95% CI: 0.117–0.923, p = 0.035) to 0.242 (95% CI: 0.078–0.754, p = 0.014). Notably, the hazard ratios decreased further with longer assumed delays, indicating that any immortal time in the original analysis may have slightly attenuated rather than inflated the observed associations. The number of patients excluded due to nonpositive adjusted follow‐up time was minimal (maximum 11 of 579 in heart failure and 2 of 168 in cardiomyopathy), confirming that the vast majority of TTE patients had survival durations far exceeding the assumed immortal time window.

Evaluation of the Discriminative Performance of TTE for Survival Prediction

To further compare the value of TTE examination in predicting patient prognosis in two subgroups, we defined patient survival time into long and short categories using two grouping methods to avoid the bias associated with a single method. The first method categorized survival time based on its median, with values greater than the median considered long survival and values less than the median considered short survival. The second method divided survival time based on quartiles, with the less than 25th percentile considered short survival time and more than the 75th percentile considered long survival time. The method refers to the length of hospitalization grouping (Klemt et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2022). Then, we compared the performance of five indicators, including TTE examination and 4 baseline features, in predicting patient survival time through ROC analysis. It should be noted that these ROC analyses are exploratory in nature, as TTE receipt is a care‐process variable rather than a biomarker or prognostic score, and the observed discrimination may partly reflect differences in clinical pathways and management intensity. Additionally, dichotomizing survival duration into median‐ or quartile‐based categories is a simplification with inherent limitations.

The ROC results showed that in heart failure patients, when survival time was divided by the median, the AUC of TTE examination for predicting the longer survival duration was 0.591, which was not the highest, and its AUC was not significantly different with sex. When divided by the 25th and 75th percentiles, the AUC was 0.664, which was the highest and significantly different from all other indicators (Table 4). In patients with cardiomyopathy, when divided by the median, the AUC was 0.629, which was the highest, and its AUC was not significantly different with sex and CCI. When divided by the 25th and 75th percentiles, the AUC was 0.665, which was the highest but only significantly different with age. Overall, the results suggested that performing TTE examination showed exploratory discriminative value in predicting the longer survival duration based on the 25th and 75th percentiles of heart failure patients (Table 5).

The Relationship Between TTE Examination and Secondary Outcome

We further explored the relationship between secondary outcomes and TTE examination in patients with different kinds of cardiovascular diseases after adjusting for potential confounding factors including age, CCI, sex, and married status. The logistic regression showed that the TTE examination was significantly associated with the first admission to the ICU in a negative relationship among all patients with or without different kinds of cardiovascular diseases (Table 6, all p < 0.05), but positively associated with the hospital readmission in all patients (all p < 0.05).

Discussion

Long‐term hyperglycemia in diabetic patients leads to changes in myocardial substrate metabolism, resulting in diffuse fibrosis and microvascular alterations, further causing left ventricular dysfunction, posing a threat to patient life (Gulmez et al. 2018). TTE examination provides detailed cardiac structural images, measurements of cardiac chamber sizes, assessment of ventricular wall thickness, and detailed recordings of parameters such as left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter, left ventricular posterior wall end‐diastolic thickness, and interventricular septum end‐diastolic thickness, enabling accurate evaluation of cardiac function (Depla et al. 2021). However, TTE examination may be overlooked in diabetic patients with concurrent cardiovascular diseases due to the absence of complications, resulting in an incomplete understanding of patient conditions by physicians, inaccurate treatment plans, and impacts on patient prognosis (Vieillard‐Baron et al. 2019). Furthermore, TTE examination allows timely correction of the latest developments in patient conditions, facilitating the formulation of optimal treatment plans by physicians and thus potentially contributing to improved patient survival rates (Main et al. 2014). Particularly in the context of other diseases complicated by cardiovascular conditions, the importance of TTE examination in its association with patient survival rates is paramount. A study has demonstrated that goal‐directed TTE examination performed preoperatively can reduce mortality rates in patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. Compared to the control group not undergoing TTE examination, patients with hip fractures deemed to have cardiac risk factors and receiving focused preoperative TTE examination by independent anesthetists experienced a significant reduction in mortality rates (Canty et al. 2012).

In this study, we found an association between TTE examination and patient survival time in diabetic patients without complications but with different diseases. In our adjusted models, we controlled for age, CCI, sex, and marital status, which were selected based on significant between‐group differences observed in Table 1. The influence of these confounding factors on the relationship between TTE examination and prognosis differs across distinct patient populations. (1) CCI: The use of TTE may be more frequent in patients with more comorbidities due to the complexity of the condition, while a high CCI is associated with a poor prognosis (Charlson et al. 1987). Therefore, the benefits of TTE examination observed when uncorrected may partly reflect the natural prognostic advantage of patients with fewer comorbidities rather than the effects of TTE examination itself. In addition, comorbidity has a nonlinear relationship with prognosis (Bannay et al. 2016). The CCI of patients with heart failure/cardiomyopathy may have reached a high‐risk threshold. At this time, the information from TTE examination may have greater value for the formulation of treatment plans for patients. Therefore, even after correcting for CCI in these populations, TTE examination findings remain significantly associated with patient prognosis. (2) Sex: Sex‐related differences may influence both the utilization of TTE and cardiovascular prognosis. A population‐based study by Cowie et al. found that women with heart failure were less likely to undergo echocardiography than men (29% vs. 40%), although this difference was attenuated after adjusting for age (Hood et al. 2000). Furthermore, sex‐specific differences in heart failure phenotypes, with women more predisposed to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and men to heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, may lead to differential diagnostic yield and prognostic implications of TTE across sexes (Regitz‐Zagrosek 2020). Therefore, adjusting for sex helps to account for potential confounding arising from both sex‐related disparities in TTE utilization and differences in underlying cardiovascular pathophysiology. (3) Age: Elderly patients are more likely to have a poor prognosis due to decreased physiological reserve and multiple medications, and the benefits may be weakened or even offset by age‐related risks even after TTE examination. (4) Marital status: Among patients with heart failure, the event‐free survival of married patients is longer than that of unmarried patients (Chung et al. 2009). Married individuals often have better social support and economic conditions, may seek medical intervention earlier (including TTE), and exhibit better treatment compliance; they are themselves associated with a lower risk of death (Wu et al. 2012). After correction, the observed association of TTE examination may partly reflect social factors. Moreover, the diagnosis of heart failure and cardiomyopathy depends on TTE examination. Since these patients often experience frequent clinical changes, TTE is used more often in their management compared to hypertension care, which relies more on other laboratory measures. This results in both higher utilization and greater clinical value of TTE in those patients.

We also found that in diabetic patients without complications complicated by heart failure, TTE examination showed exploratory discriminative value in distinguishing patients with longer versus shorter survival time. Heart failure patients themselves represent a clinically common critical condition and are in the terminal stage of heart disease, with a heightened mortality rate (McMurray and Pfeffer 2005; Tanai and Frantz 2015). Diabetes is an independent risk factor for heart failure because it can accelerate the formation of atherosclerotic plaques, destabilize them, and further exacerbate the progression of heart failure (Dillmann 2019; Filippatos et al. 2022). Therefore, we hypothesize that when diabetic patients without complications complicated by heart failure undergo TTE examination, they may receive timely and effective treatment, thereby potentially reducing the risk of death and improving survival rates. However, it should be acknowledged that TTE receipt is a care‐process variable rather than a biomarker, and the observed associations in the ROC analyses may partly reflect differences in clinical pathways, disease severity, and management intensity rather than the independent predictive value of TTE itself.

This study is the first to examine the association between TTE examination and prognosis in diabetic patients without complications complicated by cardiovascular diseases. It also provides exploratory evidence suggesting that TTE examination may have discriminative value for survival prediction in diabetic patients without complications complicated by heart failure. However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, the study population is derived from a single tertiary care center (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, lacking global representativeness and potentially introducing population bias. Secondly, the study does not apply matching or weighting methods to reduce selection bias. Thirdly, the adjusted models controlled only for age, sex, CCI, and marital status. Given that patients selected for TTE are likely to differ systematically from those not receiving TTE in terms of illness severity, hemodynamic instability, diagnostic uncertainty, treatment intensity, and underlying cardiovascular complexity, substantial residual confounding is likely. Other potential confounders, including BMI, diabetes duration, and disease severity indicators (e.g., sequential organ failure assessment), were not included in the regression models due to data limitations. Additionally, in this study, the determination of survival time based on quartiles of population survival time lacks relevant references, which may result in inaccuracies in the results. Furthermore, although follow‐up was measured from hospital admission and TTE was performed during the index hospitalization, a residual risk of immortal time bias cannot be entirely excluded. Patients in the TTE group necessarily survived to the time of examination, and the exact timing of TTE within the hospitalization was not available. However, given that the median length of hospital stay was approximately 6 days, the potential immortal time was confined to a brief hospitalization window, which is small in magnitude relative to the 5‐year follow‐up period. Nevertheless, this interval was not formally modeled using a time‐dependent Cox model or landmark analysis, and future studies should consider treating TTE as a time‐varying covariate to more rigorously address this issue. Accordingly, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal survival benefit from TTE itself. Importantly, to directly address the concern of immortal time bias, a sensitivity analysis was performed by simulating landmark delays of 2–7 days. The results demonstrated that the associations between TTE and longer survival time in both heart failure and cardiomyopathy subgroups remained statistically significant across all assumed delay scenarios, with hazard ratios even decreasing further under longer assumed delays (Table S1 and Figure 3). This provides quantitative reassurance that the observed associations were not driven by immortal time bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that TTE examination was independently associated with longer survival time in diabetic patients without complications but with heart failure (or cardiomyopathy), and exploratory ROC analyses suggested that TTE examination had some discriminative value in distinguishing patients with longer survival duration in diabetic patients without complications but with heart failure. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the observational study design, limited covariate adjustment, and potential immortal time bias. Future prospective studies with appropriate time‐dependent exposure modeling are warranted to confirm these associations.
