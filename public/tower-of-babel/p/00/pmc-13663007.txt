Perceptions and attitudes towards obesity and metabolic and bariatric surgery for weight loss in children and adolescents: a survey among a subset of Chinese residents
Authors: YuRou Yang, Wenhuan He, GuoQiang Wang, Biao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of obesity among children and adolescents and the number of cases of bariatric surgery among children and adolescents is increasing; the discussion on weight loss among children and adolescents is becoming polarized.ObjectivesTo investigate the prevalence of overweight and obesity among a sample of Chinese children and adolescents and the perceptions and attitudes of a subset of Chinese residents towards obesity, weight loss, and MBS for weight loss in children and adolescents.MethodsCross-sectional survey among a subset of Chinese residents, collected from August 5, 2022 to October 30, 2022. Participants were a subset of Chinese population, including adults, children, and adolescents.ResultsA total of 3015 questionnaires were returned. Children and adolescents with overweight and obesity accounted for 16.6% and 9.4%. Gender, education, occupation and age were associated with the perceptions and attitudes of Chinese residents toward obesity and weight loss in children and adolescents; age, occupation and BMI were associated with the perceptions and attitudes toward MBS in children and adolescents. There are 10.8% of the respondents thought MBS was the most suitable method for children and adolescents, and 34.7% were unwilling to have MBS for their children. The percentage of respondents who thought it was appropriate for children and adolescents to undergo bariatric surgery at the age of 12–18 was 18.6%; 60.96% said they did not know enough about the surgery; most were aware that the effects of the surgery included weight loss (84.78%), improved self-confidence/ improved self-image (78.97%), improved disease (e.g., reduced drug use) (70.3%) and better performance in interpersonal interactions (51.70%).ConclusionsA subset of Chinese residents are not fully aware of the harm of obesity among children and adolescents and its related complications. They have insufficient knowledge and trust in MBS and underestimate the benefits of the procedure. More health education is still required in these areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28495-7.Keywords: Metabolic and bariatric surgery, Childhood and adolescent obesity, Perceptions and attitudes, Cross-sectional survey, Health literacy

Introduction

Along with economic growth and urbanization, childhood and adolescent obesity has become an increasingly common public health problem worldwide. Some scholars estimate that the global overweight rate of children and adolescents aged 5–17 will rise from 13.9% in 2010 to 15.8% in 2025. The number of overweight children and adolescents in China will reach 48.5 million in 2025 [1]. In 2015, the overweight and obesity rates among children and adolescents in China increased to 16.23% and 10.75% [2], respectively. There are 15.3% of children in the top five cities in China were in obesity in 2017 (19.2% of boys and 11.2% of girls) [3]. Related studies show that 65%-70% of children and adolescents are still in obesity by adulthood [4–6].

Obesity-associated medical problems in children and adolescents include arterial hypertension, dysglycemia and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) in girls, and mental disorders such as depression or attention deficit disorder [7]; and increase the risk of cancer in adulthood [8, 9] and the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in midlife [10].

Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) has been widely proven safe and effective in adults. Surgery for weight loss in children and adolescents with obesity also attracts increasing attention [11–13]. Evidence suggests that surgery in children and adolescents is safe and effective [14] and is more likely to alleviate obesity-related diseases than surgery in adulthood [15]. However, MBS in children and adolescents is rarely reported in China, which may be related to the lack of popularity of the procedure, parents’ lack of understanding and low trust in bariatric surgery, the traditional Chinese concept of avoiding medical treatment and the low regard for obesity in children and adolescents. To clarify the specific reasons, we conducted a national survey on Chinese residents, aiming to understand the current prevalence of childhood and adolescent obesity in China, the perceptions and attitudes of Chinese residents towards childhood and adolescent obesity, and the perceptions and attitudes towards MBS in children and adolescents, to contribute to better public health education, prevention and control of childhood and adolescent obesity and to make the best medical decisions at the macro level.

Participants and methods

Participants of the study

The participants of this study were a subset of Chinese residents of all ages who cooperated voluntarily, were able to use electronic devices correctly, and understood the content of the questionnaire.

Research methodology

The researchers drew on relevant professional literature [16, 17] to develop a preliminary questionnaire. In terms of content validity, five experts (including one clinical expert in metabolic and bariatric surgery, two metabolic and bariatric case managers, and two public health/nursing researchers) were invited to review and collectively evaluate the questionnaire items in terms of relevance, comprehensiveness, and clarity of expression. Revisions were made item by item based on the experts’ feedback, and finally the experts rated the items. The S-CVI/Ave was 0.88, indicating good content validity. After obtaining ethical approval (KY-2022-252), a pilot survey was conducted among 35 Chinese residents to assess their understanding of the wording and phrasing of the questions. Following focus group discussions, revisions were made based on selected suggestions, resulting in the final version of the questionnaire (Appendix 1). Regarding reliability, since this questionnaire is a descriptive survey and most items involve single factual variables (such as demographic characteristics and knowledge accuracy), rather than measuring latent psychological traits as in a standardized scale, reliability tests based on internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) are not applicable. Convenience sampling was used in this study, and the questionnaire was distributed through an online questionnaire system for Chinese residents to complete online from August 2022 to October 2025, using an online questionnaire system is called Wen Juan Xing (https://www.wjx.cn), the respondents voluntarily scan the QR code to enter the questionnaire interface.According to the sample size calculation formula for simple random sampling n = Z2α/2×p×(1 − p)/d2, with a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 3%, and considering a 20% non-response rate, the minimum sample size for this study is finally determined to be 1,281.

The questionnaire consisted of four parts, include 33 items. The first part was general demographic information of participants, including gender, age, education, occupation, height, weight, and self-evaluation of weight. The second part was general demographic information of children completed by parents, and additional information on children’s lifestyle and birth weight was collected. The third part was to assess perceptions and attitudes toward obesity and weight loss in children and adolescents. The fourth part was to assess the attitudes and perceptions towards MBS performed on children and adolescents. Those without children were not required to answer all the questions, and respondents can return to change the answer before submitting it. No financial or other material incentives were offered to the respondents. This study followed the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) [18](Appendix 2). The study design is shown in eFigure 1.

Data cleaning

During the survey implementation phase, all interviewers received standardized training and were provided with standardized instructions; mandatory fields and logic jumps were configured in the electronic questionnaires to prevent omissions and errors; for paper questionnaires, a two-person data entry and verification process was implemented to ensure data accuracy. During the data collection phase, the electronic questionnaire backend monitored response progress in real time, flagging and reviewing questionnaires with completion times that were either too short (< 180 s) or too long (> 3,600 s). Invalid questionnaires were excluded if they contained repetitive answers or had missing values for key variables exceeding 20%.

Statistical analysis

After the survey, data were exported from the online questionnaire system and double-entered by two researchers using a computer respectively to ensure the accuracy of the data. The unqualified questionnaires were also removed. All data were processed using SPSS 24.0 statistical software, and the measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation; the count data were expressed as rates and percentages. The statistics of categorical data were tested by the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method, and the difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

General information of the respondents

The total number of respondents was 3100, with 85 questionnaires found to be substandard. 3015 were complete and effective (1304 males, 43.3%; 1711 females, 56.7%); age ranged from 12 to 75 years old (mean ± standard deviation: 31.37 ± 8.73); more than 87.7% of the respondents had a bachelor’s degree or higher; 42% of the respondents were parents (23.1% had one child, 17.1% had two or more children); occupations of the respondents include education (13.7%), service industry (11.4%), healthcare (11.1%), etc.; 52.6% of respondents’ per capita monthly household income is > 9000 Chinese Yuan (CNY); body mass index (BMI) of the respondents was 22.45 ± 3.969 kg/m2, with men (23.66 ± 3.613 kg/m2) slightly higher than that of women (21.53 ± 3.982 kg/m2); 60% of the respondents’ BMI was within the normal range (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0), 28.1% were overweight (20.8%) or in obesity (7.3%). Perceptions and attitudes of respondents towards MBS for weight loss in children and adolescents

47.53% of the residents had heard about treating obesity and metabolic diseases through bariatric surgery before this survey. Most of them learned about it through websites (49.96%) and television (49.96%), while school education (8.14%), government propaganda (4.75%), self-education (0.16%), and hospital propaganda (0.16%) were the less frequently mentioned channels (eFigure 2). The largest number of people (49.57%) indicated that they wanted to learn about bariatric surgery from healthcare professionals (Fig. 2). Regarding the perception of “the role of MBS in children and adolescents”, the results showed that the scores of respondents’ perceptions of the role of bariatric surgery for children and adolescents (eTable 8) and their willingness to have their children undergo bariatric surgery (eTable 9) were statistically significant in relation to gender and occupation, with females (20%) scoring higher than males (14.2%) and healthcare being the highest scoring occupation; therefore, females (61.4%) and those in healthcare (66.9%) were also more likely to have their children with overweight or obesity undergo bariatric surgery. The people with overweight and obesity people are significantly more likely to have their children undergo bariatric surgery (90.2%). The concerns of those who were not willing to have their children undergo bariatric surgery (60.96%) were mainly lack of knowledge about the procedure(eFigure 3).

We also investigated the benefits of MBS for children and adolescents according to those who could undergo the procedure (86.10%), with weight loss (84.78%), improved self-confidence/improved self-image (78.97%), and improved disease (e.g., reduced drug use) (70.3%) being relatively well known. Still, better performance in interpersonal interactions and improved academic performance mention rates were only 51.70% and 25.39%(eFigure 4). Regarding the age at which their children should undergo surgery (eTable 10), 40% of the respondents chose “age is not an issue for me”, 18.6% of the respondents thought that surgery at the age of 12–18 was appropriate, and respondents without children (68.2%) were more unconcerned about the age at which they underwent surgery. When the child was obesity-related at the age of 7–18 and had a strong desire to undergo surgery (eTable 11), 69.2% would comply with the treatment wishes of the children themselves. In comparison, respondents without children (72.8%) and those who were overweight/obesity themselves (93.5%) were more likely to opt for surgery.

Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with residents’ perception of obesity as a disease. The independent variables included gender, age, occupation, income, and BMI. The overall model was statistically significant (Omnibus test: χ² = 99.096, df = 26, p < 0.001). The classification accuracy was 81.7%, and the area under the ROC curve was 0.631 (95% CI: 0.596–0.667).Females (OR = 1.913), middle-aged and older adults (OR = 3.39–4.35), and healthcare workers(OR = 1.988) showed higher recognition of “obesity as a disease,” whereas income level and self-reported BMI were not significantly associated with this perception.(Table 4).

Discussion

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that at least one in five children worldwide is overweight, and 41 million children are in obesity [19, 20]. According to our data, the number of children with overweight or obesity has risen since 2015 [1]. In the United States, 19.3% of children (aged 2 to 19 years) were in obesity in 2018, with 6.1% in severe obesity and another 16.1% overweight [21]. During the 2012–2013 academic year, 33% of UK sixth-grade students (aged 10–11 years) were overweight or obesity [22]. This suggests that the increase in overweight and obesity among children and adolescents is lower in the regions surveyed in this study than in the other areas. The BMI of children and adolescents was related to their birth weight and the BMI of their parents, which is the same as the results of previous studies [23–25].

Our study shows that, males with lower education, older age and lower annual household income needed to be more knowledgeable about BMI. Females could assess their children’s body size more accurately than males. The older the children, the more likely their parents were to overestimate their body size class, similar to the results of foreign studies [22]. If parents misjudge their children’s weight, they will be unable to make the best interventions for their children, so we need to develop specific programs for obesity-related health education for different populations.

In our study, for the three questions—“factors causing obesity in children and adolescents,” “complications caused by obesity,” and “the role of MBS in children and adolescents”—each question is a multiple-response item with multiple options. To facilitate statistical analysis, we assigned scores to the responses for these three questions. Each option selected by a participant was awarded one point. 45% of the respondents could correctly and completely identify the factors contributing to obesity in children and adolescents, including genetic, behavioral (diet and physical activity), psychological, and environmental factors [24]. It is worth mentioning that childhood is a crucial period for forming behaviors related to eating and physical activity and that various environmental factors influence the development of good habits [26, 27].

Our data indicates that participants have incomplete knowledge about obesity-related complications and need to popularize more obesity-related diseases; obesity not only causes harm to children and adolescents’ bodies, but also affects their mental health, personality and social skills. Some studies have shown that obesity has a negative impact on adolescents’ academic performance in China, in addition, overweight/obesity is associated with poorer functional performance on campus, social skills and reduced participation in school activities [28, 29]. Adolescents with overweight/obesity have lower scores than normal adolescents regarding relationships with teachers and other students, participation in school activities, and overall mood toward school [30]. Such adolescents are more likely to have negative psychological conditions at school, especially girls with overweight/obesity, possibly because they face more weight-related stigma than boys and may be more vulnerable to teasing or bullying [31–33].

Therefore, we should actively pay attention to the negative effects of overweight/obesity on children and adolescents in all aspects, include the harm of various complications at the physical level, the negative psychological aspects such as low self-esteem caused by discrimination and ridicule from the outside world, and whether children and adolescents have experienced unfair treatment in terms of education, resource allocation and merit evaluation in personal development, etc.

In most countries, the prevalence of overweight in children is higher than that of obesity [20]. Children who are overweight at age 5 are four times more likely to be in obesity at ages 5–14 than children of normal weight [34]. Overweight children are more likely than normal-weight or children in obesity to change their BMI classification at the one-year follow-up [35]. Therefore, childhood obesity prevention should target overweight children who are at high risk of obesity but are not yet in obesity, which may be more effective than treating children with obesity; identifying risk factors and intervening in overweight children at high risk for obesity is an effective strategy to prevent childhood obesity; and, childhood weight management strategies should begin at the earliest possible age [36]. With the relative intellectual and psychological immaturity of children and adolescents, managing obesity in these groups can be difficult. Only a combination of school life, family activities, environmental and cultural practices, and management approaches supported by parents and teachers are promising policies and strategies.

The survey found that respondents lacked sufficient understanding of MBS, which is underutilized. According to one study, only 0.04% of adolescents with severe obesity meet the indications for MBS in the United States receive MBS each year [37]. The most suitable age for surgery is 12–18 years old for 18% of the respondents, 19–29 years old for 4.6%, and age is not an issue for 40%. Regarding the timing of surgery, the American Academy of Pediatrics [37] mentioned that MBS should be adopted regardless of age as long as the operation requirements are met [34].

Age was positively correlated with the recognition that “obesity is a disease.” Middle-aged and older adults may have more direct experience of the health hazards associated with obesity.The higher recognition that “obesity is a disease” and higher support rate for surgery among females may be because women typically bear more responsibility for family health care, are more sensitive to children’s health issues, or are more concerned about the impact of obesity on their children’s future quality of life. The higher support rate among certain occupational groups (e.g., healthcare workers, educators, white-collar professionals) may be related to their level of knowledge about medical interventions, health literacy, or socioeconomic status. Considering obesity as a disease implies accepting that it requires medical intervention, and bariatric surgery naturally becomes one of the options to be considered.

As in adults, MBS in children and adolescents is equally effective in reducing weight and alleviating complications [21, 38]. The average weight loss in children and adolescents after sleeve gastrectomy (SG) is 14.1 kg/m2, and the average weight loss after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is 16.6 kg/m2 [39]. The average weight loss remains 13.1–17.0 kg/m2 5–8 years after RYGB, with 95% of patients experiencing remission of type 2 diabetes, 86% of renal abnormalities, 74% of hypertension, and 66% of dyslipidemia, patients also showed significant improvement in quality of life [14]. Accepting MBS not only resulted in sustained weight loss, improved obesity-related comorbidities and weight-related quality of life [40], the improvement in depression, anxiety, cognitive and executive functioning, and self-image after MBS in adolescents was also observed [41].

In addition, when the indications for MBS are met [42], Our data indicate that children’s treatment wishes also influence the parents’ medical decisions.Respondents were concerned about postoperative complications or adverse effects like malnutrition, impact on puberty development…etc, which are common postoperative complications [43]. One study showed that the rate of complications during postoperative hospitalization decreased from 8.8% in 2005 to 2.0% in 2014 [44], with more pronounced iron deficiency anemia and vitamin B12 deficiency observed in long-term follow-up, with 46% of patients suffering from anemia and 78% reporting low vitamin D levels three years after surgery [45–48]. Another study comparing outcomes in adolescents (14–18 years old) and adults (25–50 years old) who underwent RYGB found similar weight loss in both groups (26% vs. 29%). Still, significantly higher rates of T2D remission (86% vs. 53%) and hypertension remission (68% vs. 41%) in the adolescent group at five years postoperatively [49]. This is particularly important because the youth-onset T2D subtype is more aggressive than the adult-onset T2D subtype [50, 51]. Early intervention can save potential islet function, prevent irreversible β-cell damage and prevent vascular remodelling and consequent atherosclerosis [50, 52, 53]. The results of the study also showed a greater reduction in BMI and weight in adolescents compared to adults at 4 years postoperatively [50, 52–54]. This evidence emphasizes the importance of early MBS for children and adolescents with obesity or severe complications [55].

Several studies have shown that the quality and content of information about MBS on the Internet is usually poor [56, 57]. Therefore, when we surveyed residents about the sources they would like to learn about MBS, the majority wanted to know about it from healthcare professionals (49.57%), probably because they felt that the information they received was more authoritative. Some studies have shown that people with obesity who have received services from a weight management medical team are more likely to accept MBS because these patients may be better informed about the benefits of surgery and the risks of severe obesity [58]. Most children and adolescents are still in school, so students’ knowledge of MBS and obesity can be enhanced through health education in schools. It is worth noting that 30.43% of people want to get information from government promotion materials, which is a big difference from the percentage of people who get information from government propaganda (4.75%). Therefore, to popularize the knowledge related to weight loss and MBS, we can adopt medical and nursing staff combined with various media ways to increase promotion, such as short videos and tweets released by official hospital accounts. And relevant government departments should pay more attention to the publicity of MBS to increase the public awareness rate and provide authoritative information support for people with related medical service needs [59, 60]. Weight bias and obesity stigma also hinder people from receiving treatments, which should raise our concerns [61]. Implementation of artificial intelligence such as large language models (LLMs) is also a novel way for health education [62].

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first national survey on the perceptions and attitudes of a subset of Chinese residents towards obesity and MBS for weight loss in children and adolescents. The questionnaire data collection is comprehensive, including general data from adults and adolescents, and a subset of Chinese residents' perceptions and attitudes towards adolescent obesity, weight loss and MBS, which further enriches the relevant information and evidence at home and abroad and provides a basis for improving the utilization rate of MBS for children and adolescents with obesity. However, the present study also has some limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting our results. The present study actually employed an online convenience sampling method. This sampling strategy introduced potential biases in several aspects, significantly limiting the representativeness, generalizability, and external validity of the research findings. A detailed explanation is provided below.

Selection Bias. Online convenience sampling relies on participants voluntarily clicking on links, scanning QR codes, or actively engaging in the study through specific platforms (e.g., social media, online communities, survey crowdsourcing platforms). This non-probability sampling approach means that the composition of the sample is not based on random principles but is instead constrained by factors such as participant accessibility, interest, time, and motivation. Consequently, the study sample may exhibit systematic differences from non-participants on key variables (e.g., health literacy, internet usage habits, social attitudes, economic status). This selection bias implies that any effects or associations observed in this study may be either overestimated or underestimated and cannot be simply generalized to the entire Chinese population.

Conclusions

A subset of Chinese residents are not fully aware of the harm of obesity among children and adolescents and its related complications; their attitude toward weight reduction and MBS for children and adolescents is relatively conservative, mainly because they do not know enough about the surgery and underestimate the harm of obesity in children and adolescents and the positive effects that the surgery can bring. We should join hands with hospitals, schools, communities and policy makers to focus on the physical and mental health of children and adolescents with overweight and obesity and strengthen health education on weight management and obesity prevention to regulate personal behavior better and promote public health to create a positive health culture. Objectively publicize the requirements, effects and complications of MBS in multiple ways so that children and adolescents in need and their parents can carefully choose treatment in the context of the benefits and risks associated with surgery.
