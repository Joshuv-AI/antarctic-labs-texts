A Case Report of Proximal Radioulnar Osteosarcoma in a Great Dane Dog
Authors: Prakash Adhikari, Abishek Khanal, Dinesh Kumar Singh, Binita Kunwar, Ayushma Aryal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in dogs, characterized by aggressive local invasion and early metastatic potential, most frequently affecting the appendicular skeleton of large and giant breeds. This report describes a rare case of proximal radioulnar osteosarcoma in a 3.5‐year‐old intact male Great Dane that presented with progressive left forelimb lameness, swelling distal to the elbow, and weight loss. Radiographic examination revealed an aggressive mixed lytic–proliferative lesion involving the proximal radius and ulna, with distinct margins and no radiographic evidence of pulmonary metastasis. A definitive diagnosis of proximal radioulnar osteosarcoma was established by histopathological examination following forelimb amputation with scapulectomy. The dog underwent surgery and showed good postoperative recovery, with no evidence of recurrence during a 6‐month follow‐up period. Adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended to prevent metastasis, but the owner′s financial constraints did not allow for this treatment. This case highlights the importance of early recognition, comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, and timely surgical intervention in managing canine osteosarcoma, particularly at uncommon anatomical sites.

1. Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OSA) is a malignant mesenchymal bone tumor characterized by the production of osteoid or immature bone by neoplastic cells and is associated with aggressive local behavior and a high propensity for metastasis [1, 2]. It is the most prevalent type of bone cancer in dogs, constituting 85% of all tumors that come from the skeleton [3]. The appendicular skeleton is most affected, particularly the metaphyseal regions of long bones such as the distal radius, proximal humerus, distal femur, and proximal tibia [4, 5]. Axial skeletal involvement occurs less frequently, representing approximately 20% of cases. Canine OSA frequently metastasizes, most commonly to the lungs and regional lymph nodes [6, 7]. Although middle‐aged to older dogs are most often affected, a bimodal age distribution has been reported, with a secondary peak in younger dogs between 18 and 24 months of age. Male dogs and large to giant breeds, including Great Danes, are at increased risk [8–10].

Clinically, affected dogs often present with progressive lameness, localized pain, and swelling, and pathological fractures may occur in advanced cases [4]. Radiography plays a critical role in the initial assessment of suspected OSA, typically revealing aggressive bone changes such as osteolysis, periosteal proliferation, cortical destruction, and soft tissue swelling. However, histopathology remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis [11–13].

OSA of the proximal radioulnar region is rarely reported. This case report describes the clinical, radiographic, hematological, and histopathological findings of proximal radioulnar OSA in a Great Dane dog for the first time from Nepal.

1.1. Clinical History and Examination

A 3.5‐year‐old intact male Great Dane weighing 41 kg was presented to the Central Referral Veterinary Hospital, Kathmandu, Nepal, with a 4‐month history of progressive left forelimb lameness and swelling distal to the elbow as shown in Figure 1. There was no reported history of trauma. The dog also exhibited progressive weight loss during the same period. Prior medical management with analgesics, including gabapentin and joint supplements, had failed to produce clinical improvement.

Swelling of the left forelimb over the proximal radius and ulna, just below the elbow.

On physical examination, a firm, nonmobile swelling was palpated over the proximal radius and ulna. The dog was unable to extend the affected limb fully and showed marked pain on palpation. Submandibular lymph nodes were within normal limits. Vital parameters were stable, including a heart rate of 80 bpm, respiratory rate of 21 bpm, and rectal temperature of 102.1°F. Gingival mucous membranes were pale, with a capillary refill time of approximately 2 s.

1.2. Hematology and Serum Biochemistry

A complete blood count revealed mild leukocytosis (18,100 cells/μL) with lymphocytosis (6154 cells/μL). Mild anemia was noted (RBC count: 4.8 × 106/μL; PCV: 35.6%). Serum biochemistry values were within reference ranges, indicating no overt systemic organ dysfunction at presentation.

1.3. Radiographic Findings

Mediolateral and craniocaudal radiographs of the left forelimb demonstrated marked irregular osteolysis with a mixed lytic–proliferative pattern and poorly defined margins involving the proximal radius and ulna, consistent with an aggressive bone lesion (Figure 2). Thoracic radiographs revealed no visible pulmonary metastases (Figure 3). Based on these findings, a presumptive diagnosis of primary bone neoplasia, most consistent with OSA, was made. Differential diagnoses included fibrosarcoma, osteomyelitis, osteoma, and healing fracture.

Mediolateral radiograph of the left elbow and proximal antebrachium showing aggressive osteolysis with a “moth‐eaten” appearance, severe periosteal proliferation, and surrounding soft tissue swelling.

Right lateral thoracic radiograph demonstrating no visible evidence of pulmonary metastasis.

1.4. Histopathology

Following forelimb amputation, histopathological evaluation was performed on sections of the affected radius and ulna. Microscopic examination of this bony tissue revealed a densely cellular malignant mesenchymal neoplasm composed of pleomorphic spindle to polygonal cells with moderate to abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nuclei. Marked anisocytosis and anisokaryosis were present, along with frequent mitotic figures, including atypical forms. Irregular eosinophilic neoplastic osteoid and foci of immature woven bone were directly associated with neoplastic cells. Multifocal areas of necrosis and hemorrhage were also observed (Figure 4). These findings were diagnostic of osteoblastic OSA.

Histopathological section showing a cellular malignant mesenchymal tumor composed of pleomorphic spindle to polygonal cells with moderate to abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nuclei. Irregular eosinophilic neoplastic osteoid and foci of mineralized bone are present. Abnormal mitotic figures, areas of necrosis, and hemorrhage are also observed. H&E stain.

1.5. Treatment and Outcome

Treatment options, including limb amputation with adjuvant chemotherapy, limb amputation alone, limb‐sparing surgery, and palliative care, were discussed with the owner. Scapulectomy with forelimb amputation was elected without adjuvant chemotherapy (Figure 5).

Intraoperative photograph showing a subtotal scapula resection (scapulectomy) being performed on a canine patient under general anesthesia.

Surgery was performed following standard techniques described in small animal surgical texts [14–17]. The limb was removed along with the axillary and prescapular lymph nodes. Postoperative management included antibiotic therapy (cefixime, 10 mg/kg PO BID for 10 days) and analgesia (tramadol, 3 mg/kg PO BID for 3 days). The surgical site healed uneventfully (Figure 6), and sutures were removed after 14 days. The dog adapted well to three‐limbed ambulation within 1 week. No clinical evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis was observed during a 6‐month follow‐up period.

Postoperative follow‐up of the patient 4 weeks after a left scapulectomy, demonstrating excellent surgical site healing, complete hair regrowth margins, and normal weight‐bearing on the affected forelimb.

2. Discussion

This case describes a rare presentation of appendicular OSA involving the proximal radius and ulna in a young adult Great Dane. Although OSA most commonly affects middle‐aged to older dogs, the age of this patient aligns with reports of a secondary incidence peak in younger dogs [8]. The breed and body size of the dog further support established risk factors for canine OSA [9, 10].

Radiographic findings in this case were characteristic of aggressive primary bone neoplasia and facilitated early clinical suspicion. The absence of detectable pulmonary metastasis on thoracic radiographs suggests early‐stage disease at presentation; however, micrometastatic disease cannot be excluded, as subclinical metastasis is common in canine OSA [12, 18]. A notable limitation of this case presentation involves the reliance on conventional radiography for the macroscopic identification of the tumor. While plain radiographs are excellent for detecting cortical destruction and periosteal reactions, they cannot provide an accurate macroscopic assessment of the actual tumor mass. Radiographs often underestimate the true extension of the tumor within the medullary cavity. Furthermore, it cannot accurately delineate tumor margins or map out soft tissue involvement. Advanced imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography, are essential to precisely evaluate local tumor spread and plan optimal surgical margins [19]. Unfortunately, these advanced diagnostics were unavailable in our setting, representing a clear limitation in our structural mapping of the lesion.

Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating malignant mesenchymal cells producing neoplastic osteoid, the defining feature of OSA. The degree of cellular pleomorphism, mitotic activity, and presence of necrosis were consistent with a high‐grade tumor, correlating with the aggressive biological behavior typically associated with appendicular OSA.

Although amputation combined with adjuvant chemotherapy remains the standard of care for canine OSA, the financial constraints of the owner did not allow chemotherapy in this case. Despite this, the dog showed good short‐term postoperative recovery and functional outcome. Long‐term prognosis remains guarded, as metastatic disease commonly develops despite local tumor control [20–23]. Regular re‐evaluations every 3 months were recommended for early detection of pulmonary or skeletal metastasis.

3. Conclusion

This case report documents a rare occurrence of proximal OSA of the radius and ulna in a Great Dane dog, the first time from Nepal. Early recognition based on clinical signs and radiographic findings, followed by confirmatory histopathology and timely surgical intervention, resulted in a favorable short‐term outcome. The short 6‐month follow‐up, lack of CT or MRI scans for tumor staging, and the owner′s financial constraints preventing recommended chemotherapy were the major challenges impacting long‐term evaluation in this case. This report underscores the importance of considering OSA as a differential diagnosis for aggressive bone lesions at atypical anatomical sites and highlights the value of early diagnostic and therapeutic intervention.
