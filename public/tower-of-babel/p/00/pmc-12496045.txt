Health systems’ adaptations to climate change: an umbrella review of global evidence protocol
Authors: Katarzyna Dubas‐Jakóbczyk, Nsikakabasi Samuel George, Costase Ndayishimiye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth systems’ (HS) adaptations to climate change (CC) cover two major, and interrelated dimensions: (1) Environmental sustainability—actions aimed at limiting the negative impact of HS on the environment (eg, by reducing greenhouse gas emissions) and (2) Climate resilience—adaptations focused on improving HS’ ability to cope with the impact of CC (eg, by improving HS preparedness to climate-induced natural disasters). Within both dimensions, a diversity of actions, at different HS levels, can take place. The general objective is to provide health policy makers with a comprehensive evidence-based set of recommendations on the scope and effectiveness of HS adaptations to CC.Methods and analysisAn umbrella review will be conducted. Systematic reviews will be included if: (1) They focus on HS adaptations to CC (including both environmental sustainability and climate resilience strategies/actions), (2) Were published since 2015 and (3) Report a quality appraisal of included studies. Five databases were searched: (1) MEDLINE via PubMed, (2) Scopus, (3) Web of science core collection, (4) ProQuest Central and (5) The Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Two reviewers will independently assess studies’ eligibility, conduct quality appraisal and perform data extraction. Data will be synthesised using both quantitative and qualitative methods. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses will guide the reporting of results.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required, as this study involves the collection and analysis of secondary data only. The results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and disseminated via dedicated research channels and social media platforms.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251052647.Keywords: Health, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, Climate Change, Systematic Review

Introduction

Climate change (CC) constitutes one of the most important threats to human health worldwide.1,4 It increases the frequency of extreme weather events (eg, droughts, floods, hurricanes) and modifies the transmission patterns of food-borne, water-borne and zoonotic diseases.2 4 Both climate-related long-term stress (eg, due to sea-level rise risks) and sudden shocks (eg, heatwaves) can have a major impact on a HS and its capacity to respond.5 The WHO estimates that between 2030 and 2050, CC will cause approximately 250 000 additional deaths per year from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone.4 The 2024 World Economic Forum estimated that, by 2050, CC might lead to an additional 14.5 million deaths and $12.5 trillion in economic losses worldwide.6 Health systems (HS) have been under strong pressure to adapt to CC challenges for many years now. At the same time, the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (predicts that more severe climate risks will appear sooner than previously expected, making the necessary adaptations both urgent and harder to implement.1

HS adaptations to CC cover two major, interrelated dimensions. On the one hand, there is a need to limit the negative impact of HS on the environment; on the other hand, HS must adapt to better cope with the impact of CC (figure 1). The WHO defines HS’s ‘environmental sustainability’ as its ability to ‘improve, maintain or restore health, while minimising negative impacts on the environment and leveraging opportunities to restore and improve it (…)’, while ‘climate resilience’ refers to the HS’s capability to ‘anticipate, respond to, cope with, recover from and adapt to climate-related shocks and stress (…)’.7 The WHO’s operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon HS3 defines ten components through which adaptations to CC can take place, aligned with the six building blocks of HS: leadership and governance, health workforce, health information system, medical products and technologies, service delivery and financing. The framework emphasises the need for a comprehensive approach, due to the strong mutual synergies between the environmental sustainability and climate resilience dimensions of HS.3

The dual context of the health system’s adaptations to climate change.

Within both dimensions, a variety of actions can be implemented at different HS levels. For environmental sustainability, the main focus is on reducing HS’ carbon footprint. This includes both direct greenhouse gas emissions from health facilities and indirect emissions generated by purchased energy and the supply chain.8 Examples of adaptation measures include: changes in clinical practice to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and low-value care, investments in renewable energy sources, more sustainable waste management, reducing active travel by the health workforce and incorporating sustainability rules/requirements into procurement contracts.9 10 In terms of climate resilience, adaptations focus on strengthening the HS’ preparedness for the impacts of CC, including vulnerability and risks assessment, as well as the system’s capacity to manage climate-related shock and stresses.5 Examples could include: implementing early warning systems, planning back-up systems for service delivery during climate-induced disasters, investing in climate-proofing health infrastructure, and training the health workforce in disaster management.5 11 A range of both direct and indirect measures can be applied to assess the effectiveness of strategies within both HS’s environmental sustainability and climate resilience dimensions. The most valid indicator for environmental sustainability strategies is the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions (eg, in kg per year or per specific healthcare service/product life cycle).12 However, in practice, various indirect measures are also commonly used, such as reductions in plastic waste or water usage per patient/procedure, or the inclusion of sustainability clauses in procurement regulations.12 13 For HS’s climate resilience adaptations, the WHO proposes two sets of measures: population-level outcomes (eg, the number of deaths or persons affected by climate-related disasters per 100 000 population, heat-related excess mortality) and system-level health outcomes (eg, frequency of infrastructure damage and/or the value of economic losses due to climate-related disasters).5

There is an abundance of research work conducted within this topic, including published quite recently (since 2021), numerous evidence syntheses, mostly in the form of systematic914,20 and scoping literature reviews10,1221. These reviews usually focus on one dimension of HS adaptations: either environmental sustainability910 17,20 or climate resilience1114 15 23,25. Many concentrate on specific types of adaptations (eg, telemedicine,18 workforce planning,15 behavioural change interventions19 or particular elements of the HS (eg, general practice17). There is also a recently published review of literature reviews, yet covering mostly scoping and not systematic literature reviews.25 From an evidence-informed health policy perspective, systematic reviews provide much stronger evidence base than for example, scoping reviews, as they follow a standard methodological approach that requires conducting quality assessment of the included studies.26 The primary objective of an umbrella review of systematic reviews is to provide decision makers with clear recommendations and an overarching understanding of a broad topic area.27 This type of review is especially useful when multiple, recent and not yet outdated systematic reviews are available on the topic.28

We will conduct an umbrella review of systematic reviews focused on HS adaptations to CC, published within the last decade (since 2015), in which quality assessment of the included studies was performed. The adaptations will cover both dimensions of HS’ response: environmental sustainability and climate resilience. Our general objective is to provide health policy makers with a comprehensive set of evidence-based recommendations on the scope and effectiveness of HS adaptations to CC.

The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO)29 and the open science framework30 registries were searched to confirm that no similar umbrella review has been registered or is currently in development.

Methods and analysis

This work will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) recommendations for evidence synthesis—umbrella reviews methodological guidelines27 31 and the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses checklist.26 The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251052647).32 The preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis protocols checklist33 was used as a general guidance for the protocol preparation. The preliminary searches were conducted in July 2025, with screening scheduled to begin in September 2025 and the work expected to be finalised in the first quarter of 2026.

Inclusion criteria

We will include systematic reviews that focus on HS adaptations to CC, specifically addressing: I—environmental sustainability, and II—climate resilience. For both concepts, we will apply the WHO definitions.7 For the purposes of this study, a ‘systematic review’ will be defined as a study that meets the following criteria: (1) A minimum of two databases were searched and (2) A pre-defined instrument was used to assess the quality of the included studies. The population, intervention, comparison, outcomes and study design framework34 will be used to define explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria (table 1).

Search strategy

The following databases were searched: (1) MEDLINE via PubMed, (2) Scopus, (3) Web of science core collection, (4) ProQuest central and (5) Cochrane database of systematic reviews. The search strategy was developed in accordance with PREES guidelines35 and combined multiple terms related to: ‘climate change’ AND ‘health system’ AND ‘systematic review’ (table 2). Where available, a dedicated filter for systematic reviews was applied. Terms were searched as keywords in the title and/or abstract fields. When appropriate, Medical subject headings terms were also used. We searched for studies published since 2015, with full text available in English. An example of the search strategy conducted in Medline via PubMed is provided in online supplemental file 1. Prior to finalisation, the strategy was to be tested in all five databases to ensure accuracy and relevance. In addition, the references of included studies will be screened to identify any additional systematic reviews that meet the inclusion criteria.

Study selection

All records will be downloaded and deduplicated in the Rayyan software,36 which will also support the study selection process. This process will include two stages: (1) Title and abstract screening and (2) Full-text assessment. Two researchers will independently participate in both stages and assess records/full texts based on the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria (table 1). Any discrepancies will be resolved through consensus between the two researchers.

Quality assessment

The critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews and research syntheses27 31 will be used to evaluate the quality of included systematic reviews. This tool, suitable for both quantitative and qualitative studies, comprises eleven items/questions, each rated as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’ (table 3). A scoring guide tailored to the context of this umbrella review will be developed, including defined criteria for interpreting each item and establishing a cut-off score for identifying low-quality reviews. For example, an answer of ‘yes’ will be scored as 1.0 points, ‘unclear’ as 0.5 points and ‘no’ as 0.0 points. Reviews with a total score above 75% of the maximum possible points (which may differ between reviews depending on the number of ‘not applicable’ answers) will be assessed as high quality; those scoring between 50% and 75% as moderate quality; and those scoring up to 50% as low quality. Two researchers will independently assess the quality of the included studies. Results will be compared and discussed until a consensus is reached.

Data extraction

The data extraction table will be developed based on the JBI recommendations for umbrella review methods.31 It will cover several sections reflecting the main characteristics of the included studies, along with details of the data extracted and coding for further analysis where appropriate (table 4). For studies covering a broader topic, only data related to HS adaptations will be extracted. The data extraction table will be pilot-tested on five included studies and revised as necessary. Two researchers will independently extract the data, compare their results and discuss any discrepancies to reach an agreement.

Data summary

Data will be summarised using both quantitative and qualitative methods. Both tabular and graphical data presentation (eg, structured mapping approaches) forms will be used. Initial scan indicated that most of the included reviews might have a narrative character; thus, quantitative statistical analysis of results may not be feasible to perform. We will apply thematic analysis (deductive and/or inductive) to summarise and map the data on the scope of adaptation strategies. We will use existing conceptual frameworks (eg, proposed by Braithwaite et al three categories of HS levels9 and the WHO framework for building climate resilient and low-carbon HS)3 or adjust them as appropriate. For the evidence on effectiveness of particular strategies, we will follow JBI recommendations on evidence summaries from both quantitative and qualitative research syntheses in umbrella reviews.31 This approach allows for providing a simple, visual indication of the main results, which in our case may include, for example, the effectiveness of a given adaptation strategy in reducing HS greenhouse gas emissions or improving health workers’ capabilities to deal with climate-related health shocks. The results of the quality appraisal of the included systematic reviews, together with the data extracted on the evidence certainty assessment (if available), will be used to draw final conclusions and formulate recommendations for policy.37

Patient and public involvement

In the design, writing or editing of this scoping review protocol, neither patients or the general public were involved.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required, as only secondary data will be collected. The results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal for publication and disseminated via the social media channels of the authors and their affiliated institutions, as well as dedicated research platforms. The findings will also be shared with key stakeholders through summaries (eg, via the knowledge transfer platform of the main author’s university). If the protocol needs to be modified after publication, the details and justification for any amendments will be reported.
