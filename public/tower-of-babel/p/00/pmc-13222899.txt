Herkinorin Exerted Neuroprotective Effects After Spinal Cord Injury by Suppression of NF-κB Pathway Activation
Authors: Jianyong Wang, Jiacheng Yu, Wen Han, Chaochao Ding, HaiFeng Wang, Siqi Chen, Yi Huang, Wenting Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The disruption of axonal continuity and neuronal loss at the injury site leads to structural disconnections as well as functional consequences in spinal cord injury. Herkinorin, a structurally unique non-nitrogenous opioid receptor agonist, demonstrates significant neuroprotective efficacy in models of ischemic brain injury and epilepsy. In this report, we aimed to explore the role and underlying mechanism of Herkinorin in axon regeneration and functional recovery in SCI. Oxygen–glucose deprivation and restoration (OGD/R) condition was used in cell line rat pheochromocytoma cells (PC12) and primary cortical neurons to mimic SCI context in vitro. and apoptosis of the cells were detected. A rat SCI model was conducted by Allen’s impactor. Cells were treated with Herkinorin under OGD/R condition. Footprint analysis and BBB scale was to evaluate locomotor function. Western blot and immunofluorescence staining of proteins Map2, GAP43 and Ace-tubulin to assess microtubule stabilization and Bax, Bad, Bcl2 as well as cleaved caspase3 to detect apoptosis. The TNFα was employed to determine the expression profiles of NF-κB. Herkinorin alleviated OGD/R-induced neuronal apoptosis and axonal damage in PC12 cells and primary neurons by enhancing microtubule stability and inhibiting NF-κB, effects abolished by TNFα. Crucially, in a rat SCI model, Herkinorin improved locomotor function (BBB scores, gait) and mitigated spinal cord neuronal loss and microtubule disassembly. Herkinorin confers neuroprotection and pro-regenerative effects against neuronal injury both in vitro and in vivo, mechanistically linked to microtubule stabilization and NF-κB pathway suppression, positioning it as a promising candidate for SCI therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11064-026-04799-3.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI), a chronic neural damage, causes over 2 million individuals worldwide suffering from severe burden both in health and economics [1]. Axon regeneration and reconnection with target tissues are reported as the central challenge in SCI repair [2]. Although decades of efforts like stem cell transplantation [3, 4], biomaterial transplantation [5, 6] or physical stimulation [5] have been demonstrated preclinical potential for axonal regeneration and functional recovery after SCI, few satisfactory efficacies have yet emerged to reliably restore neurological function. Although reports on mono-pharmacotherapy remain limited, significant potential still deserves attention for promoting axonal growth. Diosgenin exerted demonstrated robust axonal growth activity in cerebral cortical and hippocampal neurons as evidenced by both in vitro and in vivo studies [7, 8]. (–)-Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) mediated a neuroprotective effect attributed to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [9]. Moreover, some molecules reach clinical trial phase, highlighting their translational promise. AXER-204 (soluble Nogo-Receptor-Fc decoy) was assessed in a first-in-human, randomized trial for chronic cervical SCI in USA [10]. Two phase 3, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials were performed to evaluate fampridine-SR for treatment of spasticity in chronic SCI [11]. Double-blind randomized controlled trial of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor for neurological recovery was employed in chronic incomplete traumatic SCI after rehabilitation [12]. These pose a great interest in small molecules to the clinical treatment of SCI.

Critically, the secondary injury cascade following SCI is heavily driven by neuroinflammation and cell death, in which the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‑κB) pathway plays a master regulatory role. After SCI, persistent activation of NF‑κB within the spinal cord exacerbates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), contributes to neuronal and glial apoptosis, and creates an inhibitory microenvironment hostile to axonal growth [13, 14]. Importantly, targeted inhibition of the NF‑κB pathway specifically in SCI models has been shown to attenuate this damaging cascade, reduce lesion volume, enhance the expression of regeneration-associated genes, and ultimately promote functional recovery [13, 15, 16] Thus, modulating NF‑κB signaling represents a validated therapeutic axis for SCI.

Parallelly, the opioid receptor system, particularly the delta-opioid receptor (DOR), has emerged as a key endogenous modulator of neuroprotection. In the context of SCI, activation of DOR has been reported to alleviate injury-induced mechanical hypersensitivity [17], limit neuronal loss, and improve motor function recovery [18]. Mechanistically, DOR-mediated protection is suggested to involve the mitigation of excitotoxicity, stabilization of mitochondrial function, and importantly, the modulation of inflammatory responses [15, 19]. Notably, evidence suggests a potential crosstalk between opioid receptor signaling and the NF‑κB pathway, where certain agonists can exert anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects partly through suppressing NF‑κB activation [20]. This intersection presents a compelling strategy for developing multi-targeted therapies for SCI.

Herkinorin, a novel opioid receptor agonist, is a new generation of analgesics. Xu et al. reported the neuroprotection of Herkinorin against cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice [21]. Meanwhile, Herkinorin was proved to ameliorate neuronal injury by inhibiting NF‐κB in an epilepsy rat model [20]. However, its specific role in the complex pathophysiology of SCI, and whether its beneficial actions involve the modulation of SCI-specific NF‑κB dynamics to foster axonal regeneration, remains entirely unexplored.

In this study, we investigate, for the first time, the therapeutic potential of Herkinorin in SCI—a pathophysiologically distinct condition from previously studied brain injury models. Beyond assessing neuronal survival, we specifically focus on Herkinorin’s ability to promote axonal regeneration, a critical yet unexplored endpoint for this compound. Using both in vitro OGD/R models and in vivo rat contusion SCI models, we test the hypothesis that Herkinorin promotes functional recovery and axonal regrowth through NF-κB pathway inhibition. All together may provide novel therapeutic insights for SCI treatment.

Material and Methods

Oxygen–Glucose Deprivation/Restoration (OGD/R) Model

PC12 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China)) were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (Thermo Fisher HyClone, Utah, USA) with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientifc, MA, USA) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then cells were placed with deoxygenated, glucose-free medium in hypoxia chamber (1% O2, 37 °C) for 2 h, then back to normal conditions for 12 h.

Animals Care

Around 200 Adult female Sprague-Dawley rats (weight: 250–300 g; age: 6–8 weeks), obtained from the Animal Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Hangzhou, China). Rats were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) context (12:12 light–dark cycle, 22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity) were accessible to food and water. The care and use of all animals in this study were approved by Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (Zhejiang Province, China, Approval No. xmsq-2020-0796) and in accordance with he NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

SCI Model and Treatment

Anesthesia was induced with 10% (w/v) Chloral Hydrate Solution (Cat: G0340, Solarbio). SCI model in rats was triggered by Allen’s weight-drop impactor model under aseptic surgical conditions as described before [22]. Rats in the Sham group underwent spinal cord exposure only. For the SCI groups, a laminectomy ((MASIS impactor (W.M. Keck Center for Collaborative Neuroscience Rutgers, USA)) was performed at the T9-T10 vertebral level to expose the spinal cord, which was then compressed with forceps for 1 min (10 g weight, 50 mm height). Throughout surgery, all rats were randomly assigned to two blinded operators. Preliminary model success was indicated by tail-flick reflex, hindlimb convulsions followed by flaccidity, and visible spinal cord congestion without rupture. Incisions were then sutured layer by layer. Final confirmation was based on flaccid hindlimb paralysis and a BBB score below 2 points after recovery. Assessments were conducted within 30 min post-anesthesia and again 24 h postoperatively. The surgical procedure achieved a model success rate of 90% with a postoperative mortality of 8%. Surviving rats were randomly assigned to groups following surgery and maintained under standard conditions at five animals per cage. After injury, Her (10 mg/kg, CAS No. 862073-77-6, MedChemExpress LLC, USA) was daily administrated via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection throughout the process of SCI until sacrifice at 1,3,5,7,14 and 28 days post-injury (dpi). Postoperative care included manual bladder expression and housing in protective cages. Behavioral recovery and electrophysiological responses were assessed systematically to evaluate therapeutic outcomes.

Drug Administration

Herkinorin (CAS No. 862073-77-6, MedChemExpress LLC, USA, purity 99.99%) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution and stored in the dark at − 20 °C. For in vitro studies, the stock was diluted with complete culture medium to a working concentration of 1 µM, ensuring the final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.1%. Neurons were pretreated with Herkinorin 2 h prior to oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) and the drug was maintained throughout OGD and the subsequent 24 h reoxygenation. Vehicle controls received complete medium containing 0.1% DMSO under otherwise identical conditions. TNF-α (31–45, CAS No. 862073-77-6, MedChemExpress LLC, USA, purity 99.99%), was dissolved in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution and diluted with complete culture medium to a working concentration of 5 µM.

For in vivo administration: Herkinorin was prepared in physiological saline (0.0.1 M, Cat: P1020, Solarbio) with 5% DMSO as cosolvent. A single intrathecal dose (10 mg/kg) was administered 30 min post-injury. Sham and SCI groups received equal volumes of saline containing 5% DMSO. All solutions were sterilized by 0.22 µm filtration and tested for sterility before use.

Enhanced Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) Cell Viability Assay

PC12 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells/well in 100 μl complete medium. The system then stayed in the cell incubator for 24 h for attachment. Cells were randomly assigned as follows: vehicle group (routine culture), OGD/R group (4 h of oxygen–glucose deprivation + 24 h of reoxygenation), Her group (OGD/R + treatment with different concentrations of Her, 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 or 2 μm). Cells were treated with Herkinorin (Her) at concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 or 2 μm to evaluate cell viability. Vehicle and OGD/R groups received equivalent volumes of vehicle solution (DMSO/saline mixture). Next, cells were treated with Her, TNFα or OGD/R for 2 h before enhanced CCK-8 t reagent (10 μL/well, CAS No. CA1210, Solarbio life science) for 1 h under standard culture conditions. Finally, absorbance was measured at 450 nm. A final working concentration of Her (1 μm) was used for subsequent experiments. TNFα (5uM) was employed as a specific NF‐κB activator. Both drugs were dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were randomly assigned in the following experiments: vehicle group, OGD/R group, Her group (OGD/R + 1 μm Her), OGD/R + Her group (1 μm), OGD/R + Her + TNFα group.

Locomotor Recovery Evaluation

Basso-Beattie-Bresnahan (BBB) locomotion scale was conducted on postoperative days 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. Rats were placed in an open-field arena (100 × 100 cm) and observed by two trained evaluators (using the 21-point BBB scale) for 5 min: 0-no observable hindlimb movement; 21-normal coordinated locomotion. Final scores represented the average of three independent blinded assessments. Footprint analysis at 14dpi was to obverse the gait of animals. Hindpaws were dipped in red nontoxic dye, forepaws in blue dye. A narrow corridor (100 × 10 × 20 cm) on white paper was for rats to walk through and the whole images were captured.

Tissue Processing

Tissue samples for histopathological and molecular analysis were collected at specified time points after SCI. Animals assigned for Western blot analysis were euthanized on days 1, 7, 14, and 28 post-surgery (n = 5 per group per time point). The spinal cord segments containing the lesion epicenter were rapidly dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until protein extraction. Animals for histological assessment were processed at the same final time points. Briefly, rats were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of 110% (w/v) Chloral Hydrate Solution and transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by ice-cold 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA). A 10 mm spinal cord segment centered exactly on the lesion epicenter was then dissected and post-fixed in the same 4% PFA solution for 48 h at 4 °C. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin using Leica HistoCore Arcadia. Using a rotary microtome (Leica HistoCore AUTOCUT), serial longitudinal (sagittal) sections across the lesion region and transverse (coronal) sections were cut at 5 μm thickness. Sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides for subsequent staining.

Western Blot

Protein lysates from animal tissues or PC12 cells were quantified using Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Assay (Pierce™). 40 μg of total protein per sample was prepared in Laemmli buffer (4×). Samples were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (w/v) at 100 V for 1.5 h before transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm, Bio-Rad) for 1.5 h. Then, membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1.5 h at room temperature (RT) following incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. Primary antibodies were as follows: Bax (1:2000, Abcam, ab32503), Bad (1:2000, Abcam, ab90435), Bcl2 (1:2000, Abcam, ab194583) and Cleaved caspase3 (1:2000, Abcam, ab32042), Ace-tubulin (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technologies, CST, #5335), Map2 (1:2000, CST, #4542), GAP43(1:2000, CST, #8945), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536), CST #3033) (1:2000, CST, #3033), NF‐κB p65 (Ser536) (1:2000, CST, #8242). Then, membranes were probed with by Horseradish Peroxidase-conjugated (HRP) secondary secondaries (1 h RT) before visualized by Chemi DocXRS + Imaging System (Bio‐Rad) and analyzed by Image J. All experiments were repeated three times.

Nissl Staining

Nissl staining was performed using a commercial staining kit (Nissl Stain Kit, G1430; Solarbio Science and Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transverse spinal cord sections from rats at 28 days post‑injury were deparaffinized, hydrated, and immersed in Cresyl Violet staining solution for 10 min. After thorough rinsing with deionized water, sections were differentiated in Nissl differentiation solution for 4–10 s until most background staining was removed. The sections were then dehydrated in xylene and mounted with neutral resin for observation. Images were acquired using a light microscope (DM IL LED, Lecia).

Immunofluorescence Assay

Spinal cord specimens were collected on day 7 or 14 post‑SCI for immunofluorescence (IF) staining. IF staining was performed on the rostral spinal cord tissue (1 mm segment, located 5 mm from the epicenter). Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, washed, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 10.2 mM sodium citrate buffer at 95 °C for 20 min. After permeabilization with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X‑100 in PBS for 10 min, sections were blocked with 10% (v/v) bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h. The slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (ace-tubulin (1:1000, CST, #5335), Tyr-tubulin (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, MAB1864-I), F-actin (1:400, Santa Cruz, sc-363793), Cleaved caspase3 (1:1000, Abcam, ab32042), Map2 (1:1000, CST, #4542), Tuj1 (1:1000, CST, #4466) against CTSD (Cathepsin D) with AlexaFluor secondaries (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor®488), Abcam, ab150077; Goat anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor®488), Abcam, ab150113) and DAPI (Abcam, ab228549). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (TCS SP8, Lecia) Six random fields from three randomly selected sections per sample were captured for evaluation. Immunofluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (ImageJ 154).

Statistical Analysis

Data (mean ± SEM) were analyzed using Two-way ANOVA (BBB scores) or one-way ANOVA (other endpoints) with Tukey’s post hoc tests (Prism 9.5.1). Significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Herkinorin Improved Functional Repair in a Rat SCI Model

To investigate her’s therapeutic potential in SCI, rats were randomized into three groups (n = 5/group): sham (laminectomy only + saline (daily)), SCI (contusion injury + saline (daily)) and SCI + her (contusion injury + 10 mg/kg her i.p. (daily)). Functional recovery was assessed using: BBB scale (0 = complete paralysis; 21 = normal function); footprint analysis (coordination, stability, and gait symmetry) at 14 days post injury (dpi) and Nissl staining (quantification of ventral motor neuron survival at T10-12). As shown in Fig. 1A, sham group maintained normal function (BBB = 21 throughout), while Both SCI and SCI + her groups showed close hindlimb paralysis (BBB < 5) at 1, 3, 5 dpi, with no intergroup differences (P > 0.05). However, at 7, 14 and 28 dpi, Herkinorin -treated rats exhibited significant functional improvement versus (vs.) SCI group (P < 0.05). This functional improvement was further corroborated by footprint analysis at 14 dpi: sham group-normal coordinated gait with clear toe alignment, SCI group- hindlimb dragging with no plantar stepping, SCI + her group-partial weight-bearing with occasional staggering (Fig. 1B). Besides, sham group exhibited the baseline of thesurviving neurons density, while SCI group showed a significant drop of Nissl bodies neuron loss (P < 0.01). There was an offset of neuron loss induced by SCI (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C, D). The finding was further confirmed by HE staining (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collectively, these findings indicated that Herkinorin promoted functional recovery after SCI. The result of immunofluorescence staining of cleaved–caspase3 further confirmed these findings. Herkinorin Enhanced Axon Regeneration in a Rat SCI Model

The stabilization of microtubules plays a role in axonal regeneration. Thus, Western blot and immunofluorescence staining of axon-related proteins microtubule-associated protein2 (Map2, marker of stable microtubules), growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43, marker of nerve growth) and acetylated α-tubulin (ace-tubulin, marker of stable microtubules) were performed to determine whether Herkinorin enhances axon regeneration post SCI. As shown in Fig. 2A–D, OGD/R treatment significantly downregulated all Map2 (P < 0.0001), ace-tubulin (P < 0.05) and GAP43 (P < 0.01) compared to sham group. But Herkinorin treatment significantly upregulated all Map2 (P < 0.01), ace-tubulin (P < 0.05) and GAP43 (P < 0.05) compared to SCI group. Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that Map2 positive (Map2+) axons in SCI rats displayed fragmented and diffuse staining, reflecting cytoskeletal disassembly. In contrast, Herkinorin -treated rats exhibited denser, more continuous Map2+ axonal bundles (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting enhanced structural integrity. Quantitatively, Map2 fluorescence intensity increased by about 1.5-fold in the SCI + her group versus SCI group (P < 0.05). Herkinorin Lessened OGD/R-Mediated Damage to PC12 Cells

OGD/R model is a well-established in vitro model for mimicking SCI while the CCK8 kit was for assessing cellular viability. As shown in Fig. 1A–C, OGD/R induced a time-dependent decline in viability, with a ∼50% decrease observed after 2 h of OGD/R (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3A). Treatment with Herkinorin alone did not significantly affect cell viability at a dose-dependent manner (P > 0.05, Fig. 3B). However, it enhanced the viability of OGD/R (2 h)-treated cells, notably, at 1 μm concentration (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3C). Thus, 1 μm Herkinorin was selected as the optimal dose for further studies. Axonal length quantification was then conducted to assess Herkinorin’s effects on axonal regeneration (Fig. 3D, E). Compared with the vehicle group, treatment with OGD/R significantly reduced axonal length (P < 0.0001), while Herkinorin treatment restored it to near-normal levels (P < 0.0001). Programmed cell death occurs continuously during SCI repair cascades. To determine whether Herkinorin enhances neuron survival post-SCI, Western blot of apoptosis-related proteins (Bad, Bax and Bcl2) and immunofluorescence staining of cleaved caspase-3 were performed. Compared to vehicle group, the OGD/R group remarkably upregulated pro-apoptotic proteins (Bax and Bad) and downregulated anti-apoptotic Bcl2, indicating robust apoptosis activation. Herkinorin treatment reversed these effects (Fig. 3F–H). These findings corroborated by immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 1H and J, administration of Herkinorin attenuated the OGD/R-induced increase in cleaved caspase-3 intensity (P < 0.05), demonstrating its anti-apoptotic efficacy. Treatment of OGD/R decreased the expression of these three proteins, while Herkinorin treatment counteracted the decline (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A–D). Inhibition of NF-κB p65 phosphorylation plays a key role in Herkinorin-mediated neuroprotection in SCI The activation of the NF-κB pathway, particularly through phosphorylation of its p65 subunit, is a reported key event after SCI. Prior studies indicate that Herkinorin inhibits NF-κB p65 phosphorylation in models of epilepsy [20] and ischemic injury [23]. Thus, we hypothesized that inhibition of NF-κB p65 phosphorylation plays a key role in Herkinorin-mediated neuroprotection in SCI. First, we assessed Herkinorin’s effect on NF-κB p65 phosphorylation at Ser536 (p-p65 Ser536) in the OGD/R model. Western blot analysis revealed that Herkinorin treatment significantly attenuated the OGD/R-induced upregulation of p-p65 (Ser536) levels, using anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536), CST #3033) in (P < 0.01, Fig. 5A, B). Next, TNFα (5uM), a selective activation of NF-κB pathway, was employed as a positive control. Herkinorin treatment enhanced the protein levels of ace-tubulin, GAP43, Map2 and Bcl2, while reduced p-p65 Ser536 in OGD/R-treated PC12 cells. But these phenomena were reversed by TNFα (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E–I)). In addition, Herkinorin addition increased axon length (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5C, D), decreased OGD/R-induced p-p65 Ser536and cleaved caspase 3 intensity (P < 0.01, Fig. 5J–K) compared to OGD/R group while TNFα offset these effects. Herkinorin has been reported to engage biased κ-opioid receptor (KOR) signaling and also influence PARP1. While TNFα was used to activate NF-κB, no direct NF-κB inhibitor was used to confirm that Herkinorin’s effects are specifically through NF-κB suppression. Thus, SLL-627 (a highly selective KOR) and JSH-23 (a Herkinorin κ-opioid receptor NF-Κb p65 inhibitor) were employed to better isolate the receptor-mediated mechanism from off-target effects. PC12 cells were randomly assigned to six groups: JSH-23 alone, OGD/R alone, OGD/R + Her, SLL-637 alone, OGD/R + Herk + JSH-23, and OGD/R + SLL-637 + JSH-23. Compared with the OGD/R group, Her treatment significantly increased the protein levels of acetylated tubulin, GAP-43, Map2, and Bcl2, while reducing NF-κB phosphorylation at p65 Ser536 in PC12 cells subjected to OGD/R. In contrast, SLL-637 treatment did not differ significantly from the OGD/R group. JSH-23 alone markedly increased these protein levels. The OGD/R + Her + JSH-23 group also showed significant upregulation of acetylated tubulin, GAP-43, Map2, and Bcl2, accompanied by a decrease in NF-κB p-p65 Ser536. The OGD/R + SLL-637 + JSH-23 group exhibited comparable effects. These results suggest that Herkinorin may exert its neuroprotective effects primarily through inhibition of p65 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Discussion

This study provides several novel contributions to the literature. First, we demonstrate, for the first time, that Herkinorin exerts therapeutic effects in a clinically relevant rat model of SCI, extending its known neuroprotective utility from brain injury to spinal cord pathology. Second, we identify axonal regeneration—including enhanced axon elongation, growth cone sprouting, and microtubule stabilization—as a previously unrecognized target of Herkinorin action. Third, using TNFα rescue experiments, we establish a causal role for NF-κB inhibition in mediating these pro-regenerative effects, moving beyond correlative observations. Collectively, these findings position Herkinorin as a promising candidate for SCI therapy with a dual mechanism: anti-apoptotic and pro-regenerative (Fig. 6).Fig. 6A schematic diagram showing the potential molecular mechanism by which Herkinorin decreases apoptosis and promotes axon regeneration

Traumatic SCI is attributed to trauma injury like traffic accidents, falling damage and violent items. Functional recovery following SCI fundamentally requires the restoration of neural circuit integrity [6]. The disruption of axonal continuity and neuronal loss at the injury site leads to structural disconnections as well as functional consequences [24]. So, SCI has always focused in neural repair, especially in axonal regeneration. Disruption of neuronal network integrity destabilizes the microtubule “railroad” of axons, impairing intra-axonal transport and hindering the re-establishment of damaged neural circuits. Extensive research indicates that although axonal regeneration depends on both cell-extrinsic (microenvironmental) and cell-intrinsic (neuronal) factors, all regenerative strategies ultimately converge on microtubule stabilization and reorganization. A deeper understanding of neuronal microtubule dynamics—including the molecular regulators that promote or inhibit microtubule stabilization—could unlock novel therapies for neurological disorders involving microtubule dysfunction, such as spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury [25], multiple sclerosis, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [26]. After SCI, most axons undergo retraction, permitting only limited regenerative sprouting over short distances (typically < 1 mm). These sprouting axons develop dystrophic, swollen terminals, wherein microtubule stabilization within growth cones is impaired due to the highly inhibitory central nervous system (CNS) microenvironment [27, 28]. Acetylation of tubulin was proved to increase microtubule stabilization [29]. In our study, we explored the role of Herkinorin in microtubule stabilization and axon regeneration both in vivo and in vitro. Herkinorin treatment not only enhanced the expression of Map2, GAP43 and Ace-tubulin in the rat SCI model (Fig. 3), but also augmented axon length in PC12 cells and primary cortical neurons. Moreover, the A/T ratio revealed a about 1.5-fold increase in Herkinorin-treated group vs. OGD/R group (P < 0.05). Herkinorin treatment restored growth cone complexity and axon length and diminished the retraction of neurite in primary cortical neurons (Fig. 4). Therefore, this study suggested Herkinorin action is a potential strategy for SCI repair through the stabilization of microtubule, finally promoting axonal regrowth.

As members of the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily, opioid receptors are divided into three different subtypes-μ (mu), δ (delta), and κ (kappa). Growing evidence highlights opioid receptors their potential in managing complex CNS pathologies and inflammatory disorders. The kappa opioid receptor KOR agonists Salvinorin A (SA) was reported as an anti-inflammatory molecule for treatment of pulmonary inflammation [30]. SA showed neuroprotection via ERK/MAPK signaling activation in a piglet hypoxic/ischemic injury model [31]. Herkinorin-[(2S,4aR,6aR,7R,9S,10aS,10bR)‐9‐ (Benzoyloxy)‐2‐(3‐furanyl) dodecahydro‐6a,10b‐dimethyl‐4, 10‐dioxo‐2H‐naphtho‐[2,1‐c]pyran‐7‐carboxylic acid methyl ester] is a novel non-nitrogenous neoclerodane diterpene that acts as a selective opioid receptor agonist (molecular weight of 494.53) [32]. It was developed based on the natural scaffold of SA as the first semi-synthetic, nitrogen-free opioid receptor agonist [33]. Preconditioning of morphine demonstrated neuroprotective effects in rat cerebellar Purkinje cells during ischemia-reperfusion [34]. However, this potency was blocked exclusively by selective delta opioid receptor (DOR) antagonists, suggesting an early cerebral protection of DOR-mediated morphine pretreatment. Meanwhile, the delayed neuroprotection of morphine against ischemia was found and may be related to mu opioid receptor (MOR) activation [35]. The binding affinity of Herkinorin to MOR is 8 times that of KOR and 98 times that of DOR, respectively. Knockdown or inhibition of MOR offset the enhancement of Herkinorin in cerebral ischemic injury [21]. Our work observed the augment of BBB scale and footprint and the decline in expression of Bad, Bax and Cleaved caspase3, indicating the promotion of Herkinorin both in axonal regeneration and functional recovery and the alleviation of apoptosis after SCI.

NF-κB pathway has been established as a critical mediator of cell death across multiple disease models, including neurodegenerative disorders [36], ischemia–reperfusion injury, and diabetes mellitus [37]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that persistent NF-κB activation triggers apoptosis. The inhibition of SIRT1/NF-κB axis was reported to enhance neurofunctional recovery and suppresses neuroinflammation in ischemic stroke [24]. Opioid receptors have recently emerged as key modulators of NF-κB activity. Growing evidence indicates that diverse signaling molecules—including ions, metabolites, neurotransmitters, and hormones—regulate NF-κB priming, activation, and suppression through GPCR-dependent mechanisms. Sinomenine (an anctivator of opioid mu-receptor) was reported to trigger kidney injury by suppressing NF-κB pathway [38]. Dynorphin 3–14, the fragment of a 17-residue endogenous opioid peptide, was proved to exhibit inhibition on NF-κB pathway [39]. Jian et al. demonstrated that Remifentanil, an ultra-short-acting, potent opioid analgesic, attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-Induced Inflammation via modulation of the PARP-1/NF-κB Signaling pathway in a sepsis model [40]. Herkinorin was proved to ameliorate neuronal injury by inhibiting NF‐κB in an epilepsy rat model [20] or neuronal ischemic injury [23]. These finding suggested the potential neuroprotective effects of Herkinorin in neurological disorders and the underlying mechanism may associated with NF-κB pathway. Our study showed that Herkinorin treatment dismissed the upregulation of p-p65 Ser536 induced by OGD/R condition. But with the administration of TNFα (a selective activation of NF-κB pathway), the protective action of Herkinorin was canceled out both in microtubule stabilization and neuron death (Fig. 5). These together implied that Herkinorin alleviated OGD/R‐induced SCI by inhibiting NF‐κB pathway. But more efforts should be taken to clarify the receptor specificity of NF‐κB pathway.

There are some limits in this study. First, as a catecholaminergic cell line, PC12 cells are not a model of spinal cord neurons. Their principal value lies in their standardized, high-throughput utility for studying fundamental, conserved pathways in neuronal cells. Upon differentiation, they adopt a stable, neuron-like phenotype, making them an ideal platform for isolating specific intracellular events—such as oxidative stress and programmed cell death—from the complexity of a mixed CNS environment [41, 42]. This allows for clear, controlled mechanistic discovery. The oxygen–glucose deprivation/Reoxygenation (OGD/R) model in PC12 cells and primary cortical neurons is a well-established, reductionist system for this specific purpose [43, 44]. It allows for controlled, high-throughput analysis of intracellular signaling pathways related to oxidative stress and apoptosis, which are highly relevant across central nervous system injuries. The validation in organotypic slice cultures [45, 46] or advanced spinal cord-derived models [47, 48] is the essential and logical next step to confirm the translational potential of our findings. Second, for SCI models, 6–8-week-old female SD rats are commonly used. After injury, lower-body paralysis prevents voluntary urination, risking bladder rupture or death without timely intervention. Manual bladder expression is therefore required post-surgery, typically for 1–2 weeks depending on injury severity and treatment. Initially, reddish-brown urine may appear—a normal post-injury sign that fades during recovery. Female rats are preferred due to their shorter urethra and weaker urinary control, easing manual expression. In contrast, males have a longer urethra and stronger sphincter control, making expression more difficult and potentially compromising animal welfare and model consistency. While this controls for hormonal variability, it limits the generalizability of the findings.

Moreover, we acknowledge that a comprehensive dose–response and pharmacokinetic (PK) study is the gold standard for optimal in vivo dosing, which is indeed vital for translational interpretation. The in vivo dosing regimen for this initial proof-of-concept study was based on the following rationales. Herkinorin was reported to ameliorate neuronal damage in a pentylenetetrazol-induced epilepsy rat model [20]. The dose of 10 mg/kg Herkinorin was employed according to the finding. We fully agree with the reviewer that the absence of a full dose–response curve (e.g., testing 1, 5, and 10 mg/kg) and PK data in the SCI model is a limitation of the present study. These data are crucial to define the minimal effective dose, the therapeutic window, and to optimize the regimen. Importantly, the positive results from this initial regimen provide a strong rationale for the dedicated, in-depth pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies we are planning as the necessary next step towards translation.

Finally, our work primarily focused on neuronal outcomes. The response of glial cells—astrocytes and microglia—to Herkinorin in the injured spinal cord warrants specific investigation. Glial reactivity is a double-edged sword in SCI pathophysiology; it can exacerbate neuroinflammation and form a physical and chemical barrier to regeneration, but certain activated states also contribute to tissue repair and neuroprotection. Determining whether Herkinorin modulates glial activation, and if so, whether it promotes a more reparative phenotype, is a critical unanswered question. Experiments combining cell-type-specific analysis (e.g., RNA sequencing of sorted glial populations) and spatial transcriptomics in future in vivo studies will be crucial to elucidate these non-neuronal interactions and optimize the therapeutic window of Herkinorin.

Conclusion

This study provides the first evidence that Herkinorin exerts neuroprotection against OGD/R-induced neuronal damage and promotes functional recovery after spinal cord injury (SCI) in rats, primarily through inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. These findings identify Herkinorin as a promising therapeutic candidate for targeting the secondary injury phase in acute SCI.
