The AI-native health science institution innovation theater vs. structural redesign
Authors: Jiajie Zhang, Martin J Citardi, Xiaoqian Jiang, Sean Savitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcademic health institutions face a new constraint: cognitive labor. Many adopt AI through additive initiatives that risk becoming “innovation theater”—visible activity without structural change. We argue this reflects a mismatch between rapid AI advances and slower institutional evolution. AI-native institutions treat AI as infrastructure, requiring governance, shared platforms, and workforce readiness. The challenge is not adoption but redesign; those that fail risk widening gaps and accelerating irrelevance.Subject terms: Complex networks; Complex networks; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society; Scientific community

The cognitive constraint

Throughout history, major societal transitions have succeeded by resolving the dominant constraints of their times. The Agricultural Revolution addressed food production, and the Industrial Revolution expanded physical labor through mechanization. Academic medicine has likewise undergone structural inflection points, including the Flexnerian shift toward the biomedical model1. Today, the binding constraint is increasingly cognitive labor: diagnosis, synthesis, prediction, coordination, and judgment under conditions of rapidly expanding knowledge, data, and complexity. Universities and academic health institutions remain organized around this knowledge-intensive work, yet many now face a widening gap between what their missions require and what their legacy structures can support. Human society has entered a Cognitive Revolution driven by AI, and this transition creates not only new tools but new conditions for organizing institutional intelligence2.

This does not mean that AI mechanically dictates a single top-down model of reorganization. Major transitions are typically iterative, hybrid, and partially emergent. But in knowledge-intensive, safety-critical settings such as academic medicine, AI changes the structure, speed, and distribution of cognitive work enough to create growing misalignment with industrial-era architectures. As cognition becomes partially automatable and distributable across human–machine systems, surface-level adoption is insufficient. The central question is no longer whether AI can be added to existing workflows, but whether institutions will redesign the systems in which cognition occurs.

The trap of innovation theater

Most organizations do not intentionally engage in “innovation theater.” In many cases, efforts to adopt AI are thoughtful, well-motivated, and necessary for learning. We use the term to describe visible AI activity without corresponding architectural change: the gap between visible activity and structural transformation, a distinction developed more fully in a recent book, The Cognitive Revolution2. Signs include AI centers layered onto existing structures, pilot projects disconnected from core workflows, task forces without execution authority, leadership roles without budget or decision rights, and local solutions that bypass shared infrastructure. Early experimentation can be valuable, but it becomes theater when it remains parallel to the core system rather than reshaping it.

The costs are tangible: duplication, fragmented accountability, limited scalability, weak institutional learning, and the persistence of parallel systems that leave core operations unchanged. A simple diagnostic question is whether removing current AI initiatives would fundamentally alter core institutional workflows; if the answer is no, AI remains a layer rather than a foundation. The deeper issue is structural mismatch. AI capabilities advance rapidly, while budgeting cycles, accreditation processes, research pipelines, and governance structures move more slowly. Addressing this gap requires moving from surface change to architectural change, while using staged, hybrid transition pathways to connect pilots, shared infrastructure, governance, and scale.

Redesigning for distributed cognition

An AI-native institution treats AI as essential infrastructure rather than a peripheral initiative. Operationally, this means more than deploying tools: it requires shared data and model platforms rather than isolated pilots; executive-level governance rather than diffuse ownership; durable funding for enterprise infrastructure; explicit accountability for validation, monitoring, access control, and fallback mechanisms; and workforce expectations that responsible human–AI collaboration is a baseline competence. Overcoming current challenges in academic health institutions, therefore, requires deliberate reorganization of cognitive work to reduce decision latency and improve learning across the enterprise. This redesign follows the principle of distributed cognition3–5, reframing academic medicine as a system in which clinical diagnoses, research insights, educational processes, and operational decisions are distributed across human and machine intelligence. AI does not simply accelerate existing tasks; it alters how knowledge is represented, updated, delegated, and integrated.

Transformation need not occur in a single leap. Institutions may move through staged and hybrid forms, but good incremental progress is measured by whether pilots are linked to shared platforms, enterprise governance, redesigned workflows, and explicit criteria for scale-up or retirement. In an AI-native institution, AI must be designed and governed as a cognitive collaborator that reshapes knowledge representation, redistributes cognitive labor, and restructures coordination across roles. Oversight cannot be diffuse: executive steering groups, clinical safety boards, and research governance bodies should define responsibility for evaluation, deployment, monitoring, escalation, and human override. The objective is not merely faster decision-making, but the safe scaling of institutional intelligence.

The leadership imperative

The transformation to an AI-native institution is not a technical challenge to be delegated to the IT department; it is a mandate for the highest levels of executive leadership. It requires clear institutional commitment, cross-functional coordination to break down silos, and reallocation of financial resources toward shared infrastructure, governance capacity, and workforce development. This is no longer theoretical. Emerging efforts, such as the AI-native hospital initiative6 for the new UT Dell Medical Center in Austin, explicitly position robotics, automation, and AI as foundational elements of hospital design rather than incremental add-ons. These developments underscore that leading institutions are already moving beyond pilot programs toward architectural redesign, reinforcing that the transition to AI-native systems is both urgent and actively underway.

In prior technological revolutions, laggards had decades or centuries to adapt. In the Cognitive Revolution, the advantage compounds within years and is now visible in everyday work. The speed of AI is on a fundamentally different scale from the speed of institutional change. Delay is therefore not a neutral position; it is the accumulation of catch-up costs in capabilities, culture, and infrastructure.

Discussion

Academic health institutions exist to promote health, treat disease, and alleviate suffering within the context of learning and rigorous research. In the AI era, a generation is roughly the span of a single leadership cycle, and we are already in the middle of one. The choice is not whether change will occur, but whether institutions will shape it through architectural transformation, staged structural redesign or passive absorption through accumulating mismatch. Some will move through hybrid transition states, but they cannot remain indefinitely at the level of additive adoption. Because AI is advancing at a pace fundamentally different from institutional change, delay compounds quickly. Institutions that fail to redesign their foundations risk widening performance gaps and accelerating irrelevance in the cognitive era.
