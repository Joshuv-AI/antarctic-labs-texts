Protocol for isolation of live tumor-infiltrating immune cells from immunocompetent murine brains for high-dimensional profiling
Authors: Aimée du Chatinier, Leyla Rigamonti, Piotr Waranecki, Dennis S Metselaar, Michaël H Meel, Senna Visser, Simone Foderaro, Serena R Baglio, Eelco W Hoving, Esther Hulleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present an optimized workflow to isolate viable immune cells from tumor-bearing mouse brains, with particular emphasis on myeloid populations, to enable detailed functional analysis of the tumor-immune microenvironment. We describe steps for intracardiac perfusion and regional brain dissection. We then detail procedures for gentle enzymatic-mechanical dissociation, efficient myelin and red blood cell removal, and optional CD11b+ enrichment. This approach preserves immune cell viability and epitope diversity, supporting high-dimensional proteomic profiling via spectral flow cytometry and single-cell multi-omics.Subject areas: Cancer, Immunology, Model Organisms, Neuroscience

Before you begin

Comprehensive characterization of the brain tumor immune microenvironment is essential for understanding tumor progression and therapeutic response.1 Accurate definition of this landscape requires robust single cell immune profiling. However, isolating viable immune cells from murine brain tumors – particularly from myelin-rich regions – remains technically challenging.2 Many existing workflows rely on non-live approaches optimized for RNA recovery using fixed tissue or single-nucleus preparations from frozen samples.3,4,5 Although these strategies facilitate transcriptomic profiling, they can distort immune population ratios and limit phenotypic resolution.3 In contrast, live cell isolation enables integrated proteomic, transcriptomic, and functional analyses but is technically demanding due to the fragility of brain-resident and infiltrating immune cells and the high lipid content of neural tissue, which impairs efficient dissociation and purification.6,7

Here, we present a robust and reproducible workflow for isolating viable tumor-infiltrating immune cells from immunocompetent murine brains, exemplified in C57BL/6 mice bearing intracranial diffuse midline glioma (DMG). While all immune populations are recovered, particular emphasis is placed on myeloid cells, a dominant and functionally critical component of the glioma immune microenvironment. We detail procedures for intracardiac perfusion and region-specific brain dissection, followed by a papain-free, gentle enzymatic-mechanical dissociation designed to preserve epitope integrity. We also describe efficient myelin and red blood cell (RBC) depletion to enhance downstream analyses. Optional steps include CD11b+ enrichment and viability-based cell sorting to further improve recovery of intact target populations.

By achieving high recovery of live immune cells while preserving surface-marker integrity and transcriptomic quality within a single workflow, this method is compatible with high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry and integrated single cell multi-omics approaches (e.g., Cellular Indexing of Transcriptomes and Epitopes by Sequencing, CITE-seq). Although optimized for murine DMG, the protocol can be readily adapted to other brain tumor models or neurological disease contexts. It provides a broadly applicable platform for high-dimensional immunophenotyping and single cell multi-omics to support mechanistic studies of immune regulation and therapeutic response in preclinical brain tumor models.

Innovation

This protocol advances brain tumor immunophenotyping by establishing a live cell isolation workflow optimized for fragile brain-resident and tumor-infiltrating immune populations. Preservation of viable cells maintains native immune subset composition and enables integrated downstream applications, including combined transcriptomic and proteomic profiling, morphological assessment, evaluation of ribosomal and mitochondrial content, and ex vivo functional assays. The key innovation lies in combining and tailoring gentle dissociation with optimized purification steps to address challenges unique to brain tissue. Existing live cell protocols, typically designed for peripheral tissues, often compromise viability and yield when applied to the brain. Here, enzymatic digestion is refined, mechanical disruption minimized, and myelin depletion incorporated to preserve cellular integrity and maximize yield. By integrating these elements into a cohesive, brain-specific workflow, this protocol improves recovery and viability of cells while maintaining immune population fidelity, enabling more accurate and functionally informative analyses of the brain tumor immune microenvironment at single cell resolution.

Generation and preparation of the murine DMG model for downstream analyses

This section provides a summary of the experimental procedures performed prior to the isolation and dissociation protocol, including the generation of primary murine DMG tumors, establishment of cell cultures, development of secondary allograft models, and in vivo irradiation. These procedures were used to produce the immunocompetent C57BL/6 DMG model analyzed in this study. They are provided for context for the results obtained with the isolation and dissociation workflow and can be adapted as needed depending on the experimental model and research question.

Primary murine DMG cells (UC-BL6-B7) were generated and maintained as described by Patel et al. (2020)8 and du Chatinier et al. (2022).9 Briefly, PiggyBac DNA plasmids encoding H3f3aK27M, PdgfraD842V, and Trp53DN, along with PiggyBac transposase (pCAG-PBase), were injected into the fourth ventricle of timed pregnant C57BL/6 embryos at embryonic day 13.5 and electroporated toward the brainstem. After birth, electroporated offspring were monitored for neurological symptoms indicative of tumor formation. GFP-positive tumors were microdissected from normal brain tissue and enzymatically dissociated into single cell suspensions to establish the UC-BL6-B7 cell line. UC-BL6-B7 cells were maintained as neurospheres in complete Tumor Stem Medium (TSM), as described previously.9

Secondary allografts were generated by stereotactic injection of 5 x 105 UC-BL6-B7 cells into the pons of 4–6-weeks-old female C57BL/6 mice, as described in du Chatinier et al. (2022).9 Where applicable, mice underwent fractionated radiotherapy administered via a 5 x 5 mm collimated field using the Xstrahl Small Animal Radiation Research Platform (SARRP, Xstrahl Inc.). A dose of 2 Gy was delivered per session, 5 days per week, for 2 consecutive weeks. Animals were monitored closely and euthanized at predefined humane endpoints, including neurological impairment and body weight loss. In this model, median survival is approximately 6 to 7 weeks after tumor implantation, and the radiation regimen extends survival by about 2 weeks. Brain tissues were collected at the experimental endpoint.

Intracardiac perfusion and brain isolation

Timing: 15–20 min per mouse

This step perfuses the circulatory system with PBS to remove blood and circulating immune cells before brain extraction. Effective perfusion is essential to reduce blood contamination, preserve cell viability, and improve the quality of downstream cellular and molecular analyses.

This step enriches for viable cells by discriminating live and dead populations based on DRAQ5™ and DAPI nuclear staining. DRAQ5™ is a cell-permeant DNA dye that labels the nuclei of both live and dead cells, whereas DAPI is impermeant to live cells and stains only the nuclei of membrane-compromised (dead) cells. Following completion of part 1 through 4 of the workflow, isolated cells were analyzed by spectral flow cytometry using a 22-marker antibody panel to characterize immune populations in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing H3.3K27M DMG tumors that were treated with sham irradiation or radiotherapy (Figure 4). For each condition, midline brain structures (excluding the cerebellum) from three mice were pooled and processed together, yielding a consistently high number of viable cells that enabled robust spectral flow staining and analysis (Table S1). Using a structured gating strategy, we resolved major immune compartments, clearly distinguishing myeloid and lymphoid lineages and identifying key subsets – including microglia-like, macrophage-like, T cells, and NK cells – based on established marker expression profiles (Figure 4A). Importantly, identical spectral flow cytometry acquisition, antibody panels, and gating strategies were applied to an independent cohort of tumor-bearing mice, with the resulting data shown in Figure S1, thereby validating the reproducibility of immune population identification across separate experimental datasets. Visualization of the tumor immune landscape using t-SNE revealed broad coverage of both myeloid and lymphoid populations in both cohorts, demonstrating that the dissociation protocol preserves the surface epitopes required for high-dimensional immune phenotyping (Figure 4B; Figure S1). As expected, radiotherapy increased the proportion of CD45+ immune cells relative to sham-treated controls, consistent with radiation-induced neuroinflammation (Figure 4C).11,12 Within the myeloid compartment, radiotherapy was associated with shifts in the relative abundance of microglia-like and macrophage-like populations (Figures 4D and 4E), in line with previously reported remodeling of the brain tumor immune microenvironment.11 In addition, irradiated tumors displayed an increased frequency of CD4+ regulatory T cells compared with sham-treated tumors (Figure 4F).13,14 Collectively, these results demonstrate that the protocol enables robust, reproducible isolation and accurate characterization of immune cell populations in murine brain tumors across independent cohorts.

To further assess compatibility with single cell multi-omics approaches, part 5 and 6 of the protocol were incorporated in a separate experiment focusing on the myeloid compartment. CD11b+ myeloid cells were isolated by magnetic enrichment and cell sorting from pooled midline brain structures (including the cerebellum) collected from immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing H3.3K27M DMG tumors or from PBS-injected controls, followed by CITE-seq analysis (Figures 5 and 6). For each condition, myeloid cells from 15 mice were pooled prior to downstream processing. Transcriptomic data were projected using UMAP to visualize the major myeloid populations present in DMG-bearing and control brains (Figures 5A and 5B). Canonical microglial markers (e.g., P2ry12, Tmem119, Cx3cr1, Sall1) and macrophage-associated transcripts (e.g., Ccr2, Lgals3, Itga4) were used for supervised classification, enabling the identification of three reproducible myeloid phenotypes: microglia, macrophages, and an intermediate population (Figure 5C).15,16 Quantitative analysis revealed that PBS-injected control mice retained a predominantly microglial profile, whereas DMG-bearing mice exhibited a marked reduction in microglia accompanied by an increased frequency of infiltrating macrophages (Figure 5D), consistent with disease-associated myeloid remodeling.17 Differential expression analysis further delineated distinct transcriptional programs for each phenotype, summarized in a heatmap of the 50 most variable genes (Figure 5E). Together, these results demonstrate that the dissociation and isolation workflow maintains sufficient RNA quality in brain-resident and tumor-associated myeloid cells to allow successful sequencing and mapping for transcriptomic profiling.

To determine whether this preservation extended beyond RNA to surface protein expression – an essential requirement for integrated single cell multi-omics – antibody-derived tag (ADT) signals were overlaid onto the same UMAP embeddings (Figure 6A). Protein expression patterns closely aligned with transcript-defined microglial and macrophage regions, indicating retention of biologically meaningful surface markers throughout the isolation procedure. Unsupervised k-means clustering based solely on all 109 ADT features (k = 3) resolved three protein-defined myeloid populations corresponding to microglia, macrophages, and intermediate cells (Figure 6B). The proportional representation of these clusters closely mirrored that obtained from transcriptomic classification (Figure 6C). Furthermore, comparison of transcriptional profiles across protein-derived clusters revealed coherent and phenotype-consistent gene expression signatures (Figure 6D). To aid interpretation of myeloid populations identified, a summary of canonical marker expression patterns across relevant myeloid cell subsets is included in Table 1. Overall, the expression profiles observed in our dataset were consistent with the expected distribution of these markers, supporting the identification of microglia, macrophages, and intermediate cell populations. For example, microglia-associated populations displayed the expected lower expression of CD45 and CD49d, whilst the macrophage-associated populations displayed increased expression of both markers. Additionally, CD371 expression was upregulated in macrophage-associated populations, consistent with the immunosuppressive phenotype commonly observed in tumor-associated macrophages. Collectively, single cell multi-omics confirmed high-quality RNA and protein capture, with protein-based clustering paralleling transcript-defined phenotypes.

Together, these results demonstrate that the described dissociation and isolation protocol preserves both phenotypic and molecular integrity of tumor-infiltrating and brain-resident immune cells, enabling concordant transcriptomic and proteomic classification of diverse cell states. By maintaining cell viability and surface marker fidelity, this workflow supports a broad range of high-dimensional phenotyping applications, enabling reliable discrimination of immune cells and treatment-induced remodeling in the murine brain tumor microenvironment.

Limitations

This protocol enables robust isolation of live immune cells from immunocompetent murine brain tumors, but several limitations should be considered. Tissue quality and rapid processing are critical, as delays at any step can reduce cell viability and affect downstream analyses. Optional procedures, such as CD11b+ enrichment or viability-based cell sorting, can introduce additional stress, often lowering overall yield. The extent of cell loss is particularly influenced by the type of cell sorter employed, as certain instruments exert greater mechanical stress, resulting in reduced cell viability. Furthermore, enzymatic dissociation is optimized for midline structures and may require adjustment for other brain regions. Finally, mechanical variability during dissociation and differences in tumor burden, animal age, or strain can affect cell recovery and phenotypic representation, potentially limiting reproducibility and comparability across models.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Incomplete perfusion (step 4)

Incomplete perfusion occurs when the needle is incorrectly positioned in the left ventricle, the perfusion flow rate or pressure is suboptimal, or drainage from the liver or IVC is insufficient. These issues prevent full removal of blood and circulating immune cells.

Potential solution

Reinsert the perfusion needle into the left ventricle, ensuring it does not puncture through the heart wall. Maintain a consistent perfusion rate (∼5 mL/min). Confirm that an adequate drainage incision is made in the liver or IVC. Successful perfusion is indicated by the liver turning pale and the drainage fluid becoming clear.

Problem 2: Incomplete tissue dissociation (steps 7–11)

Undigested tissue clumps typically result from expired or incorrectly prepared enzyme mixes, suboptimal enzymatic digestion, or insufficient mechanical dissociation. These factors prevent complete breakdown of tissue and reduce total cell recovery.

Potential solution

Prepare fresh enzyme mix immediately before use and verify correct enzyme ratios. Extend incubation by 10–15 min while gently resuspending every 15 min with a wide-bore pipette. To further increase the cell yield, allow the remaining tissue to settle and transfer the supernatant to a new tube. Add 4 mL RPMI 1640 or DMEM to the C Tube containing the residual tissue pieces, place the tube onto the sleeve of the gentleMACS™ Dissociator, and run program m_impTumor_01. Combine the resulting cell suspension with the previously collected supernatant to maximize total recovery.

Problem 3: Low cell yield after dissociation (steps 7–16)

Reduced cell yield may result from over-digestion or excessive mechanical stress causing cell death, or from clogging during filtration, all of which lead to loss of viable cells.

Potential solution

Shorten enzymatic digestion time by 10–15 min if cell viability is compromised. Avoid vigorous trituration or vortexing. Use multiple strainers to prevent clogging and rinse tubes and strainers thoroughly to recover remaining cells.

Problem 4: Cell clumping after dissociation or myelin removal (steps 17–23)

Clumping often arises from accumulation of free DNA from lysed cells, residual myelin debris, or temperature fluctuations that can cause protein precipitation and aggregation during handling. These factors reduce single cell suspension quality and can impair downstream sorting and analysis.

Potential solution

Add DNase I (10–50 U/mL) to the dissociation buffer to prevent aggregation. Alternatively, perform a DNase treatment after tissue dissociation by centrifuging the cell suspension, removing the supernatant, and resuspending the pellet in 5 mL RPMI 1640 or DMEM containing 200 U/mL DNase I. Incubate for 5 min at 20–25 °C, then wash the cells with serum-containing buffer to inactivate the enzyme and remove residual debris.

Maintain all reagents and samples at 2–8 °C. Ensure complete mixing during bead labeling to maximize myelin capture. Filter the suspension through a Pre-Separation Filter or equivalent 70 μm nylon mesh to remove clumps after dissociation or myelin removal. Pre-wet the filter with buffer before use. Be cautious that filtration can lead to cell loss, particularly in low-abundance populations.

Problem 5: Excessive cell loss during myelin removal (steps 17–23)

Significant cell loss can occur due to high magnetic field retention, improper bead incubation or mixing, harsh handling, inadequate washing, or clogging of separation columns (e.g., due to air bubbles). These factors decrease recovery and purity of target cells.

Potential solution

Perform all labeling steps at 2–8 °C and adhere to recommended incubation times. Mix cells gently during bead incubation and avoid vortexing. Filter the sample before separation to remove aggregates. To mitigate column clogging, split samples across at least two columns, rinse columns additional times, or increase wash volumes (≥3 mL per wash), particularly when working with dense or debris-rich samples. Collect all flow-through and combine fractions to maximize total recovery.

Problem 6: Incomplete myelin removal (steps 17–23)

Persistent myelin contamination occurs when Myelin Removal Beads II are insufficient or LS Columns are overloaded, reducing the efficiency of magnetic separation and resulting in a cloudy, debris-rich cell suspension.

Potential solution

Increase bead volume and number of LS Columns proportionally to tissue mass to avoid saturation. Rinse columns with sufficient 1X MACS buffer before loading and wash immediately after the column reservoir empties.

Problem 7: Red discoloration after RBC lysis (steps 24–28)

Residual RBCs remain when lysis is incomplete, which can be caused by short incubation or use of cold, improperly prepared lysing buffer.

Potential solution

Confirm lysing buffer is pre-warmed and freshly prepared. Incubate for 60–90 sec while gently mixing. If RBCs persist, repeat the lysis once, then immediately dilute with 20 mL cold 1X MACS buffer and centrifuge.

Note: In therapeutic trials, chemotherapy or other myelotoxic agents can increase the number of reticulocytes (nucleated RBCs). These cells are not lysed by BD Pharm Lyse and may cause red discoloration. In such cases, exclusion of reticulocytes by FACS using erythroid-lineage markers (e.g., Ter119) is recommended rather than performing aggressive RBC lysis.

Problem 8: Loss of leukocyte viability after RBC lysis (steps 24–28)

Leukocyte death can result from prolonged exposure to lysis buffer, harsh mixing, or delayed neutralization, leading to reduced viability.

Potential solution

Strictly limit incubation to 1 min at 37 °C. Immediately quench lysis by adding 20 mL cold 1X MACS buffer. Mix gently and centrifuge under low acceleration to preserve cell integrity. Avoid vortexing.

Problem 9: Low yield or purity of CD11b+ cells after magnetic isolation (steps 29–36)

Poor recovery or contamination occurs when magnetic labeling is inadequate, cell counts are incorrect, or MicroBead binding is incomplete, leading to low yield or impure CD11b+ fractions.

Potential solution

Perform all labeling steps at 2–8 °C and adhere to recommended incubation times. Mix cells gently during bead incubation to ensure uniform labeling and avoid vortexing. Use 10 μL CD11b MicroBeads per 107 total cells and verify accurate cell counting before labeling. To mitigate column clogging, split samples across at least two columns. Elute magnetically labeled cells with sufficient buffer volume (≥5 mL) using steady plunger pressure. To enhance purity, reapply the eluted CD11b+ fraction to a new LS Column.

Problem 10: High proportion of dead cells during flow cytometric sorting (steps 37–42)

Excessive cell death during sorting often results from extended processing, warm sample conditions, or high sorting pressure, which can stress fragile primary cells.

Potential solution

Keep all buffers, tubes, and collection chamber at 2–8 °C. Minimize processing time and reduce centrifugation steps. Use low-pressure sorting (100 μm nozzle) and collect cells into chilled tubes containing BSA or FBS.

Problem 11: Poor DRAQ5/DAPI staining separation or inconsistent gating (steps 40 and 41)

Weak or overlapping nuclear staining arises from incorrect dye concentrations, presence of sodium azide, or improper cytometer filter settings, leading to poor discrimination of live and dead cells.

Potential solution

Perform DRAQ5™ staining in PBS without calcium, or magnesium, and avoid buffers containing >0.02% sodium azide. Verify DRAQ5™ (5 μM) and DAPI (1 μg/mL) concentrations. Incubate 10–15 min protected from light, then verify laser/filter configuration using single-stained controls before sorting. If DAPI contains sodium azide and DRAQ5™ uptake is suboptimal, stain with DAPI first, wash the sample, and then perform DRAQ5™ staining in a sodium-azide free buffer to improve staining efficiency.

Problem 12: Overall poor cell viability and yield

Reduced overall cell yield and viability occurs when tissue is handled for extended periods at 20–25 °C, buffers are not chilled, or mechanical trauma occurs during tissue handling or processing.

Potential solution

Perform brain isolation rapidly (within 5–10 min of perfusion). Use ice-cold PBS (2–8 °C) and chilled dissection instruments. Keep tissue submerged in cold buffer whenever possible. Avoid compressing the brain during skull removal. Use consistent low-speed centrifugation with minimal acceleration and brake to minimize cell loss throughout the protocol. Acceleration and brake settings may vary between centrifuge brands and models; adjust settings accordingly to achieve equivalent gentle deceleration.

Note: It is not recommended to introduce additional steps for debris or dead cell removal, such as using commercial reagents like the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-090-101) or Debris Removal Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-109-398). Incorporating these extra purification steps increases the risk of losing viable cells, which can significantly reduce overall yield. Dead cells and debris will be efficiently excluded during flow cytometric sorting, making these steps unnecessary.
