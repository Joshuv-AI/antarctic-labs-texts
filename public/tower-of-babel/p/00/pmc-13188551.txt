Prevalence and risk factors of sleep disturbances among adolescents with non-suicidal self-injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wenwen Wen, Yunyin Guan, Lixia Zhong, Zhulin Li, Ruoyun Ma, Xiaoling Lin, Chen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances and identify associated risk factor among adolescent with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI).MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, PsycINFO, CNKI, and SinoMed was conducted up to March 2026. Eligible studies included adolescents aged 11–19 years with NSSI that reported the prevalence or risk factors of sleep disturbances. Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies were included. Two reviewers independently conducted study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment. Pooled estimates were calculated using random-effects models.ResultsA total of 23 studies were included in the review. The pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances across 13 studies was 41% (95%CI: 39%–44%; I²= 76.8%). Overall, 11 risk factors were identified across 15 studies, of which four factors were eligible for meta-analysis, including female sex, depression, emotional neglect, and externalizing problems. Significant associations were observed for depression (OR = 1.05, 95%CI: 1.03–1.07; I²= 69.1%) and female sex (OR = 1.74, 95%CI: 1.31–2.18; I²= 78.8%), whereas emotional neglect and externalizing problems were not significantly associated with sleep disturbances.ConclusionsSleep disturbances are highly prevalent among adolescents engaging in NSSI, particularly among females and those with depression. These findings underscore the need for routine sleep assessment within adolescent mental health screening. Nurses can play a key role in early identification, sleep-health education, and timely referral, supporting nurse-led models of early detection and prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04476-8.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Adolescents, Sleep disturbances, Prevalence, Systematic review

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the intentional destruction of body tissues without suicidal intent [1], encompassing a wide range of repetitive and deliberate behaviors such as cutting, hitting, burning, and scratching [2]. Globally, NSSI has emerged as a significant public health concern among adolescents. Previous studies indicated that a lifetime prevalence of 22.1% in non-clinical adolescents [3, 4], approaching 49.5% in clinical samples [5]. Crucially, NSSI serves as a strong predictor of future suicidal thoughts and behaviors [6]. A previous study indicated that adolescents who stayed up late were approximately 1.39 times more likely to engage in NSSI and the risk of attempting suicide among them had increased by 6.69 times [7]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors within this high-risk population is paramount [8]. NSSI is understood to result from the interaction of various factors, including demographics, mental health disorders, and genetics [9]. Sleep represents one such promising and modifiable risk factor [10].

The increasing engagement in NSSI among adolescents [11, 12], is frequently associated with sleep disturbances [13–15]. Common problems such as shortened sleep duration, irregular sleep patterns, insomnia, and nightmares [16–18], are also linked to increased suicide risk [19, 20]. Although no single theory fully explains the association between sleep disturbances and NSSI, existing frameworks suggest several plausible pathways. Poor sleep may impair emotion regulation, reduce distress tolerance, and weaken adaptive coping [21], while also heightening arousal, irritability, rumination, and emotional reactivity [22]. In addition, sleep loss has been associated with reduced inhibitory control and poorer decision-making [23, 24]. Together, these changes may increase adolescents’ vulnerability to NSSI, which is often used as a rapid but maladaptive strategy to relieve emotional or interpersonal distress. Supporting this possibility, a previous study of more than 2,300 adults found that nightmares and insomnia predicted future self-harm [25]. Therefore, sleep disturbances may represent an important and potentially modifiable factor in the development and maintenance of NSSI [8].

Although previous studies have documented associations between NSSI, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation [20], most existing meta-analyses have focused on general adolescent populations or on self-harm broadly defined [26–29]. Few studies have specifically quantified the epidemiological burden of sleep disturbances among adolescents who already engage in NSSI, nor have they synthesized sleep-related risk factors within this high-risk group. Given the rising incidence of NSSI and the modifiable nature of sleep-related factors [30], a focused synthesis of the epidemiological evidence is warranted. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to (1) estimate the pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances among adolescents engaging in NSSI and (2) systematically identify and synthesize associated sleep-related risk factors within this high-risk population. By focusing on adolescents with established NSSI behaviors rather than the general population, the present study provides NSSI-specific prevalence estimates and examines whether particular psychosocial and demographic factors are differentially associated with sleep disturbances in this group. This population-specific synthesis moves beyond replication by clarifying the magnitude of sleep problems within a clinically relevant subgroup and by identifying factors that may inform targeted screening and intervention strategies in nursing and school-health settings.

Methods

Registration of protocol

The protocol for this systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), with the registration number CRD42024542886. This paper adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) reporting guidelines [31].

Study eligibility

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies included adolescents aged 11–19 years with a history of NSSI. This age range was selected in accordance with the definition of adolescence proposed by the World Health Organization (10–19 years), thereby covering early to late adolescence while maintaining developmental comparability across studies [32]. Sleep disturbances were defined as shortened sleep duration, irregular sleep patterns, or frequent nightmares, assessed using validated subjective instruments (e.g., Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI] [33], Insomnia Severity Index [ISI], Epworth Sleepiness Scale [ESS]) or objective measures (e.g., polysomnography or wearable devices). Studies were required to report either the prevalence of sleep disturbances or the association between NSSI and sleep-related risk factors, providing effect estimates such as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both cross-sectional and longitudinal designs were eligible.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they lacked full-text access or reported incomplete data. To ensure statistical reliability and precision of pooled estimates, studies with fewer than 50 participants were also excluded [34]. Additionally, non-primary or non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded (protocols, reviews, conference abstracts, editorials, and case reports). Additionally, the search strategy intentionally included the broader term “self-harm” to maximize sensitivity, further conceptual screening was conducted to ensure consistency with the definition of NSSI. Studies were included only when the self-injurious behavior was explicitly defined as non-suicidal, or when the reported sample and outcome data allowed clear separation of NSSI from suicidal self-harm or suicide attempts. Studies using the broader term “self-harm” were excluded if they: (1) combined suicidal and non-suicidal self-injurious behaviors without separate reporting; (2) explicitly involved suicidal intent, suicide attempts, or mixed self-harm outcomes that could not be disaggregated; or (3) did not provide sufficient information to determine whether the behavior met the definition of NSSI (Table 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for study selection

Search strategy

Six electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, PsycINFO, CNKI, and SinoMed, were systematically searched from inception to March 2026. The choice of databases was informed by Cochrane guidance, which identifies CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase as core sources for systematic reviews and notes that many search principles also apply to other review types and study designs. PubMed was used to cover MEDLINE, and Embase was included as a core biomedical database [35]. Given the psychological and psychiatric relevance of non-suicidal self-injury and sleep disturbances, PsycINFO was additionally searched. Web of Science Core Collection was used to broaden retrieval across disciplines, and CNKI and SinoMed were included to capture potentially eligible studies published in Chinese. Taken together, this database combination was considered adequate to cover the major biomedical, psychological, multidisciplinary, and Chinese language literature relevant to the present review. The search strategy combined terms related to (1) nonsuicidal self-injury, (2) sleep disturbances, (3) prevalence, and (4) risk factors, including both English- and Chinese-language publications. The complete search strategy is provided in Appendix A.

Study selection

All records were imported into EndNote X9.3.3 for management and duplicate removal. After automatic and manual deduplication, two reviewers (WW and YY) independently screened titles and abstracts according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies that potentially met the eligibility criteria were retrieved for full-text assessment. Studies were excluded if full text was unavailable, if essential data required for meta-analysis (e.g., sample size, prevalence of sleep disturbances, or effect estimates with corresponding confidence intervals) were missing, or if the sample size was < 50. Authors were contacted when such data were incomplete, and studies were excluded only if the required information could not be obtained. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (RY) to reach consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (WW and LX) independently assessed methodological quality tools according to study design. Cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the checklist developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), which consists of 11 items assessing selection bias, measurement validity, and data handling. Each item was scored as “Yes” (1 point), “No” (0 point), or “Unclear” (0 point), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 11. Scores of 0–3, 4–7, and 8–11 indicated low, moderate, and high methodological quality, respectively [36]. Cohort and longitudinal studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome domains with a maximum of 9 points. Studies scoring < 4, 4–6, and 7–9 were categorized as low, moderate, and high quality, respectively [37].

Although not specifically developed for adolescents, the AHRQ checklist and the NOS are widely used to assess methodological quality in observational studies [38]. Methodological evaluations support their suitability for cross-sectional and cohort designs, with generally acceptable inter-rater reliability and applicability across diverse populations [39, 40]. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (WW and YY) independently extracted data using a extraction form. The extracted information covered four domains: (1) study characteristics, including author, publication year, country, and study design; (2) participant demographics, including total sample size, age, sex distribution, and sample setting (e.g., community or clinical); (3) assessment methodology, including diagnostic criteria and tools used to assess NSSI; and (4) outcomes, including the number of participants with sleep disturbances and the ORs with 95% CIs for relevant risk factors. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (RY). For studies with incomplete or unclear data, corresponding authors were contacted, and studies were excluded only if essential data remained unavailable. Review process consistency

Two reviewers independently performed title/abstract screening, full-text assessment, risk-of-bias assessment, and data extraction. Inter-rater agreement at each stage was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and consultation with a third reviewer was undertaken when necessary.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was divided into two parts using Stata 14 software, including pooling the prevalence of sleep disturbances and synthesizing the risk factors.

The first part involved calculating the prevalence of sleep disturbances. The specific calculation formulas used were as follows: (1) P = the number of NSSI individuals with sleep disturbances/total number of NSSI individuals; (2) Se = sqrt(P * (1 - P)/the total number of NSSI individuals). Here, P represented the prevalence of sleep disturbances, and Se denoted the standard error of P. The second part consisted of sorting out the identified risk factors. The effect sizes were combined as ORs with their corresponding 95%CIs.

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistics and the I2 index, which was used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity with a threshold p-value of 0.05 considered statistically significant. Values of 25%, 50%, and 75% for I² were deemed to represent low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively. The results were graphically depicted in forest plots. An I² index value greater than 50% indicated a significant degree of heterogeneity. If I² > 50%, a random effects model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence. Otherwise, a fixed effects model was chosen [41]. To delve into the potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was conducted to explore and explain the reasons behind the heterogeneity. The subgroup analysis was based on the presence or absence of mental disorders, the quality of the literature, and countries. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s tests, with P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. The stability of the meta-analysis was evaluated by the sensitivity analysis with the stepwise elimination method. This meta-analysis synthesized study-level effect estimates rather than individual participant data, formal multicollinearity diagnostics (e.g., variance inflation factors) could not be performed [42]. To partially address confounding, the most fully adjusted effect sizes reported in each study were extracted whenever available [43].

Results

Search results

Inter-rater agreement, as measured by Cohen’s kappa, was 0.83 for title/abstract screening, 0.81 for full-text screening, 0.86 for risk-of-bias assessment, and 0.80 for data extraction. Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. The systematic search identified 1,769 records, of which 1,521 remained after duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening, 1,452 records were excluded. Seventy-one full-text articles were assessed for eligibility (69 from database searches and 2 from reference screening). Of these, 28 were excluded due to inaccessible full text (n = 4), protocol or methodological papers (n = 3), sample size < 50 (n = 6), or not meeting the target age range (n = 15). For 26 studies with incomplete sleep-disturbance data, corresponding authors were contacted via email; five responses were received (response rate: 19.2%). Four studies reported that the relevant data had not been collected, and one study provided additional usable data. An additional 25 studies were excluded due to insufficient or unextractable quantitative data. In total, 53 full-text articles were excluded. Ultimately, 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis, of which 13 reported prevalence estimates, 13 examined risk factors, and 5 reported both outcomes.

PRISMA flow diagram of the initial study selection process

Following the initial search conducted up to March 2024, an updated search was undertaken in March 2026 to identify newly published studies. The updated search, covering the period from March 2024 to March 2026, yielded 521 additional records. After removal of duplicates, 416 records remained for title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 30 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 25 were excluded due to inaccessible full text (n = 2), protocol or methodological papers (n = 3), sample size < 50 (n = 5), failure to meet the target age range (n = 9), or insufficient data on the number of adolescents with NSSI who experienced sleep disturbances (n = 6). Ultimately, 5 additional studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the risk-factor analysis of the systematic review(see Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of the updated study selection process

As a result, the final review included 23 studies, of which 8 reported prevalence estimates, 15 examined risk factors, and 5 reported both outcomes.

Methodological quality

In total, the results of the literature quality evaluation indicated that there were 2 low-quality studies [44, 45] and 5 high-quality studies [33, 46–49]. The remaining studies were medium-quality studies. All cross-sectional studies were rated between 4 and 9 using AHRQ, with 4 rated as high quality [33, 46, 48, 49] and the remaining 12 studies rated as moderate quality. Eight studies lacked clarity in the item “Describe any assessments undertaken for quality assurance purposes,” which may subsequently have led to detection bias [33, 50–56]. All longitudinal studies were rated between 3 and 8, with 1 study rated as high quality [47], 4 studies rated as moderate quality [57–60], and 2 studies rated as low quality [44, 45]. Four studies failed to clearly demonstrate that the outcome of interest was not present at the start of the study, potentially leading to the occurrence of confounding factors and inaccuracies in the findings [44, 45, 57, 60]. Participants

For prevalence analyses (13 studies), sample sizes ranged from 54 to 8,141 participants, yielding a total of 13,646 adolescents aged 13–18 years (mean age = 15.6 years). Four studies focused on adolescents with psychiatric conditions, including depression [61], bipolar disorder [61], mood disorders [64], major depressive disorder [64], post-traumatic stress disorder, and childhood sexual abuse [45]. For risk-factor analyses, 15 studies were included. The most commonly examined risk factors were depression, female sex, externalizing problems, and emotional neglect. Specifically, depression was assessed in 4,891 participants (mean age = 15.1 years), female sex in 11,528 participants (age range = 13–15 years), externalizing problems in 1,775 participants (mean age = 15.3 years), and emotional neglect in 1,437 participants (mean age = 14.4 years).

The pooled sleep disturbances prevalence

Across the included studies, the prevalence of sleep disturbances among adolescents with NSSI ranged from 31% to 47% (Fig. 3). The pooled prevalence across 13 studies was 41% (95% CI: 39%–44%), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 76.8%, P < 0.001). Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

The forest plot of the pooled sleep disturbances

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on mental-disorder status, study quality, and country. The pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances among adolescents with co-occurring mental disorders was 39% (95% CI: 34%–43%; I² = 0.0%), compared with 42% (95% CI: 39%–44%; I² = 83.4%) among those without diagnosed mental disorders (Fig. 4.). When stratified by study quality, pooled prevalence estimates were 46% (95% CI: 44%–49%; I² = 0.0%) in low-quality studies, 41% (95% CI: 40%–41%; I² = 71.1%) in moderate-quality studies, and 45% (95% CI: 41%–49%; I² = 100%) in high-quality studies (Fig. 5). By country, the pooled prevalence was 41% in China (95% CI: 38%–44%; I² = 82.9%) and 41% in other countries (95% CI: 37%–46%; I² = 59.7%) (Fig. 6). Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted based on study design. The pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances was 42.1% (95% CI: 40.1%–44.1%; I² = 27.5%) in cross-sectional studies and 40.0% (95% CI: 34.8%–45.2%; I² = 87.8%) in longitudinal studies. The subgroup difference was not statistically significant (Q = 0.715, P = 0.398), suggesting that study design did not significantly influence the pooled prevalence estimate (Fig. 7).

Risk factors of sleep disturbances

As shown in Table 4, a total of 11 risk factors related to sleep disturbances among adolescents with NSSI were identified across 15 studies. The most frequently examined factors were depression (7 studies), female sex (4 studies), emotional neglect (3 studies), and externalizing problems (2 studies), whereas social jet lag, eveningness chronotype, psychological resilience, stressful life events, sensation seeking, cytokines, and anxiety were each reported in one study. Overall, depression, female sex, externalizing problems, social jet lag, eveningness chronotype, stressful life events, sensation seeking, and anxiety were positively associated with sleep disturbances, while psychological resilience appeared to play a protective role. Findings for emotional neglect were inconsistent across individual studies, and cytokines were identified through network analysis as potentially relevant biological correlates.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to examine the prevalence of sleep disturbances and associated risk factors among adolescents with NSSI. The pooled prevalence was 41%, notably higher than the 26% reported among adolescents without NSSI [65], suggesting that adolescents engaging in NSSI may be more vulnerable to sleep disturbances. It is important to note the conceptual proximity among NSSI, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation. Although these constructs frequently co-occur and demonstrate partial psychometric overlap, they represent theoretically distinct phenomena. Depressive symptoms primarily reflect a pervasive affective condition, suicidal ideation involves cognitive engagement with thoughts of death or self-harm, whereas NSSI is conceptualized as a maladaptive behavioral strategy typically enacted without suicidal intent, often serving an emotion-regulation function [66, 67]. In the present meta-analysis, these constructs were treated as related but nonredundant based on their distinct theoretical definitions and measurement approaches. Clarifying these boundaries is essential for accurate interpretation of the observed associations.

The insomnia–depression–impulsivity model offers a theoretical framework for this co-occurrence, proposing that insomnia frequently overlaps with elevated depressive symptoms and impulsivity, which may be associated with an increased likelihood of NSSI. Moreover, experiences of shame and negative rumination following NSSI may co-occur with more severe sleep disturbances, suggesting a potentially bidirectional association [68]. However, given that most included studies were cross-sectional, the temporal and causal relationships among these variables cannot be determined. While this model provides a conceptual basis, other sleep disturbances—such as nightmares and sleep deprivation—require further investigation. The mean age of participants (15 years) closely corresponds with the peak onset of NSSI [69], indicating that mid-adolescence constitutes a high-risk developmental window for the joint emergence of NSSI and sleep disturbances. Preventive and early-intervention strategies targeted at this period may therefore be particularly relevant.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the prevalence of sleep disturbances among adolescents with psychiatric disorders was 39% (I² = 0.0%), slightly lower than the 42% (I² = 83.4%) observed among those without psychiatric diagnoses, suggesting greater statistical stability in clinical samples. The observed heterogeneity may reflect both methodological and population differences. Clinical samples are typically assessed using standardized procedures, whereas community samples are more heterogeneous and potentially subject to selection bias [11]. Additional sources of variability may include the use of different sleep assessment instruments (e.g., PSQI, ISI, ESS) [70], inconsistencies in NSSI definitions and recall periods, and differences in demographic characteristics such as age, sex distribution, and recruitment setting [3]. Unmeasured contextual factors (e.g., socioeconomic status, academic pressure, and family environment) may also have contributed to residual heterogeneity [71]. Additionally, a subgroup analysis by study design showed that the pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances was comparable between cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, and the between-group difference was not statistically significant. This finding suggests that the high prevalence of sleep disturbances among adolescents with NSSI is relatively stable across study designs, indicating that study design itself may not be a major source of variation in prevalence estimates. This interpretation is consistent with recent longitudinal evidence showing that sleep problems remain meaningfully linked with self-harm over time, rather than being restricted to cross-sectional observations [72]. However, this result is not universal. In a meta-analysis of adolescents with substance use, study design was identified as a significant moderator of sleep-disturbance prevalence [73], suggesting that the influence of study design may vary across clinical or behavioral populations and may also depend on differences in follow-up duration, assessment methods, and case definitions. Although no significant difference was found between cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, this should not be interpreted as evidence of causality between NSSI and sleep disturbances. Instead, it suggests that the high prevalence of sleep disturbances in adolescents with NSSI is relatively consistent across study designs. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the direction of this association.

Beyond methodological influences, cross-country subgroup analyses revealed considerable variability, particularly within Chinese studies. Such inconsistencies may reflect the interplay of cultural, socioeconomic, environmental, and healthcare-system factors. Notably, 17 of the 23 included studies were conducted in China, demonstrating growing scholarly attention to this population. Previous study indicated that the lifetime prevalence of NSSI among Chinese adolescents was approximately 32%, roughly double that observed among other countries [74]. The high prevalence may be linked to sociocultural pressures such as intense academic demands, restrictive parental discipline, and limited emotional support within traditional family structures [75]. These contextual stressors may also contribute to the relatively limited accessibility of mental-health services. Indeed, a national epidemiological survey reported that only 12 of 1,007 Chinese individuals diagnosed with depression (0.5%) received adequate treatment [76], underscoring a substantial treatment gap and the urgent need to strengthen mental-health service provision in China.

The identified risk factors suggest that sleep disturbances in adolescents with NSSI are multifactorial, involving emotional, behavioral, circadian, and biological domains. Depression and female sex were the most consistently reported correlates, while factors such as externalizing problems, social jet lag, eveningness chronotype, stressful life events, sensation seeking, and anxiety further indicate that both psychological distress and behavioral dysregulation may contribute to sleep problems in this population. In contrast, psychological resilience appeared to be protective, whereas the inconsistent findings for emotional neglect and the preliminary evidence on cytokines suggest that some associations remain uncertain and require further investigation.

Depression was significantly associated with sleep disturbances self-perpetuating [77–80]. Previous studies have proposed the gut–brain–microbiota axis as a potential explanatory pathway. This bidirectional communication system links the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system through neural, immune, and endocrine mechanisms. Adverse life events may disrupt microbial diversity, reducing beneficial taxa (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) and increasing harmful ones (e.g., Proteobacteria, Bacteroides). Microbial metabolites—such as melatonin, serotonin, and short-chain fatty acids—may cross the blood–brain barrier and associate with emotion regulation and circadian rhythms [81]. Conversely, sleep disturbances can further alter microbial balance, particularly the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio [82]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms should be interpreted as hypothetical associations rather than confirmed causal pathways in the context of the present meta-analysis. Collectively, these findings suggest that interventions targeting the gut microbiome could represent a promising avenue for future research on sleep disturbances among adolescents with NSSI.

A notable gender difference was also observed, with female adolescents exhibiting greater susceptibility to sleep disturbances than males. This disparity may reflect both emotional and physiological factors. Females often display heightened emotional reactivity and reduced emotion-regulation capacity [83], increasing their vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and sleep problems [84]. Hormonal fluctuations may further contribute: declining estrogen levels during the luteal or premenstrual phases are associated with mood instability and impaired sleep. Accordingly, integrating sleep-hygiene education and emotion-regulation training into clinical and school-based mental-health programs may be beneficial.

Importantly, this analysis found no significant association between emotional neglect or externalizing problems and sleep disturbances among adolescents with NSSI. This finding diverges from some previous reports [85, 86]. One possible explanation is the limited statistical power resulting from the small number of studies included for these factors. Additionally, variations in measurement tools and sample characteristics across studies may have attenuated the observed associations. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution, and future research with larger and more diverse samples is warranted to clarify these potential pathways.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the literature search was updated and covered six major databases, not all potentially relevant databases were included. As a result, some eligible studies may have been missed, particularly those indexed only in other databases. Second, variation in the definition and terminology of NSSI may also have affected study identification. In this review, we applied relatively strict eligibility criteria to distinguish NSSI from broader concepts such as self-harm or suicidal self-injury. While this helped improve conceptual consistency, it may also have resulted in the exclusion of some potentially relevant studies in which NSSI-related behaviors were not reported separately or were described using broader terminology. Third, most included studies relied on self-reported sleep measures, which may be subject to recall bias and shared method variance. Finally, differences in assessment tools, study populations, and cultural contexts may have reduced comparability and contributed to residual heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Sleep disturbances represent a significant public health concern, particularly among adolescents around age 15, females, and those with depression. These findings highlight key high-risk groups for targeted screening and early intervention. Routine sleep assessment should be integrated into school- and community-based screening. Nurses are well positioned to conduct early screening, provide sleep-health education and emotional support, and facilitate timely referral when needed. Strengthening training in sleep and psychological risk assessment may further support nurse-led early detection and prevention.
