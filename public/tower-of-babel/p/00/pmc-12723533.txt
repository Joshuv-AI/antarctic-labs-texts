Oral Intake of Plasticizer Di-(2-ethylhexyl) Phthalate Disrupts Cerebellar Myelination via Thyroid Signaling in Aging Mice
Authors: Qu-Nan Wang, Fengjie Yang, Xianyan Wang, Xiaoquan Ma, Xiaohan Yang, Lingyu Li, Shenglong Cao, Yán Wa̅ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is a significant global health challenge, with neurodegenerative diseases severely affecting the quality of life in the elderly. Di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), a commonly used plasticizer in food packaging, has been identified as an environmental contaminant with thyroid hormone (TH) disruption and neurotoxic effects. Myelination in the central nervous system is crucial for neurological function, and oligodendrocytes play a central role in this process by forming myelin around axons. THs regulate oligodendrocyte differentiation and the expression of myelin-related genes. This study investigates whether environmental exposure to DEHP in aging mice disrupts thyroid hormone signaling and contributes to myelin damage. 22-month-old male C57BL/6J mice were orally administered DEHP at doses of 0, 0.2, 20, and 200 mg/kg·bw/day (mg/kg·bw/d) for 30 days (d). Nissl staining revealed significant neuronal loss in the cerebellum at higher DEHP doses. Luxol Fast Blue staining showed notable hypomyelination in DEHP-treated mice. Immunofluorescence results demonstrated an increase in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (NG2+), while mature (CC1+) and myelinating oligodendrocytes (MBP+) were significantly reduced, indicating impaired oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination. Furthermore, KLF9, a transcriptional regulator critical for oligodendrocyte differentiation, was downregulated. TH levels (T3, T4), transporters (MCT8, OATP1C1), and receptors (TRα, TRβ) were decreased locally, while deiodinases (DIO2, DIO3) were upregulated, reflecting disruption in thyroid signaling. Environmental exposure to DEHP disrupts thyroid hormone signaling in aged mice, impairing oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelin formation. This process is accompanied by microglial activation and neuroinflammation, which may serve as a pathological basis for neuronal loss and further neurodegeneration. Daily oral intake of plasticizer DEHP may induce cerebellar demyelination, contributing to neurodegeneration in the elderly. These results highlight that inappropriate exposure to environmental pollutants, such as DEHP, could increase the risk of cerebellar dysfunction and neurodegeneration in elderly individuals.

Introduction

Population aging is one of the most critical medical and socio-demographic issues worldwide. With projections estimating that by 2050, the global elderly population (aged 65 and above) will exceed 1.5 billion, accounting for 16% of the total population. China is among the countries experiencing rapid aging, with approximately 300 million elderly individuals by 2050. Age-related cognitive decline is particularly concerning for the elderly, directly impacting their quality of life. The “Healthy China 2030” initiative explicitly emphasizes the strategic shift toward a health-centered approach and the proactive response to “healthy aging,” with “maintaining brain health” becoming a key objective.

The environment we live in is complex and contains various risk factors. Phthalic acid esters (PAEs) are synthetic chemicals widely used as plasticizers in industrial, agricultural, food, and medical applications. Studies have detected PAEs in food at concentrations ranging from 40 to 200 μg/kg, with di­(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), benzyl butyl phthalate (BBzP), and di-n-butyl phthalate (DnBP) being the most common. DEHP is one of the most extensively used PAEs plasticizers, imparting flexibility and durability to polyvinyl chloride (PVC) products, including food packaging, medical devices, and various industrial materials. , However, due to its weak binding to polymer matrices, DEHP is easily released into the environment during manufacturing, transportation, and usage. The primary route of DEHP exposure in the general population is through dietary intake. In China, the highest estimated daily dietary exposure to DEHP in the general population is 27.86 μg/kg·bw. DEHP and its metabolites have been identified as ubiquitous environmental endocrine disruptors, raising growing concerns about their potential health effects. ,

Numerous studies have demonstrated the neurotoxic effects of DEHP. Adolescent male C57BL/6 mice exposed to DEHP through diet exhibited anxiety-like behaviors, social behavior abnormalities, and dose-dependent induction of depressive-like behaviors. Recent findings also indicate that adult male mice exposed to environmentally relevant doses of DEHP experienced learning and memory impairments, as evidenced by increased latency in the Morris water maze test and decreased preference index in the novel object recognition test. However, the neurotoxic effects of DEHP exposure in aged mice remain unexplored, warranting further investigation into its mechanisms of action in elderly populations.

Additionally, DEHP is known to disrupt thyroid hormone (TH) function. The normal physiological functions of nearly all body tissues and organs, including the heart, lungs, central nervous system (CNS), and reproductive organs, depend on thyroid hormones triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). In the CNS, peripheral THs cross the blood-brain barrier via thyroid hormone transmembrane transporters, such as organic anion transporting polypeptide 1C1 (OATP1C1) and monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8). Intracellular TH levels are regulated by the deiodinase family, wherein deiodinase 2 (DIO2) converts T4 into the active T3 form, while deiodinase 3 (DIO3) degrades T4 into reverse T3 (rT3) and T3 into 3,3′-diiodothyronine (T2). , Studies have shown that DEHP exposure reduces TH levels and decreases the expression of thyroid hormone transport proteins OATP1C1 and MCT8 in juvenile and adult male rodents. − However, the effects of DEHP exposure on thyroid function in elderly animals remain unclear.

Myelin in the CNS is a concentric multilayered membrane structure formed by oligodendrocytes that enwrap neuronal axons. Oligodendrocytes originate from neural progenitor or precursor cells in periventricular and subventricular regions of the CNS and undergo differentiation stages from oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) to nonmyelinating oligodendrocytes before finally maturing into myelinating oligodendrocytes that express key myelin-associated proteins, including myelin basic protein (MBP), proteolipid protein (PLP), and myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG). , Myelin sheaths provide neuroprotection, metabolic support, and enhance the saltatory conduction of action potentials along axons. − Age-related impairments in myelination and spatial memory have been observed in mice. Epidemiological studies have reported a positive correlation between urinary DEHP metabolite concentrations and depression risk in elderly populations. Our previous cohort study on elderly individuals in Anhui Province also found a significant positive association between urinary DEHP metabolite levels and depressive states. Neuroimaging studies have confirmed early reductions in white matter signal integrity in depressive patients, and post-mortem analyses of depressed individuals have revealed decreased axonal density and MBP levels in the brain. , Whether DEHP-induced neurotoxicity in aging is linked to myelin abnormalities remains an open question.

In the brain, THs enter oligodendrocytes through transmembrane transporters, bind to thyroid hormone receptors (TRs), and regulate signaling pathways involved in myelin-associated protein gene expression. , THs play a critical role in various stages of oligodendrocytes development, particularly in differentiation. Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9), a T3-inducible transcription factor, is essential for oligodendrocytes differentiation and is one of the most consistently upregulated genes in response to T3, driving OPCs toward oligodendrocytes differentiation. , The KLF9 promoter contains functional T3 response elements and is regulated by thyroid hormone receptor β 1 (TRβ1), which is crucial for oligodendrocytes maturation. , In vitro studies have demonstrated that OPCs express both thyroid hormone receptor α (TRα) and TRβ1, with TRβ1 expression increasing as oligodendrocytes mature. Moreover, thyroid hormones regulate the expression of nearly all myelin protein genes, including MBP, PLP, and MAG, with MBP being a major structural component of CNS myelin. − TRβ1 serves as a trans-activator of MBP gene expression, further underscoring the essential role of THs and TRs in oligodendrocytes differentiation and myelination.

Given these findings, we hypothesize that the neurotoxic effects of DEHP exposure in aging may be associated with myelin damage mediated by thyroid hormone signaling disruption. Therefore, this study mainly focuses on the neurotoxicity of daily DEHP intake in the aged individuals from the perspective of THs/TRs/oligodendrocyte differentiation, and further studies are needed to elucidate other potential underlying mechanisms of DEHP-induced neurotoxicity in aged populations.

Materials and Methods

Mice Grouping and Treatment

Eight-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6J Nifdc mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Certificate No. SYXK2020–001). The mice were housed in the SPF animal facility of the Environmental Toxicology Laboratory at Anhui Medical University under controlled conditions: temperature 20–25 °C, humidity 40%–55%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice were provided autoclaved tap water and standard chow ad libitum. At 22 months of age, they were randomly assigned to four groups for oral DEHP gavage.

The lowest exposure dose of DEHP was selected based on human environmental exposure levels, as estimated daily DEHP intake in the elderly individuals of 1–20 μg/kg·bw. ,, Using a body surface area dose conversion formula, 0.2 mg/kg·bw/day (mg/kg·bw/d) was determined as the lowest exposure dose for this study. A high dose of 200 mg/kg·bw/d was selected based on previous studies on DEHP neurotoxicity. , The control solution consisted of 42.5 mL distilled water, 2.5 mL Tween- 80 (Moisture content: < 3%, CAS no.9005–65–6; BioFroxx), and 5 mL corn oil (CAS no. 8001–30–7; Solarbio). The 200 mg/kg·bw/d DEHP solution contained 41.5 mL distilled water, 2.5 mL Tween-80, 5 mL corn oil, and 1 mL DEHP (purity 99%, CAS no. 117–81–7; Sigma).

At 22 months of age, mice were randomly divided into four groups (0, 0.2, 20, and 200 mg/kg·bw/d DEHP; n = 11 per group, 3–4 per cage). DEHP was administered by oral gavage at 10 μL/g body weight for 30 d (6 d/week, 5 weeks in total). Food and water consumption were recorded weekly. Neurobehavioral Test

Open-Field Test (OFT) was conducted in a white rectangular open-field apparatus (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm). At the start of each trial, a mouse was placed in one corner of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min, with behavior recorded by a camera positioned above the apparatus. The arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. Locomotor activity was analyzed using SMART 3.0 software (Panlab). The primary parameters measured were the time spent in the center zone (defined as 25% of the total area) and the total distance traveled.

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) apparatus consisted of a cross-shaped platform elevated 50 cm above the ground, comprising two open arms (25 cm × 8 cm × 12 cm) and two closed arms of the same dimensions. At the beginning of each trial, a mouse was placed in the central square of the maze facing a closed arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Behavior was recorded by a camera mounted above the apparatus. The time spent in the open and closed arms, as well as the number of entries into each arm type, were quantified.

Nissl Staining

Cerebellar tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, rinsed with distilled water, and processed for paraffin embedding using an automated tissue processor. The tissues were sectioned coronally at 5 μm thickness and baked at 60 °C for 20 min. Sections were then deparaffinized in xylene (5 min × 3), rehydrated through graded ethanol, and rinsed with distilled water. Nissl staining was performed for 10 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water and 95% ethanol. Sections were dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. After drying overnight, slides were scanned, and neuronal density was quantified in four fixed regions of the cerebellum within a 135 × 135 μm area.

Luxol Fast Blue-Eosin Myelin Staining

Cerebellar tissues were processed and sectioned as described above. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and immersed in Luxol Fast Blue staining solution. Staining was conducted overnight at 56 °C. Sections were then differentiated in 95% ethanol and 70% ethanol until gray and white matter contrast was clear under a microscope. Eosin staining was performed for 2 min, followed by dehydration, clearing in xylene, and mounting. After drying, slides were scanned, and the percentage of myelin-stained area within a 135 × 135 μm field was quantified using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence

Fixed cerebellar tissues were dehydrated in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions until fully settled. Tissues were embedded in OCT compound, sectioned at 5–10 μm, and washed with PBS. Sections were permeabilized and blocked at room temperature for 10 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. DAPI staining was performed for 10 min, followed by PBS washes. Slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium, sealed with nail polish, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. Main antibodies used and immunofluorescent staining ratios are summarized in Tables S2–S3.

Western Blot Assay

Proteins were extracted using a lysis buffer composed of RIPA, 1% PMSF, and 1% phosphatase-protease inhibitors in a ratio of 100:1.2:1.2. Cerebellar tissues (0.1 g) were homogenized with magnetic beads in 1 mL lysis buffer at 60 Hz for 30 s. Homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 min with inversion every 5 min, then sonicated (5 cycles, 1 min each) at 4 °C and centrifuged (15,000g, 4 °C, 15 min). The supernatant was collected and centrifuged again under the same conditions for 10 min. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Protein samples were standardized to 4 μg/μL, mixed with 4× loading buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min before storage at −80 °C. SDS-PAGE gels were prepared, and electrophoresis was performed at 55 V for 60 min, followed by 110 V until bromophenol blue reached the gel bottom. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a 200 mA constant current for 90 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1.5 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using ECL reagents and analyzed using Image Pro Plus software. Main antibodies used are summarized in Table S2.

RT-qPCR Assay

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent, homogenized at 60 Hz for 2 min, and phase-separated with BCP. The aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol, followed by 75% ethanol washes and air drying. RNA was dissolved in RNase-free water, and purity (A260/280) was assessed using a spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, 500 ng of RNA was treated with DNase and reverse transcribed using Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix. qPCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. The relative gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primers for real-time RT-qPCR are listed in Table S4.

ELISA for T3 and T4 Levels in Cerebellar Tissue and Serum

Tissue samples were weighed and homogenized in PBS (pH 7.4) at a 1:50 ratio using a tissue homogenizer with two magnetic beads per tube. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. One portion was used for analysis, while the rest was stored at −80 °C. ELISA plates and standards were prepared, with standard solutions added to designated wells (50 μL per well). Blank wells contained no sample or enzyme reagent. Sample wells were loaded with 40 μL sample diluent and 10 μL sample (final dilution: 1:5). The enzyme reagent (100 μL) was added to all wells except the blanks. After sealing with a plate cover, incubation was performed at 37 °C for 60 min. After incubation, wells were washed five times with 1× washing buffer. Then, 50 μL of chromogenic reagents A and B were added sequentially, mixed gently, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of stop solution, turning the solution from blue to yellow. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. T3 and T4 levels were normalized to total protein concentrations determined by Western blot quantification.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (threshold: P = 0.05). Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. For normally distributed data with equal variances, one-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons against the control group. If variances were unequal, Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test was used. For non-normally distributed data (P < 0.05), the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was employed. Significance levels were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 compared with the control.

Results

DEHP Exposure Induces Demyelination in the Cerebellum of Aged Male Mice

To assess the general condition of aged male mice during DEHP exposure, body weight, food intake, and water intake were monitored. No significant differences in body weight, food intake, or water intake were observed among groups (Figure S1A–C). Additionally, brain weight was not significantly affected by DEHP exposure (Figure S1D), suggesting that DEHP exposure did not impact general health or brain mass. To assess the effects of DEHP exposure on neurobehavioral performance in aged mice, we conducted the OFT and EPM tests. In the EPM, no significant differences were observed between DEHP-treated mice and controls in any of the measured indices (Figure A–F). In the OFT, the total distance traveled did not differ between groups (Figure G,H). Although DEHP-exposed mice tended to spend less time and travel shorter distances in the central area compared with controls, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Notably, the duration spent in the peripheral zone was significantly increased in the high-dose DEHP group (Figure I–L), indicating potential neurobehavioral alterations associated with DEHP exposure in aged mice.

To investigate the effects of DEHP exposure on cerebellar myelination, Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) staining was performed. In control mice, LFB staining revealed densely packed deep blue myelinated fibers, while in the middle- and high-dose DEHP groups, staining intensity was noticeably reduced, appearing lighter and sparser (Figure M). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in the proportion of LFB-positive area in the middle- and high-dose groups compared to controls (Figure N). Furthermore, further validation using immunofluorescence, Western blot, and RT-qPCR revealed a significant reduction in myelin basic protein (MBP) at both the protein and mRNA levels in the high-dose group (Figures O–Q, and A,B). These results indicate that DEHP exposure leads to impaired myelination in the cerebellum of aged male mice.

DEHP Exposure Disrupts Oligodendrocyte Differentiation Leading to Demyelination

Oligodendrocyte lineage cells are essential for myelination formation. To explore the mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced cerebellar demyelination, oligodendrocyte lineage cells at different developmental stages were examined. Immunofluorescence staining for Olig2, a key oligodendrocyte marker, revealed a significant increase in Olig2+ cells in the middle- and high-dose DEHP groups compared with controls (Figure A–C), accompanied by elevated Olig2 protein and mRNA expression levels (Figure D–F), suggesting an overall increase in oligodendrocytes.

Oligodendrocyte development proceeds through sequential stages: neural progenitors differentiate into oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), which then develop into differentiated oligodendrocytes before acquiring myelination capacity through the expression of myelin-associated proteins such as MBP. To determine the stage at which DEHP exposure affects oligodendrocyte differentiation, we analyzed the expression of NG2 (a marker of OPCs), CC1 (a marker of differentiated oligodendrocytes), and MBP (a marker of mature myelinating oligodendrocytes) using immunofluorescence staining. DEHP exposure led to an increase in NG2 expression (Figure A,B), whereas CC1 expression was significantly reduced in the high-dose group (Figure C,D). Further quantification of the CC1+MBP+/CC1+ ratio showed a significant reduction in the proportion of MBP-positive oligodendrocytes in the high-dose group compared with controls (Figure E). These findings indicate that DEHP exposure promotes an increase in OPCs but impairs their differentiation into mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes, potentially due to decreased MBP expression.

To further investigate the effects of DEHP exposure on oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), we performed double immunofluorescence staining using the OPC marker NG2 and the proliferation marker Ki67. As shown in the figures, the numbers of NG2+ cells were markedly increased in the high-dose DEHP group compared with controls. However, the proportion of Ki67+NG2+/NG2+ cells showed no significant change (Figure A–C). These findings suggest that DEHP exposure increases the number of OPCs in the cerebellum without significantly altering their proliferation rate. To further examine the impact of DEHP exposure on differentiated oligodendrocytes, double immunofluorescence staining was performed with the mature oligodendrocyte marker CC1 in combination with the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase-3. As shown in the figures, the number of CC1+ cells was significantly reduced in the medium- and high-dose DEHP groups compared with controls. However, the proportions of cleaved caspase-3+CC1+/CC1+ cells were not significantly different (Figure D–F). These results indicate that DEHP exposure leads to a reduction in differentiated oligodendrocytes in the cerebellum without markedly affecting their apoptosis.

Dysregulated THs/TRs/KLF9 Signaling Underlies DEHP-Induced Oligodendrocyte Differentiation Failure

The neuronal differentiation factor Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9) plays an important role in oligodendrocyte differentiation. KLF9, a member of the KLF transcription factor family, plays a crucial role in neuronal differentiation and binds to regulatory regions of genes essential for oligodendrocyte differentiation. Immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR analyses demonstrated a significant reduction in KLF9 protein and mRNA levels in the middle- and high-dose groups compared to controls (Figure A–E). Western blot results further confirmed a marked decrease in KLF9 protein levels in the middle- and high-dose groups (Figure C,D). These findings suggest that DEHP exposure reduces KLF9 expression. The KLF9 promoter contains a functional thyroid hormone response element (TRE) regulated by thyroid hormone receptor β 1 (TRβ1). MBP, a major protein of central nervous system myelin, is also transcriptionally activated by TRβ1. To assess the effects of DEHP exposure on thyroid hormone receptors in the cerebellum of aged male mice, we examined the expression levels of TRα, TRβ, and TRβ1 using multiple approaches. Western blot analysis revealed significant reductions in TRα and TRβ protein levels across all DEHP-exposed groups (Figure F–I), whereas Trα and Trβ mRNA levels remained unchanged (Figure J,K). Immunofluorescence assay further demonstrated a marked decrease in TRβ1 expression following DEHP exposure (Figure L,M). These findings suggest that reduced TRβ1 expression may contribute to the downregulation of the transcriptional regulator KLF9 and the myelin-associated protein MBP in DEHP-exposed mice.

Thyroid hormones (THs) regulate oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination by binding to thyroid hormone receptors, forming complexes that activate the transcription of target genes such as KLF9 and MBP. THs are essential for oligodendrocyte maturation and function by modulating myelin-associated protein expression. To further investigate the effects of DEHP on the THs homeostasis in the cerebellum of aging male mice, Western blot and RT-qPCR experiments were performed. These analyses revealed a significant reduction in the protein levels of THs transporters OATP1C1 and MCT8, but increased THs deiodinase DIO2 and DIO3 (Figure ). These findings suggest that DEHP exposure impairs THs transport and metabolism, potentially disrupting the thyroid hormone signaling pathway in the cerebellum of aged mice. ELISA analysis revealed significantly lower T3 and T4 levels in the high-dose DEHP group compared to controls in the cerebellum (Figures B,C). There were no significant changes in serum T3 and T4 levels (Figure S2). Therefore, it is potentially that abnormal regulation of the THs/TRs/KLF9 signaling pathway underlies DEHP-induced oligodendrocyte differentiation failure and myelination impairment.

Demyelination Accompanied by Microglial Activation Induces Neuroinflammation and Neuronal Loss

Demyelination is often accompanied by neuroinflammatory responses. To investigate potential inflammatory mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced demyelination, we measured the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in the cerebellum. Western blot analysis showed significantly elevated TNF-α and IL-1β protein levels in the middle- and high-dose DEHP groups (Figure A–C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased microglial activation in these groups (Figure D,E), suggesting a neuroinflammatory response associated with demyelination.

DEHP exposure induces microglia activation and neuronal loss in the cerebellum of aged male mice. (A–C) TNF-α and IL-1β protein levels. (D) Representative images of CD68 and Iba-1 immunofluorescence staining. (E) Percentage of CD68+Iba-1+ cell numbers relative to Iba-1+ cells. (F) Neuron numbers per unit area (135 × 135 μm). (n = 3). (G) Representative image of cerebellar Nissl staining. (H) Cerebellar brain map. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Neurons are the fundamental structural and functional units of the nervous system. To assess the neurotoxic effects of DEHP exposure on cerebellar neurons, Nissl staining was performed. Quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction in neuronal density in the middle and high-dose groups compared to controls (Figure F–H). These findings indicate that DEHP exposure leads to neuronal loss in the cerebellum of aged male mice, contributing to neurodegeneration. The above findings suggest that DEHP exposure leads to cerebellar demyelination accompanied by microglial activation and neuroinflammation, which may serve as a pathological basis for neuronal loss and further neurodegeneration. This result highlights that oral exposure to DEHP in aging mice disrupts thyroid hormone signaling, impairs oligodendrocyte differentiation, and triggers neuroinflammation, collectively contributing to cerebellar demyelination and neuronal loss, underscoring the potential risk of environmental plasticizer exposure in accelerating neurodegenerative processes in the elderly (Figure ).

Discussion

Aging is one of the most critical global healthcare and socio-demographic challenges. Age-related cognitive decline significantly affects the quality of life in elderly populations. The “Healthy China 2030” initiative has highlighted the strategic need to proactively address healthy aging, with “maintaining brain health” identified as a high-priority objective. Increasing attention has been given to the impact of environmental factors on cognitive impairment in aging populations. DEHP, a commonly used plasticizer in food packaging and consumer products, is an endocrine-disrupting chemical with known neurotoxic effects. Adolescent C57BL/6 male mice exposed to DEHP through diet exhibit anxiety-like behavior, social behavior abnormalities, and induced depression-like behavior. Adult male mice exposed to DEHP experience impaired learning and memory abilities, with a decreased preference index for novel objects. Our study provides novel evidence demonstrating that aged mice exhibit neurotoxic damage following DEHP exposure.

Based on DEHP exposure levels reported in the Chinese population, estimated daily intake for the general population is 1–30 μg/kg·bw, and 1–20 μg/kg·bw for elderly individuals in our previously established cohort (Luan City, Anhui Province). The U.S. ATSDR estimates the human daily exposure range at 3–30 μg/kg·bw. Using body surface area dose conversion, the minimum dose in this study (0.2 mg/kg·bw/d = 200 μg/kg·bw/d) falls within the range of extrapolated human-relevant exposures for mice, though still below environmentally relevant intake when accounting for the reduced absorption from gavage administration. The dose of 20 mg/kg·bw/d is considered environmentally relevant, based on the application of an extrapolation factor of 10× from mice to humans and an additional uncertainty factor of 10×, resulting in a total adjustment of 100× from the reference dose of 0.2 mg/kg·bw/d. Our findings indicate that DEHP exposure leads to significant neuronal loss in the cerebellum of aged male mice, an effect that was not observed in the hippocampus in adult mice at similar exposure levels. Given the physiological characteristics of rapid absorption and slower metabolism of exogenous compounds in elderly individuals, combined with our observation of neuronal loss at lower DEHP doses in aged mice compared with previous findings in adults, it is plausible that aging may increase vulnerability to DEHP-induced neurotoxicity.

The mechanism underlying this neuronal loss appears to be associated with disruptions in thyroid hormone (TH) homeostasis. We observed significant reductions in the levels of TH transporters (OATP1C1, MCT8) and TH deiodinase enzyme. These findings suggest that DEHP disrupts TH signaling, which is critical for neurodevelopment and homeostasis in the aging brain. Additionally, we observed significantly decreased T3 and T4 levels in cerebellar tissues of DEHP-exposed aged mice, further supporting DEHP’s role in disrupting thyroid hormone balance. Similar disruptions in TH homeostasis have been reported in juvenile and adult mice exposed to DEHP, but the present study suggests a heightened susceptibility in aging individuals. −

TH signaling is essential for oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination. Our study revealed that DEHP exposure led to reduced MBP expression, a key component of myelin sheaths, in the cerebellum of aged mice. Histological analysis using Luxol Fast Blue staining and immunofluorescence confirmed significant myelin loss following DEHP exposure. These findings align with previous reports that thyroid hormone receptor TRβ1 is a transcriptional activator of the MBP gene, suggesting that DEHP-induced disruption of TH signaling contributes to myelination deficits in the aging cerebellum. Other environmental endocrine disruptors, such as polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), have also been shown to disrupt thyroid hormone signaling pathways and induce hypothyroidism. , Perinatal exposure to PCBs leads to downregulation of MBP expression in offspring. PCBs can interfere with TH function and reduce MBP expression, further underscoring the broader environmental relevance of our findings.

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced myelin loss, we examined oligodendrocyte lineage progression. Immunofluorescence analysis showed increased expression of Olig2, a marker of oligodendrocyte lineage cells, and NG2, a marker of oligodendrocyte precursor cells, suggesting an accumulation of OPCs in the cerebellum of DEHP-exposed aged mice. However, the number of CC1+ mature oligodendrocytes and the proportion of CC1+MBP+ cells were significantly reduced, indicating impaired differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells into mature myelinating oligodendrocytes. KLF9, a transcription factor crucial for oligodendrocyte differentiation, plays an important role in the differentiation of mammalian neuronal cells and can bind to gene regulatory regions critical for promoting the differentiation of oligodendrocytes. It facilitates the transition of oligodendrocyte precursor cells to the oligodendrocyte differentiation stage. , KLF9 was significantly downregulated in DEHP-exposed mice. These findings suggest that DEHP impairs oligodendrocyte maturation by downregulating KLF9, leading to reduced myelination in the aging cerebellum. In addition to oligodendrocytes, cerebellar glial cells include microglia, astrocytes, and satellite cells. Previous studies have shown that DEHP can impair proliferation of cerebellar granule precursor cells and affect other cerebellar cell populations in a cell type–specific manner. Recent work has further demonstrated region-specific and multitarget neurotoxicity of DEHP, including effects on both neuronal and glial populations. , Our findings add to this by demonstrating that glial cell types, in particular oligodendrocytes, are also susceptible to DEHP exposure, expanding the spectrum of known cerebellar targets for this endocrine disruptor.

Neuroinflammation is known to be closely associated with demyelination. Demyelination is always involved in neuroinflammation and responsible for glial cell activation to abnormally result in inflammatory microenvironment. We observed a significant increase in inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in the cerebellum of DEHP-exposed aged mice, accompanied by microglial activation. Also, TNF-α has been reported to modulate OPCs differentiation and may contribute to the observed accumulation of undifferentiated OPCs following DEHP exposure. This suggests that DEHP-induced neuroinflammation may further exaggerate the observed myelination deficits. Given that neuroinflammatory processes are implicated in age-related neurodegenerative diseases, our findings raise concerns about the potential contribution of environmental pollutants such as DEHP to the progression of neurodegeneration in elderly populations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides evidence that DEHP exposure disrupts TH signaling, impairs oligodendrocyte differentiation, and induces cerebellar demyelination in aged male mice. This process is associated with microglial activation and neuroinflammation, which may contribute to neuronal loss and increased susceptibility to neurodegeneration. Given the widespread presence of DEHP in the environment and its frequent use in consumer products, our findings highlight an urgent need to evaluate its potential impact on aging populations. Further studies are needed to explore the long-term consequences of DEHP exposure on neurodegenerative diseases and to establish regulatory measures that mitigate its effects on brain health in the elderly.
