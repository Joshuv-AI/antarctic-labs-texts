Explainable artificial intelligence models in predicting major cardiovascular events: insights from the PolyIran and PolyPars prospective studies
Authors: Amir Ghafari, Sadaf Sepanlou, Gholamreza Roshandel, Seyed Amir Ahmad Safavi-Naini, Fatemeh Malekzadeh, Maryam Sharafkhah, Amir Kasaeian, Aakriti Gupta, Shubhadarshini Pawar, Zahra Dehghanian, Amir Azimi, Mohammad Aghaei, Akram Pourshams, Hossein Poustchi, Nasrallah Jahangard, Hamid R Rabiee, Reza Malekzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases are among the leading causes of global mortality and morbidity, underscoring the need for accurate and explainable predictive models. This study aimed to develop and evaluate explainable AI models for predicting major cardiovascular events (MCVE) over 60 months. We conducted a secondary analysis of two large cohort studies nested within the Golestan (northeast Iran) and Pars (south Iran) Cohorts, which followed identical protocols. After applying exclusion criteria, 9,769 participants. Predictors included demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables, with class imbalance addressed via SMOTE. Machine learning models (XGBoost, logistic regression, decision tree, SVM, random forest, KNN) and a multilayer perceptron were trained and evaluated. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, AUC, and F1 score. Interpretability was examined using SHapley Additive exPlanations. Machine-learning models showed variable performance across cohorts, with XGBoost demonstrating the best and most stable discrimination. In the pooled cohort, XGBoost achieved the highest AUC (0.84), with good accuracy (0.81) and specificity (0.85). Its performance remained consistent in the PolyIran and PolyPars cohorts, with AUC values of 0.83 and 0.85, respectively, suggesting good generalizability. Random Forest was the second-best model. The MLP deep-learning model showed only modest performance. SHAP analysis identified age, creatinine, and systolic blood pressure as the most important predictors across cohorts, with higher values associated with increased MCVE risk. Higher FBS, BMI, LDL/HDL ratio, male sex, and smoking were also associated with increased risk, whereas PolyPill use and greater adherence showed protective effects. Explainable AI models, particularly XGBoost, demonstrated high predictive accuracy for MCVE and revealed creatinine as a novel risk indicator not included in traditional CVD scores. These findings support incorporating kidney function screening into CVD risk stratification and highlight the potential of AI-driven tools to enhance prevention strategies. Introduction

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of premature mortality and morbidity at both the global and national scales in Iran1,2. Traditional risk assessment models have provided valuable insights into cardiovascular risk, but often fall short in accuracy and applicability across diverse populations. Despite simplicity, they are mainly linear and encompass a few variables.

Recent advancements in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms offer promising alternatives by leveraging large datasets to identify complex patterns and interactions3–5. By integrating various demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors, these algorithms can enhance the predictive accuracy for major cardiovascular events (MCVE)6–9. Studies have demonstrated that these algorithms may outperform conventional models6,10–14..

In this paper, we explore the application of various ML and DL algorithms in predicting cardiovascular events through a comprehensive analysis of recent studies. We aim to highlight the methodologies employed, the predictive capabilities of multiple algorithms, and the implications for clinical practice.

Method

Study design and participants

In this analysis, we utilized data from the PolyIran and PolyPars studies, two pragmatic cluster-randomized trials nested within their prospective cohort studies. The PolyIran study was conducted within the Golestan Cohort Study, which consisted of 50,045 participants aged 40–75 from Golestan province, Iran15. In this cohort study, 6,838 rural participants aged 50 and older were recruited in PolyIran and followed for 5 years16.The PolyPars study was similarly nested within the Pars cohort study, which consisted of 9,264 participants aged 40–75 years in a rural district in southern Iran17. The PolyPars sampling frame included 4,415 participants aged 50 years and above, who were also followed for five years18,19. The protocols for both studies were the same, enabling us to compare and pool them in a sensitivity analysis. Participants were randomly allocated to one of the two arms in both trials. In the first arm, participants received minimal care, and in the second arm, they received a fixed-dose combination therapy, the so-called Polypill, in addition to the minimal care. The pill consisted of two anti-hypertensive agents, a statin, and aspirin.

Exclusion criteria

For this analysis, participants with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions were excluded. Participants without events who had fewer than 1,500 days of follow-up were also excluded from the study, along with those with incomplete health records, including missing values.

Outcomes definition

The primary outcome of interest in both studies was the first occurrence of major cardiovascular events (MCVE) within 60 months of enrollment. These events were defined as hospitalizations for acute coronary syndrome (non-fatal myocardial infarction and unstable angina), fatal myocardial infarction, sudden death, heart failure, coronary artery revascularization procedures, and non-fatal and fatal strokes20. For participants experiencing more than one event, only the first occurrence was included in the primary analysis.

Feature selection

The study integrated data from both trials. The dataset was thoroughly cleaned to address missing values, outliers, and incorrect entries. Highly correlated features were identified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and removed if the correlation exceeded 0.5 to minimize multicollinearity. In addition, we combined LDL and HDL cholesterol values into a single predictive feature, the LDL/HDL ratio. This decision is clinically grounded by the fact that the LDL/HDL ratio has been widely recognized as a more robust indicator of atherogenic risk than either lipid alone, reflecting the balance between “atherogenic” and “cardioprotective” cholesterol21. By using this ratio, we aim to enhance model interpretability and predictive performance while reducing collinearity among lipid-related variables.

Statistical analysis

Before model development, baseline characteristics between the two cohorts were compared using descriptive statistics. To evaluate differences in continuous variables between groups, a t-test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Univariable analysis was conducted to identify individual associations between predictors and cardiovascular events. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression was applied to adjust for potential confounders and assess the significance of each predictor.

Machine learning models

In this study, various Machine Learning models were developed and evaluated to predict MCVE. The models included XGBoost, Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree, and Naive Bayes. Each model was chosen for its distinct advantages, such as handling class imbalance, detecting complex relationships, and providing interpretability (Supplementary material for more details of models’ features).

Data imbalance handling

Given the class imbalance in the datasets, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to balance the target classes and prevent the model from biasing towards the majority class22. A proportion of 0.33 for reaching the minority class is considered sufficient to address class imbalance. In addition, SMOTE was applied only to the training data, not before data splitting, thereby preventing synthetic samples from leaking information into the validation or test data. The final test set was not used for hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, and SMOTE fitting.

Cross-validation and evaluation metrics

The machine learning data analysis applied Cross-validation with a K-fold of 5 to assess the model’s performance and ensure robustness. The data was split into five subsets: one for testing and four for training.

To assess model performance, we employed a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics to provide robust, balanced insights into their predictive capabilities. These metrics included: accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), area under the curve (AUC), and F1 score (see Supplementary for more details). The definition for all metrics is presented in the appendix.

Optimal threshold determination

Classification thresholds were optimized using threshold-based performance criteria, including the Youden index and geometric mean. The G-mean criterion was selected as the primary threshold selection method because it balances sensitivity and specificity and is well-suited for imbalanced classification problems. Threshold optimization was performed within the training/cross-validation procedure and was not based on the final test set, thereby minimizing the risk of data leakage. The threshold optimization curves are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Deep learning model development

In the first step, we developed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) model in TensorFlow, structured with five fully connected layers to enable the model to learn complex patterns within the data23.(supplementary material and supplementary Figure S1).

Model interpretation: SHAP analysis

Model selection was performed on statistical performance (higher AUC, specificity, and balanced accuracy) with clinical utility. XGBoost was also chosen for its interpretability because it integrates with SHAP analysis, enabling transparent evaluation of feature importance and directionality24.

During model training, we leveraged XGBoost’s regularization parameters to prevent overfitting and used tree-based boosting to improve the model’s accuracy iteratively. Additionally, the model’s feature importance scores provided insights into the relative contributions of each predictor. To interpret each feature’s contribution to model predictions, we employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations). SHAP values provide a clear and quantitative understanding of each variable’s importance and specific impact on individual predictions25..

Ethical considerations

This study is a secondary observational analysis based on two major clinical trials: PolyIran and PolyPars.

The PolyPars trial (Polypill for primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease: a pragmatic cluster-randomised controlled trial) involved human participants. It was approved by the Ethics Committees of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences and Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.DDRI.REC.1395.13). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Similarly, the PolyIran trial (Polypill for the prevention of cardiovascular disease: a pragmatic cluster-randomised controlled trial) was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Digestive Diseases Research Institute, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was also obtained from all participants in this trial. All data used for analysis was anonymized, ensuring no personally identifiable information was included. In addition, all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study participants

Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process for the PolyIran and PolyPars trials, forming a pooled cohort. In PolyIran, 6,838 participants were enrolled. Of these, 738 with pre-existing CVD and 116 patients with incomplete follow-up or clinical data were excluded, resulting in 5,984 eligible patients. In the PolyPars trial, out of 4,415 participants, 611 patients with pre-existing CVD and 19 patients with incomplete follow-up or clinical data were excluded, leaving 3,785 eligible patients. Both final populations were combined to create a pooled cohort with a total of 9,769 participants without pre-existing CVD, and with complete data and adequate follow-up were included in the analysis.

Univariable analysis of major cardiovascular event’s predictors in the pooled cohort

The comparison of cardiovascular risk factors between individuals with and without MCVE across the three cohorts, Pooled Cohort, PolyIran, and PolyPars, was made.

Those with MCVE were consistently older and more likely to be male (in the Pooled and PolyIran cohorts). Preexisting diabetes and hypertension were significantly more prevalent in MCVE groups, with hypertension being particularly high in PolyIran (63.8% vs. 46.3%). Metabolic differences were also evident, as individuals with MCVE had higher FBS, total cholesterol, LDL levels, and LDL/HDL ratios across all cohorts. Blood pressure, especially systolic (SBP), was notably elevated in MCVE groups, reinforcing the link between these risk factors and cardiovascular events.

Notably, PolyPill usage was significantly lower in MCVE groups (35.5% vs. 50.6% in the Pooled Cohort), suggesting a potential protective effect. Anti-hypertensive medication use was higher in MCVE groups, likely due to greater hypertension prevalence, while aspirin and anti-lipid drug use showed no significant differences (Table 2).

Correlation of variables in the dataset

The correlation of the variables is demonstrated in Supplementary Figure S3. We removed highly correlated parameters (with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.5). There was a strong correlation between LDL, HDL, and cholesterol levels. Based on clinical findings, we combined LDL and HDL into the LDL-to-HDL ratio, omitting total cholesterol. Additionally, since hypertension, antihypertensive medications, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure were highly correlated, we selected SBP as the predictor. Similarly, FBS was chosen instead of the preexisting diabetes.

Machine learning models performance

Table 3; Fig. 2 compare the performance of machine-learning models across the pooled cohort, PolyIran cohort, and PolyPars cohort. In the pooled cohort, XGBoost achieved the highest AUC among all models (0.84), with good accuracy (0.81), sensitivity (0.71), specificity (0.85), and the highest F1 score (0.66). Random Forest performed closely, with an AUC of 0.82, slightly higher sensitivity (0.73) than XGBoost, but lower specificity (0.78). In the PolyIran and PolyPars cohorts, XGBoost again had the highest AUC (0.83 and 0.85, respectively). Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression showed moderate sensitivity, but their lower specificity and precision limited their usefulness.

In the pooled cohort, age had the most significant mean impact on the outcome, followed by creatinine and SBP. As shown in Fig. 4, higher age, creatinine, and SBP were associated with a higher probability of MCVE. Additionally, higher FBS, BMI, and LDL-to-HDL ratio were associated with an increased risk of MCVE. The use of a PolyPill had protective effects, while male gender was associated with a higher risk of MCVE. Furthermore, smoking increased the risk of MCVE.

Across both cohorts and the pooled cohort, age, creatinine, and SBP were the top determinants. In the PolyIran cohort, age had the most significant impact, whereas in the PolyPars cohort, creatinine was the most influential factor. The importance of lipid profile was greater in PolyIran than in PolyPars, whereas FBS played a more significant role in PolyPars. Gender and Smoking in all cohorts had the least impact on the model’s final output.

As shown in Supplementary Figure S7, increasing adherence to the Polypill is associated with a lower risk of experiencing an MCVE, and adherence is even more important.

Discussion

In this study, we employed various AI methods to predict the CVD risk in two large cohorts. DL models achieved high sensitivity and balanced accuracy. Among the ML models, XGBoost demonstrated strong overall performance, achieving high AUC, balanced accuracy, and specificity. Age, creatinine, and SBP were the top predictors of MCVE risk. Higher FBS, BMI, and the LDL-to-HDL ratio were also associated with increased risk, while PolyPill use had a protective effect. Smoking and male gender had less impact, though they were still associated with elevated MCVE risk.

Despite sharing the same protocol, populations in the two studies exhibit notable differences. Their geographical separation is significant, encompassing populations living at substantially different latitudes within Iran, which is known for its diverse topography and climate. Northeastern Iran has a moderate climate with higher precipitation, while southern Iran typically experiences hotter, drier conditions. These distinct environments may influence lifestyle patterns. Our findings highlight significant differences between the two populations that likely reflect distinct geographical and cultural contexts, as well as differences in ethnicity.

BMI and hypertension showed a striking disparity26–28. Smoking rates displayed disparity, which reflects cultural variations in tobacco use and the type of tobacco29–32. While preexisting diabetes showed no significant difference, the pattern of medication use varied. Rural communities across Iran may engage in various agricultural and economic activities based on their geographic context, which, in turn, affects lifestyle patterns33..

SHAP analysis revealed age, creatinine levels, and SBP as the top three predictors across both populations. While age and SBP are well-established CVD risk factors included in traditional risk scores, the consistent ranking of creatinine among the strongest predictors is a clinically important finding that should be interpreted within the established cardiorenal framework rather than as an entirely novel risk factor34. Renal dysfunction is a recognized contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, and previous large cohort and meta-analytic studies have shown that reduced kidney function, commonly assessed using creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate, is independently associated with cardiovascular events, hospitalization, and death34–36. Therefore, the significance of our finding lies in the high relative importance of creatinine compared with conventional predictors in two geographically distinct Iranian cohorts and the pooled cohort. This suggests that routinely available renal function markers may provide additional value for cardiovascular risk stratification beyond traditional demographic and hemodynamic variables, particularly in primary prevention settings. The consistent high ranking of creatinine across cohorts strengthens the credibility of this observation and supports the potential role of integrating renal markers into AI-based cardiovascular risk prediction models.

The similar factors ranking between the two populations further support the biological consistency of CVD risk mechanisms. Despite differences in baseline characteristics, the relative importance of these factors remained comparable. Perhaps surprisingly, smoking ranked among the lowest predictors despite its well-established role in CVD pathogenesis. This unexpected finding might reflect regional patterns of tobacco use, interactions with other risk factors, or characteristics specific to Iranian populations.

One clinically relevant finding is the importance of medication adherence in CVD prevention. Adherence to PolyPill significantly impacts the prevention of MCVE16. Notably, a significant portion of Polypill’s benefit is attributed to its high adherence rate, which is facilitated by the convenience of a single pill37,38.

In our study, the SMOTE technique helped improve model performance39. SMOTE’s flexibility across various algorithms and ease of implementation contributed to its effectiveness. However, it also introduced limitations, including increased computational complexity, potential noise amplification, and reduced effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces. Additionally, its impact on model performance can vary across different datasets. Evidence suggests that SMOTE should be used sparingly and highlights the need for a critical evaluation of its practical application in real-world scenarios40–42. In addition, we used SMOTE in the training data to prevent data leakage between the test and training sets. In some studies, a downsampling approach is used to address data imbalance for more realistic results, but this comes at the cost of decreased model accuracy43.

Among ML models, XGBoost demonstrated superior performance44,45. It is an ensemble learning method that captures complex nonlinear relationships between predictors and the outcome variable, which simpler models may miss46,47. It incorporates regularization techniques to prevent overfitting, which is a common problem when complex models are trained on limited datasets48,49. Ultimately, it can automatically learn feature interactions, reducing the need for extensive data preprocessing and feature engineering49. Regarding deep learning models, as observed in this study, MLP did not perform as well as machine learning models. So, it can be concluded that deep learning is often better suited to complex tasks such as image processing. In addition, it is not easily explainable and can interrupt clinical decision-making. Even if the deep learning-based models like MLP show good performance, their “black box” nature makes it challenging to interpret and translate into actionable insights50. Deep learning models struggle significantly with tabular data because they lack the specific inductive biases required to handle irregular target functions and uninformative features, whereas XGBoost and other tree-based models excel by recursively partitioning the feature space. Research indicates that neural networks often generalize poorly on structured datasets, often requiring far more hyperparameter tuning and computational resources to achieve results that are equivalent or inferior to those of XGBoost. Furthermore, tree-based models perform more robustly on medium-sized datasets51.In contrast, XGBoost provides feature importance scores52. This interpretability is crucial for building trust in the model and identifying potential intervention targets. Given its superior performance, robustness, and interpretability, the XGBoost model appears to be the most promising approach. While DL architectures show remarkable success in large-scale and unstructured data applications, their performance heavily depends on extensive computational resources and hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, XGBoost’s efficiency allowed it to achieve higher accuracy while maintaining explainability53–55.

Although machine learning algorithms such as XGBoost and model interpretation techniques such as SHAP are now well established in medical data science research, the contribution of the present study lies primarily in their application within large, well-characterized Middle Eastern cohorts that remain underrepresented in existing AI-driven cardiovascular literature. Rather than proposing a novel algorithmic framework, our work provides population-specific validation and comparative evaluation across two distinct regional cohorts, enabling assessment of model performance, generalizability, and differential contributions of risk factors. This population-level contextualization enhances the translational relevance of machine learning in cardiovascular medicine and supports more tailored risk-stratification strategies across diverse settings.

Clinical implication

The proposed model may be clinically translated as a complementary decision-support tool, not a replacement for traditional cardiovascular risk scores or physician judgment. Because it uses routinely available clinical and laboratory variables, it could be converted into software, an electronic health record module, or a web risk calculator. A key advantage of the study is SHAP-based explainability. At the population level, SHAP identifies the most influential predictors and their directions of effect. At the individual level, it explains each patient’s risk by showing how specific variables increase or decrease the predicted probability of cardiovascular events. This can support shared decision-making, individualized prevention, and prioritizing high-risk patients for blood pressure control, renal assessment, lipid/glucose management, lifestyle interventions, and medication adherence support. This direction aligns with the current literature on AI-based cardiovascular risk prediction, explainable clinical decision support, and transparent reporting of machine-learning prediction models.

Another significant strength of the study is its inclusion of two geographically and culturally distinct populations, which enhances the generalizability and robustness of the predictions. However, the observational nature of the data may affect causal interpretations. Future research should focus on integrating additional variables, such as genetic markers or socio-economic factors, to further refine risk assessments and enhance model accuracy. In addition, adding external validation from a separate population can make AI-driven studies more robust and ensure their generalizability.

Conclusion

Our study reinforces the need for a multi-faceted approach to CVD prevention, incorporating affordable and accessible interventions like Polypill and CKD screening, while also addressing broader risk factors through population-wide policies and integrated healthcare services. Polypill strategies offer a pragmatic solution to medication adherence challenges while demonstrating favorable cost-effectiveness profiles. Enhanced CKD screening, particularly through albuminuria testing, provides opportunities to identify cardiorenal risk before significant organ damage occurs. Strategic shifts in health priorities from child and maternal health to address premature cardiovascular mortality and morbidity in middle-aged populations acknowledge the changing disease burden in many countries while optimizing resource allocation. AI-driven risk prediction can further refine these strategies by identifying individuals at risk and tailoring interventions accordingly.
