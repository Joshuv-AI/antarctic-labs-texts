Construction and Applications of Knowledge Graphs in Ophthalmology
Authors: Siyani Chen, Haoyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge graph (KG) is an artificial intelligence technique that provides a structured representation of medical entities and their relationships, thereby facilitating integration of heterogeneous information, knowledge discovery, and intelligent reasoning. The process of KG construction includes knowledge acquisition, knowledge extraction, knowledge fusion, knowledge inference, KG visualization, and KG evaluation. In ophthalmology, KGs have demonstrated significant potential in advancing disease understanding, supporting clinical decision-making, assisting in ophthalmic image analysis, and facilitating clinical intelligent question-answering.SummaryThis paper reviews the methodologies for constructing medical KGs and highlights their applications in ophthalmology, with particular emphasis on the integration of ophthalmic KGs with medical imaging and large language models. Furthermore, it discusses existing challenges – ranging from privacy and regulatory constraints to high construction and maintenance costs, limited fusion of imaging and multimodal data, insufficient coverage of rare eye diseases and insufficient application in education and basic research – aiming to provide insights that promote deeper research and clinical translation of ophthalmology KGs.Key MessagesThe integration of KGs with multimodal technologies represents a research frontier in the transformation of ophthalmic clinical practice, enabling more structured and precise diagnostic reasoning. Future research should focus on the construction of domain-specific KGs for rare ocular diseases and spearhead the development of multimodal knowledge frameworks to fill existing knowledge gaps, thereby ultimately enhancing both patient clinical outcomes and medical education.

Introduction

Clinical practice and medical research generate vast amounts of data that serve as crucial sources of medical knowledge. However, these data are often fragmented across isolated silos, lacking effective integration and a unified conceptual framework [1]. In recent years, knowledge graphs (KGs), an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven technology, have provided an effective tool and new perspectives to address these challenges through their powerful capabilities in knowledge representation, integration, and reasoning. Essentially, a KG is a large-scale semantic network composed of “entity-relationship-entity” triples [2]. It is usually represented by a directed graph.

In the biomedical domain, KGs systematically integrate heterogeneous information – such as genes, phenotypes, diseases, and drugs – into structured semantic networks, enabling comprehensive representation of their relationships [3]. This approach facilitates the elimination of data silos and promotes the discovery of complex biological associations [4]. KGs have been applied in clinical text mining [5], clinical decision support [6], image analysis [7], medical education [8], new drug research and development [9], protein function research [10], and gene target mining [11]. In ophthalmology, recent studies have constructed domain-specific KGs and leveraged them for various downstream applications. This review systematically summarizes current methodologies for KG construction, highlights their applications in ophthalmology, and explores future directions for the development and clinical translation of ophthalmic KGs.

Method

Literature related to ophthalmology KG construction and application published from 2012 to 2025 was retrieved from Google Scholar and PubMed, and publications meeting the following criteria were included in further analysis: (1) compliant with the data retrieval strategy: “knowledge graph” AND ophthalmology AND (application OR construction) and (2) the publications should be review or research articles. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate literature, non-review or original research articles; (2) literature in the field of bibliometric analysis; and (3) literature not relevant to the research topic. Construction of KG

With the advancement of AI techniques, methods for KG construction have evolved from complete reliance on manual curation and expert-defined rules to semi-automated and fully automated approaches that integrate natural language processing (NLP), machine learning, and deep learning. This evolution has not only improved the efficiency of knowledge extraction and integration but also markedly enhanced the scalability and practical value of medical KGs for large-scale biomedical data processing [12, 13]. Benchmark Datasets and Knowledge Acquisition

Medical KGs require domain-specific medical expertise derived from both structured data with standardized formats – such as the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) [14] and the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) [15] – and unstructured data with heterogeneous formats to enable effective applications. In ophthalmology, such expertise can be obtained from clinical practice (e.g., electronic health records [16], image reports [17], and surgical videos [18]), biomedical literature databases (e.g., PubMed and Web of Science) [19], and other comprehensive medical knowledge sources, including clinical guidelines, textbooks [20], and online resources [17]. Beyond extracting knowledge from primary sources, existing medical KGs – such as the Biomedical Informatics Ontology System (BIOS) – can also serve as direct knowledge resources [21].

Knowledge Extraction

NLP technology plays a central role in extracting knowledge from unstructured text [22]. Entity recognition, relationship extraction and attribute extraction are basic tasks in knowledge extraction. Named entity recognition is designed to automatically identify entities and classify their attributes, such as diseases, symptoms, genes, and drugs [23]. Relationship extraction focuses on detecting and categorizing the interactions among entities [24]. Representative tools and frameworks for these tasks include SpaCy [25], Scispacy [26], and Brat, which also facilitate accurate entity alignment and cross-dataset mapping [20]. Recently, large language models (LLMs) have also been applied to knowledge extraction with the advantages of multimodal language understanding and few-shot learning ability [27, 28].

Knowledge Fusion

Knowledge fusion refers to the process of integrating heterogeneous data from multiple sources into a unified entity representation, thereby mitigating semantic inconsistencies and redundancies arising from disparate data sources [6]. For instance, combining standardized biomedical vocabularies – such as UMLS and ICD-10 – with pretrained language models, such as BERT, facilitates more accurate entity alignment and cross-dataset mapping. This approach enhances the precision of entity recognition and relationship extraction [29], ensuring the reliability, effectiveness and accuracy of downstream applications [30].

Knowledge Inference

To enable computers to effectively process and understand KGs, it is essential to transform the entities and relationships within the graph into low-dimensional, dense vector representations. KG embedding preserves the semantic information among entities and relationships in a distributed vector space, thereby facilitating similarity computations and downstream analytical tasks [31]. A variety of models have been proposed for KG embedding [32], including SciBERT [33], Metapath2ve [34], RotatE [35], and TransE [36]. Once the KG has been embedded, graph neural networks can be applied to capture the underlying semantics of triples for semantic matching, and link prediction methods based on graph embeddings can be employed to infer new knowledge [37].

KG Visualization

KG visualization enables the graphical representation of entities and their relationships, facilitating knowledge application and the discovery of latent connections. Effective visualization not only improves the interpretability of large and complex biomedical datasets but also serves as a bridge between raw data and actionable medical insights. A variety of visualization tools are currently employed, including Neo4j, GraphXR [38], Matplotlib [33], and RStudio [19]. By importing medical data into these platforms, intuitive relationship mapping and interactive analysis can be achieved.

KG Evaluation

KG construction quality directly dictates the performance of downstream applications. Therefore, a robust, multi-dimensional evaluation framework is essential. This assessment typically spans two dimensions: intrinsic metrics for KG quality control, focusing on accuracy, completeness, and logical consistency, and extrinsic metrics for task-specific gains, focusing on practical value in real-world applications [39]. Intrinsic Metrics

Structural evaluation of KG focuses on its topological characteristics. Metrics such as average degree, graph density, clustering coefficient, and average shortest path length are used to quantify connectivity, compactness, and the efficiency of information propagation, thereby reflecting the structural consistency and robustness of the KG [33].

Functional evaluation, particularly in triple completion and relation prediction tasks, is commonly performed using a ranking-based framework. Precision assesses the correctness of predicted triples, while ranking metrics – including ranking score [16] and mean reciprocal rank – evaluate the prioritization of ground-truth knowledge [40]. Lower ranking score values, together with higher mean reciprocal rank, indicate superior ranking performance.

Extrinsic Metrics

Model performance in disease identification can be assessed using threshold-based metrics. Precision and recall evaluate diagnostic accuracy and completeness [41]. Mean precision at k (MP@k) quantifies the relevance of top-ranked diagnostic outputs [34]. To address class imbalance in medical datasets, the F1 score [42] and macro-average recall are employed to ensure balanced performance across both common and rare ophthalmic diseases [17]. For complex cases involving multiple comorbidities, the exact match rate provides a stringent measure of agreement between predicted and true disease sets [17].

In clinical practice, model response time and interpretability are critical. The black-box nature of highly complex models often makes their predictions difficult to understand [43]. Greater interpretability enables users to more easily comprehend and trust model outputs, which can assist clinicians in making rational, data-driven decisions and ultimately improving the quality of care [43]. Interpretability can be assessed using the 5-point Likert scale to evaluate the clarity and traceability of the model’s outputs [44].

The quality of generated ophthalmic reports can be evaluated using standard NLG metrics. BLEU measures n-gram overlap to assess lexical accuracy [45], while METEOR incorporates synonyms and morphological variants to capture semantic similarity more effectively [46]. ROUGE evaluates recall and fluency based on the longest common subsequence [47]. CIDEr further assesses consensus-based relevance between generated and reference reports [48]. Collectively, these metrics evaluate the accuracy, coherence, and clinical usability of generated narratives.

Application of Solo KG

In disease prediction and risk assessment, Li et al. [16] developed a KG-based statistical reasoning model for predicting diabetic macular edema. The KG encoded structured relationships between diabetic macular edema and its associated risk factors. To improve predictive performance, a correlation enhancement coefficient and a generalized closeness degree method were introduced to optimize risk factor weighting and multi-factor reasoning. Compared with the statistical rule-based baseline, this model improved both AUC and precision from 19.61% to 86.21%, demonstrating that KG can encode rich structured clinical knowledge and effectively support disease risk prediction. Similarly, Wang et al. [19] constructed a KG of diabetes mellitus complications using evidence-based medical data sourced from databases such as PubMed and Web of Science. In this model, relationships were represented as triplets in the form of “Risk Factor-OR/RR-Risk Outcome,” where a shorter path between a risk factor and diabetic retinopathy indicated a higher level of risk. By invoking these relationships through a logical model, the system calculates the risk probabilities of complications such as diabetic retinopathy.

In disease diagnosis, Xiao et al. [49] and Cui et al. [50] applied NLP technology to, respectively, extract knowledge related to diabetic retinopathy and dry eye disease and develop dedicated KGs. These KGs provide clinicians with an intuitive and comprehensive reference tool, enabling rapid identification of similar cases and improving the efficiency of diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making.

In disease care, Yerragunta et al. [33] extracted the “patient-symptom” and “symptom-treatment” relationships from the clinical records of glaucoma to construct a disease-specific KG. The average degree ranged from 0.0790 to 0.0836, indicating that the extracted relationships were reliable. This framework provides a useful tool for supporting targeted and personalized glaucoma treatment analysis.

Integration of KG and Ophthalmic Images

In imaging-intensive disciplines such as ophthalmology, conventional deep learning models – though effective in feature extraction – often fail to capture the intrinsic relationships among clinical entities [58]. Integrating KG can address this limitation by embedding prior medical knowledge into analytical frameworks, thereby enabling models to perform more clinically meaningful reasoning.

Recent studies have explored managing ophthalmic imaging data as entity nodes or attributes within KGs. For example, Gao et al. [34] proposed the Contrastive Graph Attention Network-based Auxiliary Diagnostic Model (CGAT-ADM), which integrates patients’ comprehensive health information from Beijing Tongren Hospital – including medical history, ophthalmic images and reports, and visual function – into a multimodal KG framework to simulate clinical reasoning and support decision-making. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets using a 7:1:2 ratio. Compared with the baseline model, CGAT-ADM achieved improvements of 13.19%, 13.25%, 13.73%, and 12.08%, respectively, under the test conditions of MP@1, 5, 10, and 20. This approach enhances the usability of imaging data for knowledge retrieval and clinical semantic association, providing a foundation for subsequent fusion of KG with visual features and facilitating ophthalmic record management.

At a deeper integration level, Li et al. [25] (for FFA images) and Wang et al. [51] (for ophthalmic B-ultrasound) both introduced clinical KGs to enhance the expression of visual features. This knowledge-driven transformer architecture not only assisted in the localization of small lesions but also optimized the long-tail deviation of high- and low-frequency terms. Their respective proposed models (Cross-modal clinical Graph Transformer [CGT] [25] and Knowledge-Enhanced Cross-modal Alignment Network [KECAN]) [51] demonstrated clinical diagnostic accuracy surpassing the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods in BLEU and CIDEr.

Furthermore, Wang et al. [52] applied the modal alignment technology for relationship perception to combine the features generated by the KG with visual features, and also combined it with large language models to develop EyeGraphGPT, which is used for automatically generating various ophthalmic reports. The results show that EyeGraphGPT outperforms the SOTA in terms of BLEU, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr. Moreover, after removing the graph relationship features, the main evaluation metric CIDEr score decreased from 0.762 to 0.729.

Integration of Ophthalmology KGs and LLMs

In recent years, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable potential in medical question-answering (QA). However, their inherent tendency toward hallucination has limited the reliability and interpretability of their responses. As structured representations of domain knowledge, KGs can provide explicit entity and relationship support, thereby enhancing the accuracy, robustness, and clinical relevance of model output [59, 60]. Meanwhile, LLMs can support KGs by generating coherent, readable text from entity and relationship information, thereby enabling the construction of effective QA systems [61].

In ophthalmology, KG-LLM integration has been applied to clinical decision support. Wang et al. [53] incorporated KG and contrastive learning into MeEYE, an ophthalmology-specific LLM, increasing recall rate from 73.1–74.8% to 88.2–90.1% and diagnostic accuracy from 70.2–74.0% to 86.1–88.6%. In addition, Wang constructed a KG for corneal diseases and surgeries based on corneal examinations and global expert guidelines. It achieved good sensitivity (92 ± 4%), specificity (94 ± 5%), accuracy (93 ± 4%), and AUC (0.95 ± 0.03) in the early detection of keratoconus. In terms of refractive surgery assessment, the F1 score was 0.90 ± 0.04. Moreover, the interpretability assessment using a 5-point Likert scale yielded extremely high scores (≥4.7/5) [44]. Wang also constructed the Oph-KG through multimodal imaging and structured cases from Zhuhai People’s Hospital, and utilized DeepSeek-R1 to generate diagnostic content. The sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score of this model were all above 0.9, and 92% of the model’s output results accurately referred to key imaging biomarkers or clinical factors [54].

In addition to the diagnosis of text and images, KG can also be applied to ophthalmic surgery videos. Wang et al. [18] developed EyePCR-MLLM, based on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and an ophthalmic KG comprising over 25,000 triples, which achieved 74.12% accuracy in ophthalmic surgical perception – 8.45% higher than ophthalmic interns and surpassing other multimodal LLMs such as GPT-4.1 (70.69%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (71.38%).

Beyond clinical decision support, several studies have explored the integration of KG with LLMs to improve intelligent QA performance. Gao et al. [17] and Wang et al. [21] achieved zero-shot QA for ophthalmic diseases by combining ophthalmology KG with LLM. OphGLM outperformed SOTA on the Comprehensive Medical Benchmark in Chinese (CMB-Exam) across nursing, pharmacy, and technician categories. Notably, it achieved 0.90 and 0.88 accuracy in single- and multi-disease classification, exceeding the baseline by 0.69 and 0.73, respectively [17]. The accuracy rate of the answers generated by the Knowledge Triple Augmented language model Prompting (KTAP) model (73.80%) proposed by Wang et al. was on average 16% higher than that of the baseline model (63.68%) [21]. Lecu et al. [55] leveraged LLMs to construct an AMD-KG and implemented a Retrieval-Augmented Generation framework to significantly enhance the factual grounding of medical AI, enabling a small-scale model (SmolLM2-1.7B) to achieve 95.6% parity with large-scale models in terms of accuracy, rising from an initial 78.2%. In order to diversify the interaction modalities, Guo et al. [20] further incorporated a digital human interaction modality, which improved user satisfaction by 23% compared with traditional systems.

In disease-specific applications, Sun et al. [56] developed a dedicated QA system for myopia prevention, extending the use of such systems to public health education. However, most existing studies focus on medical professionals. To assist laypersons in elderly eye diseases care, Zhang et al. [57] leveraged a hybrid framework integrating KG with multimodal large models. Their approach outperformed SOTA models in reasoning depth and accuracy on the ncbi/Hidden-flaws-GPT-4V benchmark, achieving accuracy improvements of 3.37% and 0.94% over MM-CoT-Base and DDCo QA models. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that integrating ophthalmology KG with LLMs substantially improves the reliability and interpretability of LLMs in clinical tasks, while also expanding their applicability to domains such as health education [56] and surgical decision-making [18].

Limitation

KG, as an advanced AI technology, offers powerful capabilities for integrating heterogeneous data, and it is widely applied in ophthalmology. Despite these achievements, several limitations hinder its broader application.

First, the practical implementation of KGs faces challenges related to data privacy, population bias, and regulatory compliance. Clinical data used for KG construction often contain sensitive patient information, requiring secure data processing to mitigate privacy risks [62]. In addition, KGs derived from geographically or demographically specific datasets may introduce population biases, potentially limiting the generalizability across diverse groups and necessitating careful clinical interpretation. As AI-based systems, KG-driven models must also meet regulatory requirements, with particular emphasis on transparency and interpretability to ensure the safety, reliability, and clinical acceptance [63].

Second, constructing and maintaining ophthalmology KG remains resource-intensive. Although various LLMs and NLP models have been developed for medical KG [64], general-purpose models often fail to capture ophthalmology-specific knowledge and may be prone to hallucinations. Developing specialized NLP models for ophthalmology, particularly for rare eye diseases (REDs), remains challenging. In addition, most KGs are static and lack dynamic updating mechanisms, limiting their adaptability to rapidly expanding data [65]. Developing optimal extraction frameworks and implementing automated, continuous KG updates represent important directions for future research.

Third, integration of ophthalmology KG with multimodal imaging data remains limited. Imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography and fundus photography are critical for diagnosis [66–68]. While ophthalmology KG can enhance intelligent image analysis models and assist report generation, their application for direct ophthalmology disease diagnosis remains in its infancy. Future ophthalmology KGs should integrate multimodal data – including imaging and clinical data – to establish comprehensive and dynamic knowledge systems. By structuring imaging data and combining it with KG reasoning and analytical capabilities, diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes for ophthalmic diseases could be substantially enhanced.

Fourth, coverage of REDs remains insufficient. REDs are major causes of vision loss in children and young adults [69], but their rarity results in limited clinical data and poorly understood pathophysiology [70, 71]. Existing KGs focus predominantly on common eye diseases, leaving REDs underrepresented. Constructing specialized KGs for REDs by integrating heterogeneous data sources could bridge the current knowledge gap, improve understanding of disease mechanisms, and support more precise diagnosis and management strategies.

Fifth, the application of ophthalmology KGs in medical education remains limited. Existing studies indicate that KGs can support the development of medical teaching case bases and virtual standardized patients, thereby enhancing students’ comprehension of medical knowledge and improving procedural skill acquisition [72, 73]. However, most ophthalmology KGs emphasize clinical use and offer insufficient educational utility. Future research should prioritize integrating multimodal data to build interactive platforms that enable case-based learning, foster clinical reasoning, and enhance instructional effectiveness.

Finally, the use of ophthalmology KGs in basic research is still limited. Evidence suggests that integrating multi-omics information – such as pathways, phenotypes, protein perturbations [74] and gene expression [75] – into a unified structured framework can facilitate more systematic investigation of disease mechanisms. Although KGs in other biomedical fields have supported basic research tasks, including drug discovery [9], protein function research [10] and target identification [11], ophthalmology KGs remain predominantly clinically oriented. Future work should focus on constructing multi-omics-integrated ophthalmology KGs to advance target discovery, disease stratification, and translational research.

Conclusion

KG enables efficient knowledge extraction and integration, supporting ophthalmic applications such as image analysis, clinical decision-making and medical QA. However, the practical application of KGs must address patient privacy, inherent population biases, and stringent regulatory compliance. Moreover, the high cost of developing domain-specific NLP models and LLMs limits large-scale automation. In addition, ophthalmology KGs currently lack deep integration of imaging data, have limited applications in medical education and basic research, and receive insufficient attention to REDs. Future research should optimize ophthalmology KGs by improving NLP and LLM frameworks, enhancing multimodal integration, expanding applications across multiple domains, and extending coverage to REDs to advance precision ophthalmology.
