Energy-controlled supercapacitors based on polybenzidine-oxidant-GO hybrid electrodes with vanadate and dichromate redox chemistry
Authors: Aisan Moshgabadi, Reza Dadashi, Khalil Farhadi, Morteza Bahram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, polybenzidine (PB) was successfully synthesized in situ on anodized graphite substrates (AGS) using dichromate (PB-C/AGS) and vanadate (PB-V/AGS) oxidants, and their electrochemical performance was systematically evaluated for supercapacitor applications. Structural analyses by ATR-IR, Raman, FE-SEM, EDX, and XRD confirmed the successful polymerization and incorporation of metal oxide species. By employing two oxidants with distinct redox potentials, the polymerization pathway, metal-polymer interaction, and resulting hybrid architecture were deliberately tuned, enabling a direct investigation of oxidant-controlled structure-property relationships. Electrochemical characterization in a three-electrode configuration using 1 M H2SO4 as the electrolyte indicated that PB-V/AGS electrodes delivered a higher specific capacitance of 1080 mF cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2, compared to 920 mF cm−2 for PB-C/AGS. In a symmetric device configuration using PVA/H2SO4 gel-polymer as both the separator and solid electrolyte, PB-V/AGS achieved a maximum capacitance of 50 F g− 1 at 0.5 mA g− 1, energy density of 12.63 mWh g− 1, and power density of 168.5 mW g− 1, surpassing PB-C/AGS with 32.098 F g− 1 at 0.5 mA g− 1 and 8.125 mWh g− 1 and 225 mW g− 1. Both devices exhibited good cycling stability, retaining about 88% of capacitance after 5000 GCD cycles. These results demonstrate that vanadate and dichromate-assisted polybenzidine growth on anodized graphite offers a promising route for in-situ fabrication of high-performance polymer-metal oxide hybrid supercapacitors.Keywords: Oxidant-controlled supercapacitor, Dicromat, Vanadat, Polybenzidine, Anodized graphiteSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The growing demand for efficient, sustainable, and high-performance energy storage devices has directed extensive research toward the development of advanced materials for supercapacitors1,2. Supercapacitors, also known as electrochemical capacitors, are distinguished by their rapid charge-discharge capabilities, high power density, long cycle life, and operational safety3,4. They bridge the gap between conventional capacitors and batteries, making them suitable for applications ranging from portable electronics to hybrid electric vehicles, renewable energy systems, and it is used in various industries such as military, medical, and manufacturing of electronic devices, etc.5–7. Supercapacitors are broadly divided into two categories based on how they store charge: electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) and pseudocapacitors8,9. EDLCs store energy through the physical adsorption of ions at the interface between the electrode and electrolyte, offering benefits like high power output and long operational life. Carbon-based materials such as activated carbon, graphene, carbon nanotubes, Graphite substrate, and carbon fabric are commonly used in EDLCs due to their excellent electrical conductivity and the potential to enhance surface area for improved ion interaction10–12. In contrast, pseudocapacitors rely on rapid, reversible redox reactions (faradaic processes) occurring at or near the electrode surface13. Materials like metal oxides, conducting polymers, and transition metal carbides are typically used in this class due to their capacity to deliver higher energy densities14,15. Each type of capacitor material has its strengths and limitations: EDLCs typically have long cycle life but lower capacitance, while pseudocapacitive materials offer greater capacitance but reduced cycling stability. To overcome these drawbacks, researchers increasingly design hybrid systems that integrate both material types, combining their complementary advantages to develop more efficient and balanced supercapacitor devices16,17. Among the various materials explored for supercapacitor electrodes, conducting polymers have emerged as promising candidates owing to their inherent redox activity, tunable structure, and favorable electrical conductivity18,19. Benzidine, a symmetrical diamine with an extended conjugated structure, represents a potentially valuable monomer for redox-active polymer synthesis20. Its aromatic nature and amine functionalities allow for facile oxidative polymerization, leading to the formation of π-conjugated polymeric networks with electrochemical activity21,22. While benzidine has been historically studied in the context of dyes and organic semiconductors23,24, its polymeric forms remain relatively underexplored in electrochemical energy storage. When polymerized under suitable oxidative conditions, benzidine can form redox-active species capable of reversible electron transfer, which is essential for pseudocapacitive behavior in supercapacitor electrodes.

In situ polymerization of monomers directly onto electrode substrates offers several advantages over traditional ex situ approaches, including enhanced electrical contact between the polymer and current collector, improved film adhesion, and controlled morphology25,26. In this study, anodized graphite is selected as the substrate for polymer growth due to its high electrical conductivity, chemical stability, and increased surface area achieved through anodization, which facilitates better polymer loading and ion accessibility. The choice of oxidizing agents plays a crucial role in determining the chemical structure, redox behavior, and electrochemical performance of the resulting polymer. Dichromate (Cr2O72−) and vanadate (VO3− or V2O5 derivatives) are two transition metal-based oxidants known for their strong oxidizing ability and potential to mediate controlled oxidative coupling reactions27,28. Dichromate is a potent oxidizer with a high redox potential, often used in oxidative polymerization of aromatic amines, while vanadate species provide a milder and more tunable redox environment28,29. By employing these two distinct oxidants, this work aims to elucidate their influence on the polymerization process, chemical structure of the resulting benzidine polymers, and their corresponding electrochemical properties for use in supercapacitor industry.

In this study, we introduce an oxidant‑engineered strategy for the in‑situ growth of polybenzidine–metal hybrid films on anodized graphite, where the redox chemistry of the oxidant is deliberately used as a design parameter to control polymerization behavior, metal‑species incorporation, and electrode morphology. By directly comparing vanadate‑ and dichromate‑mediated polymerization under identical conditions, we demonstrate that vanadate produces a more porous and fibrous PB‑V/AGS architecture with superior ion accessibility and redox utilization, leading to markedly enhanced areal capacitance and energy density. Unlike conventional polymer–metal oxide systems, the incorporated V and Cr species remain highly dispersed and predominantly amorphous within the polymer matrix, revealing a distinct hybridization pathway governed by oxidant chemistry. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on benzidine polymerization driven by vanadate and dichromate oxidants, enabling the formation of PB‑based hybrid electrodes with significantly improved charge‑storage performance.

Experimental

Materials and characterization techniques

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Benzidine, ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3), potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), sulfuric acid (98%), hydrochloric acid (37%), and potassium sulfate were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. High-purity (99%) graphite sheets with a thickness of 0.5 mm were purchased from Alpha Aesar and used as the electrode substrate. Structural and surface characterization of the synthesized electrodes was performed using a variety of analytical tools. All techniques were employed to investigate the elemental composition and chemical states to confirm the formation of polybenzidine-metal oxides (PB-M) composites on the anodized graphite substrate (AGS). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode using a Thermo Nicolet (USA) spectrometer to analyze the functional groups present on the polymer surface. Raman spectroscopy was conducted with a Thermo Nicolet Almega Dispersive Raman system to probe the molecular vibrations and assess polymer structure. The morphology and elemental composition of the fabricated materials were examined via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) using ZEISS Sigma VP and MIRA2 TESCAN instruments, coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) for elemental analysis. Crystallographic and phase information was obtained using X-ray diffraction (XRD) in grazing incidence mode (X’Pert PRO MPD, Cu Kα radiation) over a 2θ range of 10–80°. Electrochemical performance evaluations were conducted using an Autolab PGSTAT30 and a SAMA500 potentiostat/galvanostat in a three-electrode setup. The synthesized AGS-based electrode served as the working electrode, while Ag/AgCl and a platinum sheet were employed as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively.

Preparation of anodized graphite substrate

The graphite sheet served a dual purpose in this study: as a conductive current collector and as an active electrode material in the electric double-layer capacitor (EDLC) system, as well as a growth platform for the in-situ formation of polybenzidine-metal oxide (PB-M) composites. To enhance its surface reactivity and facilitate polymer deposition, the graphite substrate underwent an anodization treatment30–32. Initially, the graphite sheet was cut into strips with dimensions of 4 × 1 cm. Anodization was carried out using a two-electrode electrochemical setup, in which the graphite acted as the working electrode (anode) and a platinum sheet served as the counter electrode (cathode). The interelectrode distance was maintained at approximately 1.5 cm to ensure uniform current distribution. The electrodes were immersed in an aqueous 0.1 M K2SO4 solution, which functioned as the electrolyte. A constant DC voltage of 10 V (corresponding to ~ 0.5 A current) was applied using a regulated power supply, with each face of the graphite substrate anodized for 2 min. This anodization step promoted surface oxidation, leading to increased roughness and the formation of functional groups, thereby improving the substrate’s wettability and compatibility for subsequent chemical grafting. Following the anodization, the electrodes were thoroughly rinsed and sonicated in deionized water for 60 s to remove any loosely bound surface residues or reaction byproducts. The cleaned anodized graphite substrates were then air-dried at room temperature, and after drying, it was cut into 0.5 × 4 cm for use in subsequent polymerization reactions.

Fabrication of PB-M/AGS electrode

To fabricate the PB-M/AGS electrode and systematically evaluate their supercapacitor performance, the oxidative polymerization of benzidine was carried out using two distinct metal-based oxidants of vanadate and dichromate ions. This approach enabled comparative analysis of the influence of different oxidizing environments on the polymerization process and resulting electrochemical properties. The resulting electrodes were designated as follows: PB-V/AGS (polybenzidine synthesized in the presence of vanadate), and PB-C/AGS (polybenzidine synthesized in the presence of dichromate), each supported on an anodized graphite substrate. The fundamental difference between the two systems arises from the distinct redox potentials and coordination behaviors of vanadate and dichromate ions. Dichromate, with a stronger oxidizing character, promotes rapid oxidative coupling and dense nucleation of polymer chains, whereas vanadate enables slower redox kinetics and transient coordination with amine sites, favoring extended chain growth and porous network formation.

To ensure selective deposition of the active material, all regions of the AGS electrode except for a central 0.5 × 0.5 cm area intended for polymer growth were insulated using Teflon tape. The fabrication of the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrode was initiated by immersing the exposed portion of the AGS electrode into 40 mL of 1.0 M HCl containing 0.2 g of NH4VO3 (for PB-V/AGS) or 0.2 g of K2Cr2O7 (for PB-C/AGS), serving as the oxidant. This solution was stirred for 20 min at ice-water bath (0–4 °C) to allow complete dissolution and preconditioning of the oxidizing environment. Separately, 0.05 g of benzidine monomer was dissolved in 10 mL of 0.5 M HCl, and this solution was gradually introduced dropwise into the metal oxidant-containing medium over a period of 20 min while maintaining continuous stirring. The polymerization reaction was allowed to proceed under constant stirring for 4–5 h at 0–4 °C. Upon addition of benzidine, the reaction mixture exhibited a noticeable color transition from colorless to yellowish-brown, indicating the onset of oxidative polymerization and polybenzidine formation on the electrode surface. After completion of the polymerization, the modified electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove unreacted species and byproducts, followed by drying at 60 °C to obtain the final PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes.

To accurately determine the amount of active material contributing to charge storage, the mass loading of the deposited PB–M composites was experimentally measured. For this purpose, several PB‑V/AGS and PB‑C/AGS electrodes were fabricated, and the mass of the deposited layer was obtained from the difference between the electrode weight before and after the polymerization process. The mass loading of the PB‑V and PB‑C composites on a 0.5 × 0.5 cm area of the AGS was approximately 2.0 and 1.5 mg, respectively, with a reproducibility error of about ± 1%.

Fabrication of the symmetric supercapacitor device using PB-M/AGS electrodes

The assembly of the symmetric supercapacitor device was accomplished by sandwiching a poly(vinyl alcohol)-sulfuric acid (PVA-H2SO4) gel electrolyte between two identical PB-M/AGS (polybenzidine-metal oxide on anodized graphite substrate) electrodes. This gel electrolyte functions as both an ionic conductor and a mechanical separator, facilitating ion transport while maintaining electrode separation and flexibility. To prepare the gel electrolyte, 0.5 g of PVA was dissolved in 6 mL of 3.5 M H2SO4 under constant magnetic stirring at 80 °C for approximately 2 h, until a homogeneous and transparent gel was formed. The process was adapted based on established procedures from the literature33,34.

Once prepared, a thin layer of the PVA-H2SO4 gel was evenly applied between the two PB-M/AGS electrodes. The electrodes were then aligned face-to-face and gently pressed to ensure good interfacial contact and electrolyte distribution, forming a compact, binder-free symmetric device (It is noted that the solid-state supercapacitors were assembled in a symmetric configuration using two identical PB-M/AGS electrodes with matched geometric area and equivalent active material deposition, thereby ensuring charge balance during device operation). The electrochemical performance of the assembled device was systematically evaluated using standard techniques, including Cyclic voltammetry (CV), Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), and Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).

Electrochemical characterization

The electrochemical performance of the PB-M/AGS electrode and the assembled symmetric supercapacitor device was evaluated to analyze charge storage mechanisms, electrical conductivity, and overall capacitive behavior using a combination of cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The CV and GCD measurements were conducted using a SAMA500 potentiostat/galvanostat, while EIS was performed using an Autolab PGSTAT30.

For individual electrode analysis, a three-electrode configuration was employed, wherein the PB-M/AGS composite acted as the working electrode, a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode served as the reference electrode, and a platinum sheet functioned as the counter electrode. All measurements were conducted in 1 M H2SO4 aqueous solution as the electrolyte.

CV was carried out in a potential window ranging from − 0.2 to 1.2 V to probe the redox activity and capacitive behavior of the electrodes. In the three-electrode system, the range of − 0.2 to 1.2 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) provided reversible redox activity without signs of electrolyte decomposition or polymer over-oxidation. For the symmetric solid-state device, the operating voltage was restricted to ~ 1.4 V, which maintained stable capacitive behavior of the PVA/H2SO4 gel electrolyte while preventing polarization and degradation at higher voltages.

Results and discussion

ATR‑IR and Raman spectroscopy studies

ATR-IR and Raman spectroscopic analyses were initially employed to verify the successful formation of PB-M nanocomposites on the surface of the AGS electrode. Figure 1 displays the ATR-IR spectra obtained for a series of samples, including bare graphite substrate (BGS), AGS, PB/AGS, PB-V/AGS, and PB-C/AGS electrodes. The ATR-IR spectra for the BGS and AGS electrodes are those of our previous work, which is also discussed here21,39,40.

ATR-IR spectra of BGS, AGS, PB-V/AGS, and PB-C/AGS electrodes.

In the case of the AGS sample, a noticeable absorption band near 1700 cm−¹ is attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl (–OH) groups formed primarily at the basal plane defects and edges of the graphite surface following the anodization treatment. This modification introduces oxygen-containing moieties, enhancing surface polarity. Furthermore, distinct peaks observed at approximately 1070 cm−¹ and 1428 cm−¹ correspond to C–O (likely epoxide or alkoxy) and C = O (carbonyl) stretching modes, respectively21,32. These bands indicate oxidative transformation of surface hydroxyl functionalities, likely originating from residual oxygenated species or partially exfoliated graphene oxide domains on the ACGS surface. The ATR-IR spectrum both of the PB-M/AGS electrodes reveals distinct vibrational features characteristic of polybenzidine. A broad absorption band centered around 3480 cm−¹ is assigned to the N–H stretching vibration, indicating the presence of amine functionalities along the polymer structure34. Additionally, the band observed near 782 cm−¹ corresponds to the out-of-plane bending mode of N–H groups, further confirming the incorporation of nitrogen-containing moieties41. The spectral region around 1392 cm−¹ exhibits a prominent peak attributed to the C–N stretching vibration, which is indicative of aryl–amine linkages42. Furthermore, two significant absorption bands are present at 1502 cm−¹ and 1604 cm−¹, which are associated with C = C stretching vibrations in the quinoid and benzenoid segments of the polymer, respectively34,43. These features collectively suggest the formation of a conjugated aromatic structure comprising alternating quinoid and benzene units within the polybenzidine matrix. Notably, in the ATR-IR spectra of PB-V/AGS electrode, an additional band emerged around 665 cm−¹, which is attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of V–O–V linkages44,45. This feature is characteristic of vanadate species, specifically indicating the presence of a bridging oxygen between two vanadium atoms, consistent with a distorted VO3 coordination environment. The presence of this band further supports the incorporation of vanadium-based components within the composite structure. In the ATR-IR spectrum of the PB-C/AGS electrode, a distinct absorption band was observed near 848 cm−¹, which is attributed to the Cr–O stretching vibration46. This peak is indicative of Cr–O bonding, suggesting the presence of chromium oxide species likely originating from residual dichromate ions incorporated during the oxidative polymerization process. The appearance of this band confirms the successful integration or surface association of Cr-based moieties within the PB matrix on the AGS substrate.

To further validate the formation of PB on the surface of the AGS electrodes, Raman spectroscopy was conducted. Figure 2 presents the Raman spectra of the PB-V/AGS, and PB-C/AGS systems. In both samples, a prominent band in the 1580–1590 cm−¹ range corresponds to the G-band, which arises from the E2g vibrational mode of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms20,47. This mode is associated with the in-plane stretching of carbon–carbon bonds in graphitic domains, reflecting the structural integrity of the underlying graphitic framework. Additionally, each spectrum features a broad band between 2600 and 2800 cm−¹, attributed to the 2D (or G′) band, which originates from a second-order two-phonon scattering process16,47. The presence of this band further confirms the layered carbon structure and provides insight into the stacking behavior and degree of disorder.

Raman spectra of PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes.

Specific Raman bands observed in the PB-M/AGS electrodes at 1188 cm−¹, 1468 cm−¹, and 1609 cm−¹ can be assigned to the stretching vibrations of C–C, C–N, and N = N bonds, respectively, characteristic of the PB backbone34,48. Additional peaks at 1410, 1426, 1541, and 1341 cm−¹ are also present, corresponding to vibrational modes inherent to the polymer structure48,49. These spectral features are consistent with those reported for PB in previous studies, confirming the successful oxidative polymerization of the monomer on the AGS substrate. Notably, the Raman spectra of the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS samples exhibit similar vibrational signatures with slight shifts in peak positions. These shifts likely reflect variations in the local chemical environment or polymer chain conformation due to the differing oxidation mechanisms employed by vanadate and dichromate. Nonetheless, the retention of PB-specific bands in both cases confirms the successful deposition of the polymer across all modified electrodes. Additionally, low-intensity Raman bands were observed in the 500–1000 cm−¹ spectral region for both the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes50,51. These bands are attributed to metal–oxygen vibrational modes, suggesting the incorporation of vanadium and chromium species into the polymer matrix during the oxidative polymerization of benzidine. The presence of these metal-related features supports the interaction of the oxidants (vanadate and dichromate) with the polymer backbone and indicates their partial retention or coordination within the electrode structure.

Investigation of the surface morphology, chemical composition, and structural properties of the fabricated electrodes

To investigate the structural and compositional characterizations of the prepared electrodes, FESEM, EDX, and XRD analyses were performed, which clearly confirmed the progressive modifications introduced from the blank graphite sheet (BGS) to the anodized graphite sheet (AGS) and finally to the polybenzidine-coated electrodes (PB-C/AGS and PB-V/AGS). The SEM images of the BGS and AGS electrodes are the SEM images taken from these electrodes in our previous works, which are also discussed here21,39,40,52,53. According to Fig. 3, the FE-SEM image of BGS (Fig. 3a) reveals a relatively smooth and featureless surface, consistent with the pristine nature of the substrate. The inset of Fig. 3a provides a higher‑magnification view of the BGS, confirming the absence of significant porosity or surface roughness. Upon anodization (Fig. 3b), the surface becomes significantly roughened with the appearance of pores and irregularities, thereby providing a higher surface area and more active sites for subsequent polymer deposition. In the FE-SEM images of Fig. 3c-f, a pronounced oxidant-dependent morphological evolution is observed. The PB-C/AGS electrode (Fig. 3c, d ) synthesized using dichromate exhibits a compact and densely packed granular structure, which is likely associated with rapid oxidative polymerization and limited chain rearrangement. In contrast, the PB-V/AGS electrode (Fig. 3e, f) prepared with vanadate displays a highly porous and fibrous interconnected network. This morphology can be attributed to the milder redox potential and slower polymerization kinetics of vanadate ions, which promote extended polymer chain growth and the formation of open conductive pathways. The porous/fibrous architecture of PB-V/AGS significantly increases the electrochemically active surface area and facilitates electrolyte ion diffusion, thereby explaining its superior areal capacitance and energy density compared to PB-C/AGS. Figure 4 shows the EDX and XRD patterns of PB-C/AGS and PB-V/AGS electrodes. EDX spectra confirm the successful incorporation of PB, showing strong C and N signals along with O originating from anodization and oxidative processes. Importantly, Cr signals are observed in PB-C/AGS (Fig. 4a) and V signals in PB-V/AGS (Fig. 4b), verifying the role of the respective oxidants in the polymerization process and their partial retention within the electrode matrix. XRD patterns of both PB-C (Fig. 4c) and PB-V (Fig. 4d) nanocomposite are characterized by broad peaks at 2θ = 20–30°, typical of amorphous conducting polymers54–56, although subtle differences in peak intensity suggest variations in chain ordering and dopant interactions depending on the oxidant used. Due to the very low loading mass of electroactive PB-V and PB-C deposited onto the anodized graphite substrate (AGS) in comparison with the dominant volume fraction of the conductive graphite support, the characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the polymer-metal phases remained unresolved, even when using grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD). To overcome this limitation and reliably identify the crystalline structure of the synthesized composites, XRD analysis was performed on the bulk PB-M powders obtained from the beaker-scale synthesis after thorough washing and drying. It should be noted that no distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline vanadium or chromium oxide phases are observed in the XRD patterns. This is attributed to the extremely low loading of vanadium and chromium species, as well as their highly dispersed and predominantly amorphous nature within the PB matrix. Under such conditions, the metal species do not form long-range ordered crystalline domains detectable by XRD. Consequently, reliable indexing of diffraction peaks, calculation of dₕₖₗ values, or assignment of standard JCPDS card numbers for these metal oxides is not feasible. Similar behavior has been widely reported for conducting polymer-metal oxide hybrids synthesized via in-situ oxidative polymerization, where the metal species function as redox dopants or coordination centers rather than bulk crystalline phases57–59. Taken together, these analyses demonstrate that anodization effectively activates the graphite substrate, while the choice of oxidant governs the morphology, elemental composition, and structural ordering of PB, which in turn are expected to strongly influence the electrochemical performance of the resulting electrodes. Also, the absence of sharp diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline chromium or vanadium oxides indicates that the metal species are present in an amorphous or highly dispersed state, likely coordinated within the PB matrix rather than forming bulk crystalline phases. Such behavior is commonly observed in in-situ oxidant-driven polymerization systems, where metal species act simultaneously as oxidants and structural dopants.

Electrochemical studies

Electrochemical characterization of the fabricated electrodes

The CV technique was employed as the initial electrochemical test to investigate the capacitive performance of the fabricated electrodes. In CV analysis, the enclosed area under the CV curve is directly related to the electrode’s capacitance, thereby allowing a comparative evaluation of different electrode materials34,60. As shown in Fig. 5a, the CV curves recorded at a scan rate of 20 mV s−¹ indicate that the AGS electrode exhibits a significantly larger enclosed area compared to the BGS electrode. This enhancement is attributed to the presence of graphene oxide nanosheets formed through the anodizing process, which increases surface roughness and porosity, ultimately facilitating ion diffusion and charge storage53. Furthermore, the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes exhibit notably greater capacitance than the BGS and AGS electrode. This improvement is associated with the polymerization of benzidine in the presence of metal-based oxidants, which serve both as initiators for polymerization and as sources of metal oxides. The incorporation of these metal oxides not only enhances the electrical conductivity but also synergistically boosts the pseudocapacitive response of PB, thereby improving the overall electrochemical performance27,61,62. Figure 5b presents the specific capacitance values of the fabricated electrodes, calculated from the CV curves using Eq. 1. Based on the data, the PB-V/AGS electrode exhibits a higher specific capacitance compared to the PB-C/AGS electrode. This enhancement is attributed to the superior redox activity, pseudocapacitive contribution, and oxidant-controlled porous polymer-metal, which functions both as an effective oxidizing agent during polymerization and as a source of vanadium-based oxides. These vanadium species enhance the overall electrochemical performance by improving the conductivity, facilitating charge transfer, and contributing additional faradaic reactions, thereby resulting in higher capacitance63,64.

Cyclic voltammograms (a), and calculated specific capacitance of the different fabricated electrodes at the scan rate of 20 mV s− 1 in a 1.0 M H2SO4.

Figure 6a, b present the CV responses of the PB-V/AGS electrode, while Fig. 6c and d display the CV plots of the PB-C/AGS electrode recorded at different scan rates. As observed, increasing the scan rate results in an enhancement of the redox peak currents, which is indicative of rapid charge-discharge processes. Importantly, the minimal shift in peak potentials across scan rates suggests a high degree of electrochemical reversibility and a dominant pseudocapacitive behavior in both electrode systems34,41,65. The corresponding specific capacitance (Cₛₚ) values, derived from the CV data and presented in Fig. 6e and f for the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, respectively, reveal a decline in capacitance with increasing scan rate. Specifically, the PB-V/AGS electrode exhibits a reduction in Cₛₚ from 722.14 to 435.72 mF cm−2, while PB-C/AGS shows a similar trend from 583.88 to 362.14 mF cm−2, as the scan rate increases from 4 to 50 mV s−¹. This behavior is attributed to kinetic limitations at higher scan rates, where the shortened timescale restricts effective ion diffusion particularly that of H⁺ ions into the electrode structure. At lower scan rates, however, the electrolyte ions have sufficient time to penetrate the porous matrix, enabling more complete redox reactions and higher charge storage, hence the elevated capacitance values34,66. Figure 7a shows the CV curves of the PB‑V/AGS and PB‑C/AGS electrodes at 8 mV s−¹, along with their corresponding redox peaks (labeled 1–4). These peaks are ascribed to the redox reactions of PB, and the proposed mechanism for each peak is depicted in Fig. 7b. The redox behavior of both electrodes is predominantly governed by the pseudocapacitive characteristics of PB, which undergoes reversible proton‑coupled electron transfer reactions in the acidic electrolyte. The mechanisms outlined in Fig. 7b for all four peaks are in excellent agreement with our previous work34,41. Similar to other conducting aromatic amine polymers, PB can exist in different oxidation states including leucoemeraldine-like, emeraldine-like, and fully oxidized quinoid structures. During the anodic sweep, oxidation of amine/benzenoid segments to quinoid imine structures occurs (peak 1,2), whereas the reverse reduction process takes place during the cathodic scan (peak 3,4). These reversible transitions are accompanied by proton insertion/disinsertion from the H2SO4 electrolyte.

Another key electrochemical method for assessing the capacitive performance of electrode materials is the GCD technique. In this method, the discharge time serves as a critical parameter for estimating the specific capacitance, where longer discharge durations are indicative of higher energy storage capacity40,69. Furthermore, the shape of the GCD curves provides insight into the charge storage mechanism; ideally, symmetrical and triangular profiles are characteristic of efficient capacitive or supercapacitive behavior with good reversibility70. Using this technique, the supercapacitive properties of the fabricated electrodes were investigated in a three-electrode configuration at 4 mA cm− 2 in 1.0 M H2SO4, as depicted in Fig. 9a. The GCD curves reveal that electrodes incorporating polybenzidine (PB), namely PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS exhibit significantly longer discharge times compared to BGS and AGS electrodes. A comparative analysis of the PB-based electrodes shows that PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS in terms of discharge duration and thus specific capacitance. It should be noted that the term “enhanced performance” for PB‑C/AGS refers only to its improvement relative to BGS and AGS. However, when directly compared with PB‑V/AGS, the PB‑V/AGS electrode consistently exhibits superior capacitance, lower charge‑transfer resistance, and higher energy density. This improved performance is ascribed to the synergistic effect between PB and the metal-based oxidants (vanadate and dichromate), which not only facilitates polymerization but also introduces redox-active metal oxide species into the electrode matrix. Additionally, the integration of these pseudocapacitive materials with a graphitic substrate containing graphene oxide nanosheets enhances overall performance by combining the double-layer capacitive behavior of graphene-based materials with the faradaic activity of PB-M hybrids. This hybridization strategy effectively overcomes the limitations of individual components, resulting in enhanced capacitive characteristics. The specific capacitance values, calculated using Eq. 2, are summarized in Fig. 9b. Among the tested electrodes, PB-V/AGS exhibits the highest capacitance, which originates from oxidant-controlled polymer growth that produces a porous fibrous architecture with improved ion accessibility and efficient utilization of redox-active vanadium species71,72. Figure 9c and d present the GCD curves of the PB‑V/AGS and PB‑C/AGS electrodes, respectively, with a focus on the initial potential drop (IR drop) at the beginning of the discharge process. The IR drop values for PB‑V/AGS and PB‑C/AGS were determined to be 45 and 53 mV, respectively. These relatively small IR drops indicate low internal resistance and favorable ionic/electronic transport within the electrode structures. The observed difference in IR drop between the two electrodes can be attributed to the presence of different metal oxides incorporated into the PB matrix. Specifically, the vanadate‑derived (PB‑V) and dichromate‑derived (PB‑C) composites exhibit distinct electrical conductivities and interfacial charge transfer characteristics. The smaller IR drop in PB‑V/AGS (45 mV) compared to PB‑C/AGS (53 mV) suggests that the vanadium‑containing phase offers slightly superior charge transport kinetics, likely due to the higher intrinsic conductivity of vanadium oxide species or more favorable interaction with the PB backbone. In general, lower IR drop values are desirable for supercapacitor applications because they lead to reduced energy loss during charge-discharge cycling and improved rate capability. The modest IR drops observed for both electrodes confirm their suitability for high‑performance energy storage devices.

EIS was employed to gain deeper insight into the charge transport processes occurring at the electrode-electrolyte interface and to evaluate the comparative performance of the fabricated electrodes. As depicted in Fig. 11a, the Nyquist plots of PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes reveal that PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS exhibit significantly lower solution resistance (Rs). This reduced resistance is indicative of enhanced charge mobility and improved electrochemical performance, supporting the superior capacitive behavior of the modified electrodes. Further analysis was conducted by fitting the Nyquist plots with an equivalent circuit model, as shown in Fig. 11b and c for the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, respectively. The equivalent circuit comprises a solution resistance (Rs), a charge transfer resistance (Rct), a constant phase element (CPE) representing the non-ideal capacitive behavior primarily attributed to the anodized graphite substrate, and a Warburg impedance (W), which appears as a linear segment at approximately 45° in the low-frequency region indicative of ion diffusion processes within the electrode structure. The elements Rs, CPE, and W are connected in series, while Rct is positioned in parallel with the CPE.

Nyquist plot of PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes (a), fitted Nyquist plot of PB-V/AGS (b) and PB-C/AGS electrodes (c) with the equivalent circuit related in 1.0 M H2SO4 electrolyte solution.

In Fig. 11b and c, the impedance behavior of the electrodes is interpreted using an equivalent circuit composed of several key elements. The series resistance represents the combined intrinsic resistance of the electrode and the electrolyte. The constant phase element (CPE), which models the non‑ideal double‑layer capacitance arising from the graphite substrate and the GO‑modified AGS surface, is positioned in parallel with the charge‑transfer resistance (Rct). The low‑frequency region is characterized by the Warburg component (W), reflecting ion‑diffusion processes within the porous electrode structure.

The exponent n obtained from the CPE fitting serves as an indicator of surface heterogeneity, with values between 0 and 1 describing the deviation from ideal capacitive behavior73. As established in electrochemical theory, n = 1 corresponds to an ideal capacitor, whereas n = 0 and n = 0.5 represent purely resistive and diffusion‑controlled (W) responses, respectively74. In the present study, the CPE exponents of n = 0.98 for PB‑V/AGS and n = 0.86 for PB‑C/AGS demonstrate that both electrodes exhibit near‑ideal capacitive characteristics, consistent with the pseudocapacitive behavior observed in the CV and GCD analyses. Together, these components represent the overall electrochemical behavior of the system. Electrochemical characterization of the assembled supercapacitor device

To evaluate the practical applicability of the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, solid-state supercapacitor devices were fabricated using each electrode, and their electrochemical performance was assessed through CV and GCD techniques. Figure 12a and b display the CV curves of the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS devices, respectively. For both fabricated devices, well-defined redox peaks are clearly observed even in the presence of a gel-polymer electrolyte, indicating a robust pseudocapacitive response. As is clear from these figures, the value of the Faradaic current for the PB-V/AGS device is greater than that for the PB-C/AGS device under similar conditions, which can be attributed to the superior capacitive performance of polybenzidine when polymerized with vanadate rather than chromate. Vanadate likely enhances charge transport and redox activity more effectively due to its favorable electrochemical characteristics as both an oxidizing agent and a source of vanadium oxide species. Furthermore, the in-situ growth of PB on the AGS substrate in the presence of vanadate species induces an oxidant-controlled polymerization process. This regulates PB chain propagation and promotes the formation of a porous, fibrous morphology, which enhances electrolyte ion accessibility and facilitates effective utilization of redox-active vanadium centers. As a result, the fabricated supercapacitor by the PB-V/AGS electrode exhibits improved capacitive performance. Overall, the CV curves of both devices confirm their excellent capacitive behavior, underscoring their potential for use in high-performance energy storage applications. Additionally, the redox peaks remain largely unchanged in position with increasing scan rate, suggesting good electrochemical reversibility and stable pseudocapacitive behavior of the PB-based nanocomposites anchored on the AGS substrate. Figure 12c and d present the specific capacitance values calculated from the CV curves for the PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS devices, respectively. At a scan rate of 8 mV s−1, the PB-V/AGS-based device demonstrates a maximum capacitance of 363.59 mF cm−2 (45.44 F g− 1), whereas the PB-C/AGS device achieves 140.67 mF cm−2 (23.44 F g− 1). These results further highlight the enhanced energy storage capability of the PB-V/AGS system, making it a promising candidate for solid-state supercapacitor applications.

To further assess the electrochemical performance of the solid-state supercapacitor devices, GCD measurements were conducted at various current densities. The GCD profiles of devices assembled with PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, shown in Fig. 13a and b, respectively, exhibit well-defined symmetrical triangular shapes, which are characteristic of highly reversible charge–discharge processes and reflect dominant pseudocapacitive behavior. From these measurements, the specific capacitance values were derived and are presented in Fig. 13c and d for PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, respectively. At a current density of 0.5 mA g−1, the devices based on PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS exhibited specific capacitance values of 50.92 F g− 1 and 32.098 F g− 1, respectively, highlighting the superior energy storage performance of the PB-V/AGS configuration. To gain insight into the energy and power output of the fabricated devices, Ragone plots were constructed (Fig. 13e and f for PB-V/AGS and PB-C/AGS electrodes, respectively). These plots demonstrate the characteristic inverse relationship between energy density and power density, whereby an increase in power density leads to a corresponding reduction in energy density. For the PB-V/AGS-based supercapacitor, a maximum energy density of 12.83 mWh g− 1 was achieved at a corresponding power density of 168.5 mW g− 1 indicating its strong potential for high-performance energy storage applications. The PB-C/AGS device displayed energy–power characteristics comparable to those of the PB-V/AGS-based supercapacitor, though with slightly reduced overall values. Specifically, the supercapacitor achieved a maximum energy density of 8.125 mWh g− 1 at a corresponding power density of 225 mW g− 1. Long-term cycling stability is a critical factor for evaluating the reliability and lifespan of supercapacitors. The results of GCD stability tests were disply in Fig. 13g for both PB-V/AGS and PB-V/AGS-based supercapacitor devices, reveal that after 5000 charge-discharge cycles at a current density of 6 mA cm−2, the PB-V/AGS- and PB-C/AGS-based devices retained approximately 88.28% and 88.91% of their initial capacitance, respectively. The gradual capacitance decay observed during prolonged cycling can be attributed to possible degradation mechanisms commonly reported for conductive polymers in acidic media. Specifically, the redox activity of PB in the PVA‑H2SO4 gel electrolyte involves repeated proton‑coupled electron transfer reactions, which may induce progressive structural rearrangements within the polymer backbone, such as chain scission, loss of conjugation, or over‑oxidation. Concurrently, the gel electrolyte itself may undergo physical or chemical deterioration, including dehydration, phase separation, or proton conductivity loss, particularly under repeated potential cycling. Collectively, these degradation pathways lead to a gradual decline in pseudocapacitive performance and overall cycling stability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that oxidant redox chemistry is a decisive parameter for controlling polybenzidine growth, hybrid structure formation, and electrochemical behavior, rather than merely serving as a polymerization trigger. Structural and morphological analyses confirmed that the choice of oxidant strongly influenced the surface morphology, elemental composition, and electrochemical behavior of the resulting electrodes. Among the two, PB-V/AGS exhibited a highly porous structure and superior redox activity, leading to remarkable electrochemical performance. Specifically, PB-V/AGS delivered a maximum specific capacitance of 1080 mF cm−2, significantly higher than 920 mF cm−2 for PB-C/AGS, along with improved charge-discharge reversibility and lower internal resistance. In the assembled symmetric devices, PB-V/AGS achieved outstanding energy density (12.63 mWh g−1) and power density (168.5 mW g−1), outperforming PB-C/AGS devices, which recorded 8.125 mWh g−1 at a comparable power density. Importantly, both devices exhibited robust cycling stability with capacitance retention of nearly 88% after 5000 cycles, confirming their long-term durability. These findings highlight the critical role of oxidant chemistry in tuning polymer morphology and electrochemical properties, and they demonstrate that vanadate-driven polymerization of benzidine on anodized graphite is a highly effective strategy for designing next-generation supercapacitor electrodes.
