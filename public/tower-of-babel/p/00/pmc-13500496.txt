A longitudinal analysis of published clinical practice guidelines and their effect on disease burden
Authors: Xialei Li, Dannya Estau, Xiaoru Huang, Fuhua Deng, Xuening Liu, Xinghui Li, Jinfeng Gao, Zijian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) are intended to improve health and reduce disease burden, yet their global status and their association with disease burden remain unknown. This longitudinal ecological study analyzes the quantity and quality of CPGs and explores their associations with disease burden. From 1995 to 2023, 10,657 CPGs were published, with the number of CPGs in 2023 being 5.15 times the number of CPGs in 1995. Europe contributed 40.2% of all CPGs, while CPGs from Africa accounted for only 0.8% of and showed minimal growth. Treatment CPGs contributed the most (45.0%), while diagnosis CPGs showed the fastest growth. Prevention CPGs (8.4%) and nursing CPGs (5.3%) and rehabilitation CPGs were less common, with rehabilitation CPGs showing the slowest growth. The 3-year update rate of CPG is 23.8%. Quality assessment of 1633 CPGs using AGREE II revealed substantial variation across domains. Overall 37.8% CPGs achieved scores above 70% per domain, while the “applicability” domain had the lowest median score (36%). Cross-region CPGs and CPGs for neoplasms exhibited the highest AGREE II scores, with the median score exceeding 70% in 3/6 domains. In contrast, CPGs from Asia and CPGs for cardiovascular diseases did not exceed the 70% threshold in any domain. Neither the quantity nor the quality of CPGs was significantly associated with disease burden at the national level. In conclusion, this study reveals that the quantity of CPGs has increased over the past three decades, although geographic and thematic differences remain. Future opportunities for CPG development include improving the AGREE II scores and focusing on less represented areas including rehabilitation, nursing and prevention.

Introduction

Clinical practice guideline (CPG) is a statement that includes recommendations intended to optimize patient care. It is informed by a systematic review of evidence and an assessment of the benefits and harms of alternative care options1. As an essential healthcare tool, it standardizes clinical practice, aiming to improve decision-making quality and patient outcomes2,3. It plays an indispensable role at all levels of healthcare, from clinicians and patients to national health policy and global public health collaboration4,5. CPGs offer clinicians evidence-based guidance grounded in scientific research and clinical experience, which helps reduce variability in care, decrease error rates, and ultimately enhance the quality and consistency of healthcare services. On a national level, CPGs support policy development and optimize resource allocation, making public health responses more strategic. Internationally, they promote consistency in medical practices, enabling collaboration and standardization across countries. For the concept of clinical guideline, the 1990 Institute of Medicine (IOM) report is generally regarded as coining the term6. In 2011, the IOM further refined the definition of CPGs, marking a new phase for their development1. Presently, the development of CPGs has accelerated, and CPGs have been extensively applied across medical fields. With the growth of healthcare demands and the increase in public health challenges, the number of CPGs has rapidly expanded7,8. In response to global public health crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, around a hundred CPGs were developed within one year9.

Although many CPGs have already been published worldwide, their number, quality, and association with disease burden remain unknown. Given their widespread use and long-term development, it is crucial to evaluate whether CPGs are associated with disease burden. This study aims to analyze the status of CPGs and to evaluate their association with disease burden at the national level.

Here, we showed that annual number of CPGs grew rapidly, with the annual number of 2023 exceeding five times that of 1995. The total 10,657 publications were geographically and thematically unbalanced. Europe was the primary contributor, whereas African CPGs was minimal growth with 1/51 of European CPGs. Thematically, diagnosis CPGs expanded most rapidly, but rehabilitation CPGs grew slowest. Quality assessment of 1,633 CPGs using AGREE II revealed that overall quality was low and only 37.8% of CPGs scored above 70% across all domains. Regionally, cross-region CPGs exhibited the highest quality, while Asian CPGs were the lowest. Notably, no significant correlation was found between CPGs’ quantity or quality and disease burden at the national level. These findings suggest that global development of CPGs should transition from quantitative expansion to high-quality models, with more focus on rehabilitation, nursing, and prevention CPGs.

Results

Quantity analysis for clinical practice guidelines

Our analysis retrieved 42,679 studies, of which 10,657 CPGs were eligible for the analysis (Supplementary Fig.1a, Supplementary Data 1). The annual number of CPGs grew rapidly, with the annual number of CPGs in 2023 (n = 582) representing 5.15-fold that of 1995 (n = 113). Trends in the annual number of CPGs were best fit by exponential regression (R2 = 0.86) (Fig. 1a). The annual number of CPGs exhibited two critical turning points, with a sustained decline after 2011 followed by a recovery in 2015. Meanwhile, the cumulative publication of CPGs has surpassed 10,000. Quantity analysis for different categories of clinical practice guidelines

We conducted a subgroup analysis to compare the quantity of CPGs across different categories. For CPGs from different continents, 40.2% of CPGs were from Europe (n = 4285), while 0.8% of CPGs were from Africa (n = 84), accounting for less than 2% of those in Europe (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the number of CPGs in Africa showed almost no growth during 1995 to 2023. Of note, the EAPC of the number of Asian CPGs was up to 16.54% (95% CI 14.54%-18.56%, p value = 3.43×10−16) (Supplementary Table 1). Country-level analysis found the United States (n = 2961), the United Kingdom (n = 761), and China (n = 673) as the top three producers of CPGs (Fig. 2b). Analysis of disease-specific CPGs showed that the greatest number of CPGs was for neoplasms (n = 2147, 20.1%) and exhibited the most rapid growth trend (EAPC 9.31%, 95% CI 7.56%-11.09%, p value = 2.68 × 10−11). In contrast, the fewest number of CPGs was for sense organ diseases (n = 79, 0.7%) (Fig. 2c). For CPGs focusing on different themes, treatment CPGs were the most prevalent (n = 4793), accounting for 45% of all CPGs. The number of diagnosis CPGs increased most rapidly, with an EAPC of 7.35% (95% CI 6.03–8.68%, p value = 1.08 × 10−11). In contrast, prevention (n = 895, 8.4%), nursing (n = 568, 5.3%), and rehabilitation CPGs (n = 82, 0.8%) constituted substantially smaller proportions and exhibited relatively limited growth over time (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, 8.8% of CPGs (n = 941) focused solely on the pediatric population, and 0.8% of CPGs (n = 84) focused solely on the geriatric population. Update analysis for clinical practice guidelines

Considering that an update is essential for providing the most current evidence for clinical practice, we assessed the update status of CPGs. The results showed that out of 10,657 CPGs, 1613 (15.1%) CPGs had been updated. Despite the ratio of updated CPGs increasing annually, the highest ratio was 22.9% (Fig. 3a). Among all regions, CPGs from America demonstrated the highest update rate (n = 608, 17.7%). Across disease categories, CPGs for neoplasms exhibited the highest update rate, reaching 24.6% (n = 527) (Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, the updated interval between CPGs varied significantly, ranging from 1 year to 24 years. Among the updated CPGs identified, update interval information was available for 332 CPGs. Quality analysis for clinical practice guidelines

To reflect the quality of CPGs, we collected AGREE II six domain scores of CPGs from the published articles. Among 138 articles, 1633 assessed CPGs were evaluated by AGREE II (Supplementary Fig. 1b, Supplementary Data 2). Regarding the six domains of AGREE II, the median score of both the “clarity of presentation” domain and “scope and purpose” domain was above 70%, while the median score of the other four domains was below 70%. Among them, the median score of the “applicability” domain was the lowest (AGREE II score 36%) (Fig. 4a). All six domains of AGREE II scores changed significantly over time (p value < 0.001). The median score of the “editorial independence” domain showed the greatest improvement, while the median score of the “applicability” domain remained relatively stable (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, when CPGs were grouped by the published date before 2011 and after 2011, the AGREE II domain scores also differed markedly between the two periods (p value < 0.001) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 4). Discussion

Clinical practice guideline has a long history of development and are widely applied in health care. Given their intended role in shaping clinical decision-making, it is reasonable to explore whether CPGs were associated with disease burden. In this study, we first conducted a comprehensive assessment of the development of CPGs and found that the number of CPGs has increased substantially over the past three decades. However, CPGs of prevention, nursing, rehabilitation, and CPGs focused solely on the geriatric population remain relatively scarce. In addition, the quality of CPGs was relatively low, with significant differences across the six AGREE II domains. Substantial variation in the quality of CPGs was also observed across continents and diseases. At the national level, neither the quantity nor the quality of CPGs was significantly associated with disease burden metrics at any time lag (one to five years). It is noteworthy that our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that CPGs have no clinical effectiveness at the patient level.

Regarding the quantity of CPGs, our analysis reveals disparities despite the overall growth in the number of CPGs. First, CPGs are currently focused on treatment, while those for prevention, nursing, and rehabilitation are scarce. To improve healthcare, future development must shift to full-cycle disease management that covers all stages of disease19. Second, a critical gap exists in demographic coverage. Despite the global acceleration of population ageing and the rising burden of age-related chronic diseases, there remains a distinct scarcity of CPGs focused solely on the geriatric population. Future research should pay more attention to developing CPGs designed specifically for the geriatric population to address their unique physiological complexities and multimorbidity20,21. Third, we observed that 253 updated CPGs exceeded the three-year update interval. This may be attributed to several factors. The necessity of updates depends fundamentally on the stability of the underlying evidence base. Clinical areas with rapidly evolving evidence (e.g., neoplasms) may require more frequent updates than those with established practices (e.g., basic life support). Additionally, some CPG panels intentionally design “living guidelines” with continuous evidence surveillance rather than a fixed update cycle22,23. Moreover, many organizations face resource constraints and competing priorities that may legitimately delay CPG updates, making it challenging to maintain regular revision schedules.

In terms of the quality of CPGs, our analysis firstly aligns with previous large-scale evaluations16,17 that reveals gradual improvements in CPGs’ quality over time but persistent weaknesses in certain domains such as “applicability”. Second, our study extends the evidence base beyond pre-2014 assessments to reveal two key observations regarding quality evolution and evaluation practices. We identified a significant quality improvement in CPGs published after 2011. This positive shift likely reflects the paradigm shift triggered by the 2011 IOM definition, which mandated systematic evidence reviews. The adoption of these rigorous standards appears to have promoted improvements in methodological rigor and reporting transparency. In addition, this study also observed that the same CPG is frequently subjected to independent quality evaluations across different studies. Although these studies use the same assessment tool, differences in the composition of appraisers and evaluation processes may lead to inconsistent quality scores for the same CPG. Such discrepancies may increase the complexity of defining the quality of CPGs in clinical practice. Therefore, the standardization and precision of guideline quality assessment need to be addressed. It may be beneficial to consider evaluations led by professional academic organizations to achieve more standardized assessment practices. Standardizing evaluation frameworks and processes could help reduce variability across studies and provide more consistent quality assessments, thereby offering clearer guidance for clinical decision-making and guideline implementation.

Synthesizing the insights from the quantity and quality analyses of CPGs, it becomes evident that CPG development exhibits significant global heterogeneity. Understanding these different patterns is essential for steering the future evolution of CPGs. Geographically, the number of CPGs from Asia has grown rapidly, yet their quality remains low. Meanwhile, Africa falls behind in both quantity and quality. We propose that these disparities may be shaped by an interplay of systemic factors: (i) Resource Constraints: In low-resource settings, the guideline ecosystem is likely constrained by funding shortages and workforce deficits. For instance, qualitative evidence from South Africa suggests that the lack of technical capacity hampers both the development and implementation phases24. (ii) Evidence-Context Mismatch: A potential misalignment exists between the evidence base (derived largely from high-income countries) and local realities. Without rigorous contextualization, recommendations based on advanced health systems may prove irrelevant or unimplementable in low- and middle-income countries25. (iii) Adaptation Barriers: Divergent disease burdens and care delivery models further complicate the transnational application of guidelines, potentially exacerbating the quality gap in resource-constrained regions. Consequently, bridging these global divides requires strengthening local development infrastructure in low-resource settings while enhancing the contextual adaptability of international evidence.

The longitudinal analysis showed that national-level CPGs and disease burden have not yet achieved a positive correlation effect. CPGs are important clinical decision-making tools, and their effect on disease burden is also influenced by different levels, especially the implementation of guidelines. At the intrinsic level, the practical utility of many CPGs may be limited. Our analysis identified “applicability” as the lowest-scoring domain with a median score of 36%, suggesting insufficient attention to implementation tools, resource implications, and monitoring criteria. When guidelines are developed without clear pathways for real-world operationalization, their translation into measurable health outcomes may be constrained. Furthermore, variation in methodological rigor, particularly in CPGs not grounded in systematic reviews, may weaken their credibility and influence. The growing number of CPGs may also lead to inconsistencies. For instance, a study of eight hypertension guidelines found that 41% of recommendations differed in direction26, illustrating how heterogeneity across guidelines can complicate clinical decision-making. Extrinsically, the broader implementation environment plays a critical role. Clinician engagement remains variable, and barriers such as limited awareness and perceived complexity have been documented27–29. In addition, institutional and policy alignment is essential for guideline uptake. Reimbursement restrictions and lack of insurance coverage for recommended interventions may limit practical implementation30. Within such systemic constraints, the relationship between CPGs and national disease burden may not manifest as a significant association.

This study has several strengths. We first systematically analysed the status of CPGs. The analysis showed the dynamic changes in the quantity and quality of CPGs, and revealed disparities in different categories of CPGs. These findings are crucial for guiding the development of CPGs, addressing region and disease-specific needs. We also incorporated lag structures to explore potential delayed associations. However, several limitations should be considered. First, due to its ecological design, the analysis is susceptible to the ecological fallacy. Associations observed at the national level do not necessarily reflect relationships at the individual level, and causal inferences at the individual level cannot be made. Additionally, unmeasured national-level confounders and heterogeneity in implementation capacity may have attenuated potential associations.

In conclusion, our study characterizes the global CPG landscape as one of substantial quantitative expansion accompanied by structural imbalances and relatively low quality. The absence of an ecological association with disease burden underscores the complexity of translating CPG development into population-level health improvements. Future strategies may therefore need to focus less on the expansion of CPG output and more on the institutional and contextual factors that determine whether CPGs can meaningfully influence health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

A longitudinal ecological design was employed to analyze the status of CPGs and their associations with disease burden from 1995 to 2023. A time-series panel dataset with countries as units was constructed, incorporating variables such as the annual number of CPGs, the annual proportion of CPGs with AGREE II domain scores above 70%, and disease burden indicators. The study consisted of two main components: (1) a longitudinal descriptive analysis to show the development of CPGs’ quantity and quality; (2) an ecological association analysis to evaluate the relationship between CPGs and disease burden, while adjusting for covariates. All data were obtained from publicly available international databases. Therefore, ethical approval was not required for this study.

Data sources

CPGs were searched from Medline via PubMed, Embase via OVID, and Guidelines International Network (GIN) Library. Medline via PubMed and Embase via OVID were chosen for their comprehensive coverage of biomedical literature, encompassing published guidelines and related evaluations. GIN Library was selected as it is a specialized repository dedicated to the collection of CPGs from diverse countries and regions worldwide. The articles evaluating CPGs by the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research & Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument were searched from PubMed10,11.

Indicators of disease burden, containing disability adjusted life years (DALYs) rate and death rate, were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease Results (GBD) (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), a widely used database coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. Population density, percentage of the population living in urban areas, gross domestic product per capita, and human development index that used as covariates were obtained from Our World in Data (https://ourworldindata.org/).

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

The search strategy used medical subject headings (MeSH) and free-text words. The detailed search strategy was listed in the Methods of the Appendix. The time scope of the search was from 1 January 1995 to 31 December 2023. The inclusion criteria included both guidelines that strictly meet the IOM standards and the clinical guidelines that have clear recommendations for disease prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and medication, despite the lack of systematic reviews, to ensure the comprehensiveness of the inclusion guidelines in the real world. We excluded non-English guidelines because non-English guidelines from around the world lack operability both in accessibility and language understanding. Additionally, it does not have repeatability and scientificity for other researchers.

Studies that evaluated the quality of CPGs using the AGREE II instrument were included if they fully complied with the scoring requirements outlined in the AGREE II User’s Manual. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) unable to obtain full text; (ii) the publication was not in English; (iii) the publication did not provide standardized scores for AGREE II’s six domains. Two authors assessed all text for inclusion. Any disagreement between authors was resolved through discussion with a third author until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted information from the included CPGs, with the process cross-checked. During the process, any doubt or divergence would be resolved through negotiation with the corresponding author. The general information was extracted directly from each CPG, including year of publication, continent (America, Europe, Asia, Africa, Ocean, Cross-region), country, disease, theme (prevention, diagnosis, treatment, nursing, and rehabilitation), update status, and targeted population. Disease, theme, and target population of CPGs were determined by screening the title and abstract. As for region-specific CPGs, each unique country listed in the authors’ affiliations for a given CPG was credited. These countries were subsequently mapped to their respective continents. To account for cross-region collaboration, CPGs involving authors from over one continent were categorized as “cross-region CPGs”. AGREE II standardized scores across six domains, as well as the country of guideline development for the evaluated CPGs, were obtained from published studies assessing CPG quality. Based on the CPG dataset, the analytic samples were restricted to countries with at least one year of data on either CPG’s quantity or CPG’s quality. For those countries, national-level disease burden metrics, including age-standardized all-cause DALYs rates and age-standardized all-cause death rates, were directly extracted from the GBD database. National-level socioeconomic covariates, including population density, urban population proportion, GDP per capita, and the Human Development Index, were sourced directly from the Our World in Data platform.

Subsequently, to assess temporal differences in AGREE II six domain scores, CPGs were grouped into six categories based on publication year (before 2001, 2001-2005, 2006-2010, 2011-2015, 2016-2020, and after 2020)16,17. In addition, AGREE II domain scores were compared between CPGs published before and after 2011 using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to evaluate the potential impact of the updated IOM standards on the quality of CPGs.

As for the association between CPGs and disease burden, generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was used to analyze ecological associations between national-level CPG metrics (the annual number of CPGs and the annual proportion of CPGs with AGREE II domain score above 70%) and national-level disease burden indicators18. Given the right-skewed distribution of national-level disease burden indicators, GEE model used a gamma distribution with a log-link function. Exponentiated coefficients from the GEE model are presented as rate ratios (RR), along with their 95% confidence intervals. To account for the anticipated delay between CPG dissemination and measurable impact on national-level disease burden indicators, our analysis considers the time lags. In this framework, the exposures (CPG metrics) and all socioeconomic covariates (population density, urbanization rate, GDP per capita, and HDI) were measured at the index year (t) to represent the baseline conditions. The outcomes (DALY rates and death rates) were measured with a temporal lag of one to five years (t + 1 to t + 5). Lag 1 represents the association between CPGs in year t and outcomes in year t + 1. Lag 2 represents year t and year t + 2, and so forth up to Lag 5 (t + 5). Separate GEE models were fitted for each of the five lag periods to estimate how the association varied across different latency intervals. To address missing data, a complete-case analysis was employed for the primary analysis. During the construction of the analytical dataset, we treated “country‑year” as the fundamental unit of observation. The criterion was that for any country-year observation, if data were missing for any of the CPG indicators (quantity, quality), covariates, or outcome variables, the observation was excluded from the panel data analysis. We further performed a sensitivity analysis using imputation for missing data. Data collection was performed using Microsoft Excel (version 2021). All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.3). Specifically, we employed the following R packages: readxl for data import; tidyverse (including dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2) for data manipulation; geepack for GEE modeling; missMDA for multiple imputation with PCA; boot for bootstrapping; and effsize, car, e1071, and rstatix for effect size calculations and statistical diagnostics. Statistical significance was attributed to two-sided p values < 0.05.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, several measures were implemented to address potential sources of bias. First, to minimize measurement and information bias, we exclusively included CPG quality data from peer-reviewed studies that strictly adhered to the scoring requirements outlined in the AGREE II User’s Manual. This ensures that the quality assessments were conducted using standardized, validated instruments with high reliability. Second, to address confounding bias, we adjusted for key national-level socioeconomic factors (e.g., GDP per capita, population density, and HDI) in our GEE models to isolate the specific association between CPG metrics and health outcomes. Furthermore, the use of sensitivity analyses with missing data imputation served to assess potential attrition bias, ensuring the stability and consistency of our primary results.
