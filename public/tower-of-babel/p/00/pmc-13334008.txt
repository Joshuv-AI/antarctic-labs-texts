Flexible autonomic nervous system dynamics underlie attentional engagement in infancy
Authors: Isabella C Stallworthy, Amritha Varshini Devarajan, Lorena Santamaria, Xinchen Fu, Xing Xi Tan, Daniel Fatori, Guilherme V Polanczyk, Victoria Leong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe autonomic nervous system, relatively understudied compared to the central nervous system, is thought to play a key role in supporting attentional engagement, a cornerstone of cognitive control in early development. To address the non-trivial challenges that complex autonomic signals present to traditional modeling, we leverage recurrence quantification analysis (RQA), an established nonlinear approach for quantifying the temporal organization of heart-rate dynamics. Here, we compute recurrence metrics to examine trait, or participant level (across the task), and state level (moment-by-moment) autonomic recurrence patterns in relation to infants’ multimodal attentional engagement with objects in a cross-cultural sample from Singapore and Brazil (N = 78, Ages = 8.63–25.32 months). Across both countries, at the trait level, we observe that for most metrics, lower participant-level autonomic recurrence is related to higher infant attentional engagement and higher parent-reported regulatory capacity. Similarly, at the state level, all recurrence metrics are lower during moments of attentional engagement compared to disengagement. Further, certain metrics show sensitivity to attentional transitions and differentiate between different types of attentional engagement. Findings suggest that reduced autonomic recurrence, indicative of enhanced autonomic flexibility and stronger parasympathetic contributions, supports early attentional engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41293-w.Keywords: Recurrence quantification analysis, Autonomic nervous system, Parasympathetic nervous system, Flexibility, Multimodal attentional engagementSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Multimodal attentional engagement in infancy

In the first months of life, infants develop their capacity to engage with the world through visual attention, a collection of processes that allow for the selective prioritization of visual information1. They display early predispositions to visually attend to social stimuli, where attentional allocation is strongly driven by bottom-up features of the stimuli. Over the course of the first year of life, the visual attention system becomes increasingly complex and driven by top-down contributions1–3. With the development of hand motor control and increasing mobility, infant attentional engagement becomes increasingly multimodal4 and they begin to explore the world by combining visual attention and physical touch. Joint visual and manual engagement with objects has been shown to support learning of new words4 and interacts with parent behaviors such as object labeling5. Multimodal attentional engagement requires intricate coordination between multiple components of the sensorimotor system including visual attention, gross and fine motor skills, driven by bottom-up properties of the environment and emerging top-down attentional control.

Autonomic dynamics support behavior and regulation in infancy

Enacting multimodal engagement with the environment depends on both central nervous system activity as well as the fast-acting parasympathetic branch of autonomic nervous system, responsible for maintaining homeostasis and coordinating metabolic output. During early infancy when cortical systems are relatively immature, autonomic nervous system activity is thought to be especially important for facilitating adaptive behaviors6–9. During times of relative safety, the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) increases to support focused task-oriented engagement, conversely decreasing in the presence of threat to accommodate sympathetic output and increased mobilization8. As summarized below, existing work suggests that PNS and heart rate activity more generally support adaptive engagement within diverse contexts.

Average increases in PNS, measured using respiratory sinus arrhythmia, are associated with more positive affect and overall social engagement10–12. Meta-analysis suggests that PNS decreases during the psychosocial stressor of the “Still-Face” experiment in which caregivers assume a blank non-emotive facial expression for 2 minutes13. Existing work also finds evidence for the dynamic modulation of PNS activity to support behavior on the order of seconds. Findings suggest that increases in PNS activity directly precede an infant’s social attention to their caregiver, but only during periods when the caregiver is actively engaging her infant14. Infants with higher PNS activity also exhibit more volatility in positive affect and feedback loops between positive and negative affect, and stronger co-regulation from caregivers relative to those with lower PNS activity15.

Changes in heart rate, a more general measure of autonomic activity, have been linked to different stages of sustained visual attention3,16–19. Studies document heart rate deceleration in infants while orienting to a stimulus, continuously lowered heart rate during sustained attention, followed by a rapid return to pre-stimulus heart rate during after terminating their attention16,17,19–21. During periods of sustained visual attention, infants also reduce the variability in their heart rate19, thought to reflect increases in PNS activity. Existing work distinguishes between focused attention marked by attentive gaze and manipulation of the focal object16 and heart-rate deceleration during which distraction is less likely, and more passive casual attention during which distraction is more likely17. Together, these patterns are consistent with increased PNS activity during periods of sustained and focused attention relative to inattention19.

Recurrence Quantification Analysis for capturing autonomic dynamics

Modeling heart rate dynamics, particularly in early development and as they unfold within naturalistic settings, poses significant challenges. Given nonlinearities in their temporal patterns, signal noise, limited data length, frequent outliers, and non-stationarity, these signals do not conform well to standard parametric analytic strategies. As such, drawing on these traditional linear methods risks obfuscating meaningful variation and nuanced patterns that could provide valuable insight into how the autonomic nervous system operates to support developing complex behaviors22. Leveraging more flexible methods has the potential to reveal more precise insight into the dynamical patterns of PNS activity at multiple temporal scales, while maximizing the use of available data.

Practical challenges coupled with a growing interest in the recurrent properties of complex systems23 have fueled interest in the use of flexible analytic strategies such as Recurrence Quantification Analysis (RQA). This technique is a highly versatile method for quantifying and characterizing recurring patterns in a signal. RQA offers many notable advantages: it can accommodate relatively short time-series24, does not make parametric assumptions about time-series, can accommodate noisy and high dimensional systems, and is robust to non-linearities, non-stationarity, and time-series with outliers25–28.

As summarized in Fig. 1, RQA quantifies the overall recurrence and patterns of recurrence in a time-series signal using several recurrence metrics. The recurrence rate (RR) quantifies the overall percentage of the points in the Recurrence Plot (RP, see Fig. 1a) that recur, or revisit a state previously exhibited by the system (Fig. 1b). Two additional commonly used metrics quantify the patterns with which recurrence unfolds: Determinism (DET) represents the percentage of recurring values in the RP that recur in the form of a consecutive pattern of states repeating itself. Laminarity (LAM) represents the percentage of recurring values in the RP that recur as the exact same state visited earlier in the time-series.

Given its versatility and methodological strengths, RQA has been used successfully to capture autonomic dynamics across diverse research settings ranging from experimental cognitive-load paradigms to clinical research contexts in adults. For example, RQA of heart-rate activity has been used to detect mental arithmetic stress in young females29, classify stress conditions30, phase transitions during continuous incremental exercise31, ventilatory thresholds in adults with different fitness levels32, and to characterize heart-rate variability in end-stage renal disease33. Existing observational and pharmacological work in animals and humans indicates that relative decreases in heart rate recurrence metrics are associated with increases in PNS activity. Javorka and colleagues (2008) report relative decreases in DET and LAM while human participants were lying down in a state of greater PNS activity, compared to standing up (with RR held fixed at 5%). Additionally, Gonzalez and colleagues (2000) found increases in physiological RR and DET following administration of atropine, a pharmacological PNS blocker, in rats (LAM was not measured). Accordingly, RQA was selected here to capture the nuanced and nonlinear features of developmental autonomic signals.

Current study

We aimed to quantify the recurrent autonomic patterns that underlie multimodal attentional engagement in infancy. Autonomic recurrence metrics were examined at two time-scales, providing complementary insights on multimodal attention: (a) at the participant or trait level, capturing recurrence values across the entire task duration in relation to the proportion of time infants spend engaged in multimodal attention and to parent reports of infant regulatory capacities; (b) at the state level, capturing dynamic patterns of recurrence within 5-second windows in relation to moments of multimodal attention. We evaluate trait and state recurrence metrics with respect to states of infant multimodal attentional engagement, with a particular focus on two forms of engagement: sustaining and waning attention.

We hypothesized that lower autonomic recurrence metrics (RR, LAM, DET): (a) at the trait level, would be associated with increased infant attentional engagement and higher infant regulatory abilities; and (b) at the state level, would show dynamic modulation in tandem with changes in the infant’s state of multimodal attentional engagement. We had no a priori hypotheses about differing effects for each of the recurrence metrics.

Methods

Participants

Eighty-one typically developing infants were recruited to the study. Data collection took place at two laboratory sites: Brazil (N = 46; 23 males, 23 female infants) and Singapore (N = 35; 12 males, 23 female infants). Infants in the Brazilian cohort (part of the Germina cohort36) ranged in age from 8 to 15 months (M = 11.88 months, range = 8.64-15.87), while those in the Singaporean cohort ranged from 11 to 25 months (M = 16.38 months, range = 11.00-25.33.00.33) (Supplementary Fig. S1). The age distributions thus differed across sites. As the Singapore cohort primarily focused on older infants, the Brazilian site intentionally recruited younger infants to complement the overarching research aims of capturing early developmental stages. Age and testing site were statistically controlled in all analyses to account for these differences. Eligibility for participation was determined using a standardized screening questionnaire; only term-born, typically developing infants (2500 and 4500 g) with no known neurological conditions were included. Infants were additionally excluded if they had known cardiac or vascular conditions (e.g., congenital heart disease or other known medical significant cardiac abnormalities). Mothers were excluded if they reported any pre-existing or past neurological or psychological disorder. All mothers reported normal hearing and vision for themselves and their infants. Of the 81 infants who completed the task, 1 was excluded due to health-related complications, and 2 were excluded due to noise in the data exceeding our defined thresholds, leaving a final sample of 78 infants whose data contributed to the analyses. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Nanyang Technological University (NTU) in Singapore (IRB-2021-808), and National Commission for Research Ethics (CAAC: 49671221.2.0000.0068) in Brazil, and all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Mothers provided written informed consent prior to participation. All mother-infant dyads were reimbursed for their participation in the study.

The present study represents a secondary analysis of data drawn from a larger longitudinal, multimodal investigation of parent-infant dyads. The current analyses focus on a theoretically motivated subset of this dataset, examining infants’ autonomic dynamics and their relation to multimodal attentional engagement during a free-play task.

Infant self-regulation measure

Mothers completed the Infant Behaviour Questionnaire-Revised (IBQ-R)37–39, a parent-reported measure of infant temperament originally validated for 3-12 months but used in subsequent work up to 18 months, rating the frequency of specific behaviours over the past 1-2 weeks on a 7-point scale (1 = never, 7 = always). In line with this literature, we included IBQ-R data only from infants aged 18.0 months and below at the time of questionnaire completion. Item-level responses were first scored according to the published scoring manual, with negatively worded items reverse-coded where appropriate. Subscale scores were computed as the mean of the relevant items. Higher order composite scores (i.e., Surgency/Extraversion, Negative Affectivity, and Orienting/Regulatory Capacity) were computed as the mean of their respective subscales, following the standard IBQ scoring procedure. Higher scores indicate greater expression of the trait. The Orienting/Regulation subscale is hypothesized to represent an early developmental precursor of the effortful control system that supports attention control and is therefore of greater interest in this study37,40,41. Of the 80 infants included for processing, IBQ-R was administered only to those 18 months and below, 6 cases were marked as missing data and excluded due to incomplete questionnaires; infants older than 18 months did not receive the IBQ-R by design, and their scores are coded as missing, leaving a sample of N = 66 infants with IBQ in the sample. Of the 66 infants with IBQ-R data, 2 were excluded from the final analysis due to additional missingness or unusable data, resulting in a final analytic sample of 64 infants. Although the Early Childhood Behaviour Questionnaire (ECBQ) is validated from 18 months onward; we retained the IBQ-R up to 18 months to avoid introducing measurement discontinuities and to ensure comparability across the ages.

Attentional engagement task

We leveraged the Sequential Touching Task42,43, (Fig. 2 a) as a naturalistic context for examining infant attention and underlying physiological dynamics. Mothers and their infants engaged in this task, during which infants are presented with 8 toy objects to play freely during 2 periods (2-4 min) separated by maternal demonstration of the toy. This study focused on the play periods (one prior to and one following the demonstration) and pre-post order with respect to the demonstration is treated as a covariate given that the primary focus of this study is not on maternal interaction per se. A more detailed description of this task can be found.

Schematics of The Behavioral Task and Attentional Behavior Coding. Note. (a) Free play task during which infants played with objects for up to 4 min. (b) Example of our video-coded attentional engagement time series and its segmentation into sustaining and waning types. The upper panel shows an illustrative sequence of engagement intervals with disengagement at the task’s start and end (white), and engagement periods shaded by type: sustaining (light gray; monotonically increasing behaviour durations) and waning (dark gray; monotonically decreasing behaviour durations). The lower timeline lists example actions (e.g., gaze, holding, squeezing, bouncing) performed within the same object or across different objects.

All participant actions during the task were recorded using high-definition video cameras positioned at strategic locations around the task setup, capturing at 25 frames per second (fps). Two frontal view cameras captured recordings of participants’ facial expressions, gaze direction, and hand and arm movements. From these video recordings, attentional behaviors were manually coded using a comprehensive coding scheme (see Supplement Table S1). This scheme encompassed a taxonomy of infant behaviors during object interaction, including holding (grasp of object), repetitive movements (e.g. banging, shaking), functional interactions (using the object according to its intended play), pressing (squeezing the object), and gaze patterns. More specifically, periods of gaze only (lasting longer than 0.5 s), contact without action (e.g., holding), gross motor actions (e.g., banging), fine motor actions (e.g., squeezing), and toy-specific actions (e.g., bouncing a ball) performed on the different objects were coded. All coding was conducted frame by frame using the Adobe Premiere Pro software, and coders were trained to reliability prior to independent coding. Coders underwent supervised training using reference-coded materials prior to independent coding. Inter-coder reliability was subsequently assessed on a subset of four participants (58,168 frames), representing approximately 5% of the full sample (4/78 participants). Reliability was calculated at the raw frame-level as the percent agreement, defined as the proportion of frames receiving identical codes across coders out of the total jointly coded frames. The mean inter-coder agreement across this subset was at least 80%, after which they proceeded with independent coding. Frames were also coded for the presence of positive, negative, and neutral affect. Positive affect included smiling; negative affect included frowning or crying; neutral affect reflected no clear emotional expression. Coding was conducted frame-by-frame and used in sensitivity tests to rule out contributions from lower-level affective behaviors (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Attentional engagement behaviors

Attentional engagement vs. disengagement

Attentional engagement was quantified using manual video coding and was defined as moments when the infant was engaged in any of the behaviors coded above. Periods of disengagement were moments when the infant was not engaged in any of the above behaviors and were confined to the intervals preceding the first interaction and following the last and thus occurred mainly at the beginning and end of the play periods.

Segmentation of sustaining vs. waning engagement types

To define types of infant attentional engagement, we identified periods of sustaining and waning attentional engagement, inspired by a protocol for creative exploration in adults44. A segmentation algorithm was used to identify periods of sustaining and waning attentional engagement during attentional engagement for each play period (pre & post maternal demonstration (Fig. 2b). In this framework, the duration of consecutive attentional behaviors reflects fluctuations in attentional investment. Over time, as familiarity increases and interest declines, decreasing behavioral durations reflect a gradual attenuation of attentional focus and engagement. Following the algorithmic order and logic of existing work44, the segmentation algorithm first identifies sequences of coded attentional behaviors with the objects that were consecutively monotonically decreasing in duration, which are categorized as waning engagement. Sequences of these attentional behaviors that did not meet this criterion, and were thus instead occurring with monotonically increasing durations, were categorized as sustaining engagement.

For example, an infant could engage in the following sequence of attentional behaviors: pressing a cube for 5.35 s, banging a ball for 10.84 s, banging a cube for 15.98 s, mouthing a ball for 7.43 s, and squeezing a cube for 4.01 s. Following Hart and colleagues (2017), behaviors forming a monotonically decreasing sequence of durations (e.g., [15.98, 7.43, and 4.01] seconds) were classified as waning engagement, with a total duration of 27.42 s. The remaining sequence of durations ([5.35, 10.84] seconds) were categorized as sustaining engagement, with a total duration of 16.18 s. For each infant, we calculated the moments and total frequency of the sustaining and waning engagement, and the total duration spent in each phase engagement. Sustaining attentional engagement could indicate deepening engagement with the toys while waning engagement could indicate decreasing interest in or higher distractibility from toy engagement. The distribution of the types of attentional engagement behaviours observed across infants can be found in the supplement (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Windows surrounding attention behavior transitions

In addition to infants’ behavior, we quantified transitions between their behaviors during the task (Fig. 4c). Binary markers of transitions between attention engagement and disengagement were coded as the moments during which the behavior differs from the behavior coded at the previous second. 33% of transitions were from engagement to disengagement at the end of the session, while 67% were from disengagement to engagement at the beginning of the session. We also created binary markers of transitions between the sustaining and waning attentional engagement.

To quantify autonomic dynamics over time with respect to behavior transitions, we marked windows of time surrounding each of the transitions. We created binary codes each representing windows of different sizes, centered at and inclusive of the behavioral transitions. The window sizes were as follows: 3 s (1 on each side of the transition), 5 s (2 on each side of the transition), 7 s (3 on each side of the transition), 9 s (4 on each side of the transition), and 11 s (5 on each side of the transition) seconds. To conduct sensitivity analyses, we created surrogate random “transitions” (n = 1084) and repeated the same process of creating variables that represent different sized windows surrounding the surrogate transitions. These sensitivity tests find that recurrence metrics within random windows of equivalent size are not significantly different from outside of those windows (p’s > 0.30).

Heart rate physiology

Electrocardiogram (ECG) data was recorded from infants using a Brain Products LiveAmp system at a sampling rate of 500 Hz, employing a modified Lead I configuration with recording electrodes placed below the left and right clavicles. The ECG data were synchronized to the video data using an in-house synchronisation solution, more details of which can be found in45. ECG data were processed to extract inter-beat interval (IBI) series using Kubios HRV Scientific (version 4.1.2, Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland). Automatic artefact correction, which detects artefacts based on differences between successive RR intervals, was selected for superior accuracy over manual threshold methods and the automatic noise detection was set to “Strong.” Data quality was high across participants (N = 80): beats corrected percentage = mean 1.72% (range 0%–4.92%) pre-demonstration, mean 1.76% (range 0%–6.55%) post-demonstration; percentage of noisy segments in the data = mean 3.66% (range 0%–32.10%) pre-demonstration, mean 2.99% (range 0%–17.42%) post demonstration. Trained researchers subsequently manually corrected remaining artefacts, missing peaks, or noise segments. Because the resulting IBI data do not technically constitute a time series, the intervals were spline interpolated at 4 Hz to create a continuous, uniformly sampled time series (Fig. 3a), consistent with established guidelines46–48.

(a) Inter-beat-interval (IBI) series were prepared from the ECG recording by resampling to 4 Hz and interpolating missingness in the middle of the data, according to standard practice. (b) We then estimated optimal delayed-embedding parameters and reconstructed the phase space from the IBI series (D = 5, L = 10 samples or 2.5 s here shown in red dashed lines). (c) Lastly, recurrence plots (using max-norm, fixed radius) with two Theiler window settings were created for the state and trait analyses. Trait analysis: we compute RQA on the global RP with w = 20 (exclude |Δt| ≤ 20 samples ≈ 5 s) to remove the timescale captured by the 5-s state windows, isolating longer-range interactions and keeping trait RQA independent of the state analysis.

ECG missingness

RQA requires a continuous autonomic time-series without missing values. Given the near absence of discussion or guidance on missingness with respect to RQA in existing literature and importance of this topic for developmental work, we conducted a-priori data-driven analyses to guide our decision-making. These indicated that recurrence metrics may be sensitive to even small amounts (as absolute value and as a percentage) of missingness within a time-series, in addition to the time-series’ length. Missing data at the start or end of the time-series are not bracketed by observations and filling them would require extrapolation rather than interpolation which can potentially distort the heart-rate dynamics. As such, only the missingness in the middle of the time-series was considered for interpolation. We examined the effects of interpolating missing values on recurrence metrics as a function of the method of interpolation (linear, cubic, and spline) and the amount of data interpolated (from 10 samples or 2.5 s to 100 samples or 25 s) within these test time-series (not at the very beginning or very end of the series). A total of 41 time-series with no missing data served as test cases for this analysis. A grid search determined that interpolating continuous segments of missingness longer than 40 units (10 s) resulted in a steep drop off in correlation in the RQA features computed (see Supplementary Fig. S4). Additionally, spline interpolation had the least mean-squared error compared to linear and cubic interpolations, when calculated relative to the features calculated from the fully clean time-series (Supplementary Fig. S4).

As such, time-series with missingness segments that were 40 sampling units or shorter (median length of missing = 35 samples, range = 12–313 samples) in the middle of the data were interpolated (Fig. 3c). In the current dataset, a total of 13 data samples (6 pre and 7 post maternal demonstration) exceeded the 40-sample threshold and were hence excluded. Consistent with conventional guidance49, we then excluded 1 additional infant’s time-series from pre-demonstration for > 20% overall missingness. The final analytic sample comprised data from 78 infants.

Recurrence Quantification Analysis (RQA) of heart rate

RQA is a nonlinear, non-parametric approach for quantifying recurrence, or repetitions in a system’s dynamics. We first summarize the approach here before providing further detail in the following section (see also Fig. 3). First a phase space representation was constructed, which describes all possible states of a given multidimensional system (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, a recurrence plot quantifies and visualizes the degree of recurrence, or values that have recurred previously in the time-series (Fig. 3c). Lastly, in accordance with existing literature, we computed three key recurrence metrics (e.g., RR, DET, LAM) to characterize the recurring states (Fig. 1b). Existing work24 provides more detail on implementing RQA and guidance on parameter selection. The Python NeuroKit2 (v0.2.11) toolbox was used for parameter selection, and the MATLAB crp toolbox50 was used for creating the recurrence plots, with code adapted from the casnet51 package for calculating local recurrence metrics.

This is a symmetrical RP as we are looking at auto-recurrence of the time-series. The line of identity (grey) is typically ignored during computations. The recurring points are the RP squares in green, with example recurring points exhibiting determinism (DET; orange) and laminarity (LAM; purple). We note that the points that constitute LAM and DET are not mutually exclusive as a given value could be part of both a sequence of a repeating pattern (DET) and a repeating state (LAM). (b) The toy example reflects a simplified discrete, 3-state time-series of letters (ABABCBB), but the same concepts can be applied to continuous data (e.g., heart rate), as discussed in the Methods section. In this example, a recurring point reflects a recurring letter but for continuous signals, a recurring state can reflect repetition of the same value or a value nearby within a pre-defined radius. More details about how the recurrence metrics are derived from recurrence plots are provided in the methods section. In general, existing literature34,35 suggests that when derived from heart rate activity, these recurrence metrics are inversely related to PNS activity (and positively related to sympathetic nervous system activity).

Phase space reconstruction

In this approach, we assume that the measured heart rate time-series is only one dimension of the multi-dimensional complex autonomic system we wish to model. However, the interaction-dominant nature of the system can be leveraged to reconstruct its phase space from just a single observed time-series (Fig. 3b). Drawing on Takens’ Theorem52, time delay embedding is used to reconstruct a phase space that is topologically equivalent to the original space. This unfolds the observed heart rate time-series to extract information about the higher dimensions with which it interacts24. We evaluate the original time-series, find the lag (L) or point in the time-series at which the series begins to exhibit new information, and then create D-1 surrogate time series sampled beginning at the original time-series at lag L, or embeddings, that together with the original time-series constitute the D-dimensional system. Time-Series Lag (L). Rather than using average mutual information’s first local minimum - which can be unstable in short, noisy infant HRV series, the Rosenstein et al. (1993) “1/e autocorrelation” criterion53 was adopted. Specifically, the autocorrelation function was computed for each resampled IBI series and the first lag at which the autocorrelation fell to 1/e of its zero-lag value was identified. Across all participants, the median of such delay was 10 samples (2.5s at 4 Hz; see Supplementary Fig. S5), which we applied uniformly for comparative analyses24. This shorter, autonomically plausible L ensures that each reconstructed vector captures sufficiently decorrelated information without overextending into noisy future samples. The Python NeuroKit2 (v0.2.11) toolbox’s complexity_delay() function was used, and the argument “method” was set to “rosenstein1993.” System Dimension (D). The dimension of the system was determined via Cao’s false-nearest‐neighbors algorithm, which computes two statistics (E₁ and E₂) to distinguish true neighbors from those arising by projection54. Although some series suggested D = 4 or 6, the median optimal dimension across all infants was 5; we therefore fixed D = 5 for all analyses (see Supplementary Fig. S5). To test robustness, a surrogate‐data sensitivity check was performed to evaluate the embedding dimension by examining how determinism (DET) in the recurrence plot differed between the real IBI series and time-shuffled surrogates, across candidate dimensions D = 2 to 6 (with fixed delay L = 10). For each D, DET was computed on the original series and on a suite of surrogate series (n = 1084, randomly permuted in time), and we then quantified the separation DETorig​−DETsur​. D = 5 was found to be the smallest dimension at which this separation reached its maximum and plateaued – higher D values did not further increase the gap, ruling out artifact‐driven DET inflation (see Supplementary Fig. S6). Thus, D = 5 optimally captures genuine dynamical structure without over-embedding, and we fixed D = 5 for all RQA analyses. The Python NeuroKit2 (v0.2.11) toolbox’s complexity_dimension() function was used, and the argument “method” was set to “afnn.”

Recurrence plots

Recurrence plots are a graphical and quantitative representation of how often and the ways a system recurs or revisits its previous states (Fig. 3c). They are created from the reconstructed phase space using the above parameters and MATLAB’s CRP Toolbox function. With continuous systems, we first create a distance matrix whose entries represent the distance of each point to all others in phase space. The user is then required to specify a radius or threshold within which values of a system are considered to be recurring. A radius of 0.56 (pre-demo) and 0.50 (post-demo) in phase space were determined according to best practice55, of identifying the radius at which all time-series exhibit an overall recurrence rate (see below) of < 5%23,24,56.

The distance matrix is thresholded using the predetermined radius (see above) to determine which points are recurring. As is recommended, for each time-series, the distance matrix is first rescaled by the maximum distance in the matrix to normalize across different distance absolute values prior to creating the recurrence plots56. The recurrence plots then represent the patterns of recurring values within the physiology time-series, with each black square representing a value that reoccurs in the time-series (Fig. 3) and are created for each autonomic time-series.

Recurrence metrics

Next, the different recurrence metrics for quantifying and summarizing the patterns within the recurrence plots are identified with respect to their application to the autonomic nervous system (Fig. 1a). These metrics were selected as they are commonly used in previous literature34,35, and yielded a priori substantive interpretations with respect to heart rate physiology (Fig. 1b).

Recurrence Rate (RR) summarizes the overall recurrence of the system and represents the percentage of all values in the time-series that are recurring. This provides an indication of the extent to which autonomic dynamics demonstrate regularity with respect to its previous values over time, irrespective of temporal order. A high RR indicates that the system frequently returns to the same set of autonomic states.

Determinism (DET) reflects the percentage of all recurring points that form a diagonal line excluding the line of identity, indicative of a repeating pattern or consecutive sequence of different values. This provides an indication of the extent to which the repeating values are structured and recur with a similar local evolution57. High DET could reflect a highly structured system with stable patterns of variation, such as patterns of increasing or decreasing in values. A system with high DET is more predictable and regular over time, but the patterns exhibited may be complex.

Laminarity (LAM) indicates the percentage of recurring points that form a vertical or horizontal line, indicating repetition of the exact same value consecutively in a time-series. This is another indicator of similar local evolution, only this metric captures the extent to which a system persists in one particular state. High LAM could reflect a highly structured system with some autoregression, hysteresis, or inertia which may in turn indicate inflexibility or lower responsiveness.

Following the guidelines in literature29,58, the minimum line length considered for both diagonal (for DET) and horizontal/vertical lines (for LAM) was set to 2 recurring points in the RP. While we note that this is the minimum number of points required to form a line, this was decided in order to sensitively quantify the underlying dynamics, given the nature of the data (short time-series) and the types of analyses in this study (state/local vs. global/trait).

Levels of analysis

Lastly, the recurrence metrics were calculated at two levels of analysis and timescales:

Trait. To understand how patterns in physiology evolve over longer timescales and differ across individuals, global recurrence quantification was conducted to obtain values for RR, LAM and DET over the course of the entire time-series. Here, we calculate recurrence metrics from the recurrence plot excluding the recurrence points along the main diagonal (as is typical) as well as 20 units on either side (theiler = 20) in order to capture longer-term patterns (Fig. 3c) and omit influences from the shorter time windows employed in the state analyses (below).

State. To understand how the patterns in physiology evolve dynamically over short timescales, state recurrence quantification was conducted for RR, DET, and LAM (Fig. 3c). Given the established complementary approaches of capturing total autonomic values and variation relative to individual means, we evaluated both total levels of state recurrence metrics as well as person-mean-centered values. Code adapted from the casnet package in R51 was used for these analyses. Here, we calculate recurrence metrics from the recurrence plot using overlapping 5-second (20 units) windows with a step size of 1 s (4 units), excluding the main diagonal (theiler = 1)24,57,59–61 (Figs. 3c and 4b). The 5-second windows are inclusive, meaning that they correspond to seconds 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 (and the next window starts at 2 and extends through 6).

In aligning state RQA metrics with behavior (Fig. 4 d), the goal was to account for the various timescales inherent in our processing pipeline and analyses (as summarized in Fig. 3). Recall that behavior is sampled from the video recordings at each second. However, the state RQA timescale is more complex as it results from calculations in the phase space that was constructed using time-delay embedding. The phase space is constructed using the original time-series as well as 4 (D-1) embeddings derived from the original time-series at different lags. Per Taken’s Theorem, we created the 4 embeddings by lagging the original time-series by 2.5 s (10 sampling units at 4 Hz) for each new embedding. As such, any given time point in the phase space reflects information at that time point as well as up to 10 (4 × 2.5) seconds in the future of the original time-series and there is independent information in the phase space only every 10 s. 5-second windows along the global RP were used to calculate the state RQA. Given the 10-second future lag from the delay embedding, this means that each 5-second window on the RP is really a 15-second window from the original time-series, before the onset of totally independent information. As such, in aligning behavior with the 5-second window of RP, we selected the behavior at the center of the 15-second window, which corresponds to second 8 (midpoint of 15 = 8, seconds inclusive) for the first window, to make sure that the behavior represents the full window of time that the phase-embedded vector from that window corresponds to and to avoid implying any form of causality60. As a result, the behavior-RQA mapping begins at 8 s and each subsequent behavior is mapped to the state RQA value from the window centered 5 (midpoint of 5-second RQA window = 3 s, then behavior at 8 s-3 s = 5 s) seconds prior.

Statistical analysis

Trait RQA

Trait-level associations were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) in R (), with random intercepts for participant (ID) to accommodate the repeated-measures structure (two time-series per infant: pre and post maternal demonstration). Two-sided p-values were obtained with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; 95% Wald confidence intervals are reported. All models included the following covariates: Age (z-scored) and Country (Brazil/Singapore) were included because the recruitment differed across sites, producing different age distributions (Singapore: M = 16.38 months, SD = 4.81, n = 32; Brazil: M = 11.88 months, SD = 2.13, n = 45); pre/post demonstration (play-session): adjusts for the within-infant repeated measurement; and data length (embedded vector sampling units): recurrence metrics are sensitive to series length, so we controlled for it in all models. Upon inspecting the distributions of the trait recurrence metrics (Supplementary Fig. S7) , RR required log transformation to reduce right skew, prior to fitting in the models. RRlog, DET and LAM were subsequently fitted in the models as the dependent variable. All continuous variables were z-scored for better model stability and coefficient interpretation. Three different types of models were designed for this study:

Engagement Model:

Trait RQA metric (RRlog /DETz /LAMz ) ~ zTotal_Attentional_Engagement + data_length + PrePost + Country + zAge + (1 | ID).

Temperament Model:

Trait RQA metric (RRlog/DETz/LAMz) ~ zTotal_Attentional_Engagement + zIBQ_Regulation_Scores + data_length + PrePost + Country + zAge + (1 | ID).

Negative-Arousal Control Model:

Trait RQA metric (RRlog/DETz/LAMz)~ Total_Attentional_Engagement + znegative_emotion_duration + PrePost + Country + data_length + zAge + (1|ID).

Note:

As an additional robustness check, we replaced Total_Attentional_Engagement with the proportion of sustaining-engagement periods or “Sustaining engagement proportion” in the Engagement Model (Supplementary Tables S11 – S13).

These models test links between autonomic dynamics and (a) attentional engagement (primary), (b) temperamental regulation, and (c) negative emotion (arousal-related control), while adjusting for development, site, and data-quality factors. Fixed-effects were estimated with lmerTest (Satterthwaite df); we report two-sided p-values and 95% Wald CIs. Since age and country were included as covariates, we report their estimates briefly in the Supplementary information rather than in the main results (Supplementary Fig. S8).

State RQA

Statistical analyses were conducted in R using linear mixed effects models in the lme4 package, allowing intercepts to vary by person. Given the expected heavy right tail of the RR distribution, we log transformed state RR prior to fitting models (after setting 0 s to 0.001 and 1 s to 0.99)62. The following covariates were included: time in seconds since the beginning of the session (given that recurrence metrics are not stationary over the course of the time-series and exhibit linear increases [RR B = 0.00084, p < 0.001; LAM B = 0.00022, p < 0.001; DET B = 0.000050, p = 0.00060]), total length of the time-series used to create the recurrence plot (to account for the fact that longer time-series provide more opportunity for recurrence), number of transitions between waning and sustaining attention types (to account for individual differences in transition propensity), pre vs. post (whether the time-series was prior to or following the maternal demonstration), behavior phase duration (to account for how long each sustaining/waning phase lasts). Country and infant age were tested as covariates for the state-level models but ultimately not included due to their non-significance (p’s > 0.40).

State RQA metric (RR/DET/LAM) ~ time + time-series length + pre/post demo + n transitions + behavior + eng/diseng transition window + sus/wan transition window + (1|ID).

Results

We tested hypotheses of lower autonomic recurrence metrics (RR, LAM, DET): (a) at the trait level, being associated with increased infant attentional engagement and higher infant regulatory abilities; and (b) at the state level, showing dynamic modulation in tandem with changes in the infant’s state of multimodal attentional engagement. To do so, we quantified infants’ autonomic recurrence dynamics at the trait and state level and examined associations with their multimodal attentional engagement behavior.

Autonomic recurrence and infant attentional engagement

At the trait level, linear mixed effects models revealed that lower autonomic recurrence was significantly associated with greater overall time spent attentionally engaged for DET and LAM metrics, but not for RR (Fig. 5a-c). Specifically, higher multimodal attentional engagement was significantly related to reduced DET (B = −0.27, p < 0.001) and LAM (B = −0.26, p = 0.001), but not RR (B = −0.05, p = 0.504). Robustness checks revealed that the relations between attentional engagement and DET (B = −0.23, p = 0.013) as well as LAM (B = −0.23, p = 0.013) persisted even after accounting for infant fussiness (indexed by negative emotion duration during the task). Model results can be found in the Supplementary Tables S2 - S14.

Trait Recurrence Metrics in Relation to Attentional Engagement Behavior and Self-Regulation. Note. On average, lower trait levels of autonomic recurrence were related to more time spent in attentional engagement for DET (a) and LAM (b) but not RR (c). On average, lower trait levels of autonomic recurrence was related to higher parent-reported infant regulatory capacity scores for DET (d) and LAM (e) but not RR (f).

Autonomic recurrence and parent-reported infant self-regulation

Parent-reported infant regulation, as measured by the Orienting/Regulation dimension of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire (IBQ-R), was also significantly associated with infant trait level recurrence (Fig. 5d-f). Specifically, infants rated higher in Orienting/Regulation exhibited lower trait DET (B = −0.28, p = 0.041) and LAM (B = −0.21, p = 0.042) but no association was observed with RR (B = −0.01, p = 0.88). Supplementary analyses indicated that the findings were unchanged when restricting the sample to infants aged 12 months and below (typical range for IBQ administration), supporting the robustness of the results (see Supplementary Table S6).

At the state level, linear mixed effects models similarly revealed lower autonomic recurrence during moments of multimodal attention engagement compared to moments of disengagement, after accounting for data length, number of transitions between engagement types, and time in seconds since the start of the session. Further, moments when infants were attentionally engaged, whether sustaining or waning, were related to lower recurrence relative to moments of attentional disengagement, for all metrics: DET (Bwan_v_diseng= −0.25, p < 0.001; Bsus_v_diseng= −3.45, p < 0.001), RR (Bsus_v_diseng= −0.14, p < 0.001; Bwan_v_diseng= −0.16, p < 0.001), and LAM (Bsus_v_diseng= −0.0056, p < 0.001; Bwan_v_diseng= −0.0062, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6a).

(a) Boxplots show that all state recurrence metrics were significantly lower during attention engagement (comprising sustaining and waning types of engagement) compared to disengagement. (b) Graphical depictions of the 7-, 9- and 11-second windows, centered upon transitions in and out of (left) attentional engagement and (right) sustaining/waning engagement, within which state recurrence metrics exhibited significant differences relative to points outside of the windows. The colors represent the recurrence metric RR (green), DET (orange), and/or LAM (purple) whose values with different within those windows relative to moments outside of those windows. With respect to engagement/disengagement transitions (left), the metrics were lower within windows of different sizes. With respect to sustaining/waning transitions, DET was lower in all windows while LAM was higher only within the largest 11-second window.

Autonomic recurrence and temporal dynamics of multimodal attentional engagement

At the trait level, the proportion of time spent in either sustaining or waning multimodal attention relative to total attentional engagement (sustaining/(sustaining + waning)) was unrelated to any trait recurrence metric (DET: B = −0.03, p = 0.683; LAM: B = −0.02, p = 0.754, RR: B = -0.13, p = 0.071). Model results can be found in the Supplementary Tables S12 - S14.

However, state-level analyses revealed that autonomic recurrence metrics were indeed sensitive to transitions in attention. Specifically, recurrence was lower surrounding transitions between multimodal attention engagement and disengagement (Fig. 6a). Within 9-second windows surrounding transitions (4 s on either side), there was lower RR (B = −0.067, p = 0.0036), DET (B = −0.016, p = 0.017), and LAM (B = −0.017, p = 0.047) compared to all other time points. RR (B = −0.063, p = 0.013) and DET (B = −0.0016, p = 0.026) were also lower in the 7-second windows surrounding transitions, and all metrics were also lower in the 11-second windows surrounding these transitions. Model results can be found in the Supplementary Tables S15 - S17.

Further, state-level DET (B = −0.0098, p = 0.0067) was lower for sustaining relative to waning attentional engagement, and state level RR (B = 0.024, p = 0.042) was higher for sustaining relative to waning attention (Fig. 6a). LAM was not sensitive to these attentional types (p = 0.17). State-level results also suggest significant recurrence changes associated with transitions between these two attentional types (Fig. 6b). DET (B = −0.0010, p = 0.0048) was significantly lower in the 5-sec windows surrounding transitions, while LAM was significantly higher in the 11-second windows (B = 0.0074, p = 0.0038) surrounding transitions, compared to all other time points. Model results can be found in the Supplementary Tables S13 - S15.

Discussion

In summary, this study tested hypotheses that lower autonomic recurrence metrics (a) at the trait level, would be associated with increased infant attentional engagement and higher infant regulatory abilities; and (b) at the state level, would show dynamic modulation in tandem with changes in the infant’s state of multimodal attentional engagement. Together, study results suggest that across both trait and state levels, decreases in autonomic recurrence are consistently associated with higher multimodal attentional engagement in infants. Specifically, at the trait level, lower autonomic recurrence metrics (LAM and DET) are associated with greater attentional engagement across the task duration and with higher parent-reported regulatory capacities of the infants. At the state level, lower autonomic recurrence metrics (LAM, RR and DET) are associated with moments of attentional engagement compared to disengagement, as well as with transitions in and out of attentional engagement. Further, differential profiles of state autonomic recurrence are noted for sustaining compared to waning attentional engagement (lower DET, higher RR) and surrounding their transitions (higher LAM, lower DET). We contextualize our findings within two general categories of explanation. We first consider the recurrence metrics collectively in relation to PNS activity. We then consider interpretation of the different metrics with respect to the structure of autonomic dynamics.

Recurrence metrics inversely related to PNS activity which supports attention in infancy

Findings from adult experiments and pharmacological manipulation in animals report relative decreases in heart rate recurrence metrics associated with increases in PNS activity34,35. These studies suggest that lower recurrent dynamics allow for more PNS contributions to the heart rate output, while sympathetic responses produce more recurrent, stereotyped dynamics. Accordingly, it is possible that our results suggest lower recurrence is indicative of increased PNS activity which is associated with attentional engagement at the state and trait level.

Our findings of increased PNS activity associated with attentional engagement align with two bodies of existing research. First, research capturing RSA in infancy, an indirect measure of PNS activity, finds that trait-level average10–12 and moment-by-moment1415, increases in RSA are associated with greater emotion regulation63, focused attention18, and social engagement10–12,14. Conversely, in stressful contexts, infants tend to decrease RSA to reduce PNS input, allowing for sympathetic mobilization10,13,64,65. A second line of existing work focuses on general cardiac changes underlying different attentional states and their associations with different forms of information processing in infancy. This work finds lowered heart rate during sustained attention16,17,19–21 consistent with increased PNS activity during sustained attention relative to inattention19. Building on these two lines of work, the present findings concur in finding that decreased recurrence metrics may reflect both longer term and transient increases in PNS contributions that are important for multimodal attentional engagement.

Recurrence indicators of flexible autonomic organization

In addition to their relations with PNS contributions, recurrence metrics reflect information about the temporal organization of the autonomic system. A system characterized by overall high recurrence metrics has values that recur often and do so in a highly structured manner. This indicates a highly rigid system with frequent repeating values and predictable patterns that persist. Conversely, a flexible system would have overall low recurrence metrics, would tend to occupy new physiological states more frequently, and to do so in a less repetitive manner. Although this study does not explicitly quantify complexity, this interpretation aligns with theories linking physiological complexity to system flexibility and adaptability22. Accordingly, the decreased recurrence observed here indicates a more flexible autonomic system state, which permits more efficient recruitment and sustenance of attention across varying input modalities and attentional targets. It is possible that this increased flexibility, or complexity, could reflect changes in the coupling of the PNS and sympathetic nervous system, or other shifts in the contributions to heart rate dynamics rather than a simple increase in parasympathetic contributions alone. Below we consider how recurrence metrics as a class suggest a role for autonomic flexibility in attention before considering how each metric may capture a different aspect of autonomic flexibility.

Flexibility underlying attentional engagement

At the trait level, infants who have less structured patterns of recurrence in their autonomic activity spend more time attentionally engaged with objects. More specifically, those whose autonomic recurrence forms fewer consecutive patterns (DET) and persist less in the same consecutive value (LAM), spend more time engaged with objects. At the trait level, recurrence metrics are calculated with respect to the entire play period, providing indicators of long-term patterns of autonomic activity. A system that recurs less is less determined by any of its previous states and thus may be more attuned and responsive to shifts in both internal and external demands. For instance, whether a system recurs at a given time step is less tightly governed by how it recurred at the previous time steps. This could leave the system more responsive to shift its activity to meet ongoing metabolic needs. In the case of the present free play task, lower recurrence could indicate an autonomic nervous system that is locally adjusting at the level of seconds to support the focused cognitive resources required to engage with the different objects or to transition between types of object engagement. At the state level, during moments of attentional engagement, infant physiology recurs less often with respect to the points in the immediate past and when it does, the recurring points are less likely to persist unproductively in the same state. Here, a system that is less determined by its immediate past states may be more attuned to small shifts in attentional behaviors. Further, surrounding behavior transitions, any recurring points are less likely to form a pattern, consistent with more flexible patterns supporting transient shifts in attentional behaviors.

Some limited existing literature has examined physiological flexibility though quantifying autonomic variability, often measured using standard deviation of heart rate variability signals unfolding within individuals. These studies, most often conducted at the trait level, consider system activity to be analogous to a thermostat, whereby a system flexibly adjusts its state to move above and below a baseline level to adaptively engage with the environment. In general, findings from young children suggest that higher within-person variability, interpreted as difficulties maintaining homeostasis, are indicative of regulatory difficulties in the absence of environmental challenges, such as during free play and social interactions63,66–68. It is challenging to draw strong comparisons between this existing work and our recurrence metrics findings. We note that there is a conceptual difference between overall variability in a signal and variability in the patterns of how the signal recurs. However, our findings suggest that a physiological system which is recurring in a way that allows for more diverse patterns to occur over time might better support infant attention and regulation.

Other existing work examining the organization of autonomic activity using different methods finds positive associations between heart rate complexity and active regulation in the preschool age66, and more chaotic cardiac signals for children with ADHD, who have persistent challenges with attentional control, in relation to typical controls69. Similarly, some directly interpret a decrease in recurrence as an increase in complexity of the system34. Together, these findings and ours suggest that more complex cardiac signals at the trait level may be associated with attentional engagement at multiple timescales.

Recurrence metrics capture different facets of flexibility

Each of the recurrence metrics represents a different quantification of the recurrence patterns in heart rate signals, and thus each represents a distinct feature of autonomic dynamics. Our findings concur with this as the different recurrence metrics do not yield identical and redundant results.

At the state level, we report that different profiles of recurrence metrics are associated with different types of multimodal attentional engagement. When infants are engaged in a period of sustaining attention (that is, when the different coded attentional behaviors are sequentially increasing in duration), they exhibit relatively higher RR but lower DET. This means that, within 5 s windows, their physiology is recurring to previous states more often but doing so in a less patterned or stereotyped manner compared to when they are in a period of waning attention. This finding suggests that while the recurrence metrics might collectively indicate increased PNS contributions and underlying flexibility, these metrics can also capture more nuanced temporal hallmarks that distinguish different modes of attentional behavior.

Taking our findings together, we observe that DET, or the extent to which recurring points recur in patterns, is most consistently associated with multimodal attentional engagement across timescales and behaviors. At the longer timescales, lower DET is associated with more time spent attentionally engaged and with better parent-reported regulation. At the state level, lower DET is associated with moments of attentional engagement compared to disengagement and to periods of sustaining relative to waning attention. DET is also consistently lower in the windows surrounding behavior transitions. This suggests that the stereotyped patterning indexed by DET may reflect an idling or stagnant form of rigidity in the system that is lowered during attentional engagement. This, combined with the consistency of DET findings in existing literature, suggests that DET might be a consistent indicator of autonomic flexibility relevant for attention at multiple timescales.

Across our findings, RR exhibits mixed sensitivity across scales and behaviors. At the trait level, we did not find any associations between time spent attentionally engaged or infants’ regulation and RR. Here we note that, commensurate with past work and as is recommended to enhance comparability, we restricted the range of RR within the recurrence plot to be between 1 and 5 for the entire sample, lowering the total amount of RR variability at the trait level. However, at the state level, RR is calculated in moving windows across the recurrence plot, allowing for more variability. Accordingly, at the state level, RR was significantly lower during moments of attentional engagement compared to disengagement and surrounding their transitions but higher during sustaining compared to waning attention. While sustaining attention, physiology seems more likely to recur but less likely to do so in a pattern. This is interesting because during sustaining attention, infants are spending longer and longer periods engaging with the objects in diverse ways, which could elicit similarly more recurring physiological patterns compared to waning attention for the same object.

LAM appears to be most sensitive to overall duration of attentional engagement versus disengagement across timescales rather than to specific forms of attention. LAM is consistently associated with attentional engagement at both the state and trait levels, suggesting that simple state persistence is globally related to lower attentional engagement overall.

Methodological strengths

The non-linear RQA approach draws on strengths from the existing literature. Similar to heart rate studies, we model the full state space of IBIs likely capturing more general autonomic dynamics. Similar to studies using RSA-based measures of flexibility, we quantify recurrence, a metric of temporal autonomic organization. Our findings align with prior work reporting positive associations between PNS activity and focused attention as well as with studies employing nonlinear methods for studying autonomic dynamics that report increases in complexity associated with more focused attention14,16,19. In contrast, studies using frequentist measures of dynamics such as standard deviation report higher variability associated with more difficulties engaging63,67,68.

Findings from this study extend the literature on heart rate-defined periods of attention17,19. In addition to periods of attentional engagement being marked by lower heart rate, the present study finds attentional engagement associated with lower recurrence in the system’s state space. Importantly, our analytic approach differs from what has been done previously, with respect to the assumptions imposed upon the data as well as the dimensionality of the system under analysis. Additionally, relating recurrence metrics to variability measured through traditional parametric measures is challenging. While a lower recurrence in the system can be conceptualized as greater system variability, parametric measures such as standard deviation capture variability from a single observed dimension of the system under normality assumptions. By contrast, the RQA method captures patterns of recurrence within a multidimensional state space and does not make assumptions about the distribution of the data. Of note, it is not possible to identify the specific biological contributors to these other dimensions from this technique. Although we cannot determine the exact sources of the multiple dimensions that contribute to the autonomic phase space, this approach enables us to model the physiological signal with more biological validity, as one of many dimensions of a system interacting together. Further, we know that physiological dynamics are the output of multiple coordinated non-linear systems ranging from the autonomic and central nervous systems to endocrine, genetic, and exogenous influences. This approach assumes that all these systems contribute to, but do not map onto in a one-to-one fashion, the multi-dimensional phase space whose patterns are captured in the present study. Additionally, given that RQA allows the user to customize which regions of the RP are sampled from to calculate recurrence metrics over the long and short terms (using the Theiler window as detailed in Fig. 3 c), the present study’s measures of trait and state recurrence are independent of one another, offering another unique advantage.

Limitations and future studies

The present study also carries several limitations and tradeoffs. Given our interpolation strategy, we could not include some data at the start/end of the times-series, reducing the representation of disengagement in our analysis. Additionally, in this work, we calculate RQA using phase space reconstruction, an established technique for identifying the multiple dimensions of a given measured system. However, a challenge of this approach is aligning multiple different timescales owing to the construction of the phase space using surrogate copies of the original time-series. As detailed in the Methods section, we leverage several strategies for accommodating the multiple timescales and relating the resulting recurrence metrics to unfolding of behavior in real time. Future work replicating these findings in larger samples of diverse infants will be important. Another limitation of the phase space reconstruction approach is that we are unable to interpret the individual reconstructed dimensions in a substantive manner. While they represent the dimensions of the system, they do not easily map to deconstructed components of the system. Future work could benefit from comparisons across different heart rate indices, including RQA, RSA, entropy, to understand their interrelations and contributions to early cognition.

Since the focus of the current study is on infants’ autonomic dynamics and how this relates to attentional engagement, effects of maternal interaction were not explored. Future studies could examine the autonomic dynamics of both infants and their mothers in tandem using techniques such as Cross Recurrence Quantification Analysis to quantify physiological synchrony. This study comprises typically developing infants and it would be interesting for future studies to explore the effect of atypical development on autonomic dynamics and attentional engagement.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using RQA, we have identified signature profiles of autonomic recurrence underlying multimodal attentional engagement in infancy. At the trait level, we find that decreased recurrence is associated with more overall time spent attentionally engaged and with higher parent reports of regulation. At the state level, we similarly find that decreased recurrence is positively associated with moments of attentional engagement. Further, transitions between types of attentional behaviors (sustaining versus waning) are associated with differential profiles of recurrence. We suggest that decreased autonomic recurrence reflects relative increases in PNS activity and a more flexible autonomic nervous system that support infant attentional engagement. Findings offer a novel window into how autonomic dynamics support the development of attentional engagement and control during infancy.
