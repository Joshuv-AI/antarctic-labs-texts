A dynamic protein corona substitution strategy for activatable imaging-guided synergistic theranostics in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Xueluer Mu, Yajie Li, Shuyi Wang, Haoze Fu, Min Gao, Yingxi Lu, Xianfeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), exemplified by Trodelvy®, face significant limitations in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) therapy due to protein corona-induced targeting inefficiency and accelerated drug resistance. Herein, we present a dynamic protein corona substitution (PCS) strategy that utilizes tumor microenvironmental cues to circumvent ADC limitations while enabling phototherapy-augmented chemotherapy. Our nanoplatform, CySC, is engineered from disulfide-bridged Cy-OH and camptothecin (CPT), which can self-assemble into nanoparticles (NPs) without auxiliary molecules and undergo programmed biotransformation upon tumor accumulation. Glutathione (GSH)-triggered drug release synergizes with endogenous albumin recruitment, generating albumin-cloaked AC-Cy-OH NPs. This PCS-driven remodeling achieves real-time tumor monitoring with activated near-infrared (NIR)-I&II fluorescence, promotes tumor penetration through albumin-mediated transcytosis, and reverses CPT resistance by prolonging intratumoral drug retention. The system enables spatiotemporally precise CPT delivery, synergizing chemotherapy with photothermal-photodynamic therapy (PTT/PDT) for amplified antitumor effects. This tumor microenvironment-responsive system demonstrates superior specificity and synergistic antitumor effects in TNBC models, establishing a paradigm for protein corona reprogramming for precision oncology.Keywords: Protein corona, Redox-responsive nanoparticles, Activatable theranostics, Triple-negative breast cancer, Synergistic chemo-phototherapy

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is considered to be the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer, characterized by poor differentiation, high invasiveness, and susceptibility to early recurrence or distant metastasis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. It is also notably resistant to endocrine and molecularly targeted therapies [5]. Consequently, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment for advanced or metastatic TNBC [6,7]. However, conventional chemotherapy is limited by systemic toxicity and the frequent emergence of drug resistance [8,9]. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) leverage the tumor-specificity of antibodies to selectively deliver highly toxic small molecule drugs to the tumor site, representing a next-generation “precision chemotherapy” weapon [[10], [11], [12]]. Sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy®, IMMU-132), an ADC developed by Immunomedics Co., utilizes a CL2A linker to conjugate the active metabolite of camptothecin (CPT), SN-38, to the monoclonal antibody hRS7. This innovative conjugation enables precise targeting and therapeutic delivery to tumor cells with high expression of Trop-2, a cell surface antigen. In 2020, Trodelvy® became the first FDA-approved Trop-2-targeted therapeutic agent, primarily indicated for third-line treatment of metastatic TNBC [13]. Despite promising clinical efficacy, ADCs face challenges, including heterogeneous drug loading, unstable linkers, and susceptibility to protein interference [[14], [15], [16]]. Several studies have identified or hypothesized the formation of albumin-linker-drug complexes during in vivo or in vitro plasma studies of maleimide-containing ADCs, which negatively impact the therapeutic specificity, drug release control, drug efficacy, and overall clinical outcomes [[17], [18], [19]].

Antibody-conjugated nanoparticles (ACNPs) constitute a drug delivery system that combines antibodies with NPs, offering a solution to many of the stability and drug release control challenges inherent in traditional ADCs [[20], [21], [22]]. Unlike ADCs, in which the drug is directly conjugated to the antibody via a chemical linker, ACNPs load the drug onto the surface or core of NPs through physical or chemical means. This approach typically offers a higher drug loading capacity and allows for the formation of a protein corona that extends the drug's circulation time in the body, thereby reducing nonspecific drug release [[23], [24], [25]]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that the ability of NPs conjugated with antibodies, peptides, or aptamers to recognize specific receptors may be compromised upon the formation of the protein corona. This protein corona may obscure the binding sites of the antibody, reducing its ability to interact with tumor cell surface receptors and thus impairing specific recognition [26,27]. Additionally, the formation of the protein corona can alter the physicochemical properties of the NPs, such as surface charge and hydrophilicity, potentially interfering with their binding to target cells and ultimately decreasing the efficiency of targeted drug delivery [[28], [29], [30]].

Conventional strategies to address protein corona challenges, primarily relying on passive stealth approaches like PEGylation or zwitterionic coatings, focus on suppressing nonspecific protein adsorption. However, this comes at the cost of compromised targeting efficacy and delayed drug release kinetics [[31], [32], [33], [34]], as these “stealth” strategies inevitably compromise the function of active targeting ligands and may hinder cellular uptake. This critical limitation underscores the need for a fundamental paradigm shift: from passively avoiding the protein corona to actively programming it into a theranostic platform. This study introduces an innovative dynamic protein corona substitution (PCS) strategy that embodies this shift. Instead of blanket suppression, our approach through molecular engineering aims to selectively manage corona formation. It first suppresses early albumin adsorption during systemic circulation, then actively leverages the tumor microenvironment to trigger surface remodeling and recruit endogenous albumin at the disease site. The design features a planar Cy7 photosensitizer core that mediates both nanoparticle self-assembly through π-π stacking and selective albumin binding. Incorporation of a disulfide-linked CPT chain creates a redox-sensitive switch that maintains nanostructure integrity while suppressing albumin adsorption in circulation. This active displacement prevents the binding of the albumin, allowing the corona to dissociate in a controlled manner in response to tumor-specific stimuli. These structural and functional elements are precisely integrated in CySC (Cy-OH-SS-CPT), a redox-responsive molecular conjugate where the planar Cy-OH core and CPT are covalently coupled via a disulfide linker (Fig. 1). Upon encountering elevated GSH concentrations in tumors, the system undergoes programmed transformation programmed transformation process. Disulfide cleavage triggers concurrent CPT release, conversion to albumin-corona-coated Cy-OH NPs (AC-Cy-OH NPs), and near-infrared (NIR)-I&II fluorescence recovery or turn-on. This coordinated process enables targeted drug delivery while providing optical feedback for treatment monitoring. This work establishes a new approach to precision nanomedicine by integrating stable nanostructure design with dynamic surface regulation and built-in theranostic capabilities.

Experimental section

Synthesis of CySC

Compound 10 (250.00 mg, 0.33 mmol), CPT (159.72 mg, 0.40 mmol), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl, 51.08 mg, 0.33 mmol), and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 6.72 mg, 0.06 mmol) were added to 25 mL of anhydrous dichloromethane under nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was stirred and allowed to react at room temperature for 10 h. After the reaction was complete, the reaction mixture was poured into 100 mL of ammonium chloride solution and extracted with 150 mL of dichloromethane. The extracted solution was purified by column chromatography using a dichloromethane:methanol (30:1 v/v) solvent system, yielding CySC in 52.1 % yield. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm): 8.42 (s, 1H), 8.21 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H), 8.17 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H), 7.92 (d, J = 8 Hz, 1H), 7.86 (m, 2H), 7.68 (m, 1H), 7.21 (s, 2H), 7.03 (t, 1H), 6.8 (d, J = 8 Hz, 1H), 6.34 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H), 5.75 (d, J = 12 Hz, 1H), 5.70 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H), 5.36 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H), 5.27 (s, 2H), 4.43 (m, 2H), 4.06 (m, 2H), 2.71 (t, 2H), 2.65 (t, 4H), 2.60 (t, 4H), 2.35 (m, 4H), 2.08 (m, 2H), 1.96 (m, 4H), 1.70 (s, 6H), 1.65 (S, 6H), 1.58 (m, 2H), 0.98 (m, 3H). 13C NMR (100 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm): 176.26, 173.28, 172.75, 171.81, 167.51, 157.42, 152.44, 148.82, 147.19, 146.37, 145.92, 144.27, 131.32, 130.81, 129.42, 122.70, 122.12, 120.04, 113.21, 112.43, 111.74, 111.23, 108.13, 96.59, 95.93, 67.09, 59.91, 53.38, 50.14, 47.32, 37.41, 37.11, 32.27, 32.19, 31.78, 29.69, 28.35, 27.01, 26.49, 26.25, 24.03, 23.70, 20.89. GSH-responsive property of CySC NPs

CPT release profiles from CySC NPs were investigated using pH 7.4 PBS (containing 30 % ethanol (v/v)) as the release medium. 15 μM of CySC was incubated with 0, 1 and 10 mM of GSH in 2 mL of the release medium at 37 °C. Samples were collected at time points of 0, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 24 h and directly analyzed by HPLC. The peak areas were recorded to calculate the percentage of compounds. The analysis was conducted using an Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 1260 HPLC system equipped with a DAD detector. The specific conditions were as follows: an Agilent C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 4.0 μm); mobile phase: 60 % methanol; flow rate: 1 mL/min; temperature: 20 °C; UV detection wavelength: 364 nm. To further explore the mechanism of GSH-triggered CPT release, ESI-MS was employed to monitor the molecular weight of CySC NPs after treatment with 10 mM GSH for 6 h, and the molecular-level process of CPT release was analyzed through the changes in molecular weight.

Photodynamic performance

To assess the generation of ROS, 1,4-diphenyl-2,3-benzofuran (DPBF) was employed as a ROS probe. A 50 μM solution of DPBF in water was prepared and transferred to a quartz cuvette. The solution was irradiated with a laser (808 nm, 0.5 W/cm2) for 1-min intervals, and the absorbance of DPBF was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer after each irradiation, for a total of 11 measurements. The decrease in DPBF absorption over time indicates the generation of ROS, as DPBF reacts with ROS, leading to a decrease in its absorption at 410 nm.

Molecular docking

The molecular structures of CySC and Cy-OH were constructed using GaussView 6.0 in conjunction with Autodock Tools (ADT). Geometry optimization for both molecules was carried out using the Density Functional Theory (DFT) method. The crystal structure of HSA (2BXP) was obtained from the RSCB database. After the ligand optimization, CySC or Cy-OH was used as the ligand, while the HSA structure, without the ligand, served as the receptor. Hydrogen atoms and partial charges were incorporated into the HSA structure to complete the macromolecular model. Docking simulations were performed to generate the top 30 binding configurations and calculate the corresponding binding energies. The 3D structures of the CySC-HSA and Cy-OH-HSA complexes were then visualized and analyzed using Autodock and PyMOL software.

Measurement of enhanced NIR fluorescence in cells

CySC NPs (10 μM) were incubated with 4T1 and L929 cells in a 37 °C incubator with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. After the incubation period, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any unbound nanoparticles. The cells were then imaged by confocal microscopy (LSCM). Fluorescence images were acquired at appropriate excitation and emission wavelengths to assess the fluorescence intensity of the cells. The same procedure was repeated for cells incubated with CyCC NPs, and images were captured for comparative analysis of the fluorescence levels.

Penetration in 3D tumor spheroids

For the in vitro penetration of CySC NPs in 3D tumor spheroids, the 4T1 tumor spheroids were generated by culturing single cells in fibrin gels. After the tumor spheroids grew to a diameter of about 150–200 μm, the spheroids were incubated with 5 μM Hoechst 33342 and 10 μM CySC NPs or CyCC NPs for 24 h at 37 °C. The tumor spheroids were washed with PBS three times. The images of fluorescence were acquired using Z-stack imaging at 30 μm intervals from the top to the middle of the spheroids using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Nikon C2+) at the excited wavelength of 405 nm and 640 nm.

Measurement of ROS in cells

2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) reagent was employed to detect the cellular accumulation of ROS. 4T1 cells were treated with 10 μM CySC NPs or CyCC NPs for 24 h, then incubated with H2DCF-DA (5 μM) for 1 h in a cell incubator at 37 °C. The fluorescence due to ROS was determined using a LSCM.

Caspase-3 protein activity assay

To assess Caspase-3 activity, 4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate. The following day, the cells were treated with either CySC NPs or CyCC NPs at a concentration of 20 μM for 24 h. For the groups undergoing NIR irradiation, the cell culture plate was exposed to an 808 nm laser with a power density of 1.0 W/cm2, applied from above of the plate. After the respective treatments, caspase-3 activity was measured using the Caspase 3 Activity Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China).

Animal model establishment

All procedures were performed after receiving approval from the Department of Science and Technology of Shandong Province and the Laboratory Animal Center of Qingdao Hao Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. Female Balb/c mice (4–6 weeks, 18–20 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed under pathogen-free conditions according to AAALAC guidelines. 75 μL of 4T1 cells (4 × 106) were implanting into the right hind hip in Balb/c mice. After about 6 d inoculation, the subcutaneous mouse tumor model was well built when the tumor volume reached to nearly 75 mm3.

In vivo real-time tumor imaging

CySC NPs or CyCC NPs (200 μg) were administered intravenously into 4T1 tumor-bearing nude mice through a tail vein. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane via inhalation. We registered the in vivo NIR-I fluorescent activity (λex = 740 nm/λem = 840 nm) with an IVIS lumina Series III in vivo imaging system and NIR-II fluorescent activity (λex = 808 nm/λem = 840 nm) with a Series III 900/1700 in vivo imaging system at different time intervals. Photoacoustic images were conducted for different time using LOIS-3D imaging system (TomoWave) with 740 nm laser.

Synergistic therapy of tumor in vivo

4T1 tumor-bearing nude mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) and then received different treatments, such as Saline, Saline + 808 nm, CyCC NPs, CyCC NPs + 808 nm, CySC NPs and CyCC NPs + 808 nm. The weight of mice and the tumor volume were measured once every 2 days during the treatment.

Temperature monitoring

During irradiation, tumor temperature was monitored using an infrared thermal camera (FLUKA TiS20). The final tumor temperature was calculated with n = 5 in each treatment group for average and standard deviation.

Apoptotic cell detection in tumor tissues

Tumor tissues were first fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C and then embedded in paraffin. Sections (5–10 μm thick) were prepared and deparaffinized with xylene, followed by rehydration through a graded alcohol series. For H&E staining, the tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin for 2–3 min, and then dehydrated and mounted. To detect apoptotic cells, TUNEL staining was performed by incubating the tissue sections with 50 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture (Roche) for 60 min at 37 °C, followed by counterstaining with DAPI for 5 min. For Ki-67 staining, the tissue sections were treated with 3 % H2O2 for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity and then incubated with primary anti-Ki-67 antibody at an appropriate dilution overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, followed by DAB chromogenic development. The stained sections were observed under an OLYMPUS IX73 microscope to assess apoptotic cells, proliferating cells, and overall tissue morphology.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and self-assembly of CySC

The synthesis route to CySC is illustrated in Scheme S1. First, the small molecule photosensitizer Cy-OH was synthesized and characterized using 1H NMR (Fig. S1). Then, Cy-OH and CPT are linked by an esterification reaction through a disulfide bond, forming the prodrug molecule CySC. The chemical structure of CySC was confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), 1H NMR, and 13C NMR (Figs. S2–S4). To assess the role of the GSH-cleavable disulfide linker in tumor-specific activation, a control compound with a non-cleavable linker, CyCC, was also synthesized and confirmed by HRMS, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR (Figs. S5–S7).

The self-assembly behavior of CySC was thoroughly investigated through spectroscopic and morphological analyses. This design principle differs fundamentally from previous redox-responsive systems by integrating both microenvironment sensing and real-time imaging feedback. The observed red shift (from 830 nm to 845 nm) in CySC's absorption spectrum when transitioning from organic solvent to aqueous solution, along with spectral broadening and reduced absorption intensity (Fig. 2a), suggests the occurrence of molecular aggregation in aqueous environments. Further evidence comes from fluorescence studies (Fig. 2b), where the fluorescence of CySC in aqueous solution is significantly quenched compared to that in acetonitrile, which is consistent with aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) behavior and indicates strong π-π stacking interactions among Cy-OH chromophores. These observations collectively verify the self-assembly of CySC into aggregated nanostructures in aqueous media. The formation of CySC aggregates was confirmed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). DLS analysis demonstrated that CySC NPs displayed a uniform hydrodynamic diameter of 94 ± 5 nm in aqueous solution (Fig. 2c). Notably, these NPs exhibited excellent colloidal stability, maintaining negligible size changes over a 24 h period in pure water at ambient temperature. TEM imaging further revealed that the CySC NPs were spherical in shape, with diameters ranging from 50 to 70 nm.

GSH-responsive activation of CySC NPs

The therapeutic effectiveness of prodrug systems depends critically on their activation kinetics in target tissues. CPT release kinetics were quantitatively analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). As shown in Fig. 2d, approximately 50 % and 90 % of CPT was released from CySC NPs within 12 h at GSH concentrations of 1 mM and 10 mM, respectively, demonstrating that the tumor's redox microenvironment facilitates significantly faster and more extensive drug release compared to normal physiological conditions. To understand the mechanism, the change in molecular weight was analyzed after incubating CySC NPs with 10 mM GSH-containing release media. Mass spectrometry revealed a shift in molecular weight from 1089.327 [M + H+] to 640.745 [Cy-SH + H+] and 561.452 [Cy-OH + Na+], suggesting that a disulfide bond-thiol bond exchange reaction occurred (Fig. S8). Subsequently, the generated thiol underwent intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization on the ester moiety, leading to the formation of a five-membered ring thiolactone. This process effectively promoted the release of Cy-OH.

Notably, the observed spectral broadening and reduced absorption intensity of Cy-OH in aqueous solution compared to its behavior in organic solvent (Fig. 2e) suggest that the released Cy-OH still exhibits aggregation behavior in water, similar to that of CySC. The size and morphology of Cy-OH NPs in aqueous solution were further investigated by DLS and TEM measurements. Compared to CySC NPs, Cy-OH NPs exhibited a higher ζ-potential of −20.50 ± 2.55 mV, reflecting the increased negative charge from the remaining Cy-OH molecules post-CPT release (Fig. S9). DLS measurements indicated that the average diameter of Cy-OH NPs was 125 nm, with no significant size change after 24 h of incubation in water, indicating good stability (Fig. 2f). TEM analysis confirmed the spherical morphology of Cy-OH NPs, with diameters ranging from 70 to 100 nm, consistent with DLS results. These findings collectively support that the released Cy-OH maintains its aggregation behavior in aqueous environments, demonstrating stability and consistent morphology over time.

To elucidate the regulatory role of GSH-responsive activation on the photothermal/photodynamic properties of CySC NPs, we compared the photothermal conversion efficiency and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation capabilities of CySC NPs in their pristine (pre-activation) and GSH-activated (Cy-OH NPs) states. After 10 min of 808 nm laser irradiation, Cy-OH NPs demonstrated a significantly higher temperature elevation, reaching 57.5 °C, compared to CySC NPs (44.9 °C), while pure water exhibited negligible heating (ΔT < 1.5 °C, Fig. S10). To evaluate the long-term stability of these NPs, a five-cycle heating and natural cooling test was carried out (Fig. 2g). Throughout the test cycles, both CySC NPs and Cy-OH NPs maintained stable heat-generation capabilities, strongly demonstrating their excellent photothermal stability. Additionally, the temperature increase of Cy-OH NPs was higher than that of CySC NPs in each cycle. Quantitative analysis revealed that Cy-OH NPs achieved an outstanding photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) as high as 57.9 %, significantly outperforming CySC NPs (38.2 %) and the non-cleavable control CyCC NPs (36.5 %) (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. S11). While the comparable PCE values of CySC and CyCC NPs confirm the inherent photothermal activity of the cyanine chromophore, the dramatically enhanced efficiency of Cy-OH NPs unequivocally demonstrates that the disassembly and albumin binding triggered by GSH are indispensable for achieving maximized photothermal efficacy. ROS production was assessed using DPBF under 808 nm irradiation. Cy-OH NPs demonstrated rapid DPBF decomposition under irradiation (Fig. S12), achieving an 8.2-fold higher ROS quantum yield (1.4 %) than CySC NPs (0.17 %, Fig. 2i). This marked difference confirms the tumor-specific activation mechanism: CySC NPs maintain a “PDT-off” state during circulation to minimize off-target effects, while GSH-triggered transformation into Cy-OH NPs enables precise spatiotemporal regulation of ROS generation at tumor sites, thereby synergistically enhancing photothermal therapeutic efficacy and localized photodynamic treatment outcomes.

Formation and characterization of albumin corona-coated Cy-OH NPs

The GSH-responsive disulfide linker is a well-established strategy in controlled drug delivery. While many studies have utilized it for triggered drug release, our system advances this concept by integrating it into a programmed cascade. The cleavage does not merely release CPT; it simultaneously initiates the structural transformation of the nanoparticle itself, leading to the exposure of the albumin-binding Cy-OH core. This design ensures that the release of the chemotherapeutic agent is intrinsically linked to the subsequent formation of the theranostic albumin corona, creating a multi-stage, coordinated activation process that goes beyond simple payload release.

The dynamic PCS strategy was implemented through rational design of CySC NPs, where the GSH-cleavable disulfide linker serves as a molecular switch controlling albumin interactions. Computational docking simulations using AutoDock provided critical structural insights for this design by revealing that the planar Cy-OH core binds HSA with high affinity (ΔG = −11.9 kcal/mol) through hydrophobic interactions in subdomain IIA (Fig. 3a), while conjugation with CPT via disulfide linkage creates steric hindrance at the IIIA-IB interface (ΔG = −5.6 kcal/mol), effectively masking the albumin-binding domains during circulation. This calculated binding energy difference of 6.3 kcal/mol formed the structural basis for the tumor microenvironment-responsive transition from stealth CySC to albumin-binding Cy-OH NPs upon GSH-triggered disulfide cleavage.

Spectroscopic analysis revealed that HSA binding induced significant alterations in Cy-OH's optical properties, exhibiting a 12 nm red-shift of the NIR absorption maximum to 820 nm (Fig. 3b) accompanied by a dramatic 300-fold enhancement in fluorescence quantum yield. Notably, the emission spectrum extended into the NIR-II region up to 1000 nm (Fig. 3c), spectral changes that collectively suggest strong restriction of intramolecular motions upon incorporation into HSA's hydrophobic binding pocket. Complementary physical characterization further corroborated albumin corona formation through multiple convergent lines of evidence. DLS measurements detected a 20 nm increase in hydrodynamic diameter (from 125 nm to 145 nm, Fig. 3c), a magnitude precisely corresponding to the theoretical dimensions of HSA and strongly suggestive of monolayer protein adsorption. This interpretation was directly confirmed by TEM imaging, which clearly resolved uniform protein layers enveloping the NPs. Furthermore, the observed reduction in ζ-potential from −20.50 mV to −8.05 mV (Fig. S9) provided additional evidence for effective surface charge screening by the adsorbed albumin molecules. In contrast, parallel experiments with CySC NPs showed no significant changes in either size (maintained at 95–99 nm) or surface potential (−9.22 to −8.60 mV), conclusively demonstrating their ability to resist nonspecific protein (albumin) adsorption during circulation.

This comprehensive analysis establishes that our PCS strategy successfully integrates tumor-specific activation of both therapeutic payload release and optical property enhancement through programmed albumin corona formation. The tight correlation between structural confinement and fluorescence intensification provides new insights for developing intelligent theranostic nanosystems. Collectively, the findings presented in this and the preceding section demonstrate that our system executes a programmed cascade mechanism upon GSH activation, which fundamentally advances the design of stimulus-responsive nanosystems. Whereas a multitude of studies have utilized disulfide linkers primarily for controlled drug release, our design is uniquely engineered to transform the cleavage event into a trigger for synchronized multi-modal activation. As evidenced, the disulfide breakage not only liberates the chemotherapeutic agent but also concurrently activates the NIR-I/II fluorescence signal and directs the in situ formation of a theranostic albumin corona (AC-Cy-OH NPs). This orchestration of concurrent therapeutic and diagnostic functions, including chemotherapy release, imaging activation, and corona reprogramming, distinguishes our system from conventional single-response platforms. It represents a significant progression from merely releasing a payload to executing a coherent, multi-step biological logic that enhances both specificity and efficacy.

Cellular-level specific theranostics of CySC NPs

The intracellular behavior of CySC NPs was comprehensively studied to elucidate their GSH-responsive drug release and synergistic mechanisms. Confocal imaging of 4T1 cells co-stained with LysoTracker Green revealed pronounced co-localization of CySC NPs with lysosomes (Pearson's coefficient p = 0.88, Fig. 4a–b), confirming lysosomal accumulation. Subsequent live-cell fluorescence imaging (Fig. 4c–d) demonstrated GSH-dependent activation, with 4T1 cells (high GSH) exhibiting intense fluorescence from AC-Cy-OH NPs while L929 cells (low GSH) and CyCC NP-treated groups showed minimal signals. This selectivity highlights the key role of tumor-specific GSH levels in triggering drug release. To evaluate tumor penetration, 3D spheroids embedded in Matrigel were treated with CySC NPs or CyCC NPs. CySC NPs achieved uniform fluorescence penetration up to 160 μm depth through albumin-mediated transcytosis of AC-Cy-OH NPs, while CyCC NPs remained confined to the periphery due to size limitations (Fig. 4e) [35]. This spatial coordination enables synchronized delivery of photosensitizers to deep tumor regions, a significant advancement over conventional protein corona strategies that lack such spatiotemporal control, addressing a critical challenge in solid tumor therapy where heterogeneous drug distribution often limits efficacy. GSH-triggered photodynamic activity was assessed using the ROS-sensitive probe DCFH-DA, with laser-irradiated CySC NPs inducing robust green fluorescence in 4T1 cells indicative of efficient singlet oxygen generation (Fig. 4f–g). The absence of ROS signals in CyCC NPs groups or non-irradiated controls confirms the dual dependency on both GSH activation and light irradiation for photosensitizer activation, minimizing off-target effects.

The MTT assays and Live/Dead staining results (Fig. 5a–b) clearly demonstrate the synergistic therapeutic effect of our combined approach. The CySC NPs with laser irradiation exhibited potent cytotoxicity, reducing 4T1 cell viability to below 2 %, which represents a 33-fold enhancement compared to chemotherapy alone (CySC NPs without irradiation: 66 % viability), a 35-fold improvement over photothermal therapy alone (CyCC NPs + laser: 70 % viability), and a 17.5-fold increase versus phototherapy alone (Cy-OH NPs + laser: 35 % viability). The combined chemo-phototherapy using CySC NPs with laser irradiation showed the lowest IC50 (4.45 μM), demonstrating 3.4-fold greater potency than chemotherapy alone (15.00 μM) and significantly outperforming both PTT (6.50 μM) and PTT&PDT (5.56 μM), confirming strong synergistic effects. Combination index (CI) analysis (Chou-Talalay method, Fig. 5c) confirmed synergistic interaction (CI < 1) at therapeutic concentrations [36]. Apoptosis assays (Annexin V/PI staining, Fig. S13) further corroborated this synergy, revealing that the combination therapy induced near-complete apoptosis (100 %) in cells, in stark contrast to the partial apoptosis exhibited by single-modality groups, including chemotherapy-alone (73.37 % apoptosis rate) and phototherapy-alone (70.40 % apoptosis rate). Caspase-3 activity assays (Fig. 5d) further validated the apoptotic pathway activation, aligning with the synergistic cytotoxicity. The observed synergistic effect can likely be ascribed to the mechanism whereby phototherapy elevated ROS levels and localized thermal effects not only directly damages cellular components but also enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy through the inhibition of multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1)-mediated drug efflux [37,38]. This is substantiated by Western blot analysis, which clearly demonstrates that CySC NPs combined with laser irradiation leads to the downregulation of MRP1 expression (Fig. 5e–f, Fig. S14). Our CySC nanosystem exploits the elevated GSH in tumors (4-fold higher than normal cells) to achieve selective activation, showing <50 % viability in 4T1 tumors versus 99 % in normal L929 cells (Fig. S15). The tumor-selective biosafety profile demonstrated by negligible cytotoxicity in normal L929 cells (viability >98 % at 100 μM, Fig. S16) underscores the therapeutic window created by differential GSH levels between tumor and normal cells.

In vivo specific theranostics of CySC NPs

The tumor-targeting capability and GSH-responsive activation of CySC NPs were thoroughly validated through in vivo PA and NIR-I&II fluorescence imaging in a TNBC mouse model. CySC NPs exhibited a concentration-dependent PA signal in aqueous media (0–40 μM, Fig. 6a), confirming their suitability for real-time imaging. Following intravenous administration with 200 μg of either CySC NPs or CyCC NPs, PA signals in tumors of both NPs gradually increased over time following intravenous tail injection, peaking approximately 6 h post-injection, which can be attributed to the EPR effect-mediated passive targeting (Fig. 6b–c). Crucially, CySC NPs exhibited tumor-specific activation through GSH-triggered fluorescence recovery, as evidenced by NIR-I&II imaging showing minimal systemic fluorescence in normal tissues but substantial tumor signal amplification with a 12-fold tumor-to-background ratio at day 3, correlating with in situ formation of AC-Cy-OH NPs (Fig. 6d–g). This spatial temporal activation profile differed significantly from CyCC NPs, which accumulated nonspecifically in organs such as liver and spleen without tumor selectivity, as confirmed by ex vivo organ imaging (Fig. 6f–g). NIR-II imaging further validated CySC NPs’ extended circulation stability and sustained GSH-dependent activation, highlighting their tumor-specific imaging precision through spatiotemporal correlation between fluorescence recovery and therapeutic payload release. (Fig. 6h–j). The observed tumor-selective fluorescence recovery directly links to the biochemical specificity of the tumor microenvironment, where elevated GSH levels not only trigger drug release but also enable real-time visualization of therapeutic activation, addressing a critical challenge in monitoring nanomedicine biodistribution and activation kinetics.

The therapeutic efficacy of CySC NPs was evaluated in TNBC-bearing mice divided into six experimental groups (n = 5 per group), including saline control, saline with laser irradiation, CyCC NPs alone, CyCC NPs with laser irradiation, CySC NPs alone, and CySC NPs with laser irradiation. Laser irradiation (808 nm, 0.8 W/cm2, 10 min) induced rapid tumor hyperthermia, with CySC NPs and CyCC NPs reaching 60 °C and 55 °C, respectively (Fig. 7a–b). While CyCC NPs combined with laser irradiation group slowed tumor growth via PTT, CySC NPs group achieved moderate suppression through CPT-mediated chemotherapy. Strikingly, CySC NPs combined with laser irradiation group resulted in complete tumor ablation (100 % inhibition rate, Fig. 7c–e), demonstrating potent synergy between CPT release, PTT, and GSH-activated PDT. This synergistic effect exerts its efficacy through multiple interrelated mechanisms. Local hyperthermia can enhance the permeability of tumor blood vessels and the interstitial transport capacity, facilitating the accumulation of NPs at the tumor site and the distribution of drugs within the tumor. PDT takes advantage of the condition of GSH depletion to amplify oxidative damage by continuously generating ROS. Phototherapy inhibits the drug resistance of tumor cells by downregulating the expression of drug resistance proteins. Chemotherapy precisely eliminates the residual tumor cells that still possess repair capabilities after PTT and PDT. Histopathological evaluations (H&E staining, TUNEL staining, Ki-67 detection) have confirmed the interaction of these mechanisms. In the combined treatment group, extensive tumor necrosis, activation of apoptosis, and arrest of proliferation were observed, which stands in sharp contrast to the preserved tumor structure and limited cytotoxicity in the single-treatment groups (Fig. 7h). It is noteworthy that the disulfide-linked CySC exhibits redox-cleavable properties, which not only facilitate efficient release of CPT and Cy-OH but also consume intracellular GSH through redox reactions (Fig. S17). This characteristic may hold significant therapeutic potential for various cancer treatment strategies [39].

The therapeutic superiority of this multimodal approach was matched by excellent biocompatibility. The stability of body weight (Fig. 7f) and normal organ histology (Fig. 7i) confirmed the biocompatibility of CySC NPs, while blood biochemistry and routine tests (Fig. 7g) demonstrated no significant changes in liver function markers (ALKP, ALT, ALB) or hematological parameters (WBC, Lymph, Gran, UREA), further validating systemic safety. These results collectively highlight that the multimodal design of CySC NPs not only maximizes therapeutic efficacy through complementary mechanisms but also maintains a favorable safety profile, addressing critical challenges in TNBC treatment such as drug resistance and off-target toxicity. The absence of adverse effects on vital organs and hematological homeostasis suggests clinical translatability, particularly for aggressive tumors requiring intensive but tolerable regimens.

The superior in vivo performance of our CySC nanosystem underscores the transformative potential of the dynamic PCS strategy over conventional nanomedicine approaches. Unlike passive stealth coatings (e.g., PEGylation) that non-specifically inhibit all protein interactions and often compromise targeting efficacy, our design proactively manages corona formation by selectively suppressing albumin adsorption during circulation and then actively recruiting it within the TME. This paradigm shift from corona avoidance to corona programming effectively addresses the critical limitation of ACNPs, whose targeting moieties are often obscured by non-specifically adsorbed proteins. Furthermore, while many stimulus-responsive systems focus solely on controlled drug release, our CySC platform executes a programmed biotransformation: GSH-triggered disulfide cleavage synchronizes chemotherapy (CPT release), fluorescence activation (theranostic feedback), and most importantly, the in-situ formation of an albumin corona (AC-Cy-OH NPs) that promotes deep tumor penetration via transcytosis. This multi-event, coordinated activation cascade, integrated into a single molecular entity, achieves a level of spatial-temporal control and synergistic functionality that is difficult to attain with traditional multi-component nanocarriers or simpler prodrug designs.

Although the current results are encouraging, several aspects warrant further consideration for future translation. First, the initial tumor accumulation of CySC NPs primarily relies on the EPR effect, which is clearly demonstrated in preclinical models but may exhibit variability in clinical settings [40]. Our PCS strategy is designed to enhance deep penetration after accumulation, though improving targeting consistency could be beneficial across diverse tumor types. Regarding the disulfide-based linker, its redox sensitivity may lead to non-specific cleavage under pathophysiological conditions such as ROS-enriched inflammatory microenvironments. Furthermore, the activation threshold may be insufficient to ensure robust drug release in tumors with moderately elevated GSH levels. To enhance specificity, future designs could incorporate auxiliary targeting motifs or dual-response mechanisms that integrate additional tumor-specific signals. Finally, as current techniques still face challenges in precisely controlling the size and distribution of self-assembled nanoparticles, larger particles may be cleared more rapidly by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), thereby reducing delivery efficiency [41]. We will focus on optimizing the assembly process to achieve more uniform and stable nanoformulations and improve their pharmacokinetic behavior.

Conclusions

By redefining the role of the protein corona from an obstacle to a theranostic regulator, this study successfully demonstrates a tumor microenvironment-responsive dynamic PCS strategy that addresses key limitations of conventional ADCs in TNBC treatment. Although the immunomodulatory potential of CPT through Gas-STING pathway activation was not systematically evaluated here, this mechanism may provide additional therapeutic benefits for immunologically cold TNBC [42]. Future studies could integrate PCS with immune checkpoint blockade to exploit such synergies. By integrating GSH-triggered CPT release with endogenous albumin recruitment-driven tumor remodeling, the CySC NPs achieves spatiotemporally regulated drug activation and real-time feedback on treatment through NIR fluorescence restoration. The programmed biotransformation enables deep tumor penetration via albumin-mediated transcytosis while reversing chemoresistance through prolonged intratumoral retention, synergizing with phototherapy-enhanced oxidative damage and hyperthermia. In vitro and in vivo evaluations validate the platform's tumor-specific activation, complete ablation of TNBC xenografts through chemo-phototherapeutic synergy, and favorable biocompatibility. This protein corona -reprogramming paradigm establishes a versatile approach for precision oncology by harnessing endogenous biological cues to optimize therapeutic specificity and overcome multidrug resistance, holding promise for clinical translation potential for aggressive malignancies.
