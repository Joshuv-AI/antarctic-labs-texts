Eye tracking deciphers key factors influencing children’s implicit visual preferences for street space elements on school commuting routes
Authors: Minhui Song, Wei Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the background of the child-friendly city initiative, school commuting routes serve as the core daily space connecting families and schools for children aged 6–14, and the design of their street environments directly impacts children’s travel experiences and healthy development. Existing studies mostly focus on the macro planning of child-friendly spaces, lacking systematic decomposition and quantitative analysis of children’s visual preferences for specific environmental elements in school commuting routes. To fill this gap, this study uses eye-tracking technology (Tobii Eye Tracker 5) and mathematical statistical analysis (Friedman test, Spearman correlation analysis) to explore the patterns and key influencing factors of children’s visual preferences for four core elements (architecture, ground, greening, and facilities) of street spaces along school commuting routes. Sixteen children aged 6–14 were selected as participants, who observed 12 screened street view photos of school commuting routes (including historical style, high greening-dominated, facility-dominated, and conventional comprehensive types). Data such as eye-tracking trajectories, heat maps, fixation duration and frequency were collected, and a comprehensive analysis was conducted combined with questionnaire feedback. This study reveals children’s implicit preferences for micro-environmental elements in school commuting routes through quantitative methods and constructs a “factor indicator-visual preference” correlation model, making up for the deficiency of insufficient attention to the micro-level in existing studies. The research conclusions can provide scientific support for the refined design of school commuting routes in the construction of child-friendly cities, helping to create safe, comfortable, and child-centered daily travel spaces. Meanwhile, it enriches the research methods and theoretical achievements in the fields of child environmental psychology and urban planning.Keywords: Children, School commuting routes, Visual preference, Eye-tracking, Street space elements, Child-friendly citySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

In 1996, the United Nations Children’s Fund and the United Nations Human Settlements Programme officially proposed the “Child-Friendly City Initiative” at the Second United Nations Conference on Human Settlements1. This initiative aims to fully mobilize local governments and all sectors of society to jointly create cities and communities that are safe, inclusive, and fully responsive to the needs of children2. By 2050, it is estimated that 70% of children will live in cities3. Green and open spaces, accessibility, safety, social connectivity, play and leisure, as well as freedom, are the most frequently mentioned themes in existing papers on child-friendly environments4. Space and facilities are crucial for the healthy development of children5. Unlike adults who tend to view the environment from the perspective of form and aesthetics, children are more inclined to view it from a functional perspective, perceiving it as an opportunity for different activities6,7. As the core daily space connecting families and schools for children aged 6–148, school commuting routes are not only suitable places for children to socialize, exercise, and develop autonomy but also opportunities for them to contact the surrounding environment and interact with the outside world9. Children’s cognitive characteristics towards street spaces are precisely reflected in their preference for various environmental elements10.

Existing research has yielded a series of findings centered around children’s concerns regarding urban spaces. Since children have a different perspective on space from adults11, understanding their preferences can affect their satisfaction with the surrounding environment. Recent studies have also highlighted significant intergenerational differences in spatial perception and demand, revealing clear spatial differentiation and perceptual mismatch between children and older adults in streetscape preferences and daily use behaviors, further emphasizing the necessity of targeted design for different age groups12,13. Children’s perception of urban space exhibits a distinct tendency towards concretization and emotionalization14. Through drawing interviews and in-depth interviews, the study found that children abstract their spatial perception into seven map types, such as ordered and scattered types, and their judgments of spatial friendliness are concentrated in six dimensions, including care and respect for places, meaningful exchange with places, etc.15. Both formal and informal spaces such as community parks, sidewalks, streets, and shops are places of entertainment for children, with informal spaces being more popular among them16. Color and form are core attraction factors. Visual elements processed with cartoon or hand-drawn techniques can effectively capture children’s attention17. Most aspects that children notice during their commute are related to functionality, such as space, width, seating areas, and waiting times (e.g. crossing the street), followed by aesthetic factors such as cleanliness, trees, flowers, and lighting18. Girls tend to pay more attention to roadside green spaces and parks, while boys are more inclined to highlight elements related to the street19.

However, existing studies mostly focus on the macro planning of child-friendly spaces2,20–22, lacking systematic decomposition of the core of children’s street cognition (i.e., attention preferences for specific environmental elements)23–25. The core concerns of children about street spaces and their classification characteristics have not been clarified26, making it difficult to support targeted spatial optimization design. In particular, few studies have quantitatively decomposed children’s visual preferences for the four fundamental elements of street spaces along school commuting routes: architecture, ground, greening, and facilities. This gap limits the evidence base for child-centered street design.

Meanwhile, recent methodological advances have enabled more objective assessments of urban streetscapes. Street view image processing27 and machine learning28 have been successfully applied to quantify neighborhood environments and evaluate child-friendliness at scale. These complementary approaches, while valuable, capture primarily physical features (e.g., green view index, enclosure, walkability); they do not directly measure children‘s real-time perceptual responses. Furthermore, urban environmental perception is inherently multi-sensory—visual, auditory, olfactory, and tactile inputs jointly shape how people experience a place. For example, using social media data to analyze urban soundscapes has revealed how auditory experiences vary across different groups and built environment contexts, and how they interact with visual factors29. This multi-sensory perspective, though important, remains underexplored in child-friendly street research.

These research gaps motivate the adoption of eye-tracking technology, which can reveal children’s implicit visual preferences with millisecond-level precision, overcoming the potential biases of behavioral observation and interview methods30. The starting point of eye tracking research dates back to 1879, when French scholar Louis Émile Javal first systematically observed the eye movement patterns during human reading and discovered that the eyes do not move continuously but scan text in a “fixation-saccade” manner31,32. The first non-invasive eye-tracking device, the Photochronograph, was developed in 1901. It utilized corneal reflection light to record horizontal and vertical eye movements, laying the theoretical and optical foundation for modern eye-tracking technology33. Eye-tracking refers to the process of monitoring eye movements to determine their position and trajectory34. The technology is commonly used to observe human visual attention35. Eye-tracking metrics are typically categorized into three dimensions: spatial metrics, temporal metrics, and frequency metrics36. Eye-tracking technology can record the objective movements of the eyes with millisecond-level tracking accuracy, accurately capturing people’s rapid environmental perceptual processing of the environment37. Quantified visual attention data can serve as a bridge for studying individual interactions with the environment, providing an analytical foundation for decision-based research paradigms38.

In summary, the key research gap is that existing child-friendly space studies have focused on macro-level planning and rely heavily on subjective reports or static physical indicators, lacking systematic quantification of children’s micro-level visual preferences for specific street elements along school commuting routes. To fill this gap, this study uses eye-tracking technology (Tobii Eye Tracker 5) and mathematical statistical analysis (Friedman test, Spearman correlation analysis) to explore the patterns and key influencing factors of children‘s visual preferences for four core elements (architecture, ground, greening, and facilities) of street spaces along school commuting routes. Sixteen children aged 6–14 participated, observing 12 screened street view photos. Data such as eye-tracking trajectories, heat maps, fixation duration and frequency were collected, and a comprehensive analysis was conducted combined with questionnaire feedback. This study reveals children’s implicit preferences for micro-environmental elements through quantitative methods and constructs a “factor indicator–visual preference” correlation model, making up for the deficiency of insufficient attention to the micro-level in existing studies. The research conclusions can provide scientific support for the refined design of school commuting routes in the construction of child-friendly cities, helping to create safe, comfortable, and child-centered daily travel spaces. Meanwhile, it enriches the research methods and theoretical achievements in the fields of child environmental psychology and urban planning (Table 1).

Spatial division

Constituent elements of school commuting spaces

This study focuses on the physical dimension of school commuting spaces, whose core elements include four categories: architectural elements composed of walls, eaves, doors, windows, building signs, etc.; ground elements consisting of carriageways and sidewalks; greening elements including street trees, shrubs, vines, and other green vegetation; and facility elements composed of street furniture (such as seats and trash cans), lighting, signs, bus stops, etc.

Field investigation and selection of research samples

Using the panoramic function of Baidu Maps,18 primary schools with distinctive surrounding road characteristics were initially selected, and 460 street view photos were taken within 200 m of the schools at a height of 1 m.

The selection criteria for schools and photos in this field investigation follow three core principles:

Sample representativeness: Schools with large scale and high traffic volume were selected to ensure the diversity of street types and elements and reduce random errors;

Sample representativeness: Schools with large scale and high traffic volume were selected to ensure the diversity of street types and elements and reduce random errors;

Element adaptability: Prioritize images with complete elements and distinct focuses, covering the four types of elements (architectural, ground, greening, and facility elements) using an orthogonal design method. Specifically, the four element types were defined as orthogonal independent variables, and their visual proportions and combinations were systematically controlled to ensure mutual independence and non-interference among variables, thereby meeting the requirements of subsequent mathematical statistical analyses such as the Friedman test for multiple related samples and Spearman correlation analysis.;

Elements of school commuting spaces

We summarized all elements appearing in the 12 samples and identified four core categories of elements in school commuting spaces. Their definitions and key indicators are as follows:

Architectural elements: These include walls, eaves, doors, windows, and building signs. The core indicators are the number of building material types, the number of facade color types, and the visual proportion of architectural elements.

Ground elements: These include sidewalks, motor vehicle lanes, and non-motor vehicle lanes. The core indicator is the visual proportion of ground elements.

Greening elements: These include street trees, shrubs, lawns, potted plants, and climbing plants. The core indicators are the green view ratio and the number of green plant types.

Facility elements: These include 13 types of facilities, such as road signs and separation columns. The core indicators are the number of facility types and the visual proportion of facility elements.

Eye-tracking experiment design

Experiment preparation

The experiment was conducted in the laboratory on the 6th floor of the Civil Engineering and Architecture Building of Hubei University of Technology. All participants were children aged 6–14 (The study has passed ethical review, with the reference number HBUT20250071. Detailed participant characteristics were as follows: 16 children (9 boys, 7 girls); age distribution: 6–8 years (n = 5), 9–11 years (n = 6), 12–14 years (n = 5). All participants passed the Snellen visual acuity test (monocular eyes corrected or uncorrected ≥ 1.0). None had previous experience with eye‑tracking experiments or professional training in art or architectural design. Before the formal experiment, researchers explained the procedure to each child and their guardian, and informed consent was obtained.). Before the formal experiment, the equipment was debugged and calibrated. The instrument used was a Tobii Pro Fusion screen‑based eye tracker with a sampling rate of 120 Hz and spatial accuracy of approximately 0.3° of visual angle. The eye tracker was fixed under the screen used by the participants, and the eye distance between the eye tracker and the children was adjusted to be horizontally39(the horizontal distance from the eye tracker to the child’s eyes was approximately 60–65 cm, with a vertical angle of about 15°–20°). Then, the calibration function of the eye tracker was activated using a 5‑point calibration procedure; eye movements of each participant were captured and recorded for calibration. Calibration was considered successful when the error for all calibration points was < 0.5° of visual angle; if unsuccessful, calibration was repeated up to three times. In addition to the eye tracker, OBS Studio was used on the participant’s computer to stream screen data to another device, and EVCapture was used to record the results.

Selection of participants

A sample size of 16 children is statistically adequate for the within-subjects repeated-measures design employed in this study. Power analysis using G*Power (for Friedman test, assuming a medium effect size W = 0.3, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, 4 repeated measures) indicated a required sample size of at least 12 participants. For Spearman correlation (two-tailed, ρ = 0.6, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), a minimum of 13 participants is required. Thus, our sample of 16 provides sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful differences and correlations. This sample scale is also commonly adopted in controlled eye-tracking experiments focusing on within-group visual preference comparisons. The participants in this experiment were 16 children, evenly aged between 6 and 14 years old (All participants signed the informed consent form for the experiment), with good vision and a certain level of cognitive ability towards street space. Among them, there were 7 girls and 9 boys, all experiencing the experimental setting for the first time. The pilot experiment was conducted on May 19, 2025, and the formal experiment was conducted from July 15 to 17, 2025, at the Environmental Behavior Laboratory. The experimental procedures for both experiments were consistent, with the duration of the experimental process for each child controlled within 6 min, and researchers monitoring the entire process.

Experimental procedure

After completing the basic preparation and debugging, the formal experiment began. The 12 pre-selected street view images of school commuting routes were randomly ordered and displayed on the participants’ screens in sequence. Each image was shown at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels on a 23.8‑inch LCD monitor set to 120 cd/m² brightness and 50% contrast. The distance between the participant’s eyes and the screen centre was approximately 65 cm, with the screen centre at eye level. Each photo was given 10 s for children to observe, and a 5-second black screen interval was set between photo transitions to ensure the participants’ good observation state. During the entire experiment, the Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker, OBS Studio, and EVCapture were used to record the children’s eye movement trajectories (Fig. 2).

Experiment data processing

Eye tracking data was collected and outputted using the Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker, OBS Studio, and EVCapture. This involved capturing visual trajectory and heat map image data of street scenes, as well as numerical data on the fixation counts and proportions of various elements. The experiment yielded 192 sets of visual trajectory and heat map image data for each sample, along with 768 sets of fixation count and fixation duration data for each element.

Data preprocessing and interest area (AOI) delineation

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of data analysis, raw eye‑tracking data were pre‑processed as follows:

Data cleaning: Trials with gaze sampling rates below 70% (due to blinks or tracking loss) were excluded. Among the 192 trials from the 16 participants, 9 trials (4.7%) were removed, leaving 183 valid trials for analysis.

Data cleaning: Trials with gaze sampling rates below 70% (due to blinks or tracking loss) were excluded. Among the 192 trials from the 16 participants, 9 trials (4.7%) were removed, leaving 183 valid trials for analysis.

Interest area (AOI) delineation: Using Tobii Pro Lab software, four AOIs were manually drawn on each street view image, corresponding to the four core elements: architecture, ground, greening, and facilities. AOI boundaries were delineated according to the contours of visual elements; overlapping areas (e.g., greenery in front of a building) were assigned to a single AOI based on foreground priority. Fixation counts and fixation durations per AOI were automatically aggregated by the software.

Experiment data analysis

Visual preference analysis based on eye-tracking technology

Fixation trajectory analysis

We extracted and analyzed 192 eye‑movement fixation trajectory images (After excluding invalid trials, 183 valid trajectories were actually analysed). The core characteristics of children’s fixation trajectories on school commuting space elements are summarized below.

(1)Clear differences in the priority of element attention: Architectural elements (37.5%) and facility elements (33.5%) were significantly more likely to be children’s primary attention objects than greening (21.2%) and ground elements (7.8%). Bright colors and high‑contrast designs often appear in architectural and facility elements, effectively attracting children‘s attention. This pattern arises from the high visual salience of buildings and facilities—for instance, eye‑catching building signs and brightly colored road signs quickly capture children’s attention. At the same time, these two element types contain richer information (e.g., text and patterns), which stimulates children’s cognitive interest. In contrast, ground and greening elements usually receive secondary attention due to their lower information density. Moreover, buildings and facilities are primary activity areas in daily life, and children gradually develop a sense of priority, first attending to these main areas and then shifting to secondary areas such as the ground and greening.

(2)Visual features dominate attention allocation: Color vividness and scene proportion are key factors influencing element attention. For example, in Sample 5 (81.25% of children primarily focused on orange building signs), Sample 7 (56.25% focused on orange‑red brick walls), and Sample 11 (68.75% focused on sculpture sketches), high‑saturation colors (red, yellow, orange) and elements with large visual proportions were more likely to become visual focal points in complex backgrounds. Such elements transmit more visual information in a short time, consistent with children’s preference for distinct visual stimuli. Larger graphics or patterns also enjoy a size advantage that further attracts children’s attention.

(3)Special cases of ground element attention: Ground elements are generally the last area children fixate on, but refined pavement can increase their attention. For instance, the primary attention probability for the ground element in Sample 9 (stone paving) was 25%, significantly higher than that in Sample 12 (ordinary asphalt paving, 6.25%). This difference occurs because stone slabs and floor tiles offer richer texture details, providing more visual exploration points and better integration with the surrounding environment, thereby enhancing spatial comfort. In contrast, asphalt and cement roads have monotonous materials and a purely functional role (walking only), making them less likely to attract active attention (Table 2).

Heat map analysis

We comprehensively analyzed 192 heat maps. The results show that children’s attention to school commuting space elements follows clear patterns, and the attractiveness of sub‑elements varies significantly, as summarized below.

Overall attention ranking.

Overall attention ranking.

Children’s attention to the four element types, from highest to lowest, was: architectural elements (31.5%) > greening elements (29.9%) > facility elements (25%) > ground elements (13.4%). Architectural elements attracted the most attention due to their bright colors (e.g., the red and yellow walls in Sample 5) and rich details (signs, door and window designs). Greening elements ranked second, owing to the three‑dimensional visual hierarchy created by the arrangement of tall and low plants and the interplay of light and shadow (e.g., tall street trees combined with low shrubs in Sample 11). Ground elements received the least attention because of their monotonous colors and simple textures.

Key influencing factors:

Color attractiveness.

Color attractiveness.

Brightly colored elements are more attractive to children. Statistical comparisons confirmed these differences quantitatively. Among architectural elements, participants’ average fixation duration on the orange-red walls in Sample 5 (M = 2.34 s, SD = 0.42) was significantly higher than that on white walls (M = 1.02 s, SD = 0.31), paired t (15) = 3.68, p = 0.003. Similarly, the average fixation duration on orange walls in Sample 12(M = 2.01 s, SD = 0.39) was significantly higher than that on beige walls (M = 0.89 s, SD = 0.28), t (15) = 4.12, p = 0.002. Regarding ground elements, in Sample 1, the average fixation duration on the red asphalt non-motor vehicle lane (M = 2.23 s, SD = 0.47) was significantly higher than that on the black asphalt motor vehicle lane (M = 0.81 s, SD = 0.26), t (15) = 4.89, p = 0.004. For greening elements, in Sample 12, the average fixation duration on bright green street trees (M = 2.67 s, SD = 0.53) was significantly higher than that on dark green shrubs (M = 0.91 s, SD = 0.30), t (15) = 5.34, p = 0.002. All statistical comparisons were performed using paired t‑tests (normality verified by Shapiro–Wilk test, p > 0.05 for all difference scores). Standard deviations (SD) and exact p‑values are reported alongside each comparison. These results confirm that children show significantly higher interest and sensitivity to bright, vivid colors across all element categories.

b.Material preference.

Material preference.

Among building materials, brick and wood walls are more attractive to children than cement or concrete walls. For example, participants’ average fixation duration on the wooden grille walls in Sample 2 was significantly higher than that on concrete walls, and the average fixation duration on the brick walls in Sample 5 was significantly higher than that on concrete walls. Furthermore, walls with a greater variety of facade materials are more attractive to children than those with a single material. The number of building facade materials in Samples 9, 2, 1, and 3 was 5, 4, 2, and 1, respectively, and the corresponding attention proportions for architectural elements were 36%, 36.6%, 30.2%, and 27.1%. These findings indicate that natural materials such as brick and wood, with their unique surface textures and warmer tactility, create a more intimate and natural atmosphere that is particularly appealing to children. In addition, combining multiple materials provides visual diversity and rich sensory experiences through different textures, colors, and shapes.

Subdivided element preferences:

Architectural elements.

Walls were the most attention‑grabbing in 56.25% of the samples, and building signs were the most attention‑grabbing in 43.75% of the samples. b.Facility elements.

Facility elements.

Road signs were the most attention‑grabbing in 31.25% of the samples. Their bright colors and intuitive symbols (pedestrian and bicycle signs) align well with children‘s cognitive characteristics, making them more likely to attract attention without requiring complex details.

c.Greening elements.

Greening elements.

Among greening elements, street trees had the strongest attractiveness. In samples containing more than one type of green plant, participants’ average attention to street trees was 14.5%, higher than that to shrubs (9.3%), potted plants (7.9%), lawns (7.6%), and climbing plants (4.3%). The tall stature and rich crown textures of street trees provide children with more opportunities for observation and exploration, whereas shrubs and lawns, despite some morphological variation, remain relatively flat and monotonous.

d.Ground elements.

Ground elements.

Among ground elements, stone slab roads and floor tile-paved roads are more attractive to children than conventional asphalt roads and cement roads. For example, the road pavements in Sample 4, Sample 9, Sample 10, and Sample 12 were floor tiles, stone slabs, cement, and asphalt respectively, and the attention of participants to ground elements in each sample was 23.7%, 12.7%, 6%, and 6.2% respectively (Table 3). Compared with cement roads and asphalt roads, the surface texture details of stone slab roads and floor tile-paved roads are more abundant, and these changes provide children with rich visual experiences.

To determine whether there are statistically significant differences in fixation durations among architectural, ground, greening, and facility elements across the samples, we used the Friedman test for multiple related samples. For each sample, we calculated the proportion of fixation time devoted to each of the four element types over a 10‑second interval. The results are presented as boxplots, which effectively display differences in fixation duration and attention levels across elements. Boxplots allow side‑by‑side comparison of distribution, central tendency, and dispersion across multiple data groups. Moreover, because boxplots are based on medians and quartiles, they are insensitive to extreme values and thus better reflect the true data distribution compared to other methods.

Discussion

Core characteristics and intrinsic mechanisms of children’s visual preferences for school commuting space elements

Through eye-tracking technology, this study systematically reveals the patterns of visual preferences of children aged 6–14 for the four major elements of street spaces in school commuting routes. Its core findings are highly consistent with children’s cognitive development characteristics and spatial perception logic. Firstly, the attention priority of elements presents the characteristic of instantaneous attention allocation as “architectural elements > facility elements > greening elements > ground elements”, while the overall proportion of fixation duration is “architectural elements > greening elements > facility elements > ground elements”. This difference stems from the dual logic of attention capture: instantaneous attention depends on the visual recognizability of elements (such as high-saturation colors of building signs and distinct forms of facilities), while cumulative fixation duration depends on the information density and exploratory value of elements (such as the three-dimensional hierarchy of greening and the material details of buildings). This result confirms the conclusion existing research that “children’s spatial perception is concrete and emotional”14, and further quantifies abstract preferences into operational spatial indicators. For example, the correlation coefficient between the number of building material types and fixation time is 0.588 (p < 0.001), and the correlation coefficient between the green view ratio and greening fixation time is as high as 0.902 (p < 0.001), providing precise quantitative support for the preference mechanism. Notably, the correlation coefficient between the diversity of architectural materials and children’s gaze duration is generally higher than 0.5, and natural materials (such as wood and brick) are significantly more attractive than artificial materials (such as concrete).

Secondly, the Friedman test confirms that there are significant differences in the fixation durations of the four major elements in all samples (p < 0.01), and architectural and greening elements occupy the top two in fixation duration in 75% of the samples, which is consistent with children’s spatial cognitive logic of “prioritizing functional needs and supplementing with aesthetic experience”16. Architectural elements provide diverse visual information through rich material combinations (such as bricks and wood) and color changes; greening elements form a three-dimensional visual experience through the multi-layer configuration of “street trees + shrubs + ground covers”, both of which meet children’s needs for exploration and sensory stimulation. In contrast, ground elements generally have the lowest attention (less than 12% in most samples), and their visual proportion has a significant negative correlation with fixation time (r=-0.260, p < 0.001). This phenomenon is due to the single function and lack of visual details of ground space—conventional asphalt and cement roads lack texture changes, making it difficult to stimulate children’s active attention. Only when the pavement refinement is improved (such as the stone-paved ground in Sample 9) does the attention increase significantly. In addition, the Spearman correlation analysis clarifies the key influencing indicators of each element: the core driving factors of architectural elements are the number of material types and their visual proportion; the dominant factors of greening elements are the green view ratio and the number of green plant types; the key variable of facility elements is the visual proportion; and the attractiveness of ground elements depends on pavement refinement rather than visual proportion. This finding refines the existing cognition that “color and form are the core attractors of children’s space”17, revealing deeper influencing dimensions such as “material diversity” and “hierarchical richness”, and providing specific optimization targets for spatial design. However, it should be acknowledged that children aged 6–14 years may exhibit potential cognitive differences in instantaneous attention allocation, and aggregating all participants into a single group represents a specific constraint of the current data analysis model.

Dialogue with existing research and theoretical contributions

The core theoretical contribution of this study is to fill the gap of “emphasizing macro and neglecting micro” in existing child-friendly space research. Existing studies mostly focus on the macro planning framework of child-friendly spaces at the urban level20,21,40, lacking systematic decomposition of children’s preferences for specific environmental elements in core children’s activity spaces (such as school commuting routes). Through eye-tracking technology, this study converts children’s implicit visual preferences into explicit quantitative data, clarifies for the first time the impact intensity of four types of elements (architecture, ground, greening, and facilities) and their subdivided indicators (such as green view ratio and number of building materials) on children’s attention. Furthermore, it constructs a correlation model of “element type, key indicators, and visual preference”, enriching the theoretical system of “perception and preference” in child environmental psychology.

Compared with traditional interview and behavioral observation methods, the eye-tracking technology adopted in this study overcomes the potential biases of subjective reports30, captures children’s real visual responses with millisecond-level accuracy, and verifies the conclusion that “children pay more attention to functional and high information density elements”6,7. For example, in the facility-dominated sample (Sample 10), the visual proportion of street vendors reaches 37.4%, and their fixation duration proportion is as high as 78%, intuitively confirming the strong locking effect of functional facilities on children’s attention; while the green view ratio of the high greening-dominated sample (Sample 11) exceeds 50%, and the fixation duration proportion of greening elements is 49.7%, which supplements the new cognition that “greening hierarchy and visual proportion jointly affect preferences”, breaking through the limitation of existing studies that only focus on the existence of greening.

More broadly, urban environmental perception is inherently multi-sensory—visual, auditory, olfactory, and tactile inputs jointly shape how people experience a place. While our study focuses on visual preferences, recent research has demonstrated the value of integrating other sensory modalities. For example, using social media data to analyze urban soundscapes29 has revealed how auditory experiences vary across different groups and built environment contexts, and how they interact with visual factors. This suggests that future child-friendly street research could benefit from combining eye-tracking with sensory evaluation methods—capturing not only what children see but also what they hear, feel, and otherwise experience—to achieve more holistic assessments of their environmental encounters.

Practical implications

The results of this study provide targeted practical guidance for the optimized design of child-friendly school commuting routes:

Optimization of architectural elements: Building facades play an important role in urban space41. Prioritize the use of natural materials such as bricks and wood, increase the number of material types (≥ 3 types recommended) and color richness (≥ 3 types recommended) of building facades, reasonably set building signs with high-saturation colors (red, yellow, orange), and improve visual recognition while maintaining a unified style.

Optimization of architectural elements: Building facades play an important role in urban space41. Prioritize the use of natural materials such as bricks and wood, increase the number of material types (≥ 3 types recommended) and color richness (≥ 3 types recommended) of building facades, reasonably set building signs with high-saturation colors (red, yellow, orange), and improve visual recognition while maintaining a unified style.

Enhancement of greening elements: Systematic research has proved that trees have a great impact on preferences42, and natural spaces in cities have many positive impacts on cities43–46, indicating that they should receive more attention and space in cities. Research limitations and future prospects

This study still has several limitations.

First, regarding sample generalizability. The sample size is relatively limited (16 children), and all participants were recruited from primary schools around Hubei University of Technology in Wuhan, China. This geographic and cultural specificity limits the generalizability of our findings to other cities (e.g., first‑tier vs. third‑ and fourth‑tier cities) or countries with different street design standards, cultural contexts, and urban morphologies. Wuhan, as a large central Chinese city, features certain street characteristics (e.g., prevalence of gated communities, specific vegetation types, and a humid subtropical climate) that may not represent all urban forms. Moreover, the school commuting routes selected were all within a 200‑meter buffer of primary schools, which may not capture the diversity of longer or more complex commuting paths. Future research should expand the sample to cover different cities, school district types (urban centers and suburbs), and cultural settings to improve the generalizability of the research conclusions.

Second, regarding the laboratory setting vs. real‑world environment. The experiment was conducted in a laboratory using screen‑displayed street view photos. While this approach ensured experimental control and stimulus consistency, it cannot fully replicate the multisensory (e.g., auditory, olfactory, proprioceptive) and dynamic experiences of real street environments. In real‑world settings, children encounter moving vehicles, changing light conditions, ambient sounds, and social interactions, all of which may influence their visual attention and preferences. Our static, screen‑based presentation may therefore underestimate or overestimate certain attention patterns. In the future, real‑scene field experiments or virtual reality (VR) technology can be combined to enhance the ecological validity of the research scenario.

Third, regarding age aggregation. The age range covers 6‑14 years old, and the preference differences between younger children (6‑9 years old) and older children (10‑14 years old) are not further subdivided. There are significant differences in cognitive abilities and spatial needs between children of different age groups. Subsequent refined research should recruit larger samples to enable age‑stratified analyses.

Fourth, regarding individual differences. The impact of children‘ s individual characteristics (such as personality, daily activity habits, and prior experience with street environments) on visual preferences is not deeply analyzed. Subsequent studies can include more moderating variables to improve the understanding of preference mechanisms.

Fifth, regarding the stimulus set. The 12 street view photos were selected based on orthogonal design principles, but they represent a limited set of street scenes. Future research could use a larger and more diverse image database or continuous video recordings of actual commuting routes to capture a wider range of spatial configurations.

Future research can further expand the direction: First, explore the correlation between school commuting route element preferences and children’s travel safety and psychological well-being, and construct a complete theoretical chain of “spatial elements - visual preferences - behavioral performance”; Second, combine machine learning methods to establish a prediction model of children’s visual preferences, providing technical support for automated child-friendly space evaluation; Third, carry out long-term follow-up studies to analyze the long-term impact of spatial optimization on children’s cognitive development and behavioral habits. Fourth, the wide age range (6–14 years) was analyzed as a single cohort due to the limited sample size. Developmental psychology suggests significant differences in visual search strategies and executive function between a 6-year-old and a 14-year-old. Future studies with larger samples should stratify analyses by age groups (e.g., 6–9, 10–14) to uncover developmental trajectories in visual preferences. Fifth, while our eye-tracking paradigm offers high internal validity, the static, 2D street view images cannot fully replicate the multisensory (auditory, olfactory, kinesthetic) and dynamic (moving vehicles, changing light) experiences of real-world commuting. Future research should consider VR-based or mobile eye-tracking in situ.

Comparative analysis across different street space scenarios

To further elucidate the contextual sensitivity of children’s visual preferences, we compared attention patterns across four street typologies (Historical, High-greening, Facility-dominated, Conventional). In the High-greening samples (e.g., Sample 11), the green view ratio (≥ 50%) dominated attention (50%), while the influence of ground elements was suppressed (6.25%). Conversely, in Historical samples (e.g., Sample 5), architectural material diversity became the primary driver (attention proportion 81.25%), with greening playing a secondary role (6.25%). This scenario comparison reveals that no single element is universally dominant; rather, the ‘attention-weight’ of each factor shifts according to the spatial context. This finding provides nuanced guidance: optimizing a greening-dominated street requires different strategies (enhancing greenery hierarchy) than optimizing a historical one (preserving material diversity).

Conclusion

Taking children aged 6–14 as the research object, this study systematically explores the laws of children’s visual preferences for four core elements (architecture, ground, greening, and facilities) of street spaces in school commuting routes through eye-tracking technology, questionnaire surveys, and mathematical statistical analyses. The main conclusions are as follows:

There are significant differences in children’s visual preferences for street space elements in school commuting routes: In instantaneous attention allocation, architectural elements (37.5%) and facility elements (33.5%) are the primary attention objects; in cumulative fixation duration proportion, architectural elements (31.5%) and greening elements (29.9%) dominate, ground elements (13.4%) have the lowest attention, and there are statistically significant differences in the fixation durations of the four major elements in all samples (p < 0.01).

The key indicators affecting children’s visual preferences are clear: The number of building material types, the number of facade color types, and the visual proportion of architectural elements all have a significant positive correlation with fixation time; the green view ratio and the number of green plant types of greening elements are the core factors driving fixation behavior (correlation coefficients all > 0.47); the visual proportion of facility elements has a significant positive correlation with fixation time, while the number of facility types has no significant impact; the pavement refinement of ground elements can improve attention, but its visual proportion has a significant negative correlation with fixation time.

The core logic of children’s visual preferences is “high information density + strong visual stimulation”: Elements with bright colors, rich materials, and three-dimensional hierarchy are more likely to attract children’s attention, which is highly consistent with children’s concrete and emotional spatial perception characteristics, and also provides a core basis for the refined design of child-friendly spaces.

This study reveals children’s implicit preferences for street space elements in school commuting routes through quantitative methods, makes up for the deficiency of insufficient attention to micro-environmental elements in existing studies, and constructs a “factor indicator-visual preference” correlation model. The research conclusions can provide scientific support for the optimized design of school commuting routes in the construction of child-friendly cities, helping to create safe, comfortable, and child-centered daily travel spaces, and also enrich the research methods and theoretical achievements in the fields of child environmental psychology and urban planning.
