Vitamin D in systemic sclerosis: Why multivariable adjustment matters
Authors: Aurora Vicenzi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article by Pagnoni and colleagues on the link between vitamin D insufficiency and cardiovascular involvement in systemic sclerosis [1]. The work stands out for its clinical approach and rigorous standardization of echocardiography, and I wish to express my congratulations to the authors for their contribution.

The findings, including the high prevalence of hypovitaminosis D and the echocardiographic differences observed in subjects with reduced levels, raise awareness of the cardiovascular risk profile in SSc. While acknowledging these strengths, I believe that some methodological additions could further strengthen the message: a stratified analysis by severity of hypovitaminosis (≥30; 20–29; <20 ng/mL) would allow us to evaluate a dose-response gradient on echocardiographic variables and risk factors. Furthermore, for binary outcomes, multivariable logistic regression models (adjusting at least for age, sex, continuous BMI, season of sampling, renal function, cardiometabolic therapies, disease duration and phenotype) would provide more robust estimates than correlation alone on dichotomous variables.

In this perspective, it is useful to remember that a recent study has shown how a history of ischemic heart disease and/or atrial fibrillation and/or valve replacement is independently associated with one year mortality in patients with ARDS, reinforcing the need to rigorously control cardiovascular comorbidities when interpreting differences in indices such as PAPs and TAPSE/PAPs [2].

Furthermore, the need to adjust for PAH-specific therapies is supported by evidence that, in the portopulmonary setting, “upfront” combination therapy compared to monotherapy significantly improves haemodynamics and risk profiles and reduces clinical events [3].

Consistent with the pathophysiological rationale in connective tissue diseases, a study in pSS with interstitial lung disease documented sPAP values > 35 mmHg more frequently and a lower TAPSE/sPAP ratio, suggesting early signs of right ventricle-pulmonary artery decoupling. This reinforces the need, even in SSc, to consider the respiratory burden (DLCO, FVC, extent of ILD) and to include indices of RV–PA coupling in the interpretation of data [4].

Finally, recent evidence in pSS indicates sex differences in cardiovascular risk factors and instrumental parameters, with more marked alterations in males (pulmonary artery diameter and TVI-RVOT), despite a very low prevalence of PH when confirmed by right heart catheterization. This suggests that sex should be included as a covariate (and possibly considered in stratified analyses) in the interpretation of PAPs and TAPSE/PAPs also in SSc [5].

Because the study is retrospective, reverse causality cannot be ruled out (e.g., lower sun exposure and greater functional limitation in more severe cases). Mediation analyses (with BMI as a potential mediator) or, with due caution, instrumental approaches based on seasonality/latitude could offer clues to the direction of the effect. Looking ahead, prospective multicenter studies with clinical follow-up and "hard" outcomes, as well as pragmatic trials of supplementation targeting the most severe deficiencies, are the most suitable tools to verify whether correcting hypovitaminosis translates into measurable cardiac benefits in patients with SSc.

In summary, the study by Pagnoni et al. adds original evidence on a possible link between vitamin D and right ventricle-pulmonary circulation decoupling and early ventricular remodeling in SSc. With the caveats associated with the observational design and unmeasured confounders, further multivariate analyses, assessments of nonlinearity, and stratification by deficiency severity, disease phenotype, and sex can consolidate the interpretation and guide the design of targeted interventions.
