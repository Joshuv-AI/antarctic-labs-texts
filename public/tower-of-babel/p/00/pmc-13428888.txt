Risk prediction of postoperative acute kidney injury in patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting based on machine learning
Authors: Zhiyu Zhao, Wei Sun, Yawei Ma, Shidong Liu, Zhili Wei, Dianwei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify risk factors for postoperative acute kidney injury in patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting and to develop a machine learning model for effective risk prediction.MethodsWe extracted data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV database for patients who underwent off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting. Eight machine learning algorithms were trained, including logistic regression, support vector machine, gradient boosting machine, neural network, eXtreme gradient boosting, adaptive boosting, light gradient boosting machine, and categorical boosting. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and other relevant metrics. The best-performing model was further interpreted using Shapley additive explanations.ResultsPatients were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a 7:3 ratio. Models were trained on the training set, internally validated on the testing set, and externally validated using the electronic intensive care unit Collaborative Research Database. Independent predictors of postoperative acute kidney injury were age, body weight, bicarbonate, international normalized ratio, respiratory rate, and diastolic blood pressure. Among all models, categorical boosting achieved the highest performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.826 in the training set and 0.725 and 0.705 in the testing and external validation sets, respectively.ConclusionThe categorical boosting model, incorporating age, body weight, bicarbonate, international normalized ratio, respiratory rate, and diastolic blood pressure, provides a potentially valuable tool for predicting the risk of postoperative acute kidney injury in patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is characterized by the accumulation of atherosclerotic plaques in the coronary arteries, resulting in luminal stenosis and impaired myocardial perfusion. 1 It remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide. 2 Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the standard surgical treatment for CHD; however, acute kidney injury (AKI) is a major postoperative complication in this population. The reported incidence of AKI following CABG exceeds 40%,3,4 with approximately 2% of affected patients requiring renal replacement therapy. 5 Furthermore, the 90-day mortality among patients who develop AKI after cardiac surgery has been reported to approach 30%. 6 The pathogenesis of postoperative AKI is multifactorial, involving ischemia–reperfusion injury, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation.7,8 Its occurrence is associated with prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays, higher healthcare costs, and significantly increased mortality. 9 Off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (OPCABG), performed on a beating heart without cardiopulmonary bypass, has emerged as an alternative to conventional CABG. This approach is particularly relevant because prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time is a well-recognized risk factor for postoperative mortality. As a result, OPCABG has been increasingly adopted as a surgical strategy for patients with CHD.

The integration of artificial intelligence into the medical field is rapidly advancing. As a major branch of artificial intelligence, machine learning (ML) has become a key focus of research in clinical medicine.10,11 Compared with traditional predictive models, ML models can handle high-dimensional, large sample data and explore complex nonlinear relationships among different features. 12 Based on these characteristics, ML models can exhibit superior discriminative ability, accuracy, and stability. 13 For example, Deng et al. 14 examined the association between intraoperative hematocrit levels and postoperative AKI but did not establish an effective predictive model. Similarly, Qin et al. 15 investigated short-term outcomes, such as AKI, in patients undergoing OPCABG combined with valve surgery; however, they also failed to develop a dedicated prediction model. Although several predictive models for AKI after cardiac surgery have been proposed,16–19 these studies did not specifically isolate the OPCABG population, limiting their clinical applicability. To address this gap, we utilized patient data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database to construct and compare eight ML models: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network (NNET), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and categorical boosting (CatBoost). To further evaluate the generalizability of these models, we performed external validation using the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD). Our aim was to establish an effective and clinically applicable prediction model for postoperative AKI in patients undergoing OPCABG. The study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting guidelines.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from MIMIC-IV version 3.1 and eICU-CRD version 2.0, which include clinical information from patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2019. Institutional approval was obtained before initiating the study. Data extraction was performed using structured query language with the aid of Navicat Premium 16.0 (PremiumSoft CyberTech, Hong Kong, China). We passed the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD examinations and obtained database access. No additional ethical approval was required for this study because all patient records in the MIMIC-IV database were deidentified and time-shifted, and the investigators could not trace the data back to the identities of the patients.

Data collection

Inclusion criteria: Patients who underwent OPCABG during hospitalization were identified using the corresponding codes from the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th Revisions. The exact codes included, but were not limited to, 02100J3, 02100K3, 02110J3, 02120J3, 02130J3, etc. All independent variables were collected from the first measurement within the first 24 h after ICU admission, and the outcome data were extracted from the first 7 days after surgery.

Exclusion criteria: (a) patients with pre-existing kidney disease; (b) patients with duplicate records; (c) patients who died within 7 days postoperatively; and (d) patients with an ICU stay of less than 24 h.

We strictly adhered to the above inclusion and exclusion criteria during data collection, ultimately extracting 2638 patients from the MIMIC-IV database and 2391 patients from the eICU-CRD. The data collected for this study primarily included: (a) Demographic characteristics: sex, age, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI); (b) Medical history: myocardial infarction, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and chronic pulmonary disease; (c) Laboratory test data: hemoglobin, platelet count, white blood cell count, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), anion gap, bicarbonate, total bilirubin (TBIL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glucose, calcium, sodium, potassium, international normalized ratio (INR), and prothrombin time (PT); (d) Clinical scoring systems: Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and Charlson Comorbidity Index; and (e) Vital signs: heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Establishment and evaluation of ML models

We divided the 2638 patients obtained from the MIMIC-IV database into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, univariate analysis as well as univariate and multivariate LR analyses were performed to identify risk factors and independent risk factors for postoperative AKI in patients undergoing OPCABG. Based on the identified independent risk factors, eight ML models were developed: LR, SVM, GBM, NNET, XGBoost, AdaBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Within the training set, feature importance ranking was applied to determine the relative importance of each feature in each model. Model performance in both the training and testing sets was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, decision curve analysis (DCA) curves, and precision–recall (PR) curves. In addition, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score were calculated to further assess model performance. After the optimal model was identified, external validation was conducted using the patient cohort from the eICU-CRD to evaluate its generalizability. Finally, the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) method was employed for model interpretation.

Endpoints

Currently, the most widely accepted standard for the diagnosis of AKI is the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria proposed in 2012. 20 A diagnosis of AKI was established if any one of the following three conditions was met: (a) an increase in serum creatinine of >0.3 mg/dL (26.5 μmol/L) within 48 h; (b) an increase in serum creatinine of >50% within 7 days; or (c) urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for at least six consecutive hours.

Statistical analysis

First, data preprocessing was performed by removing duplicate samples and those containing outliers. Outliers were defined as values outside the range of 1.5 times the interquartile range. Samples with more than 20% missing data were excluded, whereas predictive mean matching was used to impute missing values in samples with less than 20% missing data. Subsequently, the MIMIC-IV dataset was divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio, and the eICU-CRD was used for external validation. Because of class imbalance between negative and positive samples in the training set, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was employed to balance the dataset. To further enhance model performance, fivefold cross-validation was conducted. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables are presented as counts (frequencies). The t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Data analysis and figure generation were performed primarily using R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, Austin, TX, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included a total of 2638 patients who underwent OPCABG from the MIMIC-IV database and 2391 patients from the eICU-CRD. Among the patients from the MIMIC-IV database, 1836 (69.6%) developed postoperative AKI. Other characteristics did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05). Table 1 presents the results of the univariate analysis for all patients in the training set. Feature selection in ML models

Univariate and multivariate LR analyses were performed on the training set, identifying age, body weight, bicarbonate, INR, respiratory rate, and DBP as independent risk factors for postoperative AKI in patients undergoing OPCABG (Table 3). The main reason BMI was significant in the univariate LR analysis but not in the multivariate LR analysis is that BMI serves as a composite measure of obesity-related metabolic disorders and cardiovascular burden (e.g. insulin resistance, hypertension, and chronic inflammatory state), rather than an independent causative factor in univariate analysis. When these more direct mechanistic variables were included in the multivariate model, the independent effect of BMI was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant.

ML models in the training set

We partitioned 70% of the data from the MIMIC-IV database to the training set. The proportion of patients with AKI in the training set was 69.6%, and there was a sample imbalance. After applying SMOTE, the numbers of positive and negative samples were both 843 cases. The SMOTE parameters were k = 5, perc.over = 50, and perc.under = 300. Using the previously identified independent risk factors, ML models were trained on the training set, and the internal feature importance of each model was ranked, as shown in Figure 3. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for each ML model evaluated on the training set was as follows: CatBoost, 0.826 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.807–0.846); LR, 0.695 (95% CI: 0.670–0.720); SVM, 0.779 (95% CI: 0.757–0.801); GBM, 0.745 (95% CI: 0.722–0.768); NNET, 0.724 (95% CI: 0.700–0.748); XGBoost, 0.752 (95% CI: 0.730–0.775); AdaBoost, 0.752 (95% CI: 0.729–0.775); and LightGBM, 0.787 (95% CI: 0.766–0.809). Among all models, CatBoost demonstrated the best performance, whereas LR showed the poorest performance. The ROC curves for all models in the training set are presented in Figure 4. Evaluation results for all models in the training, testing, and validation sets are summarized in Table 4. DCA indicated that all models provided a net clinical benefit (Figure 5). DCA showed that, within the threshold probability range of 10%–80%, the net benefit of most models was higher than the baseline of “full intervention” and “no intervention”, suggesting that application of these models in clinical practice may provide a net clinical benefit, which has potential practical value. Precision–recall (PR) curves demonstrated a good balance between precision and recall for each model (Figure 6). Within the training set, the ranking of internal feature importance for the CatBoost model was as follows: body weight, age, DBP, respiratory rate, bicarbonate, and INR. The calibration curve, calibration intercept and slope, and Brier score were used to evaluate model calibration. A lower Brier score indicates a smaller overall prediction error of the model. The results showed that most models were well calibrated, with high agreement between predicted probabilities and actual event rates (Figure 7). In addition, to improve model performance, we performed fivefold cross-validation in the training set. The cross-validation parameters were method = “repeatedcv,” number = 5, and repeats = 5. ML models in the test and validation sets

The remaining 30% of the MIMIC-IV samples were used as the testing set to evaluate the performance of the models trained on the training set. In addition, data from the eICU-CRD served as an external validation set for evaluation of the optimal model, CatBoost. Evaluation of the models on the testing set yielded the following AUCs: CatBoost, 0.725 (95% CI: 0.621–0.750); LR, 0.661 (95% CI: 0.622–0.700); SVM, 0.653 (95% CI: 0.614–0.693); GBM, 0.657 (95% CI: 0.617–0.696); NNET, 0.650 (95% CI: 0.610–0.690); XGBoost, 0.656 (95% CI: 0.617–0.696); AdaBoost, 0.639 (95% CI: 0.598–0.679); and LightGBM, 0.638 (95% CI: 0.598–0.679). Among these, the CatBoost model achieved the highest AUC, whereas the LightGBM model had the lowest. The ROC curves for the testing set are shown in Figure 8. External validation of the CatBoost model using data from the eICU-CRD, the model demonstrated good performance, with an AUC of 0.705. The ROC curve for the validation set is shown in Figure 9.

Model interpretation

After evaluating model performance in the training, testing, and external validation sets, the CatBoost model was identified as the optimal performance. The CatBoost model parameters were iterations = 40, use_best_model = TRUE, eval_metric = “AUC,” depth = 3, learning_rate = 0.1, random_seed = 123, l2_leaf_reg = 5, subsample = 0.7, and colsample_bylevel = 0.7. Discussion

We utilized the MIMIC-IV database to investigate risk factors for postoperative AKI in patients with CHD undergoing OPCABG. A total of 2638 patients were included, of whom 1836 developed postoperative AKI, representing approximately 69.6% of the cohort. This proportion is higher than the 47.3% reported in a previous study, 21 which may be attributed to differences in patient populations and variations in the level of medical care across centers. In addition, the MIMIC-IV database predominantly includes patients admitted to intensive care units, who are generally more critically ill. Through multivariate LR analysis, we identified age, body weight, bicarbonate, INR, respiratory rate, and DBP as independent risk factors for postoperative AKI in this cohort. Based on these features, eight ML models were developed: LR, SVM, GBM, NNET, XGBoost, AdaBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Among these, the CatBoost model demonstrated the best performance across the training, testing, and validation sets. Furthermore, external validation of the optimal CatBoost model using the eICU-CRD confirmed its robustness, achieving an AUC of 0.705. The main reason for the lower AUC observed in the external validation set is that the distribution of MIMIC-IV data and eICU-CRD data is not completely consistent, which also limits the generalization performance of the model.

In 2018, Lee et al. 22 used an XGBoost model to predict postoperative AKI in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, and the model demonstrated good performance. However, the high heterogeneity of surgical types included in their study substantially limited its practical predictive power, rendering it unsuitable for effective prediction in the specific cohort of patients undergoing OPCABG. In 2022 and 2023, Yu et al.23,24employed nomograms to predict postoperative AKI in patients undergoing OPCABG. Nevertheless, these studies were constrained by small sample sizes and lacked external validation to assess model generalizability. Given the scarcity of research on predictive models for postoperative AKI in patients undergoing OPCABG using large sample sizes and multiple ML algorithms, we aimed to establish an effective ML-based prediction model. We also explored the associations between each independent risk factor and postoperative AKI. As patient age increases, the glomerular filtration rate of the kidneys decreases, and the reduced renal reserve makes older patients more susceptible to postoperative AKI. Multiple studies 25 have identified age as a risk factor for AKI after CABG.26,27 Obesity is significantly associated with underlying conditions such as CHD and diabetes, thereby reducing overall patient health and increasing susceptibility to postoperative AKI through various mechanisms,28,29 including oxidative stress. 30 A low respiratory rate can lead to respiratory acidosis, which subsequently causes abnormal bicarbonate levels and ultimately resulting in altered urine acidity. Changes in urine acidity may modify oxidant substrates, enhance iron-mediated free radical activity, and promote hemoglobin cast abnormalities, thereby contributing to postoperative AKI. 31 Bradybreathing causes carbon dioxide retention and respiratory acidosis. Hypercapnia directly causes renal vasoconstriction and reduces renal perfusion. At the same time, acidosis inhibits myocardial contractility, dilates peripheral blood vessels, and further reduces effective circulating blood volume and renal blood flow. To compensate for respiratory acidosis, the renal proximal tubules absorb a large amounts of bicarbonate, leading to a secondary increase in serum bicarbonate. This process significantly increases oxygen consumption transported by the renal tubules and aggravates renal medullary hypoxia. Therefore, preoperative hyperhydrocarbonemia may be associated with abnormal breathing. Such patients have been in the “renal perfusion edge” state, and any slight hemodynamic fluctuation during the operation is easy to break the fragile balance between oxygen supply and oxygen consumption, and cause ischemic AKI.

Renal blood supply primarily depends on adequate circulating blood volume. A decrease in DBP indicates insufficient circulating volume, prompting compensatory mechanisms that prioritize blood flow to the heart and brain. This further reduces renal perfusion and promotes the development of prerenal AKI. 32 Previous studies have also identified blood pressure as an independent risk factor for AKI in patients with cardiovascular disease. 27 Therefore, we speculate that the main mechanism by which INR functions as a predictor lies in the fact that an increase in INR increases the risk of bleeding-related complications. Subsequent blood loss and insufficient circulating volume may increase the incidence of prerenal AKI through the same mechanisms described above. In addition, activation of the sympathetic renin–angiotensin system induced by hemorrhage can further constrict the renal vasculature and aggravate renal medullary ischemia. Ischemia–reperfusion subsequently triggers an inflammatory cascade that damages the renal vascular endothelium and tubular epithelium, thereby accelerating the progression of AKI through the interaction between hypoperfusion and coagulation-associated inflammation.

Limitations

Despite the significance of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. (1) The training and validation datasets for our ML model were derived exclusively from the MIMIC-IV database and the eICU-CRD; therefore, the model's generalizability requires further validation in other medical centers. (2) This study focused solely on short-term postoperative complications in the OPCABG patient cohort, without follow-up on long-term outcomes. (3) The training set exhibited sample imbalance. Although this issue was addressed using the SMOTE oversampling technique, it may still have influenced the final model performance. (4) The external validation dataset had a relatively small sample size, which could affect the assessment of model performance. (5) Because the data distributions of the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD datasets were not completely consistent, there are certain limitations in model generalization. (6) Limited by the source of data, some intraoperative and postoperative characteristics such as operative time, were not included in the analysis, which may cause bias into the model’s performance.

Conclusion

Using the MIMIC-IV database, we identified age, body weight, bicarbonate, INR, respiratory rate, and DBP as independent risk factors for postoperative AKI in patients with CHD undergoing OPCABG. Based on these features, eight ML models were developed and externally validated. Among them, the CatBoost model demonstrated the best performance, suggesting that it has potential for clinical application. However, our findings require further validation in multicenter, prospective studies.
