Influence of a Co‐Designed Alcohol and Other Drug Education Program on Clinician Attitudes Toward People Who Use AOD: A Quantitative Evaluation
Authors: Shohreh Razavy, Vasiliki Betihavas, Jo River, Suzanne Sheppard‐Law, Megan Moses, Catherine Smith, Euan Macleay, Fiona Orr, Jane Currie, Kevin Street, Maureen Steele, Olivia Hollingdrake, Robert Mason, Susan Colman, Veronica Morton, Mark Goodhew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHealth professional can hold stigmatising attitudes toward people who use alcohol and other drugs (AOD), affecting therapeutic engagement and health outcomes. Evidence suggests that education can challenge health professionals' bias. However, education is only one strategy, and its impact depends on broader system and organisational factors. Yet AOD education remains limited and rarely incorporates lived‐experience perspectives. Co‐designed and co‐delivered AOD education offers a way to support clinicians to reflect on stigma and strengthen collaborative, person‐centred care. This study aimed to assess whether a co‐designed AOD education program, delivered by lived experience educators, influenced clinicians' attitudes toward people who use AOD. Using a quantitative, non‐randomised pre–post quasi‐experimental design, this paper reports quantitative findings from a pre‐post survey that captured clinician demographics and attitudes using a modified version of the Australian National Centre for Education and Training on Addiction (NCETA) AOD Attitudinal Brief Scale. Post‐education survey results indicated that the education was associated with significant improvements in attitudes, including greater recognition of social and structural influences on substance use and reduced endorsement of personal blame, anger, disappointment, and punitive views (all p < 0.01). Overall, the program contributed to a reduction in stigma and more positive clinician attitudes toward collaborative decision‐making with people who use AOD, underscoring the value of embedding lived experience in clinician education.

Introduction

Healthcare professionals, like the general public, often hold stigmatising attitudes toward people who use alcohol and other drugs (AOD) (Neville and Roan 2014; Volkow 2020; Copeland 2020; Horner et al. 2019), which can result in disengagement of people using AOD from healthcare services (Simon et al. 2020). Clinicians receive little to no AOD education (Smyth et al. 2021; Victorian Alcohol and Drug Association 2019; Klimas et al. 2021), and when they do, it seldom addresses the impacts of stigma and discrimination or the perspectives of AOD treatment service users (Goodhew et al. 2023). Co‐designing and co‐delivering AOD education in partnership with people with a lived experience of substance use is a potential path forward, as it facilitates clinicians to reflect on their attitudes toward AOD use and learn what is important in care and treatment from the perspectives of AOD service users (Goodhew et al. 2023).

In this paper, lived experience educators refer to team members who draw on their personal experience of substance use and service engagement to co‐design and deliver the education sessions, and to contribute to the interpretation and dissemination of the study findings. Co‐design in health education strives for authentic partnerships and ensuring equal participation of people with lived experience, clinicians and academics. Stakeholders co‐decide at all stages of the initiative, including planning, design, delivery and evaluation phases (Brand et al. 2021). While co‐design has predominantly been applied to enhance mental health education (Happell, Waks, et al. 2019; Horgan et al. 2018; Parnell et al. 2023), it is rarely employed in AOD education. Our prior work (Goodhew et al. 2023) involved the co‐design and co‐delivery of an undergraduate nursing subject on AOD. Findings indicated that the approach challenged students to reflect on stigmatising views of AOD use and motivated them to work more collaboratively with people who use AOD (Goodhew et al. 2023). Building on this prior work, this current study involved co‐designing and co‐delivering an AOD study day for clinicians working in AOD and mental health services. This paper, co‐authored with lived experience educators, presents the quantitative findings of an attitudinal survey conducted as part of a broader evaluation of a co‐designed AOD education program, with qualitative components reported separately. The study examined whether the co‐designed education influenced clinicians’ attitudes towards people who use AOD and increased their knowledge of the care needs of AOD service users. The education program comprised three co‐designed modules, with the development process and module content described in the Methods section.

Background

AOD use represents a major global public health challenge, with the World Drug Report projecting a global increase in drug use of approximately 11% by 2030 (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime 2021). The global burden of disease for substance use dependence is estimated at 1.1%, with high‐income countries experiencing the highest burden at 3.1% (Our World in Data 2025). In Australia, alcohol use accounts for 1.9% of the total burden of disease, while drug use accounts for 0.9% (Our World in Data 2025). Mental health conditions and substance use disorders, which frequently co‐occur, are among the five chronic conditions contributing most to the national burden of disease (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW] 2022). AOD use can have serious impacts on individuals’ well‐being as it is associated with liver disease, cardiovascular disease, multiple cancers, blood‐borne viruses, accidental injury, and mental health conditions (AIHW 2025). AOD is also associated with a higher risk of homelessness (AIHW 2019) and can contribute to premature mortality, including the substantial increase in opioid‐related overdose deaths reported globally (The Lancet Regional Health‐Americas 2023).

Despite the high burden, health professionals reported limited training in AOD and harm reduction (Abram et al. 2025; Klimas et al. 2021; Smyth et al. 2021; Victorian Alcohol and Drug Association 2019; Watts et al. 2023). When training is received by clinicians, it is most often grounded in the biomedical model, which frames substance dependence as a chronic, relapsing neurobiological disease in which people lack control despite adverse consequences of substance use (Campbell 2012). Such training frequently overlooks aspects of care that are important to people accessing AOD services, including non‐stigmatising and trauma‐informed care, access to harm reduction interventions, and opportunities to collaborate with clinicians about treatment and care decisions (Goodhew et al. 2021). Furthermore, studies highlight that biomedical model contributes to stigma by emphasising deficits and hopelessness rather than strengths among people who use AOD (Barnett et al. 2018; Bryant et al. (2022).

Healthcare clinicians' limited AOD training may contribute to negative attitudes about substance use that reflect broader societal stereotypes. Research indicates that clinicians can view a person who uses drugs as an antisocial, criminal, manipulative and drug‐seeking person who is undeserving of care (Van Boekel et al. 2013; Copeland 2020; Horner et al. 2019). Such negative attitudes can lead to stigmatising and discriminatory treatment and low‐quality care, and to poorer health outcomes, preventing people who use AOD from engaging in and seeking future healthcare (Simon et al. 2020). Co‐designed and co‐delivered AOD education offers a potential solution, as it creates space for clinicians to reflect on the impacts of negative attitudes and to learn directly from people who use AOD, and about their priorities in treatment and care.

Most co‐design education initiatives in healthcare focus on mental health curricula. Research findings indicate that clinicians and students who engaged in co‐designed AOD programmes became more reflective and empathic, recognising alternatives to deficit‐based discourses and the impacts of stigma. They also appreciated the importance of collaborating with service users and developed greater hope for recovery (Horgan et al. 2018; Happell, Waks, et al. 2019; Happell, Platania‐Phung, et al. 2019; Parnell et al. 2023).

Most evaluations have been qualitative, and the frequency of studies using quantitative surveys to examine whether co‐designed education influences attitudes is increasing. Whitelaw et al. (2023) used an anti‐stigma scale to evaluate co‐designed mental health education, and Cheung et al. (2023) applied an ageism scale to evaluate education co‐designed with elderly people. While both studies reported statistically significant shifts in attitudes, in Whitelaw et al. (2023) the significance was observed only among student participants, whereas shifts among healthcare clinicians were not significant. Cheung et al. (2023) reported significantly improved attitudes towards older adults following the intergenerational co‐designed education program.

Co‐designed and co‐taught initiatives in AOD services and educational settings are rare and have shown promise in humanising AOD education and enhancing understanding of stigma, trauma, and evidence‐based harm reduction interventions (Goodhew et al. 2023; Roussy et al. 2015; Valenti and Allred 2020). This project addresses gaps in knowledge regarding the quantitative attitudinal outcomes of co‐designed and co‐delivered AOD education to health care clinicians. The aim of this study was to assess whether participation in a co‐designed AOD education program influenced clinicians’ attitudes toward people who use AOD.

Methods

Study Design

This study utilises a quantitative, non‐randomised pre–post quasi‐experimental design to assess changes in clinicians' attitudes following participation in a co‐designed AOD education program. The quantitative component reported in this paper forms part of a broader evaluation that also includes qualitative focus group data, which will be reported separately.

Ethical approval (ETH01833) was obtained from each of the three participating health districts, and the investigators adhered to ethical principles throughout the study.

Study Sites and Participants

The education program was implemented between March and May 2025 across three health districts in New South Wales, Australia. A purposive sampling approach was used to recruit healthcare clinicians. Participants were eligible if they were healthcare clinicians or peer workers employed in an AOD or mental health clinical setting. Each sites clinical educators promoted the study day, and attendance was approved by their manager.

Participation in the research evaluation was voluntary, and only individuals who attended the education and provided informed consent completed the survey and were included in the study. Across the three participating health districts, a total of 120 healthcare clinicians and peer workers attended the education study days, of whom 107 consented to participate and completed the survey. A total of five education study days were delivered, with two sites hosting two study days each and one site hosting a single study day, with 18–35 clinicians attending each study day.

Educational Intervention

Between 2022 and 2024, a research team comprising lived experience educators, clinicians, and academics co‐designed an AOD education study day for clinicians working in AOD and mental health services. Goodhew et al. (2025) outline the co‐design process, which was developed to provide clinicians with a clearer understanding of service user perspectives and needs for AOD treatment and care, and to highlight best practice, including harm reduction, trauma‐informed approaches, as well as collaborative approaches to working with service users including principles of person‐centred and strengths‐based assessment and care planning. The program was delivered as a one‐day, face‐to‐face study day co‐facilitated by three lived experience educators, two academics, and one clinician across three health services. Data Collection

Quantitative data were collected through a pre‐post survey. Clinicians completed a survey consisting of demographic and background questions, along with a modified version of the Australian National Centre for Education and Training on Addiction (NCETA) AOD Attitudinal Brief Scale (NCETA 2006) (see Table 1). This scale has not been validated but has been extensively used in AOD education and training contexts to assess clinicians' attitudes towards people who use AOD (NCETA 2006). More recently, a revised scale was used by Ford et al. (2021) to establish healthcare clinicians’ attitudes to mothers who are opioid dependent. The survey was revised by removing the term ‘problematic’, as it was considered stigmatising by the lived experience researchers and educators. In addition, one item measuring ‘sympathy towards people who use drugs’ was replaced with a question on support for current ‘drug laws’ that was addressed in the education. One of the objectives of the education program was to emphasise the importance of collaboration between clinicians and service users. To address this collaboration objective, the last two Questions (Q7 and Q8) were reworded from ‘deserving and entitled’ to ‘medical care’, to ‘collaborate with clinicians about decisions regarding their treatment and care’ (refer to Table 1).

Data Analysis

All quantitative data were exported from REDCap and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25). Surveys were completed on paper to maximise response rates during workshop delivery, and responses were subsequently entered into REDCap by the research team. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics. Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were employed to assess pre‐ and post‐intervention changes in clinician attitudes on the NCETA AOD Attitudinal Brief Scale. A Spearman's rank‐order correlation was conducted to examine the relationship between years of AOD experience and attitudinal change scores (Questions 1–8). An exploratory inter‐item correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among attitudinal change scores. These pairwise associations were visualised using a correlation heatmap generated in Python. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with α < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Participants were highly educated, with 37.4% holding a bachelor's degree and 36.4% holding a master's degree. The sample included a range of professional roles. The most reported AOD service setting where clinicians worked was mental health (45.8%), followed by community‐based AOD services (24.3%). While over half of the participants (56%) held a postgraduate qualification, only 19.6% reported that their postgraduate study was specific to AOD. Additionally, over half (55.7%) had previously attended education facilitated by a person with lived experience of substance use (percentages are derived from additional items within the main clinician survey that are not displayed in Table 2).

Changes in Attitudes Toward People Who Use AOD

A total of 104 participants completed matched pre‐ and post‐intervention attitudinal measures across eight domains using a 5‐point Likert scale. Changes across the eight attitudinal items are summarised in Table 3 and illustrated in Figure 2. Participants demonstrated increased agreement for Q1 ‘life circumstances’, with the median rising from 4 (IQR = 1.75) to 5 (IQR = 1), Z = −3.86, p < 0.001. This indicates very strong evidence for an awareness of the social determinants influencing substance use. Scores related to Q2 ‘personal responsibility’ show a decrease in rank distribution (Z = −3.41, p = 0.001) despite the median remaining at 3 (IQR = 1). This signifies very strong evidence for awareness of systemic contributors, instead of individual blame. With Q3 ‘anger towards people who use drugs’, the median remained unchanged, but a ranked response was observed (Z = −4.08, p < 0.001). This provides very strong evidence of an increased rejection of anger and possible alignment with nonjudgmental, trauma‐informed approaches. Attitudes related to Q4 ‘disappointment to people who use drugs’ showed median stable scores, but with a notable rank decrease (Z = −5.08, p < 0.001). This indicates very strong evidence for reduced moral judgment and greater compassion. Perceptions of Q5 ‘concern for people who use AOD’ remained unchanged in terms of median score (4, IQR = 2), but it showed a rank shift (Z = −2.31, p = 0.021). This signifies evidence of a shift in concern for service users. Support for Q6 ‘support for current drug laws that criminalise drug use’ had a median decreasing post‐intervention (Z = −4.19, p < 0.001). This suggests a significant reduction in endorsement of criminalisation of illegal drug use following the education. Attitudes regarding Q7 ‘deserve to collaborate in treatment and care decisions’ showed a non‐significant rank shift, despite stable median scores of 5 (Z = −1.606, p = 0.108). Finally, Q8 ‘entitled to collaborate in care decisions’ showed a significant rank shift (Z = −3.957, p = 0.000).

A Spearman's rank‐order correlation was also used to examine the relationship between years of AOD experience and change in attitudinal survey scores (Q1–Q8). A small but significant positive correlation was found between time working in AOD and change in Q5 scores, ρ (99) = 0.204, p = 0.043. This suggests that clinicians with more AOD experience tended to show greater improvement in their attitude related to the Q5 item (To what extent do you feel concerned towards people using drugs?). No other changes in attitudinal items were significantly associated with AOD experience.

Additionally, inter‐item correlation analysis using Spearman's rank‐order correlations was conducted to examine the relationships among change scores for the eight attitudinal items measuring clinicians' perspectives following participation in the co‐designed AOD education programme. Change scores were calculated as the difference between post‐ and pre‐intervention responses for each item. Correlations were visualised using a heatmap to identify potential patterns and co‐shifts of clinician attitudes across domains (Figure 3).

Several significant positive correlations were identified, ranging from small to moderate in magnitude, indicating that some attitudinal domains tended to shift together following the intervention. For instance, Q2 (individual responsibility) was positively correlated with Q4 (disappointment) (ρ = 0.296, p = 0.002), reflecting a small‐to‐moderate association between shifts in these constructs. Q3 (anger) showed a moderate correlation with Q4 (ρ = 0.395, p < 0.001) and a small correlation with Q6 (support for drug laws) (ρ = 0.198, p < 0.05). Notably, Q7 (deserving collaboration) was moderately correlated with Q8 (entitlement to collaboration) (ρ = 0.436, p < 0.001), suggesting that changes in clinicians' perceptions of service user deservingness were accompanied by changes in their endorsement of collaborative care principles. Although most other inter‐item relationships were weak or non‐significant, the findings highlight modest interconnected attitudinal shifts, particularly around empathy, legal framing, and collaborative care, while indicating that changes were not uniform across domains.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a co‐designed AOD education programme can produce significant positive shifts in clinician attitudes across multiple domains. The intervention led to statistically significant shifts in attitudes in all questions, except Q7 (deserve to collaborate in treatment and care decisions), which remained high both before and after the delivery of education. The lack of significant change suggests this principle may already be embedded or require deeper systemic reinforcement. This interpretation aligns with the likelihood that clinicians may already be aware of consumer participation policies and frameworks that strive for clinicians to listen and collaborate with service users, so that care and treatment meet their needs (NSW Ministry of Health 2019; Goodhew et al. 2018). Across the remaining attitudinal domains (Q1–Q6), clinicians demonstrated meaningful improvements characterised by reduced attribution of blame, lower negative emotional responses such as anger and disappointment, and decreased support for punitive drug policies. These shifts reflect a broader movement toward more empathetic, collaborative and recovery‐oriented perspectives. For Q8 (entitled to collaborate in care decisions), the significant shift reflects strong evidence that being taught by lived experience educators heightens clinicians' awareness of the importance of service users having the right to partner in decisions about their treatment and care.

Overall, participants showed a marked reduction in stigmatising views and a strengthening of support for service users collaborating in decisions regarding their care and treatment. This reflects a move away from healthcare clinicians viewing people who use AOD through a criminalised and deficit lens (Cohen et al. 2022; Van Boekel et al. 2013; Scher et al. 2023; Ellis et al. 2020) toward recognising them as individuals who are experts in their own lives, whose lives are often profoundly shaped by social determinants, trauma and punitive drug laws (Cohen et al. 2022; Scher et al. 2023). The co‐designed and co‐delivered education, which centred on lived experience perspectives, appears to support clinicians to engage with understandings of substance use that extend beyond biomedical and deficit‐based framings.

The positive shifts in clinicians' attitudes in this study align with other co‐designed education programme evaluations for students and school children (Whitelaw et al. 2023; Cheung et al. 2023). Whitelaw et al. (2023) reported improved knowledge, attitudes and behavioural intentions among health care practitioners and students, and Cheung et al. (2023) found significant improvements in students' attitudes toward older adults. These findings collectively suggest that co‐designed education incorporating lived‐experience perspectives can foster more supportive attitudes. This pattern is consistent with broader evidence that education can reduce negative attitudes among staff toward people who use drugs, regardless of clinical experience or work setting (Howard and Holmshaw 2010). Such attitudinal shifts align with an emerging evidence‐based positioning of lived experience as a transformative andragogical tool in health education, suggesting that partnering with lived experience in the design and delivery of training can catalyse meaningful shifts in perspectives, reduce stigmatising attitudes, and enhance empathy among nursing and allied health professionals (Goodhew et al. 2023; Parnell et al. 2023). These findings support the view that attributing AOD use to systemic, social and economic determinants can reduce judgmental attitudes and promote a safer, more trusting care environment, an approach widely recommended for engaging people with substance dependence (Bartlett et al. 2013).

The results also suggest that clinicians with more AOD experience showed a greater increase in concern (Q5), consistent with the small positive correlation between years of experience and change in the Q5. This suggests that prior professional exposure may shape the way clinicians respond to education about people who use substances, although the mechanisms underlying this pattern remain unclear. The attitudinal shift in ‘concern’ (Q5) appears to arise from direct, real‐world exposure, whereas other attitudes, such as views on personal responsibility or drug policy, may be more resistant to change without targeted education. Similar patterns are reflected in recent literature, where nursing students engaged in extended clinical mentorship with people who use opioids reported significant gains in empathy, described as a transformation ‘from fear to empathy’ (Schuler and Horowitz 2020). Similarly, Hamed et al. (2025), found that more experienced mental health nurses showed greater empathy, aligning with our finding that experience may foster compassion. The lack of correlation between experience and other attitude changes suggests that time in the field alone is insufficient to shift all dimensions of stigma. For example, experienced mental health nurses have been found to retain stigmatising beliefs, indicating that biases, particularly around blame or policy, may persist without targeted education (Hamed et al. 2025). This highlights the need to pair clinical experience with structured stigma‐reduction strategies, such as workshops or contact‐based training, to consistently challenge these biassed views and foster positive attitudes and empathy (Hamed et al. 2025; Fokuo et al. 2025; Mahmoud et al. 2019).

The inter‐item correlation analysis revealed that changes in clinician attitudes following the co‐designed AOD education programme tended to shift together across related constructs, highlighting the interconnected nature of stigma‐related beliefs. These interconnected attitudinal shifts are consistent with stigma theory (Link and Phelan 2001; Goffman 1974), which conceptualises stigma as a multidimensional system in which blame, negative emotion and social distancing mutually reinforce each other. The positive association between Q2 ‘personal responsibility’ and Q4 ‘disappointment’ suggests that clinicians who viewed substance use as a personal failing were also more likely to report feelings of disappointment toward people who use drugs. This pattern aligns with attribution theory, which posits that assigning personal responsibility for a condition can evoke negative emotional reactions, such as anger or disappointment, that contribute to stigmatising and rejecting attitudes (Corrigan et al. 2003). Similarly, the correlation between Q3 ‘anger’ and both Q4 ‘disappointment’ and Q6 ‘support for criminalisation’ aligns with studies showing that negative affective responses are often accompanied by support for punitive drug policies (Wogen and Restrepo 2020). Viewing service users as ‘deserving of help’ (Q7) was correlated with stronger beliefs in their entitlement to ‘collaborative care’ (Q8), indicating a distinct ‘collaborative‐care’ attitude cluster underscoring the internal consistency and conceptual closeness of beliefs about shared decision‐making, constructs that have been shown to improve with interventions that foreground lived experience and recovery‐oriented care (Goodhew et al. 2023).

These findings are supported by evidence from mental health education, where co‐produced programmes have led to simultaneous improvements in empathy (Ross et al. 2024), reductions in stigma (Morgan et al. 2021) and shifts in services toward change agency (Soklaridis et al. 2020). These findings support the view that stigma is multidimensional (Scott et al. 2025), with attitudinal changes clustering along related dimensions. In this case, an ‘attribution‐of‐blame’ dimension versus an ‘empathy/collaborative‐care’ dimension shifts in one dimension, such as reduced blame, may drive parallel changes in others, such as decreased punitive attitudes and increased empathy and collaboration. Together, these results suggest that clinician attitudes may shift as interconnected patterns, particularly when education challenges core assumptions and promotes collaborative, rights‐based perspectives.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self‐reported attitudinal measures may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants might have felt inclined to report more favourable attitudes following an intervention focused on stigma reduction. Second, the short interval between the intervention and post‐survey completion precludes conclusions about the sustainability of attitudinal change over time. In addition, certain Questions, particularly those related to collaboration (e.g., Q7 and Q8), showed high baseline endorsement, suggesting potential ceiling effects that may have limited the ability to detect further positive shifts. The question measuring ‘concern’ (Q5) toward people who use drugs also presents interpretive challenges. While changes were observed, their meaning may vary depending on whether concern is understood as empathy or as a form of moral judgement, highlighting the need for greater clarity in attitudinal scale constructs and their interpretation in future evaluations.

Additionally, more than half of the participants (55.7%) had previously attended education facilitated by people with lived experience of substance use. This prior exposure may have contributed to favourable baseline attitudes, potentially reducing the extent of measurable change observed following the intervention.

While the original NCETA AOD Attitudinal Brief Scale has been widely used in AOD education and training contexts, it has not been validated. In conjunction with people with lived experience, future research should examine the psychometric properties of our revised instrument to support its use in evaluating the impacts of AOD education initiatives.

A further limitation of this study is the absence of a comparator or control group. As a pre–post evaluation, observed changes in attitudes cannot be attributed solely to the education program and should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Conclusion

This study provides encouraging evidence that a co‐designed and co‐delivered AOD education program, co‐delivered with lived experience educators, can positively shift clinician attitudes across multiple stigma‐related domains. Participants demonstrated reduced attribution of personal blame, less support for punitive policies, and stronger endorsement of collaborative decision‐making with people who use AOD. The clustering of attitude changes suggests that shifts in one dimension of stigma, such as reduced blame, may facilitate broader attitudinal transformation. While some areas showed more nuanced or context‐dependent changes, the overall findings support the value of embedding lived experience into clinician education. These results reinforce the potential of co‐produced training to foster more compassionate, and inclusive approaches to AOD care. Future research exploring the long‐term impact of such programs on clinical practice and client outcomes would be valuable. Health services and policymakers may benefit from considering formal integration of co‐designed, stigma‐reducing education into routine workforce development.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

These findings suggest that co‐designed AOD education has the potential to support stigma‐aware and collaborative approaches to care. Potential reductions in personal blame and punitive attitudes, alongside greater openness to collaboration, may enhance therapeutic engagement with people who use AOD. Integrating lived‐experience educators into routine training offers a practical approach that may strengthen reflective practice and support more equitable, trauma‐informed AOD care across health services. Such shifts could help reduce barriers to treatment participation for many clients.
