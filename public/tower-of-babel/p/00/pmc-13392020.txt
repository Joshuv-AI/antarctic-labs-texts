Investigating clinical practice variations in coronary artery disease treatment using reinforcement and transfer learning
Authors: Peyman Ghasemi, Matthew Greenberg, Danielle A Southern, Bing Li, James A White, Joon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to investigate if reinforcement learning (RL) can detect clinical practice variations and if transfer learning (TL) can mitigate resulting distribution shifts across patient sexes and sites. Using data from over 41,000 patients with obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) in Alberta, Canada from 2009 to 2019, we conducted three experiments: 1) evaluate physician behavior policies across stratified groups using off-policy evaluation (OPE); 2) develop and evaluate RL policies using conservative Q-learning and OPE for each group; and 3) apply TL to improve RL performance on target groups. Female patients had significantly lower expected rewards under their own physician policies compared to males. Applying the physician policies learned from male patients improved rewards for females but remained lower than those for males. RL policies outperformed physician policies. TL with only 10% of target group data effectively mitigated distribution shifts. Residual confounding warrants further research to validate our findings.Subject terms: Interventional cardiology, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) continues to be a major global health concern, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality1,2. In patients with obstructive CAD, the selection of optimal treatment strategies, inclusive of medical therapy (MT), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), is a complex task that requires careful consideration of individual patient factors. These factors include patient-specific coronary artery anatomy, the location of stenosis, co-morbidities, and the patient’s overall health profile3–5. Although randomized controlled trials have provided valuable general insights6–10, they often lack the specificity needed for personalized treatment planning.

Reinforcement learning (RL), a machine learning technique that focuses on optimizing decision-making by learning from interactions with an environment11, offers a promising solution to this challenge. By analyzing comprehensive patient data, RL can develop individualized treatment policies aimed at maximizing clinical benefits, thereby assisting healthcare professionals in making more informed and patient-specific revascularization decisions. A policy π in this context represents the probability distribution over possible actions (e.g., treatment options) for a given state S (e.g., patient features such as age, symptoms, or medical history). In healthcare problems, policies can be learned to imitate physicians’ behavior or optimize treatment decision-making.

Distribution shift is a major barrier to deploying personalized RL policies trained from large-scale data to real-world clinical environments. This is related to alterations in the statistical properties of data between the environment the model was trained in and the target environment it is deployed to. Such shifts can adversely affect the model’s performance because the data patterns it learned may no longer hold true in the new environment12. In the context of healthcare RL policies, distribution shifts can occur when a treatment policy (e.g., physician policy or optimal RL policy) developed from historical patient data is applied to a unique patient population or clinical environment, potentially leading to less effective or even harmful decisions.

Transfer Learning (TL) addresses distribution shifts by leveraging pre-trained models and fine-tuning them to perform similar tasks in a target domain. TL is particularly useful when there are insufficient data to train a new model from scratch. While TL has been primarily used in supervised machine learning, it can also be a valuable tool in reinforcement learning models, particularly in offline settings, to enhance model performance in the presence of distribution shifts13,14. To date, such approaches have been applied in RL to learn tasks from offline demonstrations and accelerate or improve sample efficiency in subsequent tasks15–18.

In this study, we aimed to employ RL and TL to detect and address clinical practice variations and distribution shifts observed between cardiac care sites as well as patient sexes in the context of coronary revascularization decision-making in patients with obstructive CAD. We employed off-policy evaluation (OPE) to examine the impact of distribution shifts on both physician and RL policies, using data stratified by both patient sex and site. Finally, we explored the effectiveness of TL in resolving the observed issues.

Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 provide the distributions of the RL choices vs. physician choices of treatments. Comparing the treatment distributions recommended by the CQL policies with physicians’ actual choices showed that the RL policies systematically increased CABG recommendations, with this tendency being strongest in the least conservative models. At α = 0.01, the CQL policy recommended CABG for 49.6% of male and 23.4% of female patients, compared with physician rates of 15.8% and 14.1%, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Increasing the conservatism weight α progressively shifted the recommended distributions toward the physician rates: the CABG recommendation rate for males fell to 30.7% at α = 0.1 and 21.8% at α = 0.5, while the corresponding rate for females fell to 16.1% and 12.9%. For the site stratification, this over-recommendation was more modest and less consistent, being most pronounced at Site 1 (33.2% vs. 16.2% at α = 0.01), whereas Sites 2 and 3 showed little change (Supplementary Table 4). Across both stratifications, higher α values were associated with greater agreement between the CQL and physician policies, consistent with more conservative settings pulling the learned policy closer to the behavior policy.

As shown in Fig. 1, CQL models with an α=0.01 (least conservative setting) performed better or close to the optimal model trained solely on the entire target data, even when using only 10% of the target dataset. However, as α increased (more conservative), performance declined or showed little improvement, especially when exposed to larger portions of the target dataset. This finding is expected, as a higher α value shifts the policies closer to the physician behavior policy, which is inherently not optimal. Figure 1 shows that fine-tuned CQL models with α=0.5 generally achieved lower expected rewards, with a notable decline in one case (base model: Site 1, target data: Site 3). This is possibly due to the most conservative CQL model converging to the behavior policy of the target group. A similar pattern is observed with α=0.1 in the sex-stratified data, where the TL results for smaller subsets of target data closely resembled the behavior policy results but improved as more training data were used. We also showed that, for most data portions, training a model on the target data from scratch did not achieve significantly better results than TL.

Sensitivity analyses

We heuristically defined age>80 or single-vessel disease as infeasible for CABG. The CABG-infeasible subgroup represented a substantial portion of the test set, ranging from 30.2% to 42.6% across the evaluated strata (n = 856/2009 female, 2021/6618 male; 1381/4031 Site 1, 890/2481 Site 2, 578/1911 Site 3). Within this subgroup, the same-group transfer advantage reported in the main analysis was preserved: at α = 0.5, same-sex policies outperformed cross-sex policies (female to female WIS = 0.70 vs. male to female = 0.68; male to male = 0.64 vs. female to male = 0.61; Supplementary Table 5), and analogous same-site advantages were observed across most site strata (Supplementary Table 6). However, the unconstrained CQL policies still recommended CABG to a non-trivial fraction of infeasible patients (4.5–44.2% across all policy/group/alpha comparisons), with higher conservatism reducing this rate from a mean of 21.2% (α = 0.01) to 10.8% (α = 0.5) but not eliminating it. Together, these results indicate that the principal conclusions regarding cross-group policy transfer are robust to filtering on treatment feasibility, while also showing that explicit feasibility constraints would be required for safe deployment of any of the learned policies.

Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) corroborated the WIS-based conclusions: the CQL policy exceeded the behavior policy on the same data group for every stratification and α (Supplementary Tables 7-10) and the female-below-male gap persisted under both estimators. Effective sample sizes were large (medians ≈ 700–3600; minimum ≈ 130), indicating that the WIS estimates were not driven by a handful of high-weight trajectories. These findings were consistent across both estimators for the most stable results, including CQL outperforming the behavior policy within each group and the persistent performance gap between female and male patients. At α = 0.01, the learned policies diverged most from physician behavior (agreement of 35.9% for males and 40.4% for Site 1), a setting in which WIS is least reliable. In these cases, FQE estimates were lower than WIS estimates, and one cross-site evaluation (the Site 1 policy evaluated on Site 2 patients) produced a particularly wide confidence interval (0.314–0.645). Accordingly, these low-overlap comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

We also conducted an E-value framework sensitivity analysis for the confounding factors (Supplementary Table 11). Our E-value analysis indicates that the strongest observed CQL advantages (e.g., Site 3, E-value = 1.85; Female, E-value = 1.71) would require a moderately strong unmeasured confounder to be explained away. Smaller advantages, particularly at higher conservatism levels, are more susceptible to residual confounding (E-values 1.1–1.2). The support for RL over physicians was firmest at low α values (largest E-values), yet these policies over-recommended CABG and were the least deployable. At α = 0.5, several E-values fell to 1.15–1.19 although the policies became more reasonable. Statistical support and deployability thus ran in opposite directions. Importantly, our central finding concerns systematic practice variation across groups rather than absolute policy superiority, which would require group-specific confounding patterns to be an alternative explanation.

Discussion

In this study, we used OPE to estimate the effects of distribution shifts and clinical practice variations regarding revascularization decision-making in patients with CAD. We identified significant differences in patient management across sexes and sites, which influenced the expected rewards from the OPE of the learned physician behavior and RL policies across groups. However, we demonstrated the potential of TL in addressing these distribution shifts by fine-tuning RL models using varying amounts of target group data.

The findings from this study show that RL and OPE can elucidate clinical practice variations, even with only offline patient trajectories. Whereas RL provides a way to capture and encapsulate physicians’ clinical practice patterns hidden in retrospective clinical data, OPE helps us understand the impact of varying clinical practices. This study also demonstrated that TL can offer an effective method to minimize adverse outcomes related to clinical practice variations.

Our findings support previous studies19,20 with respect to the potential of the CQL algorithm in addressing distribution shifts. Furthermore, the application of TL to further address distribution shifts in CQL policies remains an underexplored area, and our results warrant future research in this direction.

An interesting observation from group-specific physician behavior policies is that they did not reliably demonstrate the best performance for the same patient group. For example, female patients showed significantly lower estimated rewards when managed according to the behavior policy trained from their own data. In contrast, estimated rewards were higher when managed by a policy trained from male patients. This may be explained by inherent biases of clinical practice where females are often less likely to receive aggressive treatments21 such as CABG (P < 0.001, effect size of 0.05 in our dataset), while most RL policies tended to increase the rate of CABG recommendations22. This observation aligns with the literature indicating women, despite presenting with more advanced CAD (e.g., at an older age and with more comorbidities)23–25, often receive less aggressive treatments26. Furthermore, women commonly experience delays in accessing invasive diagnostic procedures to identify the presence of obstructive CAD, propagating delays in appropriate care24,27,28. Future studies may investigate the underlying reasons for these disparities, particularly the notable difference in surgical revascularization rates between men and women. We speculate that bias toward less aggressive revascularization strategies may exist in women with obstructive CAD due to prior reports of poorer outcomes versus men29,30. This historic evidence has the potential to influence future decision-making, translating into reduced referral rates for surgical intervention. This and other historic clinical practice biases may exist that may, in part, explain the lower observed rate of aggressive revascularization in women versus men in our cohort. However, women in this cohort were also older, had a higher prevalence of diabetes, and presented later than men; some of the sex-disparity may therefore reflect differences in biological or clinical case mix as well.

We identified similar evidence of implicit bias in the management of obstructive CAD between sites. Our findings showed that, in general, unique distributions of therapeutic strategies historically existed between sites across Alberta, and that these resulted in different outcomes for patients. Specifically, the behavior policy learned from the largest site (Site 1) generally led to superior results when applied to the other sites. These variations in regional practices can be attributed to differences in observable clinical factors (e.g., patient demographics, comorbidity profiles), local resources or “unwarranted factors” such as professional uncertainty, local clinical culture (e.g., more conservative vs. more aggressive), level of professional autonomy, and differing objectives31,32. Unlike policies based solely on modeling local behavior, RL policies are optimized to achieve a mathematically defined common objective or “reward function”, supporting more balanced and consistent decision-making across varied clinical environments.

Our second experiment, focussed on the development of offline RL policies, highlighted the influence of group-specific training on the generalizability of CQL-based models. In this setting, models trained from a given group G consistently outperformed group G’s behavior policy when tested on the group G’s data, even at the highest levels of conservatism in the CQL model (ERGπCQLG]≥ERGπBG]∀G). Additionally, in most cases, the CQL policies outperformed the behavior policy of a given group G when applied to group G (ERGπCQLGˆ]≥ERGπBG]∀Gˆ for most G). More conservative policies (higher α for CQL) tended to underperform when tested on different groups, as they more closely mirrored the behavior policy of the original group. However, if a group’s behavior policy was already achieving superior outcomes for the target group (e.g., behavior policy of the male group applied to females), the corresponding high-α CQL models also performed well, as would be expected. Our findings underscore a critical need to systematically evaluate clinical practice variations and ensure CQL-based decision support tools are developed from data resources that achieve more optimal outcomes.

It is difficult to know the extent of alignment between the RL recommendations and the revascularization guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and the American College of Cardiology. Direct comparisons are difficult because the RL policies are personalized and complex (402 features are considered), whereas the clinical guidelines are one-size-fits-all and only consider a small number of risk factors. Also, the purpose of our RL modeling was to create a more optimized treatment selection strategy than the current clinical practice. This means that the RL recommendations are expected to deviate from the clinical guidelines at least in some patient cases, and this was shown in our results if we assume that the clinical guidelines are a major driver of the behavior policies. Clinician validation of the RL recommendations will be necessary to investigate the alignment with the clinical guidelines in detail.

Our final experiment explored the value of TL to fine-tune CQL models to different target groups. We found that fine-tuning a base CQL model (trained on the largest group) using a small portion of the target dataset (as little as 10%) achieved outcomes close to the optimal CQL model trained using 100% of the target data. The fact that only 10% of the target data were sufficient likely stemmed from that only the last layer of the CQL network was fine-tuned and that the encoder learned from the largest group was still mostly applicable to other groups. For site-specific models, however, this was found to be sensitive to the level of conservatism applied to the CQL model. With more conservative models, expected rewards of the base model decreased with fine-tuning as they were more strongly influenced by the target site’s behavior policy. This was not observed for sex stratification where a fine-tuned base model trained from male data outperformed the behavior policy of the female group, being consistent across all conservatism levels and target data portions. This suggests that practice variations across sites are likely more significant than those across sexes, which may explain why highly conservative TL on sites led to less optimal policies.

This present study has limitations. First and foremost, this is a retrospective observational study and as a result, residual confounding was likely present and may have impacted the results. Although the data used in this study captured clinical presentations and patient characteristics in a comprehensive way, some important aspects of coronary revascularization decision-making were not considered in RL modeling, such as socioeconomic factors, patient preferences, and existing clinical practice guidelines. Additionally, although our analysis spans three sites, all operate within a single health system (Alberta Health Services) with shared clinical guidelines. Therefore, the cross-site results reflect within-system rather than true external generalization. Further research based on propensity score matching or randomized controlled trials is warranted to validate the findings from this study.

Second, the results of this study are based on the assumption that all three treatment options are viable options for all patients. This is a strong assumption since the technical feasibility of PCI or CABG may be low in practice depending on lesion location and characteristics. Patient characteristics and preference may also preclude invasive procedures. Notably, the least conservative CQL models (α = 0.01) achieved the highest OPE rewards, yet also recommended CABG most frequently, including for patients in the CABG-infeasible subgroup. As a result, the highest-performing models were the least readily deployable in practice without incorporating explicit feasibility constraints. A manual validation of RL recommendations by clinicians is warranted and should be pursued as future work. Such validation should involve angiogram images as well so that clinicians can understand the coronary anatomy and lesion characteristics.

Third, another limitation was the restricted nature of our TL experiment, inclusive of freezing the entire encoder, and using the same α parameter for fine-tuning, and a fixed number of steps for convergence. Future studies may explore different TL strategies (e.g., progressive networks33) controlling the training steps by implementing an early stopping mechanism, and employing less conservative α during fine-tuning. The latter would be beneficial in cases where maximal utilization of the target population is needed to optimize the policy.

Fourth, the offline nature of our study was also an important limitation; expected rewards were calculated using the OPE method. Although we minimized bias by using a hold-out test set, it is crucial to validate this study in a real-world online setting (e.g., randomized clinical trial) for a better understanding of the RL models. We believe that fine-tuning in an offline setting using a small set of target data prior to deploying the model in an online environment will further reduce the risks associated with RL-based policies. It is important to keep in mind that the RL models must be used as a decision support tool, and the ultimate treatment decision lies with clinicians.

Lastly, there were other general limitations to the RL modeling of CAD treatments, which are discussed in detail in our prior study22, including the Markov Decision Process assumption, the formulation of the reward function (e.g., composition of factors, their weights, and choosing a linear time-to-event structure for simplicity), the limited action space, and not capturing intermediate clinical decisions between catheterizations.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that RL modeling with CQL effectively optimizes coronary revascularization strategies in patients with CAD, outperforming physicians’ behavior policies despite practice variations. OPE analysis highlighted significant treatment disparities in physician policies and revealed that RL can identify optimal pathways not apparent through traditional methods. Additionally, we showed that TL efficiently adapts RL models to new patient groups using minimal data; fine-tuning with as little as 10% of target data achieved outcomes comparable to full-data models, particularly with less conservative CQL settings. These findings underscore the potential of RL and TL to advance personalized CAD treatment, reduce disparities, and promote equitable healthcare delivery.

Methods

Dataset

This study used the same patient cohort as our previous study22, comprising 41,328 adult patients ( ≥ 18 years of age) with obstructive CAD who underwent 49,521 cardiac catheterization procedures for diagnostic coronary angiography. The data was sourced from the Alberta Provincial Project for Outcome Assessment in Coronary Heart Disease (APPROACH) Registry, covering the period from January 2009 to March 2019. This Registry includes comprehensive clinical, demographic, and procedural data for patients with CAD in Alberta, Canada. Additionally, Alberta Health Services (AHS) administrative databases were used to collect patient history and outcome data. Three sites with a cardiac catheterization lab in Alberta, one in Calgary and two in Edmonton, were included in the dataset. Obstructive CAD was defined as ≥50% stenosis in the left main coronary artery or ≥70% stenosis in any other major coronary artery. Patients with ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) were excluded because the emergency nature of STEMI leads to immediate PCI and precludes treatment decision-making.

We stratified the data by sex and site into 2 groups (Male and Female, with Male as the largest group) and 3 groups (Sites 1–3, with Site 1 as the largest group), respectively. Patients who appeared in multiple groups under the site-based stratification (e.g., those who underwent catheterization procedures at more than one site) were excluded (4.36% of all catheterization records and 2.10% of all patients) to ensure each patient was captured in only one group for a fair comparison among the sites. Compared with the included patients, the excluded multi-site patients were slightly younger (65.1 ± 11.2 vs 66.8 ± 11.4 years, p < 0.001), had a higher 3-year MACE rate (37.1% vs 26.2%, p < 0.001), and were less likely to undergo revascularization (CABG: 10.3% vs 16.6%; PCI: 54.1% vs 57.1%; MT: 35.6% vs 26.3%; p < 0.001).

Missing values were imputed using the median value of each feature. Our previous work22 also utilized multiple imputation by chained equations using random forests but the impact on the RL results was negligible. More details are presented in the footnote of Supplementary Table 12.

RL modeling

The RL modeling and OPE methodologies in this study followed the approach developed in our previous research22 to optimize CAD treatment in terms of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). We selected relevant features based on automatic34 and expert-based methods and used them to form the patient’s states. In total, there were 402 features available (full list provided in Supplementary Table 12). However, at each stratification, group-identifying features were excluded (i.e., removed the sex feature when stratified by sex and removed the site feature when stratified by site).

Figure 2 summarizes the Markov Decision Process (MDP) modeling process, action space, and reward formulation. We modeled CAD treatment as an MDP where each coronary catheterization procedure represented a step. We formed episodes for patients using these steps and ending them with the patient’s death or freedom from further catheterization for three years following the treatment. Possible actions were the three major treatments for obstructive CAD: MT only, PCI, or CABG.

Formulating CAD treatment as an MDP.

This schema models each cardiac catheterization procedure as a transition step, where the subsequent treatment represents the action, and the follow-up outcome serves as the reward. Transitions may lead to another catheterization or a terminal state, which could be the patient’s death or freedom from further catheterization for three years or more following treatment. The action space encompasses the set of treatments available at each step, while rewards are derived from follow-up outcomes. Negative rewards are assigned to all states based on major adverse cardiovascular events, adjusted for time, while terminal states yield positive rewards, adjusted for the patient’s survival duration. Some of the graphical components of this figure (e.g., hearts, tombstone, human hand) are AI-generated.

The composite clinical outcome used in this study was a 5-point MACE metric, which included acute myocardial infarction, stroke, repeat revascularization (PCI or CABG), cardiovascular mortality within 3 years, and all-cause mortality within 90 days of cardiac catheterization (the 90-day all-cause window is intended to capture procedure-related death, including the non-cardiac perioperative mortality of more invasive options such as CABG, whereas non-cardiovascular deaths beyond this window cannot be reliably attributed to the index treatment decision). All these five MACE components have been commonly used in previous studies35, and our previous work using the same data set as this present study found that the impact of using a different variant of our MACE definition on RL models was minimal22. The reward function was based on freedom from this 5-point MACE outcome, adjusted by the time to event: −1 if it occurred immediately after treatment, and 0 if it happened three years or more after. Additionally, a positive reward was given at the final step, linearly adjusted for survival time post-treatment: +1 for survival of three years or more, and 0 if the patient passed away immediately.

We split each stratified patient group into training, validation, and test sets with proportions of 70%, 10%, and 20%, respectively. We used Conservative Q-learning (CQL)36,37 with three different values of 0.01, 0.1, and 0.5 for the conservatism parameter (α) and a discount factor of γ = 0.99 to optimize the treatment policy. The α parameter in CQL allows us to variably restrict policy deviations from the trajectories of physician policies, making the policies align more-so or less-so with clinical practice. We tuned other hyperparameters (number of layers, number of hidden neurons, activation function, and learning rate) using grid search on a reasonable set of values (see Supplementary Table 2), based on our previous study22.

We developed the physician policies (local behavior policy model) using K-means clustering to define the unique patient states (each cluster as a patient state), selecting the smallest number of clusters that maximized the accuracy of predicting physicians’ actions (using majority voting within each cluster). Although supervised classification models may be suitable for this task, the predicted probabilities of each state-action are typically not calibrated to reflect real-world probabilities. This simpler method provides statistically meaningful ratios among similar patients.

We used the Weighted Importance Sampling (WIS) method38 with bootstrapping (1000 samples and 1000 steps) to estimate the expected value and confidence intervals of the accumulated rewards for different policies derived from various groups. To avoid undefined importance weights, we added a small constant (ε = 1e-10) to the behavior-policy probability in the denominator and returned zero if a sample’s total weight collapsed to zero. Any uncertainty in the behavior-policy probability can directly impact the WIS estimate.

For each stratification (with respect to sex and site), we conducted three experiments described in the ensuing sections. Figure 3 provides an overview of the pipeline used for data processing, policy estimation (including both physician and RL policies), and the three experiments. The source code of all three experiments is shared in our GitHub repository39. This study followed the TRIPOD + AI checklist (see the Supplementary Information).

Experiment 1: evaluating physician policies on different patient groups

For each stratification strategy (sex or site), we extracted the physician policy for each stratified group and estimated the expected rewards when that policy was applied to each group, including the one the policy was learned from. The goal of this experiment was to assess how mimicking physicians’ decisions made in specific patient groups would perform when applied to other patient groups.

Experiment 2: evaluating optimized RL policies on different patient groups

For each stratification strategy, we trained RL policies for each stratification group using CQL and estimated the expected rewards when they were applied to all groups, including the one those policies were learned from. The goal of this experiment was to assess how RL policies learned from specific patient groups would perform when applied to other patient groups.

Experiment 3: fine-tuning RL policies for different patient groups

We selected the CQL-based policy of the largest group from both sex (Male) and site (Site 1) stratification strategies as the base model and fine-tuned it with data from the other groups (target groups). To measure the effect of target dataset exposure on model performance, we fine-tuned models on 1%, 10%, 20%, 50%, and 100% subsets of randomly selected samples from the target training dataset. We repeated this fine-tuning for each subset 5 times with different random seeds to avoid selection bias. We also trained models on the data portions from scratch (no TL) and reported whether these models achieved significantly better results than the TL-based models at a significance level of 0.05.

The entire encoder of the base model was frozen, and fine-tuning was only performed on the final layer, which estimates the values of each action. This was undertaken as our initial experiments showed that fine-tuning the entire network could lead to catastrophic forgetting and result in suboptimal policies in some cases. Additionally, we used 0.1 of the original base model’s learning rates for fine-tuning, based on our preliminary experiments. The fine-tuning process was completed in just 1 epoch (10,000 steps).

Sensitivity analyses

To examine whether our findings were driven by patients for whom CABG would be clearly less feasible, we performed a post-hoc treatment-feasibility sensitivity analysis. We heuristically defined a conservative CABG-infeasible subgroup as patients with single-vessel disease or age greater than 80 years at catheterization. We then re-ran WIS OPE on this restricted subset using the previously selected CQL policies and physician behavior policies for both the sex- and hospital-stratified analyses, across all three conservatism levels. We additionally quantified how often the learned CQL policies still recommended CABG within this infeasible subgroup.In order to strengthen the WIS results, we performed the Fitted Q-Evaluation40 (FQE; γ = 0.99) for the behavior and CQL policies and reported the effective sample size (ESS) of each WIS estimate.

To assess the robustness of the RL vs. physician comparisons to unmeasured confounders, we computed E-values41 for each same-group comparison. The E-value quantifies the minimum strength of association (on the risk ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both treatment assignment and the outcome to fully explain away the observed difference between CQL and physician policy values. For each comparison, we computed the ratio RR = WIS(CQL)/WIS(Physician) and derived E-values for both the point estimate and the confidence interval lower bound.
