Influence of Charge Block Length on Conformation and Cluster Formation of Atactic Peptide Polyampholytes
Authors: Winnie H. Shi, Amanda B. Marciel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We investigate the effect of the charge block length on the chain conformation and phase behavior of atactic peptide polyampholytes (PAs) with equal numbers of cationic and anionic ionizable monomers. Atactic peptide PAs were produced by using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS), which enables precise control of the molecular weight, monomer sequence, and chain tacticity. Using circular dichroism (CD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, we observe that the atactic peptide PAs show no evidence of secondary structure formation, regardless of block length. Turbidity measurements show that the atactic peptide PAs remain in solution over a wide-range of salt concentrations. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements reveal that the chain conformation of atactic peptide PAs is compressed compared to expectations for neutral systems. Interestingly, we find that the atactic peptide PAs form block length dependent multichain clusters in solution. These results are consistent with a recent theory predicting that PAs have a block length and concentration dependent threshold for phase separation.

Introduction

Polyampholytes (PAs) are macromolecules that contain cationic and anionic monomers along their polymer backbone. , They exhibit complex material properties arising from intra- and interchain electrostatic interactions between charged monomers. For example, PA chains may swell in salted conditions due to the shielding of attractive electrostatic interactions, which is referred to as the antipolyelectrolyte effect. This behavior may be leveraged in environmental and biomedical applications where salt concentrations are non-negligible, including in critical mineral separations, wastewater treatment, and enhanced oil recovery, as well as in tuning the formation and stability of structural biomaterials. Producing PA materials for these applications necessitates controlling multiple length and time scales, which requires addressing fundamental knowledge gaps regarding the sequence-dependent molecular interactions that govern chain conformation, mesoscale structure formation, and phase behavior. Advances in this area may also lead to a more complete understanding of the role of unstructured biological PAs (i.e., intrinsically disordered proteins, IDPs) in the formation of phase-separated biomolecular condensates critical for cellular biogenesis. −

Early theoretical studies showed that dilute solution PA chain conformation is strongly influenced by the relative ratio of cationic and anionic monomers along the polymer backbone, predicting that charged-balanced (symmetric) and net-charged (asymmetric) PAs adopt collapsed and extended conformations, respectively. − Beyond charge symmetry, the charge monomer sequence has also been predicted to affect PA chain conformation. − Alternating symmetric PAs, for example, were found to be more expanded compared to random and more blocky (i.e., larger runs of like charges) sequences. More recently, theory and simulation studies have examined a broader range of charge monomer sequences and found that chain collapse increased with charge blockiness. ,− Experimental validation of symmetric PA chain conformation, however, has been difficult due to the propensity of these systems to macroscale phase separate at low solution concentrations.

The solution behavior of random PAs is well studied. It has been shown that random PAs become less soluble as the net charge of the chains decrease. It is thus expected that macroscale phase separation will occur at solution conditions, where a single isolated chain is in a collapsed conformation. Solution behavior, however, can also be affected by the sequence of charged monomers along the PA backbone. Wittmer et al. showed that solubility decreased as charge blockiness increased for symmetric PAs. Phase diagrams of symmetric PAs with varying charge blockiness have revealed that increasing block length increased the tendency to phase separate. ,, Model PAs with precise-sequence control have been recently produced using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) to scrutinize predictions from theoretical and computational models. , Consistent with these predictions, Madinya et al. found that syndiotactic peptide PAs exhibited an increased propensity to phase separate as block length increased. In a previous study, we showed that isotactic peptide PAs, used to mimic IDPs, also exhibited block length dependent phase separation. Isotactic and syndiotactic peptide PAs, however, can exhibit secondary structure formation due to the patterned chirality of the monomers along the chain. This creates a challenge in the interpretation of the electrostatic contributions to PA chain conformation and phase behavior and, more broadly, determining which mechanisms drive this instability (i.e., nucleation and growth and spinodal decomposition). Additionally, these constructs phase separate at relatively low concentrations, making it challenging to interrogate dilute solution chain conformation as a function of charge block length. Thus, there is a need to investigate the influence of block length on chain conformation and phase behavior by using peptide PAs that are more weakly associating.

In this study, we investigate the influence of charge block length on the chain conformation and phase behavior of atactic peptide PAs with equal numbers of cationic and anionic ionizable monomers. Using circular dichroism (CD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, we show that these systems exhibit no discernible secondary structure, enabling direct investigation of the electrostatic contributions that affect chain conformation and phase separation. Turbidity measurements show that the atactic peptide PAs remain in solution over a wide-range of salt concentrations. Using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), we show that atactic peptide PAs exhibit compressed chain conformations compared to expectations for neutral polymers. Interestingly, multichain clusters are observed upon increasing block length and polymer concentration. These results are consistent with theoretical predictions showing a block length and concentration dependent threshold for phase separation, and they provide mechanistic insight into this behavior.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

Synthesis

Atactic peptide polyampholytes (PAs) with varying block lengths (N p = 1–8) were synthesized using standard FMOC solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) at a 0.15 mmol scale on Rink Amide MBHA resin LL (Novabiochem) following our previously published protocols. To produce atactic PAs, a racemic mixture of l- and d-chiral amino acid monomers in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) was used during the coupling step, resulting in random stereochemistry at each monomer position. Atactic peptide PAs were then cleaved from the resin using a 95/2.5/2.5% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/H2O/triisopropyylsilane (TIPS) solution for 2.5 h and subsequently precipitated using cold diethyl ether. The precipitant was dried under N2 and dissolved in Milli-Q water. Samples were then purified using reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) and molecular weights confirmed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-ToF) spectrometry. Purified atactic peptide PAs were then ion exchanged using AG 1-X2 anion exchange resin (Bio-Rad) to replace TFA ions with Cl ions. After ion exchange, the solutions were adjusted to pH 7 using 1 M NaOH and 1 M HCl, and dialyzed using 1 kDa MWCO Spectra/por 6 Standard RC dialysis membranes to remove excess ions. Dialyzed solutions were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C prior to solution preparation. Detailed synthesis and purification protocols may be found in the SI.

Solution Preparation

Atactic peptide PAs were dissolved in desired salt solutions (0–3 M NaCl in Milli-Q water) at the polymer concentrations (0.5–5.0 wt %) specified. Each sample was mixed on a tube rotator in oscillation mode for a minimum of 12 h prior to sample characterization.

Characterization

For all characterization methods, data sets were processed using in-house Jupyter Notebook (Python) scripts. Detailed protocols can be found in SI.

Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier-Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) Spectroscopy

ATR-FTIR spectra of 2.0 wt % atactic peptide PA solutions in D2O were taken using a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer to evaluate secondary structure. Sample and D2O data were collected by performing 64 scans with 8 cm–1 resolution and air background. Spectra were normalized to the 1563 cm–1 carbonyl stretching peak after manual background subtraction.

Circular Dichroism (CD) Spectroscopy

CD measurements were taken on a JASCO J-810 or JASCO J-1500 spectropolarimeter at 0.1 wt % atactic peptide PA in H2O and at 20 °C using a 1 cm path length Spectrosil quartz cuvette (9-F-Q, Starna Cells). CD spectra were collected from 190–280 nm at a digital integration time (D.I.T.) of 1 s, data pitch of 0.5 nm, and scanning speed of 200 nm/min for a minimum of 5 accumulations. Data were then background subtracted and converted to mean residue ellipticity (MRE).

Turbidity Characterization

Solution turbidity was evaluated at pH 7 for increasing added NaCl concentrations. Absorbance of 2.0 wt % atactic peptide PA solutions was measured at 600 nm in a 96-well plate using a TECAN Infinite M1000 Pro microplate reader. The absorbance measurements were measured in triplicate where the plate was shaken orbitally at 3 mm diameter for 10 s before each measurement. Details on calculating turbidity from absorbance measurements may be found in the SI.

Small- and Wide-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS and WAXS) and Data Analysis

Static SAXS and WAXS measurements for atactic peptide PA solutions were taken using a 1.5 mm thickness flow cell setup at beamline 16-ID (LiX) at the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) at Brookhaven National Laboratory. , Data was collected at a 0.082 nm wavelength and 3.7 m detector distance resulting in a q-range of 0.007–3.2 Å–1. Each sample was measured 10 times at 0.5 s exposure time. The data were then reduced to 1D and averaged with at minimum 7 of the 10 measurements taken using the python packages liXtools, py4xs, and the Jupyter Notebook script provided by the LiX beamline. The averaged spectra were then background subtracted using the corresponding (H2O or added NaCl in H2O) solvents. I(q) is then converted to absolute units using the solution transmission values from the SAXS experiments.

The radius of gyration R g and the forward scattering intensity I(0) of the soluble atactic peptide PAs and clusters were determined by fitting 1 ln[I(q)]=ln[I(0)]−Rg23q2 to the linear region of a Guinier plot (ln­(I) vs q 2) bounded by q max R g < 1.3. I(0) from Guinier analysis was then used to calculate the molecular weight M w , and second virial coefficient A 2. Kratky plots were normalized using R g and I(0) determined from Guinier analysis. Detailed protocols may be found in SI.

The form factor P(q) for a semiflexible polymer with excluded volume interactions , and an added cylinder cross-sectional radius was used to model the atactic peptide PAs. The form factor was implemented as a plugin model in SasView. This model is parametrized by the cylinder cross-sectional radius R, chain contour length L c, and Kuhn length b. In brief, we assumed a log-normal distribution for L c to capture dispersity and multichain clustering. The log-normal distribution f(x; x̅, σ) is implemented by 2 P(q)=scale⟨V⟩∫Rf(x;x̅,σ)F2(q,x)dx+background where F(q, x) is the scattering amplitude, ⟨V⟩ is the average particle volume, x̅ is the mean, and σ is a parameter describing the width of the underlying normal distribution. The fits were performed using the Differential Evolution Adaptive Metropolis (DREAM) algorithm within the SasView software package using sampling parameters provided in SI and a fixed Kuhn length of b = 10.0 Å, as estimated by previous studies on polypeptides. − Convergence of the model parameters was confirmed for all fits.

Results and Discussion

To study the influence of block length on the dilute solution chain conformation and phase behavior of PAs, we used standard FMOC solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) to produce a series of atactic peptide PAs with varying block lengths from N p = 1 to N p = 8 at a constant chain length of N = 32 (Figure a). SPPS enables precise control over the monomer sequence, molecular weight, and chain tacticity. We used a racemic mixture of d/l-lysine and d/l-glutamic acid to produce atactic chains (Figure b). All peptide PAs were purified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and their molecular weight assessed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight spectroscopy (MALDI-ToF) following our previously published protocols (SI).

(a) Charge sequence and (b) chemical structure of atactic peptide polyampholytes (PAs). All atactic peptide PAs consist of 32 monomers arranged in block lengths N p. Each anionic monomer (blue circles) consists of a randomly ordered d/l-chiral glutamic acid residue and cationic monomer (red circles) of a randomly ordered d/l-chiral lysine residue.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy were used to determine the degree of secondary structures present in the atactic peptide PAs. FTIR spectra show two peaks at ≈1646 cm–1 and ≈1564 cm–1 indicative of random coil chain conformations with no appreciable secondary structure formation for all block lengths (Figure a). CD spectra show no preferential absorbance of left or right circularly polarized light, and thus no distinct peaks in molar residue ellipticity [θ] are observed for all block lengths (Figure b). We also observe no appreciable secondary structure formation with added NaCl (Figure S2), compared to our previous observations in l-chiral peptide PAs. This further confirms that introducing d-chiral residues to produce atactic peptides is effective in disrupting secondary structure formation, consistent with previous studies incorporating d-chiral amino acids. , This synthetic system thus minimizes the role of additional interactions and enables a more direct study of the block length dependent electrostatic effects on PA phase behavior and chain conformation.

(a) ATR-FTIR spectra of 2.0 wt % atactic peptide PAs in D2O. Spectra were normalized to the 1563 cm–1 carbonyl stretching peak. (b) CD spectra of 0.1 wt % atactic peptide PAs in H2O.

Next, we assessed the qualitative phase behavior of the atactic peptide PAs at 2.0 wt % as a function of block length N p and added NaCl concentration using turbidity measurements (Figure ). Low turbidity is observed for all block lengths, indicative of single phase solutions of atactic peptide PAs. This is in contrast to previous studies that show isotactic and syndiotactic peptide PAs have strong block length dependent phase behavior. , For both of these systems, secondary structure formation plays a role in the phase separation behavior. Thus, a combination of block length and tacticity influences the phase behavior of PA solutions.

Chain Conformation

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) was used to examine the effects of block length on chain conformation at 2.0 wt % atactic peptide PA. We first determined the radius of gyration (R g) as a function of block length using the model independent Guinier analysis of the scattering spectra I(q) in the region qR g > 1.3 for an approximately spherical or coil conformation. For small block lengths we see R g values ≈15–16 Å, which are similar to those observed in our previous study of isostatic peptides with chain length N = 32. As block length increases, however, we observe an increase in R g (Figure ). This is in contrast to theoretical predictions and simulation results that show a decrease in chain size as block length increases due to strong attractive electrostatic interactions. ,,− We therefore carried out an analysis using the absolute I(0) determined from Guinier analysis to assess the molecular weight of the scattered particles. For block lengths N p < 4, the molecular weight is equivalent to single peptide PA chains (MW ≈ 4.2 kDa). As block length increases (N p ≥ 4), we find the molecular weight increases beyond that of an individual chain. For N p = 4, we observe molecular weights averaging to 2 chains at low peptide PA concentration (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt %), whereas for N p = 8, we observe molecular weights that dramatically increase from 2–4 chains within the measured concentration range (Figure S6). Additionally, we find that the second virial coefficient A 2 becomes negative at N p = 4 denoting an increase in associative interactions with an increasing block length. A 2 could not be determined for N p = 8 because the relationship between c/I(0) and c is nonlinear, where c is the atactic peptide PA concentration (Figure S3). This suggests that higher order terms in the virial expansion are necessary to describe multibody interactions. Thus, the measured R g represents multichain clusters and not individual polymers. This is consistent with recent simulation results that showed charge-balanced PAs form clusters even at low solution concentrations and that asymmetric PAs form clusters dependent on block length. Here, we show experimentally that charge-balanced PAs also exhibit block length dependent cluster formation.

Table reports the averaged absolute scattering derived molecular weight at low atactic peptide PA concentrations (0.5, 1.0, 2.0 wt %) and second virial coefficient A 2 as a function of block length N p. We report an approximated range for N p = 8 due the large increase in molecular weight with peptide PA concentration from 0.5–2.0 wt %.

We then applied Kratky analysis to qualitatively evaluate the shape of the atactic peptide PAs (or clusters) by plotting the SAXS data on a normalized and dimensionless Kratky plot ((qR g)2 I(q)/I(0) versus q). We chose to plot against q instead of the dimensionless qR g for easier visual comparison of length scales across all block lengths and concentrations. Figure shows comparison between the Debye form factor for a Gaussian chain (dashed-dotted line) and the SAXS profiles for the atactic peptide PAs at 2.0 wt %. We observe good agreement with the Debye form factor at low-q for the atactic peptide PAs. However, as q increases, the scattering profiles begin to deviate from the ideal Gaussian chain prediction. For example, the atactic peptide PAs exhibit a peak with a slight downturn indicative of a more compact conformation compared to the ideal Gaussian chain prediction. For all block lengths, this downturn occurs at q ≈ 0.20 Å–1, which is comparable to 2R g for a single chain. Lastly, we observe a slight upturn at q ≈ 0.35 Å–1, which suggests local chain stiffening. This length scale is comparable to values reported for the persistence length of peptides and peptoids with similar chemistry as those used here. ,, Scaling exponents at high-q also indicate that chains are more collapsed than ideal Gaussian chains (Figure S4), however PAs with greater molecular weights need to be studied to ensure the high q-range is large enough to reach the asymptotic limit.

Kratky plots of SAXS profiles for N p = 1, 2, 4, and 8 in 0 mM added NaCl at 2.0 wt % PA are shown. The dashed-dotted lines represent the Debye form factor for Gaussian chains, and the gray dashed lines denote q 2 R g 2·I(q)/I(0) = 1.1 and qR g = √3, where the peak of the globular conformation model would lie.

Interestingly, the atactic chains behave more ideally compared to our previously published data set on isotactic peptide PAs that showed more collapsed chain shapes. We hypothesize that these differences stem from a lack of additional secondary structure interactions and, at N p ≥ 4, multichain interactions that shield intrachain electrostatic interactions and expand the atactic chains.

Cluster Size and Distribution

To better understand the role of electrostatic interactions on multichain cluster formation, we fit a semiflexible cylinder form factor to I(q) for each block length (Figure ). This model has been used extensively to describe electrostatic affects on chain conformation in aqueous solution. The model is parametrized by the cylinder cross-sectional radius R, chain contour length L c, and Kuhn length b. To capture the distribution of chains associated with multichain clusters, we fix the Kuhn length b at 10.0 Å and fit the contour length L c using a polydisperse log-normal form to find an effective L c. We assume that chain conformation in the cluster is similar to single chains in solution and that the persistence length of the chains is not influenced by block length and cluster formation. The former is supported by the cross-sectional radius remaining between 5.0–8.0 Å and the similarity between the Kratky representations as a function of peptide PA solution concentration (Figure S5). The latter is supported by a recent experimental study on polypeptoids that showed little change in persistence length with charge block size and increased electrostatic repulsion. Figure a shows the fits for N p ≤ 8 as a function of PA concentration (0.5–5.0 wt %). For block lengths N p ≥ 4, we see a significant increase in the distribution of the effective L c with an increasing concentration. This increase in the effective L c distribution is consistent with results from our molecular weight analysis and the formation of multichain clusters (Figure b). Our results corroborate recent simulation results that showed that PA cluster size is dependent on concentration. Additionally, we observe larger effective L c distributions at lower atactic peptide PA concentrations as the block length increases. Figure S6 shows that an increase in effective L c distributions also corresponds to an increase in R g and molecular weight of the multichain clusters. This behavior is akin to recent theoretical and simulation results that revealed the critical concentration for phase separation decreases as block length increased.

For ideal noninteracting systems, it is expected that the scattering profiles collected at different concentrations will collapse onto a master curve when normalized by concentration (I(q)/c vs q). For systems that interact, however, deviations in the normalized forward intensity become apparent, where I(q → 0)/c decreases with increasing concentration. For our peptide PAs, we observe deviations in the normalized forward scattering intensity with concentration (Figure S7), suggesting that interactions between chains are appreciable, even under highly dilute conditions that are well below the overlap concentration. This behavior highlights the challenge in practically realizing conditions under which charged polymers behave as ideal, noninteracting systems.

Influence of Ionic Strength

We posit that cluster formation is in part driven by electrostatic interactions. To test this hypothesis, we studied the influence of added NaCl concentration on atactic chain conformation using SAXS (Figures and S8). Figure S8 shows, for block lengths N p ≥ 4, that the R g decreases with increasing added NaCl. This behavior is in contrast to expectations and results for dilute solution charge-balanced PAs where the addition of salt is predicted to shield intrachain electrostatic interactions resulting in chain expansion. The results for the atactic peptide PAs corroborate multichain cluster formation for block lengths N p ≥ 4, where increasing NaCl concentration results in shielding of interchain electrostatic interactions and dissolution of multichain clusters. Dissolution is evidenced by single chain R g values at high ionic strength for all block lengths. Additionally, the effective L c distributions show a block length dependent dissolution of the multichain cluster with increasing NaCl concentration (Figure ).

For small block lengths N p < 4, Kratky analysis reveals similar chain shapes with increasing NaCl concentration. For N p > 4, however, we observe a more prominent peak shape in the scattering profiles at low NaCl concentration compared to high NaCl concentration (Figures and S9). Together with the R g values, effective L c distributions, and high-q scaling exponents (Figure S4), these results indicate that the multichain clusters exhibit more condensed conformations compared to single chains in solution. Simulations of charge-balanced PAs showed more condensed cluster formation compared to asymmetric PAs with unequal numbers of cationic and anionic monomers. Figure also shows that the form factor fits the Kratky scattering profiles relatively well, capturing both the observed downturn and upturn. This behavior provides further support for the assumption that chain properties, including the persistence length, are similar in the cluster to those of individual chains in solution.

Conclusions

We investigated the solution behavior of charge-balanced PAs with varying block lengths. We used solid-phase synthesis to produce well-controlled peptide PAs with a precise molecular weight, block lengths, and tacticity. We found that atactic PAs exhibited no discernible secondary structure, which enabled a more direct investigation of charge block length dependent phase behavior and chain conformation. Turbidity measurements showed that the N p = 1–8 remained in a single solution phase.

SAXS measurements were used to assess the chain shape and size as a function of block length and added salt concentration. Kratky analysis showed that atactic peptide PAs have more condensed chain conformations compared with the prediction for an ideal chain. Additionally, we observed that chains with larger block lengths (N p = 8) adopted the most compact conformations. Guinier and molecular weight analyses of SAXS spectra revealed the presence of multichain clusters at a minimum block length of N p = 4. We also showed that cluster size depends on PA concentration by fitting the SAXS spectra to a modified semiflexible form factor with excluded volume interactions. These results are consistent with a recent theoretical prediction that phase separation is block length and concentration dependent. Lastly, we showed that multichain clusters are disrupted by increasing the salt concentration of the solution, providing further evidence that this is an electrostatically mediated process.

In summary, we demonstrated that the solution behavior of PAs can be controlled by varying the block length, tacticity, and polymer concentration. In particular, phase separation at these block lengths and molecular weights was inhibited compared to the identical but isotactic versions of these PAs. We posit that phase separation was suppressed due to the low molecular weight and concentrations studied here. We are currently exploring higher solution concentrations and longer chain lengths, where we would expect to observe a wider phase separation window. Additionally, future studies will focus on investigating solution dynamics as a function of block length and chain tacticity using single particle tracking (SPT), dynamic light scattering (DLS), 2D nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and neutron spin–echo (NSE) spectroscopy. These studies have the potential to reveal multimode relaxations previously identified for polyelectrolyte solutions. − Lastly, preliminary evidence suggests increasing the blockiness (i.e., diblock PAs) may result in interesting phase behavior, including microscale and/or macroscale phase separation, which we will address in future studies.
