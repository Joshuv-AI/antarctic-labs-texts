Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is a driving force regulating gene expression in bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) larvae development
Authors: William Koven, Eran Yanowski, Luke Gardner, Oriya Nixon, Barbara Block
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study elucidated the role of DHA-modulated genes in the development and growth of Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) larvae ingesting increasing levels of DHA in their rotifer prey. The effect of feeding low, medium, and high rotifer (Brachionus rotundiformis) DHA levels (2.0, 3.6 and 10.9 mg DHA g−1 DW, respectively) was tested on 2–15 days post hatching (dph) bluefin tuna larvae. Larval DHA content markedly (P < 0.05) increased in a DHA dose-dependent manner (1.5, 3.9, 6.1 mg DHA g−1 DW larva, respectively), that was positively correlated with larval prey consumption and growth (P < 0.05). Gene ontology enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) demonstrated dietary DHA significantly (P < 0.05) affected different genes and biological processes at different developmental ages. The number of DHA up-regulated DEGs was highest in 10 dph larvae (491), compared to 5 (12) and 15 dph fish (34), and were mainly involved in neural and synaptic development in the brain and spinal cord. In contrast, DHA in older 15 dph larvae elicited fewer DEGs but played critical roles over a wider range of developing organs. The emerging picture underscores the importance of DHA-modulated gene expression as a driving force in bluefin tuna larval development and growth.Keywords: Gene regulation, Bluefin tuna, Larvae, Docosahexaenoic acid, Neurons, Synaptic functionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Molecular biology, Physiology

Introduction

The omega 3 fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3) has been reported to promote better growth in the larvae of marine teleosts in aquaculture than the other essential long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LCPUFA); eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; 20:5n-3) and arachidonic acid (ArA; 20:4n-6)1. This is largely due to the physiological function of DHA in the membranes of the eyes, brain, and other neural tissues1,2. In general, larval eyes are particularly enriched in DHA3,4, where di-22:6n-3 phosphoglycerides in the phosphatidylethanolamine fraction of the fish retina can represent more than 70% of retinal lipid3. The importance of DHA for the proper development of neural tissues has been demonstrated in larval Atlantic herring (Clupea harengus)3,5, European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax)6, gilthead sea bream (Sparus aurata)5,7, and turbot (Scophthalmus maximus)7,8. We have previously reported a direct correlation between retinal opsin abundance and the level of rotifer DHA fed to bluefin tuna (BFT; Thunnus thynnus) larvae9. DHA, as an integral part of biomembranes of retinal photoreceptors, interacts directly with opsin proteins10 of the photopigments as well as imparting fluidity to the biomembrane11,12due to its 22 carbons and 6 double bonds resisting orderly packing12. Membrane fluidity facilitates conformational change in the photopigments, comprised of opsin covalently linked to retinal, which initiates an intramembrane enzyme cascade in the phototransduction process. This results in the conversion of photons to electrical signals in the cones and rods that ultimately send messages to the brain that contribute to vision1. Taken together, the emerging paradigm is that DHA affects retinal photoreceptor function in the larvae of marine species, improving visual acuity leading to enhanced prey recognition, capture, and larval growth in early life history stages.

DHA as well as other LCPUFAs, also modulate cellular metabolic functions, eicosanoid and signaling pathways as well as regulating gene expression13,14 through controlling the activity or abundance of key transcription factors. For example, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors of the alpha sub-type (PPARα) play a central role in regulating genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism. Activation of PPARs by DHA occurs when this LCPUFA binds as a ligand to retinoid x receptors (RXRs) that heterodimerizes with PPAR. This influences transcription in pleiotropic genes affecting brain development15, cognitive function in the hippocampus of mammals16,17, cell differentiation, metabolism, apoptosis, and increased survival of photoreceptors18. DHA influences not only neurite growth and synaptogenesis, but also synapsin and glutamate receptor subunits16. However, little work has been done on DHA modulation of key genes in developing marine teleost larvae, particularly BFT, which has a considerable DHA requirement9. This is in contrast to numerous studies focusing on DHA gene modulation of neural function in the CNS and brain in mammals and humans19–21. Consequently, the aim of this study was to elucidate the central role of DHA regulation of key genes in the development and growth of different age BFT larvae fed increasing levels of DHA in their rotifer prey.

Results

Increasing rotifer DHA content was directly associated with larval hunting success of rotifers (mastax number) and was significant (P < 0.05) on each day from 3 to 9 dph (Fig. 1a). In fact, the quadratic curves of the three DHA rotifer treatments were significantly (P < 0.05) different from each other. The larvae fed the control rotifers (2 mg DHA g−1 DW rotifer) consistently exhibited the lowest prey consumption from 3 to 6 dph. After this point, it was not possible to further sample larvae from the upper water column as very few remained in the experimental tanks indicating that this dietary level of DHA was insufficient to maintain survival. In contrast, larvae feeding on rotifers with moderate DHA levels (3.6 mg DHA g−1 rotifer) ingested significantly (P < 0.05) more prey than the control on 3 and 4 dph (Fig. 1a) but maintained a similar or slightly decreased feeding rate on 6, 8 and 9 dph (Fig. 1a). Importantly, larvae fed the high DHA rotifers exhibited the highest consumption rate, which continued on an upward trajectory (P < 0.05) in 6, 8 and 9 dph (Fig. 1a). DHA content in rotifers and 10 dph BFT larvae feeding on them, markedly (P < 0.05) increased in a DHA dose dependent manner. The low, medium, and high DHA larvae exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) different values (1.5, 3.9, 6.1 mg DHA g−1 DW, respectively) from each other (Fig. 1b). Figure 1c demonstrated, after regression analysis, the marked (P < 0.05) effect of the rotifer DHA treatments on mean larval length, as a function of age. This was confirmed in Fig. 1d, which showed that the pattern of larval consumption of rotifers largely reflected the dry weight (DW) at the end of the study in 15 dph fish. Larvae fed the control and moderate DHA rotifers (73.2 and 72.5 mg, respectively) grew significantly (P < 0.05) less than the high DHA cohorts (106.4 mg).

The larvae transcriptional profile revealed major differences between age groups and DHA-fed concentrations. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot (Fig. 2) illustrated the distinct patterns in gene expression dynamics related to both temporal progression (5, 10 and 15 dph) and DHA dosage (2, 3.6, 10.9 mg DHA DW rotifer−1). Principal component 1 (x axis) demonstrated distinct clustering of samples according to the time points of the treatment regimen, while principal component 2 (y axis) depicts the segregation of samples based on the administered DHA dosage to the BFT larvae.

Variability in 5 and 10 dph larvae was highest between the low and medium DHA treatments while the largest variability was between the medium and high DHA treatments in 15 dph fish.

The calculation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was always the result of comparing a higher DHA treatment to a lower DHA treatment within the same larval age. Only those DEGs that were significant (P < 0.05) were considered. The pattern of DEGs, which was distinctly variable as a function of age, indicates that DHA is regulating the synthesis of different developmental genes according to the biological processes required at a given age. Variability in the DEGs in 5 and 10 dph larvae was highest between the low and medium DHA treatments while the largest variability was between the medium and high DHA treatments in 15 dph fish.

In Fig. 3 the MA plots show the differential expression (Y- axis) and expression levels (X-axis) of multiple genes simultaneously. DEGs analysis between medium DHA levels (3.6 mg g−1 rotifer) and low DHA levels (2.0 mg g−1 rotifer) at 5 dph, revealed considerably more down-regulated (79) than up-regulated (12) DEGs at this age (Fig. 3a). Importantly, there were very few significant (P < 0.05) DEGs when high-DHA fed larvae were compared to cohorts ingesting the low-DHA diet. The lack of significant DEGs in these treatments was also demonstrated by the low variability between these diets in the PCA plot (Fig. 2). At 10 DPH, differential expression analysis between larvae fed the medium DHA levels (3.6 mg g−1 rotifer) and those consuming low DHA levels (2.0 mg g−1 DW rotifer), exhibited markedly more up-regulated (491) than down-regulated (43) genes (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, comparing high to low DHA levels highlighted just a few significant DEGs, consistent with the low variability observed between these treatments in the PCA plot in Fig. 2. Finally, when comparing the medium and high DHA treatments in 15 dph larvae, the MA plot (Fig. 3c) shows a significant number of up-regulated (34) and down-regulated (21) DEGs. In contrast, there were considerably fewer significant DEGs when comparing larvae fed the low and medium DHA treatments (data not shown).

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on up-regulated and down regulated DEGs. Up-regulated DEGs from 5 dph larvae (Fig. 4) were annotated to biological processes that included those associated with keratinocytes in the epidermis and collagen in the dermis as well as the process that determines left/right organ asymmetry. The genes involved in the retinoic acid receptor signaling pathway, which modulates the development of spinal cord, heart, and eyes, were also up-regulated. Genes that were down-regulated in 5 dph larvae (Fig. 4) were almost all linked to the processes of neuron function, synthesis, and development. More specifically, the down-regulated processes included the modulation of the pre-synaptic and post-synaptic membrane potential differences, synaptic plasticity, and the exocytosis release of pre-synaptic vesicular glutamate, which is regulated by a voltage-dependent calcium channel, leading to the subsequent binding to glutamate receptors at the post-synapse. In addition, axonogenesis or the de novo synthesis of the long process of a neuron was also down-regulated.

The graph displays significantly enriched biological processes (y-axis) and their selected probabilities (-log10 (P value) (x axis).

Surprisingly, up-regulated DEGs in 10 dph larvae (Fig. 5) involved many of the same or similar neuron biological processes (GO) that were down-regulated in 5 dph. This included the processes of pre-synaptic dense core vesicle exocytosis, regulation of pre-synaptic membrane potential, regulation of neuronal synaptic plasticity, and neuronal action potential. Moreover, the up-regulated detection of visible light in 10 dph fish was likely a response to the high DHA requirement in the outer membranes of photoreceptors membranes. The biological process: cytoplasmic translation, strongly supports the notion that increased dietary DHA markedly up-regulated protein synthesis in the cytoplasm of tissues. In contrast, the down-regulated biological processes in 10 dph larvae, when prey DHA is increased from 2 to 3.6 mg DHA g−1 DW rotifer, were extracellular matrix organization and cartilage development.

In 15 dph larvae, when the fish were fed rotifer DHA increasing from 3.6 to 10.9 mg DHA g-1 DW rotifer, the variety of annotated, up-regulated biological processes of various tissues expanded considerably (Fig. 6). This is evidenced by genes involved in the neural and muscle tissues (oculomotor nerve innervation), eyes (optic cup formation), pancreas (morphogenesis), inner ear (otic placode formation adjacent to developing hind brain), liver (hepatocyte cell migration), heart and vascular system (smooth muscle cell differentiation, systemic arterial blood pressure), forebrain and neuron differentiation as well as inflammation (histamine secretion mediated by immunoglobulin), erythrophore differentiation (chromatophore containing carotenoid pigment) and heat generation pathways. On the other hand, feeding rotifers with increasing DHA to 15 dph larvae down-regulated insulin receptor internalization and the negative regulation of intestinal lipid absorption (Fig. 6). In the latter, this means that the inhibitory influence on lipid absorption by a protein is reduced leading to an overall effect of increased intestinal lipid absorption. In fact, there were a number of DHA down -regulated genes that code for proteins that inhibit pathways, resulting in an up-regulation of these biological processes. These include the negative regulation of intestinal cholesterol absorption, peptidyl-tyrosine autophosphorylation and inward rectifier potassium channel activity. In addition, there was DHA modulated down regulation of cell adhesion processes, synthesis of vitamin A compounds through oxidative cleavage of carotenoids and the directed movement of proteins along microtubules from the cell periphery towards the cell body in nerve cell axons. Another DHA modulated down-regulated gene at this developmental age, was involved in base excision repair (BER) or the maintenance of genomic integrity by eliminating damaged or inappropriate bases that are generated endogenously, mainly by reactive oxygen species (ROS).

Discussion

In the present study, there was a rotifer DHA dose-dependent effect on DHA accumulation in 10 dph BFT larval tissue, which was positively associated with rotifer ingestion rate. The 8–9 dph BFT larvae ingesting the high DHA rotifers showed very good rotifer feeding rates reaching over 50 mastaxes 90 min−1. This is much higher compared to other species such as gilthead sea bream (Sparus aurata), where 13 dph larvae ingested about 20 mastaxes 90 min−12 and agrees well with our previous publication on BFT9.

In support of this, there was a marked (P< 0.05) increase in larval length in 2–9 dph fish, that was associated with larval DHA content and indicative of normal growth at this developmental stage22. In addition, 15 dph BFT larvae fed the highest DHA rotifers demonstrated significantly (P< 0.05) improved growth, as previously reported by our group9. Moreover, the results obtained by larvae RNAseq analysis demonstrated the influence of DHA on a broad range of biological processes, that were age-dependent, and emphasized the upregulation of genes associated with neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and synapse function in BFT larvae. Interestingly, we observed a major expression shift in 67 DHA down-regulated genes in 5 dph larvae that were up-regulated in 10 dph fish, highlighting the importance of DHA at specific stages of larval development.

The critical importance of DHA in the prey of marine fish larvae has been well documented1,23 and is widely believed to be a more effective larval growth promoter during development than the other essential fatty acids; eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5n-3) and arachidonic acid (ARA; 20:4n- 6)1,24,25. However, how DHA contributes to growth in marine fish larvae appears to be broadly linked to its physiological function in the cellular membranes of the eyes, brain, and other neural tissues. DHA impacts the structural development and function of the nervous system and promotes neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and synapse function in fish larvae. This suggests that DHA greatly enhances the biological processes of visual acuity, learning and neuromuscular function, which are all necessary for successful prey recognition, behavior, and capture. This was demonstrated by several authors that reported increased dietary DHA or n-3 long chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LCPUFAs) improved feeding behavior in herring larvae3 and larval prey consumption in gilthead sea bream (Sparus aurata)26 as well as bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus)9.

A common thread tightly woven into the benefits of DHA is the contribution of its unique 22 carbon, 6 double bond structure, which resists orderly packing, to increased membrane fluidity. In phototransduction, membrane fluidity facilitates the rate of photopigment conformational change27,28 and a more rapid processing of light. In addition, the abundant levels of DHA in neuronal membranes affect the activity of neurotransmitter receptors and ion channels, which modulate neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity. DHA can also modify the physical organization of lipid rafts, within cell membranes, that are comprised of cholesterol, glycophospholipids and receptors that serve as organizing centers for the assembly of signaling molecules29. In addition, DHA has anti-inflammatory properties and can modulate immune responses such as the production of cytokines. Moreover, DHA can prevent oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in photoreceptors through its conversion to neuroprotectin D1 30 and/or its upregulation of Bcl-2 proteins, which inactivate pro-apoptotic proteins31.

However, the effect of DHA on biological processes and pathways is not always linked to membranal or signaling function but can be more complex and often overlaps with its role in modulating gene expression. In the present study, the transcriptome in BFT larvae fed various levels of DHA at different larval ages demonstrated (PCA plot) significant (P< 0.05) variability in both of these covariants and points to a central role for DHA in BFT larval development. Comparing the DEGs in 5 and 10 dph BFT larvae, the highest variability was between the low and medium DHA treatments, while the largest variability in 15 dph fish was between the medium and high DHA treatments. This suggests that the high DHA treatment provided an excessive amount of DHA during early larval development, in terms of regulating genes, and didn’t demonstrate significant variability in DEGs between these treatments in 5 and 10 dph fish. Conceivably, the excess DHA was retro-converted to EPA, a 20 carbon LCPUFA that may be less effective at regulating the expression of specific genes or may exert its action through different mechanisms other than DHA. Allam-Ndoul et al.32 reported that EPA and DHA regulate genes involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, immune response, inflammation, and oxidative stress in a differential and dose-dependent manner. This is not to say that excess DHA wouldn’t be contributing, simultaneously with its gene modulation, to membranal function and improved physiological processes. Having said that, in older larvae the rate of development may be more rapid and DHA demand higher as a modulator of gene expression. This might explain why there was a significant change in larval DEGs only between medium and high DHA treatments in 15 dph fish. On the other hand, the lack of a significant change in larval DEGs between the low and high DHA treatments was likely due to one of the low DHA replicates being expressed close to the cluster of high DHA replicates in the PCA plot, which reduced the DEGS variability between these treatments.

The 5 dph MA plot showed a greater number of significantly down-regulated (78) than up- regulated genes (12), while the MA plot of 10 dph larvae exhibited an impressive abundance of DHA up-regulated genes (491) and only 43 DHA down-regulated genes. In 15 dph larvae, the MA plot presented relatively similar numbers of up- and down-regulated genes. The emerging picture is that DHA modulates different genes, albeit with some overlap, at different developmental stages. This further strengthens the notion that the sequence and timing of different stages of development dictates which genes are modulated and are highly conserved, while the degree to which they are modulated is influenced by DHA. It is noteworthy that gene ontology analysis showed that the down-regulated genes in 5 dph larvae were mainly linked with neuron development and synaptic synthesis that included pre- and post-synaptic membrane potential, axonogenesis as well as neuron plasticity. Conversely, many of these down-regulated genes associated with neurons and synaptic biological processes, were then significantly up-regulated in 10 dph fish. In fact, there were 67 shared genes between 5 dph down-regulated and 10 dph up-regulated genes. The delay of DHA modulated expression of key genes in neural development in 5 dph BFT reinforces our previous notion that the sequence of developmental events in this species is strictly adhered to.

DHA modulated genes predominantly influenced neural development in 10 dph BFT larvae through various essential biological processes, which are supported in a number of mammalian and human studies. DHA is important for brain development and function33, particularly the hippocampus of the mammalian brain34, which is responsible for storing short term memories and transferring them to long term storage. Although fish lack a hippocampus, BFT larvae appear to share some of these mammalian genes and biological processes expressed in different parts of the larval fish brain. Studies in mammals found that DHA increased synaptic vesicle density, hippocampal neurite growth, dendritic spine density16, synaptic proteins35 as well as cell migration36. Cao et al.16 studying mice hippocampal neurons in culture reported that DHA influenced neurite growth, synaptogenesis, synapsin proteins and two types of glutamate receptors: N-methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA) and a-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methylisoxazole (AMPA). Glutamate receptors mediate fast excitatory synaptic transmission in the central nervous system and regulate a broad spectrum of processes in the brain, spinal cord, retina, and peripheral nervous system.

One of the biological processes up-regulated in 10 dph larvae was the detection of visible light. Gaon et al.2 and Koven et al.9 reported that rotifer DHA contributed to opsin biosynthesis in the gilthead sea bream and BFT larvae, respectively, in a dose dependent manner, which was correlated with prey consumption. In the current study, rotifer DHA stimulated the up-regulation of the opsin (OPN5) for ultraviolet A (UVA) and blue light in 10 dph BFT larvae. However, DHA in BFT larvae also up-regulated genes involved at different stages of the phototransduction process. This included the genes activating voltage dependent calcium channels (Cacna1f), the G protein coupled receptor signaling pathway (Gpr52), the synthesis of cGMP (Guc2f), the glutamate receptor signaling pathway (Gnaq), ion channel modulating, and the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway (Gna11). Interestingly, the latter two genes were also involved in the entrainment of the circadian clock.

The biological process of atrial cardiac muscle cell action potential includes the genes Cacnb2, Scn5a and Kcna5, which modulate calcium, sodium, and potassium transport in excitable membranes, respectively, as well as the involvement of Ank2 in signal transduction. The DHA up-regulated genes; Mmp24, Grik2, Ephb1, Ano3, which are involved in neuron biological processes such as the glutamate receptor signaling pathway, modulation of neuronal action potential, synaptic transmission, angiogenesis, eye morphogenesis, axonogenesis, dendritic spine development, immunological synapse formation, optic nerve morphogenesis and retinal ganglion cell axon guidance. The biological process: cytoplasmic translation, which was linked to the 67 up-regulated genes found in 10 dph larvae, had the highest probability (-log10 (P-value)) of being associated with cytoplasmic ribosomal mRNA translation and the synthesis of a large array of proteins.

DHA modulated genes in 15 dph BFT larvae continued to modify neural development as well as influencing an increasing number of developing organs. In the biological process of otic placode formation, DHA up-regulated Prox1. In mammals, this gene is involved in brain development particularly the hippocampus37 and the cerebellar cortex38. However, in fish the otic placode forms the inner ear, which is responsible for hearing and balance. Studies have shown that fish larvae can detect physically and biologically generated sounds from specific habitats such as thriving coral reefs through the interaction of the otolith and the neuromast sensory bed of the inner ear. This allows the larvae to orient themselves towards such favorable ecosystems that are rich in prey and provide shelter39,40. Tuna larvae, even at the early flexion stage, were 100 times more abundant in near reef waters than in the open sea41–43. In addition, Prox1 mediates uniquely a dual function of initially promoting neuronal differentiation of neural progenitor cells, and then suppressing neurite and axon outgrowth in nascent neurons, a critical process for neuronal maturation during central nervous system development44.

The DHA up-regulated gene Tfap2a is broadly involved in eye development and elicited the biological processes of optic cup structural organization, optic vesicle morphogenesis and oculomotor nerve formation45. Moreover, DHA-modulated Tfap2a likely up-regulated bmp7a46. This gene promotes differentiation of stato-acustic ganglion (SAG) precursors in the inner ear47, which contribute to balance and hearing.

These genes were associated with GO biological processes that were mainly involved in synaptic function in terms of pre-synaptic vesicle formation, exocytosis, and binding to glutamate receptors. Interestingly, these genes were also involved in human cognitive impairment but their modulation by DHA has not yet been investigated in humans suggesting a promising line of research in future studies on neurodegenerative diseases. Although early developing fish are clearly not afflicted with age related cognitive decline or neural synaptic dysfunction, a DHA deficiency in BFT larvae would likely affect their behavior, vision, hearing, learning and memory. All of these attributes would be necessary for mastering the learned process of larval prey recognition and capture48.

It is noteworthy that, in contrast to the normal growth experienced by larvae feeding on the high DHA rotifer treatment, fish consuming lower DHA rotifers likely exhibited transcriptomes directly linked to reduced neural, brain, muscle, vision and hearing functions leading to restricted rotifer feeding and a potential state of malnutrition and starvation. Although these are secondary factors, they can also modulate transcriptome expression. However, the evidence strongly suggests that the DHA modulation of genes is a key and major driving force affecting the BFT larval transcriptome.

Taken together, the picture emerging is that DHA modulated genes were intimately involved in a wide array of critical biological processes occurring in a range of developing organs in different age BFT larvae. These results shed light on the essential role of DHA modulated gene expression in the promotion of BFT larval growth.

Methods

Experimental system

The experimental system consisted of nine 1500 l polypropylene V-tanks that tested three rotifer (Brachionus rotundiformis) DHA levels (2.0, 3.6 and 10.9 mg DHA g−1 DW rotifer) on BFT larvae from 2 to 15 dph in replicates of 3 tanks per treatment. The experimental tanks were in a flow-through system fed by 37‰, filtered (10 μm), UV treated seawater, where the temperature (incrementally raised 1 ⁰C /day from 22 to 25 ⁰C) and salinity were computer controlled (Gavish, Israel). Prior to being fed to the larvae, the rotifers were enriched on one of three enrichment preparations containing 5.5, 15 or 32% DHA of total fatty acids (TFA). These were produced by different dilutions of a 32% DHA of TFA custom preparation of the commercial product “Red Pepper” (Bernaqua, Olen, Belgium), which has 5.5% DHA of TFA. In addition, all rotifers were enriched on taurine (MERCK, Rehovot, Israel), which was given as a suspension (600 mg taurine l−1 enrichment medium) for 21 h. All rotifer enrichment was carried out according to protocols developed at the NCM.

Stocking and sampling

Eggs that originated from spawns from caged BFT broodstock at Malta Fish Farms (MFF, Ltd., Marsaxlokk, Malta), were air shipped to Israel and arrived at the NCM facility approximately 26 h after collection from the sea. The BFT eggs, after adjusting seawater pH to 7.8 through water exchange with filtered (10 μm), UV-treated, 40‰ seawater, were then stocked (20 viable and floating eggs l−1) in 1500 l conical V-tanks. The hatching rate (%) of stocked BFT eggs and survival of the pre-larvae at the end of the day of hatching (0 dph) was calculated9 by placing a fertilized egg, at the gastrula stage, in each of 12 wells (5 mL) in each of three plastic well-plates. The plates were covered and placed in a temperature-controlled incubator (22 ⁰C) until hatching where the emerging larvae and surviving newly hatched larvae at the end of 0 dph were counted. After hatching in the tanks, water salinity was progressively lowered to 37‰ and the flow rate reduced to one tank water exchange per day (Gavish, Israel). At 2 dph, once the yolk sac was depleted, the larval eyes pigmented and the mouth and anus opened. From this point until 15 dph, lighting over the tanks was turned on and provided 1000 lx (14 h light day−1) at the water surface. BFT larvae were offered the three rotifer treatments (10 rotifers ml−1) together with microalgae (Nannochloropsis oculata) that were added twice daily to maintain a concentration of 0.5 × 106cells ml−1. In order to analyze sufficient biological material, the larvae were representatively sampled on 5 (60 larvae), 10 (60 larvae) and 15 dph (15 larvae) from each of the tanks, in random fashion to avoid confounding factors, and divided into two groups. The first group was euthanized in excess MS222 (Sigma-Aldrich, Rehovot, Israel), placed in 2.5 ml Eppendorf tubes and immediately frozen at -80 οC under nitrogen until fatty acid analysis. The second group was placed in 2.5 ml Eppendorf tubes with RNA later for later transcriptome analysis at the Hopkins Marine Station (Pacific Grove, California, USA).

Rotifer ingestion and growth

Rotifer consumption was calculated according to9. In this procedure 5 larvae were representatively sampled from each tank in the experimental system 90 min after feeding them with the DHA enriched rotifer treatments. Larvae were then euthanized in excess MS222, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, and stored at 4 °C until counting the mastaxes (indigestible part of the rotifer’s feeding apparatus) in the fish’s digestive tract. Anecdotal observations determined that mastaxes can be expelled 2–3 h after feeding suggesting that 90 min would allow a good approximation of rotifer consumption. Counting mastaxes in the larval gut was carried out from 2 to 9 dph. After this age, mastaxes were not measured as increasing pigmentation in the gut region obscured the ability to count them.

Fish growth was measured as length from all larvae taken for mastax number determination and as dry weight (DW) when the larvae were 15 dph. The latter was carried out according to49 by collecting ca. 130 larvae per tank, euthanizing them in excess MS222, and then rinsing them with distilled water. Fish samples were then dried at 70 ⁰C for 24 h and weighed (A&D HD-120 analytical balance, Japan).

Fatty acid analysis

Total lipids in freeze dried tissue samples (20 mg) were chloroform-methanol (2:1) extracted50 preparation of the fatty acids has been previously described9. Briefly, the lipid-containing chloroform upper phase was removed and evaporated to dryness under a stream of nitrogen and the remaining total lipid weighed (GH-120 analytical balance, A&D, San Jose, CA, USA). This was followed by the addition of the internal standard (17:0; heptadecanoic acid) to each of the samples. The samples were then transmethylated to their fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) by adding 1 mL mg lipid−1 of a 14% solution of boron trifluoride methanol and sonicated for 1 h at 50 ⁰C. Double distilled water (0.5 ml) and 1 mL mg lipid−1 of analytical grade hexane (99%) were added to samples, which were vortexed and then centrifuged (2,300 rpm) for 2 min. The top hexane layers, containing FAME, were transferred to a vial and stored at -20 ⁰C until analysis in a Varian 450 − 220 GC/MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Injected FAME samples (1 µl) were separated on a Varian WCOT fused silica column (50 m x 0.32 mm) at a flow rate of 1.5 ml min−1 and identified by known purified standards and quantified using a response factor to the internal standard (17:0; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). The 30 min oven temperature program began at 70 ⁰C for 4 min following injection and then increased to 300 ⁰C at 10 ⁰C min−1 for 3 min.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from whole body larvae using Tri-reagent. Briefly, tissues were homogenized in Tri-reagent using a tissue homogenizer. Chloroform was added, and the mixture was centrifuged to separate the phases. The aqueous phase containing RNA was collected, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol. After washing with ethanol, the RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in RNase-free water. RNA quality, quantity and integrity of the isolated RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Only RNA samples with high integrity (RNA Integrity Number, RIN > 8) were used for subsequent analyses.

Library preparation for next-generation sequencing (NGS)

cDNA libraries for NGS were prepared using the TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina). Briefly, the cDNA was fragmented, and adapters were ligated to the ends. The resulting libraries were subjected to size selection using AMPure XP beads to obtain the desired insert size. Library quality and quantity were assessed using qPCR and Bioanalyzer. The libraries were PCR-amplified to enrich for adapter-ligated fragments. The amplified libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads to remove undesired fragments. The final libraries were validated for size distribution and concentration.

Sequencing

The prepared libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform, generating paired-end reads. Sequencing depth and coverage were optimized based on the experimental design and the requirements of downstream analyses.

Data analysis

Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control, including adapter trimming and quality filtering. The processed reads were subjected to a Blast search against the Blastnr (non-redundant) database. Subsequently, reads with high-quality annotations were selectively retained, ensuring that the dataset was filtered to focus on sequences with robust and reliable similarity to known genes. Differential gene expression analysis and other relevant analyses were performed using bioinformatics tools and software packages. Deseq2 analysis from Count-matrix was performed using UTAP51, where Gene-ontology enrichment analysis for biological function was performed on differentially expressed genes using PANTHER (via https://geneontology.org/).

Transcriptomic analysis

Deseq2 analysis from Count-matrix was performed using UTAP51, where threshold for significance was set above/bellow log2FC of 1/-1 and above 50 reads (base mean). Gene-ontology enrichment analysis for biological function was performed on differentially expressed genes using PANTHER (via https://geneontology.org/).

Statistics

One-way ANOVAs, using all replicate values on rotifer and larva DHA levels, mastax number and larva dry weight were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 5.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego California USA, www.graphpad.com). ANOVA analyses and Barlett’s test for equal variances were carried out concurrently. If ANOVA was significant (P < 0.05) while Barlett’s test was not significant (P > 0.05), then testing differences between groups was carried out by Newman-Keuls Multiple Comparison test. If ANOVA and Barlett’s test were both significant (P < 0.05), then the non-parametric Kruskal Wallis Test was used followed by Dunn’s multiple Comparison test to determine significant (P < 0.05) differences among treatments. All data are presented as mean ± SEM.
