Effects of six traditional Chinese mind-body exercises on obesity and cardiovascular risk factors in middle-aged and elderly hypertensive patients: a network Meta-Analysis of 49 RCTs
Authors: Haojie Li, Xinyu Lin, Xie Wu, Rui Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing global aging population, the prevalence of hypertension among middle-aged and older adults has significantly risen, with over one-third of individuals aged 40 and above diagnosed with hypertension. Uncontrolled hypertension contributes significantly to cardiovascular morbidity and other health problems, placing a heavy burden on society. Therefore, the prevention and management of hypertension in middle-aged and elderly people has become a public health priority. Traditional Chinese mind-body exercise is gradually gaining attention due to its low damage and high compliance, and is particularly suitable for the middle-aged and elderly.MethodsWe screened relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published after 2000 by searching five English and three Chinese databases. Study inclusion criteria included hypertensive patients (≥ 45 years old, SBP/DBP > 130/80 mm Hg) receiving at least 8 weeks of Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Liuzijue, Shuxinpingxuegong, Qigong, or Daoyinyangshengshu interventions to assess SBP, DBP, WC, WHR or BMI indicators. Risk of bias and quality of evidence were assessed using the Cochrane tool and the GRADE framework. Effect values and heterogeneity (I² statistic) were calculated by random-effects multivariate Network Meta-Analysis combined with Review Manager and STATA software, and SUCRA values were used to rank intervention effects.ResultsA total of 49 randomized controlled trials covering 4,224 hypertensive patients were included. Risk of bias assessment showed that 77.55% of the studies were low risk, 14.28% were intermediate risk, and 8.16% were high risk. The NMA results showed that in terms of systolic blood pressure reduction, Tai Chi performed best (SMD = -1.10, 95% CI [-1.23, -0.98], SUCRA = 86.9), followed by Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = -1.00, 95% CI [-1.34, -0.67], SUCRA = 67.4) and Liuzijue (SMD = -0.97, 95% CI [-1.28, -0.66], SUCRA = 62). Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = -1.26, 95% CI [-1.65, -0.87], SUCRA = 88) and Liuzijue (SMD = -1.06, 95% CI [-1.42, -0.70], SUCRA = 69.2) ranked highly in terms of reducing diastolic blood pressure. In terms of improving waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), Tai Chi had a significant effect (SMD = -0.50, 95% CI [-0.72, -0.28], SUCRA = 90.4; WHR: SMD = -1.75, 95% CI [-2.65, -0.84], SUCRA = 89.8), though the GRADE assessment indicated low-quality evidence for WHR outcomes due to limited studies and potential heterogeneity. Tai Chi (SMD = -0.35, 95% CI [-0.50, -0.20], SUCRA = 80) and Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = -0.58, 95% CI [-1.14, -0.01], SUCRA = 60.3) outperformed the other interventions in terms of reducing body mass index.ConclusionThis study supports integrating traditional Chinese mind-body exercises like Tai Chi and Baduanjin into hypertension management for middle-aged and elderly patients. Tai Chi effectively lowers blood pressure and reduces abdominal obesity, while Baduanjin improves BMI. Personalized interventions with culturally adapted protocols could optimize adherence and global adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05084-x.Keywords: Middle-aged and elderly, Physical and mental exercise, Hypertension, Abdominal obesity, Tai Chi, Baduanjin

Background

With increasing global aging, the prevalence of hypertension in the middle-aged and elderly population has increased significantly. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than one-third of adults aged over 40 globally have hypertension, with prevalence exceeding 60% in those aged ≥ 65 years [1]. Concurrently, obesity affects 9% of older adults, further compounding cardiovascular disease (CVD) risks [2]. Uncontrolled hypertension not only causes CVD such as atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, but also increases the risk of stroke, renal failure, and retinopathy [3]. These diseases not only directly endanger the health of middle-aged and elderly people, but also lead to an increase in healthcare costs and a decline in the labor force, placing a heavy burden on society [4].

Studies on hypertension and cardiovascular risk factors in middle - aged and elderly populations have made remarkable progress, with the strong link between abdominal obesity, hypertension, and CVD well - established [5]. In abdominal obesity, fat accumulates around internal organs, measured by Waist circumference (WC) and Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). This obesity type increases peripheral vascular resistance, induces insulin resistance and inflammation, fueling metabolic syndrome and heightening CVD risk. WC, a key abdominal obesity marker, correlates strongly with CVD risk in hypertensive patients [6]. Each 10 cm increase in WC raises the coronary heart disease incidence by 11% [7], and is associated with elevated blood pressure, atherosclerosis, and left ventricular hypertrophy. The compression of organs by abdominal fat, triggering chronic inflammation, boosts the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke [8]. WHR, indicating fat distribution concentration, also matters [9]. A 0.1 increase in WHR elevates cardiovascular event risk by 13%, more so in hypertensive patients [10]. Higher WHR is linked to a heavier cardiac burden, vascular endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis [11]. Wei’s study showed abdominal - obese individuals had a 30% higher incidence of cardiovascular events than those with evenly - distributed obesity [12]. Thus, controlling WC and WHR is crucial for preventing hypertension and CVD in the middle-aged and elderly, highlighting the importance of abdominal obesity management in clinical practice.

While traditional pharmacological interventions for hypertension in middle-aged and elderly populations - primarily ACE inhibitors and calcium antagonists - effectively lower blood pressure, their long-term use carries risks of adverse effects including renal injury and electrolyte imbalances [13, 14]. Consequently, non-pharmacological alternatives have gained increasing importance. Among these, aerobic exercise (such as brisk walking, running, and swimming) and resistance training represent common approaches. Specifically, aerobic exercise enhances cardiopulmonary function, with studies demonstrating that moderate-intensity sessions three times weekly can reduce systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 5–7 mmHg [15]. Nevertheless, significant challenges exist: middle-aged and elderly hypertensive patients often struggle to maintain the required exercise intensity long-term, while also facing risks of joint injuries and cardiovascular events [16]. Similarly, while resistance training improves muscular strength and metabolism with some blood pressure benefits, improper intensity control may dangerously elevate blood pressure and increase cardiovascular risks. Thus, despite their effectiveness, both aerobic and resistance exercises present notable limitations in terms of safety and sustainable compliance for this vulnerable population [17].

Traditional Chinese mind-body exercises - including Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Liuzijue, Shuxinpingxuegong, Qigong, and Daoyinyangshengshu - provide multiple health benefits through their emphasis on synchronized movement, breath control, and mental focus. These low-impact exercises are particularly well-suited for middle-aged and elderly populations due to their gentle nature and holistic approach to wellness. Clinical evidence shows Tai Chi can lower systolic blood pressure by an average of 11 mmHg and diastolic pressure by 6 mmHg, while improving heart rate variability and autonomic nervous system function [18]. Similarly, an 8-week Baduanjin program demonstrates significant reductions in blood pressure alongside measurable mental health improvements [19].

The safety profile and comprehensive benefits of these traditional Chinese mind-body exercises make them superior to conventional high-intensity workouts for hypertension management. Their low-impact nature minimizes joint stress and cardiovascular strain, substantially reducing risks of exercise-related injuries [20]. Compared to aerobic or resistance training, they show better compliance rates and fewer adverse events in elderly populations while simultaneously addressing mental health by reducing anxiety and stress [21]. This combination of safety, efficacy, and psychological benefits positions traditional Chinese mind-body exercises as an optimal long-term health management strategy for hypertensive older adults.

This study represents the first systematic Network Meta-Analysis (NMA) to comparatively evaluate traditional Chinese mind-body exercises (TCME) for modulating cardiovascular risk markers in middle-aged and elderly hypertensive populations. Through rigorous analysis of 49 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we aim to: (1) establish comparative efficacy profiles of six distinct TCME interventions (including Tai Chi, Baduanjin, and Liuzijue); (2) quantify their differential impacts on comprehensive risk indicators (SBP, DBP, BMI, WC, WHR); and (3) develop an evidence-based ranking of therapeutic options. Our NMA approach addresses these limitations by enabling direct multi-intervention comparisons while expanding the evaluative framework to incorporate multiple anthropometric and hemodynamic risk parameters, thereby providing clinically actionable insights for personalized exercise prescription in cardiovascular risk management.

Method

Registration

The research protocol for this Network Meta-Analysis (NMA) was registered with the PROSPERO International Systematic Review Registry Platform (registration number: CRD42024590583) and was conducted in strict adherence to the preferred reporting specifications for systematic evaluations and NMA analyses as stated in the PRISMA statement [22].

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane, EBSCO, Embase, China Knowledge Network (CNKI), Wanfang Digital Journals (WANFANG), and Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP). Two authors independently completed the screening of included studies. In PubMed/Cochrane and Embase, searches were performed using relevant terms from the MeSH and Emtree vocabularies, whereas in the CNKI, WANFANG and VIP databases, searches were performed by combining subject terms and free words. The specific search strategy for each database is detailed in Appendix 1. The search strategy was designed based on the PICOS framework:

(P)Study population: hypertensive patients;

(I)Interventions: Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Liuzijue, Shuxinpingxuegong, Qigong, Daoyinyangshengshu;

(C) Control: The control group involved usual nursing care, routine treatment, or no structured exercise, with patients maintaining their daily habits;

(C) Control: The control group involved usual nursing care, routine treatment, or no structured exercise, with patients maintaining their daily habits;

(O) Outcome indicators: Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), Waist circumference (WC), Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and Body Mass Index (BMI);

(S) Study type: RCTs.

(S) Study type: RCTs.

In addition, we manually checked the reference lists of selected articles and related reviews to ensure that any potentially relevant studies missed in the electronic search were added.

Eligibility criteria

Studies that met the following inclusion criteria were eligible to be selected for analysis: (1) age ≥ 45 years; (2) the study population was hypertensive (SBP/DBP > 130/80 mmHg) [23]; (3) the use of Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Liuzijue, Shuxinpingxuegong, Qigong or Daoyinyangshengshu as interventions; (4) at least one of the following metrics was assessed: SBP, DBP, WC, WHR, or BMI; (5) the experimental group underwent structured exercise training that lasted greater than or equal to 8 weeks; and (6) English-language RCTs published between January 2000 and June 2024 were included.

The following studies will be excluded: (1) repetitively published studies, literature reviews, conference abstracts, animal experiments, or acute intervention studies containing only a single session of training; (2) articles with unavailability of full text or incomplete study data; (3) studies that did not report the core metrics of interest for this NMA; (4) studies in which exercise interventions were mixed with other interventions (e.g., novel antihypertensive medications, diets, etc.); and (5) non-RCTs.

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted information on each study that met the inclusion criteria, including the following: information on the literature (lead author, year of publication, country of study), demographic characteristics of the subjects (sample size, age, and comorbidities in the experimental and control groups), specific information on the exercise intervention (type of intervention, intensity, duration, frequency, period, and whether or not the intervention was supervised), and the main outcome indicators. Where necessary, we contacted the authors of the original studies for more detailed information.

Risk of bias and GRADE assessment

According to the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool, two authors assessed the risk of bias (ROB) for all eligible studies [24]. Participant blinding was not assessed due to the specific nature of exercise interventions, which make it difficult to blind participants [25], but was analyzed for six other categories of risk of bias, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors, data completeness, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Based on the assessment results, we classified the overall risk of bias of the study into three categories: (1) low ROB: no high risk of bias, or up to three domains rated as unclear risk; (2) medium ROB: up to one domain of high risk of bias, or no high risk of bias but four or more domains of unclear risk; and (3) high ROB: the presence of two or more domains of high risk of bias, or five or more domains unclear risks exist [26].

In addition, we systematically assessed the certainty of evidence for network estimates of primary and secondary outcomes through the GRADE framework [27]. In the GRADE system for rating quality of evidence, the levels are classified as High, Moderate, Low, and Very Low, with the following meanings.

High certainty: We are very confident that the true effect lies close to the estimate of the effect.

High certainty: We are very confident that the true effect lies close to the estimate of the effect.

Moderate certainty: We are moderately confident in the effect estimate, but the true effect may be substantially different.

Moderate certainty: We are moderately confident in the effect estimate, but the true effect may be substantially different.

Low certainty: Our confidence in the effect estimate is limited, and the true effect may differ significantly.

Low certainty: Our confidence in the effect estimate is limited, and the true effect may differ significantly.

Very Low certainty: We have very little confidence in the effect estimate, and the true effect is likely to be substantially different.

Very Low certainty: We have very little confidence in the effect estimate, and the true effect is likely to be substantially different.

These ratings are determined based on an assessment of study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The present NMA combined pre- and post- changes in the experimental and control groups to systematically assess the effects of traditional Chinese mind-body exercise therapy on SBP, DBP, WC, WHR, and BMI. In addition to network estimation, a two-by-two meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.3 (Nordic Cochrane, Denmark) to assess inter-study heterogeneity [28]. To ensure the accuracy of the intervention effect, we calculated the standardized mean difference (SMD) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each indicator and combined effect estimates using a random effects model to account for differences between study populations and intervention modalities [29]. Heterogeneity was quantified by the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with an I² greater than 50% or a Q test p ≤ 0.10 considered significant, and the presence of publication bias was assessed using funnel plots [30].

Data analyses were performed in STATA 17.0 software (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), implementing random-effects multivariate NMA in a frequency-based framework [31, 32], following the PRISMA NMA guidelines [1]. The results of the analysis included the combined effect estimates and their 95% confidence intervals and prediction intervals. Relationships between interventions were visualized through network diagrams, where the size of nodes and the thickness of connecting lines were proportional to the number of studies, visually demonstrating the relative strength of each intervention in relation to its position in the network. The network contribution graph further quantifies the contribution of each direct comparison to the overall network, facilitating assessment of the impact of each intervention.

Inclusion criteria for the studies were scrutinized to ensure that participants were randomly assigned to the different intervention groups. The key assumptions of the NMA were transmissibility, i.e., that indirect comparisons effectively represent unobserved direct comparisons [33], and the assumption that effect modifiers across studies were evenly distributed across studies [12]. Consistency tests were performed by calculating the inconsistency factors (IFs) and their 95% confidence intervals for each closed loop, and if the inconsistency was not significant (p > 0.05), the consistency model was used [34]. Local inconsistencies were checked by node partitioning analysis to ensure the robustness of the analysis (p > 0.05).

To compare and rank the effects of different exercise interventions, we analyzed them using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Area (SUCRA) [35]. SUCRA values range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating a more effective intervention [36]. In addition, publication bias in NMA was detected using network funnel plots and visually assessed by the symmetry criterion.

Results

Literature search and selection

A total of 2900 potentially relevant documents were obtained through the electronic search, of which 2893 were from databases and 7 originated from reference lists. After de-duplication, the remaining 1,278 documents entered the title and abstract screening stage, and 1,123 documents were excluded during the screening process. According to the inclusion criteria, further full-text review was conducted to exclude 106 non-compliant documents, and 49 were finally included [37–85]. Characteristics of the included studies

Six traditional Chinese mind-body exercises (Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Liuzijue, Shuxinpingxuegong, Qigong, and Daoyinyangshengshu) were used and compared with a no-exercise control group in 49 randomized controlled trials. Tai Chi was the most frequently applied with 52.22%, followed by Baduanjin with 26.53%. The duration of each exercise ranged from 15 to 90 min, with a mean of 47.15 min.

The average duration of exercise interventions was 20.6 weeks, ranging from 8 to 48 weeks; more than 85.71 per cent of the studies implemented a 12-week intervention. The average frequency of exercise per week was 3.9 sessions. Only three studies did not have a supervised intervention and used a combination of family interventions; the remaining studies were supervised interventions. The primary outcome indicators of interest in this study included Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), Waist circumference (WC), Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and Body Mass Index (BMI).

Risk of bias assessment results

The results of the overall ROB assessment are detailed in Table 2. Of the 49 trials, 38 (77.55%) were rated as low risk (ROB), 11 (22.45%) as moderate risk. Specifically, 38 literatures explicitly reported random sequence generation, 33 mentioned group concealment, and 35 blinded the outcome assessor. In terms of data completeness, 35 literatures were rated as low risk. In addition, 42 literatures had a low risk of selective reporting. However, 7 studies were rated as high risk due to other bias factors.

Direct pairwise meta-analyses

This Network Meta-Analysis (NMA) began with a two-by-two meta-analysis and the funnel plot is shown in Fig. 2. The results are presented as a forest plot showing the effects of different exercise categories on SBP, DBP, WC, WHR and BMI see Appendix 4 (1–5).

Funnel plot for SBP, DBP, WC, WHR and BMI in pairwise meta-analysis

The meta-analysis of SBP showed that Tai Chi (SMD = −1.10, 95% CI [−1.21, −1.00], p < 0.00001, I² = 16%) significantly reduced SBP in hypertensive patients with low heterogeneity and high concordance of efficacy. The results of the meta-analysis of SBP were similar to those of the meta-analyses of SBP by Baduanjin (SMD = −0.96, 95% CI [−1.14, − 0.78], p < 0.00001, I² = 51%), Liuzijue (SMD = −0.99, 95% CI [−1.35, −0.63], p < 0.00001, I² = 46%), Shuxinpingxuegong (SMD = −0.88, 95% CI [−1.28, −0.47], p < 0.00001, I² = 0%), Qigong (SMD = −0.68, 95% CI [−1.30, −0.05], p = 0.03, I² = 77%), and Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = −0.98, 95% CI [−1.31, −0.66], p < 0.00001, I² = 21%) also had a significant effect on reducing SBP. Overall, all interventions significantly reduced SBP (SMD = −1.01, 95% CI [−1.10, −0.92], p < 0.00001, I² = 40%).

The meta-analysis of DBP, Tai Chi (SMD = −0.89, 95% CI [−1.00, −0.77], p < 0.00001, I² = 29%) significantly reduced DBP in hypertensive patients. Baduanjin (SMD = −0.94, 95% CI [−1.21, −0.67], p < 0.00001, I² = 76%), Liuzijue (SMD = −1.08, 95% CI [−1.48, −0.69], p < 0.00001, I² = 54%), Shuxinpingxuegong (SMD = −1.04, 95% CI [−1.46, −0.63], p < 0.00001, I² = 0%), Qigong (SMD = −0.76, 95% CI [−1.06, −0.46], p = 0.00001, I² = 6%) and Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = −1.18, 95% CI [−1.45, −0.90], p < 0.00001, I² = 0%) were also significantly effective in reducing DBP. Overall, all interventions significantly reduced DBP (SMD = −0.94, 95% CI [−1.03, −0.84], p < 0.00001, I² = 51%).

The meta-analysis of WC resulted in a significant reduction in WHR in Tai Chi (SMD = −0.52, 95% CI [−0.82, −0.22], p = 0.0008, I² = 0%) and Baduanjin (SMD = −0.97, 95% CI [−1.24, −0.69], p < 0.00001) compared to the control group, and in Qigong (SMD = −0.37, 95% CI [−0.71, −0.03], p = 0.03, I² = 0%) also showed a significant effect. Overall results showed a significant reduction in WC for all interventions (SMD = −0.59, 95% CI [−0.84, −0.33], p < 0.00001) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 46%).

A meta-analysis of WHR resulted in significantly improved HR in Tai Chi (SMD = −0.50, 95% CI [−0.71, −0.30], p < 0.00001, I² = 0%) compared to Baduanjin (SMD = −0.27, 95% CI [−0.84, 0.30], p = 0.35) compared to controls, Liuzijue (SMD = −0.20, 95% CI [−0.57, 0.17], p = 0.29), and Qigong (SMD = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.52, 0.32], p = 0.64) did not show a significant effect. Overall analysis showed a SMD = −0.37 (95% CI [−0.54, −0.20], p < 0.00001) for improvement in WHR for all interventions, with low heterogeneity (I² = 9%).

The results of the meta-analysis of BMI, compared to the control group, showed a significant effect on reducing BMI in Tai Chi (SMD = −0.36, 95% CI [−0.51, −0.21], p < 0.00001, I² = 29%), Baduanjin (SMD = −0.26, 95% CI [−0.49, −0.03], p = 0.03, I² = 2%) and Daoyinyangshengshu (SMD = −0.52, 95% CI [−1.09, −0.06], p < 0.03) had a significant effect on lowering BMI. Qigong (SMD = −0.23, 95% CI [−0.65, 0.19], p = 0.28) had no significant effect. Overall analysis showed a significant overall effect of all interventions on BMI reduction (SMD = −0.30, 95% CI [−0.45, −0.14], p = 0.0002) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 58%).

This NMA assessed the consistency of SBP, DBP, WC, WHR, and BMI through loop-specific heterogeneity estimation, inconsistency modelling, and node-split analysis (see Appendix 6). The results of the ring-specific heterogeneity estimation showed that the heterogeneity between the closed loops of SBP and DBP was not significant (P > 0.05), indicating good intra-closed-loop agreement and supporting the continuation of comparative analyses. Inconsistency modelling results showed that the difference between direct and indirect comparisons of SBP, DBP, WC, WHR and BMI was not significant (p > 0.05). Nodal split analyses further indicated that there was no significant inconsistency (p > 0.05) between direct and indirect evidence for SBP and DBP, supporting the reliability of the results.

In our NMA, funnel plots (Fig. 5) for SBP, WC, WHR and BMI were largely symmetrical, indicating minimal likelihood of publication bias or small-study effects. Egger’s regression tests further confirmed this, with p-values for SBP (p = 0.389), WC (p = 0.582), WHR (p = 0.366), and BMI (p = 0.301) all exceeding 0.05, suggesting robust findings. For DBP, Egger’s test indicated potential publication bias (p = 0.038). The trim-and-fill analysis estimated 11 missing studies on the right side of the funnel plot. While the effect size attenuated from Hedges’s g = −0.931 (95% CI [−1.034, −0.829]) to Hedges’s g = −0.801 ([−0.918, −0.684]), the pooled effect sizes for DBP remained significant (p < 0.05) both before and after adjustment, confirming the stability of our results (See Appendix 7). In summary, our NMA results are robust against publication bias and small-study effects, with DBP results being stable despite potential bias.

In our study, we conducted a rigorous assessment of the transitivity assumption to ensure the validity of the NMA results. To evaluate transitivity, we systematically compared key variables across different trials, including age, intervention duration, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and systolic blood pressure (SBP). We utilized box plots to visually examine the distribution of these critical characteristics among the included studies. Through this graphical approach, we confirmed that the distribution patterns of age and baseline blood pressure values were relatively consistent across studies. For the transitivity assumption, age, SBP and DBP were compared using box-and-line plots, indicating that the studies had comparable baseline characteristics (see Appendix 8). For baseline comorbidity and degree of supervision, we redid the forest plots after excluding studies with comorbidities as well as unsupervised studies, and the direction of the effect sizes was unaltered and small in magnitude, as detailed in Appendix 9 and Appendix 10. For intervention intensity, we conducted regression analyses for intervention period, intervention frequency, and total intervention duration, which showed no significant moderating effect of intervention intensity (all p-values > 0.05), suggesting that intervention intensity did not have a significant effect on treatment outcomes, as detailed in Appendix 11.

In sensitivity analysis, results’ robustness was evaluated by excluding studies one-by-one. Effect estimates mostly stayed in a range without direction change, but some indicators’ confidence intervals crossed the null - effect boundary of 1. This might be due to small sample size of excluded studies, their role as main heterogeneity sources, or issues with statistical model assumptions and multiple - comparison adjustments. For the sensitivity analysis, the one-out method was first employed, and the results did not cross zero, indicating the robustness of the findings (Appendix 12). Subsequently, studies with moderate-to-high risk of bias were excluded for pooled effect size estimation, and the combined results showed no directional change, suggesting that such studies had no significant impact on the overall outcome (Appendix 13).

Minimally contextualized framework

The control group was set as the reference. Interventions were classified into three categories: category 0 had no significant clinical difference compared to the control; category 1 was clinically superior; and category 2 outperformed other interventions. Category 0 indicated no notable disparity relative to the control. Then, a secondary classification was done based on intervention differences, with more effective ones from category 0 reclassified into category 1. After that, interventions were hierarchically grouped as high or low - reliability, and the classification’s consistency was verified by ranking. This study aimed to ensure top - ranked interventions were among the most effective (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, the largest Network Meta-Analysis (NMA) on the effects of cardiovascular risk factors in middle-aged and elderly hypertensive patients, we assessed the effects of six traditional Chinese mind-body exercises on vascular health by integrating 49 RCTs.

The results of the publication bias test showed that the funnel plots were largely symmetrical, suggesting that our estimates of the effects of traditional Chinese mind-body exercises were relatively accurate and did not underestimate their potential impact. However, although most of the included studies were of low risk, there were still some studies that were rated as moderate or high risk due to design and implementation problems. These medium- to high-risk studies may have had unstable and poorly reproducible results due to design and implementation flaws, such as unclear randomization methods, insufficient sample sizes and short follow-up periods. It may also be related to the different geographical distribution of the included literature, as there are also differences in culture and lifestyle between different geographical areas.

In summary, all the traditional Chinese mind-body exercises examined significantly reduced both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) in hypertensive patients, with Tai Chi showing a particularly prominent effect. Qigong also showed some efficacy but to a lesser extent. When it comes to generalized obesity measured by Body Mass Index (BMI), Tai Chi and Baduanjin were significantly effective in lowering BMI, while Qigong failed to show a significant effect in some of the studies. Regarding abdominal obesity, indicated by Waist circumference (WC) and Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), Tai Chi had a significant effect in reducing both WC and WHR, highlighting its potential in improving abdominal obesity and reducing the associated cardiovascular disease risk.

Blood pressure

In our study, we found that all interventions significantly reduced SBP and DBP, with a mean reduction of − 1.10 (95% CI [− 1.23, − 0.98], p < 0.00001) in SBP and − 0.89 (95% CI [− 1.00, − 0.77], p < 0.00001) in DBP after Tai Chi intervention. Our findings robustly demonstrate the antihypertensive efficacy of traditional Chinese mind-body exercises in hypertensive patients, which is consistent with previous studies. Li et al. in their study found that Tai Chi training was effective in reducing BP levels in hypertensive patients, which supports the findings of our study [86]. In addition, Larsen’s study also pointed out that traditional Chinese mind-body exercises help to improve endothelial function and reduce the risk of CVD. The improvement of endothelial function may be achieved through mechanisms such as increasing vasodilation and improving hemodynamics, which leads to a decrease in BP [87]. The significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP) observed with Tai Chi and other mind-body exercises suggest that these interventions could be integrated into hypertension management guidelines as adjunctive therapy.

From the perspective of physiological mechanisms, conventional exercise affects HR and BP by modulating the autonomic nervous system and reducing sympathetic excitability [88]. For example, Stanley’s study showed that low-intensity aerobic exercise improves autonomic balance and promotes parasympathetic activity, which is important for BP control [89]. In addition, conventional exercise can dilate blood vessels by increasing nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, which in turn reduces BP [90]. In one study, Tai Chi was found to significantly increase peripheral vascular NO levels, which provides a physiological basis for its antihypertensive effect [91].

Although our results showed a relatively small effect of Qigong on BP (SMD = − 0.68, 95% CI [− 1.03, − 0.30], p = 0.03), it still demonstrated some efficacy when compared to Park’s study results. Possible reasons for this may lie in the exercise habits of the participants and the frequency with which the intervention was delivered. The average frequency of all interventions in our study was 3.9 times per week, whereas the lower frequency in Park’s study may have led to a less pronounced effect [74].

Daoyinyangshengshu ranked second for SBP reduction, suggesting its potential as an effective mind-body intervention for blood pressure management [92]. This may be attributed to its characteristic slow movements combined with breath regulation, which could enhance vascular relaxation and autonomic balance—a hypothesis warranting further physiological studies.

Generalized obesity

For systemic obesity, our meta-analysis demonstrated significant BMI reductions from Tai Chi (SMD = − 0.36, 95% CI [− 0.51, − 0.21], p < 0.00001) and Baduanjin (SMD = − 0.26, 95% CI [− 0.49, − 0.03], p = 0.03), consistent with Mielke’s findings in hypertensive patients [93]. These effects likely occur through increased energy expenditure and enhanced fat metabolism, as regular aerobic exercise elevates basal metabolic rate and fat oxidation [94]. The third-place ranking for BMI improvement indicates Daoyinyangshengshu’s moderate but clinically relevant metabolic effects. Its inclusion of gentle torso-twisting and limb-stretching components may promote energy expenditure and body composition changes, though its mechanisms likely differ from higher-intensity exercises [95]. Future trials should standardize intervention protocols to clarify its dose-response relationship.

While Qigong showed nonsignificant effects (SMD = − 0.23), possibly due to intervention duration (mean 20.6 weeks) or participant exercise habits, combining traditional Chinese mind-body exercises with dietary modifications may optimize weight control and cardiovascular risk reduction [96].

Notably, these exercises offer dual physiological and psychological benefits - improving mental health by reducing anxiety/depression [97], which Stagi found subsequently promotes better weight management and overall health [98]. The significant reductions in BMI, Waist circumference (WC), and Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) highlight the role of mind-body exercises in mitigating obesity-related cardiovascular risks. Since abdominal obesity is strongly linked to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis, Tai Chi’s efficacy in reducing WC and WHR suggests it may help lower metabolic syndrome risk. This effect is attributed to Tai Chi’s unique combination of deep diaphragmatic breathing and controlled core engagement, which enhances oxygen utilization, stimulates gentle yet sustained abdominal muscle activation, and promotes visceral fat metabolism—all while maintaining low joint stress. The slow, meditative movements further regulate stress hormones like cortisol, reducing fat accumulation in the central adiposity region.

Abdominal obesity

For abdominal obesity, Tai Chi significantly reduced WC (SMD = − 0.50, 95% CI [− 0.71, − 0.30], p < 0.00001) and WHR (SMD = − 0.52, 95% CI [− 0.82, − 0.22], p = 0.0008), aligning with Zhang’s demonstration of traditional Chinese mind-body exercises reducing abdominal fat and improving cardiovascular health [99]. The observed reductions in WHR should be considered in the context of methodological limitations. Our GRADE evaluation classified this evidence as low quality, reflecting both the limited number of contributing studies (n = 6) and heterogeneity in assessment protocols. These factors may influence the generalizability and reliability of the WHR-related findings.

Since abdominal obesity strongly correlates with cardiovascular risk [100], these reductions may decrease metabolic syndrome risk through multiple mechanisms: regulating fat metabolism, improving endocrine function [101], and enhancing circulatory/autonomic nervous system regulation during low-intensity exercise. While Qin’s study reported nonsignificant WHR effects (potentially due to sample characteristics or intervention duration), our results highlight Tai Chi’s potential for managing abdominal obesity and cardiovascular risk [102].

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our analysis compared the efficacy among different traditional Chinese mind-body exercises, we did not account for potentially influential factors such as exercise frequency or supervision status. Future research should investigate how these implementation parameters may modify treatment effects. Secondly, some comparative results had low certainty of evidence and were at potential risk of bias. Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the fact that most included studies were conducted in China, where cultural familiarity with traditional mind-body practices could influence both intervention adherence and outcomes. Future multinational studies would help determine whether these interventions are equally effective in other cultural contexts. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data in most included studies precludes conclusions about the sustainability of observed effects. Subsequent studies should incorporate extended follow-up periods to evaluate the durability of therapeutic benefits.

Conclusion

This NMA supports the integration of traditional Chinese mind-body exercises into hypertension management for middle-aged and elderly patients, though effectiveness varies across interventions. Tai Chi demonstrates the most consistent benefits for both blood pressure and abdominal obesity reduction, while Baduanjin specifically improves BMI. Considering these variations in effectiveness, we recommend tailoring interventions to individual patient preferences and health status. Culturally adapted protocols, including simplified Tai Chi sequences or hybrid Western-Chinese programs, may improve global adoption and adherence.
