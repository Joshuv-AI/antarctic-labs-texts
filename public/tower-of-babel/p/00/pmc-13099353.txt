Extraskeletal osteosarcoma arising in the globe of an irradiated, heritable retinoblastoma survivor
Authors: Joana E Andoh, Faith E Stone, Idara Wade-Vallance, Loka Thangamathsavaran, Frank Mei, Shazia Dharssi, John M Gross, Nicholas Mahoney, James T Handa, Charles Eberhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIndividuals with hereditary retinoblastoma (RB) carry a heightened risk of developing subsequent malignant neoplasms that is amplified by prior radiation treatment. Here, we present a novel case of intraocular extraskeletal osteosarcoma (ESOS) arising in the phthisical eye of an irradiated, heritable RB survivor.ObservationsA 23-year-old man with a history of bilateral RB presented with a year of left orbital and eye pain. On examination, the left eye had mucopurulent discharge and exposed orbital tissue. On imaging, the left globe, periocular structures, optic nerve, and optic nerve sheath were surrounded by abnormal contrast enhancement. As a result, the left eye was enucleated. On histopathology, an ESOS filled most of the globe with extraocular extension. The tumor was associated with focal, benign-appearing intraocular bone of the type commonly seen in phthisical eyes. The patient was found to have pulmonary metastases, consistent with metastatic stage IV osteosarcoma. He was started on systemic chemotherapy and underwent an exenteration of the left orbit.Conclusions and importanceThis case highlights a rare manifestation of osteosarcoma in an irradiated, heritable RB survivor. It underscores the need for longitudinal ophthalmic examination that potentially includes periodic echography when eyes are phthisical, and oncologic surveillance in RB survivors to facilitate early diagnosis and intervention for life-threatening secondary malignancies. The findings also suggest that osteosarcoma can arise from metaplastic bone within phthisical globes.Keywords: Ocular oncology, Ocular pathology, Osteosarcoma, Retinoblastoma

Introduction

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of childhood, with a global incidence of 1 in 16,000–18,000 live births.1 Approximately 65%-75% of children with RB have the non-heritable form while the remainder of children with RB (25%-35%) have the heritable form.2 The heritable form of RB arises from a mutation on chromosome 13 of the gene that encodes the tumor suppressor RB1 gene.3 Along with an increased risk for bilateral RB, the heritable form of RB is associated with an elevated risk of developing subsequent malignant neoplasms (SMNs), with a cumulative incidence of 33% after 50 years.4,5 Radiation therapy, especially before the age of one year has been reported to increase the risk of SMNs among heritable RB survivors.6, 7, 8 Although osteosarcoma is the most common SMN among RB survivors, these generally arise far from the globe and cases of orbital extraskeletal osteosarcoma (ESOS) are rare.9, 10, 11 Here, we report a novel case of intraocular ESOS with metastasis arising in a phthisical eye of an irradiated, heritable RB survivor.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old man presented with one year of left eye pain and swelling. Three weeks prior to presentation, he experienced mucopurulent discharge and difficulty with closing his eye, prompting an emergent evaluation. His ocular history was significant for bilateral RB with a pathogenic RB1 variant known as c. 1951del p.(Tyr651IIefsTer7). He had prior radiation in both eyes and systemic chemotherapy during childhood (3 cycles of vincristine, etoposide, and carboplatin). He underwent enucleation of the right eye at the age of 7 months. His left eye had cataract extraction and had been no light perception following a retinal detachment more than 10 years before presentation. His medical history included obstructive sleep apnea and class III obesity (body mass index of 48 kg/m2). His mother, maternal aunt, and maternal grandfather had bilateral RB, and paternal relatives had congenital cataracts. Notably, the patient had housing insecurity and he had not seen an ophthalmologist in over 8 years.

On examination, the patient was no light perception in the left eye. His right orbit was anophthalmic with healthy conjunctiva overlying his prosthesis and no masses appreciated. His left eye had an intraocular pressure of 31 mmHg. The left eye was phthisical and had protruding tissue with mucopurulent discharge along the inferior orbital rim (Fig. 1A). On ultrasound, no identifiable ocular structures were seen while focal, circumscribed areas of highly reflective non-mobile and dense opacities were visualized in the orbit (Fig. 1B). A right maxillary mucocele and T1 hyperintensity of the left globe with an irregular contour and circumferential thickening were seen on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast of the brain and orbits (Fig. 1C). T1 hyperintensity surrounding the periocular structures (Fig. 1D) and T2 enhancement of the left optic nerve and sheath were also observed, which suggested acute inflammation of the left globe (Fig. 1E).

The patient was hemodynamically stable and afebrile with a white blood cell count within normal limits. With imaging concerning for acute inflammation/infection, he was admitted for intravenous antibiotics (vancomycin 20mg/kg daily and ceftazidime 1g q8 hours for 5 days total) and started on topical antibiotics (tobramycin 15mg/mL and vancomycin 31mg/mL every 2 hours). Moderate skin flora (no pathogenic organisms identified) grew from a conjunctival swab culture. Blood cultures did not grow infectious organisms. Without clinical improvement after three days, the suspicion for underlying malignancy was raised and the decision was made to enucleate the left eye. Intraoperatively, the globe resembled an encephaloid mass with chalky white areas of scar tissue (Fig. 2). The superior, lateral, and inferior rectus muscles were unable to be isolated and an implant was not placed. The globe, sections of the orbit, and left optic nerve were obtained for histopathology.

Intraoperative examination of the left orbit and tumor of a 23-year-old man with a history of bilateral retinoblastoma treated with childhood radiation and chemotherapy. A. Preoperative photo of the left orbit and tumor prior to enucleation. B. Gross specimen of the intraocular tumor status post enucleation demonstrates loss of visible anatomical structures.

On gross and microscopic analysis of the enucleated eye, a tan to chalky white high-grade osteosarcoma filled the eye with both anterior and posterior extraocular extension through scleral defects to the inked margins (Fig. 3A). Intraoperative cultures grew one colony of Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes. The tumor cells were large and pleomorphic, with both mineralized and non-mineralized malignant osteoid production, frequent mitoses, and some multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 3B and C). Neoplastic cells immunostained for Special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (SATB2), supporting osteoblastic differentiation, with loss of RB protein (Fig. 3D and E). The tumor also focally stained for S100, but was negative for epithelial membrane antigen, desmin, cluster of differentiation (CD) 31 and CD99. No residual RB was identified. Mature bone similar to that typically seen in phthisical eyes was also present within the globe, with no surrounding neoplastic cells in some areas, suggestive that it was present before the tumor formed (Fig. 3F).

With a systemic work-up, pulmonary metastases were identified, indicative of stage IV metastatic osteosarcoma. The patient was initiated on ifosfamide, etoposide, and methotrexate. Five months after initial presentation, the patient underwent a lid-sparing exenteration of residual orbital tissue of the left orbit with oculoplastic surgery and otolaryngology. Resection pathology demonstrated residual osteosarcoma limited to small foci within a background of scar and post-treatment changes, with approximately 70% viable tumor and negative surgical margins. The remaining orbital specimens were negative for malignancy. The patient subsequently completed 5 cycles (25 weeks) of the aforementioned systemic chemotherapy. Eight months after initial presentation, he underwent left video-assisted thoracoscopic resections and a chest wall biopsy. Pathology revealed benign lung parenchyma with papillary adenoma, focal fibrosis, and bronchiolar metaplasia, with no evidence of metastatic osteosarcoma. Thirteen months after initial presentation, the patient's orbital MRI demonstrated stable post-exenteration changes without evidence of residual or recurrent orbital disease. Sixteen months after initial presentation, the patient remains clinically well without symptoms, off therapy and is undergoing surveillance with serial orbital imaging and chest computed tomography every 3 months per pediatric oncology guidelines.

Discussion

Heritable RB survivors have a unique predisposition to SMNs with an 11-20x higher risk compared to the general population.6 Osteosarcomas, rhabdomyosarcomas, leiomyosarcomas, other soft tissue sarcomas, and meningiomas comprise the majority of SMNs in heritable RB survivors.12,13 The elevated risk is thought to be due to germline mutations in RB1 that can impair tumor suppressor function, which can result in uncontrolled cell proliferation during tumorgenesis.6 In contrast, studies have shown that patients with non-hereditary RB have no elevated cancer risk as compared to the general population, given that their mutations are confined to retinal cells.6

The risk of SMNs in heritable RB survivors is also related to the treatment modality. External beam radiation therapy (EBRT) was a standard treatment for RB until the 1990s, when numerous studies identified increased SMNs with EBRT.14,15 Additionally, an increased risk of SMNs has been associated with heritable RB survivors who were treated with EBRT before the age of one year.7,14 Overall, compared to non-irradiated heritable RB survivors, irradiated heritable RB survivors have a 3-fold elevated risk of SMNs.6 Chemotherapy, notably alkylating agents and anthracyclines, has also been reported to increase the risk of SMNs in heritable RB survivors.6 However, small sample sizes and short follow-up has limited major conclusions regarding chemotherapy's isolated SMN risk.16, 17, 18.

Among irradiated heritable RB survivors, the majority of bone and soft tissue sarcomas occur in the head/neck, skin, breast, and central nervous system.13,19 The occurrence of SMNs in hereditary RB has been reported to be 8.5% at 18 years and 36% at 50 years after diagnosis.13 ESOS, arising from soft tissue without connection to the skeleton, are rare and account for 2-4% of all sarcomas.20 We found only eight reported cases of ESOS in the orbit.9, 10, 11,20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Of these cases, one patient was an 11-year-old male with RB who underwent prior radiation as an infant, but the others had no history of RB.21 Two patients without systemic disease developed osteosarcoma within phthisical eyes.9,24

We report a unique case of intraocular ESOS arising in the phthisical eye of an irradiated, heritable RB survivor. In phthisis, metaplastic bone formation from retinal pigment epithelium cells is common. Salomão and colleagues recently suggested that intraocular osteosarcoma could arise via malignant transformation of this generally benign bone-forming process.9 Our case supports this concept, as we observed metaplastic benign-appearing bone that may have provided a nidus for the development of osteosarcoma in a genetically predisposed individual with a history of radiation.

Although the patient's history of RB and prior radiation were significant risk factors for his metastatic disease, the diagnostic delay related to loss of follow-up and absence of routine ophthalmologic and oncologic surveillance were likely contributing factors. Details regarding his initial chemotherapy in childhood were unknown. Of note, his current chemotherapy treatment, including an alkylating agent, poses a potential risk for SMNs. In general, RB patients should undergo dilated fundus exams every 4 weeks during treatment, every 2-3 months during the first year after treatment, every 3-6 months until age 6 or 7, and annually thereafter.25, 26, 27, 28, 29 For survivors of hereditary RB, surveillance for SMNs is essential. The American Association for Cancer Research recommends a multimodal approach, including detailed history, physical and skin examinations, as well as whole-body MRIs at least annually.30 Although no formal guidelines exist, we suggest that B-scan imaging of phthisical eyes be considered a part of the annual ophthalmic examination especially for those with heritable RB given the risk of malignant transformation, to optimize the surveillance for SMNs. In select cases, prophylactic enucleation of a phthisical eye may also be considered as a risk-reducing option, acknowledging that patient-specific factors and clinical context may preclude this approach.

This case underscores a rare presentation of intraocular ESOS with metastasis arising from the phthisical eye of an irradiated, heritable RB survivor. This case also highlights the potential for malignant transformation within metaplastic bone in a phthisical eye and the treatment challenges posed with delayed presentation. Overall, long-term and vigilant surveillance in RB survivors is warranted given the higher risk of secondary malignancies. A multidisciplinary approach involving patient education, primary care providers, ophthalmologists, oncologists, and pathologists is crucial for improving outcomes in this vulnerable population.
