Application of life history theory to explain the association between childhood maltreatment and adulthood sleep problems in Chinese men with drug abuse: multiple mediating roles of emotion regulation, future-oriented coping, and anxiety
Authors: Chun Feng, Anise M S Wu, You-Ping Chen, Yong Xing, Rui Zhai, Xiang-Yang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Despite the practical importance of addressing the drug user’s sleep problems to enhance the efficacy of treatment and rehabilitation, little is known about whether and how history of childhood maltreatment relates to this issue. This study takes an evolutionary perspective to investigate the associations between history of childhood maltreatment and sleep problems in adults with drug abuse via their emotion regulation difficulties, future-oriented coping, and anxiety.Methods: Participants were 604 male adults with drug abuse between the ages of 18–58 years (M = 36.20, SD = 8.17) in a drug rehabilitation centre in China. In addition to bivariate correlation analysis, path analysis was conducted to examine goodness-of-fit of the conceptual model, controlling for the effect of demographic characteristics.Results: Thirty-two percent of participants (n = 194) reported poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5), whereas sleep disturbance (81.3%), daytime dysfunction (77.3%), and sleep latency (66.5%) were the three most common problems among them. Correlation analysis supported the hypothesised positive correlations between poor sleep quality and childhood maltreatment, emotion regulation difficulties, and anxiety, and a negative correlation with future-oriented coping. Results of path analysis showed a significant indirect effect of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems via both emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety, whereas such effects via both future-oriented coping and anxiety were statistically nonsignificant.Conclusions: The findings suggest life history theory is applicable to understanding drug users’ sleep problems, and interventions regarding both emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety can lessen the risk posed by childhood maltreatment on sleep problems.KEYWORDS: Evolutionary theory, childhood experience, emotion regulation, anxiety, sleep problems

Introduction

As a necessary behaviour of human beings, sleep is influenced by both external and internal factors, including neurobiological mechanisms, life experiences, personal traits, and psychological states (Lundh & Broman, 2000). Sleep is also a form of maintenance, which facilitates two primary evolutionary goals (survival and reproduction; Griskevicius et al., 2011). Due to individual differences, some people may suffer from abnormal, problematic sleep conditions (e.g. sleep problems). Inadequate sleep is detrimental to behavioural functioning, and physiological and mental health (Banks & Dinges, 2007). Substance abuse has been found to disrupt regular sleep mechanism (Worthman, 2008); hence, it should not be surprising that drug-using populations report more pronounced sleep problems (Morgan et al., 2006; Putnins et al., 2012). For example, over half of the methamphetamine users (54%) had sleep disturbances, whereas only about one-fourth of the healthy controls (26%) did in China (Tang et al., 2015). Many drug users in treatment also suffer from sleep disturbances, which not only adversely affects treatment outcomes but also drives them to use substances, particularly alcohol and tranquilisers, in order to self-medicate for sleep problems (Tripathi et al., 2020). Interventions like insomnia management are therefore known to contribute to the success of detoxication (Maher, 2004; Peles et al., 2006). From an evolutionary perspective, this study aimed to determine the associations between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems via psychological mechanisms (i.e. emotion regulation and future-oriented coping) and the outcomes of such traumatic experience (i.e. anxiety) on male adults with drug abuse in rehabilitation. The findings can inform more cost-effective interventions.

Childhood maltreatment and sleep problems

Evolutionary theorists have asserted that organisms allocate a limited amount of resources (e.g. food, water, money, time and attention) to various primary life tasks (i.e. growth, maintenance, and reproduction) across their life spans, aiming to maximise their evolutionary fitness (Del Giudice, 2016; Hill, 1993). Considering the daily threat of death, people growing up in harsh environments (e.g. with maltreatment) tend to divert resources to short-term survival systems (e.g. searching for food, water, money) to facilitate rapid maturation and reproduction rather than through slow development and maintenance of processes that improve long-term survival (Ellis et al., 2009). Given its primary function of maintenance, sleep carries high costs (e.g. time, in adverse environments). People growing up in such environments would rather spend time acquiring the necessary nutrients (e.g. food or water) for their short-term survival than sleep a little longer (Griskevicius et al., 2011), which inevitably leads to greater sleep deficits and circadian disturbances in their childhood (Schønning et al., 2022) and adulthood (Dishakjian et al., 2021). Thus, childhood maltreatment is assumed as a general unchangeable and distal factor in one’s life course that influences one's sleep problems in adulthood.

In support of evolutionary theory, retrospective cohort studies have found a significant ‘dose-response’ relationship between childhood maltreatment (i.e. being abused or neglected and sleep problems later in life; McGowan & Coogan, 2018; Steine et al., 2017; World Health Organization, 2020). A systematic review study (including 73 studies) also revealed a robust association between a history of childhood maltreatment and sleep problems across life-span (Brown et al., 2022). Moreover, a history of childhood maltreatment is shared by individuals with substance use disorders (Vilhena-Churchill & Goldstein, 2014), as well as other addictive disorders (e.g. Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang & Wu, 2022). Those substance users (e.g. drug users) who experienced childhood maltreatment may not be able to access a good sleep environment due to the insufficiencies of economic resources or social support (He et al., 2023).

Childhood maltreatment, anxiety, and sleep problems

Childhood maltreatment, as a general unchangeable and distal factor, is also supposed to increase one’s vulnerability to anxiety in adulthood. From a neuropathological perspective, the persistent sensitisation of the central nervous system circuits as a result of early life stress may be the biological basis for one’s increased vulnerability to subsequent psychological distress and disorders, including anxiety, in adulthood (Heim & Nemeroff, 2001). As expected, anxiety disorders were consistently found more prevalent in adults who have experienced childhood maltreatment (e.g. Felitti et al., 1998). Subsequently, a meta-analysis of prospective studies (including 199 papers) indicated a positive association between childhood maltreatment and adulthood anxiety (Li et al., 2016).

Because anxiety makes getting and staying asleep difficult for individuals (Johnson et al., 2006; Luo et al., 2013), anxiety may mediate the effect of childhood maltreatment on sleep. Indeed, in previous cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, anxiety mediated adverse childhood experiences and sleep problems among adolescents (McPhie et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021) and adults (Ashour et al., 2024). This mediating effect is worth testing among drug users in rehabilitation because anxiety disorders are prevalent in this population (e.g. 24–67%; Mohamed et al., 2020; Wittchen et al., 1994; Zhao et al., 2011); drug users with a history of childhood maltreatment report significantly higher levels of anxiety than their counterparts without a history of childhood maltreatment (Chen et al., 2022).

Impacts of childhood maltreatment via emotion regulation and anxiety

Early adverse environments also affect one’s emotion regulation development (Kim & Cicchetti, 2010). Emotion regulation is a developing trait formed in one’s childhood (Girme et al., 2021), which is defined as being able to recognise when and how emotions arise, as well as how emotions are expressed and experienced (Rottenberg & Gross, 2003). According to LHT, children who grow up in an unstable and harsh environment will naturally maximise their early maturation (e.g. reaching puberty earlier) and reproduction (e.g. finding a mate and having children earlier) at all costs, including at the expense of having lower levels of emotional competence (Chisholm et al., 1993). This is the reason why people in such environments require trade-offs between physical maturity/reproduction and emotional development. Compared to emotional development, early maturation allows people to adapt more quickly to the environment to improve their chances of short-term survival, while reproduction allows people to pass on their genes to the next generation as soon as possible (Hill, 1993).Thus, in such environment, resources (e.g. time, attention and energy) are more likely to be spent on short-term survival (e.g. finding food, water and shelter) rather than on fostering control over impulses (craving for drug; Szepsenwol et al., 2022) and emotions (e.g. anxiety) that may have long-term benefits (e.g. better control over one’s impulses).

In support, previous cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have consistently found that childhood maltreatment can hamper emotion regulation development in childhood. Furthermore, such effects endure inevitably, and are expressed as emotion regulation difficulties (i.e. the lack of ability to control one’s emotions and behaviour in upsetting situations; Gratz & Roemer, 2004; Warmingham et al., 2023) in adolescence and adulthood (Hahm et al., 2010).

Emotion regulation difficulties are broadly regard as a risk and determining factor (Aldao et al., 2010; Cicchetti et al., 1995) in the realms of both physical (e.g. sleep disturbance; Fairholme & Manber, 2015) and mental health (e.g. anxiety disorder; Cisler & Olatunji, 2012). Longitudinal studies have found that it increases the risk of sleep problems (Tavernier & Willoughby, 2015). A neuroscientific study also indicated that the brain structures and neurochemicals that regulate emotions also regulate sleep (Goldstein & Walker, 2014). Moreover, a plenty of studies found that emotion regulation difficulties are predictive of generalised anxiety disorder (e.g. Daros et al., 2021; Mennin, 2004). Hence, emotion regulation difficulties are potential risk factors of sleep problems via enhancing anxiety. However, in previous studies, this risk factor (emotion regulation difficulties) was not considered as a serial mediator along with anxiety (another mediator) in the association between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems.

Impacts of childhood adversity via future – oriented coping and anxiety

Future-orientated coping is defined as individuals’ effort to prepare for and mitigate potential arising events before they occur (Gan et al., 2007). Similar to emotion regulation difficulties, lower future-orientated coping is also a developing trait formed in the context of harsh environment in one’s childhood (Griskevicius et al., 2011). According to LHT (Figueredo et al., 2006), people who grow up in adverse environments are less future-oriented (i.e. consider the future and plan/prepare for future events) because the difficult and unpredictable environments would diminish their ability to anticipate future gains in favour of short-term survival (e.g. finding food, water, and shelter; Brumbach et al., 2009) or immediate rewards (e.g. drug, gamble; Griskevicius et al., 2011; Oshri et al., 2018b).

Both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have consistently supported above notion (Burger & Mortimer, 2021; Copersino et al., 2006; Oshri et al., 2018a). For example, child abuse survivors have been found to think less about the future than those without experiences of abuse in childhood (e.g. Klein & Janoff-Bulman, 1996). Furthermore, a 4-wave longitudinal study also provided evidence that people experiencing childhood maltreatment are much more likely to be attracted to the immediate gratification of getting drugs in their childhood and adulthood (Oshri et al., 2018b), suggesting that drug users with childhood maltreatment may have relatively stable lower future-orientated coping in their childhood and adulthood.

Future orientation, like emotion regulation, is also considered to be beneficial for one's long-term survival from an evolutionary perspective. People who live in an environment with sufficient resources (e.g. food, money, residence) have enough time and energy to think about the future (Brumbach et al., 2009). For example, keeping up with daily health maintenance for the sake of future health. As Temporal Self-Regulation Theory (Hall & Fong, 2007) suggested, one’s temporal connectedness beliefs may affect health problems (e.g. believe that present behaviour will shape later health outcomes). Therefore, people with future orientation tend to think about maintenance-related behaviours (e.g. sufficient sleep; Alberts & Dunton, 2008), as well as report more regular sleep schedules and better sleep quality, compared to present-oriented people (Milfont & Schwarzenthal, 2014). A neuropathological study also revealed that future orientation (e.g. future thinking) has a moderate correlation with greater sleep spindle density in young adults, even when controlling for gender (Fillmore et al., 2021). It is plausibly because those with future orientation and related coping styles are less likely to sacrifice sleep for immediate rewards brought on by impulsive or unplanned acts (Zhang & Wu, 2022).

As mentioned above, like emotion regulation difficulties, future-orientated coping would be also another underlying mechanism between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems. However, no empirical research has been conducted the two mediation paths simultaneously. Thus, we regarded emotion regulation difficulties and future-orientated coping as two parallel mediators to better understand the association between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems.

Another plausible mechanism is the potential protective effect of future orientation against anxiety, which is a risk factor for sleep problems (e.g. Johnson et al., 2006; Luo et al., 2013) as discussed earlier. Previous research has shown that the tendency and capacity to visualise the future, plan for future goals, and carry out the plan is mildly related to anxiety in the negative direction (Soltis et al., 2017; Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999). Papastamatelou et al. (2015) also found that lower levels of future orientation were reported by men with generalised anxiety disorder compared to their healthy counterparts. Therefore, future-oriented coping may exert a risk effect on sleep problems via enhancing anxiety disorder. Notably, similar to emotion regulation difficulties, previous studies did not consider both future-orientated coping and anxiety as serial mediators in the relationship between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems.

The present study

This cross-sectional study was the first to investigate the associations between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems in adults with drug abuse using an evolutionary perspective. We proposed three potential pathways underlying the association: via (i) anxiety, (ii) emotion regulation (and anxiety), as well as (iii) future-oriented coping (and anxiety) in a sample of Chinese male adults with drug abuse from a drug rehabilitation centre. The conceptual mediation model is presented in Figure 1, with the following hypotheses of this study:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Childhood maltreatment and sleep problems are positively correlated.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Childhood maltreatment is positively associated with anxiety and emotion regulation difficulties and negatively associated with future–oriented coping.

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Anxiety and emotion regulation difficulties are positively correlated, while future-oriented coping is negatively correlated, with sleep problems.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): (a) Emotion regulation difficulties and (b) future-oriented coping mediate the effect of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems.

Hypothesis 5 (H5): Anxiety mediates not only the effect of childhood maltreatment but also those of (a) emotion regulation difficulties and (b) future-oriented coping on sleep problems.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Male adults with drug abuse from a residential drug rehabilitation centre in Sichuan Province, China, were recruited to participate in a survey from November 2020 to January 2021. The participation was totally voluntary, with no monetary reward provided. The participants were briefed on the study aims and their participation rights, and then they signed a written informed consent form before filling out the questionnaire. This study was approved by the Human Subjects Review Committee of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. A total of 793 male adults with drug abuse were recruited; however, 189 questionnaires were considered to be invalid (26% of the questionnaires were excluded as their insufficient reading proficiency) or incomplete (74% of them were excluded as non-completion rates were above 20%). Therefore, data from 604 participants were used in the data analyses of this study.

Measures

Sleep problems

The validated Chinese version (Ho & Fong, 2014) of the 19-item Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; Buysse et al., 1989) was used to assess sleep quality, over the last month, in seven domains: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication use, and daytime dysfunction. Based on the recoding scheme, the item responses were scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores representing more sleep problems in the corresponding component. Except for subjective sleep quality (in which fairly or very poor ≥2), the cutoff score of each component for defining a sleep problem was ≥1 in this study. By summing the scores of all seven components, a total PSQI score (range = 0–21) can be obtained, with higher scores indicating lower overall quality of sleep. The total PSQI score distinguishes good sleepers (≤5) from poor sleepers (>5; Backhaus et al., 2002; Buysse et al., 1989).

Childhood maltreatment

The validated Chinese version (Zhao et al., 2005) of the 28-item Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF; Bernstein et al., 1998) was used to assess history of childhood maltreatment in the present study. Participants rated items, such as, ‘I was sexually abused’ on a 5-point Likert response scale (never true = 1 to very often true = 5). A higher mean score indicated a higher level of adversity and maltreatment during childhood. In this study, CTQ-SF’s α = 0.84.

Emotion regulation difficulties

This study used the validated Chinese version (Liu et al., 2018) of the 36-item Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004) for measuring emotion regulation problems. Participants rated items, such as ‘I have difficulty making sense out of my feelings’ on a 7-point Likert response scale (almost never = 1 to almost always = 5). A higher mean score represented a greater emotion regulation problem. In this study, DERS’s α = 0.84.

Anxiety

This study measured participants’ anxiety symptoms during the past week using the validated Chinese version (Cheng et al., 2002; Fydrich et al., 1992) of the 21-item Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck & Steer, 1993), on a 4-point response scale (not at all = 0 to severely = 3). A higher total score represented a higher level of anxiety symptoms, whereas the standardised cutoffs are as follows: 0–7: Minimal; 8–15: Mild; 16–25: Moderate; 26–63: Severe (Beck & Steer, 1993; Fan et al., 2016). In this study, BAI’s α = 0.88.

Future-oriented coping

This study used the 16-item Future-oriented Coping Inventory (FOCI), which was developed and validated in Chinese adults (Gan et al., 2007), to assess participants’ future-oriented coping strategies. Participants respond to items, such as, ‘I will save money to prevent suffering from poverty in my old age’, on a 5-point Likert response scale (strongly disagree = 1) and (strongly agree = 5). A higher mean score represented more self-initiated behaviours for achieving positive long-term goals and avoiding possible future negative events. In this study, FOCI’s α = 0.89.

Demographic and drug use information

Demographic items gathered information on participants’ age (years) and education (years), and marital status (married = 1, others = 0). The drug use data included the major drug type used: (methamphetamine = 1, others = 0), poly-drug use (yes = 1, no = 0), rehabilitation duration (refers to the length of time [months] our participants have been at the rehabilitation centre), and the age of first drug use (years: ≤10 = 1, 11–20 = 2, 21–30 = 3, ≥31 = 4).

Statistical analysis

Preliminary analyses (i.e. descriptive and correlational analyses) were conducted in SPSS 26. Differences by demographic subgroups (e.g. poly drug-use yes = 1, no = 0) and major drug type (methamphetamine = 1, others = 0) on major variables were also explored by correlation analyses (i.e. r = 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 signifies mild, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively; Cohen, 1988), which aimed to identify the strength and direction of the linear relationship between demographic and major variables. The hypothesised path model (Figure 1) was tested with Mplus 8.3, and all missing data, if any, were handled using the full information maximum likelihood (FIML) method. We used path analysis because it provides greater statistical power, compared to Baron and Kenny's steps, especially when testing complex mediation models with multiple mediators (Hayes & Scharkow, 2013). In our path analysis, childhood maltreatment as a distal factor was the independent variable, while emotion regulation difficulties and future-oriented coping as two developmental traits, together with anxiety (a proximal state) were three potential mediators. Sleep problem was the outcome variable, with background variables being controlled for on the outcome variable. In our hypothesised partial mediation model (Figure 1), in which the mediator(s) are expected to account for some, but not all, of the relationship between the independent and outcome variables, we tested three types of effects: (1) direct effects (i.e. those effects are not transmitted by any mediator on another variable), including those non-mediated effects from the independent variable (childhood maltreatment) to the three mediators and/or the outcome variable; (2) indirect effects (i.e. those effects of one variable on another variable via mediator(s)), including the effects from our independent variable (childhood maltreatment) on the outcome variable (sleep problems) through one or more potential mediators (e.g. emotion regulation difficulties); and (3) total effects, which in fact is the combination of direct and indirect effects of our independent variable (childhood maltreatment) on the outcome variables (sleep problems) in this study. As a sensitivity analysis, the same path model, without controlling for the background effects, was also examined and its results would be compared with the corresponding model in which background variables were controlled for. To evaluate the model fit, the following fit indices were examined (Browne & Cudeck, 1992; Byrne, 1994): X2 (p value > 0.05), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), comparative fit index (CFI ≥ 0.90), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI ≥ 0.90), and the standard root mean-square residual (SRMR < 0.08). Given that the bootstrapping has been proven to effectively control the type I error rate compared to the causal step approach and the Sobel test (Preacher et al., 2007) and to be a good method to examine multiple mediation effect (Cheung, 2007; Lau & Cheung, 2012), our mediation hypotheses were tested by bootstrapping analyses (Preacher & Hayes, 2004). The 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the indirect effects were obtained from 5000 bootstrap re-samples.

Results

Background characteristics of the sample

A total of 793 male adults with drug abuse were recruited; however, 49 (6.2%) of the questionnaires were invalid as their insufficient reading proficiency and 140 (17.7%) of them were excluded as non-completion rates were above 20%. Therefore, data from 604 participants were used in the data analyses of this study. Table 1 shows that the participants’ mean age was 36.20 (SD = 8.17), 23.3% of them were currently married, and they had, on average, approximately 9 years of education (SD = 2.18). The age of first drug use was primarily during adolescence to early adulthood (81.5%), and their average duration of stay at the rehabilitation was 13.19 months (SD = 5.75). The primary drug used was methamphetamine (77.3%), and half were poly-drug users. In this sample, 194 participants (32.0%) had sleep problems (PSQI > 5). The majority of our participants reported sleep disturbance (81.3%), daytime dysfunction (77.3%), and sleep latency (66.5%). Furthermore, 23.8% of the participants reported mild or above levels of anxiety (16.7% mild, 6.1% moderate, and 1.0% severe).

Sleep quality of the sample

Results of the analysis of participants’ sleep problems (PSQI) total score revealed that 194 participants (32.0%) had sleep problems (PSQI > 5). The majority of our participants reported sleep disturbance (e.g. waking up in the middle of the night or early morning; 81.3%), daytime dysfunction (e.g. having trouble staying awake while driving, eating meals, or engaging in social activity; 77.3%) and sleep latency (e.g. cannot fall asleep in 30 minutes; 66.5%). Almost half sample (49.2%) had relatively short sleep duration (i.e. 6–7 hours or less). A smaller but still significant proportion of the sample reported problems regarding other components. About one-fourth of the sample (19.0%) evaluated their sleep quality as fairly bad or very bad, whereas 19.6% reported low sleep efficiency (i.e. the ratio between the time a person spends asleep and the total time dedicated to sleep >85%), and 2.2% used sleep medications.

Bivariate correlates of sleep problems

Table 2 shows that H1 was supported because childhood maltreatment was positively associated with sleep problems (r = 0.11, p < .05). Moreover, childhood maltreatment was positively correlated with emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety (r = 0.30, r = 0.22, p < .01), respectively, and negatively correlated with future-oriented coping (r = −0.24, p < .01), supporting H2. Emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety were positively correlated with sleep problems (r = 0.28 and r = 0.49, respectively, p < .01), whereas future-oriented coping was negatively correlated with sleep problems (r = −0.15, p < .01), supporting H3. Moreover, demographic and/or drug use variables were not significantly correlated with sleep problems (p = .07–.44).

Testing the hypothesised model with path analysis

The hypothesised model (Figure 1) show a satisfactory fit with the current data, χ2(21) = 21.750, p = .414; χ2/df = 1.04; CFI = 0.998; TLI = 0.997; RMSEA = 0.008, 90%CI[0.000, 0.036]; SRMR = 0.030, while controlling for those background variables on sleep problems and allowing a residual covariance between the two parallel mediators (i.e. emotion regulation difficulties and future-oriented coping; West et al., 2012). As a sensitivity analysis, the hypothesised model was further examined without those background variables being controlled for. Results showed that this model was a saturated model (i.e. zero degrees of freedom; χ2 [0] = 0.00, p = 0), which always provides a perfect fit to the data (i.e. CFI = 1.00; TLI = 1.00; RMSEA = 0.00). Comparing this model with the former model with demographics controlled for, each coefficient of the corresponding path in the two models shared the same direction with similar magnitude (Figure 2).

In the hypothesised path model in which the effect of background factors on were considered, the direct effect from childhood maltreatment to emotion regulation difficulties, β = .306, future-oriented coping, β = −.248, and anxiety, β = .111, were significant (p < .05). Moreover, there were significant direct effects from emotion regulation difficulties to anxiety, β = .382, and sleep problems, β = .107, and a significant direct effect from anxiety to sleep problems, β = .471, as expected (p < .05). However, the standardised coefficient of the direct path from childhood maltreatment to sleep problem was nonsignificant (p = .458). Moreover, those coefficients of the direct paths from future-oriented coping to both anxiety and sleep problems were also nonsignificant (p = .941 and p = .664 respectively). Therefore, H4b and H5b were not supported.

The hypothesised mediation effect of emotion regulation difficulties (proposed as H4a and H5a) was further tested with the bootstrapping approach (Table 3). The results showed that the total effects and total indirect effects of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems were statistically significant, β = .106, 95%CI[0.005, 0.211] and β = .145, 95%CI[0.092, 0.202], respectively. Specifically, the indirect effects of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems through either emotion regulation difficulties, β = .033, 95%CI[0.001, 0.066], or anxiety, β = .052, 95%CI[0.007, 0.101], respectively, were statistically significant. Furthermore, the indirect effects of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems through both emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety, β = .055, 95%CI[0.035, 0.082], were statistically significant, supporting H4a and H5a.

Discussion

Our study was the first to investigate the associations between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems of adults with drug abuse from an evolutionary perspective. Consistent with both Chinese and Western studies (Bolla et al., 2008; Roncero et al., 2012; Tang et al., 2015), overall sleep problems (i.e. PSQI > 5) were prevalent among our Chinese participants with drug abuse (32.0%). With additional evidence for some psychological mechanisms underlying the positive association between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems, the present study thus provides insight into the need to properly assess for and target these sleep problems in interventions among Chinese adults with drug abuse in rehabilitation settings.

The present results also support for the long-term adverse impacts of childhood maltreatment on one’s wellbeing, by demonstrating that childhood maltreatment had indirect effects on sleep problems among adults with drug abuse via two psychological mediators (i.e. heightened anxiety and poor emotion regulation). The mediating role of anxiety is consistent with previous findings (Park et al., 2021). Based on our findings, reducing anxiety may alleviate the negative effects of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems. Therefore, cognitive behavioural therapy (e.g. imagery-based exposure; Carroll, 1998) for reducing post-traumatic anxiety and sleep problems may be incorporated into rehabilitation-based intervention programmes for drug users with histories of childhood trauma.

Consistent with LHT and previous findings (Vettese et al., 2011), our participants with childhood maltreatment were more likely to develop the traits of fast life history strategy (i.e. more emotion regulation difficulties and less future-oriented coping). However, our resultant path model revealed that emotion regulation difficulties, but not future-oriented coping, mediated the effect of childhood maltreatment on sleep problems. Such mediating effects involved not only a direct but also a serial one via increased anxiety. The link between emotion regulation difficulties and anxiety has been reported in previous studies (Daros et al., 2021), but this study was the first to demonstrate how these two acted as serial mediators in the relationship between childhood maltreatment and sleep problems.

Previous studies have supported our findings. For example, among individuals with drug abuse, childhood maltreatment is prevalent (40% to 50%; Dube et al., 2003; Santo et al., 2021). They also often use drugs due to inappropriate emotion regulation functioning (Kober, 2013). Despite considerable improvement in access to emotion regulation strategies during treatment, individuals with drug abuse may still encounter problems identifying and describing their own emotions (Khakbaz et al., 2016). Consequently, their poor emotional regulation may lead to negative consequences on their wellbeing and health (e.g. higher anxiety and poorer sleep quality in our cases). It is therefore recommended that rehabilitation-based intervention programmes integrate emotion regulation training (Cerolini & Lombardo, 2022) and foster better sleep quality in individuals with drug abuse.

Childhood maltreatment was associated with lower levels of future-oriented coping as predicted, but the latter was not associated with anxiety or sleep problems in this study. Thus, this study demonstrated that future-oriented coping did not appear to be a salient protective factor against anxiety or sleep problems of individuals with drug abuse when emotion regulation was considered. However, it is important to note that our participants were rehabilitation centre residents who received drug detoxification and rehabilitation services and whose specific and short-term goals were in the interests of timely recovery and set by the treatment programmes they were in. Thus, they were probably inclined to engage in present-oriented rather than future-oriented coping, which may be common and somehow adaptive in such settings.

Limitations

First, this study recruited a Chinese male sample from a single rehabilitation centre, and thus its findings may not be generalised to the general population of drug users. Large-scale research efforts, preferably cross-cultural studies involving both male and female samples from multiple sites, are warranted to test the replicability and generalizability of the present findings.

Second, this study focused on psychological mechanisms. But previous studies (e.g. Simon & Admon, 2023) found the correlation between childhood maltreatment and sleep-wake cycle, and suggested some physiological mechanism (e.g. depression molecular mechanisms; Mendlewicz, 2009). Hence, future research should pay more attention to the physiological mechanism.

Third, this study used a cross-sectional design, which does not allow a test for causality (including temporal line and direction among variables), although the LHT proposes some variables (e.g. childhood maltreatment and personal traits) as antecedents of others (e.g. mental and sleep problems).

Fourth, regarding the variables assessed, some measures used (e.g. childhood trauma questionnaire) were vulnerable to self-report bias. Meanwhile this study did not measure whether the sleep problems, emotion regulation and future-oriented coping formed in childhood, soon or concomitant with childhood trauma, and when they occurred in samples who experience childhood maltreatment because we used self-reported measures (participants may not recall the specific time precisely).

Last but not least, this study did not measure history of sleep, although sleep problem could occur across lifespan (e.g. our participants may have sleep problems before admission in the rehabilitation centre). Future research should consider including sleep history in the model. While some useful demographic factors (e.g. number of children and employment) and sleep characters (e.g. onset age or previous time intervals) were not included.

For the latter three limitations, longitudinal and experimental studies from childhood to adulthood, with LHT-related factors (e.g. family variables) controlled for, are recommended to verify the present findings.

Conclusion

The present results support the applicability of LHT to sleep problems to improve our understanding of how childhood maltreatment brings harmful effects on adulthood wellbeing (i.e. anxiety and sleep) via psychological mechanisms (i.e. poorer emotion regulation but not future-oriented coping) among individuals with drug abuse, who are a vulnerable population. Our findings underscore the need for both increased prevention of childhood trauma as a primary prevention strategy to reduce incidents of drug dependence and increased sensitivity towards trauma issues in treatment programmes for individuals with drug abuse. Furthermore, assessing for anxiety and promoting emotion regulation skills would be helpful components in insomnia management programmes for individuals with drug abuse, offering promising contributions to the success of these programmes by preventing relapse.
