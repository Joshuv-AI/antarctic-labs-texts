Status and influencing factors of obstetric violence: a mixed study
Authors: Shan Huang, Meihui Li, Mingyang Zhang, Wenjun Zhang, Xueyan Wang, Wenting Yang, Jing Zhang, Jinguo Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew researches have focused on obstetric violence in the Asia-Pacific region, necessitating an understanding of the current situation of obstetric violence and exploration of factors influencing its occurrence. This study aimed to investigate the current status and influencing factors of obstetric violence.MethodsWe used a modified obstetric violence questionnaire to understand the status of obstetric violence in women and the objective sampling method to select qualified interviewees for face-to-face semi-structured in-depth interviews.ResultsSeven hundred twenty-one women completed the online questionnaire, among whom 10 women participated in the in-depth interview. The current prevalence of obstetric violence was 51.6% (n = 372), with verbal violence being the most common type (82.0%, n = 305). In binary logistic regression analysis, living in countryside [OR (95% CI) = 0.592 (0.402–0.872)], private hospitals [OR (95% CI) = 0.422 (0.233–0.765)], multiparas [OR (95% CI) = 0.405 (0.273–0.601)], and having a family member accompaniment during delivery in the labor room [OR (95% CI) = 0.324 (0.177–0.59], having prenatal maternal education [OR (95% CI) = 0.583 (0.393–0.865)], and active expression of needs during childbirth [OR (95% CI) = 0.415 (0.252–0.685)] were protective factors for obstetric violence. Maternal or family members having workplace violence against medical staff during childbirth [OR (95% CI) = 5.642 (2.148–14.818)], and familiarity with obstetric violence [OR (95% CI) = 2.138 (1.360–3.360)] were risk factors for obstetric violence. Based on family support during adolescence, the risk of obstetric violence in the “Strongly supportive” group was only 0.029 times that of the “Very unsupportive” control group. Women in other age groups are at higher risk of obstetric violence than women aged ≥ 45 years. The qualitative research has analyzed five themes: needs to be strengthened humanistic care, process that needs optimization, culture of obstetric violence, needs to be strengthened human resources, and needs to be strengthened medical facilities.ConclusionThe current incidence of obstetric violence is moderately common level, multilevel measures are urgently needed. Efforts should focus on promoting the coverage of the “Internet + Prenatal Education” program in rural areas to raise maternal awareness. Concurrently, training for medical staff should be enhanced, and preventive measures should be incorporated into healthcare quality assessment systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24169-y.Keywords: Obstetric violence, Childbirth experience, Mixed study

Background

Obstetric violence (OV) is a widespread and serious public health problem and human rights violation [1]. The term “obstetric violence” first appeared in the form of “disrespect”. In 2015, the WHO (World Health Organization) defined OV as the abuse of women by health professionals and disrespect and violation of women’s dignity because of the abuse [2]. In 2020, Garcia specifically published a conceptual analysis of OV and defined it as the abuse of women engaged in fertility treatment, preconception care, pregnancy, labor, or postpartum by healthcare providers. It is also defined as any invasive maneuver or surgical procedure performed on women throughout the birthing process without their informed consent, through coercion, or in violation of their refusal [3, 4]. This definition was adopted in this study because Garcia emphasized that “invasive operation without informed consent” belongs to the category of violence, which is consistent with the mainstream trend of emphasizing “patient autonomy” in international ethical research in recent years.

Current research indicates significant regional variations in the prevalence of OV. Multiple studies report particularly high rates of OV in low- and middle-income countries. In 2022, the reported incidence was 79.3% (n = 2,105) in Chile [5] and 79.7% (n = 661) in Ethiopia [6]. Notably, an Italian study in 2022 reported a similarly high prevalence (78.4%, n = 282) [7] despite high-income settings. Methodological variations across studies (e.g., differing definitions of violence or data collection approaches) may contribute to observed fluctuations, as evidenced by Ethiopia’s 2023 study [8], which reported 77.8% incidence despite implementing stricter assessment criteria.

The OV types experienced by women vary among reports. In 2010, Bowser and Hill classified disrespectful behavior toward women into seven types [9]. The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) [10] categorizes disrespect and abuse during childbirth into seven different types. In this study, we adopted the Spanish scholars Martínez-Galiano et al.’s [11] categorization of OV, which is divided into physical, verbal, and psycho-emotional violence based on the manifestations of violence. This is because the categorization retains the core dimensions and avoids research redundancy caused by over-segmentation. Verbal violence refers to the use of insulting and discriminatory language, such as abuse, denigration, contempt, and ridicule, resulting in emotional and psychological aggression and damage to the mother. This behavior falls under the categories of moral injury, ineffective verbal expression, inappropriate verbal treatment, criticism of emotional expression, and the inability to communicate or ask questions. Psycho-emotional violence refers to the psychological damage that medical personnel can inflict on a woman in labor without speaking, such as ignoring, condescending attitudes and expressions, scornful glances, and impatient looks.

Obstetric violence affects women, families, society, health professionals, and medical institutions. For the individual woman, OV has been associated with postpartum depression and post-traumatic stress disorder [3, 12–15]. Notably, OV can lead to various complications for women, such as postpartum depression and hemorrhage [16, 17]. It can also lead to fear of childbirth [18], which can affect the willingness to have another child [3], and it can affect couples’ lives, such as permanent pain during intercourse after unnecessary episiotomies [19]. For health professionals, witnessing the trauma experienced by women can lead to compassion fatigue and secondary traumatic stress [20].

The current situation of OV and its influencing factors have not yet been systematically revealed. A domestic qualitative study [21] found that some mothers encountered different forms of violence during labor and delivery, including verbal attacks (e.g., berating and belittling) and indifference by medical staff. However, there is still a lack of empirical studies with large samples. Therefore, we hope that this study will help us understand the influencing factors of OV, and establish a localized framework of the influencing factors of OV to provide a direction for the adoption of targeted measures.

Method

Study design

This was a mixed study to understand the current situation of OV and to analyze its influencing factors.

Participants

In this study, we administered an online questionnaire to eligible women of reproductive age using convenience sampling from October 2023 to December 2024. The study principal generated a QR code for the questionnaire through Questionnaire Star. Then, the surveyors were given unified online training to make sure that they mastered the content of the questionnaire, understood the related concepts, and were aware of the important precautions to be taken. Finally, the investigator pushed the QR code to the eligible respondents, who scanned the QR code through their cell phones to read the purpose, significance, and requirements of the survey while explaining to the participants that the survey was anonymous and they had the right to withdraw from the survey at any time. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital under ethical approval number 2023-045. The study included women who (1) had given birth at least once in the past 12 months, (2) were aged ≥ 18 years and were of childbearing potential, and (3) gave birth in a hospital. We excluded women who (1) had memory or cognitive difficulties, (2) were at high risk of maternity, (3) had a history of stillbirth or whose child was a stillborn fetus, and (4) had a history of psychiatric illness. The qualitative research subjects were women who had participated in this questionnaire survey and had given birth in local city. Because our questionnaire survey was conducted at the time of the women’s postpartum reexamination, we would briefly understand that their situation at the time of delivery was through communication during the questionnaire survey. Then, we would look for the target population for the interview. If consent is obtained, contact information will be retained for subsequent appointments at the specific interview time and location.

Measures

General information questionnaires

The sociodemographic characteristics questionnaire was designed by the researchers. This section contained both demographic characteristics and obstetric characteristics. The demographic characteristics included eight items on women’s age, education, and marital status. Obstetric characteristics included 15 items, such as place of delivery, family presence during hospitalization, previous experience of giving birth, and maternal or family members engaging in workplace violence against medical staff during childbirth (verbal abuse, threats, and attacks against obstetric medical personnel at the workplace by women in labor or their family members).

Questionnaire on obstetric violence

The Obstetric Violence questionnaire used by us was based on the Obstetric Violence questionnaire designed by Martinez-Galiano et al. [11] and the NorVold Abuse Questionnaire designed by Swahnberg et al. [22], and some items were added or removed to tweak the questionnaire according to domestic culture. For example, we have refined the subcategories of verbal violence by expanding the original item “inappropriate verbal treatment” from the Martínez-Galiano questionnaire into more specific behaviors like “being scorned,” “being slandered,” “being loudly reprimanded,” and “being insulted.” Furthermore, considering the unique characteristics of China’s healthcare environment, we have supplemented the psychological-emotional violence dimension with an additional item addressing “non-dignified care through failure to provide covering during genital exposure,” which reflects a culturally specific manifestation of OV.

The questionnaire was prepared in consultation with domestic obstetrical experts and on the basis of the findings of the preliminary survey, and the test-retest reliability was 0.935. The questionnaire comprised two parts: the first part on the occurrence of OV (3 questions) and the second part on the incidence of OV (8 questions). Notably, in the third question on the occurrence of OV, in cases wherein women provided uninformed consent, women were required to select the type of uninformed consent they had given. If the woman has not experienced OV, the second part was skipped altogether.

Interview outline

The interview outline was developed based on the investigation’s purpose, a review of domestic and foreign literature, and the initial outline construction. Then, obstetrics experts were consulted to further modify the inappropriate content to form the final interview outline. The specific contents of the interview were as follows (Supplementary file 1):

Sample size

The calculation formula is N = Za².p.(1-p)/δ2, wherein N is the required sample size, the test level is 0.05, bilateral Za equals 1.96, and P is the incidence of previous OV. The incidence rate in similar studies varies greatly, ranging from 15 to 91% [11]; therefore, we considered the mean value of 53% as the incidence rate of OV in foreign countries. δ is the tolerance error, generally taken as 10% or 20% of P. In this study, we considered a δ of 10%. The sample size obtained was 341 people, and considering the 10% attrition rate of the questionnaire, a sample size of at least 379 people was needed for this study. Qualitative research confirms the sample size based on the principle of saturation of information.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0. For normally distributed data, categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Variables with statistical significance (P < 0.05) were included as independent variables in a binary logistic regression equation. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and identify predictive factors for OV. The interview content was transcribed verbatim by two team members and repeatedly checked for accuracy. The transcribed data were then summarized, categorized, and extracted using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method.

Results

General information on women

The study received responses from 721 participants, most of whom were aged 30–34 (40.9%) years. More than half of the participants had university experience (55.6%), with a significant proportion of them being freelancers (23.9%). The majority reported a per capita monthly household income in the range of 5001 yuan to 10,000 yuan (40.9%). Women from the countryside accounted for 56.2% of the respondents. Family support during adolescence was classified as “Very unsupportive,” “Unsupportive,” “Moderate,” “Relatively supportive,” and “Strongly supportive”. The overall level was moderate, with “moderate” support (53.3%) constituting more than half of the responses, and participants rarely reported a “Very unsupportive” (2.9%) experience of family support. Most births occurred in public hospitals (87.4%, n = 630). During hospitalization, almost all women had family members accompanying them (98.9%, n = 713), but the situation at the time of delivery was reversed, and 86.3% of the women did not have family members present in the delivery room. Among the 721 women, primiparas (58.0%) slightly outnumbered multiparas (42.0%). A small proportion of women experienced domestic violence during pregnancy (13.3%). During childbirth, a minority of women or their family members engaged in workplace violence against medical staff (9.2%). See Table 1 for details.

Univariate analysis of obstetric violence

Univariate analyses identified the following to be significant factors influencing OV: patient’s age, per capita monthly household income, residential area, family support during adolescence, place of delivery, childbirth experience, family accompaniment during delivery in the delivery room, experienced domestic violence during pregnancy, violence against medical staff during childbirth, prenatal education for pregnant women, familiarity with OV, active expression of needs during childbirth, time of delivery, and complications during childbirth (all, P < 0.05, Table 1).

Binary logistic regression analysis of factors influencing obstetric violence

Variables that were statistically significant (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis of variance were included in the binary logistic regression equation as independent variables with OV as the dependent variable. The model construction was valid (P < 0.05), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test showed a good fit for the model (P > 0.05). The results of the analysis revealed the following as protective factors of OV: living in the countryside [OR (95% CI) = 0.592 (0.402–0.872), P < 0.01], using private hospitals [OR (95% CI) = 0.422 (0.233–0.765), P < 0.01], being a multipara mother [OR (95% CI) = 0.405 (0.273–0.601), p < 0.01], being accompanied by a family member in the labor room during delivery [OR (95% CI) = 0.324 (0.177–0.59, P < 0.01], having prenatal maternal education [OR (95% CI) = 0.583 (0.393–0.865), P < 0.01], and active expression of needs during childbirth [OR (95% CI) = 0.415 (0.252–0 0.685), P < 0.01]. Conversely, the following were risk factors for OV: maternal or family members engaging in workplace violence against medical staff during childbirth [OR (95% CI) = 5.642 (2.148–14.818), P < 0.01] and women having knowledge of OV [OR (95% CI) = 2.138 (1.360–3.360), P < 0.01]. The better the family support during adolescence, the lower the risk of OV. The risk of OV in the “Strongly supportive” group was only 0.029 times that of the “very unsupportive” control group. Women in other age groups had a higher risk of OV compared to women aged ≥ 45 years, with the risk of OV in women aged 20–29 years being 13.74 times higher than that in women aged ≥ 45 years (Table 4).

Qualitative findings

A total of 10 women were interviewed, and five themes of causes of OV were distilled, namely (1) needs to be strengthened humanistic care (feedback from seven women), (2) process that needs optimization (feedback from two women), (3) culture of OV (feedback from three women), (4) needs to be strengthened human resources (feedback from three women), and (5) needs to be strengthened medical facilities (feedback from two women).

Theme 1: needs to be strengthened humanistic care

Humanistic care represents sincere concern and care for patients’ health, needs, personality, and dignity in the process of medical behavior of healthcare personnel. Particularly in nursing, humanistic care is an important indicator of quality nursing service. In this study, most women reported a lack of medical care in the delivery room.

In the case of sensitive operations, such as cervical examination and fundal pressure, even though the mother has informed medical personnel that the thought of undergoing these operations causes her fear and distress and has thus politely refused to consent to the operation, the medical personnel still did not take the mother’s thoughts and used all kinds of reasons to encourage her compliance.

“None of them listened to me seriously; they just did things from their point of view and didn’t respect me at all and didn’t consider my feelings.” (W01). “They ignored everything I said; I said don’t ask me and they still kept asking.” (W03).

During delivery, the mother’s heart is very fragile, but the medical staff did not show sufficient concern. Nine out of 10 women interviewed reported that the medical staff lacked sufficient emotional support during labor, and they reported that when they were in severe pain, they did not given words of comfort.

“When I didn’t get relief from the pain all over my body, I felt like I was isolated.” (W04). “It all hurt until I was in spasms, and my whole body was curled up there, cold and hot.” (W06). “Their task is how to make the laboring woman give birth to the baby quickly, and they seldom think about the feelings of the laboring woman.” (W07). “Lack of concern for the laboring woman, you see I was there for a long time and no one cared about me.” (W08). “And then I felt that the whole process when I was most helpless and in need of help, just no one paid any attention to me.” (W10).

Theme 2: process that needs optimization

Optimal process not only reduces the efficiency of the work but also causes a lot of waste in terms of human, material, financial and time. The laboring woman in W02 experienced pain and isolation for longer because of the insufficiently optimized payment system, her family members did not arrive in time, and they did not have the cash with them, consequently, the hospital could not use the analgesic device and provide accompanying labor service to the woman.

“She said I had to pay for it to work, and I was kind of pissed off because I was already in a lot of pain.” (W02).

Some women reported that the education system needed improvement.

“The department has flaws in the teaching system; it is okay for the older nurses to teach and let the newer nurses accompany them; however, they cannot let the newer nurses operate directly on their own alone while the older nurses just walk away and let it go, which ultimately results in the perineal lateral incision not being sutured properly and affects the healing process.” (W05).

Theme 3: Culture of obstetric violence

Some medical professionals believe “obstetric violence” is stigmatizing and therefore avoid mentioning it, keeping their distance from the matter. Others think that most so-called “delivery room violence” is imagined or exaggerated. Some medical staff even consider OV as a catalyst to help women deliver smoothly.

“It might be that they’ve just gotten used to it, because they don’t think about it or consider it from the perspective of the mother, like, ‘Would we feel awkward or uncomfortable in this situation?’” (W03). “It’s probably because they’re usually like this, so they don’t consider whether the mother might feel uncomfortable about it. It’s just become a habit for them.” (W04). “They don’t tell me anything; they just suddenly do things without warning.” (W09).

Theme 4: needs to be strengthened human resources

Manpower shortage and a serious imbalance in the doctor-patient and nurse-patient ratios are major problems faced by some hospitals and are the root causes of OV. In many cases, women in labor are left unattended because the medical staff is busy with other things.

“Maybe it’s really understaffed, I see they are also too busy to manage so many laboring mothers, especially at six o’clock in the morning.” (W03). “Because maybe there’s no time, to be honest, it’s too hard for you to deal with lots and lots of this kind of labor.” (W04). “They are also busy in their usual work, in addition to excessive pressure in their work life, they also need to cope with all kinds of inspections, exams, promotions and so on on top of their normal work every day.” (W08).

Theme 5: needs to be strengthened medical facilities

The discomfort caused by suboptimal medical equipment and facilities may indirectly contribute to communication barriers between patients and healthcare providers, thereby increasing the risk of OV. Specifically, the rigid design of delivery beds exacerbates lumbosacral pain (in addition to labor contractions). When pain intensity exceeds the tolerance threshold, parturients may experience stress responses like irritability and difficulty concentrating. This state of physiological distress significantly impairs effective communication with midwives, manifesting as inability to clearly articulate needs or properly comprehend medical instructions. This may subsequently trigger negative emotional reactions from healthcare staff, creating a potential catalyst for violent behavior. This mechanism has been corroborated by in-depth interviews with multiple parturients.

“I don’t think I can use my strength, I don’t think I can use my strength on that frame, and then that hand grip doesn’t seem to be so smooth, but if there is a place where the hand can use its strength, it may be a little bit better, or there is a place under the buttocks to use its strength, and then there is a place underneath the stirrups where the stirrups can be used.” (W04) “The delivery bed is really hard, and there is no place to support your waist; your whole waist just hangs there. It’s very tiring; it feels like your waist is going to break.” (W07).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the prevalence of OV is moderately high. It is lower than that in countries like Ghana (65.3%), Morocco (59.2%), and Chile (79.3%) [5, 23, 24], which may be related to differences in the timeframes used by researchers when defining violent incidents. For example, Yalley et al.’s study in Ghana included OV experienced by women between January 2020 and December 2021, whereas Cárdenas et al.’s study in Chile had a broader scope, including incidents of OV that occurred at any time. In contrast, this study only investigated OV experienced by women within the past 12 months. The observed disparities in prevalence rates may also stem from methodological and definitional differences across studies. Specifically, Yalley et al.’s mixed-methods approach (combining online and offline surveys) implemented in child immunization centers likely enhanced participants’ understanding of OV’s complex dimensions through in-person interactions, thereby increasing case reporting rates. Furthermore, their comprehensive 49-item assessment instrument encompassed broader categories of violence, including “detention of women in health facilities for their inability to pay medical bills or bring the required materials”; this category accounted for 7.7% of cases in their study but was excluded from our research due to its incompatibility with China’s universal healthcare system. These fundamental variations in both data collection methodologies and operational definitions contribute significantly to the observed prevalence differences between studies.

However, the incidence of OV is higher than that in countries like Spain (38.3%), Mexico (33.3%), Ecuador (32.8%), and Turkey (43.6%) [25–28].

Studies have shown that women aged 20–29 years and 30–34 years are the most vulnerable to OV, a finding consistent with research from other countries [1, 5, 7]. This may be related to the higher likelihood of pregnancy and childbirth within this age group. In addition, research has indicated that first-time mothers are more likely to experience OV compared to those with prior childbirth experience [11, 29], and younger mothers are predominantly first-time mothers. Due to their lack of experience, first-time mothers may struggle to fully understand the instructions of doctors and midwives during delivery, leading to communication barriers and potentially triggering violent language or behavior from medical staff. This study’s multivariate analysis revealed that compared to being a first-time mother, having previous childbirth experience is a protective factor against OV.

Women living in urban areas are more likely to experience OV, which is a finding consistent with previous studies [30, 31]. This difference mainly stems from the uneven distribution of education levels and medical resources. Urban women are more able to accurately recognize and actively report OV because of their higher levels of education and more frequent access to medical resources, as well as their significantly higher awareness of their rights and expectations of the quality of medical services. In contrast, rural women, who have limited access to medical resources and less awareness of their rights, are significantly less sensitive to OV and less willing to report it. The overload of urban healthcare workers increased the risk of violent conflicts due to service attitude problems, ultimately leading to a higher rate of reported OV in urban areas. Ravaldi et al. [32] also found the incidence of OV to be associated with women’s higher perception of it. However, this does not mean that women should avoid learning about OV; low reporting is not the same as low incidence and mainly results from reporting bias and normalization of negative experiences among rural women; conversely, urban women tend to report more incidents of OV due to greater awareness of the risks of the phenomenon. A low reported incidence does not mean it does not occur; it may simply go unreported in surveys. Therefore, relevant training should be conducted in rural areas to help rural women understand OV, prevent and identify it, and eliminate hidden, unreported instances of OV.

This study found that women with a “strongly supportive” home environment were less likely to experience OV than women who experienced a “very unsupportive” home environment, indicating that strong family support is a protective factor against OV. Ribeiro et al. [33] also found that women who were exposed to unsupportive and helpless family environments during adolescence were more prone to experiencing OV. At the core of adolescents’ physical and mental development is a well-functioning family system which directly promotes the psychological health and behavioral performance of its members and significantly reduces negative emotions [34]. Related studies have shown that effective family care not only enhances the quality of life and social adaptability of anxious people [35] but also helps establish healthy lifestyles and harmonious family relationships, which in turn maintain a good psychological state and reduce the risk of negative emotions. In addition, a family care intervention study for anxious adolescents showed that adolescents showed significant enhancement in the dimensions of emotional connection, cooperative awareness, environmental adaptation, and psychological growth after the systematic intervention [36]. This finding confirms that family care can effectively strengthen adolescents’ communication and understanding skills and promote their emotional expression. This positive effect may carry over to the adult stage of labor and delivery, resulting in the development of a more positive cognitive and coping model of labor and delivery, with which the adult can not only deeply understand the complexity of the natural process of labor and delivery but also cope with the challenges of labor and delivery with a more relaxed mindset, thus potentially indirectly reducing the incidence of OV. Research by Chinese scholar Han Xiao [37] revealed that children from single-parent families, if not provided with proper guidance and emotional support, such as familial and friendship bonds during adolescence, may gradually develop traits like timidity, suspicion, and distrust of others. Women with such personalities are more likely to face communication barriers with medical staff, thus increasing their risk of experiencing OV. Therefore, parents should strengthen family support for girls during their adolescent years to foster positive and healthy personalities, helping to prevent OV.

Women giving birth in public hospitals experience more severe OV than those giving birth in private hospitals, a finding consistent with previous studies [1, 5, 38]. This may be related to the difference in the nature of public and private hospitals. Private hospitals, being profit-driven, prioritize attracting more patients by providing high-quality, patient-centered medical services that cater to the diverse needs of mothers. In addition, private hospitals generally have a better staff-to-patient ratio than public hospitals. In contrast, public hospitals often face a high patient volume with relatively limited staffing, leading to insufficient infrastructure, heavy workloads, and poor communication. These factors can contribute to disrespectful or violent behaviors in practice [39]. During interviews, many mothers also mentioned that staff shortages are one of the reasons for violent behaviors by medical personnel and expressed hope that healthcare institutions would increase staffing to alleviate the pressure on medical staff. In China’s current healthcare system, most pregnant women continue to choose public hospitals for delivery. This is because most private hospitals are not included in the designated health insurance coverage or have significantly lower reimbursement rates and higher costs than public hospitals. This imbalance in healthcare choices not only further increases the load and pressure on public hospitals but also affects the quality of their services. To address this uneven distribution of healthcare resources and enhance accessibility and equity, it is recommended that the government adopt a multi-pronged strategy. This strategy would involve the inclusion of private obstetrics institutions that meet the quality-of-service standards into the scope of designated healthcare insurance to divert patients from public hospitals. Furthermore, it emphasizes focusing on strengthening the capacity of obstetrics services in primary healthcare institutions by systematic optimization of the allocation of healthcare resources.

The present study showed that women who were not accompanied by their families in the labor and delivery room were more likely to experience OV, which is consistent with the findings of Tunçalp et al. [40]. Wahdan et al. [41] reported that mothers without birth partners were more likely to report abuse and non-consensual episiotomy compared to those with birth partners. Singh et al. [42] also noted that having a birth partner reduced the chances of abuse by the mother. Accompanied labor is a back-to-nature way of giving birth; it has become very popular in many countries today and is now gradually emerging in our country. It is adopted with a guided model, which allows family members of the mother to walk into the delivery room and improve the quality of obstetric services. Birth companions are also encouraged in the World Health Organization’s Safe Childbirth Checklist to enhance the labor experience and safety of mothers [43]. Despite the significant benefits of the paternity system, there are some obstacles to its popularization in China. First, there are hardware limitations as family members need a separate space to protect the privacy of the mother; however, some public hospitals are unable to provide enough exclusive areas for paternity services due to space constraints, thus preventing the widespread introduction of paternity services. Second, there are human resource pressures. The integrated paternity service in the paternity model needs to follow the service standard of “one midwife for each parturient”. However, a shortage of nurses in the obstetric departments of some hospitals, which makes the implementation of the integrated paternity service further increase the burden of human resources. Hu et al. [44] conducted a nationwide survey of 1,213 hospitals in China, revealing that many institutions still fail to meet the nationally mandated standard of a 1:3 ratio between delivery beds and midwives [45]. Dou et al. [46] further emphasized that this midwife shortage has significantly hindered the widespread implementation of midwifery clinics and doula-assisted delivery services across the country. To address these challenges, adopting a progressive improvement plan is recommended. In terms of hardware, we can optimize the spatial layout of the existing delivery room through a flexible partition design. This will create a relatively independent environment for the accompaniment of labor while promoting the simple mode of accompaniment with the participation of family members, which can not only avoid the additional expenses of hiring extra manpower in midwives but also help family members to complete the basic care work, thus effectively alleviating the workload of medical staff.

It was concluded in this study that women who had not attended prenatal maternal education programs were more likely to experience OV. Lulu Kong [47] reported that prenatal education promotes pregnant women and their families to consciously adopt relatively healthy behaviors, adequately prepare for labor, speed up the labor process, and reduce complications. Therefore, more prenatal education—both online and offline—can be conducted to reduce OV. In addition, during childbirth, women are advised to take the initiative to express their needs so that medical personnel can more easily attend to them and obtain timely assistance in solving problems encountered during labor to avoid “neglect” and other undesirable behaviors.

Studies have shown that women whose mothers or family members have engaged in workplace violence against medical staff are more likely to experience OV. More interestingly, the relationship between the two was also explored in the author’s previous study, wherein it was identified that nearly half of the OV incidents experienced by mothers during hospitalization were directly related to obstetric nurses having experienced workplace violence from the mother’s direct relatives [48]. This shows that both OV and obstetric nurses’ workplace violence are associated with each other. Some studies have also found that behaviors of disrespect and insults to the midwife’s profession can lead to OV [49, 50]. Therefore, building a good doctor-patient and nurse-patient relationship is not only the basis for ensuring access to quality healthcare but also for preventing the occurrence of OV.

From the qualitative study, we found the lack of humanization to be closely linked to the OV experienced by women. The high prevalence of verbal and psycho-emotional violence shown in the quantitative data is highly consistent with this theme in the qualitative study. For example, the inappropriate words and behaviors of medical staff and lack of patience in communication mentioned by mothers during the interviews were forms of verbal violence in the quantitative data, while the narratives of disregarded pain and exposure to privacy aptly corroborated the type of psycho-emotional violence. Alnabilsy et al. [51] stated that women’s adverse birth experiences were mainly related to the lack of good communication between healthcare professionals about the feelings and needs of women in labor. The women interviewed in Henrique et al.’s [52] study also mentioned the lack of help and support from healthcare professionals when experiencing pain in labor and stated that healthcare professionals were impatient and did not care about whether or not the woman was in pain. Therefore, to infuse humanistic action into every aspect of the work, effective training in the humanistic system should be further strengthened. Unreasonable system processes are also relatively insidious contributors to OV. When the system process is defective, it can easily cause medical disputes and bring adverse effects to patients [53]. In this study, many mothers and their families gave feedback in interviews that several systems in the obstetrics department were defective and irrationally formulated, which caused them a lot of inconvenience, and some mothers experienced even more pain and suffering because of problems with the payments system and the education system. Therefore, managers of the department should listen more to the feedback from the mothers, eliminate or minimize unreasonable parts of the process, and optimize the system. The shortage of human resources is one of the main issues in healthcare industry, particularly in the nursing sector, which faces high work pressure and a challenging medical environment [54]. Mena-Tudela [26] also suggests that healthcare administrators should strive to eliminate factors contributing to OV, such as staff shortages, inadequate infrastructure, or stressful working conditions. Therefore, support can be provided by increasing manpower and fostering team building, among other measures, to improve the working conditions and professional environment of medical staff. This would help alleviate their stress and regulate negative emotional states, thus fostering harmonious doctor-patient and nurse-patient relationships.

In the face of OV, some healthcare professionals not only fail to take it seriously but also feel that it is a part of the job. The culture of acceptability of OV is a major driver of OV, normalizing it and making healthcare workers ignore it [23]. The phrase “it’s all for your own good” is often used in labor and delivery rooms, and some midwives do not see OV as abuse but as a strategy that can help mothers have a successful delivery, even some mothers believe that the use of violence by the midwife can help them deliver their babies and have a positive effect. This view was corroborated in a meta-analysis of OV by Hakimi et al. [55]. The culture of normalization of OV will not only make medical staff ignore its existence but also have a synergistic effect, making other colleagues follow suit. Therefore, there is a need for the government and medical schools to educate medical professionals and women’s groups about OV to eliminate the culture of normalization of OV.

In this study, we found through quantitative research that maternal factors (e.g., lack of prenatal education) increase the risk of OV. Qualitative research further revealed that healthcare system issues (e.g., healthcare workers’ perceptions of OV, the hospital system, manpower shortages, and equipment deficiencies) are also important contributors to OV. The mixed study comprehensively identified the influencing factors and provided an in-depth understanding of how these factors specifically contribute to OV occurrence, compensating for the limitations of a single approach.

Limitations

In terms of sample size, although this study covers a wide range of data, the distribution across different hospital levels remains unclear. Future research could benefit from adopting a combination of stratified sampling methods to conduct multicenter studies across a wider range of provinces and regions, thereby expanding the sample size to address data limitations.

Conclusions

In this study, by assessing the prevalence and influencing factors of OV, we reveal that women in labor require increased attention to ensure they do not experience OV. In addition, the problem should be comprehensively recognized and addressed at multiple levels, and measures should be taken from multiple perspectives, including women, families, and medical personnel, to improve the maternal experience during childbirth through education, training, supervision, and systems to reduce the incidence of OV. In the future, researchers can apply artificial intelligence to this field to establish a real-time identification and early warning system for OV and carry out cost-benefit analyses of different intervention modes. This will provide an evidence-based basis for the construction of a precise prevention and control system for OV that is specifically tailored for China.
