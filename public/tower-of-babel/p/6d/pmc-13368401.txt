Periprosthetic fractures after hip and knee arthroplasty in France
Authors: Bernard de Geofroy, Julie Bulsei, Pierre-Alban Bouché, Lolita Micicoi, Eric Fontas, Jean-François Gonzalez, Louis Dagneaux, Grégoire Micicoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to provide a nationwide assessment of the incidence, risk factors, morbidity, and mortality of hip and knee periprosthetic fractures in France over the past decade, and to project their future burden up to 2070 under different epidemiological scenarios.MethodsThis nationwide retrospective study used the French National Health Data System to identify all patients who underwent reoperation for hip or knee periprosthetic fracture in France between January 2015 and December 2024. Fracture episodes occurring within three months of each other were considered a single event. Annual incidence rates were calculated per 100,000 inhabitants, and cumulative incidences of complications and mortality were assessed at three months and one year. Long-term projections were generated for 2030 to 2070 using two scenarios: a static model assuming stable incidence, and a dynamic model extrapolating the 2015 to 2024 linear trend adjusted for demographic forecasts.ResultsOver the ten-year period, 15,029 periprosthetic fractures were identified. The mean age was 81 years (SD 11) and 70% (10,571) of patients were female. The incidence doubled from 1.73 to 3.94 per 100,000 inhabitants between 2015 and 2024. Mortality reached 8% at three months and 16% at one year, primarily associated with older age (HR 1.1) and higher comorbidity burden (HR 1.2). Postoperative complications occurred in 16% of patients within three months, including pressure ulcers, procedure-related complications, and cardiovascular events. Projections indicated a 33% and 248% increase in periprosthetic fractures by 2070 under the static and dynamic incidence scenarios, respectively.ConclusionThe incidence of periprosthetic fractures in France has risen steadily and is expected to increase substantially in the coming decades, driven by demographic ageing. Given their high morbidity and mortality, optimizing perioperative and post-fracture care, along with implementing measures to prevent both fractures and related complications, should be considered a public health priority.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):934–943.Keywords: Periprosthetic fracture, Hip arthroplasty, Knee arthroplasty, Incidence, Epidemiology, Projection, Mortality, Morbidity, PMSI, periprosthetic fractures, hip and knee arthroplasties, hip and knee arthroplasties, Hip, Knee, morbidity, comorbidities, revision surgeries, postoperative complications, arthroplasty

Introduction

Periprosthetic fractures (PPFs) following hip and knee arthroplasties represent one of the most frequent and severe complications, coming with increased morbidity, risk for functional impairment, and mortality.1 Their rising incidence mimics the growing number of hip and knee arthroplasties performed in ageing populations.2,3 Recent registry data from North America and Europe report similar trends, highlighting the rising burden linked to arthroplasty,4,5 putting at risk patient-care management strategies and hospital resource allocation.6

National Hospital Discharge Databases from France have provided robust insights following hip7 and knee arthroplasty,8 while comparable trauma-related epidemiological data have been reported using national hospital registries in other European countries.9 These databases are now considered reliable sources for large-scale epidemiological studies and long-term projections. As such, comprehensive trends and projected numbers are of primary importance for each national health system, particularly in anticipating increasing needs and resources, but also in targeting risk factors for PPFs around the hip and the knee to mitigate the consequences.

As such, the current study aimed to provide a comprehensive nationwide overview of the incidence, risk factors, morbidity, and mortality of periprosthetic hip and knee fractures in France over a ten-year period, and projected numbers of PPFs in 2030 and 2070 under different demographic and epidemiological scenarios. Our objectives were to quantify national trends in the incidence of hip and knee PPFs in France between 2015 and 2024; assess short- and mid-term mortality rates and identify associated risk factors; characterize postoperative complications and determinants of morbidity; and project the future burden of PPFs up to 2070.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective nationwide cohort study was conducted using data from the French National Health Data System (Système National des Données de Santé (SNDS)), which covers approximately 99% of the French population. It provides comprehensive information on individual reimbursed healthcare consumption, including outpatient care (medical consultations, pharmacy, laboratory tests, imaging) and hospital care across both public and private sectors via the French Hospital Discharge Database (Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d’Information (PMSI)), which contains standardized information on hospital stays, including patient demographic details, diagnoses coded using the ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases of the World Health Organization, Tenth Revision),10 and surgical procedures coded with the French Common Classification of Medical Procedures (Classification Commune des Actes Médicaux (CCAM)).11

Each individual was identified by a unique encrypted number, allowing longitudinal tracking of hospitalizations, subsequent surgical procedures, and vital status. National demographic data from INSEE (French National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies) were used to calculate annual incidence rates and generate long-term projections. Due to the anonymized nature of the data, patient consent was not required for this study.

Research teams within university hospitals have permanent access to the SNDS, in accordance with Decree No. 2016-1871 of 26 December 2016 on the processing of personal data within the framework of the National Health Data System. Our study has been registered in the internal registry of the Nice University Hospital Centre under reference number IRB00014528_2025_43, and complies with all applicable regulatory requirements.

Population

All patients aged 20 years or older hospitalized in France between January 2015 and December 2024 were screened for inclusion (Figure 1). Eligible cases were identified based on a principal diagnosis coded as ICD-10 M966 (bone fracture following insertion of an implant, joint prosthesis, or osteosynthesis plate), in association with either ICD-10 Z966 (presence of orthopaedic joint implants) or T840 (mechanical complication of an internal joint prosthesis). To specifically isolate hip and knee PPFs, the CCAM procedure codes in the Supplementary Material Appendix 1) were retained. Information regarding the type and laterality of the initial arthroplasty could not be retrieved from the PMSI database, as many index procedures were performed decades earlier and were not systematically recorded. Consequently, fracture attribution relied on the anatomical location of the surgical procedure performed during the index hospitalization, which is accurately captured by CCAM codes but does not allow confirmation of prosthesis side concordance. This assumption is justified because PMSI procedure codes accurately reflect the site of reoperation, and the operated segment (hip or knee) is highly likely to correspond to the prosthetic joint involved in the fracture. This approach allows reliable identification of surgically treated PPFs at the population level but does not permit implant-specific risk estimation. Each hospitalization meeting these criteria was considered a distinct fracture event, identified by a composite key (patient ID plus hospitalization date). When two fractures occurred within three months, they were considered part of the same clinical episode. This approach does not introduce a competing risk, as grouping multiple fractures into a single episode does not preclude the occurrence or the ascertainment of our outcomes of interest (mortality or morbidity).

Incidences

The primary outcome was the annual incidence of hip and knee PPFs in France between 2015 and 2024. Annual incidence rates were calculated per 100,000 inhabitants, using population estimates from the INSEE for each corresponding year. Trends over time were analyzed globally and stratified by age group, sex, and anatomical site (hip/knee – acetabulum/femur/tibia). Regarding the descriptive analysis of fracture localizations, ‘hip’ refers to surgical procedures involving the femoral stem and/or the acetabulum; ‘femur’ corresponds to involvement of the metaphysis or diaphysis without articular extension; ‘knee’ refers to surgical procedures involving the knee joint, including revision arthroplasty; and ‘tibia’ refers to involvement of the metaphysis or diaphysis without articular extension. Regarding the surgical procedures, they were classified as ‘osteosynthesis’ or ‘revision’ to best reflect the surgical interventions coded. Incidence evolution was assessed using a linear regression hypothesis, excluding the years 2020 to 2021 to minimize bias related to the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on surgical activity.

Morbidity and mortality

Secondary outcomes included postoperative morbidity and mortality at three months and one year following reintervention for PPF. Mortality was collected from the health insurance national database, including the full exhaustivity of death certifications in France. The main causes of morbidity were identified through secondary diagnostic codes recorded during readmissions within three months after the index hospitalization. The ICD-10 codes retained for three-month morbidity are reported in the Supplementary Material Appendix 2). To ensure consistency and avoid double counting, only the first PPF per patient was retained.

Projection

Long-term projections were developed to estimate the future burden of PPFs in France by decade from 2030 up to 2070, based on two complementary approaches. The first approach, based on a static hypothesis, assumes that the incidence rate observed in 2024 remains stable over time. In this case, variations in the number of fractures are driven only by demographic changes, particularly population ageing and growth, with age-specific distributions derived from INSEE forecasts. The second approach, based on a dynamic hypothesis, incorporates a linear trend observed in the PMSI databases between 2015 and 2024 (excluding 2020 to 2021 due to COVID-19 disruptions), reflecting a gradual increase in incidence rates. This trend is extended into the future and combined with demographic projections, as in the first approach, to estimate the expected number of fractures. Together, these approaches provide distinct yet complementary projections, helping to anticipate future healthcare needs.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide software v. 7.1 (SAS Institute, Inc., USA). Continuous variables are reported as means with SDs, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to estimated mortality and morbidity at three months and one year. Independent predictors of mortality were identified through multivariate Cox regression models, incorporating age, sex, body weight status, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),12 and the occurrence of new fractures. The CCI is a validated composite score that estimates the burden of comorbidities by assigning weighted points to 17 chronic conditions (cardiac disease, diabetes, malignancy, renal failure), with higher scores reflecting greater severity and increased mortality risk.13 Temporal trends from 2015 to 2024 were assessed with linear regression stratified by age group. Finally, projections up to the year 2070 were generated by combining observed trends with national demographic forecasts to generate two scenarios: a static hypothesis (based on 2024 rates) and a dynamic hypothesis (extrapolated linear trend). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Incidence

Between 2015 and 2024, a total of 15,029 PPFs in 14,860 patients were recorded in France. Women accounted for 70% of cases, and the mean age was 81 years (SD 11) (Table I). There were 161 patients (1.1%) who experienced two distinct fractures and four patients (0.03%) who experienced three distinct fractures. A total of 115 fractures occurred less than three months after the previous one and were therefore removed. As such, the annual incidence of PPFs has shown a relative increase of more than 125% over the ten-year period, ranging from 1.73 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2015 to 3.94 per 100,000 in 2024, respectively (Figure 2, Table II). In all, 275 patients (1.8%) sustained two distinct PPFs, and nine patients experienced three or more during follow-up. The interval between two successive fractures was most frequently less than three months (40.5%), as shown in Table III. The most frequent procedures included open reduction and internal fixation of femoral shaft fractures (NBCA007, 24.6%), and revision or reconstruction of total hip arthroplasty components associated with osteosynthesis (NEKA005, 14.4%; NEKA006, 9.6%). When stratified by anatomical site, femur and hip largely predominated. An isolated femoral location accounted for 49.0% of all cases (7,364/15,029), while combined hip and femur involvement represented 36.2% (5,437/15,029). An isolated hip location was less frequent (8.7%, 1,312/15,029). Altogether, hip-related PPFs (hip and/or femur) represented 94% of all cases. In comparison, a knee-related location accounted for 4.8% (715/15,029) and tibial or multisegment combinations for 1.3% (201/15,029). Among all PPFs, osteosynthesis procedures were the most common (7,668/15,029; 51.0%), followed by combined revision and osteosynthesis (5,493/15,029; 36.5%). Isolated revision accounted for 9.5% (1,430/15,029).

Morbidity and mortality

Among the 12,816 patients analyzed, the mortality rate following a PPF was 8.0% (n = 1,019) at three months and 16.1% (n = 2,067) at one year. At three months, the mean follow-up duration was 2.9 months (SD 0.5), with a mean follow-up period until death of 1.3 months (SD 0.9). The Kaplan–Meier estimated survival probability was 92% at three months. At one year, the mean follow-up duration was 10.9 months (SD 3.3), and the mean time to death was 4.1 months (SD 3.5), with an overall survival probability of 84% (Table IV).

Multivariate Cox analysis identified older age and comorbidity severity (CCI) as independent predictors of mortality (Table V). Each additional year of age increased the three-month risk of death by 10% (hazard ratio (HR) 1.09; p < 0.001) and the one-year risk by 8.5% (HR 1.08; p < 0.001). The CCI was also strongly associated with mortality at both three months (HR 1.15; p < 0.001) and one year (HR 1.18; p < 0.001). Female sex appeared protective (p < 0.001), while obesity/overweight was not significantly associated with mortality. The occurrence of new fractures was associated with a lower risk of one-year death (p = 0.002).

Regarding morbidity, 2,079 patients (16.2%) experienced at least one complication within the three-month period after the PPF (Figure 2, Appendix 1). The most frequent were pressure ulcers (16.9%, n = 351), procedure-related complications (15.6%, n = 324) and cardiac failure (8.2%, n = 170). The mean follow-up until a complication occurred was 1.2 months (SD 0.8), with an overall morbidity-free survival of 83% at three months (Figure 3 and Figure 4). Between six and 12 months, 112 patients (0.9%) presented a nonunion or malunion of the fracture, predominantly affecting the pelvic or femoral region (57.6%; coded M8415).

Projections

Considering the static incidence hypothesis, the number of fractures would increase solely as a function of population growth and ageing, reaching 3,035 cases by 2050 (+27% compared with 2024) and 3,215 cases by 2070 (+63%). When considering the dynamic evolution hypothesis, the incidence would continue to rise over the coming decades, with a doubling of annual cases by 2050 (+106%), and up to 11,483 cases by 2070 (+244%), corresponding to an overall threefold increase compared with 2024 (Table VI).

Discussion

This nationwide study identified 15,029 hip and knee PPFs between 2015 and 2024. The incidence more than doubled over the decade (1.73 to 3.94 per 100,000 inhabitants). Patients were elderly (mean age 81 years) and predominantly female (70%). Outcomes confirmed the severity of these fractures, with 8% mortality at three months and 16% at one year, largely driven by age and comorbidity. Early morbidity affected 16% of patients. Projections suggest a continued rise, reaching up to 11,483 cases by 2070 under the dynamic scenario. These results support the hypothesis that PPFs are increasing and will continue to do so due to population ageing and growing arthroplasty rates.

The results observed in our study are consistent with those reported in recent international literature. The incidence of PPFs after hip and knee arthroplasty ranges from 0.3% to 5.5%, with higher rates in revision surgeries and elderly populations.2,9 National and international registry data show a steady increase in PPFs, with projections indicating a continued rise over the next decades as arthroplasty procedures become more common; for example, periprosthetic hip fractures are predicted to rise by over 200% from 2016 to 2032 in the USA, and similar trends are expected in Europe.14-17 PPFs are associated with high morbidity, mortality (up to 15% one year post-surgery), and substantial healthcare costs.18,19 A Finnish registry reported a cumulative mortality rate after proximal PPF of 8.2% at one year and 47.3% at ten years, and after distal PPF of 14.8% at one year and 67.8% at ten years.20 Major risk factors include advanced age, female sex, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, revision arthroplasty, uncemented stems, and comorbidities such as diabetes.21-23 A single-centre German study on periprosthetic proximal femur fractures reported results consistent with our series: risk factors for mortality included older age, male sex, dementia, cardiac disease, diabetes, and malignancy, whereas a higher preoperative haemoglobin level was identified as a protective factor. The time to surgery (cut-off: 68 hours) did not influence survival.24 Most cases require complex surgical management, often revision arthroplasty or internal fixation.25-28 Among our results, some findings deserve particular attention. The occurrence of a new fracture as a protective factor for one-year mortality is most likely explained by several biases, including survivor selection bias and the better physiological profile of more active patients who can sustain another fracture, since no known biological mechanism can account for this association. The protective effect of female sex may be explained by the generally higher life expectancy and lower prevalence of comorbidities among women compared with men in this age group. This finding is consistently reported in the literature.29

The continuous increase in PPFs observed in this study reflects a growing burden on the healthcare system. The number of cases doubled between 2015 and 2024 and is expected to triple by 2070, following trends reported in the USA, Scandinavia, and the UK.14-16 This rise will lead to higher healthcare costs related to the complexity and volume of revision surgeries, longer hospital stays, and greater postoperative rehabilitation needs.18,19 Anticipatory measures are essential, including surgeon training, optimization of care pathways, and the establishment of dedicated national registries. Preventive strategies should combine osteoporosis screening, fall-risk assessment, and implant-preserving surgical techniques, as previously recommended in the literature.21,22 Strengthening multidisciplinary management involving geriatric and rehabilitation teams could also help reduce morbidity and mortality. The projection models developed in this study followed a dual-scenario approach similar to that described by Erivan et al7 for knee surgery in France, distinguishing a constant scenario reflecting demographic growth alone and a continuous evolution scenario accounting for ongoing epidemiological and surgical trends. Although based on linear modelling and subject to uncertainties related to future technological or healthcare policy changes, these projections provide valuable insights to guide public health planning, surgical workforce organization, and hospital investment strategies over the coming decades.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design and reliance on administrative data. Case identification was based on PMSI coding and may therefore have resulted in misclassification,30 despite the use of specific ICD-10 diagnostic codes combined with anatomically targeted CCAM procedure codes. Information regarding the type and laterality of the index arthroplasty was not consistently available, limiting implant-specific analyses and requiring cautious interpretation of hip- versus knee-related fracture distributions. In addition, the absence of detailed clinical data (fracture patterns, implant characteristics, bone quality, or surgical timing) restricted subgroup analyses. Postoperative morbidity was limited to complications requiring hospital readmission, potentially underestimating less severe events managed in outpatient settings. Finally, cases from the most recent year lacked complete follow-up, and long-term projections were based on linear models that may not fully capture future changes in surgical practice, implant technology, or healthcare organization. These projections should be interpreted as scenario-based estimates intended to inform public health planning, rather than as precise forecasts of future incidence.

Ideally, projections would incorporate expected trends in arthroplasty volume. However, robust long-term national forecasts stratified by procedure type were not available. Despite these limitations, the SNDS remains a robust and internationally recognized data source. It has supported numerous high-impact studies published in leading peer-reviewed journals.31,32 One of its key strengths lies in its near-complete coverage of the French population, achieved through the integration of national health insurance data. This comprehensive scope enables large-scale epidemiological research with substantial statistical power and minimal selection bias.

The incidence of hip and knee PPFs in France has doubled over the past decade and is projected to increase by up to 250% by 2070 according to a dynamic incidence hypothesis, reflecting the ageing population and increasing arthroplasty rates. Beyond its growing epidemiological burden, PPF remains associated with a high risk of morbidity and mortality, particularly among patients with pre-existing comorbidities or frailty. These findings emphasize the urgent need for preventive strategies, perioperative optimization, and strategic planning of healthcare resources to meet the expected increase in surgical demand.

Take home message

- This study highlights the growing clinical burden of periprosthetic hip and knee fractures, which have doubled in incidence over the past decade and are associated with substantial early morbidity and mortality.

- By identifying age and comorbidity burden as major prognostic factors, these findings support the need for optimized perioperative pathways, multidisciplinary geriatric care, and targeted prevention strategies.

- The projected rise in cases up to 2070 further emphasizes the importance of anticipating surgical capacity, rehabilitation needs, and healthcare resource allocation.
