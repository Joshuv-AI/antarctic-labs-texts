Disease phenotype and management of axial psoriatic arthritis in Japan compared with other regions, particularly other Asian countries: results of the ASAS-PerSpA study
Authors: Haruki Sawada, Mitsumasa Kishimoto, Kurisu Tada, Gautam A. Deshpande, Daiki Kobayashi, Masato Okada, Diego Benavent, Chamaida Plasencia-Rodriguez, Victoria Navarro-Compán, Clementina López-Medina, Anna Molto, Maxime Dougados, Naoto Tamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluated the disease phenotype and treatment of axial psoriatic arthritis among patients from Japan compared with those from different geographic regions.MethodsData from the ASAS-PerSpA study were analyzed. Patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) with axial involvement, according to a rheumatologist´s judgment, were included. Patients were further categorized by four geographic regions: Europe/North America, Latin America, Middle East/North Africa, and Asia, split into Japan and other Asian countries. Disease and patient characteristics, disease activity, function, and treatment were compared by region.ResultsOf the 4,465 patients with SpA, 1,033 (23%) were diagnosed with PsA by their rheumatologist. Among those with PsA, 367 (35.5%) had axial involvement (axPsA). Disease activity and function ranges were 4.1–5.4 for BASDAI, 2.5–3.2 for ASDAS, and 3.0–4.7 for BASFI, by regions. In Japan, disease activity and function were relatively lower, indicated by a mean BASDAI of 3.5 (SD 2.4), ASDAS of 2.2 (SD 1.0), and BASFI of 1.6 (SD 2.3). These indexes were also significantly lower than those in other Asian countries, with scores of 4.8 (SD 3.0), 2.2 (SD 2.4), and 3.2 (SD 1.5) respectively. All regions showed variations in the use of csDMARDs and bDMARDs, the utilization rate of csDMARDs was significantly lower in Japan than in other Asian countries (51.4% vs. 78.1%, p = 0.02).ConclusionPatients with axPsA in Japan showed relatively lower disease activity and function than those from different geographic regions, especially in other Asian countries with less frequent csDMARD use.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory musculoskeletal disease that falls under the umbrella of the spondyloarthritides (SpA). PsA is closely associated with psoriasis, which is itself associated with distinctive inflammatory features such as peripheral arthritis, dactylitis, and enthesitis [1]. Axial involvement, considered one of the clinical features of PsA, was first recognized in 1973 [2]. The existence of axial involvement in PsA (axPsA) has been the subject of some debate. SpA is classically categorized into axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) and peripheral spondyloarthritis (pSpA) based on the primary sites of symptom manifestation. However, the classification can become challenging in cases where axial involvement occurs in pSpA, such as concurrent psoriasis in axSpA, or when axial manifestations are present in PsA, which is typically associated with peripheral involvement. Despite both being forms of SpA, treatment recommendations differ.While interleukin (IL)-23 inhibitors are included in the options for PsA in terms of biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), they are not recommended for axial symptoms [3, 4]. Recently, an ancillary analysis of the ASAS-PerSpA study database, which encompassed various geographic regions, revealed differences between axPsA and axSpA and suggested further differences between PsA with and without axial involvement [5]. Based on these studies, these conditions must be properly characterized for appropriate treatment; however, a definitive method for distinguishing them has not currently been established, and this remains a topic of discussion among Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) and Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) [6].

Psoriasis is considered relatively rare in Japan, with a reported prevalence of 0.1%, which is remarkably lower than that in Western countries with a prevalence of 2% [7, 8]. Based on 2002–2014 data, the mean prevalence of PsA among patients with psoriasis in Japan has increased from 1% to 14.3%, likely due to increased rates of diagnosis stemming from greater awareness [9]. However, the prevalence remains lower than that in Caucasian populations, suggesting that continued underdiagnosis is possible and efforts for greater awareness are warranted [9, 10]. Furthermore, the prevalence of HLA-B27 among the general population in Japan is extremely low at 0.3% in comparison to 10% in some other regions, although this has been associated with axial manifestation in patients with PsA. It is even lower than that in other Asian countries at 2%–9% [11, 12].

To date, there has been little in-depth investigation concerning regional differences in axPsA. Given that axPsA can present differently and may be treated differently across regions, understanding these variations is crucial for informing region-specific diagnostic strategies and therapeutic decisions. In particular, the Japanese population, with its unique genetic and epidemiologic background, offers an opportunity to better understand the phenotype and management of axPsA in a low-prevalence, under-recognized setting. This study utilized global data from the ASAS-PerSpA study cohort to investigate regional differences in axPsA [13]. By comparing disease characteristics and treatment patterns across geographic regions, with a particular focus on Japan, we aimed to clarify how axPsA is managed in different healthcare contexts, to identify gaps in care, and to inform international consensus on diagnosis and treatment. Our study addresses an important gap in the literature by providing a comparative analysis of real-world axPsA management across diverse regions.

Materials & methods

Study design and population

This study is an ancillary analysis of the ASAS-PerSpA study [13]. The ASAS-PerSpA study was an observational, cross-sectional, multicenter, international study that evaluated the clinical peripheral rheumatological features of SpA.

Data from the ASAS-PerSpA study recruiting patients with SpA between July 2018 and February 2020 from 68 facilities in 24 countries across four continents (Africa, the Americas, Asia, and Europe), were analyzed. For this analysis, patients aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with SpA (including axial SpA, peripheral SpA, and PsA, with or without meeting classification criteria such as ASAS or CASPAR) by a physician and could understand and fill out the questionnaires independently were included [14].

Among the 4,465 patients with SpA enrolled in the study, 1,033 (23.1%) had PsA. Patients with PsA presenting with axial involvement at any time per physician judgment (by answering the question, “Do you consider that this patient has ever suffered from axial involvement of SpA?”) were defined as axPsA and were included in this study. Patients with axPsA were further categorized by regions: Europe/North America, Latin America, Asia, and Middle East/North Africa. Asia was also divided into Japan and the rest of Asia. The countries included in each region were as follows: Canada, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Portugal, Romania, Spain, the Netherlands, the UK, and the USA for Europe/North America; Argentina, Chile, Colombia, and Mexico for Latin America; Japan, China, India, South Korea and Taiwan for Asia; and Egypt, Lebanon, Morocco, and Turkey for Middle East/North Africa.

Patient and disease characteristics, disease activity, physical function, overall health and functioning, and treatment were compared. All patients provided written consent, and the ethics committees at each facility approved the protocol of the ASAS-PerSpA study.

Data collection

Data were collected from questionnaires completed by each patient and their treating provider (either a rheumatologist or rheumatology nurse practitioner) during a single study visit. This included a medical records review, as described in the original study [13].

Demographic data included country, age, sex, smoking, alcohol intake, and education level. Disease characteristics included HLA-B27 status, family history of SpA features (ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, uveitis, reactive arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease [IBD]) among first- or second-degree relatives, diagnostic delay (time between first disease-related symptom and diagnosis), disease duration (time between the first disease-related symptom and study visit), inflammatory back pain, radiologic evidence of sacroiliitis according to the modified New York (mNY) criteria on X-ray and per ASAS definition in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the sacroiliac joints (SIJ) [15, 16]. The judgment of radiographic evidence of sacroiliitis was independently assessed by each facility. Information regarding the presence of peripheral manifestations including arthritis, enthesitis, and dactylitis; extra-musculoskeletal manifestations (EMMs) including uveitis; psoriasis confirmed by a dermatologist; and endoscopy-confirmed IBD was collected. Additional information regarding symptom localization and current presence or past occurrence was also collected, and investigators were also asked if those required specific treatments. All patients were assessed for fulfillment of ASAS and CASPAR classification criteria [17, 18]. The history of abnormal C reactive protein (>5 mg/dL) at any time in their disease course was collected. Secondary fibromyalgia, per physician diagnosis, was collected, and the self-reported fibromyalgia rapid screening tool (FiRST) was also assessed [19].

Disease activity at the time of the study visit was measured using the patient global assessment of the disease (PGA), Bath ankylosing spondylitis disease activity index (BASDAI), and axial spondyloarthritis disease activity score-C reactive protein (ASDAS) [20, 21]. The Bath ankylosing spondylitis disease activity function index (BASFI) and overall health and functioning ASAS-Health Index (ASAS HI) were also assessed [22, 23].

Treatment-related information was collected, including the current use of glucocorticoids, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), bDMARDs, and apremilast. Specific treatment previously and/or currently used for axial involvement was also collected.

Statistical analysis

The number and percentage of patients with axPsA in each region was calculated. Patients with axPsA were then divided by region. For comparisons between the four regions, including Europe/North America, Latin America, Middle East/North Africa, and Asia, the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables and the analysis of variance for continuous variables, according to data distributions. For comparisons between Japan and other Asian countries, Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables, according to data distributions. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations (SD). All analyses were performed using STATA version 16 MP. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The characteristics of the study populations are presented in Table 1. Among 4,465 patients with SpA, 1,038 (23.1%) had PsA. Of these, 165 (16.9%) were from Asia, 489 (29.2%) from Europe and North America, 176 (32.7%) from Latin America, and 203 (15.9%) from the Middle East and North Africa. Among patients with PsA, 367 (35.5%) had axial involvement, including 69 (41.8%) from Asia, 164 (33.5%) from Europe and North America, 56 (31.8%) from Latin America, and 78 (38.4%) from the Middle East and North Africa. Thirty-seven patients had PsA with axial involvement from Japan.

The overall patient characteristics in the four regions are summarized in Table 2. Patients were predominantly male: 83 (50.6%) in Europe/North America, 32 (57.1%) in Latin America, 33 (42.3%) in the Middle East/North Africa, and 48 (70.6%) in Asia, which had the highest percentage compared to non-Asian regions. The mean age ranged from 47.2 to 51.5 years among regions. The body mass index was the lowest in Asia (24.2 kg/m2) compared with other regions (27.4–29.6 kg/m2). HLA-B27 positivity ranged from 22.0% to 36.4%. No differences in family history of SpA and inflammatory back pain were found among regions. Smoking (34.6%) and alcohol (12.8%) were less frequently identified in the Middle East/North Africa. The longest disease duration among the four regions was seen in Latin America (22.1 years), followed by the Middle East/North Africa (17.1 years). Diagnostic delay was the longest in Latin America (11.9 years). As for sacroiliitis, radiographic evidence per the mNY criteria ranged from 50% to 65.3% across the regions. However, per ASAS definitions of SIJ on MRI, the Middle East/North Africa demonstrated relatively more evidence of sacroiliitis (88.9%) than other regions (51.0%–65.9%). With respect to the classification criteria, only 37.8% of the patients fulfilled either the ASAS clinical arm criteria or the ASAS imaging arm criteria in Europe/North America compared with other regions (50.0%–69.2%). The proportion of patients with axPsA fulfilling the clinical arm of the ASAS criteria ranged from 11.1% to 15.4% among the four regions, which was less frequent than the fulfillment rate of the imaging arm criteria. Moreover, 64.6% of the patients with axPsA in Europe/North America fulfilled the CASPAR criteria compared with other regions (76.9%–87.0%). Patients in Europe/North America (23.8%) showed a lower rate of abnormal CRP levels than other regions (46.4%–53.6%). In terms of peripheral involvement, no differences were found in the presence history of peripheral arthritis (Table 3). In contrast, presence of dactylitis at any time was more frequently seen in Asia (49.3%) and Latin America (51.8%). As for EMMs, IBD and uveitis demonstrated no differences between regions. Patients in Europe/North America (15.2%) and Middle East/North Africa (17.9%) had higher rates of fibromyalgia according to their treating rheumatologists. The diagnosis rates of fibromyalgia according to the FiRST questionnaire ranged from 23.1% to 41.4 among regions, which were higher than the diagnosis rate according to the treating rheumatologist, which ranged from 2.9 to 17.9% among regions.

Data on disease activity, physical function and overall health and functioning at the time of study visit are shown in Table 4. Concerning disease activity, the BASDAI scores were 4.1 (2.8) in Asia, 4.6 (SD 2.4) in Europe/North America, 5.4 (SD 2.8) in Latin America, and 4.8 (SD 2.8) in the Middle East/North Africa. The ASDAS scores were 2.7 (SD 1.3) in Asia, 2.5 (SD 1.0) in Europe/North America, 3.2 (SD 1.2) in Latin America, and 2.9 (SD 1.0) in the Middle East/North Africa. In terms of overall health and functional scores, the BASFI scores were 3.0 (SD 3.1) in Asia, 3.2 (SD 2.5) in Europe/North America, 4.7 (SD 3.0) in Latin America, and 4.2 (SD 2.6) in the Middle East/North Africa. The ASAS-HI scores were 8.2 (SD 5.0) in Asia, 7.9 (SD 4.5) in Europe/North America, 8.8 (SD 4.9) in Latin America, and 8.2 (SD 4.9) in the Middle East/North Africa.

Current treatments at the time of the study visit are shown in Table 5. With regards to NSAID use, 55 (79.7%) in Asia, 92 (56.1%) in Europe/North America, 50 (89.3%) in Latin America, and 44 (56.4%) in the Middle East/North Africa. For glucocorticoid use, 11 (15.9%) in Asia, 32 (19.5%) in Europe/North America, 15 (26.8%) in Latin America, and 16 (20.5%) in the Middle East/North Africa. For csDMARDs use, 44 (63.8%) in Asia, 82 (50.0%) in Europe/North America, 34 (60.7%) in Latin America, and 44 (56.4%) in the Middle East/North Africa. For bDMARDs use, 25 (36.2%) in Asia, 107 (65.2%) in Europe/North America, 24 (42.9%) in Latin America, and 42 (53.9%) in the Middle East/North Africa. Among csDMARDs, no significant differences were noted among the four regions regarding sulfasalazine or methotrexate use. In contrast to csDMARDs, a lower proportion of patients in Asia used bDMARDs (36.2%), and Europe/North America used bDMARDs the most (65.2%). Among bDMARDs, tumor necrosis factor inhibitors are most frequently used among the four regions (27.5%–47.0%). In Europe/North America and Middle East/North Africa, 15.2% and 20.5% of the patients used IL-17 inhibitors, respectively, which were relatively higher than those in other regions. Few patients were using IL-12/23 inhibitors (0%–3.1%) at the time of their study visit. Apremilast was used only in Europe/North America (4.3%) and Asia (8.7%). With respect to specific treatments (previous or current) for axial involvement, patients in the Middle East/North Africa less frequently used NSAIDs (67.9%), although its use was quite prevalent in other regions (90.9%–92.8%). In Europe/North America, the use rates of csDMARDs and bDMARDs (61.0%, 59.8%) were higher than that in other parts of the world. Asia demonstrated less bDMARD use (36.2%) as a specific treatment for axial involvement.

Differences between Japan and other Asian countries

When comparing Japan and other Asian countries, among patients with axPsA in Asia, 37 patients were from Japan and 32 were from other Asian countries. Among those who were analyzed, the HLA-B27 positivity rate in Japan (7.1%) was lower than that in other Asian countries (52.9%), although only 14 of 37 patients had HLA-B27 assessed in Japan. Japan showed a particularly low percentage of fulfilling the clinical arm at 2.7%, and 94.6% of patients with axPsA fulfilled the CASPAR criteria in Japan, compared with 78.1% in other Asian countries. Patients in Japan (27.0%) showed a significantly lower rate of abnormal CRP levels than other Asian countries (68.8%). In Japan, disease activity and functional index were relatively lower, indicated by a mean BASDAI score of 3.5 (SD 2.4), ASDAS score of 2.2 (SD 1.0), and BASFI score of 1.6 (SD 2.3). Moreover, these scores were significantly lower than those in other Asian countries, which recorded scores of 4.8 (SD 3.0), 2.2 (SD 2.4), and 3.2 (SD 1.5), respectively. The health index was relatively lower in Japan, with an ASAS HI score of 7.1 (SD 4.9) than in other Asian countries (9.5 L; SD 5.3). As regards treatments, in Japan, only 2.7% of patients used oral glucocorticoids, whereas in other Asian countries, 31.3% used oral glucocorticoids, indicating less frequent use. In Japan, the usage rate of csDMARDs was significantly lower than that in other Asian countries (51.4% vs. 78.1%, p = 0.02), and for the usage rates of bDMARDs, no significant difference was found when compared with those in other Asian countries (43.2% vs. 28.1% p = 0.19). Regarding specific treatments (previous or current) for axial involvement, Japan showed significantly more frequent use of csDMARDs (62.2% vs. 31.3% p = 0.02) and bDMARDs (51.4% vs. 18.8 p = 0.01) than other Asian countries.

Discussion

In this study, the clinical characteristics of patients having PsA with axial involvement were examined to clarify the differences in clinical features, disease activity, overall health and functioning. and practice patterns among four global regions, particularly between Japan and other Asian countries. While a recent ancillary analysis of ASAS perSpA data revealed differences in axSpA and axPsA in the international setting, to the best of our knowledge, a detailed study of patients in each region has not been performed [5].

The prevalence of PsA in Japan was 41.1%, while it was 42.7% in other Asian countries, and other regions showed an axPsA prevalence of 31.8%–41.8%. This result corroborates previous reports that 25%–70% of patients with PsA have spondylitis depending on the inclusion criteria of the study and the timing of the evaluation [24]. Disease duration ranged from 14.6 to 22.1 years in this study, implying that this cross-sectional analysis involved a population with long-standing PsA.

Overall, no differences were seen between men and women, which is compatible with a recent study that reports no sex dominance in axPsA [25]. However, axPsA was more prevalent in men in Asian countries (70.6%), including Japan (64.9%). Previous report revealed that PsA was more prevalent in male in Japan (59.9%), which was similar to our reports [9]. This regional variation may be explained by the lower BMI of female patients in Asia (24.2) than in other regions (27.4–29.60), as psoriasis is strongly related to obesity [26]. Smoking and alcohol consumption were less prevalent in the Middle East/North Africa (34.6% and 12.8% respectively) as compared with the other regions (smoking, 50.0%–54.3%; alcohol, 46.4%–58.0%). Although these lifestyle factors are reported to be risk factors for psoriasis, they do not appear to exert a significant effect on the disease activity in this cohort [27, 28].

SpA was strongly associated with HLA-B27, in which 20% of the patients with PsA and 50% of the patients with axSpA were HLA-B27 positive [29]. A positivity rate of 40% for HLA-B27 in axPsA was also reported [30]. In this study, the positivity rate of HLA-B27 varied slightly by four regions, ranging from 22.0% to 36.4%, and 29.6% overall, which is lower than that in a previous study. In Japan, the HLA-B27 positivity rate was substantially low at 7.1%. The number of patients undergoing HLA testing in Japan and other parts of Asia is relatively lower compared to other regions, which can lead to variability in the frequency of positive results and potential overestimation or underestimation. However, despite the very low prevalence of HLA-B27 in Japan, the positivity rate is significantly higher among patients with psoriatic arthritis [31]. Consequently, our results may be underestimating the actual prevalence. In Japan, the cost of HLA-B27 test is not covered by the current Japanese medical insurance system, despite its central role in diagnosis and classification per the clinical arm of the ASAS criteria; debating this is challenging. Thus, the sample size must be increased for future verification. This explains why most of those in Japan fulfilling the ASAS criteria met the imaging arm ASAS criteria. Nonetheless, the proportions of patients who fulfilled the ASAS criteria (67.6%) and CASPAR criteria (94.6%) were higher in Japan than in other Asian countries. This might suggest that rheumatologists in Japan utilize the criteria more in clinical practice. With regards to radiographic evidence of sacroiliitis, results were similar among regions, except that 88.9% of patients in the Middle East/North Africa were positive for ASAS-defined sacroiliitis on MRI. This discrepancy may be from underreporting of negative results given the amount of missing MRI data from this region.

As for EMMs, uveitis and IBD were rarely seen in any region, which differs from previous reports. For example, a single-center study reported that 26% of the patients had uveitis and 11% had IBD [30]. Because a definitive definition of axPsA is still under discussion, various criteria have been used, and patient populations have varied depending on the study. Differences in EMMs were attributed to the comparison of heterogeneous study populations, which further emphasizes the need to establish diagnostic criteria for axPsA. The proportion of patients with fibromyalgia according to rheumatologists was low in Latin America and Asia. However, in those countries, the proportions of patients who filled out the FiRST questionnaire were similar to those in other regions. It may be because disease awareness was not as high as in other regions. Additionally, pain perception has social and cultural dimensions, which could explain variations in results.

In terms of treatments in axPsA, some interesting points warrant discussion. The use rates of glucocorticoids and csDMARDs were comparable among the four regions; however, these regions demonstrate lower use rates of bDMARDs, perhaps due to low access to bDMARDs for various systemic reasons. Europe/North America and Asia showed relatively better disease control than other regions. In Europe/North America, this may be due to the more frequent use of bDMARDs (65.2%) in these regions. On the contrary, Asia showed less frequent use of bDMARDs (36.2%) despite better disease control. The possible reason is that Asian patients are generally smaller with lower BMI (24.2) and may be more susceptible to medication effects. Although this association has not been consistently observed with other biologic agents, several studies have suggested that being overweight may negatively impact the therapeutic response to TNF inhibitors in patients with PsA and axSpA [32, 33]. Compared with other countries in Asia, Japan showed better disease control with relatively higher bDMARD use rates. IL-17 inhibitors are used more frequently than IL-12/23 inhibitors. This result is supported by the fact that the first randomized controlled study, which evaluated secukinumab for patients with axPsA inadequately responding to NSAIDs, showed significant improvements in axial symptoms; a phase 3 trial of ustekinumab for patients with axSpA was terminated without meeting endpoints [34, 35].

As for the history of specific treatment for axial involvement, Asia appeared to have less frequent use of bDMARDs, and this may be explained by the higher use of apremilast instead of bDMARDs compared with other regions. Despite NSAIDs being the first choice for axial disease in most patients, in the Middle East/North Africa, only 67.9% of patients used NSAIDs.

Compared with other countries in Asia, Japan showed better disease activities and less frequent use of glucocorticoids (2.7%) and csDMARDs (51.4%). The possible reason was that oral glucocorticoids tended to be avoided in Japanese patients with psoriasis to prevent occurrence of pustular psoriasis. With respect to a history of specific treatment for axial involvement, csDMARDs (62.2%) and bDMARDs (51.4%) are more frequently used in Japan than in other Asian countries. These results provide a similar tendency as Europe/North America with csDMARD and bDMARD use rates of 61.0% and 59.8%, respectively. Thus, axial symptoms tend to be more concerning than other symptoms such as peripheral arthritis, enteritis, and dactylitis.

Factors that can affect these results should be considered for accurate interpretation of these data, such as differences in diet, environment, lifestyle, genetics, access to and quality of care, and medical insurance systems. In addition, treatment strategies vary depending on each country’s healthcare system and adherence to guidelines, making direct comparisons inherently difficult. In Japan, for instance, treatment practices generally follow national or international recommendations, with systemic therapies including csDMARDs typically used as initial treatment. If disease activity cannot be controlled with one or more csDMARDs, escalation to bDMARDs is considered. Due to the universal health insurance system in Japan, the government subsidizes part of the cost of treatments, including high-cost options such as bDMARDs and tsDMARDs, thereby reducing financial barriers to access. National treatment guidelines for psoriatic arthritis were not published in Japan until 2019. Prior to that, physicians often referred to international guidelines such as those from EULAR and GRAPPA, although widespread implementation remained limited. Moreover, methotrexate was not officially approved for psoriatic arthritis in Japan until 2018 and was frequently prescribed off-label. Similar differences in treatment accessibility and the adoption of recommendations likely exist in other regions as well, further complicating direct international comparisons. Second, this is a cross-sectional study, and axial symptoms may emerge over time. Despite this population having a relatively long-standing disease, some patients with psoriasis without axial symptoms at the time of evaluation may later exhibit axial symptoms. Moreover, data were not consecutively accumulated; thus, some data were probably missing from the records since the patient’s first visit, hindering proper evaluation. Third, the number of patients in each geographic region varied. Both Japan and other countries in Asia are represented by a relatively small number of patients. Europe and North America were combined regardless of their geographically disparate locations, which leads to a more diverse ethnic background. Forth, clinical judgment of whether patients had axial symptoms, and the diagnosis of SpA and PsA, was made independently by each treating rheumatologist in each region. However, given the lack of clear criteria defining axPsA, we consider the definition used in this study as both reasonable and helpful to understand how rheumatologists consider axPsA in real practice. Importantly, this study provides one of the first detailed cross-regional comparisons of axPsA in real-world clinical settings, particularly highlighting data from Japan. Rather than drawing direct comparisons, our aim was to illustrate the heterogeneity of clinical practice and patient characteristics across regions. We believe that recognizing these patterns is a crucial first step toward building a context-sensitive understanding of axPsA. Such insight may serve as a foundation for future research and international consensus-building efforts to refine its classification and management. Finally, spinal involvements such as spondylitis were not evaluated despite it being an important pathological site in patients with SpA. Nonetheless, given the large sample size, this study will provide much-needed information regarding the clinical characteristics of axPsA to help understand its regional differences.

Conclusion

This study uses the worldwide ASAS-PerSpA dataset to demonstrate that patients with axPsA exhibited various characteristics, disease activities, physical function, overall health and functioning, and treatments depending on regions. This study also revealed clinical characteristics of axPsA among patients from different geographic regions. Compared with patients from other parts of the world, Asian patients with axPsA showed lower disease activity despite the relatively frequent use of bDMARDs. Patients with axPsA in Japan had relatively lower disease activity and function compared to patients from different geographic regions, particularly in other Asian countries with less frequent csDMARD use. These findings contribute to furthering the understanding of axPsA and increase disease awareness to facilitate better diagnosis and treatment recommendations. Furthermore, they highlight the importance of establishing a standardized and consensual definition of axPsA, such as through collaborative efforts by GRAPPA and ASAS, to facilitate the accurate identification of axPsA patients and enhance cohort comparability across regions.
