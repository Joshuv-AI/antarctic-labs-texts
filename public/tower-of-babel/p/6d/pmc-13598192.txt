cfDNA-derived gene signatures as surrogate for microvascular invasion in HCC
Authors: Ruijie Gong, Linchen Wang, Dongdong Xue, Jiabin Cai, Yurou Jiang, Jianhang Huang, Jinjin Zhu, Zhongchen Li, Aiwu Ke, Guoming Shi, Jie Wang, Wentao Wang, Jiaping Zheng, Weijian Yang, Zhou Zhang, Jian Zhou, Jia Fan, Wei Zhang, Pingting Gao, Lei Chen, Danjun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsMicrovascular invasion (MVI) is a critical prognostic risk factor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We evaluated the performance of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) modifications in circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in preoperative assessment of MVI.MethodsA total of 907 patients with HCC were enrolled from two centers, including 671 in the training cohort, 152 in the internal validation cohort, and 84 in the external validation cohort. Preoperative clinical data, laboratory parameters, and cfDNA-derived 5hmC profiles were collected. Feature selection was performed using XGBoost, and modeling was conducted using a multilayer perceptron neural network. Survival analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the MVI prediction model. RNA sequencing analysis was performed to explore the potential mechanism underlying the proposed model.ResultsThe 181-5hmC-modification signature demonstrated strong discriminatory performance, achieving an area under the curve of 0.852, 0.862, and 0.864 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. Univariate and multivariate analyses identified the alpha-fetoprotein level (odds ratio [OR] 1.576, p = 0.039), Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage (OR 3.051, p <0.001), and the 5hmC signature (OR 46.891, p <0.001) as independent predictors of MVI. The 5hmC signature demonstrated significantly higher predictive accuracy than alpha-fetoprotein levels or BCLC stage alone. Survival analysis showed that the 5hmC signature significantly stratified both recurrence-free and overall survival in patients with resectable HCC. Furthermore, interpretability analysis based on RNA sequencing revealed that lower MVI prediction scores were associated with immune-related pathways and immune infiltration levels.ConclusionsWe developed and validated a circulating cfDNA-derived 5hmC signature that non-invasively predicts preoperative MVI status, with potential clinical utility in the management of resectable HCC.Impact and implicationsIn this study, we present the first integration of cfDNA-derived 5hmC profiling with machine learning for preoperative MVI prediction in resectable HCC. The proposed 5hmC signature demonstrates potential for predicting MVI status and prognosis before surgery. Integration of RNA sequencing analysis provides biological support for the model’s predictions, strengthening its clinical relevance. As a blood-based assay, this approach offers practical advantages for potential routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a critical histological feature associated with early recurrence and mortality in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1 On histopathological examination, MVI is defined as the presence of tumor cell clusters within the portal venous system of the peritumoral hepatic parenchyma.1 Unlike macrovascular invasion, which is readily identified using conventional imaging modalities, MVI detection requires comprehensive pathological assessment of surgically resected specimens. This inherent diagnostic limitation underscores a major clinical challenge of accurately predicting MVI preoperatively.

Preoperative evaluation of MVI status plays an essential role in prognostic stratification and therapeutic decision-making for patients with resectable HCC. Retrospective studies have demonstrated improved survival outcomes with wide-margin or anatomic liver resection in MVI-positive patients.2,3 Furthermore, among patients with early-stage HCC and high risk of MVI, surgical resection yields superior survival benefits compared with radiofrequency ablation.4,5 Reportedly, adjuvant sintilimab significantly prolongs recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared with active surveillance in MVI-positive patients.6 Moreover, neoadjuvant strategies can be considered when reliable preoperative MVI detection is available.7,8

Given the prognostic importance of MVI, extensive efforts have been undertaken to identify non-invasive predictors. Conventional serum biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II) show modest associations with MVI status,9,10 yet their diagnostic sensitivity remains inadequate for reliable clinical utility. Emerging molecular signatures, including a six-gene classifier derived from RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded biopsies, offer improved predictive accuracy;11 however, the limited adoption of biopsy in routine HCC management restricts its applicability.12 Radiomic- and deep learning-based models also exhibit promising associations with MVI,2,13 but challenges such as labor-intensive image annotation, algorithmic complexity, and interobserver variability hinder their widespread clinical implementation.

Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has emerged as a powerful biomarker that reflects tumor-derived epigenetic alterations and enables non-invasive assessment of disease biology, prognosis, and therapeutic response.14,15 Among these epigenetic signals, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), an oxidative derivative of 5-methylcytosine generated by ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes,16 exhibits dynamic cancer-associated patterns in cfDNA. Accumulating evidence indicates that cfDNA-derived 5hmC signatures can effectively track disease progression, therapeutic response, and survival outcomes across multiple malignancies, including HCC, lung adenocarcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and colorectal cancer.15,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]

In this large-sample, dual-center study, we developed and validated a non-invasive predictive model based on genome-wide 5hmC profiling in circulating cfDNA to distinguish MVI-positive from MVI-negative patients prior to surgery. We further compared long-term outcomes according to predicted MVI status. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply a cfDNA-derived 5hmC signature for preoperative MVI assessment in patients with resectable HCC.

Patients and methods

Patient enrollment

In this retrospective study, we analyzed 907 patients with HCC from two medical centers (Fig. S1). The primary cohort included 671 patients who underwent hepatectomy at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China; Fudan cohort 1). An independent validation cohort included 152 patients who received surgical resection at the same institution (Fudan cohort 2; January 2017 to July 2018). Additionally, 84 surgical cases were included from Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital (Shanghai, China; Eastern cohort). Patients undergoing hepatectomy were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) histologically confirmed HCC; (2) complete pathological assessment of MVI status and corresponding clinical data; (3) availability of plasma samples (Fudan cohort 2) or 5hmC profiles (Fudan cohort 1 and Eastern cohort) within 2 weeks before hepatectomy. Key exclusion criteria were: (1) receipt of any preoperative anticancer therapy; and (2) radiographic evidence of extrahepatic metastasis. Because of prolonged wait times for donor organs and the frequent use of bridging therapies before liver transplantation, patients who ultimately underwent liver transplantation were excluded.

Genome-wide 5hmC profiles were obtained from two sources: (1) publicly available data for Fudan cohort 1 and the Eastern cohort (NCBI/Gene Expression Omnibus Database, GSE112679), and (2) de novo 5hmC-Seal sequencing for Fudan cohort 2. The raw 5hmC profiles of Fudan cohort 2 have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive23 in the National Genomics Data Center24 (HRA017268).

For predictive model construction, 671 patients from Fudan cohort 1 were used as the training cohort. Model performance was validated using two independent cohorts: an internal validation cohort (Fudan cohort 2; n = 152) and an external validation cohort (Eastern cohort; n = 84). The Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) granted ethical approval for this retrospective study (B2024-183) and waived the need for written informed consent.

Pathology evaluation and data collection

Tumor status was assessed using the recommended “7-point” baseline sampling method, but the actual sampling sites and number of submitted sections was increased according to tumor size and the number of tumor nodules.25 MVI is histologically defined as the presence of clusters of tumor cells within the portal venous system, hepatic venous system, or branches surrounding the hepatic tissue lined by the endothelium.2 Two independent pathologists evaluated the MVI status for all cases according to the unified pathological assessment standards.2,25 When findings were equivocal, ancillary immunohistochemical evaluation with vascular or lymphatic endothelial markers could be further performed.

Demographic characteristics (sex, age, and HBV/HCV status) and preoperative laboratory parameters (routine blood tests, blood biochemical tests, blood coagulation function tests, and AFP) of enrolled patients were collected. Tumor characteristics, such as tumor size, number, and Edmondson-Steiner grade were also recorded. In patients with multiple tumors, tumor size was defined according to the largest diameter among all lesions. Child-Pugh scores and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stages were calculated and registered. Liver cirrhosis status was confirmed by histopathological assessment of paracarcinoma tissues.

5hmC profiling and data processing

Genome-wide 5hmC profiles for the training (Fudan cohort 1) and external validation (Eastern cohort) cohorts were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (accession GSE112679). In the internal validation cohort (Fudan cohort 2), 5hmC-Seal profiling was performed on retrospectively collected preoperative plasma samples. All plasma samples for 5hmC profiling among three cohorts were collected within 2 weeks before liver resection. Sample preparation, 5hmC-Seal library construction, and sequencing followed protocols described in our previous study.20 Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Shanghai Epican Genetech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw sequencing data were processed using standard bioinformatic procedures. Quality control of FastQ files was performed using FastQC (v0.11.8) (RRID: SCR_014583). Raw reads were aligned to the UCSC hg19 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.0)26 (RRID: SCR_004463) with parameters “--alignIntronMax 1 --alignEndsType EndToEnd”. The resulting BAM files were sorted and indexed, and duplicate reads (i.e. fragments with the same start, end, and index barcode) were removed using Picard tools. The 5hmC-enriched regions (peaks) were identified for each sample using MACS2 (v2.1.1) (RRID: SCR_013291).27 Only paired cfDNA reads with a mapping quality score ≥30 were retained. Scores for gene body were computed using the computeMatrix and plotProfile functions of deepTools (v3.5.0) (RRID: SCR_016366).28

For gene body features, all protein-coding genes in hg19 from the transcription start site (TSS) to the transcription termination site (TTS) were included, covering exons and introns but excluding promoter and downstream termination regions. Peak feature regions were generated by merging all peak regions across sample to create a union covering all 5hmC-enriched areas. Read counts for these features were obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.6) (RRID: SCR_012919)29 with GTF format region files, resulting in a matrix of the above features. The genome was segmented into 26,232 non-overlapping 100-kb bins. Annotation and genome files (UCSC hg19) were downloaded from iGenomes.

Developing a 5hmC-based prediction model for MVI

The 5hmC modifications in promoters, enhancers, gene bodies, and other genome-wide fragments are associated with gene expression changes, serving as markers for cancer detection and pathway alterations.20,30,31 Herein, three categories of 5hmC features including gene body region, isometric bin (genome divided into 100-kb segments), and 5hmC peak were initially defined and selected. Feature selection was conducted using embedded XGBoost, implemented via Python scripts (v3.6.9; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA; https://www.python.org/) utilizing the XGBoost API from scikit-learn 0.24.2 (RRID:SCR_002577).27 Within the training cohort, repeated K-fold cross-validation was used to calculate feature importance scores using the F-score metric, which measures the frequency with which each feature is utilized to split nodes within the decision trees. During feature selection, the F-score served as an indicator of the relative contribution of individual features to the model’s predictive accuracy. To determine the optimal number of features in each feature category, an adjusted composite score was computed based on predictive performance (AUC), model stability (SD of AUC), improvement rate of AUC (growth rate), and model complexity (number of features). Predictive performance (AUC) was evaluated using the multilayer perceptron (MLP) classifier from scikit-learn 0.24.2. The feature number with the highest adjusted score was selected for each feature category, resulting in selections of 58 features for gene body regions, 36 for isometric bin features, and 87 for 5hmC peak, totaling 181 selected features (Fig. S2–S4). These selected features were subsequently combined to form the final training dataset used for further modeling and validation.

The optimal hyperparameters selected through five-fold cross-validation in the training cohort were used to train the best model. Model training was performed using an MLP classifier implemented with key hyperparameters, including one hidden layer of 100 neurons, a ReLU activation function, Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 0.001, L2 regularization (α = 0.0001), and a maximum of 200 training iterations. The final model was trained on the entire training cohort, then validated on both internal and external validation cohorts. Follow-up

Postoperative follow-up was recommended at 1 month and subsequently every 2 or 3 months. Evaluations included tumor biomarkers (AFP and/or PIVKA-II), abdominal ultrasonography, dynamic-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging, etc. Recurrence was defined by characteristic imaging findings and elevated AFP levels. RFS was measured from hepatectomy to radiologic recurrence or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was measured from surgery to death or last follow-up contact.

Biological pathway-related interpretability

To investigate the biological mechanisms underlying the cfDNA-derived 5hmC prediction model and to establish continuity between epigenetic signals and tumor transcriptional programs, we performed two complementary analyses: (1) transcriptomic profiling by RNA-seq and (2) genome-wide differential 5hmC analysis in cfDNA, followed by multilayer integrative assessment at the gene and pathway levels.

We conducted RNA-seq on a randomly selected subset of patients predicted by the model as MVI-positive (n = 15) or MVI-negative (n = 15). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with significantly altered genes defined as those exhibiting an absolute fold change >1.5 and a p value <0.05. Functional characterization was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) over-representation analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) implemented in the clusterProfiler package in R (v3.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Additionally, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) (RRID:SCR_026610) was used to estimate relative immune cell infiltration and to compare tumor microenvironment features between predicted MVI groups. To identify the key molecules shaping different tumor microenvironments, we compared the expression levels of 42 co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory molecules between the two predicted groups. Co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory molecules were extracted from the previous study.32

To complement model-based feature selection and capture genome-wide epigenetic alterations associated with MVI status, we performed differential 5hmC analysis (MVI-positive vs. MVI-negative) using gene-body–level 5hmC-Seal count matrices. For each protein-coding gene (TSS to TTS, excluding promoter and downstream termination regions), aggregated 5hmC counts were analyzed using DESeq2.

Next, we performed a simulation analysis to evaluate the concordance between 5hmC and RNA-seq data. Differentially modified 5hmC genes identified in the 5hmC analysis and differentially expressed genes identified in the RNA-seq analysis were ranked in descending order according to the absolute fold change. A sliding threshold was then applied (N = 10–2,000, step size = 10), and the cumulative number of overlapping genes within the top N genes of both ranked lists was calculated as the observed overlap. The expected random overlap was estimated via 5,000 Monte Carlo permutations, in which two gene sets of size N were randomly sampled from the background gene pool to construct a null distribution. Only genes detected by both platforms were included in the ranking and served as the background gene set. Statistical significance was determined when the observed overlap exceeded the upper bound of the 95% CI of the simulated mean. To quantify the strength of association between systemic 5hmC alterations and tumor transcriptome changes, we calculated the fold enrichment (FE) of the observed overlap relative to its expected value under the null distribution.

Estimation of sample size

For construction of the cfDNA-derived 5hmC predictive model, the minimum required sample size was determined using the following parameters: 90% statistical power (β = 0.1), a two-sided significance level (α = 0.05), an expected AUC of 0.750 under the alternative hypothesis compared with a null AUC of 0.500, and an allocation ratio of 1.5 between MVI-negative and MVI-positive groups. This estimation indicated that at least 55 cases (22 MVI-positive and 33 MVI-negative) would be required for each dataset.

The actual study cohorts included 671 patients (294 MVI-positive) in the training cohort, 152 (53 MVI-positive) in the internal validation cohort, and 84 (31 MVI-positive) in the external validation cohort. Given these sample sizes, the analyses were sufficiently powered (at least 80%) to detect an AUC significantly greater than 0.500, assuming a true AUC of at least 0.750.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with IQR, whereas categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, as appropriate. Principal component analysis was conducted to assess whether age and tumor clinical staging influenced the distribution of cfDNA-derived 5hmC features.

Model performance for predicting MVI status was evaluated using AUC analysis. In the training cohort, receiver operating characteristic curves were generated for each cross-validation fold, and the mean AUC ± SD and corresponding 95% CI were reported. Additional performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, were also calculated. The optimal cut-off for the 5hmC classifier was set at 0.50 based on the training cohort and subsequently applied to the internal and external validation cohorts.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify clinical variables associated with MVI, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were generated, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Multivariate Cox analysis was used to examine the prognostic relevance of predicted MVI status. Variables achieving a significance level of p <0.05 in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess the differences in immune infiltration between predicted MVI groups.

All statistical analyses and model constructions were performed using R (v3.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Python (v3.6.9; Python Software Foundation). Sample size estimation was performed using PASS 2021 (RRID:SCR_019099). A two-sided p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The proportion of patients with histopathologically confirmed MVI was comparable across cohorts, with 294/671 (43.82%) in the training cohort, 53/152 (34.87%) in the internal validation cohort, and 31/84 (36.90%) in the external validation cohort. Further comparison of clinicopathological features among patients in the training cohort (Table S1) revealed that the MVI-positive patients were significantly younger than the MVI-negative patients (55.99 vs. 58.55 years, p = 0.003). MVI-positive patients also exhibited a distinct BCLC stage distribution, characterized by a higher proportion of intermediate-stage disease (35.71% vs. 26.79%) and a lower proportion of very early-stage disease (8.50% vs. 26.26%) compared with MVI-negative patients.

Tumor characteristics indicated more aggressive disease in MVI-positive patients, including a higher frequency of multifocal tumors (41.16% vs. 32.63%, p = 0.023) and significantly larger median tumor diameter (4.50 [3.00–8.00] vs. 3.00 [2.00–5.00], p <0.001). Serum AFP levels were significantly elevated among MVI-positive patients (55.70 [7.22–999.58] vs. 14.30 [3.60–174.90], p <0.001; Table S1). Differences in platelet count and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were also observed. No other baseline variables differed significantly between groups (Table S1).

Prediction performance of the 5hmC signature

To assess whether age, tumor stage, or tumor differentiation influenced cfDNA-derived 5hmC patterns, we compared the distribution of 5hmC modifications across age groups (≤60 vs. >60 years), BCLC stages (0, A, and B stages), and tumor differentiation (Edmondson-Steiner grade I-II and III-IV). No significant distributional differences were detected for gene body, isometric bin, or 5hmC peak features (Fig. S5).

Using repeated five-fold cross-validation with XGBoost-based embedded feature selection, we identified 181 genome-wide 5hmC features associated with MVI (Table S2). These features were used to train the MLP neural network classifier.

The resulting 5hmC signature demonstrated a strong predictive performance across all cohorts. In the training cohort (cut-off = 0.50), the signature achieved an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI 0.824–0.879), with a sensitivity of 75.2%, specificity of 79.6%, and accuracy of 77.6%. In the internal validation cohort, the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and AUC were 62.3%, 97.0%, 84.9%, and 0.862 (95% CI 0.790–0.931), respectively. We next evaluated the predictive performance of the 5hmC signature relative to that of AFP and BCLC stages. The 5hmC signature consistently outperformed AFP and BCLC stages across all cohorts (Fig. 2A–C). In the training cohort, the AUC values were 0.852 (95% CI 0.824–0.879) for the 5hmC signature, 0.581 (95% CI 0.545–0.614) for AFP, and 0.545 (95% CI 0.508–0.578) for BCLC stages. These findings demonstrated the superior discriminatory ability of the 5hmC-based model.

Survival analysis by predicted MVI status

Given the prognostic relevance of MVI, we examined the association between predicted MVI status and patient outcomes. Patients with BCLC stage C were excluded from the external validation cohort because macrovascular invasion confers markedly worse survival.33 Median follow-up duration was 68.57 months overall, including 71.47, 62.80, and 17.00 months in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively.

Univariate Cox regression demonstrated that the 5hmC signature was significantly associated with RFS in the training (HR 1.530, 95% CI 1.236–1.894, p <0.001), internal validation (HR 1.838, 95% CI 1.064–3.177, p = 0.029), and external validation (HR 2.798, 95% CI 1.179–6.640, p = 0.020) cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3A–C). Significant variables, such as sex, Child-Pugh stage, albumin, alanine aminotransferase, AST, international normalized ratio, AFP, tumor number, tumor size, BCLC stage, and cirrhosis status, were included for adjusted multivariate model. Consequently, multivariate Cox regression showed that the 5hmC signature remained an independent predictor of RFS in the training (adjusted HR 1.356, 95% CI 1.087–1.692, p = 0.007) and internal validation (adjusted HR 1.832, 95% CI 1.001–3.353, p = 0.049) cohorts, but not in the external validation cohort (p = 0.309). Discussion

Preoperative assessment of MVI is critical for prognostic stratification and therapeutic decision-making in HCC.[2], [3], [4], [5], [6],34 Existing approaches for preoperative MVI prediction, including clinical variables, radiological features, and biopsy-derived sequencing, have shown some utility,13,[35], [36], [37] but they often remain impractical for routine use or demonstrate limited predictive accuracy. Although a urinary protein signature has recently been proposed as a non-invasive biomarker,38 its clinical value requires further validation owing to the small sample size. Given the ease of collection and minimal invasiveness, peripheral blood samples are generally considered a preferred source for biomarker development in clinical applications. In this study, we developed and validated a genome-wide cfDNA-derived 5hmC signature for preoperative MVI prediction in patients with resectable HCC. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply cfDNA-based 5hmC profiling for this purpose, and the signature showed consistent performance across validation cohorts.

Liquid biopsy has become an important tool for molecular profiling, allowing for the non-invasive assessment of tumor heterogeneity and dynamic disease monitoring.39,40 Previous work demonstrated that circulating tumor DNA allele frequency can serve as an independent risk factor for MVI,41 and that plasma cfDNA chromosomal instability can predict MVI with reasonable performance.42 However, these findings require broader validation and may not fully capture epigenetic alterations that reflect active tumor biology. Although plasma-derived cfDNA analysis has proven effective for genetic profiling in advanced HCC,43 emerging evidence highlights the clinical value of 5hmC profiling for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and postoperative monitoring across several malignancies, including HCC.15,20,44 Prior studies have also suggested associations between cfDNA-derived 5hmC patterns and MVI status,44 supporting the rationale for a dedicated 5hmC-based predictive model.

Using an XGBoost algorithm combined with an MLP classifier, we constructed an MVI prediction model that integrates three categories of 5hmC features, namely gene body, isometric bins, and peak regions. The model demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in both internal and external validation cohorts (AUC values of 0.862 and 0.864, respectively). We observed a decrease in sensitivity in the validation cohorts, which may be attributable to higher proportions of multifocal disease and more advanced tumor stages in the training cohort, as both factors are strongly associated with MVI risk. Despite this, the model maintained robust accuracy overall, supporting its potential use as a clinically practical and non-invasive biomarker.

The 5hmC signature also performed well across key clinical subgroups, including patients with early-stage HCC (BCLC 0 stage), those with tumors ≤3 cm, those with solitary lesions, and AFP-negative patients. Given that up to one quarter of small HCC tumors harbor MVI,45 the ability to preoperatively identify high-risk patients has major implications for surgical decision-making. In patients predicted to be at high risk of MVI, more proactive treatments such as wide-margin tumor ablation, anatomical hepatectomy with extended resection margins, or adjuvant transarterial chemoembolization may improve oncologic outcomes.3,4,46

Consistent with prior studies, AFP and BCLC stage were confirmed as significant clinical predictors of MVI.36,47 However, the cfDNA-derived 5hmC signature demonstrated substantially superior predictive accuracy compared with AFP or BCLC stage alone across all cohorts. These findings indicate that 5hmC-based cfDNA biomarkers may better capture biological processes that drive MVI risk.

Numerous studies have identified MVI as an independent prognostic risk factor associated with reduced RFS and OS in HCC.1,36,47 A recent study further emphasized that MVI and/or satellitosis (mVI/S) is a major risk factor for recurrence, aggressive recurrence, and mortality.48 Our previous work corroborated these findings, demonstrating significantly worse RFS and OS in patients classified as MVI-positive by our prediction model.2 In the current study, the 5hmC signature effectively stratified patients by RFS and OS. Although the association between the 5hmC signature and survival outcomes was not statistically significant in the external cohort, this may be owing to its smaller sample size, shorter follow-up, and inter-cohort variability. Overall, the signature shows promise both as a preoperative diagnostic tool for MVI and as a prognostic biomarker for long-term risk stratification.

To gain biological insights into the model, we analyzed RNA-seq data from a subset of patients. Predicted MVI-negative tumors exhibited increased immune infiltration and enrichment of immune-related pathways. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for the better prognosis observed in the predicted MVI-negative group. The findings also agree with previous radiogenomic research that reported stronger immune infiltration in low-risk MVI groups.13 Simulation analysis revealed that the cfDNA-derived 5hmC profile exhibits significant concordance with MVI-associated variability patterns across both epigenetic and transcriptional layers.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced bias. Although all samples were processed using uniform protocols and sequencing platforms, potential batch effects and cohort heterogeneity cannot be completely excluded. Future work should use a prospective, multicenter design with predefined standard operating procedures, centralized quality control, and explicit batch modeling to minimize bias and heterogeneity. Second, all patients were Chinese and most cases were HBV-related HCC, which limits generalizability to other populations and etiologies. External validation in geographically diverse cohorts, including HCV-related and non-viral HCC, is required to assess transportability and to recalibrate the model if necessary. Third, radiological features known to correlate with MVI, including pseudocapsule formation, non-smooth tumor margins, and peritumoral enhancement, were not included.13,47,49 Future work should integrate radiological variables into a multimodal framework that combines cfDNA 5hmC, imaging features, and clinical factors, followed by decision curve analysis to evaluate clinical utility. Finally, although transcriptomic analyses provided biological interpretability, the RNA-seq sample size was small. Larger transcriptomic cohorts and orthogonal validation, including immunohistochemistry or multiplex imaging of immune markers, are warranted to confirm pathway-level findings and to strengthen mechanistic linkage.

In summary, the cfDNA-derived 5hmC signature developed in this study shows strong potential as a non-invasive biomarker for preoperative MVI prediction in patients with resectable HCC. It provides meaningful advantages over conventional clinical parameters and may support personalized surgical planning and prognostic risk stratification in clinical practice.
