Perspective: Depression in Persons with Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Albert FG Leentjens, Gregory M Pontone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression is a prevalent and disabling syndrome characterized by sustained sadness and/or anhedonia, as well as cognitive and physical symptoms. In Parkinson's disease (PD), depression is both common and clinically challenging due to overlapping symptoms and complex etiologic interactions. Major depressive disorder occurs in approximately 17% of PD patients, while clinically significant depressive symptoms affect over one‐third. Both PD‐specific and general risk factors contribute to this burden, with the latter playing a greater role. Diagnosis is complicated by symptom overlap, prompting recommendations for an inclusive diagnostic approach and use of rating scales specifically validated in PD. Differentiation from related syndromes such as apathy, demoralization and anxiety disorders is essential for appropriate management. Treatment follows general population algorithms, incorporating pharmacotherapy—particularly selective serotonergic reuptake inhibitors, serotonin and noradrenalin reuptake inhibitors, and tricyclic antidepressants—and psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioral or interpersonal therapy, though adjustments are needed to account for PD‐related vulnerabilities. Access to adequate care is often hampered by the lack of mental health specialists with specific expertise in PD. From a research standpoint, PD provides a unique model for studying the neurobiology of mood regulation, implicating dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic systems, as well as hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysfunction. Emerging methods, including deep brain stimulation, ecological momentary assessment, neuroimaging, and machine learning, are expanding insights into emotional processing and its neurophysiological underpinnings. Clinically, routine screening and evidence‐based treatment have improved care. Future research promises to refine diagnostic criteria, optimize therapies, and deepen understanding of affective dysregulation in Parkinson's disease. © 2026 The Author(s). Depression is a syndrome characterized by a sustained period of sadness and/or anhedonia, accompanied by both physical and cognitive symptoms. Physical symptoms can include fatigue and loss of energy, loss of appetite, weight loss, and sleeping disturbances. Cognitive symptoms can include reduced attention and concentration, rumination, preoccupations, and delusions. The diagnostic criteria of the ‘Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision’ (DSM‐5‐TR) arbitrarily require a minimum duration of 2 weeks for a syndromal diagnosis of ‘major depressive disorder.’ 1 Major depressive disorder is a common syndrome in the general population, with a point prevalence of 5.7%. It accounts for 6.2% of years lived with disability (YLD) worldwide, ranking second only after low back pain. 2

The etiology of depression is complex and multifaceted with genetic, metabolic, hormonal, and neurophysiological contributions that interact with early childhood adversity, traumatic experiences, personality and coping styles. Patients with physical disease, including Parkinson's disease (PD), have a greater risk of developing depression. In PD, the prevalence of major depressive disorder is 17%, but the prevalence of clinically significant depressive symptoms, whether or not these meet the DSM criteria for a specific disorder, is much higher and such symptoms occur in 35% of patients. 3 Whereas PD is a risk factor for depression, this greater risk is not only due to PD itself. As can be expected, known risk factors for depression in the general population, such as female sex, a previous history of anxiety and/or depression, family history of depression, worse activities of daily living, and worse cognitive status, also play a role in PD. The only study to date addressing the relative contribution of Parkinson‐specific and non‐specific risk factors for depression showed that non‐specific general population risk factors constitute a three times higher contribution to the overall risk of depression in PD patients than Parkinson‐specific risk factors. This implies that PD patients most at risk for depression are those patients that already have other, not Parkinson‐related risk factors, for depression. 4

Not only is the prevalence of depression higher in patients with diagnosed PD, depression may also precede the onset of PD. This also is not specific for PD, but holds true for a large variety of physical diseases. Depression may precede, or be a presenting symptom of, Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative disorders, viral infections, diabetes, cancer, hormonal dysregulation, and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. 5 Patients with a history of depressive disorder have a more than three times greater chance of developing PD than those who have never been depressed. Also, more than twice as many patients who are diagnosed with PD have a prior history of depression than those not diagnosed with PD. 6 , 7 The increased prevalence of depression in patients with diagnosed PD, together with the fact that depression may precede PD, implies that the relationship between depression and PD is probably bidirectional.

Depression in PD patients is associated with a number of negative outcomes, including more severe motor symptoms, worse functioning on activities of daily living, and worse cognitive status. 4 Increased physical exercise and activity by PD patients is associated with less depressive symptoms as well as a reduced severity of a number of other non‐motor symptoms. 8 , 9

Clinical Aspects

In PD, the syndrome of depression has always received special interest. This is probably due to the clinical challenges in establishing its diagnosis and administering a treatment, as well as the intriguing scientific and research possibilities that the combination of these two syndromes provide.

Clinical challenges include establishing the diagnosis of depressive disorder, which is sometimes hard to recognize due to the considerable symptomatic overlap with the core symptoms of PD. Fatigue, loss of energy, concentration difficulties, mental slowness, loss of appetite, weight loss, and sleeping disturbances can all be symptoms of both depression and PD. This symptomatic overlap has led to the discussion of whether an inclusive, exclusive, or attributional approach regarding symptoms of depression should be followed. The DSM‐5, which is the international standard for psychiatric diagnosis and classification, explicitly follows an attributional approach asking the clinician to attribute a symptom to either the physical disease or depression. There is broad consensus among clinicians that in PD (as well as in many other diseases for that matter) such an attributional approach is simply not possible. In 2003, a National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) working group on the subject recommended deviating from the DSM‐5 approach and following an inclusive approach: every symptom that can occur in depression counts towards that diagnosis, irrespective of whether this symptom may also occur in PD. This approach is not only easier to apply clinically, but also more in line with the definition of depression as a syndrome, which is defined as a constellation of symptoms irrespective of its etiology. 10 The same working group also de‐emphasized ‘loss of interest’ as a criterion, to avoid overlap with apathy.

The severity of depression can be reliably assessed in PD patients with commonly used depression rating scales, such as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, the Beck Depression Inventory, and the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale. These, and most other commonly used rating scales, have been specifically validated in PD populations. Adjustment of cut‐off values is recommended to compensate for the symptomatic overlap and increases validity and reliability in this patient group. 11

It is important to distinguish depression from other syndromes that may occur in PD, such as apathy and demoralization, which share a number of their symptoms with depression. Apathy is a condition characterized by lack of motivation, initiative, and perseverance with decreased interest in new things or even one's own health. 12 It may be part of depression but can also occur on its own. More recently, the syndrome of ‘demoralization’ has been described in PD, characterized by profound hopelessness, helplessness, feelings of being overwhelmed, and unable to cope effectively with these feelings. 13 Another syndrome that may be mistaken for depression is pseudobulbar affect characterized by uncontrollable episodes of crying triggered by emotionally trivial or neutral stimuli that are incongruent with the patient's emotional experience. These syndromes require different approaches and have different prognoses.

The course and prognosis of depression in PD may be different from that in the general population. PD as a risk factor for depression will not disappear over time. Instead, with increasing disease duration and progressing disability, the risk of depression increases over time. PD, being a progressive disease, requires continuous adaptation to an ever‐increasing disability. As such, coping with the disease constitutes a greater challenge for PD patients than in many other physical diseases. The increased disability may lead to impairment in fulfilling social roles such as employment, household management, friendships, and other relationships. The resultant social isolation and loneliness is not only a risk factor for depression, but also for cognitive decline and overall mortality. 14 Given the impact of depression on disability and quality of life, experts suggest proactively screening PD patients for depression in order to enhance recognition and improve treatment, for example, by asking patients to complete a depression questionnaire prior to each visit. 15 , 16

From a treatment point of view, the multidimensional character of PD and the partially overlapping pathophysiology makes selective pharmacological targeting of depression without affecting motor or other neuropsychiatric symptoms of PD difficult. In addition, access to adequate care is often hampered by the lack of mental health specialists with specific expertise and experience in the field of depression in PD.

It is known that dopaminergic medication, prescribed to treat motor symptoms, may affect mood. Dopamine agonists and monoamino oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) may improve mood, while levodopa may have an unpredictable, either positive, neutral, or negative, effect on mood. 17 Pramipexole has been proven effective in reducing depressive symptoms in PD, 18 but with a small effect size. However, increasing pramipexole to levels above those necessary for the optimal treatment of motor symptoms is not recommeneded, because of the risk of overtreating motor symptoms and side effects such as psychosis, impulse control disorders, and sedation. There is some evidence from mostly small‐scale clinical trials that commonly used antidepressants, such as selective serotonergic reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin and noradrenalin reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are effective in reducing depression, with comparable effect sizes across drug clusters, but none of these treatment options are ideal. 19 , 20 SSRIs and SNRIs may theoretically worsen tremor and parkinsonism, though in clinical practice this seldom poses a problem. 21 TCAs have anticholinergic properties that on one hand may improve tremor, but on the other hand may worsen cognition and increase the risk or severity of hallucinations. TCAs with the strongest anticholinergic properties, such as amitriptyline, should therefore be avoided. In therapy‐resistant cases of depression, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) can be considered as well. ECT is safe and effective in treating depressive symptoms in PD, and may have beneficial effects on psychosis and motor symptoms as well, although cognitive side effects are sometimes reported. 22 , 23 , 24 In spite of the potential limitations of pharmacological treatments and ECT, in principle, the same algorithm for stepwise treatment of depression can be followed in PD patients as in the general population. 22 , 25 However, more attention should be given to known side effects, for which PD patients may be more vulnerable. 26 Because of these potential side effects, psychotherapy is often considered the treatment of choice, in line with the American Psychological Association (APA) guideline for major depressive disorder. 27 Evidence from small‐scale trials shows that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), both in‐person and virtually by telephone or video, and interpersonal therapy are effective in treating depression. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Unfortunately, psychotherapeutic treatment is often not available or reimbursed, and it requires motivation and active participation by the patient. There is also some evidence that physical activities such as aerobic exercise, tai chi, and mindfulness may improve mood in non‐depressed PD patients. 32 , 33 , 34

Neurocircuitry of Emotion in PD

From a research perspective, the combination of PD and depression provides a unique opportunity to gain a better understanding of our emotion regulation pathways and the role of dopamine in these circuits. The emotion regulation pathway is complex and extensive. Apart from the well‐known limbic cortico‐striatal‐thalamo‐cortical (CSTC)‐loop, there are many additional structures providing input to this loop, such as the prefrontal cortex, the hippocampus, cingulate gyrus, the amygdala, the ventral tegmental area, substantia nigra, nucleus accumbens, and so forth. 35 , 36 Several pathways within this network are dopaminergic pathways that are affected in PD. Studying these may clarify the role of dopamine in emotional processing and depression. 37 However, dopamine is not the only neurotransmitter relevant for mood regulation, and serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways are equally important and affected in PD as well. 38 The serotonin hypothesis for depression in PD poses that since serotonin reduces striatal dopaminergic release, reduced serotonergic activity may be a compensatory mechanism to improve motor symptoms, while at the same time constituting a risk for depression. This is supported by reports of upregulation of the 5‐hydroxytryptophan transporter (5‐HTT) in the raphe nuclei, limbic, and frontal structures (that reflect lower extracellular serotonin levels), 39 , 40 although no evidence for this theory was found in an experimental study. 41 Another theory focuses on overactivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis with overproduction of cortisol due to loss of negative feedback inhibition of the hypothalamus and pituitary. 42 This is a known mechanism involved in depression but has also been proposed as a risk factor for developing neurodegenerative diseases such as PD. 43 According to this theory, early life adversity could lead to both depression and PD, being a common risk factor for both syndromes. Deep brain stimulation (DBS), especially of the subthalamic nucleus (STN), has also increased our knowledge of mood disturbances in PD. In early reports of emotional side effects of STN‐DBS, it was shown that unintentional stimulation of the substantia nigra could lead to acute and transient depression, whereas stimulation of the anteroventral area of the STN may cause acute and transient hypomanic behavior. 44 These nuclei were previously considered pure motor nuclei, but it is now acknowledged that these structures have a functional subdivision and play a role in emotion regulation as well. As a result, placement of DBS electrodes is now more dorsolateral in the STN and these emotional side effects are much less frequent.

Leaving the syndromal approach, emotional processing itself is changed in both depression and PD. One of the core features of depression is altered emotional processing in which depressed patients are less sensitive to positive experiences and focus more on negative experiences. From experimental studies we know that emotion recognition for both verbal and visual stimuli is decreased in PD, even in the absence of depression. Contrary to depression in non‐PD patients, this deficit appears to be greater for negative stimuli than for positive stimuli. The ventral regions of affective neurocircuitry, including the orbitofrontal cortex, appear to play an important role in this process. These structures are more strongly dependent on dopaminergic neurotransmission than the dorsal structures of affective neurocircuitry, which are more related to the cognitive and regulatory aspects of emotion and which appear to remain largely intact in PD patients. 37 However, the brain is adaptive, and even after having contracted the disease, Parkinson‐related changes in certain structures involved in emotional processing, such as reduced activity in the putamen, may be compensated by changes in other structures, such as increased prefrontal activity. 45 Altered emotional processing affects the way patients deal with adverse life events. Although there is no direct comparison between PD patients and the general population, the risk of depressive disorder after a major life event appears to be greater in PD patients than in non‐PD patients. 46 Altered emotional processing may also contribute to alexithymia, which in addition to impairment in affective emotional processing also includes impairment of cognitive emotional processing. 47

Future Perspectives

Clinical research can focus on the etiology and pathophysiology of moods swings in PD, which may or may not be related to on/off fluctuations. Another research topic can be the way depression and other neuropsychiatric syndromes influence the effectiveness of treatment for motor symptoms, disease course, and the onset of cognitive symptoms.

Experiential research into the subjective experience of symptoms and their treatment may provide insight regarding patients' needs and wishes. From the field of psychiatry, ‘ecological momentary assessments’ (EMA), also known as the ‘experience sampling method’ (ESM) is increasingly used to study motor and affective symptoms and their fluctuations in PD. The patient downloads an app on his smartphone that is programmed to generate a specific number of beeps per day at semi‐random moments for a number of days. At these moments the patient is requested to answer a number of questions on the actual experience of motor and affective symptoms, and context. Such an approach makes fluctuations in mood (and other) symptoms visible as well as their association with other (motor, cognitive) symptoms and contextual factors. 48 , 49 , 50 If data are collected for a longer time frame it may also reveal longitudinal and potentially causal associations between symptoms. 51 Such an approach helps us to understand coherence between symptoms, which may in turn affect treatment. Given the fact that on/off fluctuations are the main indication for DBS, EMA could also be useful in quantifying the effect of DBS treatment on these fluctuations. Moreover, the method could also be useful in the development of ‘closed loop’ or adaptive DBS. Such treatment is aimed at optimal motor control, which does not necessarily imply optimal subjective wellbeing. 52 Information on the patient's subjective wellbeing generated by EMA could be included in the automated adjustment of stimulation parameters in order to optimize patient wellbeing.

Neurophysiological and neuroimaging research may increase our understanding of the physiology and pathophysiology of alterations in mood processing necessary for developing transdiagnostic interventions. Recently, studies of local field potentials (LPFs) in STN DBS showed a resting‐state increase in alpha power and a decrease in theta power in depressed PD patients. 53 This increase in alpha power was positively correlated with the severity of depressive symptoms, while theta power was negatively correlated with depressive symptoms. 54 Also, compared with high‐frequency stimulation (130 Hz) in STN DBS, stimulation with low frequency (<80 Hz) increased mood and in an experimental setting also increased the positive ratings of negatively charged emotional stimuli. 55 Wang et al. (2023) found a relationships between stimulation site and affective rating: arousal ratings increased with stimulation from anterior to posterior, and positive valence ratings increased with stimulation from dorsal to ventral. 56 Even though the meaning and relevance of these findings are still obscure, cumulative evidence may lead to new insights into emotional processing at a cellular and molecular level. Neuroimaging studies as well have so far not revealed consistent alterations in depressed PD patients compared with non‐depressed patients. Studies into functional neurocircuitry involved in mood regulation and depression, as well as alterations after successful treatment of depression, may provide new insights in mood pathophysiology. For instance, although not yet shown for depression, successful CBT for anxiety in PD patients affects neuronal pathways, especially reinforcing corticothalamic pathways which are thought to strengthen cognitive control over emotion. 57 Another potential is the use of machine learning on big multimodal databases, such as the expansion of the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) of The Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research to include 100,000 patients, which through non‐attributional analyses may identify associations not suggested by hypothesis‐driven studies.

With respect to novel treatments, no studies on the treatment of depression with esketamine in patients with PD have yet been published. There is some evidence that treatment with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is effective in reducing depression in PD, as well as in improving motor and cognitive symptoms, but its effect on depression may be smaller than that of treatment with an antidepressant. 58 , 59 There is weak evidence that transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) may be effective in reducing depressive symptoms in PD. 60 Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) has been studied in PD as a potential treatment for motor symptoms, but not as a treatment for depressive symptoms. 61 Invasive treatments such as DBS for depression or FUS lesioning are effective in the treatment of depression in the general population, but may not be preferred for use in PD because it is uncertain whether this might interfere with future DBS for motor symptoms. Increased knowledge about these novel treatment methods may lead to adjustments in treatment guidelines.

Conclusions

In the past decades, the awareness of depression in patients with PD among clinicians has increased and experts now recommend periodic screening for depression as part of routine care in PD. The diagnosis of depressive disorder can be reliably made using adapted diagnostic criteria and validated rating scales. Treatments commonly used in the general population, such as CBT or interpersonal therapy, but also pharmacological treatments with SSRIs, SNRIs, and TCAs, have been proven effective in PD patients as well. Given these advances, and in spite of the complex interrelation of mood and motor symptoms in PD and the need for research to optimize management of depression, the basic requirements for adequate diagnosis and treatment have largely been established. A problem is the lack of mental health specialists with specific expertise and experience in the field of depression in PD, which may limit access to adequate care. The further development and application of telehealth may be helpful in facilitating access.

From a scientific standpoint, the study of mood processing and mood disorders in PD patients remains as interesting and necessary as it always has been. New research methods using local field potentials in DBS, neuroimaging, and machine learning will increase our knowledge and insight in mood processing and mood disturbances, not only in PD, but also in a general sense. This may in turn change our views on diagnosis and treatment and possibly the development of preventive strategies.
