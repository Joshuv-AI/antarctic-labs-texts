The addition of encapsulated Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecium, and Lactobacillus plantarum postbiotics, either alone or with inulin, improves slaughter weight, gut function, and microbiota in broilers
Authors: Helin Atan Çırpıcı, Figen Kırkpınar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostbiotics have emerged as innovative feed additives that can replace antibiotics and probiotics. Postbiotics derived from distinct probiotic species may exhibit varying mechanisms of action due to differences in their composition. Moreover, the combined effects of encapsulated postbiotics and inulin on gut function, microbiota composition, and nutrient digestibility in broilers have not been comprehensively investigated. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of supplementing a basal diet with different encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, on slaughter and digestive organ weights, small intestinal morphology, cecal microbiota and short-chain fatty acid concentrations, as well as nutrient digestibility in broilers. A total of 588 one-day-old male broiler chicks (Ross 308) with similar initial body weights were randomly assigned to seven dietary treatments. Each treatment included six replicates, with 14 birds per replicate. The experimental diets consisted of a basal diet (C), the basal diet supplemented with 0.3% encapsulated postbiotics derived from Bacillus subtilis (EBS), Enterococcus faecium (EEF), or Lactobacillus plantarum (ELP); and combinations of these encapsulated postbiotics with 1% inulin: EBSI (EBS + inulin), EEFI (EEF + inulin), and ELPI (ELP + inulin), fed for six weeks.ResultsEncapsulated various postbiotics and inulin, increased slaughter weight, feed intake and the relative weights of the duodenum and jejunum + ileum compared with the C (P < 0.05). In contrast, the relative weights of the proventriculus, gizzard, colon, and cecum were unchanged (P > 0.05). Villus height, villus width, crypt depth and villus height/crypt depth in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum improved with encapsulated postbiotics (P < 0.05). Cecal Lactobacillus spp. and acetic, butyric, and propionic acid concentrations increased, while Escherichia coli levels reduced (P < 0.05). No significant differences in organic matter, protein, or fat digestibility were found (P > 0.05). Moreover, although no significant difference was observed between the groups with and without inulin supplementation, an improvement was noted (P > 0.05). ConclusionsEncapsulated different postbiotics (particularly those derived from Bacillus subtilis and Lactobacillus plantarum), either alone or combined with inulin, positively impacted broiler slaughter weight, gut function, and microbiota. These results support the theory that they could be used as potential feed additives in broiler chickens.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis, Encapsulated, Enterococcus faecium, Inulin, Intestinal morphology, Lactobacillus plantarum, Microbiota, Postbiotic, Short-chain fatty acids

Background

For many years, antibiotics and probiotics have been utilized as feed additives to improve growth performance, regulate the gastrointestinal (GIT) microbiota, and strengthen the immune system of broiler chickens [1]. Nevertheless, the intensive utilization of antibiotics has resulted in the presence of residues in animal products and the emergence of antibiotic resistance in pathogens [2]. Consequently, the use of antibiotics to improve growth performance has been prohibited [3]. Moreover, the challenges associated with the stability and storage of probiotics, which are used as antibiotic alternatives, along with advancements in feed and feed additive technology, have prompted the search for new feed additives.

Postbiotics have come into focus with the discovery that the metabolite byproducts and components released by probiotics have mechanisms of action similar to those of probiotics [4, 5]. Postbiotics may include byproducts such as metabolic cell-free supernatants, enzymes, amino acids, proteins/peptides, extracellular polysaccharides, cell wall components, short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), organic acids, vitamins, bacteriocins, neurotransmitters, lipids, biosurfactants, and flavonoids [6, 7]. The composition of postbiotics can vary depending on the production method as well as on the genus and species of probiotics from which they are derived [6]. Humam et al. [8] reported that the addition of postbiotics derived from different Lactobacillus plantarum strains to the finisher diet of broiler chickens under heat stress resulted in improvements in growth performance (R11; body weight, body weight gain and feed conversion ratio), intestinal structure, microbiota composition, and SCFA concentrations. Nevertheless, the effects were inconsistent across the different strains, leading the researchers to conclude that the composition and mechanisms of action of postbiotics may differ depending on the specific strain used. Therefore, in this study, postbiotics derived from three different probiotic species were investigated.

Bacillus subtilis is a probiotic species known for its ability to produce high levels of enzymes during fermentation. Studies have shown that postbiotics derived from Bacillus subtilis may enhance performance, microbiota, and meat quality in broiler chickens [9, 10]. Enterococcus faecium, a lactic acid bacterium that is naturally found in humans and animals, is known for its strong adaptability and ability to produce enterocin and antibacterial peptides, such as bacteriocins, during fermentation [11]. It has been reported that the inclusion of Enterococcus faecium in broiler diets enhances performance, immune response, and microbiota [12–16]. Due to their potential to produce metabolites such as bacteriocins and their effects in probiotic form, they have been used in postbiotic form in this study [17]. Lactobacillus plantarum can produce high amounts of SCFAs, protein compounds, hydroxyl fatty acids, and phenolic compounds during fermentation. For this reason, Lactobacillus plantarum is one of the most preferred microorganisms in commercial postbiotic products and scientific research. Postbiotics derived-Lactobacillus plantarum have been shown to positively influence intestinal morphology, cecal microbiota, and SCFAs levels in broiler chickens [18–22].

The poultry digestive system is characterized by its short and compact structure, which underscores the critical importance of maintaining a balanced gut microbiota [23, 24]. The close interrelationship between the gut microbiota and the host has led researchers to propose that the composition of an individual’s gut microbiota is as unique as a fingerprint [25]. The gut microbiota plays an fundamental role in shaping intestinal morphology and preserving the integrity of the gut barrier. This function makes it a key determinant of GIT health while protecting against pathogenic microorganisms [26, 27]. In broiler chickens, the population dynamics of the gut microbiota are directly associated with growth performance and overall health [28, 29]. Factors such as species, environmental conditions, housing systems, and dietary composition can significantly influence the structure and diversity of the gut microbiota [30, 31]. Recent research has shown that supplementing broiler diets with postbiotics can effectively alter the gut microbiota, leading to better health outcomes and improved growth performance [4, 5, 8, 9, 20, 29]. Enhanced gut morphology, characterized by increased villus height and a higher villus-to-crypt ratio, improves nutrient absorption efficiency, thereby enhancing FCR and increasing slaughter weight [4, 5, 8]. Inulin, a well-known prebiotic, may complement this effect by serving as a substrate for beneficial gut bacteria. When inulin reaches the cecum, it is fermented by certain members of the gut microbiota, particularly those associated with positive effects on the host, such as Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp., stimulating the production of SCFAs. This process suppresses pathogen growth, strengthens the beneficial bacterial population, and contributes to a more stable and resilient gut microbiota [32, 33]. The synergistic effect of postbiotics and inulin may further enhance nutrient digestibility by improving enzyme activity and strengthening intestinal barrier function [34]. However, not all studies have reported significant effects of postbiotics. For instance, Chang et al. [35] found that postbiotic supplementation did not significantly alter Lactobacillus abundance in the cecum. Similarly, Jansseune et al. [36] reported that under a standard diet (SD), neither probiotics nor postbiotics significantly affected cecal microbiota composition or diversity. In contrast, under a challenging diet (CD), both additives influenced the relative abundance of multiple operational taxonomic units (OTUs), with Postbiotic additionally affecting β-diversity indices. These results suggest that the effects of postbiotics are context-dependent and may be influenced by factors such as bacterial strain, dosage, diet composition, species, age, and physiological status of the birds. Considering both positive and negligible effects provides a more balanced perspective on the current literature and indicates that postbiotic efficacy is most pronounced under suboptimal or stressful conditions.

Microencapsulation is a technique designed to protect sensitive substances, such as enzymes and amino acids, by preserving their quality while also enabling the creation of materials with enhanced, valuable characteristics [37]. This method has become increasingly popular in the formulation of feed additives, as it enhances product profitability and extends shelf life, ensures the controlled release of active ingredients, and shields these substances from environmental influences. Several studies have indicated that microencapsulation boosts the effectiveness of feed additives, such as probiotics and prebiotics, in broiler chickens [38, 39]. Consequently, the postbiotics used in this study were encapsulated.

Previous studies have shown that supplementing broiler diets with postbiotics can improve gut microbiota by increasing beneficial bacterial populations (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) and inhibiting pathogenic bacteria; moreover, it can exert positive effects on health, slaughter weight, and intestinal morphology. Although several studies have investigated the effects of postbiotics from individual microbial species, comparative studies on the mechanisms of action of postbiotics derived from different microbial species remain limited. Encapsulation has been reported to enhance the stability and bioavailability of postbiotics [40]; moreover, due to their complementary mechanisms postbiotics modulating gut microbiota and immune responses, and inulin serving as a fermentable substrate for beneficial bacteria the combined effects of postbiotics from different microbial species with inulin on broiler performance and gut health remain to be evaluated. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of incorporating encapsulated postbiotics (0.30%) derived from Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecium, or Lactobacillus plantarum, alone or in combination with inulin (1.0%), on slaughter weight, feed intake (FI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), the relative organ weight of the digestive system, intestinal morphology, cecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations, microbiota composition, and nutrient digestibility in starter and finisher diets of male broiler chickens, and was thus aimed at providing a clear understanding of these effects.

Materials and methods

This study complied with specific guidelines approved by the Farm Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Agriculture at Ege University, Türkiye (Approval No. 2022/001).

Preparation of feed additives

Postbiotics (encapsulated) derived from Bacillus subtilis (DSM 5611), Enterococcus faecium (NCIMB 10415), and Lactobacillus plantarum (DSM 1055), along with inulin, were used as feed additives. Probiotic stock cultures were prepared at the Hektaş High Technology Center Laboratory (Ankara, Turkey). Postbiotic production and encapsulation were performed as described by Atan Çırpıcı and Kırkpınar [17]. Briefly, probiotics were cultured in appropriate media and incubated at 30 °C for 48 and 24 h, respectively, followed by centrifugation and filtration (0.22 μm). The encapsulation process for postbiotics was carried out at the R&D Laboratory of the Hektaş High Technology Center using a revised method by Reineccius [41] and Gouin [42]. Postbiotics were encapsulated at room temperature using 0.5 M CaCl2and 2% w/v sodium aliginate. After being filtered, the encapsulated postbiotics were allowed to dry naturally for one hour and stored at 4 °C until use [34, 43]. Inulin was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (No. I2255). The dose of the feed additive (postbiotic and inulin) was determined based on the study conducted by Kareem et al. [34]. Postbiotics at a level of 0.3% and inulin at a level of 1% were added to the basal complete feeds. 0.3 kg postbiotic + 1 kg inulin was added to every 100 kg of basal complete feed or 3 kg postbiotic + 10 kg inulin was added to every 1000 kg of basal complete feed. The final postbiotic concentration is 0.3% and inulin concentration is 1%.

Experimental design and treatments

A total of 588 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks (average initial weight: 40.85 ± 0.56 g) were randomly allocated to seven different dietary treatments. Each treatment consisted of six replicates (per replicate: 14 birds). The experimental groups included a control receiving only the basal diet (C) and six groups supplemented with 0.30% encapsulated postbiotics derived from Bacillus subtilis (EBS), Enterococcus faecium (EEF), or Lactobacillus plantarum (ELP), either alone or combined with 1.0% inulin (EBSI, EEFI, ELPI). The dose of the feed additive (postbiotic and inulin) was determined based on the study conducted by Kareem et al. [34]. All birds had ad libitum to feed and water throughout the 42-day trial. A granulated starter diet (days 1–21) and a finisher diet (days 22–42) were provided, with formulation details presented in Table 1. All feed additives were thoroughly blended into the basal diets using a premixing process to ensure homogeneity. The ambient temperature started at 32 ± 1 °C and was gradually decreased to 24 ± 1 °C. Lighting was maintained on a 23-hour light and 1-hour dark cycle, and humidity was kept at 65%. Each pen (1.4 m × 1.2 m) was equipped with wood shavings, a round hanging feeder (AyTav, 10 kg capacity, Ø 44 × h 49 cm, product code 302409), and four to five hanging nipple drinkers (AyTav, product code 302200), which were supplied with water from a central reservoir.

The feed intake (FI) and body weight (BW) of broilers were recorded throughout the experimental period (days 1–42). These data were used to determine body weight gain (BWG) and to calculate the FCR.

Data collection and relative weights of the digestive organs

On day 42, the chickens were weighed as a group, and 12 animals with an average body weights were selected from each treatment group, with two birds taken from each pen replicate. They were individually weighed using a precision scale (± 1 g) to determine slaughter weights. After an 8-hour feed and water withdrawal, the birds were humanely euthanized by severing the jugular vein without prior anesthesia. This method was selected for its rapidity and effectiveness in minimizing distress. The digestive organs, including the proventriculus, gizzard, duodenum, jejunum + ileum, colon, and cecum, were carefully excised emptied of their contents, and weighed to assess true tissue development. The relative weights of the organs were calculated using the following formula:

Relative weights of the organs (%) = organ weights/slaughter weight × 100.

Intestinal morphology

To determine the small intestine morphology (villus height [VH], villus width [VW], and crypt depth [CD]) of the slaughtered animals, a 5 cm section was taken from the middle part of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum after the intestinal contents were emptied. The sections were washed with physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl) and then fixed in 10% formalin buffer solution [44]. The fixed tissue samples were trimmed and placed into metal cassettes before being embedded in paraffin. Here, 4-µm tissue sections were prepared via a microtome device (Leica, RM2155, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were subsequently placed on a glass slide (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E). The stained samples were examined under a light microscope (Zeiss Scope A1, Oberkochen, Germany), and images were captured with a ZEISS AxioCam 208 color camera (Oberkochen, Germany) for morphometric analysis. In each group, 20 villi were evaluated. VH, VW, and CD were measured, as shown in Fig. 1.

Microbiota in the cecum

Immediately after slaughter, the caeca contents were rapidly collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until the levels of Lactobacillus spp. and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were determined using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) [8, 45]. The determination of cecal microbial populations followed the procedures described by Navidshad et al. [45] and Humam et al. [8]. DNA isolation was performed using the Qiagen QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Hilden, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The concentrations and purity levels of the DNA extracted after isolation were determined using a Nanodrop device (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA).The primers used for E. coli and Lactobacillus spp. are listed in Table 2.

The qRT-PCR assays were performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Each 10 µL reaction contained 2.5 µL of DNA template, 5 µL of LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix, 1.9 µL of PCR-grade water, and 0.3 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µmol/L). The amplification conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s; followed by a melting curve analysis from 60 °C to 97 °C with increments of 2.5 °C every 2.5 s; and a final cooling step at 40 °C for 30 s. Data analysis was performed using the LightCycler 480 II software. The presence and quantification of microorganisms were determined from amplification curves with specific threshold cycle (Ct) values, based on a standard curve generated using plasmids with known copy numbers. The accuracy of the results was verified by melting curve analysis (Tm).

SCFAs in the cecum

Immediately after slaughter, the cecal contents were rapidly removed and shock-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and the samples were stored at −80 °C until the day of analysis to determine the levels of certain SCFAs such as acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), and butyric acid (BA). Analysis was conducted via the method described in the application note titled “Agilent Hi-Plex Columns for Carbohydrates, Alcohols, and Acids.” Agilent Hi-Plex H columns (300*7.7 mm; Organic Acid column; 8 μm particle size) were utilized for analysis. The column temperature was 45 °C. Each sample was filtered through a porous (0.45 μm) membrane with a 10-µL injection volume for 20 min at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min and diluted in ultrapure water (10 mM H2SO4; 100%). The collected data were processed via OpenLab CDS ChemStation Edition. HPLC sample peaks were identified by comparing retention times with those of organic acid standards [48]. Double injections were performed, and average peak areas were used for quantitation. The standards for AA, BA, and PA were purchased from Vetec, and they all exhibited a purity of ≥ 99%. Ultrapure water was obtained from a Mille® system (EMD Millipore), and sulfuric acid was purchased from Merck.

Digestibility of nutrients

During the starter (days 17–21) and finisher (days 38–42) periods, seven male broilers with similar body weights from each group were housed in individual metabolic cages. The total feed intake and excreta output were measured. Excreta samples were dried, ground, and stored in airtight plastic bags at − 20 °C until analysis. The organic matter, crude fat, and crude protein contents of the excreta and basal feed (starter and finisher) were analyzed according to AOAC [49] methods. Excreta and urinary nitrogen were chemically separated to determine protein digestibility. As described by Açıkgöz et al. [50], digestibility was calculated based on these measurements and chemical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7. For a one-way ANOVA with seven groups, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (1–β) of 0.80, a minimum total sample size of 588 animals was required to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25). Accordingly, 84 birds were allocated to each group (total: 588 birds). For all data, the pen was considered the experimental unit. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. Since the data showed a normal distribution, all assumptions were satisfied and data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA (IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 [51], significant differences between means were determined by Duncan’s multiple range test at p ≤ 0.05. Duncan’s test was selected for post-hoc comparisons due to its sensitivity in detecting differences among multiple groups while controlling Type I errors, particularly in analyses involving a large number of groups.

Results

Slaughter weight and relative weight of the digestive organs

The effects of different postbiotics in encapsulated form alone or in combination with inulin on the slaughter weight (g), feed intake, feed conversion ratio and relative weight (%) of the digestive organs (proventriculus, gizzard, duodenum, jejenum + ileum, colon, and cecum) of broilers are presented in Table 3. Slaughter weights were greater in all the additive–supplemented groups than in group C (P < 0.001). The highest (P < 0.001) slaughter weights were observed in the EBSI and EBS groups, followed by the ELPI, ELP, EEFI, and EEF. Although feed additives increased FI (days 1–42) (P = 0.005), no significant differences were detected in FCR (days 1–42) compared with the C group (P = 0.075). No differences in the relative values of the proventriculus (P = 0.471), gizzard (P = 0.087), colon (P = 0.368), or cecum (P = 0.885) among the experimental groups were observed. However, the relative weights of the duodenum were highest (P < 0.001) in the ELP and ELPI groups, significantly lower in the EBS, EBSI, EEF, and EEFI groups, and lowest in the C group. The highest (P < 0.001) jejunum + ileum relative weights were observed in the ELP and ELPI groups, significantly lower in the EBS, EBSI, EEF, EEFI, and C groups.

Intestinal morphology

The effects of different postbiotics in encapsulated form alone or in combination with inulin on VH, VW, CD and VH/CD in the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) of broilers are listed in Table 4; Fig. 2. The highest (P = 0.014) duodenum VH values was observed in the ELP, ELPI, EBS and EBSI groups and were significantly lower in the EEF, EEFI and C groups. It was noted that duodenum VW and CD values increased (P < 0.001) in feed additive supplemented groups compared to C group. The highest (P < 0.001) jejunum VH value was recorded in ELP and ELPI groups, while it was significantly lower in EBS, EBSI, EEF and EEFI groups and was lowest in group C. The highest (P < 0.001) jejunum VW value was observed in ELP, ELPI, and EBSI groups, while it was significantly lower in EBS, EEF and EEFI groups and was lowest in group C. The jejunum CD value of ELPI, ELP, EBSI, and EBS groups increased (P = 0.002) compared to EEFI, EEF, and C groups. Furthermore, it was noted that the EEFI and EEF groups were similar to the EBSI and EBS groups. Ileum VH (P < 0.001), VW (P = 0.016), and CD (P < 0.001) values of the feed additive-supplemented groups were higher than those of group C. Discussion

Slaughter weight is a crucial indicator of growth performance and directly influences production efficiency in broiler chickens. In this study, supplementation with encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, resulted in increased slaughter weight in broiler chickens. This finding suggests that postbiotics can positively influence broiler performance, particularly through mechanisms related to gut health and microbial modulation. Indeed, postbiotics possess antibacterial properties, including bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities, which help in combating pathogenic bacteria and inhibiting toxin production in the GIT tract [5]. In addition, postbiotics promote a healthy gut microbiome, which may enhance nutrient absorption and utilization, ultimately contributing to improved growth performance and higher slaughter weights. Previous studies have reported that postbiotics derived from various microorganisms, including Bacillus subtilis [9, 10], Lactobacillus plantarum [8, 34], Lactobacillus acidophilus [5, 19], and Saccharomyces cerevisiae [52], improved the body weight (42 day) of broiler chickens when used as feed additives. In the present study, groups supplemented with postbiotics derived from Bacillus subtilis presented higher slaughter weights than those receiving other encapsulated postbiotics. This observation may be related to the enzymatic potential of Bacillus subtilis–derived postbiotics, as this bacterial species is known to produce high levels of enzymes during fermentation. Such enzymatic activity, although not measured in this study, might enhance the functional properties of these postbiotics and contribute to their effects on growth performance. Although no statistically significant differences were observed between the postbiotic-only and postbiotic + inulin groups, slaughter weight appeared slightly higher in the postbiotic + inulin group. This suggests that inulin may have a synergistic effect when combined with postbiotics, potentially influencing growth performance positively in further studies.

In the present study, postbiotic supplementation increased FI (days 1–42), which may be related to improved nutrient utilization and gut morphology associated with their anti-inflammatory effects. However, no differences were observed between encapsulated postbiotics with or without inulin. Previous findings are inconsistent, with some studies reporting no effect [34] or an increase only during the starter phase [4]. Such discrepancies may be explained by variations in probiotic strains, production methods, or supplementation levels. In agreement with several earlier reports, encapsulated postbiotics in our study did not significantly influence FCR during days 1–42, further highlighting the inconsistency of results regarding the impact of postbiotics on broiler performance [8, 19, 32, 34].

The relative weights of the digestive organs may increase with the acceleration of metabolism [53]. In the present study, supplementation with encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, did not significantly affect the relative weights of the proventriculus, gizzard, colon, or cecum of male broiler chickens. These findings align with those of previous studies, which have consistently reported that postbiotic supplementation has no significant effect on the weights (% or g) of digestive organs [4, 8, 54]. However, contrary to these findings, Mohammed and Kareem [55] reported an increase in the relative weight of the gizzard when postbiotics derived from Lactobacillus plantarum (0.30%) were included in the basal compound feed of broilers. The small intestine is crucial for nutrient absorption and immune function, making its structural development crucial for optimal growth and overall health. The relative weights of specific small intestine segments can serve as indicators of their functional development [56]. In our study, the addition of encapsulated postbiotics increased the relative weights of the duodenum and jejunum + ileum, suggesting enhanced development of these intestinal segments and potentially greater nutrient absorption capacity. However, the increase in small intestinal weight did not correspond to a significant increase in slaughter weight, indicating that intestinal development alone may not directly determine final body weight. Other factors, such as feed intake, energy utilization, and metabolic efficiency, likely also contribute to overall growth performance. These findings highlight that while postbiotics can improve intestinal morphology, their effects on final production parameters may be influenced by multiple interacting factors. However, Li et al. [10] reported that supplementation with postbiotics produced from Bacillus subtilis ACCC 11,025 at various concentrations (0.015%, 0.030%, or 0.045%) did not affect the relative weights of the duodenum, jejunum, or ileum. These inconsistencies may reflect differences in postbiotic strains, dosages, or experimental designs.

The integrity of the intestinal morphology is crucial to animal health and performance. An increase in VH and VW enhances the absorptive surface area, thereby improving nutrient uptake and optimizing production efficiency in broiler chickens [57]. In the present study, supplementation with encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, was found to have a beneficial effect on the VH, VW, and CD values in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. These improvements can be attributed to the antimicrobial properties of postbiotics, which inhibit or kill pathogens adhering to the intestinal cell wall, thereby maintaining gut integrity. In agreement with our findings, numerous studies have reported improvements in intestinal morphology following postbiotic supplementation in broiler chickens [58, 59]. For example, Humam et al. [8] demonstrated that the addition of postbiotics to finisher diets during heat stress had a beneficial effect on intestinal morphology. Similarly, Monika et al. [5] reported that supplementing diets with Lactobacillus acidophilus–derived postbiotics improved the VH, VW, and CD values in the jejunum of slow-growing broilers on days 21 and 42. Doski and Kareem [20] reported that a combination of postbiotics derived from Lactobacillus plantarum (0.15%) and thyme oil (0.15%) positively influenced the intestinal morphology of broilers. However, no significant effects from inulin supplementation were observed in the present study.

In the present study, dietary supplementation with encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, exerted inhibitory effects on pathogenic bacteria, as evidenced by a significant reduction in E. coli counts. This suppression of E. coli was likely associated with a concomitant increase in Lactobacillus spp., suggesting a competitive exclusion mechanism. These findings align with previous reports. For instance, Humam et al. [8] demonstrated that postbiotic supplementation under heat stress conditions enhanced Lactobacillus populations and reduced E. coli in the cecum of broilers. Similarly, Bastamy et al. [29]. reported increased Lactobacillus spp. levels in the crop, ileum, and cecum contents at both 15 and 35 days of age following postbiotic inclusion. However, Soren et al. [4] observed a reduction in E. coli without changes in Lactobacillus abundance in response to Saccharomyces cerevisiae-derived postbiotics (1.25 g/kg). These inconsistencies across studies may be attributed to differences in the microbial composition, origin, or bioactive profiles of the postbiotic preparations used. The observed improvement in microbial balance may also reflect the synergistic interplay between postbiotics and inulin in modulating the intestinal environment. Postbiotics contain antimicrobial compounds such as organic acids and bacteriocins that directly inhibit pathogens, while inulin serves as a selective substrate for beneficial bacteria [60]. When fermented in the colon, inulin enhances the production of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, which serves as an energy source for intestinal epithelial cells and promotes mucosal integrity. Moreover, the reduction in luminal pH induced by SCFA production may enhance the antimicrobial activity of postbiotics, amplifying their effectiveness. This dual mode of action could explain the greater reduction in E. coli and the increase in Lactobacillus spp. levels observed in the postbiotic + inulin treatment group compared to postbiotics alone. These findings underscore the potential of combining postbiotics with fermentable fibers as a strategy to support gut health and microbial homeostasis in poultry production systems.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including AA, BA, and PA, are key end-products of microbial fermentation and play essential roles in host physiology [58]. These SCFAs are predominantly absorbed in the colon, where they contribute significantly to the host’s energy supply. Beyond their role as metabolic substrates, SCFAs regulate various physiological processes, including feed intake, energy metabolism, cell proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis, and immune function [61, 62]. Owing to their antimicrobial properties and capacity to shape microbial communities, SCFA production has been recognized as a critical indicator of gut health modulation by postbiotics. In the present study, dietary inclusion of encapsulated postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, increased cecal concentrations of AA, BA, and PA in broiler chickens. The observed differences among treatment groups were likely due to variations in the composition and concentration of postbiotic formulations derived from different probiotic strains. The highest SCFA concentrations were detected in birds supplemented with postbiotics derived from Lactobacillus plantarum (ELP and ELPI groups), likely reflecting the high SCFA-producing capacity of this strain. Supporting our findings, Kareem et al. [18] reported that postbiotics derived from Lactobacillus plantarum RG14, when combined with 1% inulin, significantly increased cecal AA levels in broilers, although no significant effects were observed on BA or PA. In contrast, Guan et al. [27] found that postbiotic supplementation did not significantly alter cecal SCFA concentrations. Additionally, the inclusion of inulin did not appear to have a significant effect on SCFA production in the present study. These inconsistencies may arise from differences in experimental conditions, postbiotic source, bacterial strain specificity, or inclusion rates.

In the present study, the supplementation of different postbiotics, either alone or in combination with inulin, did not significantly affect the DOM, DCP, or DCF during both the starter and finisher periods. However, a trend toward increased values was observed compared to the control group’s (P > 0.05). In contrast, Khayoon et al. [63] reported that postbiotic supplementation increased DDM, DCP, and DCF. The discrepancies between our findings and those of Khayoon et al. [63] may be attributed to differences in supplementation levels, postbiotic composition, or experimental conditions. Khayoon et al. [63] attributed the improved digestibility to an increase in villus height and lactic acid bacteria in the small intestine, leading to an enhanced absorption surface area and improved gut health. It is possible that the specific postbiotic formulations employed in the current study did not elicit the same magnitude of effect on gut microbiota and morphology. Although slight improvements in nutrient digestibility were observed with postbiotic supplementation, these changes translated into significant increases in slaughter weight. This indicates that nutrient digestibility alone may not be the sole determinant of growth performance, and other factors such as energy utilization, feed intake, and metabolic efficiency likely play important roles. The lack of a significant effect of inulin supplementation on nutrient digestibility is also noteworthy. While inulin is often considered a prebiotic that promotes beneficial gut bacteria, its impact can be highly context-dependent. The existing gut microbiota composition, the specific type of inulin used, and the duration of supplementation can all influence its effectiveness [32, 33]. In conclusion, while postbiotics have the potential to enhance growth performance, their effects on nutrient digestibility may not always be statistically significant, highlighting the complex interactions between gut health, nutrient absorption, and overall growth in poultry.

Conclusion

Supplementation of encapsulated postbiotics (0.30%) derived from Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecium, and Lactobacillus plantarum in the basal diet of male broiler chickens, either alone or in combination with inulin (1.0%), resulted in significant improvements in slaughter weight, feed intake, gut morphology, cecal SCFA concentrations, and microbiota composition. Postbiotics-Lactobacillus plantarum notably enhanced gut morphology, whereas postbiotics-Bacillus subtilis led to the highest slaughter weights. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups with and without inulin supplementation, a tendency for an increase was noted. These findings indicate that targeted encapsulated postbiotics represent promising alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters in broiler production.
