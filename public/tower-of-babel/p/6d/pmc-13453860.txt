A lost art—The biatrial technique for adult orthotopic heart transplantation

The biatrial technique implant is an important tool for a heart transplant surgeon.

Central Message

Although the bicaval technique is the current standard for heart transplant, technical familiarity with the biatrial technique is important for scenarios such as redo sternotomy or vena cava mismatch.

Perspective

Although the bicaval technique is the current preferred approach for heart transplantation, proficiency in the biatrial technique is an important tool for a heart transplant surgeon. Cases such as complex redo sternotomy or size mismatch between donor and recipient vena cava may necessitate a biatrial implant, and successful implant while avoiding significant atrial or valvular distortion is crucial.

Orthotopic heart transplantation remains the definitive treatment for end-stage heart failure. After its introduction by Drs Lower, Stofer, and Shumway in the 1960s, the biatrial anastomosis technique was the standard surgical approach for decades.1 However, recognition of postoperative complications—most notably atrial enlargement, tricuspid regurgitation, and conduction abnormalities—prompted the development of the bicaval technique in the late 1980s.2

The bicaval approach preserves the integrity of both donor atria and allows for direct anastomosis of the superior (SVC) and inferior vena cava (IVC), maintaining more physiologic atrial and ventricular geometry.2 Despite slightly increased operative time, the bicaval method has been increasingly adopted as standard practice in many centers and is now the generally preferred technique of choice.3 This is highlighted by the fact that the bicaval technique was used in 0.2% of heart transplants in the United States in 1997 and used in 62.0% of transplants in 2007.4 Numerous studies have also established the benefits of the bicaval technique compared with the biatrial technique, including lower rates of requiring permanent pacemakers and tricuspid regurgitation in patients undergoing heart transplants with the bicaval technique.4,5 Nevertheless, variations in outcomes persist across reports, particularly in pediatric and marginal donor populations.3

Nonetheless, performing a biatrial technique implant is an important tool for a heart transplant surgeon to have in their armamentarium for unique situations, particularly in a scenarios of a hostile redo chest or when there is significant size mismatch between donor and recipient vena cava. In patients with previous cardiac surgeries, adhesions and scarring could increase the risk of injury to structures such as the aorta, right pulmonary artery, innominate vein, and lungs when pursuing a standard bicaval heart explant and subsequent donor heart implant. In such cases, using the biatrial technique can reduce the risk of these injuries. Pursuing a bicaval anastomosis in cases of cavae size mismatch is known to lead to complications such as SVC syndrome and pseudo-gradients across the anastomosis.6 This may be especially pertinent in pediatric heart transplantation, where the biatrial technique can be used to avoid SVC stenosis.6 In this study, we highlight our biatrial heart transplant technique.

Operative Technique

Institutional review board and informed consent were not required for this study. The recipient operation typically begins only after the donor heart has been harvested and inspected on the back table at the donor hospital and deemed suitable for transplant, while following the traditional principle to try and minimize cold ischemic times to less than 4 hours.7 The recipient operation is conducted similar to other traditional cardiac surgery cases. We also place a 5-Fr left femoral arterial sheath and a 5-Fr right femoral venous sheath in patients with previous chest surgeries in case we need to institute cardiopulmonary bypass peripherally. It is important to note pulmonary artery pressures before beginning the surgery, because this can help inform appropriate inotrope and pulmonary vasodilator therapy selection when weaning off cardiopulmonary bypass. A standard median sternotomy and cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass via the distal ascending aorta and bicaval drainage is performed. Drainage can be achieved through the SVC and IVC directly or through an SVC cannula along with multistage femoral venous cannula inserted through the femoral vein via Seldinger technique. In cases in which a biatrial technique is planned and the chest is hostile (cases of redo sternotomy and prior cardiac surgeries with significant adhesions that make dissecting out structures difficult and dangerous), a single femoral venous cannula advanced into the SVC through the IVC is adequate as long as the perfusion team is adept at using vacuum-assisted drainage and managing air in the venous line. Caval tapes are placed around the SVC and IVC as able.

The recipient cardiectomy is performed in the standard fashion. The aortopulmonary window is developed and under low flow conditions, the aortic crossclamp is applied as high as possible to preserve aortic length. Caval snares are deployed. The aorta is transected at the sinotubular junction just above the coronary ostia. The aorta is carefully dissected free from the surrounding tissues back to the aortic crossclamp to allow for maximal length and mobility, with care taken to avoid injury to the right pulmonary artery during posterior dissection. Next, the pulmonary artery is transected close to the level of the pulmonic valve and the artery is separated from surrounding tissue. Next, the right atrium is entered obliquely along the free wall. This atriotomy continues caudally to the coronary sinus, with particular attention paid to stay as far away from the IVC as possible. The right atriotomy is continued cephalad to the SVC. Indwelling devices or pacing leads are transected and removed as close to the SVC as possible. The pulmonary artery catheter is externalized. The foramen ovale is then identified and incised in the midportion to enter the left atrium. This incision is carried cephalad over the dome of the left atrium to the base of the left atrial appendage and caudally to the coronary sinus orifice. Next, the left atrium is transected between the mitral orifice and the pulmonary veins to complete the recipient cardiectomy. A left ventricular vent is placed via the right superior pulmonary vein. Hemostasis is thoroughly policed and the atrial cuffs freshened, with care taken to avoid debris falling into the pulmonary veins. The donor heart preparation is performed in the standard fashion. The donor heart is inspected to ensure no surgical damage occurred during the donor heart explant. The valves are inspected to ensure there is no evidence of endocarditis or deformity. The left ventricle is examined to ensure there is no evidence of thrombus. The SVC and right atria are inspected to ensure there is no retained central venous catheter or thrombus. The interatrial septum is inspected for a patent foramen ovale; if this is found, it is closed on the right atrial side with a 4-0 or 5-0 PROLENE suture (Ethicon). The left atrial appendage is closed if it was opened during the donor procurement for a left-sided vent. The left atrial cuff is trimmed to create a smooth cuff. For the biatrial technique, a forceps is placed between the SVC and IVC, and the right atrium is incised directly posteriorly between the two to avoid injury to the sinus node (Figure 1, B). This will allow creation of the right atrial chamber to ensure appropriate biatrial configuration.

The left atrial anastomosis is performed first using a long running 3-0 PROLENE suture (Figure 2). We begin this suture line on the recipient at the left atrial cuff adjacent to the left superior pulmonary vein and through the donor atrial cuff at the level of the atrial appendage. The first few bites are performed with the donor heart outside the recipient's chest in a parachute fashion, and the heart is subsequently lowered into the pericardial space. The posterior suture line is performed and carried inferior to level of the IVC caudally (Figure 2, A). The left atrium is irrigated free of debris and the suture line is inspected and additional interrupted sutures placed as needed. The left ventricular vent is positioned in the left ventricle across the mitral valve. The anterior wall of the left atrial anastomosis is completed and the ends are tied (Figure 2, B).

Posterior left atrial suture line (A) with completed anterior left atrial suture line (B).

The right atrial anastomosis in the biatrial implant is next (Figure 3). Particular attention should be paid to ensure no twisting of the atrial geometry, because this will cause tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction. This is aided by having the assistant hold the donor SVC adjacent to the recipient SVC. If there is significant blood in the area that makes the anastomosis difficult, additional pump suckers can be placed into the SVC and IVC to aid in drainage in cases where caval snares are unavailable. We perform the right anastomosis with a long 3-0 PROLENE suture, which is begun at the inferior border of the neo-interatrial septum (Figure 3, A). This anastomosis is carried cephalad along the neointeratrial septum (a common suture line), and once cephalad, carried onto the medial wall of the SVC (Figure 3, B). To ensure appropriate spacing and length, the assistant should be holding the donor SVC next to the recipient SVC. This is then carried caudally along the right atrial free wall of the recipient. This free wall should be trimmed to length to ensure the remainder of the right atrial cuff is sized appropriately to the remaining donor heart right atrial cuff (Figure 4, A). The pulmonary artery catheter is passed through the tricuspid valve and externalized through the open pulmonary artery. The right atrial anastomosis is then completed and suture tied (Figure 4, B and C).

The donor and recipient pulmonary arteries are trimmed to ensure no redundancy in this anastomosis. The backwall of the pulmonary artery anastomosis is performed with a 4-0 PROLENE suture reinforced with a strip of bovine pericardium (Figure 5, A). The anastomosis is then completed along the anterior wall and the suture is tied.

Posterior suture line of pulmonary artery (A) and posterior (B) and anterior (C) suture lines of aortic anastomosis.

The aortic anastomosis is performed with a running 4-0 PROLENE suture reinforced with a strip of bovine pericardium. The posterior suture line is performed first (Figure 5, B), following by the anterior wall (Figure 5, C). A root vent is then placed for deairing and under low flow conditions, the aortic cross clamp is removed, thereby restoring perfusion to the donor heart.

The heart is reperfused, inotropes and pulmonary vasodilators are started and vasopressors are started to target a mean arterial pressure of 80. Hemostasis is policed and repair stitches placed as needed. Cardiopulmonary bypass is weaned and vents removed once the heart is deaired completely. Transesophageal echocardiography is used to assess the ventricular function, valvular function, and for any anastomotic issues. Atrial and ventricular pacing wires are placed. Mediastinal and pleural tubes are placed. Protamine is administered for heparin reversal. Bypass cannulas are removed, hemostasis is again policed and repair stitches placed as needed.

At our institution, approximately 7% of heart transplants are performed using the biatrial technique, with the remaining 93% being performed with the bicaval technique. Importantly, 98% of the patients undergoing biatrial heart transplant had a previous sternotomy, and 42% of these patients were undergoing a redo heart transplantation. Rates of requiring a permanent pacemaker were 15.1% in our biatrial cohort, and rates of moderate/severe tricuspid regurgitation or moderate/severe right ventricular dysfunction at 1-year posttransplant were 7.9% and 2.6%, respectively. Although the pacemaker rates in the biatrial cohort were greater than that in our bicaval cohort, the rates of tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction were similar.

Conclusions

Standard orthotopic heart transplant with bicaval technique is the gold standard for heart transplantation and should be performed when possible. In situations in which there is a hostile mediastinum from multiple previous surgeries or in which there is significant size mismatch between donor and recipient hearts, the ability to perform a biatrial implant without significant atrial or valvular distortion and with preservation of the sinus node is an important tool in the armamentarium of a transplant surgeon. Here, we present the reproducible steps of a biatrial technique implant for practicing transplant surgeons to be able to replicate this technique in cases in which the biatrial technique is needed.
