Evaluation of black soldier fly larvae frass on broiler performance and meat yield, quality, and sensory characteristics
Authors: NM Beckman, HG Walters, BJ Bench, S Cho, A Morey, J Valenta, SJ Rochell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsect meal is an alternative feed ingredient of interest due to its nutrient content and sustainability potential. Frass (BSFLF) is produced during black soldier fly larvae meal and consists of larval excreta, exoskeleton sheddings, and leftover larval feedstocks that may be a viable feed ingredient for broilers. This study was conducted to determine the growth performance and subsequent meat yield, quality, and sensory characteristics in Cobb MX × 500 broilers fed graded BSFLF inclusions. Four treatments consisted of a corn and soybean meal-based control diet and diets with 5, 10, or 15 % BSFLF. Treatments were fed across the starter (0 to 13 d), grower (13 to 28 d), and finisher (28 to 41 d) phases and were replicated with 12 pens of 25 birds. Feed intake (FI), BW gain, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were measured at the end of each phase. At 41 d, 12 birds per pen were selected for footpad dermatitis scoring and processing. After deboning, breast fillets were kept from 5 birds per pen to evaluate meat quality and sensory characteristics, including a consumer panel and electronic nose and tongue senses. Data were analyzed by ANOVA and a Tukey’s means comparison test in addition to linear and quadratic contrasts. For the overall trial period, FI was not affected (P > 0.05) by BSFLF inclusion. Body weight quadratically decreased (P = 0.008) and FCR was quadratically impaired (P = 0.018) with increasing BSFLF inclusion. For meat quality, increasing dietary BSFLF linearly increased the occurrence of mild WB (P = 0.036) and decreased fillet yellowness (P < 0.001). Frass had no effect (P > 0.05) on white striping, spaghetti meat scores, cook loss, drip loss, texture, or color. The highest overall liking by the consumer panel was for meat from birds fed 5 % BSFLF (P = 0.017). Thus, BSFLF can be fed without affecting meat quality or acceptability, but future work should better characterize its nutrient and energy utilization to prevent impacts on bird performance when fed above 5 %.Keywords: Insect meal, frass, broiler performance, meat quality, sensory characteristics

Introduction

Insect meal has recently gained interest as an alternative feed ingredient for multiple species of livestock and fish due to its high protein and fat content (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Fukuda et al., 2022), as well as its potential to upcycle nutrients from inedible biowaste to animal protein (Lopes et al., 2022). In particular, black soldier fly larvae has proven to be a valuable feed ingredient for poultry with a high metabolizable energy content and amino acid digestibility (Matin et al., 2021). Production of black soldier fly larvae meal results in a by-product, frass (BSFLF), that consists of remaining larval feed, exoskeleton sheds, and excreta after the larvae have been harvested (Yildirim-Aksoy et al., 2020). Although nutrient content of frass products are influenced by the feedstocks that the source larvae were fed, previous studies have reported BSFLF comprising 21.6 to 23.5 % crude protein, 6.3 to 8.3 % crude fat, 7.0 to 8.7 % crude fiber, and 1.3 to 2.5 % calcium (Yildirim-Aksoy et al., 2020, 2022; Adams and Koutsos, 2024). Aquaculture studies have shown that feeding dietary BSFLF to channel catfish increased feed intake (FI) and final BW when fed up to 30 % of the diet, but reduced feed efficiency, protein efficiency, and protein retention (Yildirim-Aksoy et al., 2020). In a similar study done with Pacific white shrimp, body weight gain (BWG) showed a negative quadratic trend as dietary BSFLF increased up to 30 %, though body composition was not affected (Yildirim-Aksoy et al., 2022).

To our knowledge, only one published report addresses feeding BSFLF to broilers, and these researchers found no differences in FI, BWG, or feed conversion ratio (FCR) when birds were fed 2.5, 5, and 10 % dietary BSFLF in the starter, grower, and finisher phases, respectively, indicating that BSFLF was a viable and safe feed ingredient (Adams and Koutsos, 2024). However, more data is needed to determine the efficacy of feeding BSFLF to broilers at higher inclusions, as well as its effect on processing yields, meat quality, and consumer acceptability. Therefore, the objective of this trial was to evalutate graded dietary concentrations of BSFLF on broiler growth performance, carcass and part weights and yields, as well as meat quality and sensory characteristics evaluated by a consumer panel and electronic senses. Our hypothesis was that BSFLF would not adversely influence broiler performance, meat quality, or consumer acceptance.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures involving live animals and humans were approved by the Auburn University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #2023-5256) and Institutional Review Board (protocol # 23-382), respectively.

Live performance and processing

Cobb MX × 500 male chicks (1,300 total) were acquired from a commercial hatchery (Tyson Foods, Albertville, AL) for a 41-d rearing period. Birds were housed in a barn at the Charles C. Miller Poultry Research & Education Center (Auburn, AL) in 1.52 × 1.83 m pens providing 0.11 m2 per bird. Birds were distributed among 48 pens (25 birds/pen, 1,200 total) such that group weights were within 2 % of mean group weight at placement, and any chicks deemed abnormal or unhealthy were excluded from the trial. Pens were equipped with a commercial-type feed pan and a nipple water line to provide ad libitum access to feed and water. Additionally, supplemental feed trays and jar waterers were placed in each pen for the first 7 days. Birds were provided lighting of 23:1 L:D for 0-7 d and 18:6 L:D for 8-41 d. Experimental diets (Table 1) were fed from 0 to 41 d in three phases: starter (0-13 d), grower (13-28 d), and finisher (28 to 41 d). Black soldier fly larvae frass was analyzed (University of Missouri Experiment Station Chemical Laboratories) prior to formulation for amino acids (Method 982.30 E[a-c], AOAC, 2006), crude protein (Method 990.03, AOAC, 2006), moisture (Method 934.01, AOAC, 2006), crude fat (Method 920.39 [A], AOAC), crude fiber (Method 978.10, AOAC, 2006), and ash (Method 942.05, AOAC, 2006). Results are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Amino acid digestibility coefficients were provided by the BSFLF supplier, but the expected ME value of the product was unknown. Therefore, a value of 2,375 kcal/kg was used based on several papers evaluating insect-derived ingredients (Do et al., 2020; Matin et al., 2021; Schiavone et al., 2017a). All diets were formulated to be isocaloric, and a target AMEn of 2,976, 3,042, and 3,086 kcal/kg were used for starter, grower, and finisher diets, respectively. Starter diets were pelleted and crumbled while grower and finisher diets were fed as pellets. All finished diets were sampled after manufacturing. Birds were monitored twice daily for mortality. Actual barn temperature averaged 32.78°C on d of placement and gradually decreased an average of 19.0°C over the last 5 d of the trial. Relative humidity ranged from 19.9 % to 80.6 %, with an average of 49.6 % throughout the study period.

Pen weights and FI were measured on 0, 13, 28, and 41 d to measure FI, BWG, and FCR adjusted for mortality. At 41 d, 12 birds were randomly selected from each pen (576 total) for footpad dermatitis (FPD) scores. Scores were based on a modified scale of 0 to 2 (0 = no lesions; 1 = mild lesions less than 1.5 cm; 2 = severe lesions greater than 1.5 cm) (Nagaraj et al., 2007). These same birds were then fasted overnight and processed (Fortenberry Processing Plant, Auburn, AL) at 42 d. Birds were electrically stunned and exsanguinated via jugular vein, scalded in a hot water bath at 53.9°C for 2 minutes 25 seconds, defeathered with an inline defeatherer, and bled for 4 minutes. The head, neck, and feet were removed and birds were mechanically eviscerated and placed in ice water for 2 hours. After the 2- hour chill, carcasses and fat pads were weighed and replaced on ice in a 3.3°C cooler overnight to be professionally deboned on 43 d. Parts weights (breast, tenders, thighs, drumsticks, wings, and total white meat) were recorded and part yield was calculated for each bird relative to individual live weights taken on 41 d.

Meat quality

To measure meat quality, deboned right-side breast fillets from 5 of the original 12 birds per pen were individually weighed and categorized by experienced personnel for the prevalence and severity of breast myopathies. Each fillet was visually inspected for white striping (WS), hand-palpated for wooden breast (WB), and visually and physically analyzed for spaghetti meat (SM). Myopathies were scored according to the classification system used in previous studies using a 4-point (0-normal, 1-mild, 2-moderate, 3-severe) scale for WS and WB (Kuttappan et al., 2016; Tijare et al., 2016) and absence (0) or presence (1) was used in SM determination (Che et al., 2022a). After scoring, each sample was stored in a plastic bag under refrigeration (for 24 h at 4°C) overnight. The next day, immediately after opening the plastic bags, samples were reweighed to determine drip loss, and color parameters were measured on the cranial surface of each whole breast fillet using a Minolta colorimeter (model CR-300/DP301, Minolta Corporation, Ramsey, NJ, USA) with the CIE L*a*b* color space. Drip loss was calculated as the percentage weight loss from the time of deboning to after 24 h of storage at 4°C. For cook loss analysis, whole breast fillets were placed on a raised stainless steel wire rack in a stainless-steel pan (53.02 × 32.54 × 10.16 cm; Vollrath Co., LLC, Sheboygan, WI, United States), covered with aluminum foil and cooked in a pre-heated (176.6°C) forced air convection oven (Vulcan HEC5D, Troy, OH, United States) to an internal temperature of 74°C as measured with a stainless-steel digital thermometer (Taylor 1470FS Digital cooking thermometer and Kitchen Timer, Las Cruces, NM, United States). After cooking, samples were cooled to room temperature and reweighed to determine cook loss. Cook loss was calculated as the percentage difference between the raw weight after storage and the cooked weight measured at room temperature. Subsequently, fillets were placed individually in plastic bags and stored overnight at 4°C. Two days post-deboning, cooked whole breast fillets were equilibrated to room temperature (22 ± 2°C) for 4–5 h. Texture measurements were performed on the cranial region of each fillet using the Blunt Mullenet-Owens Razor Shear (BMORS) method with a texture analyzer (Model TX-XT2i, Technologies, Scarsdale, NY, USA). The following parameters were recorded: force (N), area (N.mm), height (mm), and peak count.

Pen was considered the experimental unit, with 12 replicate pens per treatment for all performance and processing data. Myopathy scores, FPD scores, and mortality data were analyzed as percentages birds within each classification, with percentage data transformed by taking the square root of the Arc Sin value to obtain normality for statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were performed on transformed data, though raw means are also reported for reference. All data were analyzed by ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s means separation and linear and quadratic contrast statements using SAS v 9.4 (Cary, NC). Means with a P < 0.05 were considered significantly different.

Sensory characteristics

The deboned left-side breast fillet was used to assess sensory characteristics. Two fillets per pen were subjected to E-tongue (α-Astree II, Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France) and E-nose (Heracles, Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France), analyzed as ‘never frozen samples’. 30 grams of each chicken breast sample, raw and cooked forms, were homogenized with 300 mL of deionized water for 1 minute using a blender (Waring Commercial Blendor, McConnellsburg, PA, USA). Homogenized samples were then centrifuged (Thermo Scientific Sorvall Legend RT + Centrifuge, Waltham, MA, USA) at 1,500 rpm for 20 minutes and filtered using a 1,000 mL PES 0.4 μm filter (VWR International, LLC, Radnor, PA, USA). 20 mL of filtered sample and potentiometric sensors were used for the E-tongue analysis to determine the analyte concentration of the chicken breast sample. The E-nose analysis utilized a concentrating trap for volatile organic compounds of ground sample with subsequent flash gas chromatography. Results were analyzed in a Principal Component Analysis (AlphaSoft Software).

The remaining 3 left-side breasts were stored at −80°C to minimize distortion of the meat sensory profile (Sujiwo et al., 2025) until further analysis by a consumer panel (n = 99) to assess color, texture, aroma, taste, and flavor. Panelists were recruited from Auburn University’s faculty, staff, and student body as well from the local community (Auburn, AL, USA) to assess oven-baked chicken breast at the AU Sensory Lab. Fillet samples were oven-baked with an internal temperature probe (Viking Ranges, Greenwood, MS, USA) at 191°C until the internal temperature reached 74°C. Samples provided were cut from the middle third of the chicken breast, trimmed to ensure consistency in size and shape, and labeled using a random three-digit code. Panelists were placed in an individual sensory cubicle with white, incandescent lighting, and asked to evaluate overall liking, chicken flavor intensity, and texture intensity on a 9-point hedonic scale (1 = extremely dislike, bland, or tough, respectively; 9 = extremely like, intense, or tender, respectively). Off-flavor intensity was evaluated on a 6-point scale (1 = extreme off-flavor, 6 = none detected). These attributes were used previously in a similar study (Jessup et al., 2025). Unsalted saltine crackers and room temperature water were provided to cleanse palates between samples. Lastly, panelists answered demographic questions including age, gender, education, ethnicity, annual household income prior to tax, and consumption rate of chicken breasts. All answers for the consumer panel were recorded into RedJade Software (RedJade Sensory Solutions, LLC, Pleasant Hill, CA, USA) and sensory data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with treatment as the fixed effect and consumer panel as a random effect using XLSTAT (Version 2023.0 1415; Lumivero, Denver, CO).

Results

Feed intake (Table 2) was not significantly influenced by BSFLF inclusion during the starter phase or the overall trial period, but from 0 to 28 d, FI linearly decreased (P < 0.05) as dietary BSFLF increased. Body weight and BWG linearly decreased (P < 0.001) in the starter phase and from 0 to 28 d as BSFLF increased. In the overall trial period, BWG quadratically decreased (P < 0.05) from 3.63 kg in the control group to 3.39 kg when BSFLF was fed at 15 %. During the starter and 0 to 28 d periods, FCR linearly increased (P < 0.05) as dietary BSFLF increased. For the overall trial period, BSFLF inclusion quadratically (P < 0.05) increased FCR, with birds fed 15 % BSFLF having a 7-point higher FCR than those fed the control diet.

Results of WB, WS, and SM myopathy scoring as well as texture characteristics are shown in Table 5. Mild WB occurrence linearly increased (P < 0.05) with increasing BSFLF inclusion from 13.3 % in the control group to 21.4 % in the 15 % BSFLF group, whereas severe WB linearly decreased (P < 0.05) from 10.0 % in the control group to 1.7 % in the 15 % BSFLF group. Water holding capacity characteristics and color measurements are shown in Table 6. No differences in drip or cook losses were observed (P > 0.05) among treatment groups. Although the b* (yellowness) value of the breast fillets linearly decreased (P < 0.001) with increasing BSFLF inclusion, the L* (lightness) and a* (redness) value did not differ (P > 0.05) among treatment groups.

Discussion

The search for sustainable feed ingredients has led to interest in the use of insect meals, especially black solider fly larvae meal, as a way to upcycle inedible feedstocks into high quality animal protein. The production of insect meals can result in various co-products, including frass. The nutritional composition of BSFLF is highly dependent on the nutritional density of the feedstocks provided during larvae production; hence, the quality and economical value of the frass by-product may vary according to production practices (Lopes et al., 2022). The BSFLF utilized in this study contained 22.48 % CP, with 0.85 % lysine, 0.25 % methionine, 0.73 % threonine, 1.01 % valine, 0.90 % arginine, 1.28 % leucine, and 0.78 % isoleucine. The protein content and amino acid profile of BSFLF is considerably less than protein sources that are frequently used such as soybean meal. As a percentage of CP, BSFLF contains 0.85 percentage points more lysine than corn. Methionine, valine, arginine, and leucine concentrations, however, were greater in soybean meal and corn than BSFLF. The BSFLF used also contained 5 % moisture and crude fat, 10.90 % crude fiber, and 8.57 % ash of which 2.69 % was calcium. Thus, incorporating BSFLF at graded levels up to 15 % allowed for partial replacement of corn, soybean meal, and limestone, but additional fat to maintain calculated ME levels across the diets as well as adjustments in feed-grade amino acid inclusions.

Regarding the impact of BSFLF on performance, body weight quadratically decreased as BSFLF increased; however, the impact of the 15 % BSFLF diet relative to the control diet was greater from 0 to 13 d (8 % reduction) compared to the weight difference seen in the overall period (6 % reduction). This was due to the somewhat diminishing effect of BSFLF on BWG during the grower and finisher phases. Indeed, when BSFLF was fed up to 10 % there were no differences in BW for the overall period. The feed intake response to BSFLF inclusion was not significant in the first 13 d nor in the overall trial period; however, cumulative FI between 0 and 28 d linearly decreased as dietary BSFLF increased. One possible explanation for variation intake is that birds fed 10 and 15 % BSFLF may not have been able to eat to their nutritional requirement in the first 28 days of the study and were able to compensate for the lost gain in the last phase of the trial. As a result of decreased BWG and no difference in FI, FCR quadratically increased as BSFLF was increased up to 15 %. In a previous study in which 2.5, 5, and 10 % BSFLF in the starter, grower, and finisher phases, respectively, Adams and Koutsos (2024) reported no influence of frass inclusion on BW or FCR. This supports the BW response up to 10 % BSFLF for the overall period in the current study, though we did observe an increase in FCR in birds fed 10 % BSFLF. While there is limited information on the use of BSFLF in broiler diets, multiple studies have been conducted in aquatic species. In Pacific white shrimp diets, BSFLF was included up to 30 % with no effect on bi-weekly BWG or mortality; however, Yildirim-Aksoy et al. (2022) did report a quadratic reduction in weight gain for the overall trial period among treatment groups. An additional study using channel catfish reported an increase in FI with a decrease in feed efficiency in groups fed 20 and 30 % BSFLF, however, the increase in FI was coupled with an increase in BWG (Yildirim-Aksoy et al., 2020). The two aforementioned papers propose that source insect feedstocks and insect-derived antinutritional factors, such as chitin, were the drivers of lost performance.

Amino acid digestibility values used to formulate with BSFLF in the current study were provided by the producer, but ME content for product tested herein was not available. Based on ME values of insect-based ingredients with similar compositions surveyed from various sources, an AMEn value of 2,375 kcal/kg (as-fed) was used in the current experiment. However, the impaired weight gain and feed conversion may reflect an overestimation of digestible amino acid content, ME content, or both, used in current diet formulations. Indeed, a study by Matin et al. (2021) demonstrated that there can be significant variation in nutrient composition and amino acid and energy availability among multiple samples of even the same types of insect-based ingredients, depending on when they are harvested. Other factors that affect the quality and composition of frass is the feedstuffs that are fed to the insects as well as the type of insect from which the frass is derived (Kagata and Ohgushi, 2011, 2012). In this current study, feedstuffs used to rear the black soldier flies included bakery meal and wet distiller’s grains with solubles. Additionally, the aforementioned digestibility studies postulated that chitin, a primary component of exoskeletons, could be the primary casue of the decrease in DM digestibility that sometimes results from feeding insect meals in broiler diets, potentially also contributing to the decreased performance observed in the current study (De Marco et al., 2015; Schiavone et al., 2017a; Gasco et al., 2019; Xiong et al., 2023). Although chitin has been considered and discussed as a possible antinutritive component of frass, there is a lack of studies to confirm this (Safitri et al., 2024), and we did not measure chitin content of the BSFLF for this current study. Future research is needed to determine amino acid and energy digestibility values with particular attention on which feedstocks were provided to the larvae and timing of harvest in the growth cycle as these two factors have been shown to affect product value and use.

Mortality linearly decreased as dietary BSFLF increased from 13 % in the control group down to 7 % at the highest BSFLF inclusion, indicating no adverse effects on livability when including BSFLS in broiler diets to 15 %. The decrease in mortality corresponds with the effect of BSFLF on BW and BWG, potentially due to an inverse relationship between growth rate and livability. Indeed, slower growing birds have been reported to have fewer incidences of mortality and lameness compared to fast growing birds at the same age (Wilhelmsson et al., 2019). On the other hand, the severity and occurrence of FPD lesions increased as dietary BSFLF increased. While we did not measure litter quality, general visual observations showed the birds fed BSFLF produced feces that appeared to be darker in color and higher in moisture, leading to more caked litter than observed in control pens. These differences in litter quality likely contributed to the increase in severity and occurrence of lesions, warranting further investigation. Additionally, FPD lesions have previously been shown to negatively affect BWG, FI, FCR, and carcass weight of broilers (de Jong et al., 2014), which may have been associated with reduced performance and yield of birds fed BSFLF in the current study. Future research should evaluate factors potentially contributing to poor litter quality in frass-fed broilers, including chitin content of the frass and its influence on DM digestibility.

Whole breast meat represents the most valuable and profitable body cut of modern meat-type chickens marketed in the United States (Baldi et al., 2020). However, over the last decade, various abnormalities in chicken breast quality have been observed in broilers, causing downgrades and significant economic losses (Petracci et al., 2019; Hammemi et al., 2024). In the current study, the incidence of myopathies ranged from 31.39 to 47.92 % for WB, 33.33 to 48.61 % for WS, and 13.33 to 22.08 % for SM fillets across severity levels and treatments. The occurrence of these myopathies are influenced by many factors such as genotype, age, sex, slaughter weight, the classification system used, and other factors (Barbut, 2019; Petracci et al., 2019; Bordignon et al., 2022). Nonetheless, the prevalence of these myopathies in the current study aligns with recent reports (Che et al., 2022b; Valenta et al., 2023). Feeding BSFLF had no effect on the occurrence of SM and WS myopathies. However, severe WB, which has the most damaging effect on consumer acceptability of breast fillets, decreased with increasing BSFLF level. The lower prevalence of severe WB samples from birds fed 10 and 15 % BSFLF level may have been partly due to the lower weight of breast fillets in these treatment groups as the relationship between fillet weight and myopathy score of WB has been previously demonstrated (Bowker et al., 2019; Valenta et al., 2023).

Additional meat quality parameters such as water holding capacity, color, and texture of breast meat were also measured in the present study due to their importance on overall consumer acceptance of poultry products. However, no differences in water retention or texture properties were found among BSFLF treatments, except for number of BMORS peaks. The relationship between the increase in the number of peaks and the severity of WB condition in cooked fillets by the BMORS method has previously been reported (Bowker and Zhuang, 2019; Wang et al., 2023). In this study, the highest number of peaks was found for the BSFLF 5 % treatment group, which also had the highest prevalence of moderate and severe WB and the highest breast fillet weight. In similar experiments evaluating the effect of insect-derived ingredient on meat quality, Schiavone et al. (2019b) and de Souza Vilela et al. (2021) observed no effect of dietary defatted and full-fat black soldier fly larvae inclusion on water holding capacity or shear force of breast fillets. Regarding color, only changes in yellowness values were observed in the current study. Specifically, the yellowness of breast fillets decreased as dietary BSFLF inclusion increased. This finding aligns with those of Schiavone et al. (2019b) and can likely be explained by the lower inclusions of corn, the primary contributor of yellow pigment to the diet, as BSFLF inclusion increased in the experimental diet.

Consumers showed a significant overall preference for breast fillet cuts from birds fed 5 % BSFLF. Meanwhile, electronic tongue senses indicated no differences among cooked or raw chicken samples from any of the four treatment groups, whereas the electronic nose senses indicated a difference among the cooked samples, but not the raw chicken samples. While specific volatile compounds are not reported for this current study it is well established that diet and cooking processes have the ability to change the aroma and flavor profiles of chicken meat samples (Brunton et al., 2002; Lyon et al., 2004). Specifically, corn-based diets can alter flavor profiles compared to other carbohydrate sources such as wheat and milo. In this study, corn was replaced as BSFLF increased, thus, potentially contributing to flavor differences and the trend of consumer overall liking towards the 5 % BSFLF chicken meat. The Maillard reaction as well as lipid oxidation that occurs during the heating process of cooking can alter and create new aromas (Brunton et al., 2002) that are more easily discernible by the electronic-nose that are not characterized by the electronic-tongue whose compounds are less volatile to chemical changes thus explaining why there was a significant difference between raw and cooked e-nose senses but not e-tongue senses. Although consumer panels are still considered the gold standard for assessing consumer preference, electronic senses can be useful in determining if certain volatile compounds are associated with particular consumer responses. To our knowledge, no previous studies have reported sensory characteristics of meat from broilers fed BSFLF, and research on the influence of other insect-derived products on sensory characteristics is somewhat conflicting. When broilers were fed black soldier fly meal up to 15 %, a consumer panel reported no differences in aroma, taste, tenderness, or overall acceptability of cooked chicken samples, though raw chicken sample aroma or taste utilizing electronic senses were not reported (Onsongo et al., 2018; Pieterse et al., 2019). In contrast, previous studies have shown that meat from black soldier fly meal fed broilers initially had a more intense chicken flavor that decreased as soon as 3 days post processing (Altmann et al., 2018). In a choice survey, “environmentally conscious consumers” were more likely to purchase breast fillets from broilers fed insect-based products when they were made aware of the potential sustainability benefits of using these alternative ingredients (Altmann et al., 2022).

In conclusion, the current study demonstrated that BSFLF can be fed to 15 % without negative impacts on broiler livability or meat quality and consumer acceptability. However, growth performance deteriorated and footpad lesion severity increased as dietary inclusion of BSFLF increased to 15 %. Therefore, this study shows that BSFLF can be safely included in broiler diets, but future work to better characterize this ingredient and its nutrient and energy availability is needed to optimize its use at meaningful inclusion rates without impacting bird performance. The influence of BSFLF on litter characteristics also warrants further investigation.
