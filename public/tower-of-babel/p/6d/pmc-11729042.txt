Prognostic accuracy of lactate and procalcitonin in addition to national early warning score in patients with suspected sepsis – A cross-sectional study in a tertiary care center
Authors: Nilanjana Das, Mukesh Bairwa, Ravi Kant, Bela Goyal, Yogesh Bahurup
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Sepsis, a major global health concern, leads to millions of deaths annually, hence the need for early and reliable prognostic tools to assess patient risk and guide clinical decision making becomes crucial. This cross-sectional study evaluated the prognostic accuracy of integrating blood lactate and serum procalcitonin (PCT) levels with the National Early Warning Score (NEWS) for predicting mortality in sepsis patients. The objective was to assess whether this lactate and procalcitonin integrated with NEWS score (LP NEWS) could serve as a more effective early prognostic tool compared to established severity scores.Methods:Spanning 12 months, the study enrolled adult patients meeting the criteria of sepsis in the ICU and medicine ward of a tertiary care hospital in North India. Data collection included demographics, clinical characteristics, and blood samples for lactate and PCT at admission. NEWS, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and LP-NEWS scores were calculated with treatment administered per Surviving Sepsis-3 guidelines.Results:The research included 200 participants, uncovering significant correlations between blood lactate, PCT levels, and mortality. Survivors had a mean lactate of 2.12 ± 0.70 and PCT of 11.27 ± 11.75, while nonsurvivors had 3.30 ± 1.17 and 30 ± 18.48, respectively (P < 0.001). LP-NEWS significantly differentiated survivors from nonsurvivors (8.23 ± 2.02 vs. 14.12 ± 2.23), with a cutoff of 11 showing 96.9% sensitivity and 88.5% specificity for predicting mortality. LP-NEWS had the highest odds ratio = 3.12, P < 0.001, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve value (0.966), outperforming APACHE II and SOFA scores.Conclusion:The LP-NEWS score which integrates blood lactate and serum PCT levels could serve as an effective standalone bedside score, particularly in the initial risk stratification of sepsis.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis, characterized by a range of physiological and biochemical disturbances triggered by infection, represents a significant but often overlooked public health crisis. According to the latest data from the World Health Organization, approximately 48.9 million cases of sepsis are reported annually worldwide, resulting in 11 million deaths.[1] The 2021 Surviving Sepsis guidelines define sepsis as “a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by an inappropriate host response to infection,” with a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of 2 or above indicating organ dysfunction.[2]

The diverse manifestations of sepsis make its timely diagnosis and management challenging, highlighting the need for novel biomarkers and clinical scoring systems improve severity assessment and guide early interventions. Lactate, a byproduct of anaerobic metabolism, has been extensively studied as an indicator of critical illness severity over years. Lactate levels indicate tissue hypoperfusion caused by sepsis and are linked to poor outcomes, even without hypotension.[34] Recent research suggests that elevated lactate levels during sepsis may result from the stimulation of beta-2 adrenergic receptors and reduced clearance.[56] The revised sepsis guidelines categorize septic shock as a condition requiring vasopressors to sustain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg or higher and a serum lactate level exceeding 2 mmol/L, assuming no hypovolemia is present, underscoring the significance of lactate clearance in treatment approaches.[2] Besides tissue hypoxia, various factors have been identified as possible contributors to elevated lactate levels, including mitochondrial dysfunction in utilizing oxygen, a compromised pyruvate dehydrogenase function, a disparity between oxygen supply and demand, and increased aerobic glycolysis triggered by inflammation related to sepsis.[7]

Procalcitonin (PCT), another promising biomarker, aids in the early detection of bacterial infections. It is a precursor of calcitonin and is released by various cells during an inflammatory response to bacterial infection, helping to distinguish bacterial sepsis from other systemic inflammations. Its specificity for bacterial infections is valuable in guiding antibiotic therapy, as emphasized in the 2021 Surviving Sepsis guidelines. The rise in serum PCT levels correlates with the severity of infection and organ dysfunction. Consequently, it may aid in the earlier and more accurate stratification of septic patients at risk of death. The standard scoring systems, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), have traditionally been used to evaluate the severity of sepsis due to their high degree of sensitivity and specificity. However, their complexity and dependence on extensive laboratory investigations limit their bedside applicability. Developed by the Royal College of Physicians, the National Early Warning Score (NEWS) offers a simpler alternative to predict clinical deterioration in different clinical scenarios, including sepsis. This score combines seven vital parameters: temperature, systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, oxygen supply, heart rate, and level of consciousness with a score ranging from 0 to 20.[8] Although it was initially not designed for mortality prediction, recent studies have started exploring its potential in this regard, with NEWS demonstrating variable sensitivity in predicting short-term mortality among emergency department patients initiating antimicrobial therapy.[9] However, it has not been extensively studied for either the diagnosis of sepsis or as a prognostic tool for sepsis. Our study aims to investigate the combined prognostic utility of blood lactate and serum PCT when integrated with NEWS in patients with suspected sepsis, offering a potentially more easily accessible tool for early mortality risk assessment. While lactate is a mirror to metabolic derangements and tissue hypoperfusion, PCT reflects both the presence and severity of bacterial infections. By incorporating these biomarkers into a clinically pragmatic scoring system, this study aims to develop an improved bedside prognostic model that will enhance clinical decision-making and promote optimized patient-centric outcomes in the early stages of sepsis.

METHODS

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Internal Medicine at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Rishikesh, India. It included patients suspected of having sepsis in the medical wards and ICU from April 1, 2023, to April 30, 2024. The study received ethical approval from the institute’s Ethical Committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Given the insufficient evidence on PCT as a prognostic marker for mortality, we set the area under the curve at 0.6. With a target confidence level of 95% (α =0.05) and a study power of 80% (1−β), and allowing for a 10% error margin, we determined a sample size of 194, subsequently adjusted to 200.

The study included adult patients aged 18 years or older, of both sexes, who fulfilled the criteria for sepsis as outlined by the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines. Patients taking drugs affecting lactate metabolism (e.g., metformin, antiretrovirals, linezolid), or with burns, trauma, anaphylaxis, cardiogenic shock, thyroid malignancies, chronic kidney, or liver disease were excluded.

At enrolment, demographic data, including age, sex, and comorbidities, along with baseline clinical characteristics were recorded. Blood samples were collected on the day of admission to determine serum PCT levels using radioimmunoassay kits and blood lactate levels via arterial blood gas analyzers. Heparinized blood was drawn from either the radial or femoral artery and processed within 15 min of collection. A comprehensive baseline laboratory assessment was conducted for all patients. The study enrolled 200 sepsis patients, followed them over a period of 14 days, categorized them into two 2 groups: survivors (Group 1) and nonsurvivors (Group 2).

Disease severity was assessed using the NEWS, Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, and APACHE-II scores at admission and subsequent evaluations for 14 days or until mortality. Standard treatment guidelines were followed throughout the study.

A new LP-NEWS score – a combination of lactate, PCT, and NEWS scores – was devised in the present study. No additional points were added to the NEWS score for patients with a lactate level <2 mmol/L and a PCT level <2 ng/mL. One point was added for lactate levels between 2 and 4 mmol/L or PCT levels between 2 and 7 ng/mL. Two points were added for lactate levels above 4 mmol/L or PCT levels between 8 and 15 ng/mL. Finally, three points were added when PCT levels exceeded 15 ng/mL. APACHE II, SOFA, NEWS, and LP-NEWS scores were calculated at admission, while blood lactate and PCT levels were measured concurrently. The study aimed to assess the predictive power for mortality using lactate, PCT levels, and the LP-NEWS score and to compare their effectiveness with established prognostic scoring systems such as the SOFA and APACHE II. Blood samples were specifically drawn for the study, and the investigator covered the expenses of these tests.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 25 (IBM Corp., IL, USA). Categorical variables were presented as percentages and continuous variables as means ± standard deviation. We employed the Fisher’s exact test and the Chi-square test for categorical data comparison as needed, while t-tests and ANOVA were used for analyzing continuous variables where applicable. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to calculate bivariate correlations. Significant factors identified from this univariate analysis were used as input variables in the logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of mortality. The optimum cutoff level of the biomarkers and scores was determined by receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis.

RESULTS

The study included 200 patients who satisfied designated inclusion and exclusion criteria. Statistical tests and measures of effect size were utilized to examine the associations between different variables and outcomes, comparing the groups of survivors (n = 102) and nonsurvivors (n = 96). Various critical variables were analyzed to discern distinctions between these groups. First, there was a statistically significant distinction in age (survivors: 44.60 ± 15.00 years, nonsurvivors: 50.77 ± 14.25 years); however, no such distinction was found in gender (survivors: 44.2% male, 55.8% female; nonsurvivors: 54.2% male, 45.8% female, P = 0.160). The sources of sepsis were similar across both survivor and nonsurvivor groups, with respiratory infections being the most common in both (47.1% in survivors and 72.9% in nonsurvivors, P < 0.001). This was followed by infections of the genitourinary tract, which occurred in 22.1% of survivors and 9.4% of nonsurvivors, a difference that was statistically significant (P = 0.014). Gastrointestinal sources were reported in 10.6% of survivors and 5.2% of nonsurvivors, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.162). Central nervous system sources were present in 6.7% of survivors and 8.3% of nonsurvivors (P = 0.019), and catheter-related bloodstream infections were identified in 9.6% of survivors compared to 7.3% of nonsurvivors, with no significant difference (P = 0.556). Sepsis originating from skin and soft-tissue infections was observed in 8.7% of survivors and 1% of nonsurvivors (P = 0.019).

Hypertension was found to be the most common comorbidity affecting 39.5% of the population studied, with no significant difference between the survivor group (40.4%) and the nonsurvivor group (38.5%).

The prevalence of diabetes was similar between survivors (15.4%) and nonsurvivors (19.8%), showing no significant difference. However, the incidence of chronic lung diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, and others, was significantly greater in nonsurvivors (14.6%) than in survivors (4.8%), with P = 0.018. Nonetheless, there was no significant statistical disparity seen among other comorbidities. The mean Charlson Comorbidity Index in survivors (4.20 ± 1.60) versus nonsurvivors (5.42 ± 2.34) also showed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.122). The study identified statistically significant differences in the occurrence of organ failures between the groups. Acute kidney injury was present in 56.7% of survivors compared to 92.7% of nonsurvivors. Similarly, acute hepatitis was found in 10.6% of survivors and 25.0% of nonsurvivors. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) occurred in 12.5% of survivors and 44.8% of nonsurvivors, and acute encephalopathy was noted in 1.0% of survivors versus 44.8% of nonsurvivors. Significant differences were also observed in additional clinical indicators.

Nonsurvivors had a substantially higher usage of vasopressor support (survivors: 52.2%, nonsurvivors: 94.1%) and a statistically significant shorter duration of hospital stay (survivors: 10.86 ± 5.84 days, nonsurvivors: 8.81 ± 2.95 days). There were notable differences in mean lactate levels (2.12 ± 0.70 mmol/L in survivors vs. 3.30 ± 1.17 mmol/L in nonsurvivors) and mean PCT levels (11.27 ± 11.75 ng/mL in survivors vs. 30.04 ± 18.48 ng/mL in nonsurvivors). Moreover, nonsurvivors had significantly higher SOFA (survivors: 5.42 ± 1.81, nonsurvivors: 9.58 ± 2.90), APACHE-II scores (survivors: 13.50 ± 3.86, nonsurvivors: 21.68 ± 4.00) and NEWS scores (survivors: 5.94 ± 1.41, nonsurvivors: 10.16 ± 2.01). For the primary objective, the newly devised LP-NEWS score, i.e., a combination of lactate and PCT levels and NEWS, was calculated at admission. The scores varied from 4 to 19, with a mean LP-NEWS score of 11.06 ± 3.63. The mortality increased significantly when the score was > 11. In our study, mortality reached 100% for cases where the score exceeded 16. The mean of the LP-NEWS score in the nonsurvivor group (14.12 ± 2.23) was found to be significantly higher than that in the survivor group (8.23 ± 2.02) using the independent t-test [Table 1].

The regression analysis in our study was designed to investigate the relationships between various predictor variables and the outcome, divided into “survivors” and “nonsurvivors.” The analysis incorporated multiple variables. For age, the mean age was similar between the survivor and nonsurvivor groups. Univariate analysis showed a statistically significant difference in odds ratios (OR = 1.03, P = 0.004); however, this significance diminished after adjusting for other variables (P = 0.090). Gender distribution was approximately equal across both groups, and the ORs indicated no significant differences (P = 0.161), which remained nonsignificant even after adjustments (P = 0.617). For lactate levels (mmol/L), initial analysis indicated a significant difference in ORs = 6.22, P < 0.001, but this significance decreased upon adjusting for additional factors (OR = 4.22, P = 0.317). Similarly, PCT levels (ng/mL) showed an initial significant association with mortality (P < 0.001), but this association weakened after controlling for other variables (P = 0.146) [Table 2 and Figure 2].

Correlation between the NEWS score and other prognostic markers was evaluated. A significant correlation was found (P = 0.001) between NEWS and both the SOFA and APACHE II scores, with Spearman’s correlation coefficients of 0.78 and 0.76, respectively. Additionally, lactate levels showed a weak positive correlation with both SOFA and APACHE II, with Spearman’s correlation coefficient values of 0.3 and 0.49, respectively (P < 0.001). PCT levels also correlated weakly with both SOFA (rho = 0.51) and APACHE II scores (r = 0.59). However, the newly proposed LP-NEWS score showed a significant correlation with SOFA score (r = 0.71) and APACHE II (r = 0.74) [Figure 1].

ROC curves were generated for the prognostic markers and scores to determine cutoffs that optimally predict mortality with high sensitivity and specificity. A larger area under the ROC curve (AUROC) indicates a more effective prognostic marker. Among all evaluated parameters, the LP-NEWS score was the most predictive of mortality (AUROC = 0.966, 95% confidence interval = 0.942–0.991, P < 0.001). An LP-NEWS score >11.5 was associated with a mortality prediction sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 88% [Tables 3 and 4].

DISCUSSION

The prognostic implications of various clinical and biochemical parameters in the management of sepsis have been a subject of ongoing research. There are a plenty of studies available on scoring systems and markers to assess the severity of sepsis but very limited studies to predict outcomes and mortality. Our study primarily aimed at evaluating the prognostic accuracy of the NEWS when integrated with lactate and PCT measurements (LP-NEWS) for predicting 14-day mortality in patients with suspected sepsis. Additionally, the study aimed to compare the predictive value of LP-NEWS with established prognostic models, such as the APACHE II and SOFA scores. One of the primary observations was the apparent age-related difference in survival outcomes. Age has been considered a critical factor in the prognosis of many conditions, and our study emphasizes its role in the context of sepsis. The mean age of survivors in our study was 44.60 ± 15.00 years whereas 50.77 ± 14.25 years in nonsurvivors. The survival analysis showed a significant difference in the age distribution between survivors and nonsurvivors, suggesting that younger patients might have better outcomes. This could be multifactorial, including a stronger immune response and fewer comorbidities in younger individuals.

Our detailed analysis showed the comparison of comorbidities in both survivor and nonsurvivor groups. Hypertension was found to be the most common comorbidity affecting 39.5% of the population studied, comprising 40.4% of patients in survivor and 38.5% in the nonsurvivor group. It was followed by diabetes which was present in 17.5% of the patients.

The demographic profile of our study correlated well with the study by Daga et al., with mean ages in the mid-40s and a balanced gender distribution. The 14-day mortality rate in our study was 48%, which was the same as that reported by Daga.[10] The results of our study reveal that organ failures such as AKI, ALI, ARDS, and encephalopathy were all significantly associated with mortality.

The requirement for vasopressor support was also identified as a significant variable associated with mortality.[11] The association between vasopressor support and mortality reveals the importance of aggressive hemodynamic management in septic patients to mitigate the risk of organ failure and subsequent death. However, the use of prior antibiotics had no significant association with mortality. A plausible reason for this could be that while antibiotics are essential in treating the underlying infection, their effectiveness can be influenced by several factors such as the timing of administration, the appropriateness of the antibiotic choice, and the presence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens.

Further, our findings demonstrate that the levels of both lactate and PCT hold prognostic value. The outcomes of the study align perfectly with our initial expectations and strongly support our hypothesis. We hypothesized that there would be a significant relationship between the mortality rate within 14 days and the levels of blood lactate and serum PCT among patients with suspected sepsis. Our results not only affirmed this relationship but also revealed compelling patterns that strengthen our hypothesis. The significant correlation between increased lactate levels and higher mortality observed in our study aligns with results from previous research which have also demonstrated a similar association in various disease contexts.[12131415] Our study specifically examines the sepsis patient population, thereby reinforcing the significance of lactate as a prognostic marker in this context. The mean lactate level for nonsurvivors was 3.30 ± 1.17 mmol/L, significantly higher than the 2.12 ± 0.70 mmol/L observed in survivors (P < 0.001). Similarly, Daga et al. in their study observed that nonsurvivors had higher mean lactate levels (4.32 ± 2.35 mmol/L) compared to survivors (2.27 ± 0.91 mmol/L), indicating a trend where increased lactate levels correlate with worse outcomes.[9] Mortality rates in our study dramatically escalated with rising lactate levels: 4 (7.84%) of 51 patients with a lactate level between 0 and 2 mmol/L died, 74 (56.9%) of 130 patients with a lactate level between 2 and 4 mmol/L died, and 18 (94.7%) of 19 patients with a lactate level ≥4 mmol/L died. Among 96 patients who did not survive, lactate >4 mmol/L was seen in 18 patients (18.8%). The lactate level of ≥2.6 mmol/L in our study had 78% sensitivity and 79% specificity in predicting mortality. Shapiro reported 36% sensitivity and 92% specificity for lactate ≥4 mmol/L for any death compared to our finding of 100% mortality at ≥4.5 mmol/L, suggesting that our cohort experienced more severe sepsis outcomes.[12] This indicates that while lactate is a strong indicator of severe outcomes, it may not distinguish well between very high-risk groups who have extremely high lactate levels. Mikkelsen, in his study, found that serum lactate levels were significantly correlated with 28-day mortality: <2 mmol/L (reference), 2–3.9 mmol/L (OR = 2.05), and ≥4 mmol/L (OR = 4.87).[13] The ORs for lactate levels of 2–4 mmol/L and > 4 mmol/L in our study are significantly higher (OR = 15.53 and OR = 211.50, respectively) compared to those reported by Mikkelsen. This comparison highlights that while both studies found increasing lactate levels to be associated with higher mortality, elevated lactate levels may be a more critical prognostic indicator in our patient cohort. However, Mikkelsen’s study involved a larger patient population and stratified lactate more specifically, providing a more granular risk assessment. As stated earlier, although increased lactate levels indicate the severity of sepsis and increased mortality, they may not differentiate between high-risk groups when lactate levels are very high, as found in our study. This limitation suggests the need for additional biomarkers to enhance the prognostic accuracy and risk stratification in patients with severe sepsis.

Furthermore, previous research on lactate clearance as a predictor of mortality in sepsis found that patients with a lactate clearance > 10% within the first 6 h had significantly lower mortality rates in comparison to those with lower clearance.[615] The focus on lactate clearance rather than static or initial lactate levels adds an additional dimension to the assessment of sepsis severity and response to treatment, suggesting that dynamic changes in lactate may provide further prognostic value beyond static measurements alone.

On the other hand, serum PCT has been widely utilized as a biomarker of bacterial infection in the management of sepsis. Many studies have demonstrated its role in antibiotic de-escalation However, until recently, there are few studies which signifies its role as a predictor of mortality.[16171819] The significant associations found between elevated PCT levels and higher mortality rates strongly support our initial hypothesis. The mean PCT level in our study among nonsurvivors was significantly higher at 30.04 ± 18.48 ng/mL compared to 11.27 ± 11.75 ng/mL among survivors. Among patients with PCT levels between 7 and 15 ng/mL, 22.2% did not survive. This mortality rate increased sharply to 83.7% for patients with PCT levels > 15 ng/mL. Suranadi et al. used a cutoff of ≥7 ng/mL to categorize high PCT levels, reporting a 28-day mortality rate of 68.5% above this cutoff.[18] This is slightly higher compared to our study, which found a mortality rate of 59.6% for PCT levels > 7 ng/mL. Similarly, Jain et al. observed a mortality rate of 60.5% above this cutoff aligning closely with our findings.[19]

In the systematic review by Williams et al., a PCT level greater than 10 ng/mL was associated with a 25% mortality rate, demonstrating a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 68%.[20] Our study showed significantly higher PCT levels among nonsurvivors and a stark increase in mortality rates for PCT levels >15 ng/mL, which aligns with the findings of Williams et al.’s study. Thus, the significant association between elevated lactate and PCT levels at admission and increased mortality indicates that monitoring these parameters can help in the early identification of high-risk patients.

Our initial hypothesis posited that these biomarkers when integrated with scores such as NEWS would be more effective in predicting mortality than these prediction tools alone, and our results provide strong validation for this assumption. The findings of our study provide valuable insights that align with previous research and existing theoretical frameworks in the field of sepsis and mortality. Prior research has highlighted the importance of diverse biomarkers and clinical assessment tools in forecasting mortality among sepsis patients. Our research enhances this understanding by establishing distinct associations between certain variables and mortality outcomes, thereby supporting established hypotheses.

The study by Dadeh et al. demonstrated that the NEWS score, when integrated with lactate (NEWS-L), outperforms NEWS alone in predicting short-term mortality and need for critical care, highlighting the added value of lactate as a biomarker. Specifically, the findings showed that NEWS-L had a higher AUROC of 0.839 for predicting inhospital mortality compared to 0.822 for NEWS alone.[21] In our study, the L-NEWS, which similarly incorporates lactate measurements, demonstrated an even higher AUROC of 0.963 for predicting 14-day mortality. This is significantly higher than the AUROC of 0.70 reported in the Noparatkailas study for predicting 28-day mortality using a composite of blood lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L and NEWS ≥ 7.[22] Till date, no previous research has incorporated lactate and PCT together in the NEWS score for predicting mortality in sepsis. Our study hypothesized that adding PCT to the combination of lactate and the NEWS score would further enhance the prediction of mortality in patients with sepsis. Additionally, we aimed to compare the effectiveness of this integrated score with established scoring systems such as APACHE and SOFA. Our study showed that all six scores – NEWS, L-NEWS, P-NEWS, LP-NEWS, APACHE II, and SOFA – were significantly linked to mortality. When these scores were incorporated as input variables in a multivariable logistic regression model, the SOFA and LP-NEWS scores stood out as significant predictors of mortality. LP-NEWS was found to be an independent predictor with the highest OR of 3.12 (P < 0.001) compared to SOFA (OR = 2.01), APACHE II (OR = 1.53), and NEWS (OR = 1.64) alone.

Our analysis also uncovered a strong positive association between lactate, PCT levels, NEWS, LP-NEWS scores, and APACHE II/SOFA scores. Higher levels of lactate, PCT, NEWS, and LP-NEWS scores are associated with higher APACHE II and SOFA scores, all highly significant (P < 0.001). This suggests that as the severity of a patient’s condition increases as indicated by the APACHE II or SOFA scores, the NEWS score that is integrated with lactate and PCT (LP-NEWS) also tends to be higher. Furthermore, on comparing the ROC curve analysis between the variables, LP NEWS demonstrated the highest AUROC of 0.966 with a diagnostic accuracy of 92% and PPV and NPV of 89% and 97%, respectively.

Overall, our study’s findings align with previous research, underscoring the significant prognostic value of lactate levels and PCT in sepsis management. However, the demographic uniqueness of the Indian setting, with a relatively younger patient population and different comorbidity profiles, necessitates localized validation of global scoring systems. The integration of lactate and PCT into the NEWS score (LP-NEWS) offers a promising, accessible tool for early mortality risk assessment. Considering the need for two simple and easily accessible bedside tests (lactate and PCT) along with physiological parameters, this scoring system can potentially shift the prognostication process from the laboratory directly to the patient’s bedside. Moreover, the prompt availability of results could save crucial time in managing sepsis, especially in resource-constrained settings like ours. To further investigate these findings and validate this new scoring system, large-scale longitudinal studies are required.

CONCLUSION

The LP-NEWS, which incorporates biomarkers such as blood lactate and serum PCT, shows promise as an effective bedside tool for prognostic evaluation in sepsis. It may offer a better-graded prediction of mortality using the dynamism of sepsis progression. The simplicity of calculating LP-NEWS makes it a feasible alternative in resource-limited settings where the use of complex scoring systems such as APACHE II and SOFA might be challenging due to their complexity and requirement for numerous variables. Further research is needed to validate these findings in larger populations and to explore its integration into clinical practice.
