Being There: Danish Nurses' Lived Experiences of Companionship With Families in Home‐Based Paediatric Palliative Care in Regional Settings
Authors: Anne Alm, Malene Beck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPaediatric palliative care (PPC) is a relatively new field in many countries, including Denmark, where regional home‐based PPC teams were established between 2015 and 2016. While research on PPC is growing, the lived experiences of nurses providing care in the family home remain underexplored. Understanding how nurses experience this form of care is essential for developing practices that support both families and professionals in this demanding field.AimThis study asks what it is like for nurses to accompany children and families through illness, dying, and bereavement in the context of home‐based PPC.MethodA qualitative phenomenological–hermeneutical approach informed by Max van Manen guided the study. Ten nurses from the Danish regional PPC teams participated in individual phenomenological interviews. Analysis followed van Manen's holistic, selective, and detailed reflection, allowing meaning to emerge through attentive engagement with the lived experiences expressed in the interviews.ResultsThe analysis brought forward three ways in which the experience showed itself: The experience of coming close by walking alongside families; The experience of letting care arise through presence rather than action; The experience of sensing what is at stake.ConclusionsNurses' accounts show that home‐based PPC unfolds through ways of being that move between doing and being present. Their care is shaped by the ability to come close as relationships develop, to remain with families when no further action is possible, and to sense what is lived but not spoken. These forms of attentiveness draw on professional experience as well as an openness to what the situation calls for, supporting families through illness, dying, and the time that follows.Keywords: embodied care, home‐based nursing, knowledge, lived experience, Paediatric palliative care, phenomenology of practice, shared space

Background

Paediatric palliative care (PPC) is a comprehensive, multiprofessional approach to children and adolescents with life‐limiting or life‐threatening conditions, addressing physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs to enhance quality of life and support families from diagnosis to bereavement [1, 2]. PPC provides holistic support for both child and family, managing symptoms, coordinating short breaks, and ensuring coherent care pathways throughout the illness trajectory, death, and mourning [3]. Internationally, PPC remains characterized by variability in organization, access, and professional preparation [4, 5]. A Nordic research collaboration, established in 2023, echoes global calls to strengthen evidence, ensure coherent services, and improve education for healthcare professionals [6].

In Denmark, PPC has been prioritized since 2015, leading to the establishment of two children's hospices and specialized hospital‐based PPC teams in each of the five regions to ensure home‐based PPC‐ regardless of geography [7]. Research consistently shows that most children and families wish to remain at home for as long as possible [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], and children supported by PPC teams are significantly more likely to remain at home until death [13]. A recent Danish report shows that home deaths among children have increased notably since 2017 [14], a development that aligns with the implementation of PPC teams. Healthcare professionals working in PPC face emotional strain, roles of ambiguity, and challenges in interprofessional collaboration [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Studies highlight that caring for children during transitions from curative to palliative care is especially demanding, and paediatric nurses report feelings of guilt, helplessness and limited time [20, 21, 22, 23], while nurses trained in adult palliative care report insufficient paediatric‐specific competencies [24]. Nurses may also struggle with the emotional impact of a child's death, often resorting to strategies such as compartmentalization to manage grief [23]. Beyond the clinical demands, the existential dimension of PPC, related to hope, meaning, and the emotional cost of being present with dying children, has been well documented [25, 26].

These challenges appear to be particularly intensified in home‐based PPC, where nurses provide care within the intimate lifeworld of the family home, often working in professional and physical isolation. Research provides detailed insight into this complexity: nurses frequently work alone in the home, make decisions without immediate collegial backup, and navigate emotionally charged family dynamics within a setting where boundaries, roles, and expectations are constantly negotiated [6]. Further, studies expand this picture by showing that nurses in home‐based PPC express a need for clearer structures, better interprofessional coordination, and reliable technological support to sustain high‐quality care in the home setting [9, 27].

Although PPC research is increasing, little is known about nurses' practical experiences of providing home‐based care for children and their families. Existing studies often focus on clinical or organizational issues or draw on institutional settings. As international standards stress the need for experienced personnel in family‐centred PPC, understanding nurses' experiences in the home is essential for informing education, policy, and service development that support families who wish to remain at home.

Aim

This study asks what it is like for nurses to accompany children and families through illness, death, and bereavement in the context of home‐based paediatric palliative care.

Human Science Approach

This study employs a qualitative hermeneutic‐phenomenological approach guided by Max van Manen [28]. This design is well suited to exploring what it is like for nurses to accompany children and families in home‐based PPC. Phenomenological individual interviews were conducted to elicit concrete narratives from nurses' everyday practice. Van Manen guided the analysis by attending to the embodied, relational, and existential dimensions of these experiences. Drawing on his notion of existentials as fundamental lifeworld structures—lived body, lived relation, lived space, and lived time—these served as reflective orientations in the analysis. Thus, accompaniment was considered in terms of the nurse's situated bodily presence, her being‐in‐relation with children and families, and her engagement with fundamental human conditions such as vulnerability, responsibility, life, and dying. In this way, the meaning of accompanying families was allowed to come forward from the nurses' own accounts.

Participants With First‐Hand Experiences

Each of the five PPC teams employed two nurses at the time of the study, yielding a total of 10 eligible participants. All nurses working within these teams were invited to participate, and all agreed (10/10). The participants thus comprise the nurses practicing within this specific clinical context. As home‐based PPC is a highly specialized field, the group consisted of experienced clinicians, reflecting the character of the practice setting itself. The interviews were conducted by the first author (AA) between November 2023 and March 2024. The participants reflected variation in age, experience, and regional context. Some nurses worked full‐time in the PPC teams, while others balanced their palliative care responsibilities with roles in neonatal units, paediatric oncology, or intensive care. Their professional backgrounds in nursing ranged from 11 to 41 years, with an average of 28.2 years. Their experience within PPC teams ranged from one to 9 years, reflecting the relatively recent establishment of PPC services. All participants were women aged 37 to 65, which reflects the gender distribution within paediatric palliative nursing.

Gathering Experiential Material

Following van Manen [28], the interviews aimed at eliciting concrete, pre‐reflective descriptions through examples and anecdotes from nurses' everyday practice. The interview style was tactful and sensitive, encouraging participants to stay close to lived experience rather than general opinions [28].

An interview guide was developed as a guide for thematic reflection, structured by the existential themes consistent with the methodology [28]. The guide served as preparatory material and was not brought into the interview room, but contributed to formulate questions, support structure, and guide conversation. As such, the interview followed a conversational model, where wonderings led the dialogue, aiming to explore the participant's examples and anecdotes about their experiences. A wondering in this context could be: ‘What is it like to step into a dimly lit room where a child lies in bed, and everything has been quieted down to protect them?’

The interviews were conducted face to face in small conference rooms at five hospitals across Denmark. Meeting participants in person helped create a conversational space where experience could show itself. No notes were taken during the interviews to maintain a direct and open connection with the participants. After each interview, observational notes were made regarding the setting, including significant actions or moments that could inform the understanding of the interview context.

The duration of the interviews differed between 52 min and one hour and eight minutes. Interviews were audio‐recorded to capture not only the verbal content but also non‐lexical vocalizations like sighs and grasps and paralinguistic cues like pauses and tone of voice, all contributing to the richness of the data. Transcriptions were carried out verbatim, including gestures and pauses, to reflect the totality of the lived experience.

Analysis as a Thematic Reflection

We were inspired by Van Manen's thematic reflection as an analytical approach, aiming to obtain insight into meaningful lived experiences through a dynamic and creative writing process [28]. The analysis was guided by three modes of interpretive reading: holistic, selective, and detailed reading. The authors are experienced nurses holding joint positions in academia and clinical practice, with a primary focus on nursing science and palliative care. This dual positioning provided contextual familiarity with the field, while also calling for sustained reflexive attentiveness to our professional pre‐understandings. In line with van Manen's interpretive phenomenology, pre‐understandings were not bracketed out but thoughtfully examined as part of the interpretive process [28]. Understanding evolved through iterative movement between the experiential material, reflective writing, and dialogical engagement within the research team.

In the holistic reading, the transcripts were read repeatedly to let an overall sense of the material and atmosphere of the material show itself. In the selective reading, the text was broken into smaller units to identify meaningful expressions. In the selective approach, the text was read several times to attend to phrases, sentences or passages that seemed especially meaningful for the phenomenon. The material was divided into smaller meaning units, allowing expressions of what it is like for nurses to provide home‐based PPC to come forward. Certain phenomenologically powerful formulations were attended to with particular care, and these guided the shaping of the themes and the overall framing of the article. In the detailed reading attention was given to specific words and expressions that carried experiential significance, such as ‘to walk in step with’, and these were reflected upon for the ways they illuminated the meaning of the nurse‐family relationship.

Ethics Statement

The study followed the Helsinki principles and was approved by the relevant authorities. In accordance with Danish legislation, ethics approval is not required, as its non‐biomedical nature. Participants received written and verbal information and provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time without consequences. Interview material was anonymized to ensure that neither nurses nor children and families can be identified. AI‐assisted translation tools supported the translation of interview material from Danish to English. All translations were carefully reviewed and revised by the first author to ensure accuracy, clarity, and fidelity to the original meaning.

Results

What comes into view in this study is how home‐based PPC takes shape in the intimate space of the family home, where everyday life and illness intermingle. What also shows itself in the nurses' descriptions is not a fixed or fully graspable reality, but glimpses of how nursing takes form through attunement, nearness, and willingness to dwell with families in what unfolds moment by moment. From these lived accounts, three themes offered themselves as pathways into the phenomenon: The experience of coming close by walking alongside families; the experience of letting care arise through presence rather than action; the experience of sensing what is present.

The Experience of Coming Close by Walking Alongside Families

This theme points to an extended flexibility in the nurses' ways of being with families, which involves attuning to the rhythm and tone of the family and the home. This attunement allows the relationship to shift gradually, from being an external professional presence to becoming someone who belongs, briefly, within a significant moment in the family's life. This role differs markedly from traditional professional roles. It also means that being alongside does not end with the child's death; the relationship must be ended with delicate care, as part of the shared experiences.

In the nurses' accounts, what stands out is how the relationship with the child and family gradually takes shape, not as something established, but as something unfolding through shared time, gestures, and presence in the home. These relationships are described as unique to those formed in more formal healthcare settings. In the home, something different comes into being, a closeness that, over time, moves beyond roles and responsibilities, becoming an experience of companionship between humans. Phrases such as ‘It is like walking together’ (B) and ‘to walk in step with the family’ (A) are used to express how this closeness takes shape. Through such imagery, nurses give voice to how they attune themselves to the family's pace and offer their presence, not trying to steer future outcomes, but as someone who walks alongside the family as they move through challenging times.

That's the most important thing in palliative care‐ to have the courage to be present and to show your, well, your interest and your love, right? Yes, and your wish for things to go as well as possible, and that I'm willing to go the extra mile for you. (H)

The home appears as more than a setting or a place, but instead it shapes how the relationship evolves. The family restructures familiar rhythms as they adjust to the child's illness and the home‐based care, and the nurses enter carefully into this quiet symphony of everyday life concerns woven into existential awareness of death and life. What unfolds is not planned but becomes possible through the ordinariness and intimacy of the surroundings. In this unstructured and often unpredictable lifeworld, the nurses practice a form of being alongside, a presence that does not require invitation or naming, but simply make room for what is unfolding. The calm and familiarity of the home seem to soften the space between nurses and families, allowing a closeness to unfold that do not ask to be named, only to be shared.

I entered a home that held both love and chaos. The father was pacing, the mother calm, the siblings quietly present. She kept her attention on all of us at once, holding everything together. I remember thinking that I needed to keep a steady hand because their everyday life, the mess, the care, the illness was unfolding right in front of me. This was their life, their normality, and I was stepping into it. (D)

Flexibility in schedules, tasks, and presence shapes how nurses respond to what matters to each family. Adjusting plans, staying longer, or visiting the family once more on the way home is not experienced as a burden, but as a part of what makes caring for the child and family meaningful. Nurses describe adaptability as essential for connectedness and for maintaining professional integrity and well‐being.

Let me tell you a story about when I went to the movie with Augusta [Fictional name]. She asked for me specifically, and I wondered if I had come too close. But she had few friends, and the family was challenged. I didn't go to show ‘Oh, look how good I am.’ It was for her sake and probably also my own because I truly care for her, and I guess I see who she is as a person, and that is what she wants. (J)

As a child dies and time with the family comes to an end, the relational aspect to the family remains meaningful to the nurses. Some nurses speak about how they return to homes after a child had died, not to explain or do anything, but simply to share and to ‘be within the story’ (D) with the family. These relationships do not conclude in any final sense. Even years later, the children remain present in memory, not as patients, but as someone who shaped the nurses' ways of being with others. As one nurse puts it, ‘I never really ended those relationships, it's been years, and still, I don't have to try to remember—they're just there’ (I). Rather than relying on protocols, nurses speak about drawing on what their body remembers, ways of sensing and responding that have grown from years of caring for children. ‘There's no manual for this. You just know, from having been there before’ (J).

Quiet practices also show themselves—small ways of carrying what these companionships call forth: walking in nature, seeking a moment alone with the child or a parent, or sitting together in shared silence. Some find themselves drawn to these simple gestures as moments that let the weight of the work settle for a while. Others describe finding small, personal ways of letting go when a relationship comes to an end.

When he died, it felt a bit like being amputated, because I hadn't really had the chance to say goodbye to the family. And he was a boy I had a very close relationship with. I had an incredibly strong and close bond with him. And I remembered from my earlier time as a pain nurse all that about ending a relationship that you don't get to see to the end. I wrote a letter to the family, expressing my sorrow, and I wrote about this lovely boy, how wonderful he was, and about our relationship (…) I also wrote a letter to myself, where I processed and closed off the feelings I had for him. And that was truly insightful, because he taught me so many things. (C)

The closeness that develops between the nurses, children and families is not confined to the professional sphere. Nurses speak of how these encounters linger, as if they carry families with them into other moments of their lives. What unfolds with one family seems to fold into how nurses later enter the homes of others. This experience of coming close, of being there when nothing can be done, does not end when the child dies. Some nurses describe this as a felt sense that their way of being had been moved by what they had shared. In this way, what is gathered in one home seems to carry into the next, shaping not only their practice, but their very way of being.

I carry all those experiences with me from my work in these difficult situations. That's part of what has shaped into who I am today, in the relationships I have. And I carry all of them with me in some way. As a nurse and as a human being. (F)

The Experience of Letting Care Arise Through Presence Rather Than Action

Care shows itself when the usual urge to act gives way to a quieter way of being. In home‐based PPC, care follows the family's lived rhythm, where doing very little can still mean doing a great deal. This shift is not only linked to the home setting but to the nature of palliative care itself, where control gives way to being with what comes. Nurses often arrive at the families' homes without a fixed plan. Rather than following a checklist, the encounter develops in response to what the child and family seem to need in the moment. At times, this involves practical tasks such as drawing blood or changing a wound dressing; other times, it calls for silent presence, holding a hand, or drinking a cup of coffee. The absence of a strict agenda allows nurses to attune to the rhythms of the home and the pace and presence of those who live in it. Here, care appears to be relational and responsive, marked by attentiveness and by the sense that, in that moment, being there is enough, as a notion of staying close without taking up any space.

I was just present, like a fly on the wall, because they had it all under control and I really didn't have to do anything, I just had to be there, as safety or precaution, if that's the right word. Just the fact that there was somebody there that kind of, eh, approved of what they were doing or… They were more than capable of handling it themselves, they just lay there in bed, next to him, the older brother and dad and mom, and then he just died, peacefully and quietly (…) I was just present, like, occasionally they asked me a question, but nothing like… eh, I was just there. (B)

Moments when ‘just being there’ takes precedence over ‘doing’ carry a particular weight. The nurses speak about how practice has taught them to remain quietly present, not from certainty, but from the willingness to be open and to acknowledge what cannot be fully known. Their experience allows them to move gently between practical tasks and moments of stillness, trusting that presence can sometimes be the most responsive form of care.

I always think that the most courageous and really the best thing to do, is to dare to be present. To sit down and just be there. And that's what I see as the biggest difference between novel and experienced staff: that you have the courage to stay, even when everything is ugly and chaotic and overwhelming. To just sit quietly and listen, without that urge to fix things, you know, that urge that's so deeply rooted in our profession. (H)

In home‐based PPC this calm and steady way of accompanying families becomes central. It grows from encountering complex and unpredictable situations that call for intuitive response and quiet, reassuring presences. Such presence acknowledges what the family is living through as the child's death approaches and asks the nurse to face difficult realities with sensitivity and awareness of their own limits.

… He could not live, that is a fact we just must acknowledge. No matter what we did, he just could not live. But we could be there for the family – to make the situation as good as possible for them. So, that they could live on afterwards. (B)

As death draws nearer, nurses describe a form of being with families that feels different in contrast to the remainder of their work. It is not driven by tasks but by a readiness to stay close and to meet whatever arises. In these moments, presence takes the shape of companionship, as a way of accompanying the family by being there in the midst of what they are living through. This closeness opens a different kind of attentiveness, one nurse explains, a way of sensing the tenderness that gathers around a dying child

You become aware in a different way. And of course, it is tough that these parents are about to lose their child. But you also notice the tenderness in what happens around dying… the way people stay close because there has been so much love. (G)

The Experience of Sensing What Is at Stake

There are moments when care takes form in ways that are not spoken but sensed. In the family home, these moments unfold in the spaces where nurses and families find themselves together (e.g., the living room, at the edge of a bed). When a child is dying, or when difficult conversations arise between parent and child, the nurses describe an intensity that can be felt, a closeness that shows itself without needing words.

It was like a ‘bubble’, we are sitting there, in their living room. It is dark, the light was very dim and that also makes you feel the intimacy. We were all together, grandparents, sister, and it was so intense, just being present in that moment. And then, suddenly, I was back in reality; as if time had stood still, and it was as if I had been to the moon and back, that's how intense it was. (F)

Certain moments draw nurses and families into a shared concentration, as if the room itself becomes denser and time gathers into the present. In this enclosed atmosphere, nurses describe relying on bodily awareness to sense what is being expressed, not only through words, but through tone, movement, breath, and the shifting of the room. They speak of using ‘my intuition (J), a hunch (F), my antennas (B), my superior glance (I) and sensing the situation (E)’ to give insight to these experiences and what is needed. This attunement often leads into small, concentrated moments where everything centres on what is happening right now. These islands of intensity can arise during physical care or in a brief exchange, when the room seems to draw closer and those present orient themselves toward the same point.

The parents had always said they wanted to wash and dress Kate [Fictional name] themselves when the time came. So I helped and guided the parents in doing so. Then suddenly, there was a knock at the door. It was the younger sister, just five years old, and she said, ‘Mom, you promised I could help’. So of course, she came in. She washed her sister's hands and feet and helped put on her socks and shoes. And it was… it was truly one of the most beautiful experiences I've ever had. (I)

In other moments, this sensitivity creates openings for children's ways of speaking about death. Their understandings appear in images rather than explanations—small pictures of where they might be going or how they imagine staying connected and a part of the family left behind. These images stay with nurses as expressions that give shape to what is otherwise difficult to articulate. Nurses also describe listening for slight shifts, a pause, a change in tone, subtle ‘clues or signals that a child is reaching for words. When such openings appear, they follow them gently, letting the child's language unfold in its own way. In this shared space, images of houses, colours, named tumours, or familiar objects beyond the physical world can arise and settle between those present.

We are sitting at the table, Lily [Fictional name], Mom and me. I gently ask Lily: ‘Are you afraid of dying?’ No, Lily answered, she was not because she knew that she was going to see her granny again. ‘Granny has a large house in Heaven and a pool. A pool filled with Cheetos’, she told me. Mom lets out a gentle laugh and says: ‘That's right, Granny – she always had Cheetos for you’, so this is how Lily sees death. It was such a precious moment for me. (J)

Discussion

In home‐based PPC, care is shaped by the home itself and by the relations that unfold within it. The nurse's embodied way of entering the room, settling into the family's rhythm, and responding to what arises becomes part of how care is made possible. Across the findings, presence, being there, and accompaniment appear not as fixed actions but as relational practices that take form within the flexibility and unpredictability of everyday family life. Taken together, these insights open two central questions for this discussion: What do our findings suggest about professionalism in home‐based PPC? And what does this mode of professionalism require from nurses in home‐based settings?

Existing literature has examined nurse–family relationships in PPC [11, 21, 27, 29], yet few studies have approached this relationship as a form of companionship shaped through presence, accompaniment, and being there, particularly in home‐based PPC. As discussed by Errasti‐Ibarrondo, Lemermeyer, and Van Manen [30], healthcare work often involves emotionally demanding situations, yet practitioners frequently return because they experience meaning and fulfillment amid this complexity. Our findings echo this; being there, accompaniment, and presence appeared not only as professional competencies but as lived phenomena shaped by space, bodily presence, relational sensitivity, and an extended flexibility that unfolded with each visit. Rather than depleting the nurses, this relational closeness often became a source of purpose and meaning.

Although emotional exhaustion is often described as a risk in PPC [15, 29, 31], the nurses in our study experienced their close relational involvement as meaningful—even when extending themselves beyond standard expectations, such as responding during off‐hours or holidays. Flexibility emerged not only as a practical requirement but as something that sustained them. This aligns with Chong and Abdullah [32], who underline the need for organizational support, and with Rico‐Mena et al. [29], who describe compassion satisfaction as an outcome of deeply meaningful care. Kaye et al. [33] argue that nurses' responsiveness is grounded in a sense of profound responsibility, captured in the feeling of ‘one chance to get it right.’ Our findings support this and add that this sense of ‘one chance’ encouraged thoughtful attunement and personal investment rather than emotional withdrawal.

The nurses' acceptance of death also shaped their ability to remain present in emotionally charged encounters. Unlike the distress often described during transitions from curative to palliative goals [20], these nurses were grounded within a palliative orientation, which afforded clarity and a focus on quality of life. Their paediatric expertise provided a steady base, in line with Kaye et al. [33] and Porter et al. [21]. Hammer et al. [12] show that parental acceptance of a child's dying can facilitate a home death with lower symptom burden, often described as a good death. The nurses' companionship, supporting acceptance, shared meaning‐making, and relational safety seem central in this process.

Our study shows that in the family home, nurses' knowledge and their way of being are interwoven, shaping how companionship, presence, and accompaniment become possible. Empirical and paediatric expertise offer the ground for safe action, yet what becomes salient is how this solid ground supports a form of being that is intuitive, embodied, and relationally responsive. Their sensitivity to atmosphere, attunement to subtle cues, and ability to remain present in uncertainty point toward a form of professionalism that cannot be reduced to knowledge alone. This resonates with descriptions of accompaniment as a form of bearing witness within the liminal spaces of illness, where professional practice unfolds through attentive presence rather than intervention alone [34].

Carper's patterns of knowing [35] and Chinn & Kramer's emphasis on aesthetic knowing [36] help illuminate this complexity. However, our findings also resonate with critiques suggesting that aesthetic practice may be less a distinct form of knowledge and more a way of being that arises through experience, relational involvement, and repeated encounters in families' everyday spaces. Seen this way, professionalism in home‐based PPC may represent a shift from professionalism as something one knows to professionalism as something one is. This ontological dimension of practice, visible in flexibility, presence, and relational sensitivity, shows how nurses' way of being becomes central to how care unfolds in home‐based settings.

Bollnow's [37] understanding of ‘human spaces’ offers a useful lens for interpreting our findings. His work emphasizes that spaces are not neutral settings but shape how human experiences and relations become possible. Seen in this light, home‐based PPC can be understood as a situated practice in which the home itself becomes a co‐constituting element of care. The rooms, the spatial arrangements, and the familiar environment form conditions for how presence, accompaniment, and being there unfold. The nurses' movements, their positioning in the room, and their attunement to the family's rhythms are not merely practical adjustments but part of how care takes form within these human spaces. Understanding the home as an active participant in the encounter helps clarify why companionship in home‐based PPC cannot be separated from the places in which it is lived. Seen this way, professionalism in this context arises not only from what nurses know but from how they can be in the spaces that shape the relational and existential dimensions of care.

Conclusion

This study asks what it is like for Danish nurses to accompany children and families through illness, dying, and bereavement in home‐based PPC. What emerges is a form of practice in which companionship, presence, and being there are not additional elements of care but its very conditions. Within the lived spaces of the family home, nursing takes shape through attuned responsiveness, where clinical expertise, embodied presence, and relational openness become inseparable. Accompanying families involves navigating shifting rooms, subtle atmospheres, and moments where action and non‐action carry equal significance. Flexibility, sensitivity, and a willingness to remain with families through the changing contours of everyday life and impending death do not weaken nurses' professional grounding but deepen it. Rather than being depleted, nurses often find meaning and continuity in this relational closeness. Understanding home‐based paediatric palliative nursing in this way points toward a mode of professionalism shaped not only by what nurses know but by how they are able to be with families in the intimate and existentially charged spaces of the home.

This shift, from professionalism as knowledge to professionalism as a form of being, highlights the relational and spatial foundations of practice in home‐based PPC. These insights open a way of thinking about how presence and companionship are lived as part of nurses' professionalism and how this mode of practice takes shape within the spaces and relations of home care.

The study includes nurses practicing within a highly specialized PPC context, and the participants are experienced clinicians reflecting the composition of this field. While this provides coherent insight into accompaniment as lived within specialized practice, nurses with less experience or working in other contexts might articulate different challenges or meanings. The findings should therefore be understood as situated within this particular practice setting. A limitation of the study is that it cannot capture the full complexity of nursing practice. The insights are based on the experiences shared by the participating nurses, who represent those available and willing to participate, and therefore reflect how the phenomenon appeared within this particular group and in the Danish context. The transferability of the insights may therefore lie in the extent to which the described meanings resonate with or are recognizable in other nursing contexts.
