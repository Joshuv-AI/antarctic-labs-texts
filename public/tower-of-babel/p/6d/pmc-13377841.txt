BMI-dependent norepinephrine trajectories during vasopressin therapy in septic shock persist independent of weight-normalization and body composition
Authors: Max Melchers, Peter Pickkers, Arthur Raymond Hubert van Zanten
Published: 2026

We thank Wendel-Garcia et al. for their thoughtful comments on our recent study investigating body mass index (BMI)-dependent differences in norepinephrine (NE) trajectories following initiation of arginine vasopressin (AVP) in septic shock [1]. They raise relevant concerns regarding the potential bias introduced when expressing NE dose in weight-normalized units (mcg/kg/min). Normalization of vasopressor dose to actual body weight introduces a structural dependency on body mass that may not reflect the pharmacologically relevant distribution volume. Importantly, there is no consensus regarding the optimal unit for expressing NE dose in septic shock. While current guidelines commonly report dosing in weight-based units [2], several clinical studies use absolute dosing [3–5]. Expressing NE dose in mcg/min represents a valid and clinically meaningful alternative, particularly when comparing populations with differing body composition.

To address this concern, we re-analysed our data, now using absolute NE dose (mcg/min), thereby removing body weight from the NE dose metric. In this analysis, normal weight patients received lower median [IQR] NE infusion rate upon AVP initiation compared to obese patients (33 [25–43] vs. 40 [30–50] mcg/min, p = 0.019), but similar to overweight patients (33 [27–42] mcg/min, p = 0.505). This finding contrasts with the distribution observed when NE was expressed in mcg/kg/min [6], confirming that weight-normalization may influence cross-sectional comparisons of vasopressor dose and thereby mask severity of shock [1, 7]. We subsequently applied the same mixed-effects modelling strategy using absolute NE infusion rate (mcg/min), adjusting for baseline covariates. Importantly, the interaction between BMI group and time remained statistically significant both in the early phase (0–2 h, p = 0.001) and extended observation period (0–5 h, p = 0.004), confirming differential NE trajectories across BMI groups independent of weight normalization of NE infusion rate (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the three-way interaction between time, BMI group and AVP exposure remained significant as well (p = 0.003 and p < 0.001, respectively).

Although conversion to mcg/min increases the magnitude of absolute dose changes in obese patients because of higher body weight, it removes the influence of weight normalization itself. If the observed BMI-dependent difference were solely a mathematical artifact of expressing dose per kilogram, one would expect these between-group differences to disappear when using absolute dosing. However, the persistence of similar directional effects and group contrasts in mcg/min argues against such bias.

Alternative approaches to account for body composition include body composition metrics, such as fat-free mass (FFM), which may better reflect the distribution of hydrophilic drugs than total body weight. In addition, the ratio of extracellular water (ECW) to total body water (TBW) may capture fluid distribution relevant to AVP pharmacodynamics. In a subset of patients (n = 40) treated at the ICU of Gelderse Vallei Hospital, Ede, the Netherlands, routine whole-body bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA InBody S10®, Inbody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) at ICU admission provided measurements of FFM, ECW and TBW. Compared to the remainder of the cohort, these patients had lower SOFA-scores (9 [7–11] vs. 10 [9–13], p = 0.011) and had more frequently received steroids at AVP initiation (80% vs. 55%, p = 0.004; Table S1). In this subset, BMI remained significantly associated with NE trajectories over time both in the early (0–2 h, p = 0.017) and extended (0–5 h, p = 0.042) observation periods. Incorporation of FFM index (kg/m2) into the models did not result in a significant interaction between FFM index and time (0–2 h, p = 0.722 and 0–5 h, p = 0.586) and adjustment for FFM index did not materially attenuate the association between BMI and NE trajectories after AVP initiation (0–2 h, p = 0.019; 0–5 h, p = 0.064). Replacing FFM index by ECW/TBW ratio did also not yield significant interactions (0–2 h, p = 0.097 and 0–5 h, p = 0.182). In addition, the three-way interaction between time, AVP exposure, and BMI group remained significant over 5 h (p = 0.008), whereas the corresponding interactions with FFM index and ECW/TBW were not (p = 0.121 and p = 0.298, respectively). No significant associations were observed between other BIA-derived parameters and NE trajectories (Table S2). These findings do not support a distribution-volume or body composition-driven explanation for the BMI-specific differences in hemodynamic responses to AVP. Instead, they are consistent with differences in vasopressin sensitivity, although this hypothesis requires prospective confirmation. Clearly, interpretations are limited by the modest sample size and should therefore be viewed as exploratory. Furthermore, BIA parameters in the critically ill may be significantly impacted by fluid overload which cannot be fully accounted for [8]. Definite conclusions regarding the role of body composition in AVP responsiveness will require larger cohorts and more robust body composition assessment techniques.

Wendel-Garcia et al. further correctly note that NE is titrated to an hemodynamic endpoint rather than a target concentration normalized to body mass. In clinical practice, changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP) over time during AVP-therapy may be relatively delayed and subtle, while NE is continuously titrated to maintain a target MAP. We therefore selected NE trajectory as the primary outcome in this real-world dataset, as it reflects clinical decision-making regarding vasopressor support. In exploratory analyses, we assessed MAP trajectories among BMI categories after AVP initiation and observed no between-group differences [6]. To add to these findings, we have analysed arterial lactate and heart rate trajectories using exploratory linear-mixed effect models and also observed non-significant interactions between time and BMI category (p = 0.369 and p = 0.997, respectively; Fig. 1). Compared to normal weight patients, arterial lactate trajectories after AVP initiation of overweight and obese patients after AVP initiation were largely similar (p = 0.371 and p = 0.558, respectively). Heart rate decreased after AVP initiation across the cohort without between-group differences (normal weight vs. overweight p = 0.982 and vs. obese p = 1.000).

The authors also propose that the observed differences in vasopressin sensitivity may reflect delayed AVP pharmacokinetic equilibration in obese patients. While it is conceivable that an increased volume of distribution could prolong time to steady-state, despite not confirmed in our body composition subgroup analysis, we deliberately focused on the early phase (first 2 h) to isolate the immediate hemodynamic response to AVP. This minimizes the influence of confounding factors such as fluid management, source control or treatment of the underlying infection for which we could not control for. Importantly, extending the observation period to 5 h yielded consistent findings, with persistent differences in NE trajectories between obese and normal weight patients. Moreover, in our original analysis, median NE dose of patients classified as non-responders did not return to baseline NE requirement within 18 h of AVP initiation, suggesting a sustained attenuation of vasopressor response rather than a transient delay [9].

Finally, we note that the studies cited by the authors included smaller sample sizes and short early observation windows (MAP at 1 h after AVP initiation as primary outcome), limiting their ability to exclude BMI-dependent differences [10, 11]. In contrast, our findings are derived from a larger cohort with repeated measurements and consistent results across multiple analytical approaches.

While further research is warranted to disentangle pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms during AVP therapy in septic shock patients, our findings suggest that the previously observed BMI-dependent differences in NE trajectories are not explained by weight-normalization artifacts, limited observation duration, or body composition. The persistence of these findings after adjustment for FFM index and fluid distribution supports BMI-dependent intrinsic AVP sensitivity as a more plausible explanation than altered distribution volume alone.
