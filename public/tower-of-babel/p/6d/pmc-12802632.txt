Peak Frequency–Guided Cardioneuroablation and Pulmonary Vein Isolation in Sinus Node Dysfunction and Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Yoshihiro Sobue, Eiichi Watanabe, Keisuke Murata, Toru Araki, Akane Miyazaki, Takehiro Ito, Masataka Yoshinaga, Wakaya Fujiwara, Hideo Izawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSinus node dysfunction (SND) commonly coexists with atrial fibrillation (AF), leading to bradycardia, syncope, and pacemaker implantation. Effective therapies addressing both arrhythmic triggers and autonomic factors remain limited.Case SummaryA 49-year-old woman with paroxysmal AF, symptomatic sinus bradycardia, and frequent sinus pauses showed prolonged sinus node recovery time. High-resolution electroanatomic mapping with peak frequency analysis identified fractionated, high-frequency atrial potentials in the anterior right superior pulmonary vein. Selective cardioneuroablation and pulmonary vein isolation normalized heart rate, improved recovery time, and maintained sinus rhythm without pacemaker implantation over 12 months.DiscussionThis case illustrates that mapping-guided autonomic modulation can restore sinus node function in AF with SND.Take-Home MessagesDual ablation with cardioneuroablation and pulmonary vein isolation can treat AF with SND while avoiding pacemaker implantation. Combining high-resolution fractionation mapping with peak frequency analysis enables precise localization of autonomic targets to optimize outcomes.

History of Presentation

A 49-year-old woman with a 2-year history of episodic palpitations and dizziness, worsening over time, experienced resting palpitations with transient lightheadedness. Holter monitoring detected paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) episodes (duration: 10 seconds to 1 minute) interspersed with sinus bradycardia and pauses of up to 2 seconds after AF termination. Her baseline heart rate was 40 beats/min, with occasional escape beats in addition to sinus bradycardia (Figure 1). Given the symptomatic nature of her bradycardia and recurrent AF episodes, she was referred to our hospital for catheter ablation or pacemaker implantation.Take-Home Messages•A dual ablation strategy that integrates PVI and targeted CNA, guided by combined high-resolution fractionation mapping and peak frequency analysis, can effectively restore sinus node function and maintain sinus rhythm in patients with AF and bradycardia due to SND.•This mapping-guided approach may offer a valuable alternative to pacemaker implantation by precisely addressing both arrhythmogenic triggers and autonomic contributors, and it highlights the importance of individualized autonomic modulation in appropriately selected patients.Figure 1Baseline ElectrocardiogramSurface electrocardiogram in lead II before the procedure showing sinus bradycardia, with a heart rate of 40 beats/min.

Baseline Electrocardiogram

Surface electrocardiogram in lead II before the procedure showing sinus bradycardia, with a heart rate of 40 beats/min.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant medical history and was previously healthy, without any known cardiovascular or metabolic conditions.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for this patient presenting with paroxysmal AF episodes included tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome, vagally mediated functional sinus node suppression, primary sinus node fibrosis, and autonomic dysregulation triggering both bradycardia and AF.

Investigations

Laboratory results, including thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T3, and free T4 levels, were within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no structural heart disease. Ambulatory 24-hour Holter monitoring revealed paroxysmal AF episodes interspersed with repeated sinus pauses lasting up to 2 seconds, occurring even during daytime wakefulness. A preprocedural electrophysiological study revealed a markedly prolonged sinus node recovery time of 4,730 ms (Figure 2), confirming sinus node dysfunction (SND).Figure 2Preprocedural Electrophysiological StudyIntracardiac electrogram from the baseline electrophysiological study demonstrating a prolonged sinus node recovery time of 4,730 ms.

Management

After the electrophysiological study, high-resolution three-dimensional mapping was performed using the EnSite X EP system (Abbott) and sensor-enabled Advisor HD grid mapping catheter (Abbott) under high right atrial pacing to detect fractionated atrial potentials (FAPs). FAPs were defined as electrograms with ≥4 fragmented deflections, using criteria that included a fractionation threshold of 4, width of 5 ms, refractory period of 6 ms, and sensitivity of 0.04 mV.

These potentials were localized to the anterior wall of the right superior pulmonary vein (RSPV) (Figure 3). In addition to conventional high-resolution fractionation mapping, we applied the Emphasis Map (Omnipolar Technology Near Field algorithm, EnSite X system). The frequency analysis was performed by setting the peak frequency threshold to 400 Hz to identify regions with prominent high-frequency components (Figure 4).Figure 3Electroanatomic Mapping of Fractionated Atrial PotentialsThree-dimensional electroanatomic mapping showing fractionated atrial potentials localized to the anterior wall of the right superior pulmonary vein.Figure 4Fractionation and Peak Frequency Maps of the Left AtriumThree-dimensional electroanatomic maps integrating high-resolution fractionation mapping and peak frequency analysis. The intraprocedural endpoints included an abrupt increase in sinus rate by >20 beats/min or >20% from baseline, and local elimination of FAPs identified on high-resolution spectral mapping.1,2 Catheter ablation was first performed at the anterior aspect of theRSPV, which was identified as demonstrating concordant FAPs and high-frequency components on the combined fractionation and peak frequency maps. Radiofrequency energy was delivered at 40 W for 15 seconds per application. After these ablations, the sinus node cycle length shortened by 200 ms.

Subsequently, pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) was performed, successfully isolating all pulmonary veins. After the PVI, cardioneuroablation (CNA) was conducted in the superior vena cava region, contralateral to the site of the FAPs in the RSPV. Outcome and Follow-Up

After the procedure, the patient's sinus node function exhibited a significant recovery, obviating the need for a permanent pacemaker implantation. At the 1-year follow-up, she remained asymptomatic with stable sinus rhythm (Figure 6). Discussion

This case highlights the successful application of a dual ablation strategy that combines PVI and CNA in a patient with SND and paroxysmal AF. The integration of high-resolution fractionation mapping with peak frequency analysis enabled precise identification of autonomic targets, facilitating selective CNA tailored to the patient's specific substrate. Although PVI is an established approach for AF treatment, its effectiveness in patients with concurrent SND remains limited, as it does not address underlying autonomic dysfunction. CNA, by modulating vagal tone through selective ablation of ganglionated plexi (GP), offers a novel adjunctive strategy for restoring sinus node function and preventing AF recurrence. Notably, the improvement in sinus node function after CNA in this patient prevented the need for pacemaker implantation.

The link between autonomic dysfunction and AF is well established.3,4 Short-long-short (SLS) intervals in bradycardia-induced AF contribute to atrial ectopy by prolonging the diastolic intervals (Supplemental Figure 1), leading to a calcium (Ca2+) overload in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. The trigger delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) and subsequent AF initiation. SLS intervals are frequently observed before AF onset, especially in vagally mediated AF, highlighting their role in arrhythmogenesis. This mechanism underscores the importance of autonomic modulation in AF management.

The acetylcholine-sensitive potassium (I[K,ACh]) channel, found in atrial myocytes, is also key to autonomic regulation of atrial electrophysiology. Increased vagal tone activates I(K,ACh), shortening the action potential duration and refractory periods, promoting re-entrant circuits and AF initiation. Unlike ventricles, where these channels are absent, atrial vulnerability to fibrillatory conduction increases with vagal activation. Several studies have demonstrated that I(K,ACh) hyperactivation contributes to AF onset and maintenance, linking autonomic imbalance to arrhythmogenesis.5 Combined with an SLS-induced Ca2+ overload, I(K,ACh) activation creates a highly arrhythmogenic substrate, highlighting the role of autonomic modulation in AF management.

CNA has emerged as a promising therapeutic option to address this autonomic component. The effectiveness of CNA relies on the precise identification and targeted ablation of GP, which are clusters of autonomic neurons that regulate the atrial electrophysiology and sinus node function. GP are predominantly located around the superior vena cava, interatrial septum, and pulmonary vein ostia, which are areas that play a crucial role in autonomic modulation.6 In the current case, the integration of fractionation mapping with peak frequency analysis provided an additional level of confidence in the identification of autonomic targets. Specifically, the combination of these 2 mapping modalities revealed concordant high-fractionation and high-frequency sites at both the anterior aspect of the RSPV and the anterior left atrial wall.

Previous studies have demonstrated that regions exhibiting prominent high-frequency components—particularly those exceeding 100 Hz—correlate closely with dense GP innervation and parasympathetic neural input.7,8 The concept of “AF nests” refers to regions of autonomic innervation characterized by highly fractionated and high-frequency atrial electrograms, forming the electrophysiologic substrate of vagally mediated atrial fibrillation. This framework, supported by both anatomical and spectral mapping studies, has been used to identify candidate targets for cardioneuroablation with improved precision.7, 8, 9 In line with this strategy, our case applied high-resolution fractionation mapping combined with peak frequency analysis (threshold: >400 Hz) to identify and ablate presumed autonomic regions showing spatial concordance. However, an optimal peak frequency cutoff has not been universally standardized and is likely influenced by multiple factors, including myocardial thickness, degree of fibrotic remodeling, electrogram amplitude and morphology, and anatomical context.

In the current case, we empirically set the threshold to 400 Hz to enhance detection specificity for near-field autonomic signals. Although both regions were highlighted on the combined maps, ablation was selectively performed only in the RSPV anterior segment. This decision was guided by anatomical studies indicating a higher density of GP in this area, whereas ablation of the anterior left atrial wall may carry a greater theoretical risk of collateral injury to adjacent structures, including the phrenic nerve and aorta. In this case, targeting only the RSPV allowed us to pursue effective autonomic modulation while minimizing unnecessary ablation of noncritical sites. Moreover, atrial GP are known to be heterogeneously distributed throughout both atria, with overlapping autonomic functions and varied impact on sinus and atrioventricular (AV) nodal modulation.9, 10, 11 However, this case highlights the possibility that comprehensive ablation of all atrial GP may not be necessary to achieve clinically meaningful vagal denervation in patients with SND. By using high-resolution fractionation and spectral mapping to identify spatially concordant autonomic sites, we selectively targeted only the anterior RSPV and superior vena cava regions.

Despite this limited lesion set, we observed marked improvement in sinus node recovery time, resting heart rate, and AV nodal conduction. These findings suggest that functionally dominant GP may be sufficient targets in appropriately selected patients, and that individualized CNA strategies based on electrophysiological mapping may reduce the need for extensive ablation while maintaining efficacy. This mapping-based approach may offer a more reproducible and selective method of parasympathetic modulation compared with purely anatomic strategies.

The dual-ablation strategy combining pulmonary vein isolation with high-resolution mapping–guided CNA provides a comprehensive approach to address both arrhythmogenic triggers and the autonomic substrate sustaining AF and SND. By targeting FAPs within the RSPV anterior and superior vena cava regions, we achieved durable sinus node recovery and stable sinus rhythm without pacemaker implantation. This case underscores the clinical relevance of advanced mapping techniques in optimizing CNA outcomes and highlights the importance of individualized, mechanism-based strategies for complex arrhythmia syndromes.

This case has several limitations. First, an atropine challenge test was not performed, which limits our ability to definitively differentiate between intrinsic sinus node disease and functional, vagally mediated bradycardia. Nonetheless, the normalization of sinus node recovery time and resting heart rate after selective CNA, in the context of preserved sinoatrial and interatrial conduction, supports a functional etiology. Second, vagal denervation was assessed using indirect physiological markers such as heart rate response and a shortened AV Wenckebach cycle length, as extracardiac vagal stimulation was not available.12 These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the diagnostic certainty and generalizability of this approach.

Conclusions

This case highlighted the efficacy of a dual-ablation strategy combining PVI and CNA in a patient with SND and AF. By integrating high-resolution fractionation mapping and peak frequency analysis to accurately localize autonomic targets, this approach successfully addressed both arrhythmogenic triggers and autonomic dysfunction, resulting in restoration of sinus node function and prevention of bradycardia-induced AF recurrence and pacemaker implantation.
