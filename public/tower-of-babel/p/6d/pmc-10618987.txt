Dietary diversity and associated factors among school-aged children and adolescents in Lokossa district of southern Benin: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Herbert Sagbo, Paulin Kpodji
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe main objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of dietary diversity and associated factors.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingLokossa district, southern Benin.ParticipantsA survey conducted in a probabilistic sample of 612 students (345 boys and 267 girls), aged 8–17 years old from 26 primary schools. Dietary Diversity Scores were calculated by summing the number of food groups consumed by the schoolchildren and adolescents over a 24-hour recall period. Adequate dietary diversity can be assigned to a consumption that is greater than or equal to five food groups, and inadequate dietary diversity can be assigned to a consumption that is lower than five food groups. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, mean and SD were computed. Statistical analysis was performed on the data to determine which variables were associated with dietary diversity as well as the results of the adjusted OR with a 95% CI. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe proportion of schoolchildren and adolescents with inadequate dietary diversity was 25.8% (95% CI 15.1% to 40.5%). Schoolchildren and adolescents living in rural areas, having low household socioeconomic status, eating breakfast less than 5 days per week and no eating school meals were significantly associated with inadequate dietary diversity.ConclusionsThe results of this study revealed that the diet among schoolchildren and adolescents lacked diversity and is associated with socioeconomic status, rural living, eating breakfast and school meals. Public health interventions, such as school meal programmes in rural and isolated areas, in association with poverty-reducing programmes and nutrition education, are recommended to improve the quality of diet and limit the consequences of poor diet diversity.Keywords: adolescent, epidemiology, public health

Introduction

Dietary diversity has been defined as the number of different food groups consumed over a given period.1 It reflects the idea that diversifying one’s diet by including different food groups (vegetables, fruits, grains and animal source foods) is necessary for achieving nutrient adequacy and optimal growth, physical and mental development.2 Inadequately diversified diet is the main nutritional problem among schoolchildren and adolescents in developing countries, especially in Africa.3 4 Also, schoolchildren and adolescents in developing countries consume monotonous starchy staple diets with limited intake of animal food sources and few fruits and vegetables.5–7 Many studies have shown that an inadequately diversified diet characterised by the deficiency of macronutrients and micronutrients results in poor nutritional status, especially in schoolchildren and adolescents from developing countries.3 8 9

Focusing on childhood and adolescence is necessary because these are important periods for growth, pubertal and social development. These periods require an adequate and diverse diet to support the growth spurt with normal pubertal development, good health and productive adult life.5 10 These stages of life may also offer a window of opportunity for at least, partially offsetting the effects of malnutrition and delaying or preventing the risk of non-communicable diseases.6

Dietary Diversity Score (DDS) is used in broad studies and surveys to assess dietary quality.11 DDS is a scale used to measure Dietary Diversity4 and consists of a simple count of food groups that a household or an individual has consumed over 24 hours.12 Individual DDS is a good predictor that reflects nutrient adequacy and nutritional status.7 12 Studies in different age groups conducted in developing countries according to the guidelines of FAO12 using children and adolescents in South Africa,13 and Nigeria14 as well as children and adolescents in Tehran15 showed positive relationships between the macronutrient and micronutrient adequacy of diet and DDS.

DDS is associated with several factors such as parental level of education, household socioeconomic status, sanitary conditions, dietary intakes, food choices and access to food.16 17 Inadequately diversified diet has potentially lifelong negative effects on health, physical growth, social skills and intellectual development of schoolchildren and adolescents.6 An inadequately diversified diet may lead to an increased risk of malnutrition,18 predispose schoolchildren and adolescents to opportunistic infections and severe illnesses,19 as well as micronutrient deficiencies.6 Optimal growth and weight gain in childhood and adolescence are dependent on the appropriate quality and quantity of foods consumed. These are highly impacted by easy accessibility to food in the environment and socioeconomic factors.20 21

Benin is a sub-Saharan African country with a population of approximately 10 million people. According to the World Food Programme, 9.6% of the population experienced food insecurity in 2017.22 A study from 2018 showed that stunting, being underweight and wasting persisted among children under 5 years old with a prevalence of 32%, 17% and 5%, respectively.23 Greater rates were observed in 2015 in rural areas of Benin, where being underweight, stunted and wasted were found to be 40%, 31% and 22%, respectively, among school-aged children aged 5–10 years.24 Additionally, Beninese adults, schoolchildren and adolescents have a monotonous diet that is limited in diversity, predominately composed of starchy staples with little or no animal products, fruits and vegetables.5 25 26 Information on the dietary diversity of schoolchildren and adolescents in Benin is incomplete with previous articles focusing on dietary diversity as a determining factor of nutritional status,27 28 while the factors that influence dietary diversity were not well explored. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of dietary diversity and associated factors among schoolchildren and adolescents in Lokossa-Benin.

Methods

Study design, setting and period

This is a cross-sectional study using data from the survey ‘Nutritional and Health Status of Primary Education Students in the City of Lokossa, Benin’. Data collected were performed between December 2018 and January 2019 in a representative sample of students from the sixth and final grade of primary School, from public and private schools, from the rural and urban area of the district of Lokossa, located in the Mono department, in the south region of Benin. This district is located 106 km away from Cotonou, the economic capital of Benin, which is also the country’s largest urban centre. In 2017, the district had a total population of 104 961 comprising 5 arrondissements (Benin administrative units in the district), 121 primary schools with 45% of the Lokossa population aged less than 15 years old.29

Study participants and sampling technique

Schoolchildren and adolescents were selected using conglomerate probabilistic sampling with two strata (urban and rural). Schools (conglomerates) with probability of selection proportional to the number of schoolchildren and adolescents were chosen in each stratum. All sixth-year schoolchildren attending the selected schools on the data collection day were invited to participate.

To estimate the outcomes of interest, a significance level of 5%, 10% prevalence of the interest condition, and a maximum error of 3.25% were considered. A 1.25 design effect, based on a previous survey, was also considered.30 A calculation method accounting for maximum possible sample size (sample size for the finite population correction, fcp) was adopted using the sample formula31 [n=z²×N×P (1−P)/e² (N−1)+z²×P (1−P)]. Based on these parameters, the required sample size was 667 individuals. Only 660 schoolchildren attended the 26 selected schools that school year. Of these, 35 refused to participate and 10 were absent on the day of data collection. The final sample comprised 615 schoolchildren and adolescents aged 8–17 years.

Data collection

Data were collected by trained and certified undergraduate nutritional science students. Instruments used in research conducted in Benin, Brazil and Burkina Faso were used to construct the structured questionnaire in this study.32 The questionnaire developed was adapted considering the language and cultural context of Benin and the participants’ education level. The questionnaire was translated into French by a native researcher (HS) and revised by a French senior researcher (NK).

The final version of the questionnaire comprised the following thematic areas: sociodemographic characteristics, family and school context, dietary behaviours, alcohol use, physical activity, protective factors, mental health, hygiene and oral health, and an overall appreciation of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was pretested and adapted before administration. Students completed the structured questionnaire in the classroom.

Household socioeconomic status was estimated by adding the following household characteristics and goods to the questionnaire: type of house construction, number of bedrooms, type of bed, access to treated water, access to electricity, type of toilet, household car availability and pocket money of the participants. It was assigned a value ranging from one to three; adding up values yielded a score ranging from 8 to 21.33

The DDS was estimated from a 24-hour dietary recall data. Foods identified in recalls were 16 food items and aggregated with the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations/Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance Project (FAO/FANTA 2010) Guidelines. Nine food groups were formed; the formed food groups were: cereals, milk and dairy products, eggs, legumes and nuts, vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, meats and fish, other fruits and vegetables, dark green vegetables and organ meat. For score calculation purposes, ‘1’ and ‘0’ were attributed to the presence or absence of a given food group in the diet, respectively. The values attributed to each of the nine food groups were therefore summed.12 Scores were categorised into two levels of dietary diversity: inadequate dietary diversity (<5 food groups) and adequate dietary diversity (≥5 food groups).12

Measurements

Dietary diversity score

A consumption of less than five food groups from the nine food groups within the 24-hour recall period of the survey was categorised as inadequate dietary diversity and consumption greater than or equal to five food groups was categorised as adequate dietary diversity.12 34

The explanatory variables used for determinant analysis were the following variables selected to identify factors associated with dietary diversity.

Household socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic conditions derived from household characteristics and goods are categorised into tertiles. Socioeconomic conditions were considered as low (0–8) in households with no access to tap water or electricity or toilet and with residents sleeping on the floor and in one bedroom. Medium (9–16) was attributed to households with access to tap water or electricity, latrines and with residents sleeping on a mat, in two bedrooms and brick houses. High (17–21) was attributed to households living in brick houses with storey, having access to tap water and electricity, toilet, car and sleeping on beds, the schoolchildren receive pocket money.33

Demographic-related variables

Schoolchildren and adolescents sex: male or female and age range in years grouped into age categories (8–10, 11–13 and ≥14).

Household and family-related variables

Maternal and paternal level of education (non-educated, primary education, secondary education and above), parental work status (employed, unemployed), living situation with parents (both, mother only, father only, others), family size (ie, number of household members) categorised into up to 7 or ≥8, and household location (urban or rural).

Dietary and school-related variables

Weekly breakfast frequency (<5 days, ≥5 days per week) and school meals (no, every day).

The DDS was categorised into a dichotomous variable: inadequate dietary diversity=1 and adequate dietary diversity=0.

Quality control

A pretest was conducted in two school classes with 26 and 27 schoolchildren and adolescents each in Lokossa-Benin, a pilot study was carried out in two different school classes to test and refine all data collection methods and procedures. The school classes participating in the pretest and the pilot studies were not included in the study sample. The principal investigator and supervisor inspected the data collection process. Data were double-entered by two independent personnel.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed using Stata V.14.0 software (Stata). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sociodemographic factors. Frequency and percentage were calculated for the categorical data. The descriptive statistics of DDS (ie, mean±SD) were compared by sex and age, using independent sample t-test and analysis of variance, respectively. The proportions of inadequate and adequate DDS were compared by sex and age using a χ2 test.

Binary logistic regression was used to estimate ORs and 95% CIs. Variables having p<0.25 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model and checked for multicollinearity by calculating the variance inflation factor. Unadjusted and adjusted ORs with 95% CI were computed and variables having a p<0.05 were considered as significant. The model data fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Discussion

This study shows that the diet of schoolchildren and adolescents living in rural and urban areas of Lokossa-Benin was mainly composed of cereals, legumes and nuts, meats and fish, as well as dark green vegetables with low intakes of micronutrient sources of food (vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables). Our study results showed that the prevalence of inadequate dietary diversity among schoolchildren and adolescents was 25.8%. The study also identified different factors associated with inadequate dietary diversity, which were; living in a rural area, having low socioeconomic status, eating breakfast less than 5 days per week and not eating school meals.

In this study, cereals were consumed by all students, while animal food sources were lacking in foods consumed. This was similarly reported in studies done in Ethiopia,7 Uganda10 and Cambodia.35 Similar findings were also reported by Ochola and Masibo in a systematic review that analysed limited dietary diversity mainly comprising plant-based diets with limited animal food sources among schoolchildren and adolescents in developing countries.5

The mean DDS found in this study (5.7) was consistent with a study done in South Africa (5.8).36 Studies in South-East Nigeria14 and Northeast Ethiopia7 reported a mean DDS of 4.9 and 4.7, respectively, which are lower than our study. Studies in northern Ghana37 and Iran,38 respectively, reported a higher mean DDS of 6.9 and 6.8 among schoolchildren and adolescents. The difference could be due to the variation of food groups included, the season in which the data was collected, socioeconomic, environmental and cultural differences between study settings.

The proportion of inadequate dietary diversity among schoolchildren and adolescents was 25.8% in the current study. This finding concurs with previous studies among Beninese in all age groups and settings reported to have low dietary diversity.28 39 Studies in Southwest Ethiopia3 and Amhara region, Ethiopia40 reported an inadequately diversified of 20% and 8.3%, respectively, which are lower than our study. Studies in eastern Uganda4 and South Africa,3 both reported a higher dietary diversity of 45.3% and 61%, respectively, among schoolchildren and adolescents. The variation observed may have occurred due to the use of different methods for assessing dietary intake and determining DDS.

The prevalence of low DDS was significantly higher in rural areas compared with urban areas. This finding is in line with a study done in Ethiopia.3 This fact might be related to food insecurity, as in Benin, a significant proportion of rural households (12%) have been previously described as food insecure.41 Furthermore, urban areas may have greater availability and access to food, which could influence dietary diversity.

This study revealed a positive association between eating breakfast and dietary diversity. Similar findings have been observed in studies done on the relationship between breakfast and dietary diversity among schoolchildren and adolescents.42 43 It is a fact that breakfast is the most important meal of the day, especially among schoolchildren and adolescents, because it plays a role in alleviating short-term hunger, thereby improving cognition, short-term memory and concentration.40 44 Moreover, as the breakfast consumption of schoolchildren and adolescents increased, they had a better chance of getting adequate nutrient intake, especially micronutrients and also, a better overall diet quality.45 46

The relationship between household socioeconomic status and dietary diversity in this paper is in compliance with findings of previous studies.10 40 Similarly, a review concluded that the household socioeconomic status positively correlated to the dietary diversity of the schoolchildren and adolescents.4 On the other hand, a low socioeconomic household had a diet with inadequately diversified because poorer households often have a less frequent consumption of most food groups. Food sources mainly come from home-grown staples and legumes without access to other important foods which are available on the local markets.10

The results evidence the relevance of school meals to reduce food insecurity, as the school meals were associated with dietary diversity. In this regard, reverse causality appears as the most likely explanation for the strong association between school meals for 5 days a week and inadequate dietary diversity. In 2017, in Benin, a National Integrated School Feeding Programme was implemented in areas related to food insecurity, including schools located in rural areas.32 According to our study, however, it appears that this programme remains of limited reach, as only 6% of schoolchildren and adolescents reported eating school meals.

In this study, the age range of the participants was 8–17 years, while the expected age range for primary education in the country is 6–11 years.47 This discrepancy could be attributed to the probable combination of late entry to school and high retention rates. A study with same participant observed the relationship between age and undernutrition, which states the fact that schoolchildren and adolescents with undernutrition could suffer chronically from low energy levels and nutrient intake, which can lead to a reduced interest for learning and negative effects on academic achievement.31

Expanding school-feeding programmes is imperative to reach all vulnerable schoolchildren and adolescents, and prevent undernutrition—one of the highest prevalence rates in the country.32 Evidence indicates that School Feeding Programme provides school meals, which can have important short-term and long-term health implications for schoolchildren and adolescents, including promoting optimal growth and cognitive development and reducing the risk of food insecurity.48 In addition to reducing undernutrition, school feeding may also improve school enrolment, drop-out and attendance.49

The representative sample of students in the last year of primary school was the key strength of this study. The study should be interpreted inconsideration of the following limitations. Even though we have tried to control for potential confounders there may still be residual confounders from unmeasured variables. Also, the dietary diversity was estimated according to a single 24-hour dietary recall, which may not indicate the usual dietary habit and seasonal variation of the schoolchildren’s diets. It only included schoolchildren and adolescents in urban/rural areas, young children out of school were not considered, making the sample not representative of all schoolchildren and adolescents. The use of cross-sectional studies may make it difficult to establish a true causal link between dietary diversity and the correlating factors.

Conclusion

This study, conducted at primary schools located in rural and urban areas of Lokossa-Benin, demonstrated inadequate dietary diversity in more than a quarter of schoolchildren and adolescents. The main predictors affecting the dietary diversity among the schoolchildren and adolescents were living in a rural areas, low socioeconomic status, eating breakfast less than 5 days per week and not eating school meals. Low intakes of micronutrient sources such as vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables as well as animal food sources were characteristic of their diets. Public health interventions, such as school meal programmes in rural and isolated communities in association with nutrition education programmes and poverty-reducing programmes, are likely to improve dietary diversity and reduce the risk of malnutrition among Lokossa schoolchildren and adolescents.
