CAR in R/R FL: is real-world efficacy meeting expectations?
Authors: Estelle Bourbon, Emmanuel Bachy
Published: 2026

Follicular lymphoma (FL) therapy has undergone a profound transformation over the past 5 years. Two major advances in cellular immunotherapy and T-cell–redirecting strategies, particularly anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells and anti-CD20/anti-CD3 bispecific antibodies, have reshaped expectations in the relapsed or refractory (R/R) setting. For a disease long characterized by repeated relapses and shorter response durations, the possibility of inducing deep and durable remissions after multiple prior lines represents a paradigm shift. In this issue of Blood Advances, Sharp et al1 report a large real-world evidence (RWE) series of US patients with R/R FL, treated in third line or more with anti-CD19 CAR T cells. Their study is timely and highly relevant, as the hematology community now needs to contextualize outcomes from pivotal phase 2 trials within routine clinical practice.

The registration studies established an impressive benchmark. In the ELARA trial evaluating tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), the overall response rate (ORR) and complete response rate (CRR) were 86% and 69%, respectively.2 With extended follow-up of 61 months, the median progression-free survival (PFS) reached 53 months, an outstanding figure in multiply relapsed FL.3 In ZUMA-5, axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) produced ORR and CRR of 94% and 79% in the FL cohort, with a median PFS of 57 months after 65 months of follow-up.4,5 These data suggested not only high initial response rates but also durable disease control, raising the provocative question of whether a subset of patients might achieve long-term remission approaching functional cure.6

Sharp et al now provide complementary evidence from routine US practice. In their cohort of 136 patients, 36 received tisa-cel and 100 axi-cel. No patient received lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel), reflecting its later approval in FL. Reported ORR/CRR were 96%/88% with axi-cel and 80%/71% with tisa-cel. With a relatively short median follow-up of 14 months, an important limitation in a disease with an indolent but long natural history, median PFS was 12 months for tisa-cel and was projected at 30 months for axi-cel.

Toxicity outcomes were broadly consistent with prior experience. Any-grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred in 75% of the patients treated with either product. Any-grade immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) was observed in 42% receiving axi-cel compared with 17% receiving tisa-cel. Grade ≥3 CRS and ICANS were reported in 5% and 19% receiving axi-cel and 6% and 3% receiving tisa-cel, respectively. Grade ≥3 neutropenia occurred in 85% and 58% of the patients receiving axi-cel and tisa-cel. These findings reaffirm the well-described differential neurotoxicity profile between the 2 products.

Recognizing potential imbalances between treatment groups, the authors appropriately applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), a robust statistical method to mitigate confounding in observational comparisons. Baseline differences were indeed notable. Patients treated with tisa-cel were older (median age 68 vs 60 years) and more frequently received bridging therapy (61% vs 43%), suggesting either higher tumor burden, more aggressive disease kinetics, or more cautious product selection in frailer individuals despite similar lactate dehydrogenase or C-reactive protein levels, and progression of disease within 24 months after immunochemotherapy start frequencies. Importantly, bendamustine was used far more often as lymphodepletion in the tisa-cel group (36% vs 9%).

After IPTW adjustment, ORR remained significantly higher with axi-cel (96% vs 84%; P = .03), whereas CRR was not significantly different (87% vs 78%; P = .23). Median PFS was 30.5 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 13.6 to not reached) with axi-cel and 11.9 months (95% CI, 8.8 to not reached) with tisa-cel, without statistically significant difference (P = .17) (see figure).

Several important messages emerge. First, despite the absence of strict trial eligibility criteria, response rates in routine practice appear broadly consistent with those observed in ZUMA-5 and ELARA.2,4 The IPTW-adjusted CRR of 87% for axi-cel and 78% for tisa-cel are at least comparable, and possibly numerically superior, to those reported in the pivotal trials (79% and 69%, respectively). This concordance reinforces the external validity of the registration studies and confirms that high-quality CAR T-cell therapy outcomes are achievable beyond highly selected trial populations.

Second, toxicity findings mirror prior RWE comparisons in large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), with consistently higher rates of severe ICANS observed with axi-cel.7 The grade ≥3 ICANS rate of 19% with axi-cel vs 3% with tisa-cel aligns with broader experience and supports construct-related differences rather than disease-specific effects.

Third, and perhaps most striking, is the unexpectedly short median PFS observed with tisa-cel (12 months), a figure markedly inferior to the 53-month median reported in ELARA. This discrepancy is particularly surprising given that only 19% of the patients in this cohort would have failed ELARA eligibility criteria. The high ORR suggests adequate initial tumor reduction; therefore, the shortened PFS implies early relapse after response in a substantial fraction of patients. Interpretation must remain cautious, given the limited sample size (n = 36) and short follow-up. Nonetheless, the signal warrants attention. The frequent use of bendamustine as lymphodepletion in the tisa-cel group emerges as a plausible contributor (36% vs 5% in the ELARA trial). In multivariate analyses adjusted for CAR T-cell product, bendamustine-based lymphodepletion was significantly associated with inferior PFS. Bendamustine exposure before leukapheresis has previously been implicated in impaired T-cell fitness in LBCL and FL among other histologies.8,9 Conflicting data exist regarding the prognostic impact of bendamustine compared with fludarabine- and cyclophosphamide-based lymphodepletion. Therefore, its broader use as lymphodepletion in FL may deserve careful consideration.

Altogether, the study by Sharp et al provides meaningful real-world insight into the efficacy and safety of anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy in R/R FL. Larger cohorts, particularly for tisa-cel, are needed to clarify the duration of response and disentangle product-specific from patient- or lymphodepletion-related effects. Real-world data will be essential for patients treated with liso-cel, whose outcomes in the pivotal phase 2 TRANSCEND FL study appeared particularly promising in terms of both efficacy and safety.10 As bispecific antibodies combined with immunomodulatory drugs increasingly enter the therapeutic landscape of R/R FL, benchmarking these strategies in real-life will also be critical for hematologists. Importantly, long-term follow-up remains essential in this indolent lymphoma, where the true measure of therapeutic impact lies not only in initial response depth but in sustained disease control. As enthusiasm grows around cellular immunotherapy in FL, continued rigorous RWE analyses will be crucial both to confirm durable benefit and to critically appraise whether the hope of functional cure represents a realistic end point or an overextended extrapolation of early success.
