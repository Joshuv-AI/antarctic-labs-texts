Impact of pyriproxyfen on the reproduction of pyrethroid-resistant Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations in benin: what implications for vector control?
Authors: David Mahouton Zoungbédji, Alphonse Keller Konkon, Rock Aikpon, Isidore Hoyochi, Steve Zinsou Hougbé, Albert Sourou Salako, Arthur Sovi, Bruno Adjottin, Roseric Azondekon, Lamine Baba-Moussa, Martin Akogbéto, Germain Gil Padonou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy of LLINs is compromised by the emergence of pyrethroid resistance in malaria vectors. New insecticides that target other sites of action in the mosquito, including pyriproxyfen (PPF), are needed to maintain this efficacy.MethodsVarious populations of An. gambiae s.l. collected from April to November 2022 in 4 Beninese communes and the susceptible strain (Kisumu) were used to assess their susceptibility to PPF via CDC bottle tests and to assess the bioefficacy of Royal guard® (RG) alpha-cypermethrin + pyriproxyfen-impregnated nets via WHO cone tests. Molecular analyses were carried out to observe the frequency of resistance genes, and the oviposition and oviposition inhibition rates of these different populations to PPF were calculated. Ovaries of An. gambiae s.l. from Bohicon and Lokossa exposed to RG were dissected, and egg development was examined via Christopher stages to determine the fertility status of the mosquitoes.ResultsAcross all analyzed An. gambiae s.l. populations, the mean kdr allele frequency was approximately 85.08%. The rate of oviposition inhibition by PPF was approximately 98% for the wild populations of Djougou and Malanville and for the susceptible strain of An. gambiae Kisumu. In the cone test, RG showed optimal efficacy with the susceptible Kisumu strain. However, the mortality rate of wild populations with RG ranged from 12.5% [95% CI: 5.18–24.07] to 32.08% [95% CI: 19.92–46.32], whereas it varied between 0% [95% CI: 0–7] and 4% [95% CI: 4.9–14] with Interceptor®. All An. gambiae s.l. populations in Bohicon and Lokossa exposed to the RG net were infertile (100% [95% CI: 95.07–100]), whereas with Interceptor® and the untreated net, the majority had fully developed their eggs up to Christopher stage V.ConclusionUnder laboratory conditions, PPF had a considerable effect on mosquito reproduction, rendering all An. gambiae s.l. populations exposed to the insecticide and the RG net infertile.

Introduction

Despite decades of control efforts and diverse interventions, malaria remains a major public health concern. While implemented measures have contributed to limiting its spread, the disease persists globally, with a disproportionately high burden in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. In the absence of a fully safe and highly effective vaccine, vector control continues to be the cornerstone strategy for preventing malaria transmission from mosquitoes to humans [2]. This strategy relies on a range of tools, among which long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) are the most widely deployed within communities [3]. Owing to their strong community acceptance and favorable cost-effectiveness profile, LLINs have been massively distributed over the past decades, contributing to the prevention of an estimated 2.2 billion malaria cases and 12.7 million related deaths [2]. Although IRS has not achieved the same level of success as LLINs, it has nevertheless played a critical role in malaria control, particularly in high-transmission settings [4]. However, these achievements are increasingly undermined by the emergence and rapid spread of insecticide resistance in malaria vectors, which remains one of the main drivers of malaria persistence and resurgence worldwide [5, 6]. Most insecticides used in public health vector control act through neurotoxic mechanisms [7]. Pyrethroids and organochlorines act by targeting sodium channel receptors located on neuronal axons, whereas carbamates and organophosphates interfere with acetylcholinesterase, an enzyme critical for synaptic transmission [8, 9]. Resistance to these insecticides is associated with several genetic mutations, particularly in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene (Vgsc-995 F, Vgsc-1570Y, Vgsc-402 L) and the gene encoding acetylcholinesterase (Ace1-280 S) [10, 11]. To preserve the effectiveness of vector control tools in the face of insecticide resistance, several strategies have been recommended to slow the emergence and spread of this phenomenon. These include combining multiple interventions, rotating insecticides, implementing mosaic spraying, using insecticide mixtures, and, most importantly, introducing compounds with novel modes of action [12]. In 2017, the World Health Organization recommended the use of new insecticidal molecules, particularly chlorfenapyr and pyriproxyfen. Because their modes of action differ from that of pyrethroids against which malaria vectors have developed multiple resistance mechanisms these two insecticides have been combined with a pyrethroid to develop two new types of long-lasting insecticidal nets: Interceptor® G2 (alpha-cypermethrin + chlorfenapyr) and Royal Guard® (alpha-cypermethrin + pyriproxyfen). Unlike Royal Guard®, Interceptor® G2 has received strong endorsement due to its demonstrated efficacy against resistant vector populations, as documented in several studies conducted in Benin and Tanzania [13, 14]. Although the Royal Guard® LLIN has not demonstrated remarkable efficacy against vectors compared to standard pyrethroid-based tools [15], it would be premature to dismiss pyriproxyfen, particularly at a time when few insecticides with novel modes of action are available. Pyriproxyfen, classified as a juvenile hormone (JH) analogue, functions as a growth regulator by disrupting female reproduction, egg viability, and larval development [16]. In agriculture, it is widely employed as a pesticide due to its effectiveness against a variety of pests and harmful insects [17, 18]. Pyriproxyfen acts by binding to the juvenile hormone receptor in exposed mosquitoes. This interaction profoundly disrupts normal hormonal regulation by altering the sensitivity of insects to both JH and 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E), two key hormones involved in reproductive control [19]. Such dysregulation results in inappropriate and excessive activation of major genes within hormonal signaling pathways, including Krüppel homolog 1 (Kr-h1) induced by JH, as well as HR3 and E75A in response to 20E, particularly during the previtellogenic stage [20]. Numerous studies have shown that juvenile hormone analogues, such as pyriproxyfen, disrupt insect developmental cycles by blocking metamorphosis and maintaining individuals in an immature stage [21]. However, few investigations have specifically addressed their impact on malaria vectors. Further studies are therefore essential to elucidate the role of pyriproxyfen in vector control. In light of widespread malaria vector resistance in Benin [22], the present study aims to analyze, under laboratory conditions, the effect of pyriproxyfen and the biological efficacy of the Royal Guard® LLIN on the fertility and fecundity of different Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations across the country.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The present study was carried out in four randomly selected communities in Benin (Lokossa, Bohicon, Djougou and Malanville), where the high resistance of An. gambiae to pyrethrinoids has already been reported [23, 24]. Lokossa and Bohicon are two communes in southern Benin in the Mono and Zou departments, respectively. Both communities enjoy a subequatorial climate favorable to cereal crops, with two dry seasons (July-August and December-March) and two rainy seasons (April-June and September-November). Rainfall in the region averages between 1,300 and 1,500 mm/year. With a population of 104,961 according to the fourth population and housing census (RGPH-4) of 2013, Lokossa is crossed by 5 km of waterways, the most important of which is the Mono River, which offers riverside populations the opportunity to engage in fishing activities [25]. The commune of Bohicon, with a population of 171,781, is considered the most densely populated commune in the Zou region because of its trading [26]. However, the communities of Djougou and Malanville are essentially agricultural communities in northern Benin and are located in the Donga and Alibori departments, respectively. With an estimated population of over 267,812, the commune of Djougou enjoys a Sudano Guinean climate, with a rainy season (April-October) and a dry season (October-April). Rainfall averages between 1,200 and 1,300 mm/year [27]. Cotton is the dominant crop in Djougou, ahead of other crops such as cereals and tubers. While Malanville has a Sudanese climate with a rainy season (May-October) and a dry season (November-April), its average rainfall is 750 mm/year. Its population is estimated at 168,641, and fishing is considered a secondary activity because the town is crossed by the Niger River [28], which allows local populations to grow rice (Fig. 1).

Study area

Mosquito larvae collection and breeding

Larvae and pupae of An. gambiae s.l. were collected with a ladle from positive larval sites identified in each study community. They were transported to the insectarium of the Centre de Recherche Entomologique de Cotonou (CREC) for rearing to the adult stage. After morphological identification via the Coetzee determination key [29], only the An. gambiae s.l. were used for various tests.

Detection of kdr mutations in various populations of An. gambiae s.l.

Mosquito samples collected from various populations were analyzed via PCR to determine the molecular species of An. gambiae complex [30] and the Kdr L1014F mutation [31].

Sterilizing properties of pyriproxyfen (PPF) in different mosquito populations

Induced oviposition inhibition in An. gambiae s.l. females after exposure to PPF was evaluated on a total of 200 mosquito specimens according to the WHO 2022 protocol [32]. Kisumu-susceptible populations of An. gambiae served as controls. To perform the test, 16 Wheaton® 250 ml bottles with caps, 8 bottles per mosquito strain, were used. For each strain of mosquito, 4 bottles were coated with 1 ml of acetone (for control bottles), and 4 others were coated with 1 ml of PPF solution at a concentration of 100 µg after dilution of the stock solution with acetone [33]. Each vial and cap were labeled (name, insecticide concentration, date), wrapped in aluminum foil and left open to dry for 2 h under air-conditioned conditions. The female mosquitoes were left in a cage for one week with virile males to promote good insemination. The females were blood fed for the first time on the evening of their seventh day and a second time 48 h after the first blood meal. Twelve hours following second blood meal, the mosquitoes were introduced in batches of 20–25 into the control and test bottles for one hour of exposure. They were then transferred to labeled 440 ml paper cups and fed a glucose solution (10%), where mortality was monitored and recorded 24 hourly for 72 h. After this observation period, mosquitoes of both strains (control and test) were individually transferred to 100 ml paper cups (spawning chambers containing 30 ml distilled water) covered with a piece of mosquito netting and fed glucose (10%). Mosquitoes were maintained under oviposition conditions and monitored every 24 h for four consecutive days. The number of eggs in positive (with eggs) and negative (without eggs) oviposition chambers was recorded to determine the oviposition rate and oviposition inhibition rate. Tests were carried out at a temperature of 27 °C ± 2 °C with 75% ± 10% humidity.

Bioefficacy of Royal guard®

Two strains of An. gambiae s.l: the susceptible laboratory strain (Kisumu) and the wild strain were used to assess the bioefficacy of the nets according to the WHO cone test protocol [34]. For this purpose, 5 coupons of each mosquito net (Royal guard® and Interceptor®) were tested. The coupons measured 30 cm × 30 cm, with one cut on the roof and the other four on the lateral sides. Each coupon was labeled, individually wrapped in aluminum foil and stored in a refrigerator prior to testing. A total of 50 mosquitoes were tested on each Royal guard® and Interceptor® net. During the test, two standard cones are attached to each net coupon via a plastic plate. Five female An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes, aged 2 to 5 days and fasting, were introduced into each cone for a 3 min exposure to the mosquito nets. The mosquitoes were gently removed and transferred to cups. They were fed on 10% sugar solution and placed under observation for 24 h at 25 °C ± 2 and 75% ± 10 humidity. The number of mosquitoes knock-down was counted every 5 min for 1 h of observation, and mortality was read after 24 h [35].

Sterilizing properties of pyriproxyfen with Royal guard® mosquito netting

The bioefficacy of the Royal guard® mosquito net was compared with that of LLIN Interceptor® (positive control) and untreated LLIN (negative control) via a WHO cone bioassay. This test was carried out on adult populations of An. gambiae s.l., 12 h after their second blood meal. Batches of 5 well-fed mosquitoes were exposed to samples of each type of net for 3 min. A total of 50 mosquitoes were used per net. After exposure, the mosquitoes were transferred and fed (sugar solution) in vials, which were labeled (LLIN type and localities). Mortality was recorded 24, 48 and 72 h after exposure. Mosquitoes surviving 72 h after exposure to each type of net were dissected, and data on egg development status in the ovaries of each mosquito according to Christopher stages were reported. Fertile, infertile and inconclusive mosquitoes were recorded on a data recording sheet, and photographs of the ovaries were taken with a digital camera looking through the ocular lens. Bioassays were carried out at 25 ± 2 °C and 75 ± 10% humidity [35]. Figure 2 shows a schematic of the fertility study in An. gambiae s.l. (Fig. 2).

Results

Distribution of related species of An. gambiae s.l. complex

A total of 198 An. gambiae s.l. collected in Djougou, Lokossa and Malanville were analyzed. Three molecular species were observed. Two were the majority species, An. coluzzii (53.03% [95% CI: 45.83–60.14]) and An. gambiae (45.96% [95% CI: 38.87–53.17]), and a minority species, An. arabiensis (1.01% [95% CI: 0.12–3.6]), was observed only in the commune of Malanville. Of 49 An. gambiae s.l. from Djougou, only two species were observed: An. coluzzii (14.29% [95% CI: 5.94–27.24]) and An. gambiae (85.71% [95% CI: 72.76–94.06]). In Lokossa, with 99 An. gambiae s.l. analyzed, An. coluzzii (50.51% [95% CI: 40.27–60.71]) and An. gambiae (49.49% [95% CI: 39.29–59.73]) were the two species observed. In Malanville, with 50 An. gambiae s.l. analyzed, the two species represented were An. coluzzii (96% [95% CI: 86.29–99.51]) and An. arabiensis (4% [95% CI: 0.49–13.71]) (Table 1).

Frequency of Kdr (L1014F) mutations in different populations of An. gambiae s.l.

A high frequency of the L1014F mutation in the Vgsc gene was observed in all the communities studied. Out of 248 An. gambiae s.l. tested, the frequency of the L1014F mutation was 85.08% [95% CI: 81.64–88.1]. Among the different species of An. gambiae complex, it was 83.59% [95% CI: 78.54–87.86] in An. coluzzii (86.96% [95% CI: 81.91–91.02]) in An. gambiae s.s. and 75% [95% CI: 19.41–99.37]) in An. arabiensis. No significant difference in the frequency of this mutation was observed between the different populations of An. gambiae s.l. analyzed, nor between the different species of An. gambiae complex (Table 2).

Mortality rate of An. gambiae s.l. populations after exposure to pyriproxyfen

The mortality rates of different populations of An. gambiae s.l. (wild population and sensitive Kisumu strain) exposed to PPF and acetone was monitored for 72 h. This percentage did not exceed the 20% required by the WHO PPF evaluation protocol for the two strains tested. With respect to the wild population, the mortality rates were 10.38% and 8.13% at Djougou and 1.2% and 1.2% at Malanville with PPF and acetone, respectively. The sensitivity of the Kisumu strain was 10% with PPF and 2.1% with acetone (Table 3).

Discussion

This Phase I study examined the effects of pyriproxyfen on the reproductive abilities of different populations of An. gambiae s.l. with the L1014F mutation and the effectiveness of Royal Guard® double-treated mosquito nets (alpha-cypermethrin and pyriproxyfen) in Benin. The results clearly showed that An. gambiae s.l. populations in Djougou and Malanville are susceptible to pyriproxyfen. Two sister species, An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii, are equally represented in the An. gambiae complex, followed by An. arabiensis, the least represented species. The average frequency of the L1014F mutation in all An. gambiae s.l. populations studied was approximately 85.08% (95% CI: 81.64–88.1), indicating a high level of resistance to pyrethroids and organochlorines in Benin [22]. The effects of this phenomenon are clearly perceptible in Africa, the continent where resistance to the main malaria vectors is most widespread. These effects are justified by a high rate of malaria morbidity and mortality, making the African region the most endemic despite the deployment of control measures whose effectiveness is now compromised [2]. In the absence of an effective vaccine, vector control is the most reliable means of preventing and controlling the disease [2]. For this reason, it must be preserved at all costs. To preserve the effectiveness of vector control tools in the face of resistance, it is recommended that several strategies be employed to slow down the emergence and spread of this phenomenon. These strategies include combining different interventions, rotating insecticides, using mosaic spraying, mixing insecticides, and introducing molecules with new modes of action [12]. Pyriproxyfen is a juvenile hormone receptor agonist known for its role in regulating insect growth and development. It is one of the new molecules recommended by the World Health Organization for this purpose [37]. As in most countries facing insecticide-resistant vectors, new products are being evaluated. This study, one of the first in Benin, demonstrates the impact of pyriproxyfen on populations of An. gambiae s.l. resistant to insecticides, achieving an egg-laying inhibition rate of up to 98% after exposure to the insecticide. In the past, similar studies have shown the potential role of pyriproxyfen in suppressing whitefly egg hatch and larval development in these insect pests after exposure to the insecticide [38]. There is evidence that pyriproxyfen is a pioneering pesticide for the control of insect crop pests [39–41]. Recently, research evaluating the toxicity of pyriproxyfen to insect pests has been extended to the control of mosquitoes, the insect vectors of several public health pathologies (malaria, dengue fever, yellow fever, Zika and chikungunya) [42, 43]. Under laboratory conditions, a study carried out in Thailand revealed the excellent larvicidal properties of juvenile hormone analogs, including pyriproxyfen, against Aedes aegypti larvae [44]. Another study based on a self-dissemination approach using pyriproxyfen as a larvicide revealed a drastic reduction in the population density of An. gambiae and An. arabiensis, two malaria vector species in Africa [45]. Given that most available studies have demonstrated that pyriproxyfen exerts a stronger effect on the juvenile stages of insects (eggs and larvae) than on adults, the present study highlights its sterilizing action, preventing all populations of An. gambiae s.l. exposed to pyriproxyfen and to the Royal Guard® LLIN from ovipositing and from developing their eggs to Christopher’s larval stage V. The majority of the eggs examined remained arrested at stage III, indicating infertility induced by the pyriproxyfen incorporated in the Royal Guard® net. In contrast, most mosquitoes exposed to Interceptor® LLINs or to an untreated net completed the development of their eggs to Christopher’s larval stage V, suggesting that the pyriproxyfen contained in the Royal Guard® LLIN significantly impaired the reproductive function of the mosquitoes. The high proportion of eggs observed at stage III following mosquito exposure to the Royal Guard® LLIN may be explained by hormonal synchronization induced by blood feeding, which triggered simultaneous egg maturation that was subsequently disrupted mid-development by the action of PPF [46]. Mosquitoes that received a blood meal one hour prior to exposure to PPF had sufficient time to initiate the physiological processes of reproduction before PPF interfered with egg development. In contrast, in mosquitoes exposed to the control LLINs (Interceptor® and untreated nets), this disruption was not observed, which may explain why the majority of these females successfully developed their eggs to Christopher’s stage V. Consistent findings were reported by Ngufor et al. [47] during laboratory and experimental hut trials in Covè, Benin, where the Royal Guard® LLIN not only induced mortality rates exceeding 80% but also reduced egg production by 83% and progeny emergence by 95% in An. gambiae s.l. prior to washing. Similarly, Zoungbédji et al. [48] recently demonstrated that the Royal Guard® LLIN was able to sterilize up to 100% of An. gambiae s.l. populations exposed in Ifangni. Although phase I studies highlighted the potential efficacy of pyriproxyfen-based tools, phase III trials conducted under field conditions in adult populations have yielded less conclusive outcomes. Randomized controlled trials in Benin and Tanzania showed that Royal Guard® LLINs, incorporating pyriproxyfen, did not provide superior protection against malaria transmission over multiple transmission seasons compared to standard pyrethroid-only LLINs [49, 50]. Similarly, a recent study conducted in Tanzania by Messenger et al. [51] reported that pyriproxyfen-treated LLINs (Royal Guard®) evaluated under field conditions did not exert a significant impact on resistant vector populations. A significant and comparable increase in pyrethroid resistance of An. gambiae s.l. was observed in areas covered by Royal Guard® LLINs (containing pyriproxyfen) and standard pyrethroid-only nets, in contrast to areas where Interceptor® G2 LLINs were deployed. Another recent study demonstrated that pyriproxyfen (PPF) disrupts the reproductive capacity of An. gambiae s.l.; however, its integration into adult-targeting interventions such as the Royal Guard® LLIN appears to have only limited effects on epidemiological indicators of malaria transmission in humans [15]. Consequently, it seems unrealistic to expect conclusive epidemiological outcomes from pyriproxyfen-based adult-targeted vector control tools, which may explain the poor performance of Royal Guard® LLINs observed in the present cone bioassays as well as in community trials. Within an integrated vector management framework, it may be more appropriate to exploit the larvicidal properties of pyriproxyfen or to pursue further investigations to clarify its potential role in the management of resistant vector populations. This study would have been more comprehensive if the larvicidal effects of PPF had been assessed against resistant An. gambiae s.l. larvae in Benin. Nevertheless, the findings highlight once again the potential impact of PPF on malaria vectors.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the significant impact of pyriproxyfen on the fecundity and fertility of resistant An. gambiae s.l. populations. Although Phase III trials have shown that mosquito nets treated with pyriproxyfen provide limited protection against malaria transmission, this study’s results highlight pyriproxyfen’s (PPF) ability to disrupt mosquitoes’ reproductive processes by blocking eggs at stage III and inhibiting egg laying. These results suggest that, when used appropriately, pyriproxyfen could be an effective tool in combating vector populations resistant to conventional insecticides. Rather than using it solely as an adulticide, we could exploit its larvicidal properties as part of an integrated vector control approach.
