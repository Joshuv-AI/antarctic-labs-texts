Optimizing patient outcomes and empowering the patient-clinician partnership
Authors: Michael Thorpy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment landscape for narcolepsy is rapidly evolving with the introduction of new therapies featuring unique mechanisms of action and innovative formulations of established treatments. The recent approval of a once-nightly sodium oxybate formulation (SO-ER) has expanded the spectrum of oxybate therapies to 3, including the traditional immediate-release formulation (OXB) and a low-sodium version containing calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium oxybates (LXB). While the availability of diverse options allows clinicians to tailor oxybate-based treatments, it also raises challenges in selecting and fine-tuning these therapies. Involving patients in the decision-making process through shared decision-making (SDM) platforms can be an effective approach to optimizing oxybate therapy. This review article aims to provide clinicians with a comprehensive overview of the unique characteristics of available oxybate formulations and potential strategies for delivering empathetic care that incorporates SDM into the selection and optimization of oxybate-based treatment regimens.

Introduction

Narcolepsy is a rare chronic sleep disorder characterized by symptoms such as excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic hallucinations [1]. Narcolepsy impacts about 37.7 out of every 100 000 people, equating to approximately 126 191 individuals in the current US population [2]. EDS often leads to intense sleep urges lasting from a few minutes to several hours [3]. Although patients with narcolepsy usually fall asleep quickly, they experience disrupted nighttime sleep (DNS), which involves frequent awakenings and results in poor sleep quality [3]. Symptoms typically emerge in late adolescence or early adulthood and continue into adulthood [4, 5]. Narcolepsy significantly lowers quality of life and is linked to higher mortality rates and various comorbidities, including psychiatric, metabolic, and cardiovascular (CV) issues, leading to functional impairment [4, 6]. Additionally, it often coexists with other sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, and rapid eye movement (REM) behavior disorder, complicating its management.

The International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) identifies 2 distinct types of narcolepsy. Type 1 narcolepsy (NT1) is characterized by EDS coupled with cataplexy and/or a decrease in the levels of hypocretin (Hcrt) in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [3, 7]. While type 2 narcolepsy (NT2) presents with similar EDS and abnormal REM sleep patterns, cataplexy is absent and Hcrt levels in the CSF are either normal or slightly lower than normal [7]. Delayed diagnosis, which ranges from 10 to 15 years following initial onset of symptoms, is common among patients with narcolepsy, especially among pediatric patients [4, 8]. These delays are attributed to the association of narcolepsy with a broad spectrum of symptoms and the lack of precise diagnostic criteria [5].

The diagnosis of narcolepsy typically follows criteria set by the International Classification of Disease (ICD), the ICSD-3, or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [3, 9–11]. The diagnostic process often includes an overnight polysomnography (PSG), a multiple sleep latency test (MSLT), and, rarely, measurements of CSF Hcrt levels. The pathogenic mechanisms underlying narcolepsy are not well understood. A growing body of studies has highlighted the significance of orexin A and B/Hcrt neuropeptides in the disease’s pathophysiology [3]. Postmortem studies have shown that patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy often experience a selective loss of Hcrt cells in the hypothalamus of the brain. However, the precise mechanisms behind the loss of Hcrt neurons are still not fully understood [1].

Narcolepsy is associated with a substantial socioeconomic burden that is marked by the increased utilization of healthcare services, lower employment rates, and reduced income [12]. There is currently no cure for narcolepsy, and the available drugs do not modify the disease course. Patients require lifelong symptomatic pharmacotherapy to manage symptoms and improve their quality of life (QoL). The therapeutic landscape for narcolepsy is evolving, with the development of new drugs featuring innovative mechanisms of action and the introduction of novel formulations of existing treatments aimed at boosting patient adherence and QoL.

Sodium oxybate, the sodium salt of gamma-hydroxybutyric acid, is an established treatment option approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment to narcolepsy [4]. However, its high sodium content and the need for twice-nightly dosing led to the emergence of alternative formulations with the goal of improving safety and patient adherence. The availability of multiple options of sodium oxybate poses new challenges to clinicians and patients in terms of treatment selection and individualization [4].

This review article is designed to offer clinicians a thorough insight into the principal distinctions among the existing oxybate formulations and to emphasize the importance of empathy and shared decision-making when tailoring treatment with an oxybate formulation.

Impact of narcolepsy disease burden on quality of life

Narcolepsy is associated with a high disease burden that adversely affects patients’ QoL. Analysis of population data from the 2011, 2012, and 2013 National Health and Wellness Survey (NHWS) showed that patients with narcolepsy experience a notably higher prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) impairments, long-term disability, absenteeism, and presenteeism, with rates 2 to 4 times greater than those of matched controls [6]. Corroborating these findings, Thorpy and Hiller reported that the annual direct medical costs for patients with narcolepsy are roughly double those of matched controls ($11 702 vs $5261, respectively; p < 0.0001) [12]. Moreover, the severity of symptoms drives the burden of narcolepsy, negatively impacting self-care and daily activities and leading to pain, discomfort, anxiety, and depression [13].

The delay in diagnosing narcolepsy significantly exacerbates the burden of the disease [8]. A United States survey involving 1699 patients with narcolepsy revealed that the majority were diagnosed over a year after symptom onset, with pediatric onset being a major predictor of this delay [14]. Additionally, depression emerged as the most frequently reported comorbidity, affecting 25% of the participants. This considerable burden of narcolepsy and its detrimental effect on patients’ QoL highlight the necessity for patient-centered approaches in managing this lifelong condition and mitigating its impact [15]. However, implementing patient-centered care in practical settings is difficult due to a disconnect between patients’ and healthcare providers’ perceptions of the disease [16]. Moreover, the evolving therapeutic landscape of narcolepsy presents clinicians with challenges in optimizing treatment, but it also opens up opportunities for personalizing treatment plans according to patient needs and objectives. By customizing treatment strategies, clinicians have the potential to enhance patient quality of life and lessen the overall burden of the disease.

The evolving role of sodium oxybate in narcolepsy management

Narcolepsy, as a chronic condition, requires lifelong management that includes both behavioral and lifestyle modifications as well as pharmacotherapy. The treatment objectives for narcolepsy, as outlined by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s practice parameters and guidelines, focus on reducing EDS, managing cataplexy, addressing other REM-related symptoms, and minimizing nocturnal sleep disturbances [4]. Achieving these goals is crucial for enabling patients to function normally during the day [17]. Nonpharmacological approaches include educating patients about maintaining good sleep hygiene, such as adhering to a consistent nighttime sleep schedule, as well as staying physically active throughout the day [1].

A variety of pharmacological treatment options are available for narcolepsy, including oxybates and pitolisant, which are used to treat EDS and cataplexy. Modafinil, armodafinil, and solriamfetol are specifically used to treat EDS. Methylphenidate, amphetamine, and dextroamphetamine are FDA-approved for EDS treatment but are often considered second- or third-line options due to limited or low-quality evidence supporting their effectiveness in narcolepsy management [4]. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends modafinil, pitolisant, sodium oxybate (without specifying a formulation), or solriamfetol for the treatment of patients with narcolepsy [4]. The FDA has approved 3 distinct sodium oxybate formulations: Xyrem, an immediate-release sodium oxybate (IR-SO); Xywav, a low-sodium formulation composed of calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium oxybates (LXB); and Lumryz, a novel extended-release formulation of sodium oxybate (SO-ER). The exact mechanism by which oxybates treat narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia remains partially unclear, partly due to an incomplete understanding of the pathophysiology of these disorders [4].

SO is generally well tolerated, but there are concerns that its high sodium content, which contributes 1100 mg to 1640 mg of sodium to daily intake, may increase the risk of hypertension and CV issues, which is particularly concerning for patients with existing CV comorbidities [18–20]. This led to the development of LXB, a multi-cation formulation of oxybate with 92% less sodium than SO. This formulation maintains the same active component at an equivalent concentration [21]. Like SO, LXB is administered twice nightly. Based on the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles, these doses should be spaced 2.5 to 4 hours apart [22]. Most patients can seamlessly transition from SO to LXB without dose modification, but some may require adjustments to achieve comparable efficacy [22]. The FDA recently approved SO-ER, the first extended-release formulation designed to allow for once-nightly dosing, for treating cataplexy or EDS in adult patients with narcolepsy [23]. This approval was based on the pivotal phase 3 REST-ON trial, which demonstrated its efficacy in improving disease symptoms in patients with NT1 and NT2 [24].

The availability of multiple oxybate formulations introduces complex considerations to treatment selection and optimization for narcolepsy. A key unresolved issue is whether patients on SO therapy should modify their sodium intake. Currently, there is an absence of detailed data on the sodium content in SO and its potential impact on CV risk. While high daily sodium intake is generally associated with increased CV risk—and patients with narcolepsy often have comorbid conditions that could heighten their CV risk—research on SO in narcolepsy has shown minimal CV side effects, such as hypertension, and no substantial correlation with elevated CV risk. In light of the current data gap regarding the specific CV risks posed by SO’s sodium content, the prevailing clinical evidence does not indicate that SO treatment produces a significantly increase CV risk for patients with narcolepsy [25].

Decision-making and preferences in therapy selection

As the number of treatment options for narcolepsy grows, choosing and fine-tuning treatments pose challenges for clinicians. Typically, the choice of initial and follow-up therapies is determined by the intensity and nature of the symptoms exhibited by the patient. Factors such as the patient’s age, lifestyle, comorbid conditions, medication effectiveness, side effects, and cost also play crucial roles in this decision-making process. Tailoring pharmacological treatment for narcolepsy involves carefully adjusting the timing, regimen, and dosage of medications to align with the specific needs of each patient [26].

While SO has significantly influenced narcolepsy treatment, its requirement for twice-nightly dosing can affect patient compliance. Real-world studies have shown that patients often struggle with the second, middle-of-the-night dose of immediate-release (IR) oxybate, leading to nonadherence to their treatment plan [27]. In a study involving 351 patients on SO, 113 (32.2%) had clinical records of stopping SO, with 71 (20.2%) citing specific reasons for discontinuation. The most frequent reasons for stopping (≥5 instances) included lack of effectiveness (n = 11), side effects (n = 10), gastrointestinal issues (n = 10), neurological side effects (n = 9), psychiatric side effects (n = 8), access issues (insurance/cost, n = 10), and resolution or absence of cataplexy (n = 5). Among the 38 patients (10.8%) noted for missing the second SO dose, reasons were documented for 24 (63.2%), such as inability to wake up (n = 14), forgetting the dose (n = 3), and needing to wake up early the next day (n = 3). The documented consequences of missing the second dose, although for a smaller group, included increased cataplexy (n = 4), reduced daytime alertness (n = 3), earlier awakening (n = 1), and tiredness (n = 1) [27].

Supporting these observations, a recent survey of patients who transitioned from SO to SO-ER evaluated preferences between once- and twice-nightly oxybate therapy, as well as experiences with the second nightly OXB dose [28]. The survey revealed a strong preference for a once-at-bedtime dosing schedule among patients with narcolepsy who had previously used IR oxybate. Specifically, 92.5% (49 out of 53) of those who switched favored the once-nightly regimen. In the past 3 months, 66.6% (62 out of 93) of the participants had inadvertently missed their second IR oxybate dose, with 83.9% (52 out of 62) reporting feeling worse the following day. Additionally, 38.7% (36 out of 93) took their second IR oxybate dose more than 4 hours after the first, with half experiencing significant grogginess or unsteadiness the next morning. For 76.3% (71 out of 93), the second nighttime dose was somewhat to extremely inconvenient. Despite being advised to stay in bed, 90.3% (84 out of 93) got up after taking the second dose, resulting in falls for 6 individuals and injuries for 3. Furthermore, anxiety about the second dose was reported by 26.9% of participants, and 22.6% needed assistance from someone else to wake up for it [28].

The recent introduction of the once-nightly extended-release formulation, SO-ER, presents an opportunity to tailor treatment based on patient preferences. A discrete choice experiment (DCE) was conducted to explore the dosing preferences associated with SO [29]. This study involved 75 participants, including 50 current and 25 former users of the twice-nightly SO regimen. It found that dosing frequency was the most significant factor influencing their preference for SO. While participants acknowledged the efficacy and safety of the twice-nightly SO, many reported stress and anxiety due to the need to wake up for the second dose. The DCE indicated that the number of doses was the primary determinant in product selection, adherence to medication instructions, and stress/anxiety levels. The preference for a once-nightly dosing schedule was significantly higher (p < 0.001) in terms of likelihood to adhere to the medication as prescribed, compared with the twice-nightly regimen (relative preference weight, ±119.6). Overall, the preference leaned more towards once-nightly dosing. The top reasons for this preference included not having to wake up for a second dose in the middle of the night (48%), experiencing fewer side effects (46%), and the ease of taking/handling the medication (32%).

The results from this DCE study conducted with patients with narcolepsy align with a similar DCE study carried out among physicians treating narcolepsy [30]. In both groups, once-nightly dosing was markedly preferred over twice-nightly dosing in terms of overall product choice, patient QoL, and patient anxiety/stress. In the physicians’ DCE analysis, dosing frequency emerged as the most critical factor influencing oxybate treatment choices, impacting overall product preference, patient QoL, and patient anxiety/stress (with relative attribute importance scores of 46.1, 41.7, and 44.0, respectively). Other significant factors for overall product choice included adverse reactions and sodium content (with relative attribute importance scores of 19.7 and 18.6, respectively). These factors were also linked to reductions in patient anxiety and stress (relative attribute importance of 18.2 and 14.2, respectively). Beyond dosing frequency, adverse reactions and clinical efficacy were identified as key factors affecting patient QoL [30].

The marked preference for once-nightly dosing among patients with narcolepsy could be attributed to the prevalence and burden of DNS in narcolepsy. Prevalence estimates for DNS vary widely, ranging from 30% to 95%, while EDS is a universal symptom among those with narcolepsy [4, 31]. DNS is often reported by patients as frequent awakenings or difficulty maintaining sleep and can be objectively measured using methods like PSG and actigraphy [31].

The importance of empathy and collaborative decision-making in narcolepsy treatment

The adoption of SDM in healthcare has advanced the management of many challenging diseases (Figure 1) [32]. SDM frameworks allow patients and their caregivers to collaborate with healthcare professionals in formulating treatment plans tailored to their specific needs and objectives [33]. An increasing amount of research shows that incorporating SDM into patient care not only enhances patient satisfaction and adherence to treatment, but also leads to an overall improvement in QoL [34, 35]. Furthermore, there is evidence suggesting that SDM contributes to increased compliance with prescribed treatments and improves long-term health outcomes [34–36].

Proposed SDM model in the management of narcolepsy.

There are several challenges to the implementation of an SDM model in the care of patients with narcolepsy. An evaluation of how narcolepsy symptoms are perceived and their impact on patient QoL uncovered a discrepancy between the views of patients and healthcare providers on the disease and its effect on patients’ lives [16]. In this study, patients often gauged the severity of narcolepsy based on the level of functional impairment rather than the frequency of symptoms. Notably, the study revealed that patients with narcolepsy often suffer from “self-stigma,” stemming from societal undervaluing of sleep issues and the mistaken association of sleepiness with laziness [16]. Additionally, patients tended to view their doctors more as passive prescribers than as active participants in managing their condition. This misalignment in disease perception can adversely affect the patient-clinician relationship and treatment outcomes. These findings underscore the need for healthcare providers to practice empathy in treating patients with narcolepsy. Empathy involves recognizing, understanding, and sharing another person’s feelings and viewpoint [37]. It can enhance various aspects of healthcare, including patient satisfaction, adherence to treatment, symptom relief, and overall emotional well-being.

A key component to implementing SDM in the treatment of narcolepsy involves educating patients on managing their condition and understanding treatment choices. Tailored, comprehensive education that involves patients, their families, and psychologists can be instrumental in providing insights into both the medical and psychosocial facets of the disease [38]. Additionally, it is vital for clinicians to actively educate family members, teachers, and employers about the accommodations and support needed for patients with narcolepsy. This can help reduce the impact of the patients’ “self-stigma” on managing their condition [16]. Support groups for narcolepsy also play a valuable role in enhancing empathetic patient care within the SDM framework. Various organizations offer information and support globally, including the Narcolepsy Network and National Sleep Foundation in the United States, Wake Up Narcolepsy (worldwide), and the European Narcolepsy Network [4].

The practical application of SDM in choosing treatments for narcolepsy was recently highlighted in a study by Roy and colleagues. This survey-based research, focusing on oxybate prescription practices, found that a majority of physicians (68%) treating patients with narcolepsy regularly discussed modifying oxybate treatment to suit changes in patients’ lifestyles, including alcohol consumption and travel. Additionally, these physicians reported that tailoring oxybate dosing not only positively influenced patient care, but also actively involved patients in their treatment decisions [17]. The study interestingly revealed that, on average, 50% (±30%) of patients were on a twice-nightly dosing schedule, 39% (±29%) on a once-nightly schedule, and 10% (±17%) on a thrice-nightly schedule. However, it is crucial to note that the sodium content in SO-ER is comparable to that in SO. Therefore, in certain patients, notably those with comorbid conditions that can be impacted by increased sodium intake, clinicians need to discuss the preferred treatment choice with their patients.

Expert insights and outlook for the future

Although narcolepsy is a rare condition, its pharmacological treatment options are evolving rapidly, driven in part by advances in the understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis. The discovery of the role of orexins in the wake–sleep cycle has led to the development of orexin modulators, such as the oral orexin receptor 2 agonist, TAK-994, which has shown promising results in clinical trials for treating NT1 [39, 40]. As new therapies enter clinical use, the treatment landscape is expected to become more complex. Experts predict that the emergence of treatments targeting different pathological mechanisms could pave the way for the development of combination therapeutic regimens aimed at specific narcolepsy symptoms. These developments may provide further opportunities for tailoring treatment plans to meet individual patient needs. Until such time as these new therapies enter the treatment arsenal, clinicians and their patients can continue to rely on oxybate formulations to manage the main symptoms associated with narcolepsy with/without cataplexy. The availability of 3 formulations gives clinicians and patients the flexibility to choose the treatment that will optimize efficacy, safety, and outcomes.
