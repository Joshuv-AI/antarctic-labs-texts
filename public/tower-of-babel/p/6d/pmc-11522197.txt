Selecting the Best Pharmacokinetic Models for a Priori Model-Informed Precision Dosing with Model Ensembling
Authors: Bram C Agema, Tolra Kocher, Ayşenur B Öztürk, Eline L Giraud, Nielka P van Erp, Brenda C M de Winter, Ron H J Mathijssen, Stijn L W Koolen, Birgit C P Koch, Sebastiaan D T Sassen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveWhen utilizing population pharmacokinetic (popPK) models for a priori dosage individualization, selecting the best model is crucial to obtain adequate doses. We developed and evaluated several model-selection and ensembling methods, using external evaluation on the basis of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) samples to identify the best (set of) models per patient for a priori dosage individualization.MethodsPK data and models describing both hospitalized patients (n = 134) receiving continuous vancomycin (26 models) and patients (n = 92) receiving imatinib in an outpatient setting (12 models) are included. Target attainment of four model-selection methods was compared with standard dosing: the best model based on external validation, uninformed model ensembling, model ensembling using a weighting scheme on the basis of covariate-stratified external evaluation, and model selection using covariates in decision trees that were subsequently ensembled.ResultsOverall, the use of PK models improved the proportion of patients exposed to concentrations within the therapeutic window for both cohorts. Relative improvement of proportion on target for best model, unweighted, weighted, and decision trees were − 7.0%, 2.3%, 11.4%, and 37.0% (vancomycin method-development); 23.2%, 7.9%, 15.6%, and, 77.2% (vancomycin validation); 40.7%, 50.0%, 59.5%, and 59.5% (imatinib method-development); and 19.0%, 28.5%, 38.0%, and 23.8% (imatinib validation), respectively.ConclusionsThe best (set of) models per patient for a priori dosage individualization can be identified using a relatively small set of TDM samples as external evaluation. Adequately performing popPK models were identified while also excluding poor-performing models. Dose recommendations resulted in more patients within the therapeutic range for both vancomycin and imatinib. Prospective validation is necessary before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-024-01425-9.

Key Points

As one dose might not fit all, one population pharmacokinetic model may not fit all. Model ensembling may solve this problem.We evaluate four different model-ensembling methods that can be applied prior to obtaining pharmacokinetic samples using a small TDM dataset for external evaluation.Applying a priori model ensembling results in more patients within the therapeutic range in silico compared to conventional dosing or using a single model.

Introduction

To minimize toxicity and avoid subtherapeutic treatment, various methods for individually optimized dosing strategies have been developed. One of these methods is the use of model-informed precision dosing (MIPD). Typically, plasma concentrations, obtained after reaching steady-state concentrations, are imputed into population pharmacokinetic (popPK) models to predict future drug concentrations in plasma and to adjust the dose accordingly. However, in an ideal situation, a model-informed dose recommendation can be provided a priori. An individualized model-informed dosing regimen at the start of treatment might lead to less aberrant concentrations, faster attainment of adequate therapeutic exposure, and improvement of patient outcomes. In this case, the dose recommendation is solely based on patient’s covariates, which are proven to affect exposure. However, due to the lack of imputed PK information, i.e., information informing the model on unexplained (inter-individual) variability, employing a model with a good fit for the patient is crucial to obtain trustworthy dose recommendations. However, with a growing number of popPK models in literature it becomes increasingly challenging to select an adequately performing popPK model.

popPK models are developed on a specific population under specific conditions, and therefore the PK predictions cannot be directly extrapolated to different or even to seemingly similar populations without proper validation. Implementing a priori MIPD using a model that is not suitable for the target population can lead to aberrant plasma exposures and might lead to patient harm in the form of toxicities or subtherapeutic treatment. In a perfect situation, a popPK model can be implemented that is developed on a perfect representation of the target population and has been prospectively validated in the target population. However, this is unfortunately often not the case. On the basis of the characteristics of the dataset used for model development, such as population and clinical situation, the most appropriate model is selected. In some cases, when PK data describing the target population are available, a numerical external evaluation can be performed, identifying the most appropriate model to describe the population [1]. Other methods to determine the best model using artificial intelligence are also being developed but have not yet been brought to the clinic [2, 3].

However, even the model with the best predictive performance in the overall population may not perfectly describe each patient in the population. The inability to describe each individual patient might be caused by a limited number or range of covariates in the model-development dataset, latent variables, or clusters that were not available or could not be identified during model development for a variety of reasons. As a single model most often does not provide the best fit for all patients, the question arises whether selecting a single model is sufficient to obtain trustworthy dose predictions for each individual patient. Uster et al. showed that model ensembling is a feasible approach to obtain reliable model-informed dose recommendations (Fig. 1) [4]. Uster et al. used PK samples to select and weigh different popPK models. When performing MIPD a priori, no samples to inform model selection are available. To perform model ensembling for a priori dose recommendations, a new methodology has to be developed and evaluated.

Therefore, we developed and evaluated various model-selection and ensembling methods to perform a priori dosing individualization. These methods rely on external evaluation, utilizing therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) samples from a prior population, to identify the optimal model or set of models to be used for dose recommendations for each patient.

Methods

The workflow for evaluating model-ensembling methods involved four steps: (1) collecting retrospective samples and models, (2) evaluating the fit of the models on the retrospective samples, (3) weighing and selecting the models, and (4) calculating a single individual dose advice from selected models.

Clinical Datasets

The model selection methods were studied in two distinct patient cohorts describing a cohort of hospitalized patients treated with vancomycin and an outpatient population treated with imatinib. The selection of these different cohorts aimed to assess the clinical applicability of the a priori model-ensembling approaches across diverse settings. The cohorts will be divided into a method-development cohort, which will be used to develop the methods and adjust the methods on the basis of sensitivity analyses, and an independent validation cohort that will not affect model weighing or selection.

The first cohort consists of patients receiving intravenous vancomycin at the intensive care unit (ICU) at the Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Collection and handling of data were according to protocol and approved by the medical ethical review board (MEC-2021-0793). Patients received a vancomycin bolus dose of 15 mg/kg infused over 1.5 h followed by a continuous infusion of 3 g/day, which was reduced in case of poor renal function. The second cohort consisted of patients treated with imatinib for gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) in an outpatient setting followed in the multicenter DPOG-TDM study [5]. The development cohort consisted of patients treated at the Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, whereas the validation set was made up of patients treated at the Radboud Medical Center in Nijmegen, the Netherlands.

As the goal was to predict the starting dose, only the first PK trough sample at steady state per patient was considered for both vancomycin and imatinib. Only information regarding patient characteristics that was available prior to therapy were used in this analysis. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, pregnancy, treatment with continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Additionally, patients were excluded who were treated with vancomycin 48 h prior to the investigation period and patients who had undergone dose adjustments prior to the first PK sample.

Population PK Models

A systematic literature search was conducted to select suitable popPK models describing vancomycin and imatinib PK. Models were excluded if they were developed using only pediatric data, if covariates were used that are not available in a standard clinical setting (e.g., α1-glycoprotein plasma levels or genetic information), if the full article was not available, or if the model was not reproducible. In case covariates were missing, the covariate was set to the median of the cohort of which the model was developed. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was maximized to 130 mL/min when calculated with the Cockcroft–Gault method [6].

As our aim was to provide a priori dose predictions, the population prediction, based on patient’s individual covariate values, was used in this research.

Model-Ensembling Methods

In total, four different methods were evaluated that relied on external evaluation. First, an external evaluation of the models on the development dataset was performed. The best model was determined on the basis of relative bias and the root mean squared error (RMSE) of the model prediction. Second, all models were ensembled with equal weights (uninformed model ensembling), meaning that the median of the optimal dosing recommendations of all models predictions per patient was used as outcome. Third, we developed a weighting method on the basis of covariate stratified external evaluation. Using these weights, models were ensembled (Fig. 2). In this method the method-development population was divided into subgroups per influential covariate (tertiles for imatinib and quartiles for vancomycin due to the size of the cohorts) resulting in one subgroup per covariate per patient [a patient can be appointed to a low-C-reactive protein (CRP) group, a high weight group, etc.]. Using the relative bias and the RMSE, every model was scored for each subgroup and these metrics were combined using a negative exponential function into an importance score in each subgroup (equations and R code in the supplementary files). Thereafter, to identify whether models performing adequately in a specific subgroup should be given more influence on the dose recommendation to other models, the proportion of predictions/observations outside of the 80–125% interval (∉) in each subgroup was computed. A larger proportion of predictions outside this interval indicates that well-performing models in this subgroup should be given more weight in the final dose recommendation compared with well-performing models in subgroups in which almost all models showed good predictions. All model weights were multiplied using this subgroup-specific influence score. The obtained model-score was divided by the total of weights of all models per patient, leading to a percentual influence of each model for each patient. In case a covariate was missing, the influence of that covariate was omitted in the final dose prediction. The influence score was used to obtain a dose recommendation.

Lastly, a decision tree method was used for model selection and ensembling using R v4.3.0 [R Core Team (2021), Vienna, Austria] and the Rpart package (v4.1.21) (Fig. 2, code available in the supplementary materials). The decision trees created subgroups that were adequately described per model (≥ 50% of patients on target). All relevant covariates could be incorporated as a decision node in the decision tree. Predictions were deemed correct when the prediction/observation for vancomycin was within the 81–119% interval for vancomycin, reflecting a 17–25 mg/L interval for a target of 21 mg/L. As the therapeutic interval for imatinib is larger, the 80–125% bioequivalence interval was used for imatinib. When a patient was classified into a leaf node with ≥ 50% of the patients on target, the dose prediction of this model was deemed adequate, and this model was included in the final dose prediction. The complexity parameter was set to 0.01, the minimum number of observations that must exist in a node for a split to be attempted, and the minimum number of patients in any terminal leaf node were both set to 10% of the method-development group. In case a patient was not adequately described by any model in the decision tree method, the leaf node with the highest proportion of patients exposed to concentrations inside the therapeutic window of all models was selected and that model was used for dose predictions. See Figs. S1 and S2 for a depiction of evaluations performed in the weighing steps of both the weighed model ensembling and decision tree method.

Dosing Targets and Simulations

The therapeutic interval for vancomycin is 17–25 mg/L, which is used in clinical practice when vancomycin is given by continuous infusion [7]. Therefore, the dose predictions for vancomycin aimed to obtain a concentration of 21 mg/L in the middle of the range. For imatinib a lower boundary of 1100 mg/L has been described as predictive of treatment outcomes [8]. An upper boundary that could be predictive of toxicity has not been described in scientific literature. Therefore, steady-state trough levels 1100–3000 ng/mL were set as therapeutic interval. The target trough concentration was set to 1600 ng/mL.

The optimal dose per model was obtained using dose iterations. All doses were simulated in NONMEM for each model and patient (100–800 mg/day in steps of 10 mg for imatinib, 500–4500 mg/day in steps of 10 mg for vancomycin). The dose with the least absolute difference with the target concentration was used as dose prediction specific for that model and patient. In the unweighted model-ensembling and decision tree method the median of all (selected) model dose predictions was used as dose recommendation. For the model-weighing method, the weighted optimal dose, rounded to 100 mg, was used as final dose recommendation.

To evaluate the results of implementing the dose predictions, the imatinib concentrations were extrapolated to true trough concentrations using linear extrapolation with a half-life used of 18 h for imatinib, as is clinical practice at the Erasmus Medical Center. This has also been shown to be feasible and to result in acceptable predictions of true trough concentrations [9, 10]. Additionally, as both imatinib and vancomycin show dose proportionality [11, 12], the new concentration was obtained by multiplying the (extrapolated) concentration by the ratio of predicted optimal dose over actual administered dose.

Population PK Models

In total, 42 vancomycin and 17 imatinib models were identified. After examination of the models, 26 vancomycin models [13–38] and 12 imatinib models [39–50] were selected for inclusion. The included models are summarized in Tables S1, and S2. Reasons for exclusion per model are presented in Table S3.

External Evaluation

The available models from the literature showed a wide variety of fits on the clinical data. All fits are depicted in Fig. 3. For vancomycin, 36.9% of the predictions were within the 81–119% prediction/observation interval. In total, 31.9% of all imatinib predictions were in the 80–125% range. For vancomycin, the model created by Lim et al. showed the lowest sum of absolute median bias (−1.9%) and RMSE (35.7), and was therefore ranked as the best.

Boxplots showing the prediction/observation ratio for the vancomycin (top) and imatinib (bottom) PK models. The tables at the bottom depict relative bias and relative RMSE. The dashed red lines represent the 81–119% interval for vancomycin and the 80–125% prediction/observation interval for imatinib

The popPK imatinib model by Wang et al. showed the lowest median relative bias of − 5.0% compared with the other imatinib popPK models. The RMSE of Wang et al. was second lowest, with 42.5%. Only the RMSE of Gotta et al. (42.1%) was lower, but this model showed significant relative bias (− 36.5%). On the basis of this external evaluation, the model of Wang et al. showed the best predictive performance.

Sensitivity Analyses

When obtaining dose predictions for the vancomycin cohort in the weighted ensembling method, using a penalty of − 5 for relative bias and − 25 for relative RMSE resulted in the best performance in the method-development cohort (Fig. S3). Changing the interval in which the weight of each covariate was determined did not affect the outcomes and was therefore kept at 81–119% (Fig. S4). For imatinib, the penalty for relative bias was set to − 20, whereas the penalty for RMSE was set to − 4 (Fig. S5). Varying the correct interval in the weighing method did not result in any significant changes and was therefore kept at the initial interval of 80–125% (Fig. S6).

For the weighing method, the weights of each model used for dose predictions are presented in Tables S4 and S5. Weights given by the weighing method did not result in a similar ranking of models compared with the external evaluation. For example, Demetri et al.’s model, which was ranked third in the external evaluation, only influenced the final predictions by 4.86%, and ranked seventh out of 12 models. Vancomycin model predictions showed similar patterns of models getting more or less weight compared with the expected weights on the basis of the external evaluation of the total cohort. For example, the model by Kovacevic et al. was the most influential model in the model-weighing and ensembling method. However, the model was ranked 13th out of 26 models in performance in the external evaluated. The model adequately described patients with a low body weight (< 22.9 kg/m2), a creatinine just above the median (80.0–114.3 µmol/L), and low albumin (< 18.8 g/L).

The weights of each model for the decision tree method differed substantially from the weights given in the weighing and ensembling method. In the vancomycin cohort, the most influential model was Lim et al. for the weighted model ensembling, with 15.4% of weight on the final model predictions (third in the decision tree method), whereas this was Medellin-Garibay et al. in the decision tree method with 7.74% (sixth in the weighted ensembling method). On average, patients were described by an average of nine adequately performing models per patient in the decision tree method. One patient was solely adequately described by the model of Ji et al. In the imatinib cohort, the decision tree method did not identify any leaf nodes for the model of Judson et al., resulting in a score of 29% correctness, and this popPK therefore had no influence on dose recommendations for any patient in the decision tree method. The model-weighing and ensembling method identified that although the model by Eechoute et al. had the second most weight in the model weighing method, it showed sixth-best predictive performance for imatinib in the overall population. The model was the best-performing one when describing imatinib plasma concentrations in patients with albumin plasma levels < 34 g/L, and in patients with a WBC count of 4.95–6.75 × 109. In the decision tree method, Eechoute et al. performed well for patients with a weight ≥ 69 kg, and an albumin level < 36 g/L (26% of patients). Both methods identified the model to be adequately performing in patients with albumin values lower than the median. Validation Cohort

The results of the vancomycin validation cohort showed similar results compared with the method-development cohort. All methods increased the proportion of patients exposed with concentrations inside the therapeutic window compared with the observed plasma sample. The relative improvement from the observed concentrations in the vancomycin cohort was higher in the validation cohort compared with the model-development cohort (Table 2). The decision tree and ensembling method resulted in the largest proportion of patients exposed to concentrations within the therapeutic window. All methods decreased the median vancomycin concentration toward the therapeutic window, except for the unweighted ensembling method, which resulted in a median Css of 25.9 ng/mL.

In the imatinib validation cohort, the unweighted ensembling resulted in the largest proportion of patients exposed to concentrations in the target range (improvement of 38.0%). The method in which the best model was used resulted in the smallest proportion of patients exposed to concentrations within the therapeutic window. However, this was still an increase of 19.0% compared with standard dosing. In the imatinib validation set, each method led to at least two patients > 5000 ng/mL. These two patients were the same two patients in each method and were the two patients above the therapeutic window when no dose prediction method was used.

Discussion

Multiple model selection and ensembling methods for a priori dose individualization were developed and evaluated. Using these methods, the best set of popPK models per patient for a priori dosage individualization can be identified using a relatively small set of TDM samples as external evaluation. All methods resulted in an increase in the proportion of patients exposed to concentrations within the therapeutic window compared with conventional dosing. Weighted ensembling performed best for imatinib, whereas the decision tree method performed best for vancomycin. This research shows that it is possible to individualize dosing a priori by using model selection and ensembling using a set of TDM samples. In different populations, settings, treatments, and administration routes, performing dose recommendations using the weighing or decision trees model ensembling methods can result in approximately 50% more patients inside the target range compared with current clinical practice.

Whereas TDM and a posteriori MIPD react to a clinical situation, this research offers the opportunity to proactively provide a model-informed dose prediction for each individual patient at the start of their treatment. Implementation can lower the amount of blood samples that are necessitated by TDM, as ±50% fewer patients would need a second TDM sample. Nonetheless, as not all patients get into the therapeutic window with the predicted dose, patients should still be followed up using TDM or a posteriori MIPD. This could also be performed using model ensembling as proposed by Uster et al. [4]. Whereas all methods increased the proportion of patients exposed to concentrations within the therapeutic window, the results varied between the different methods. The most appropriate popPK model, as evaluated by an external evaluation, correctly predicted drug concentrations (80–125% prediction/observation interval) for ±50% of patients. This would result in ±50% of the patients receiving an imprecise dose prediction. The unweighted ensembling approach did not distinguish the adequately performing models from the non-adequately performing models. Whereas this method did not substantially increase the proportion of patients exposed to concentrations inside the therapeutic interval for both vancomycin cohorts, it did increase this proportion in both imatinib cohorts. As most models underpredicted the observed imatinib concentration, and most observed concentrations were below the therapeutic interval, patients were mostly dose-augmented, leading to more patients in the therapeutic interval. However, when most models do not describe the individual patients in a population well, which is often the case when working with special populations such as the vancomycin ICU cohort, the unweighted ensembling method performs poorly.

Interestingly, although the outcomes of the model ensembling using the weighing method and decision tree method were similar in the method-development cohort of imatinib, the weights of each model differed substantially. The decision tree method could distinguish patients on a continuous scale and select patients on multiple covariates and incorporated categorical covariates. This renders the decision tree method sensitive to overfitting. However, a balance needed to be found as setting too rigid constraints resulted in no branches in the decision tree. However, the similarity in the relative improvement in both the development and the independent external validation cohort showed that overfitting seemed to be limited. Both the weighing and ensembling and the decision tree method adequately censored poor-performing models in specific situations; these did not adequately describe our population.

In literature, multiple model-ensembling methods have been presented. However, to our knowledge, this research is the second to do so a priori and the first to do this on the basis of clinical data. The synthetic model combination (SMC) of Chan et al. previously described an a priori model ensembling method for vancomycin based on patient characteristics [3]. This method is based on a comparison between the characteristics of the individual patient and the characteristics of the model-development population of each popPK model, with models made with similar patient populations as the target patient gaining more weight in the final dose prediction. The presented methods utilize external evaluation, requiring TDM data from a population similar to the population in which a dose recommendation is needed. A major advantage of this approach is that models with poor performance are excluded, even though these models might be describing a similar patient population on paper. In addition, models adequately describing the PK profile of a patient outside of the model-development population can be identified. Prior research has shown that implementing a model without proper external validation leads to poor predictions [51–54]. This is particularly the case in populations in which PK is hard to predict, such as patients in the ICU or neonates [55–57]. This highlights that choosing the right (set of) models is of paramount importance when a dose prediction should be given in an a priori setting. The required dataset for external validation will be easily obtained in centers that already perform TDM or a posteriori MIPD, especially for vancomycin, for which TDM or MIPD is already implemented in clinical practice in a large number of medical centers. Most available TDM datasets solely consist of trough concentrations, which is typically not informative enough to construct a stable and reliable population PK model. Additionally, important covariate relationships might be missed in small datasets. Therefore, using this dataset for external evaluation may be the most effective use of these datasets.

Major limitations of this study are the absence of a prospectively performed validation and limitations in data. The current tool is currently in the process of being developed to meet the requirements of the Medical Devices Regulation to obtain further prospective validation. A particular strength of the study is that it shows the broad clinical applicability in two very different clinical settings, for both an intravenously inpatient setting and an orally administered outpatient treatment setting. However, a change in methodology might be required when implementing the described techniques in settings with more complicated PK, patient characteristics, or treatment regimens. Additionally, using a single point as target may not always be the optimal approach for achieving as much adequate exposure as possible [58, 59]. As mentioned before, whereas using model evaluation has the disadvantage of requiring data, it also poses a strength, as the best performing models are selected on the basis of the performance in the target population. In addition, ill-fitting models will be discarded or given minimal weight. Another strength of this research is the performance in the validation cohort, which was independent of the method-development cohort.

In conclusion, this research shows the ability to perform a priori model-informed precision dosing with model selection and ensembling. Dosing with the use of model selection and ensembling by weighing or decision trees resulted in a major increase in the proportion of patients attaining adequate PK concentrations compared with standard dosing. Using relatively simple statistical methods, we may be able to increase the proportion of patients with adequate exposure by approximately 50%. The methods reached similar improvements in different clinical settings with differently administered treatments, which underlines the broad applicability of performing these methods. Implementation may contribute to faster PK target attainment, leading to less TDM samples and potentially improving treatment outcomes.
