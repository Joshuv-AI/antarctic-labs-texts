Evaluation of serum MUC5AC for identifying high grade cervical lesions in a prospective cohort from Türkiye
Authors: Ilkbal Temel Yüksel, Gazi Güner, Neçirvan Çağdaş Çaltek, Semih Tek, Ayşe Hazırbulan, Gözde Şahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbnormal expression of mucins and changes in their glycosylation are linked to the onset and advancement of cancerous conditions. This research sought to assess the diagnostic significance of serum MUC5AC levels in differentiating between high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions, low-grade lesions, and normal cervical findings, as well as to investigate its potential as a non-invasive biomarker to enhance cervical cancer screening and early detection methods.MethodsA total of 138 women were stratified into three groups: CIN 1 (LSIL) (n = 38), CIN 2–3 (HSIL) (n = 54), and controls (n = 46). Serum levels of MUC5AC were assessed through enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). All participants underwent cytological assessment, HPV testing, and colposcopy with targeted biopsy; endocervical curettage and LEEP were performed when indicated.ResultsSerum MUC5AC levels were significantly higher in the CIN 2–3 group compared to both the control and CIN 1 groups (p = 0.016), while no significant difference was found between the control and CIN 1 groups. ROC analysis demonstrated that MUC5AC had moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing CIN 2–3 from controls (AUC = 0.634, p = 0.022) and from CIN 1 (AUC = 0.658, p = 0.011), with high sensitivity but low specificity. No significant discriminative value was observed in the comparisons involving the CIN 1 and control groups.ConclusionSerum MUC5AC exhibited significant sensitivity in detecting high-grade cervical lesions; however, its specificity was somewhat restricted. Although it may not serve as an adequate standalone marker for early lesions, it shows potential for identifying advanced pathology.Keywords: Mucin biomarker, Early cancer detection, Mucin 5AC, MUC5AC, Prognosis, Diagnosis, Cervix uteri

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and approximately 350,000 deaths reported in 2022, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. The burden of this disease is disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries, where limited access to HPV vaccination, cervical cancer screening programs, and early treatment options persists. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary etiological factor, with HIV-positive women carrying a significantly higher risk [1]. Despite being largely preventable through prophylactic HPV vaccination and effective screening strategies, cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality. Early detection and prompt treatment can lead to a complete cure in many cases [1, 2]. In Türkiye, cervical cancer continues to represent a significant public health concern, as documented in national reports aligned with WHO initiatives aimed at eliminating cervical cancer by 2030 [3]. High-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIL)/(CIN 2–3) represents a critical precursor to invasive cervical cancer [4]. A study conducted in Samsun, Turkey, analyzing data from 1,072 women who underwent cervical biopsies between 2015 and 2019, found that 39.6% were diagnosed with pre-invasive lesions (CIN 2–3) [5]. Although advances have been made in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic strategies, cervical cancer still exhibits high rates of morbidity and mortality, highlighting the critical need for further research to improve early detection and enhance patient survival.

Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC), a high-molecular-weight, gel-forming glycoprotein, belongs to the secreted mucin family and is physiologically expressed in the epithelium of the stomach, respiratory tract, and endocervix [6]. Aberrant expression of MUC5AC has been documented in a variety of malignant conditions, including colorectal, pancreatic, gallbladder, and biliary tract cancers [7–10]. Its upregulation is believed to influence tumor behavior by modifying the tumor microenvironment, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing metastatic potential [6]. Studies have also suggested its role as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in cancers such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma, particularly when detected in serum [8, 11, 12]. However, variability in detection methods and lack of standardized positivity thresholds have limited its clinical application. Although MUC5AC is primarily associated with glandular epithelia, previous studies have demonstrated aberrant and heterogeneous expression of this mucin in cervical carcinomas, including squamous subtypes [13, 14]. Such findings suggest that MUC5AC may reflect broader tumorigenic changes within the cervical microenvironment, thereby providing a rationale to explore its diagnostic role across both squamous and glandular lesions. Nevertheless, the presence of MUC5AC in tissues that are normally negative for this mucin may serve as a surrogate marker of neoplastic transformation.

Previous studies have shown that while cytology and HPV-based tests provide valuable screening tools, their sensitivity and specificity remain limited. Rokita et al. compared conventional cytodiagnostics, high-risk HPV DNA/mRNA tests, and p16/Ki-67 dual-staining (CINtecPLUS) in identifying low-grade versus high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions. They found that while conventional Pap smears achieved 85% sensitivity but only 23% specificity for CIN2+, the use of HPV DNA increased sensitivity to 91% (specificity 33%), and HPV mRNA testing achieved 79% sensitivity with 67% specificity. Remarkably, the CINtecPLUS test demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 67% specificity for CIN2 + detection [15]. Del Pino et al. reported that promoter methylation of CADM1, MAL, and miR-124 increased progressively with cervical lesion severity, achieving 84.6% sensitivity and 74.0% specificity for detecting HSIL/CIN2-3 and carcinoma, with specificity further improving to 85.1% when combined with hr-HPV testing [16]. Despite growing interest in MUC5AC expression across various malignancies, few studies have explored its role in cervical neoplasia. Preliminary data indicate that MUC5AC can be expressed in cervical carcinomas, albeit in a heterogeneous manner [13, 14]. Given its immunoreactivity in other glandular and mucinous tumors, and its association with high-grade neoplastic changes, further investigation into its potential role in cervical intraepithelial neoplasia is warranted. Understanding the expression profile of MUC5AC in cervical lesions could contribute to improved risk stratification and biomarker development, particularly in distinguishing high-grade lesions that require timely intervention.

Accordingly, this prospective study aimed to investigate the predictive value of serum MUC5AC levels in detecting high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions. Serum MUC5AC concentrations were measured and analyzed in relation to cytological assessments, HPV status, cervical biopsy findings, endocervical curettage, and loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) results. By evaluating the diagnostic performance of serum MUC5AC, we sought to explore its potential as a non-invasive biomarker to support early detection and inform clinical decision-making in cervical precancerous lesions. We further hypothesised that elevated serum MUC5AC in high-grade lesions may reflect aberrant secretion by dysplastic epithelial cells, disruption of epithelial integrity, or local inflammatory changes, making it a potentially informative circulating biomarker.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, single-centre, longitudinal cohort study involved follow-up of patients with histologically diagnosed with high-grade intraepithelial lesions, and was conducted at the Gynecologic Oncology Clinic of Basaksehir Cam and Sakura City Hospital between December 1, 2024, and May 1, 2025. The study was designed to evaluate the predictive value of serum MUC5AC levels in diagnosing high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions. It involved no additional procedures beyond routine clinical care. The only research-specific parameter measured was the serum MUC5AC level. No therapeutic interventions were modified as part of this study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Basaksehir Cam and Sakura City Hospital, Istanbul, Türkiye (Study Protocol Number: E-96317027-5514.10.10-259603251), and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and all individuals provided informed consent.

Study population

All participants were prospectively and consecutively recruited at the time of diagnostic evaluation. Peripheral blood samples were collected immediately after histological confirmation of cervical pathology and prior to any therapeutic interventions. Inclusion criteria comprised women with histologically confirmed CIN or benign findings, while patients with cervical cancer, uterine fibroids, or polycystic ovary syndrome were excluded. The temporal relationship between biomarker sampling and diagnostic confirmation was thus standardized across the cohort. A total of 138 women were stratified into three diagnostic groups based on histopathological and clinical findings:

Patients diagnosed with high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), (CIN 2–3), based on cervical biopsy,

Patients diagnosed with high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), (CIN 2–3), based on cervical biopsy,

Patients with low-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions (LSIL), (CIN 1),

Patients with low-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions (LSIL), (CIN 1),

Control subjects with benign findings confirmed by cervical cytology and negative HPV test results.

Control subjects with benign findings confirmed by cervical cytology and negative HPV test results.

Patients with a diagnosis of cervical cancer, uterine fibroids, or polycystic ovary syndrome were excluded. Additionally, HPV vaccinated participants were not included in the study.

Data collection

Sample collection and biochemical analysis

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected following diagnostic confirmation. The serum samples were labeled, stored in cold-chain containers, and transported to the central laboratory. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, the sera were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum MUC5AC levels were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. All serum samples were anonymised and coded before analysis, and the laboratory personnel performing the ELISA measurements were blinded to the participants’ clinical and histopathological data to minimise measurement bias.

Data collection parameters

The demographic information, clinical characteristics (e.g., age, smoking history, medication history), cervical cytology results, HPV test results, colposcopic biopsy findings, and serum MUC5AC levels were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, frequency, and percentage) were used to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test assessed the normality of data distribution. Independent numerical variables were compared using the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were applied where necessary. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. IBM Corp., Armonk, NY., and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 138 women were included in the study: 46 in the control group, 38 with CIN 1, and 54 with high-grade cervical pathology (CIN 2, CIN 3, or cervical cancer). The mean age across all participants was 39.96 ± 10.24 years. There were no statistically significant differences between groups in terms of age (p = 0.920), gravida (p = 0.456), or parity (p = 0.495). Serum MUC5AC levels

Serum MUC5AC concentrations varied significantly across the three groups (p = 0.016). The mean serum MUC5AC level was 5.21 ± 0.92 in the control group, 5.21 ± 1.10 in the CIN 1 group, and 6.38 ± 3.23 in the CIN 2–3/cervical pathology group. Post-hoc analysis demonstrated that MUC5AC levels were significantly elevated in the CIN 2–3/cervical pathology group compared to both the control and CIN 1 groups, while no significant difference was observed between the control and CIN 1 groups. Post-hoc analysis revealed that MUC5AC levels were significantly elevated in the CIN 2–3 group compared to both the control and CIN 1 groups, with no significant difference between the control and CIN 1 groups. ROC curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of serum MUC5AC levels in distinguishing between different patient cohorts. In the comparison of the CIN 2–3/cervical pathology group with the control group, the area under the curve (AUC) was determined to be 0.634 (p = 0.022). Utilizing a cut-off value of 4.521, the test demonstrated a sensitivity of 99.9%, specificity of 28.3%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 99.9%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 61.6%, and an overall diagnostic accuracy of 66.7%. These results are depicted in Fig. 1, which illustrates the ROC curve for this group comparison. When comparing the CIN 2–3/cervical pathology group to the CIN 1 group, the AUC was found to be 0.658 (p = 0.011), indicating statistical significance. With a cut-off value of 4.589, the test achieved a sensitivity of 98.1%, specificity of 31.6%, PPV of 99.9%, NPV of 66.3%, and an accuracy of 70.3%. The corresponding ROC curve is shown in Fig. 2, emphasizing the test’s capability to differentiate between high- and low-grade lesions. In contrast, the comparison between the CIN 1 and control groups revealed no statistically significant discriminatory value, with an AUC of 0.473 (p = 0.669). This lack of distinction is illustrated in Fig. 3, where the ROC curve closely follows the diagonal line, indicating limited diagnostic utility. Finally, when all lesion groups (CIN 1 and CIN 2–3 combined) were compared to the control group, the AUC was calculated to be 0.567, which was not statistically significant (p = 0.202). As depicted in Fig. 4, the ROC curve indicates a restricted ability of serum MUC5AC to identify any cervical pathology from healthy controls. Discussion

In this prospective study, we observed that serum MUC5AC levels were significantly higher in the CIN 2–3 (HSIL) group compared with both the control and CIN 1 groups, while no significant difference was detected between controls and CIN 1. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis confirmed that serum MUC5AC had considerable sensitivity but limited specificity, reflecting only moderate discriminative capacity in identifying high-grade lesions. This restricted specificity reduces its value as a standalone diagnostic marker; however, its high sensitivity suggests potential utility as a supportive tool when combined with established approaches such as cytology, HPV testing, or other biomarkers. Although serum MUC5AC demonstrated very high sensitivity in detecting high-grade cervical lesions, its notably low specificity raises concerns regarding potential false-positive results and the risk of unnecessary diagnostic procedures in real-world screening. This limitation reduces its feasibility as a stand-alone biomarker. However, its strong sensitivity suggests that MUC5AC may have value as part of a combined diagnostic algorithm. Integrating MUC5AC measurement with established modalities such as cytology, HPV testing, or p16/Ki-67 dual staining could help optimize overall diagnostic performance by maintaining high sensitivity while improving specificity. These findings highlight the exploratory role of MUC5AC as a complementary rather than definitive diagnostic assay, warranting validation in larger, multi-marker studies.

Zhao et al. examined the expression patterns of mucins in both nonneoplastic and neoplastic glandular epithelium of the uterine cervix, specifically focusing on MUC1, MUC2, and MUC5AC [13]. Their findings indicated that MUC5AC is typically expressed in the surface epithelium of the endocervix, but its expression becomes inconsistent and often diminished in cases of cervical adenocarcinomas. Conversely, MUC2, which is not present in normal endocervical tissue, was frequently found to be upregulated in mucinous carcinomas, especially of the intestinal type. While their research offers significant insights into mucin dynamics at the tissue level through immunohistochemistry, it does not explore the potential of these markers for serum-based diagnostics. In this regard, our research builds upon the existing knowledge by assessing serum levels of MUC5AC as a non-invasive biomarker for high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions. Unlike Zhao et al., who concentrated exclusively on glandular malignancies, our research encompasses both squamous and glandular lesions and evaluates the diagnostic utility across the entire spectrum of cervical pathology. Therefore, while the findings of Zhao et al. enhance the understanding of mucin biology in tumorigenesis, our results indicate that MUC5AC may also be valuable for the early detection and risk stratification of cervical precancerous lesions through a clinically accessible, serum-based method. Although MUC5AC expression is heterogeneous in cervical tissues and sometimes reduced in invasive carcinoma, its upregulation in preinvasive glandular and squamous lesions has been documented and may account for the serum rise observed in our study. Nevertheless, confounding factors such as glandular metaplasia or systemic inflammatory processes could also contribute to elevated serum levels. Future research should aim to disentangle these mechanisms by correlating serum concentrations with tissue expression patterns and controlling for inflammatory markers or benign conditions that may affect mucin levels.

A study by Baker et al. investigated the expression of several mucins, including MUC1, MUC2, MUC4, and MUC5AC, across a diverse range of benign, premalignant, and malignant glandular lesions in the uterine cervix and endometrium [14]. The findings revealed that MUC5AC was consistently present in benign endocervical lesions such as tubal metaplasia and microglandular hyperplasia, but its expression markedly diminished in adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and invasive endocervical carcinomas. Notably, well-differentiated endocervical adenocarcinomas maintained focal positivity for MUC5AC, while the majority of endometrial adenocarcinomas showed a complete absence of MUC5AC expression. These results indicate that MUC5AC could be instrumental in differentiating between endocervical and endometrial tumors, as well as distinguishing benign from malignant glandular lesions. In contrast to the tissue-based immunohistochemical methodology employed by Baker et al., our study assessed MUC5AC levels in serum, with the objective of establishing it as a non-invasive biomarker for high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia. While Baker et al. concentrated solely on glandular pathology, our investigation included both glandular and squamous precancerous lesions, revealing significantly elevated serum MUC5AC levels in the CIN 2–3/cervical pathology cohort. Consequently, our results not only enhance the histopathological findings presented by Baker et al. but also broaden the clinical relevance of MUC5AC as a prospective serum-based diagnostic instrument for cervical preinvasive disease.

In contrast to earlier research conducted by Zhao et al. and Baker et al., which focused on MUC5AC expression in cervical tissue through immunohistochemical techniques, our investigation represents the inaugural effort to evaluate its diagnostic capabilities in serum as a non-invasive biomarker for high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions. While MUC5AC appears to be a promising marker for glandular transformation, it is not commonly assessed in clinical settings, likely due to the scarcity of studies validating its diagnostic or prognostic relevance. Nevertheless, growing evidence indicates that MUC5AC expression may provide insights into the underlying biology and progression of cervical neoplasia. By establishing a significant correlation between increased serum MUC5AC levels and high-grade lesions, our research seeks to fill this void in the existing literature and underscore the potential of MUC5AC as a clinically valuable biomarker that deserves further exploration.

Beyond cervical pathology, MUC5AC has been investigated as a potential biomarker in various gastrointestinal malignancies. In pancreatic cancer, studies have demonstrated that serum MUC5AC levels are significantly elevated in patients compared to healthy controls and those with benign pancreatic diseases. Notably, combining MUC5AC with CA19.9 has been shown to enhance diagnostic accuracy, particularly in differentiating pancreatic cancer from chronic pancreatitis [17]. Similarly, in biliary tract cancers, elevated serum MUC5AC concentrations have been associated with advanced tumor stages and poorer prognoses, suggesting its utility in disease stratification [18]. In colorectal cancer, aberrant expression of MUC5AC has been linked to tumor progression and chemoresistance, indicating its potential role in therapeutic decision-making [19]. Despite these promising findings, MUC5AC has not yet been adopted into routine clinical practice, primarily due to the limited number of large-scale, prospective studies validating its efficacy and the lack of standardized assay protocols. Our study addresses this gap by evaluating serum MUC5AC levels in the context of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, providing evidence for its potential as a non-invasive biomarker for high-grade lesions. By demonstrating a significant association between elevated serum MUC5AC levels and high-grade cervical lesions, our findings suggest that MUC5AC could serve as a valuable tool in the early detection and management of cervical neoplasia.

Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), particularly CIN 2 and 3, is primarily caused by persistent infection with high-risk HPV types and poses a risk for progression to invasive cervical cancer if not detected and treated [20]. This risk is further increased in women living with HIV due to impaired immune clearance of HPV, resulting in higher rates and faster progression of CIN lesions [20]. Although current screening methods such as cytology and HPV testing have reduced cervical cancer incidence, they are limited by either low sensitivity or specificity and may not be universally accessible [20–22]. MUC5AC, a secretory mucin, has shown diagnostic and prognostic relevance in other malignancies such as pancreatic and biliary tract cancers, yet it has not been adopted into routine clinical use due to limited validation [4]. Our findings, showing significantly elevated serum MUC5AC levels in high-grade CIN compared to low-grade lesions and controls, suggest its potential as a non-invasive biomarker to aid early diagnosis and risk stratification in cervical neoplasia.

This research has several significant strengths. It stands out as one of the limited prospective studies investigating the diagnostic value of serum MUC5AC levels in cervical intraepithelial lesions, offering new insights into its potential role as a non-invasive biomarker. The study’s design included well-defined control, LSIL (CIN 1) and HSIL (CIN 2–3), facilitating comprehensive comparisons across the entire range of cervical pathology. Moreover, the application of histopathological confirmation for diagnosis enhances the credibility of our results. The novelty of our study lies in investigating serum MUC5AC as a potential non-invasive biomarker. Unlike tissue or cervical mucus-based assessments, blood-based biomarkers are less invasive, more easily repeatable, and potentially more accessible in routine clinical practice. Nonetheless, certain limitations must be recognized. Although the sample size is sufficient for preliminary analysis, it restricts the generalizability of the findings and may not fully represent the population’s diversity. No formal a priori power calculation was performed, as this investigation was exploratory in nature. The absence of long-term follow-up data precludes the evaluation of MUC5AC’s prognostic significance over time. Another limitation of our study is that patients with invasive squamous cell or glandular cervical carcinoma were not included. Considering that glandular and endocervical lesions pose significant diagnostic challenges, future studies should incorporate these patient groups to fully explore the potential of serum MUC5AC as a biomarker. Lastly, as serum MUC5AC has not yet been standardized for clinical application, the cut-off values and assay consistency may differ across various environments. Despite these constraints, our results underscore the potential of MUC5AC as a significant complement to current cervical cancer screening methods.

Conclusion

The current research indicated that serum MUC5AC exhibits high sensitivity but limited specificity in differentiating patients with high-grade cervical lesions (CIN 2–3) from those with low-grade lesions or normal cervical findings. While MUC5AC alone may not effectively distinguish early-stage lesions, its increased levels in advanced intraepithelial neoplasia indicate a potential role as a complementary diagnostic biomarker. Given its limited specificity, serum MUC5AC may be best considered as an adjunctive biomarker, with future studies needed to evaluate its integration into combined screening algorithms alongside cytology, HPV testing, or p16/Ki-67 dual staining.
