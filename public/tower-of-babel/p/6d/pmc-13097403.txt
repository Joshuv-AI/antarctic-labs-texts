‘Navigating Tiny Paths, Overcoming Huge Hurdles’: The Struggles of Nurses in Paediatric Intravenous Intervention
Authors: Abdullah Sarman, Suat Tuncay, Ferhat Abay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim of this study was to determine the problems experienced by nurses during intravenous intervention in children.DesignA descriptive, cross‐sectional study.MethodsThis study was conducted with nurses employed in primary and secondary healthcare institutions in a provincial centre situated in eastern Turkey. Data were gathered between March 2024 and April 2024, utilising socio‐demographic characteristic forms and intravenous intervention application forms developed by the researchers.ResultsThe study comprised 402 nurses. The predominant challenge noted during intravenous interventions was children exhibiting distress, manifesting as yelling or crying before/during the procedure. Furthermore, 74.3% of the nurses reported encountering family reactions when facing intravenous access challenges, 71.4% attributed intravenous access problems to professional experience, 58.4% associated these issues with the clinic/unit of employment and 52.3% linked them to educational factors.Patient or Public ContributionThe high rate of unsuccessful interventions, within the initial hours of a shift, suggests a need for attention to this issue. Factors such as the child's restlessness before or during the procedure, as well as lack of cooperation, were found to negatively impact the success of intravenous interventions. It is suggested that providing support and fostering cooperation from both the child and parents may enhance the success of such interventions.Keywords: child, intravenous intervention, nurse, peripheral catheterization

Background

Most patients admitted to hospitals or hospitalised have intravenous access for the administration of drugs or fluids (Teshome et al. 2023). This can lead to physical and mental complications for patients (Park et al. 2022). Repeated or unsuccessful intravenous interventions exacerbate these issues (Walsh 2008). When children's problems remain uncontrolled or unresolved, health professionals and parents may experience stress, helplessness and guilt (Semerci et al. 2020). Atraumatic care is one of the guiding principles of paediatric nursing (Furdon et al. 1998), wherein alleviating the child's potential difficulties is paramount for nurses (Semerci et al. 2020). Unsuccessful intravenous interventions pose a significant challenge for nursing practice (García‐Expósito et al. 2021). Pain and fear experienced by patients can result from tissue injury during intravenous procedures (Alsbrooks and Hoerauf 2022). Therefore, effective pain management and the implementation of appropriate nursing interventions are essential aspects of care, enhancing patient satisfaction (Mann and Carr 2008).

The nurse should be cognizant of the potential trauma the IV procedure may inflict upon the child (Naik et al. 2019). Unsuccessful IV access attempts can exacerbate the child's stress levels and diminish their cooperation during the procedure (Nakamura et al. 2020). Additionally, it has been documented that negative emotional states such as anxiety and fear may escalate for the child during this process (Yurdakul and Esenay 2023). When performing intravenous interventions, maintaining a calm demeanour, attempting to soothe the patient, and providing clear explanations and informative activities are believed to yield beneficial outcomes in mitigating the negative effects experienced by the child, their family and healthcare personnel (Wong et al. 2019).

It is a crucial responsibility of paediatric nurses to align their actions with the developmental characteristics of the child and to thoroughly comprehend the impact of pain, particularly in assessing the heightened pain associated with interventions causing traumatic effects on tissues such as vascular access (Pancekauskaitė and Jankauskaitė 2018). Successful evaluation has been shown to reduce errors during interventions (Lok et al. 2020). Despite the significant role nurses play in managing pain, fear and anxiety during invasive procedures, it has been noted that they may encounter challenges in employing independent nursing practices effectively during interventions for various reasons (Gheshlaghi et al. 2021).

Intravenous interventions in children pose unique challenges for nurses, particularly when children experience distress, resist procedures, or when family reactions add complexity to the process (Sharp et al. 2023). Despite the critical role of nurses in ensuring successful IV access, challenges related to professional experience, workplace conditions and educational background persist. Research indicates that inadequate training and experience contribute to unsuccessful IV attempts, leading to increased stress for both nurses and families (Alexander and Manno 2003; Brasaitė et al. 2016; Sharp et al. 2023). This study aimed to identify the specific challenges nurses encounter during intravenous interventions in paediatric patients and examine contributing factors, including professional experience, workplace environment and family involvement.

Methods

Design

This descriptive, cross‐sectional study was conducted, and data were collected between March 7, 2024 and April 30, 2024.

Sample and Population

The study population comprised nurses employed in multiple primary and secondary healthcare institutions located in an eastern Turkish centre. These institutions included both hospitals and outpatient clinics providing healthcare services in the region. No sampling was undertaken; the goal was to encompass the entire population of nurses working in these institutions, maximising volunteer participation. However, to ensure representativeness, a minimum sample size was estimated prior to data collection using power analysis (Pourhoseingholi et al. 2013). Based on the literature, it is reported that for descriptive and cross‐sectional studies, a sample size of 385 individuals is sufficient when drawn from an unspecified population, assuming a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, and a population proportion of 50% (Cohen 1992). Following completion of the study, power analysis was conducted utilising the G*Power 3.1 and OpenEpi Version 3 software packages.

Inclusion Criteria

This study was conducted in urban health care facilities located in the eastern region of Turkey. The study included public and private hospitals as well as outpatient clinics providing paediatric care. All nurses who performed intravenous interventions on paediatric patients were eligible for inclusion, regardless of the primary population served in their assigned units. Although some participants were employed in units predominantly serving adult patients (such as emergency departments or adult intensive care units), only nurses with direct experience in performing intravenous interventions on children were included in the study. Participants were explicitly instructed to respond to all study questions based solely on their experiences with paediatric patients and not on their experiences with adult patients.

Exclusion Criteria

The data of the nurses who filled in the questionnaire form inconsistently and incompletely were excluded from the study.

Data Collection Tools

Data were collected using the ‘socio‐demographic characteristics form’ and ‘intravenous intervention application form’.

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics Form

This form was developed by the researchers a priori, prior to data collection, based on an extensive review of the relevant literature (Gheshlaghi et al. 2021; Nakamura et al. 2020). The purpose of the form was to capture key personal and professional characteristics that may influence nurses' experiences with paediatric intravenous interventions. The form included questions regarding age, gender, marital status, family structure, educational attainment, professional tenure, willingness in choosing the nursing profession, perceived income level, unit of employment and duration of service in the current unit. All items were structured as closed‐ended questions to ensure clarity and consistency in responses.

Intravenous Intervention Application Form

The intravenous intervention application form was developed by researchers a priori, prior to data collection, following a comprehensive review of the literature on paediatric intravenous interventions and nurses' clinical experiences (Kuensting et al. 2009; Larsen et al. 2010; Sabri et al. 2013; Schults et al. 2019). The form included items assessing whether nurses had received specific training related to intravenous interventions, the frequency and types of problems encountered during paediatric intravenous procedures, perceived reasons for these problems (e.g., child‐related, family‐related, environmental and professional factors), and nurses' self‐perceived competence in performing intravenous interventions on paediatric patients. To ensure face and content validity, the preliminary version of the form was reviewed by an expert consisting of paediatric nursing academicians and experienced clinical nurses. Based on expert feedback, minor revisions were made to improve item clarity and relevance, and consensus was achieved on the final version of the form prior to data collection.

Data Collection

Data were collected through face‐to‐face administration of a questionnaire. The data collection process was carried out in various hospital settings, including paediatric units, emergency departments and outpatient clinics where nurses routinely performed intravenous interventions on children. The researchers visited each institution during regular working hours on weekdays, specifically between 09:00–10:30 in the morning and 13:30–15:00 in the afternoon, to maximise participation. Questionnaires were completed in a quiet and private environment to allow participants to respond freely without external influence. The questionnaire was self‐administered by the participants. The researchers did not verbally ask the questions nor record the responses on behalf of the participants. Prior to administration, standardised verbal instructions were provided, emphasising that all responses should be based solely on experiences related to intravenous interventions performed on paediatric patients. The role of the researchers was limited to providing procedural guidance and clarifying instructions when necessary. This procedure was implemented to ensure conceptual clarity and consistency in responses, particularly among nurses working in mixed or non‐paediatric units. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 8–10 min. Data collection was conducted over an 8‐week period, from March 7 to April 30, 2024, and included both day and night shifts.

Data Analysis

Data were evaluated using IBM SSPS Statistics 26.0 program. Descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage) were used in the analysis. Normal distributions of the variables were determined by Skewness and Kurtosis tests. In study, nurses were prompted to categorise the encountered problems based on their perceived importance using a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), a subjective evaluation method. The NRS assesses the effect size using numerical values; higher numbers indicate a positive effect, while lower numbers indicate a negative effect (Beltramini et al. 2017). Researchers commonly employ such scales across various domains, including pain assessment, satisfaction levels and affect dimensions (Beltramini et al. 2017). As these scales aim to quantify subjective experiences, they lack validity and reliability coefficients typical of scales with predefined cut‐off scores. Nurses were instructed to select a number between 0 and 5, signifying the importance they attributed to each problem: with 0 indicating ‘not at all important’ and 5 indicating ‘very important’ (Farrar et al. 2001).

Ethical Statements

Ethical approval was acquired from the Bingöl University Rectorate Health Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee for the study's execution (Approval number: 01.11.2023‐E.129874). Additionally, the requisite institutional permissions were secured for conducting the study in primary and secondary healthcare institutions within the central area of Bingöl province (Permission number: 07.03.2024‐149122). Moreover, nurses were duly informed about the study's objectives, and their consent was obtained prior to their participation. All procedures of this study were carried out in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The study sample consisted of 402 participants, which met the minimum sample size criteria required for representativeness. The mean age of the nurses who participated in the study was 27.95 ± 7.47 years, and the mean number of children they had was 0.92 ± 0.48. It was observed that 68.9% of the nurses were female, 57.7% were single, and 79.4% belonged to a nuclear family structure. Additionally, 80.1% of the nurses were university graduates, 73.9% had a medium income level, and 33.8% had a professional working period of 0–1 year. Furthermore, it was noted that 69.7% of the participants chose the nursing profession willingly, 23.6% worked in surgical services, and 52.2% worked 40 h per week (See Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, conducted to identify challenges encountered by nurses during intravenous intervention in children, it was revealed that only 18.1% of nurses succeeded on the first attempt, aligning with findings from previous studies (Black et al. 2005; Kuensting et al. 2009; Sabri et al. 2013; Yen et al. 2008). Kuensting et al. (2009) noted that peripheral catheterization in children poses difficulties for nurses, often requiring multiple attempts to place the cannula accurately (Kuensting et al. 2009). Black et al. (2005) underscored that failure in the initial attempt to place intravenous catheters in children heightens the risk of subsequent failures, potentially causing stress among nurses (Black et al. 2005). Sabri et al. (2013) found that nurses encountered failure rates ranging from 24% to 54% when administering drugs to children, attributing the challenges to factors such as analysis, medication, or blood transfusion (Sabri et al. 2013). de la Vieja‐Soriano et al. (2022) reported that the average number of attempts for intravenous intervention in children ranged between 1.75 and 2.3 (de la Vieja‐Soriano et al. 2022). These findings suggest that nurses encounter difficulties with intravenous access procedures in children, emphasising the need to implement proven methods to enhance first attempt success rates.

In this study, 26.1% of the nurses reported encountering challenges with intravenous interventions, while 24.8% highlighted the difficulty of managing children's shouting and crying before or during the procedure. Sabri et al. (2013) noted that when children are not sedated, cooperation is typically lacking, thereby complicating intravenous catheterization (Sabri et al. 2013). Hjelmgren et al. (2022) emphasised that blood collection from children demands specialised training or heightened attention from staff (Hjelmgren et al. 2022). Harnik and Moreiras (2014) observed that invasive procedures induce pain and stress in children, hindering compliance (Harnik and Moreiras 2014). Rybojad et al. (2022) found that children undergoing intravenous interventions often experience significant pain, impacting their cooperation (Rybojad et al. 2022). Larsen et al. (2010) stressed that successful intravenous interventions hinge upon the child's cooperation with the nurse (Larsen et al. 2010). Collectively, these findings underscore the importance for nurses to employ strategies for calming children before, during and after procedures to facilitate successful intravenous interventions.

One of the most significant findings of our study is that intravenous (IV) intervention difficulties occur more frequently during daytime hours (08:00–17:00). This trend may be attributed to multiple factors, including higher workload, increased patient volume, and frequent interruptions from routine hospital activities (Raun et al. 2025). Additionally, staff‐related dynamics—such as shift changes, varying experience levels among nurses on duty and potential fatigue—could further exacerbate these challenges. Larsen et al. (2010) found that the majority of unsuccessful intravenous interventions (63%) occurred during the day shift (07:00–19:00), attributing the increased error rate to inexperienced nurses who perceived themselves as inadequate (Larsen et al. 2010).

Supporting this observation, Schutijser et al. (2019) tracked nurses and documented interruptions during medication administration. Their study found that nurses experienced disruptions from both colleagues and patients, particularly during peak hours (Schutijser et al. 2019). The high‐intensity environment and constant movement during these periods may contribute to elevated stress levels among nurses. Moreover, the demand for IV interventions is inherently greater during daytime hours, which could also influence the frequency of complications.

It was determined that when nurses were unsuccessful in intravenous interventions, they faced reactions from families and complained to administrative units. Sabri et al. (2013) reported that repeated intravenous interventions caused anxiety and dissatisfaction in parents, frustration in nurses, and pain and anxiety in the child (Sabri et al. 2013). Another negative consequence of this situation is that the procedural pain experienced by all children, including newborns, may have physiological and psychological consequences in the future. Ng et al. (2022) reported that failed intravenous interventions caused frustration and distress in both patients and nurses, an increased number and cost of interventions, longer procedure completion times, and the need for more healthcare professionals (Ng et al. 2022). Repeated attempts can traumatise children (Chu et al. 2023). Some study results indicate that this procedure is an important quality indicator in healthcare delivery and that failure in the first attempt increases the anxiety of both nurses and parents (Taşdelen et al. 2024). Bragadóttir et al. (2017) asserted that delays stemming from repeated attempts due to the challenges of intravenous intervention in children directly impact the quality of care, patient safety, as well as patient and family satisfaction (Bragadóttir et al. 2017). In studies involving parents whose children underwent intravenous intervention, some parents noted that nurses focused solely on the procedural aspects, neglecting the emotional and physical needs of the child and family (Abdolkhaleghi et al. 2018; Taşdelen et al. 2024). Hence, nurses conducting intravenous interventions on children should prioritise practices such as family‐centred care throughout the procedure.

Among the nurses involved in this study, 71.4% believed that intravenous intervention issues were associated with professional experience. Larsen et al. (2010) noted that nurses with over 1 year of clinical experience exhibited a higher success rate in intravenous interventions. Similarly, the study found that inexperienced nurses experienced feelings of inadequacy, leading to decreased intervention success (Larsen et al. 2010). Likewise, Jacobson and Winslow (2005) observed that experience, age and a high level of confidence significantly influenced the success of intravenous catheterization procedures (Jacobson and Winslow 2005). Taşdelen et al. (2024) highlighted that novice paediatric nurses encountered challenges with intravenous interventions due to their lack of experience and apprehension towards practice (Taşdelen et al. 2024).

While participants in our study attested to the impact of experience on procedure success, there exist conflicting findings in the literature. de la Vieja‐Soriano et al. (2022) and Black et al. (2005) reported no significant correlation between nurses' professional experience and the success of intravenous interventions (Black et al. 2005; de la Vieja‐Soriano et al. 2022). It is suggested that engaging in intravenous interventions across diverse patient demographics during professional practice enhances nurses' procedural skills and diminishes the need for repeated attempts.

In this study, over half of the nurses indicated that issues with intravenous interventions were linked to education. Schults et al. (2019) emphasised that proficient personnel are essential for successful intravenous interventions (Schults et al. 2019). Larsen et al. (2010) noted that trained nurses encountered fewer challenges during intravenous interventions and experienced a reduction in the number of repeated attempts (Larsen et al. 2010). These findings underscore the impact of education and experience on the success rates of intravenous interventions, highlighting the potential benefits of implementing orientation programs, particularly for novice nurses.

Given the correlation between experience and success rates in intravenous interventions, it is clear that additional educational programs for novice nurses will positively impact patient outcomes. These programs not only enhance nurses' technical skills but also enable them to respond more effectively to challenges encountered during procedures. In particular, simulation‐based training and mentoring programs can offer valuable practical experience, leading to improved success rates and a reduction in procedural errors (National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine 2021).

Future research should delve deeper into the impact of structured training programs on skill development and error reduction in intravenous interventions. Additionally, the long‐term effects of such training on nurses' professional confidence, stress levels and overall patient safety should be comprehensively assessed. Moreover, examining factors such as sustainability, cost‐effectiveness and seamless integration of these programs into the workflow of healthcare organisations is essential to determine their broader applicability and effectiveness (National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine 2021).

Limitation

The main limitations of this study include its cross‐sectional design and its restriction to nurses working within a single province, which limits the generalizability of the findings to nurses in other regions. In addition, the predominance of participants in their first year of professional practice may indicate limited clinical experience, an important factor to consider when interpreting the results. Another limitation is that the study did not examine the specific content of the intravenous intervention training reported by participants, making it impossible to evaluate skill development following training. Furthermore, the study relied on self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall and response bias. Information regarding intravenous intervention attempts and related experiences was reported by participants based on their recollection and perceived typical practice, rather than extracted from electronic health records or direct observation. Although data collection procedures emphasised restricting responses to paediatric intravenous experiences—particularly for nurses working in mixed clinical settings—the possibility of recall bias cannot be entirely ruled out and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Implications for Practice

The findings of this study highlight the multifaceted challenges nurses face during intravenous (IV) interventions in paediatric patients, emphasising the need for comprehensive strategies to improve practice. First, it is crucial to provide ongoing in‐service training that focuses on paediatric IV skills, child‐specific vein anatomy and communication techniques for managing child and family anxiety. Simulation‐based training may enhance clinical competence and confidence in difficult IV access situations. Second, the presence and behaviour of family members during the procedure significantly affect nurses' performance. Developing institutional policies that guide family involvement—such as training parents on supportive behaviour or assigning a liaison to communicate with families—can create a more controlled and less stressful environment. Third, addressing environmental and systemic barriers such as staffing shortages, time pressure and lack of appropriate equipment is essential. Hospital administrations should ensure the availability of paediatric‐specific IV equipment and promote teamwork and adequate staffing to support safe and effective IV interventions. Finally, fostering a supportive work environment that values nurses' emotional well‐being and encourages open communication can reduce stress and increase job satisfaction. Integrating these findings into nursing protocols, educational programs and hospital policies has the potential to enhance patient safety, improve the success rate of IV interventions, and ensure a higher quality of paediatric nursing care.

Conclusion

In this study, it was determined that 26.1% of nurses encountered challenges while performing intravenous interventions in children. It was found that inexperienced nurses experienced greater difficulty during these procedures and required additional training in this area. It was observed that the primary cause of difficulties during intravenous interventions was the noncompliance of children with the procedure. Future research should investigate the effectiveness of targeted education programs designed for inexperienced nurses, assessing both immediate and long‐term improvements in procedural success rates. Moreover, studies evaluating patient and family satisfaction in relation to nurse training levels would provide valuable insights for optimising care quality. It is believed that education and experience play vital roles in the success of intravenous interventions in children, highlighting the need for comprehensive training programs to enhance the knowledge and skills of nurses in this regard.
