Splenic rupture secondary to pancreatic malignancy invasion: A rare case
Authors: Jason Zhou, Matthew Wilhide, Katherine Howard, Abigail Solom, Seema Anandalwar, Jaclyn Clark, Samantha Olafson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignancy is a rare etiology of splenic rupture, with most documented cases resulting from hematologic cancers. There have been very few reports of splenic rupture resulting from invasion or metastasis of adenocarcinoma and even fewer reports resulting from specifically pancreatic adenocarcinoma. In this case report, we outline the clinical course of a 60-year-old male with splenic rupture and hemoperitoneum following a ground level fall who was transferred to the Shock Trauma Center (STC) from a local emergency department. Outside of the ruptured spleen, no other traumatic injuries were found on examination or imaging. Due to the initial concern for traumatic etiology, exploratory laparotomy was performed with splenectomy and distal pancreatectomy. Postoperative pathology results revealed pancreatic adenocarcinoma with splenic invasion staged pT3N0. This report provides a novel example of splenic rupture in the background of locally advanced pancreatic adenocarcinoma and further solidifies the importance of maintaining a broad differential in cases of seemingly innocuous trauma.Keywords: Trauma surgery, Hemoperitoneum, Splenic rupture, Splenectomy, Distal pancreatectomy, Pancreatic adenocarcinoma

Introduction

Traumatic injuries are by far and away the most common etiology of spleen rupture, especially car accidents, which cause anywhere from 50 to 75 %. Sports injuries, classically after EBV mononucleosis, and blunt or penetrating traumas are significant causes too. Spontaneous splenic rupture is markedly rarer, but also possible – infections like mononucleosis and malaria, chronic inflammatory conditions like lupus, and to a lesser extent cancer, specifically lymphomas and leukemias, are documented etiologies [[1], [2], [3]]. This case report describes an adult male who presented after ground level fall found to have an advanced splenic injury managed by traditional trauma methodologies, but ultimately was found to have a postoperative pathologic finding of locally advanced pancreatic adenocarcinoma into the spleen. A case on metastatic pancreatic cancer being the primary etiology of splenic rupture has only been previously described once [4]. By documenting the trajectory of our patient's condition in the acute care setting, our report contributes to the ongoing literature on spleen rupture etiologies as well as pancreatic cancer pathophysiology by exploring a novel presentation that involves both.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male with past medical history of spinal stenosis causing chronic paresthesia of his hands, chronic cervical, lumber, and lower extremity pain, and end stage renal disease on hemodialysis presented to an outside emergency department (ED) complaining of nausea, vomiting, and severe generalized abdominal cramping that began after dialysis the night prior. He reported several episodes of non-bloody, non-bilious emesis, mild constipation, and fatigue. He denied any fevers or chills. He also denied any bleeding, weight loss, night sweats, shortness of breath, diarrhea, melena, or hematochezia. Though he did not endorse any recent traumatic event, he did recall multiple ground level falls, likely due to worsening paresthesia, with the most recent being one week prior to presentation.

Per outside hospital records, he initially appeared well, alert, and oriented, with stable vital signs. His physical exam was notable for an abdomen that was soft, non-tender, and non-distended. Bowel sounds were present and normal, and there was no organomegaly nor were there any masses appreciated. The head and neck exam showed slightly dry mucus membranes but was otherwise normal. All other exams were also non-significant.

As part of the investigation for the patient's abdominal symptoms, a CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was obtained revealing a large splenic laceration with subcapsular hematoma and hemorrhagic ascites. He received 1 unit of packed red blood cells and was subsequently transferred to this Level 1 trauma center by ground with a 10-min transport time.

Upon arrival, a standard trauma activation was initiated. On primary survey, he had a patient airway, breath sounds equal bilaterally, bilateral distal radial and dorsalis pedis pulses palpable, a GCS of 15, and mild abdominal distention. His primary set of vital signs were stable with a heart rate of 92, blood pressure of 159/95, respiratory rate of 21, oxygen saturation of 89 % on room air, improved with two liters of nasal cannula, and a temperature of 37.5C. The secondary exam was significant for abdominal distention and tenderness to palpation. He also noted cervical and lumbar back tenderness, which was consistent with his chronic spinal stenosis pain. There was also 2+ edema up to proximal shins bilaterally.

Ultimately, it was felt that the patient may have had a severe splenic laceration at the time of his fall a week prior. Since at that time no medical attention was obtained, the injury underwent a compulsory trial of non-operative management. His presentation now indicated a failure of this trial of non-operative management and operative intervention was indicated. This was discussed in an interdisciplinary setting between the trauma and vascular services and shared decision making was completed with the patient and his family who agreed.

The patient expeditiously went to the OR for exploratory laparotomy. Upon entry to the abdomen, two liters of hemoperitoneum was evacuated and a splenectomy with distal pancreatectomy was performed. Intraoperatively, it was noted that the spleen and distal pancreas were densely adhered with posterior fibrosis. The patient tolerated the procedure well and went on to a successful recovery with normalization of laboratory values, advancement to a normal diet, and normalization of bowel function. The post-operative pathology report for the removed specimen showed a 5 cm^3 ductal adenocarcinoma in the pancreatic tail invading into the spleen. Lymphovascular and perineural invasion was present, but lymph node dissection and margins were negative. The cancer was staged pT3N0. Oncology was consulted and recommended formal staging workup follow up outpatient treatment planning.

Discussion

Based on our patient's history, it is not clear whether the initial mechanism of injury was indeed traumatic or whether the splenic rupture was spontaneous, leading to an unclear timeline of hemoperitoneum. Regardless, the post-operative diagnosis of splenic rupture turned out to have a rare presentation of pancreatic cancer with locally invasive disease into the spleen. Not surprisingly due to location of disease, the patient was asymptomatic and didn't show any signs or symptoms of late-stage disease including abdominal pain radiating to the back, anorexia, weight loss, jaundice, pruritis, etc. [5]. Any imaging findings suggestive of malignant diagnosis within the distal pancreas were difficult to appreciate at the time of presentation secondary to the dramatic splenic injury and extensive hemoperitoneum. As a result of his initial traumatic presentation, our patient happened to receive the correct procedure for treatment of his, at that time, unknown pancreatic cancer. Much to our luck, the margins on pathology came back negative as well.

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma by itself is already an uncommon but deadly gastrointestinal (GI) cancer, accounting for 3 % of malignancies in the US, but 7 % of cancer deaths [6]. The one and five-year mortality rates are 24 % and 6 %, respectively, with 80 % of patients having local invasion or metastatic disease at time of diagnosis [[7], [8], [9]].

This case raises questions regarding whether local invasion of pancreatic adenocarcinoma predisposed our patient to splenic rupture seemingly out of proportion to the mechanism of trauma initially described. Ruptured spleens because of metastasis are exceedingly rare but have previously been reported with chronic lymphocytic leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma [10], breast cancer [11], and even pancreatic cancer [4] [12], but no reports to date have described rupture from local invasion. Similarly, few reports have identified cases of minor trauma as a potentially inciting event with malignancy acting as a possible predisposing factor.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for rare presentations of pancreatic cancer, particularly in cases of splenic rupture without a clear or typical traumatic mechanism. Regardless of how rare splenic rupture due to malignancy is, it should always be considered in the differential of acute abdomen, regardless of whether the patient has a history of cancer.
