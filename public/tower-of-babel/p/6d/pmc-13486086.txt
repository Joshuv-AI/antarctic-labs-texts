Altered Busulphan Exposure Associated with Concomitant Omeprazole during Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation Conditioning: A Two-Case Report
Authors: Ibrahim El-Serafi, Rui He, Ying Zhao, Jonas Mattsson, Moustapha Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBusulphan is eliminated through glutathione conjugation, forming tetrahydrothiophene, which is subsequently oxidized to tetrahydrothiophene 1-oxide and sulfolane via cytochrome (CYP)-mediated pathways, including CYP2C19 and CYP3A4. Omeprazole is a known inhibitor of CYP2C19 and may interfere with the metabolism of busulphan metabolites, thereby altering systemic busulphan exposure. Given the narrow therapeutic index of busulphan, such interactions may have clinically significant consequences.Case PresentationWe describe two adult patients who were diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia and received oral busulphan (twice daily [b.i.d.]) at a dose of 2 mg/kg for two consecutive days (168 mg and 150 mg, respectively) as part of fludarabine/busulphan conditioning prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Both patients received concomitant treatment with omeprazole.ResultsUnexpectedly elevated busulphan plasma area under the curve (AUC) values were observed following the initial dose despite standard dosing (14.311 and 19.378 mg·h/L, respectively). Dose reduction in one patient did not normalize systemic exposure, whereas discontinuation of omeprazole in the second patient only was followed by a marked decrease in busulphan levels. On the second day, the corresponding AUC values were 19.320 (135%) and 13.772 mg·h/L (71%), respectively.ConclusionsIn these cases, a potential drug–drug interaction was observed between omeprazole and busulphan, possibly mediated through inhibition of CYP-dependent metabolism of busulphan metabolites. Concomitant use of omeprazole during busulphan conditioning should be avoided whenever possible. Therapeutic drug monitoring remains essential to optimize busulphan exposure and minimize the risk of treatment-related toxicity.

Key Points

Unexpected high busulphan exposure was observed in two HSCT patients receiving concomitant omeprazole.Withdrawal of omeprazole was associated with reduced busulphan exposure in one patient, suggesting a potential drug–drug interaction.Therapeutic drug monitoring remains essential to optimize busulphan dosing and minimize treatment-related toxicity.Concomitant medications should be carefully reviewed during busulphan conditioning, particularly in patients with altered drug metabolism.

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is a potentially curative treatment for hematological malignancies such as leukemia and lymphomas, certain solid tumors, and nonmalignant disorders, including genetic/metabolic disorders and aplastic anemia. However, the clinical outcomes remain limited by acute and chronic complications, including graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), infections, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS), and graft rejection. Conditioning regimens administered prior to HSCT are essential to eradicate malignant cells, create space for donor cells, and suppress host immunity to prevent graft rejection [1, 2].

Busulphan (Bu) is a cell cycle-nonspecific alkylating agent widely used in conditioning regimens. Busulfan was shown to have a narrow therapeutic window and high inter- and intra-patient pharmacokinetic variability [3, 4]. Busulphan exposure, measured as area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), varies up to 5–7-fold among pediatric patients and twice among adults, likely reflecting differences in metabolic pathways. Low exposure has been associated with increased graft rejection and relapse, whereas high exposure correlates with toxicity, particularly SOS, oral mucositis, and poor overall outcomes. Therefore, individualized treatment guided by therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is an important strategy [1, 2]. A typical Bu AUC when administered twice daily (2 mg/kg) ranges between 9 mg·h/L and 12 mg·h/L [2, 5].

Busulphan is predominantly metabolized through conjugation with glutathione (GSH), catalyzed by glutathione S-transferase A1 (GSTA1). This reaction forms an unstable sulfonium ion that decomposes into tetrahydrothiophene (THT) and N-acetyl-cysteine sulfonium ion. THT undergoes sequential oxidation to THT 1-oxide, sulfolane, and ultimately 3-hydroxysulfolane. Our studies and several others have shown that mainly hepatic enzymes, including flavin-containing monooxygenase-3 (FMO3) and cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, contribute to THT oxidation and hence influence Bu pharmacokinetics. Several CYP- and/or FMO3-metabolized drugs, including itraconazole, metronidazole, voriconazole, phenytoin, and ketobemidone, have been reported to alter busulphan plasma concentrations [6–9].

Omeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor that suppresses gastric acid secretion via inhibition of the parietal cell H⁺/K⁺-ATPase. The drug is commonly used for gastrointestinal ulcers, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and Helicobacter pylori infection. Omeprazole has a short plasma half-life (30–60 min) but achieves rapid and sustained pharmacodynamic effects. The drug is primarily metabolized in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes, including CYP2C19 and CYP3A4, and eliminated mainly in the urine [10, 11].

In the current report, we describe two patients who received oral busulphan concomitantly with omeprazole and demonstrated unexpectedly high busulphan AUC values. Despite multiple reports of drugs interacting with busulfan metabolism, data on busulfan–omeprazole interaction remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to describe such an interaction.

Case Presentation

We report two adult patients diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Both patients received oral busulphan (twice daily [b.i.d.]) at a dose of 2 mg/kg for two consecutive days (168 mg and 150 mg, respectively) as part of a fludarabine/busulphan conditioning regimen prior to HSCT at Karolinska University Hospital, Huddinge, Sweden. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Karolinska Institutet (Dnr. 65/03 and 616/03) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from both patients to participate in the study and publish it.

Blood samples for therapeutic drug monitoring were collected immediately before dosing and at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h after the first dose, and at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h after the third dose, according to institutional protocol. Busulphan plasma concentrations were determined using gas chromatography coupled with electron capture detection (GC–ECD, Varian 3800; Varian, Inc. Palo Alto, CA, USA) as previously described [12]. This method has been previously validated and is routinely employed for Bu TDM. Linearity of the calibration curve was demonstrated over a concentration range of 15–1500 ng/mL. The precision of the analytical method was ±3.9% (coefficient of variation [CV]) at the 10-ng/mL level and ±2.3% (CV) at the 100-ng/mL level. The limit of quantification (LOQ) was 5 ng/mL.

Busulphan pharmacokinetics parameters, including AUC, clearance, half-life, distribution volume, etc. were calculated using one-compartment open model. (WinNonlin software, version 2; Pharsight, CA, USA) as well as a validated limited sampling model [13]. The limited sampling model was previously validated in patients with leukemia who received Bu orally (1.90–6.02 mg/kg/day as a single dose or twice daily). Busulphan kinetics were found to be linear within the studied range. A high correlation (r = 0.998, P < 0.0001) between the estimated and the determined AUC was reported using multiple linear regression: AUC = 1:69 × C1 + 1:45 × C2 + 7:28 × C3, where C1 is the concentration at 1 h, C2 is the concentration at 3 h, and C3 is the concentration at 6 h [13].

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 365® (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

The busulphan doses were subsequently reduced to 158 mg and 130 mg, respectively. However, omeprazole was discontinued only in the second patient without any replacement drug. On the second day, the corresponding AUC values were 19.320 (135%) and 13.772 mg·h/L (71%), respectively (Fig. 2A). Detailed Bu concentrations for both doses are illustrated in Fig. 2B.

Similarly, the corresponding clearance values were 8.18 and 9.44 L/h, respectively. At this dose, the expected clearance should be approximately 15.8 and 13.0 L/h, respectively, provided that Bu concentrations and AUC were within the therapeutic window. This finding supports the observed improvement in the second patient after omeprazole was discontinued.

Discussion

In the present report, we describe a potential pharmacokinetic interaction between omeprazole and busulphan associated with altered busulphan pharmacokinetics and increased exposure. These findings further support the involvement of FMO3 and CYP enzymes in the oxidative steps of busulphan metabolism. As previously described, both FMO3 and CYP enzymes participate in the oxidation of THT to THT 1-oxide and may also contribute to downstream oxidation steps [6]. In addition, polymorphisms in CYP enzymes, particularly CYP2C9, have been reported to influence overall busulphan metabolism [9].

Busulphan has previously been reported to interact with several CYP- and/or FMO3-dependent drugs, including metronidazole, fluconazole, itraconazole, ketobemidone, and voriconazole [6–8]. In a patient undergoing HSCT who received busulphan concomitantly with voriconazole, busulphan exposure was significantly increased. Following discontinuation of voriconazole, busulphan concentrations returned to expected levels. Early appearance, increased accumulation, and delayed clearance of THT were also observed [6]. Although glutathione S-transferase plays a central role in busulphan metabolism, increasing evidence suggests that FMO3 and CYP enzymes, including their genetic polymorphisms, may contribute to pharmacokinetic variability, particularly in patients exposed to polypharmacy. In some transplant centers, empirical dose reduction (e.g., administration of 80% of the standard dose) is considered when potential interacting drugs are co-administered.

CYP polymorphisms have also been shown to influence omeprazole metabolism. Variants such as CYP2C19*2 and CYP2C19*3 significantly affect omeprazole pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [14]. Moreover, CYP3A activity has been reported to be modulated by CYP2C19 genotype in patients receiving omeprazole. Omeprazole has also been shown to interact with substrates of CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 [10, 11].

From a mechanistic perspective, the rapid disappearance of THT suggests that electron transfer from cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) to CYP enzymes may represent a rate-limiting step in THT oxidation. POR serves as the primary electron donor for microsomal CYP monooxygenases, and POR polymorphisms have been associated with altered CYP-mediated metabolism, including CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 [15, 16]. Although THT is typically undetectable owing to rapid oxidation, its accumulation during busulphan therapy has been associated with reduced busulphan clearance, possibly via feedback inhibition mechanisms [6, 9]. Variability in CYP and POR activity may therefore contribute to the well-documented interindividual variability in busulphan pharmacokinetics, in addition to potential drug–drug interactions in selected patient populations.

In contrast, fludarabine is administered as fludarabine phosphate, a prodrug that is rapidly dephosphorylated in plasma to the active nucleoside F‑ara‑A. Consequently, fludarabine administration is not expected to affect busulfan clearance [17].

The present report has some limitations, including the small sample size, as we present only two patients, precluding definitive conclusions regarding causality between prophylactic treatment using omeprazole and altered busulfan pharmacokinetics. It would have been more informative if the patients had received a 4-day busulphan conditioning regimen rather than only 2 days. Moreover, pharmacogenetic profiling of CYP, FMO3, GSTA1, and POR enzymes was not performed, which limits the mechanistic interpretation of the observed variability. Future studies should include prospective evaluation in larger patient cohorts, systematic pharmacogenetic characterization, and metabolite profiling to clarify the enzymatic mechanisms underlying this interaction. Controlled clinical studies assessing the impact of proton pump inhibitors on busulfan exposure are warranted, with the aim of developing evidence-based recommendations for comedication management during conditioning therapy.

Conclusions

Drug–drug interactions during busulphan conditioning may substantially alter systemic exposure, increasing the risk of treatment-related toxicity and potentially compromising the clinical outcomes. This risk may be further amplified by genetic polymorphisms affecting drug-metabolizing enzymes, contributing to considerable interindividual variability in busulphan pharmacokinetics and therapeutic response. Improved identification of clinically relevant pharmacogenetic and metabolic factors may facilitate more individualized conditioning strategies and enhance treatment safety and efficacy. Until precision-guided approaches become routinely available, therapeutic drug monitoring remains the most reliable and practical tool for optimizing busulphan exposure, minimizing toxicity, and maximizing therapeutic benefit.
