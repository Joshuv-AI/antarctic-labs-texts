Delayed presentation of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis: a case report
Authors: Goytoom Kahsay Berhe, Reiye Esayas Mengesha, Hadush Tesfay Negash, Yirgalem Teklebirhan Gereziher, Teklit GebremedhinTeklu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceHypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) is characterized by hyperplasia of the smooth muscle fibers of the pylorus, leading to narrowing of the pyloric canal and obstruction of gastric emptying. It is the most common cause of surgical vomiting in infants. Classically, infants present with non-bilious projectile vomiting; the child is hungry after vomiting and suckles well but becomes dehydrated and wasted. IHPS usually presents between the 3rd and 5th weeks of life. Cases of delayed presentation are very rare (1/100,000) and usually diagnosis is delayed. We are reporting atypically late age presentation of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis case.Case presentationWe report the case of a 10-month-old male infant who presented with non-bilious vomiting of nine months' duration. On examination, he was wasted and stunted, with no palpable “olive.” Abdominal ultrasound revealed a hypertrophied pyloric muscle. A diagnosis of hypertrophic pyloric stenosis was made, and Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy was performed. The infant resumed feeding eight hours postoperatively, with no recurrence of vomiting.DiscussionInfantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS) results from hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the circular muscle fibers of the pylorus, causing progressive narrowing of the gastric outlet and subsequent obstruction. It is one of the most common causes of non-bilious, projectile vomiting in infants, typically presenting between the second and eighth weeks of life. Aboagye et al. reported that 97.5 % of IHPS cases are diagnosed by 12 weeks of age, with a peak at around five weeks. Presentation beyond this window is unusual and often leads to diagnostic delays.ConclusionDelayed presentation of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is uncommon but critical to recognize. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IHPS in infants with persistent vomiting, even beyond the typical age range. Early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention are essential to prevent complications and ensure favorable outcomes. This case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical presentations to avoid diagnostic delays.

Introduction

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS) is caused by hyperplasia of the pyloric smooth muscle fibers, leading to elongation and wall thickening that result in pyloric luminal narrowing by redundant pyloric mucosal tissue, causing gastric outlet obstruction [1]. With a predominance in first-born males and an incidence of approximately 1–4 per 1000 live births [2,3].

However, delayed infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is very rare, with an estimated incidence of about 1 in 100,000 [4]. The term ‘delayed infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis’ generally refers to presentation beyond the 6th week of life [4]. Few cases have been reported in the literature, with ages ranging from a median of eight months up to as late as 17 years [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Such late presentations often result in diagnostic dilemmas and delayed management [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

Diagnosis relies on a thorough clinical history and examination, especially for atypical presentations. Definitive diagnosis is commonly made with abdominal ultrasound, which has high specificity and sensitivity; rarely, a contrast meal and other modalities are needed [1].

Here, we report a 10-month-old male infant diagnosed with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and delayed infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, who was successfully managed with open standard Ramstedt pyloromyotomy and showed satisfactory outcomes on subsequent follow-up. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the SCARE guidelines (16).

Case report

A 10-month-old male infant presented with projectile, non-bilious vomiting that began two weeks after birth. The vomiting typically occurred three hours after feeding and was associated with epigastric fullness before vomiting. Despite vomiting, the infant remained eager to feed. He also experienced reduced bowel movements, sometimes defecating only once per week.

He was initially admitted to a nearby hospital for one month and received unspecified intravenous medications without improvement, prompting his referral to our facility.

He is the fourth child in the family, and his mother was 29 years old at the time of delivery. He was unable to sit unsupported and remained exclusively breastfed. There was no history of similar illness among his siblings.

On examination, the infant appeared chronically ill, although vital signs were within normal limits. Anthropometric measurements indicated severe stunting and moderate wasting. The abdomen was flat and moved with respiration. Visible peristalsis was observed moving left to right across the epigastrium, but no palpable mass was detected.

Laboratory investigations revealed a normal complete blood count and electrolyte panel. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a thickened pylorus, 7 mm (D2 on Fig. 1) and an elongated pyloric canal, 22 mm (D1 on Fig. 1).Fig. 1Ultrasound of the abdomen showing thickened and elongated pylorus.Fig. 1

Ultrasound of the abdomen showing thickened and elongated pylorus.

A diagnosis of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS) was made, along with marasmus, severe stunting, moderate wasting and Dehydration. The patient was resuscitated with Normal saline 20 ml/kg bolus then he was put on calculated maintainance fluid, after correction of dehydration, after making sure that his electrolytes are within normal range and achieving adequate urine output, he was taken to surgery. Intraoperatively, a thickened and elongated pylorus was confirmed, and a Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy was performed (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Intraoperative view showing Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy.Fig. 2

Intraoperative view showing Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy.

The infant resumed oral feeding eight hours after surgery, and no vomiting was observed postoperatively. His recovery was uneventful, and he was discharged on the fourth postoperative day. At one-month follow-up, he was tolerating feeds well and remained symptom-free. For nutritional rehabilitation he was linked to pediatric and child health unit and he was started on high protein diet (palpynut) and then his family were counseled on subsequent nutritional rehabilitation and what to feed him.

Discussion

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis has been known since Hirschsprung's time causing most common surgical vomiting in children from obstruction of gastric outlet. There are several worldwide studies and literature done on the disease etiology implicating genetic, hormonal and environmental as risk factors in all age groups. But no exactly known cause identified yet [2,5,6,10,11].

The disease pathology is progressive. However, it is not well established how long it will take for symptoms to appear. But the average duration described in different literature is (1−10) weeks usually begins at 4 weeks. The thickness and elongation of the pyloric wall is crucial for diagnosis. The primary pathology is hypertrophy and typical hyperplasia of the pyloric smooth muscle fiber, lead to redundant pyloric mucosa causes further luminal narrowing. Eventually result in pyloric obstruction that hinders emptying of gastric content antegrade and further retention of content with continuous peristalsis of stomach against obstructed pylorus induce vomiting of ingested matter together with electrolyte result in significant emaciation & electrolyte imbalance [3,6,12].

The classic presentation of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is classically observed between the third and fifth week of life. During this time, the infant may experience progressive non-bilious projectile vomiting of ingested matter, which is accompanied by hunger feed and severe wasting and dehydration. This was reported from different case report and research article that this classic presentation is noticed almost more than 90 % of patients [7,13].

However, delayed infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is very rare, with an estimated incidence of about 1 in 100,000 [4]. The term ‘delayed infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis’ generally refers to presentation beyond the 6th week of life [4]. According to a few case reports in the literature, the majority of cases involve people who are older than five months or who are as old as seventeen years [10,12]. In our case, we are reporting the diagnosis of IHPS in a 10-month-old. Delayed IHPS is a rare clinical entity with similar clinical features that is overlooked primarily due to atypical age of presentation.

The primary clinical and non-invasive imaging methods for diagnosing infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is ultrasoundThese methods have become standard in patients with classic presentations at typical ages with high diagnostic accuracy and sensitivities, even though some presentations are atypical or even recurrent. Thus, we must further investigate with upper gastrointestinal contrast meals, endoscopies, or CT scans to have a clear differentiation from possibly existing differential diagnosis.

When a patient presents with the classic presentation, the most important diagnostic evidence is the clinical evidence of progressive non-bilious vomiting of ingested matter. Usually associated with body waste, dehydration, even shock, severe acute malnutrition. Often a palpable olive mass on the epigastric area under calm conditions (50–90 %), but the absence of a palpable mass is not uncommon in malnourished patients, where muscle wasting may obscure clinical findings, like in our case.

This is supported by laboratory findings of hypokalaemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis and ultrasound confirmation of pyloric thickness of ≥3–4 mm and elongation of ≥14–6 mm in term infants. Abdominal ultrasonography in this case demonstrated a 22 mm long and 7 mm thick pyloric wall, and clinical data corroborated our conclusion that final surgical intervention was necessary [1,8,14].

Surgery is the mainstay of treatment even though there is a place of medical treatment options. Since the disease known there are different surgical techniques applied for IHPS management in different literatures. However, Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy is the standard procedure with excellent outcome for a century. Here in our case, we applied the same standard technique, and it was successful with satisfactory outcome patient relieved symptom on subsequent follow up [4,7,9,15].

Conclusion

Delayed presentation of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is rare but should not be overlooked. Clinicians must consider IHPS in any infant with persistent non-bilious vomiting, regardless of age. Prompt diagnosis and surgical intervention are crucial to prevent morbidity. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and recognizing atypical presentations to minimize diagnostic delays.
