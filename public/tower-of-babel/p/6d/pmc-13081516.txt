Comparative evaluation of Anyplex™ MTB/NTM, Xpert MTB/RIF ultra, culture, and microscopy for the diagnosis of pediatric tuberculosis using gastric aspirates
Authors: Matúš Dohál, Věra Dvořáková, Michaela Hromádková, Peter Kunč, Jaroslav Fábry, Igor Porvazník, Ivan Solovič, Karolína Doležalová, Michaela Krivošová, Juraj Mokrý
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPaediatric tuberculosis (TB) diagnosis remains a challenge due to the paucibacillary nature of the disease, resulting in 51.00% of TB cases remaining undiagnosed, rising to 58% in children under five years of age. MethodsThis study evaluates the effectiveness of different microbiological methods (Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra, Anyplex™ MTB/NTM, culture and microscopy) for TB detection in gastric aspirate (GA) samples collected from 483 paediatric patients in Slovakia and the Czech Republic. The sensitivity of each diagnostic method was further analyzed according to patient age.ResultsThe highest sensitivity was observed with the Anyplex MTB/NTM assay (38.94%). Interestingly, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra exhibited lower sensitivity than culture (16.67% versus 21.27%). Considering all methods together, the positivity was not significantly associated with age. The overall positivity rate of all diagnostic methods combined was higher in children under five years (46.7%) and adolescents aged 16–18 years (54.2%) compared with those aged 6–15 years (31.2%). Similarly, the sensitivity of individual diagnostic methods (except Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra) followed the same trend.ConclusionThese data indicate that gastric aspirate microbiology provides only moderate confirmation of paediatric TB in routine practice, and that nucleic acid amplification-based technologies (NAAT) should be used as part of a complementary diagnostic strategy alongside culture and smear microscopy, with culture remaining essential for drug susceptibility testing. Because NAAT platforms were applied in different centre-specific cohorts, the findings should be interpreted as real-world yields rather than a head-to-head comparison, and future work should prioritise standardised sampling and evaluation of non-invasive alternatives (e.g., stool) across age groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12941-026-00854-7.Keywords: Pediatric tuberculosis, Gastric aspirate, Diagnostics, Sensitivity, Anyplex™ MTB/NTM

Introduction

Global data from the World Health Organization (WHO) for 2023 reveals a significant resurgence of tuberculosis (TB), with 8.2 million new cases diagnosed, marking the highest incidence since 1995. Notably, an estimated 1.25 million cases occurred in children and adolescents. Paediatric TB diagnosis remains a significant challenge, primarily attributed to non-specific clinical presentations, the paucibacillary nature of the disease, and difficulties in obtaining adequate diagnostic specimens. This diagnostic gap is substantial, with an estimated 51.00% of paediatric TB cases remaining undiagnosed, rising to 58% in children under five years of age [1]. These statistics underscore the urgent need for the development of new diagnostic tools.

Children generally cannot produce sputum or tend to swallow it, necessitating invasive procedures to obtain alternative clinical specimens, such as gastric aspirate (GA) or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Alternatively, non-invasive methods, such as stool sampling, can be employed, as bacilli pass through the gastrointestinal tract and are excreted in the stool. GA samples have been reported to achieve the highest detection rates; however, this depends on the sensitivity of the laboratory test utilized. The gastric aspirate smear-microscopy has the lowest sensitivity, followed by culture and molecular methods in clinically diagnosed paediatric TB patients [2–5].

Several nucleic acid amplification-based technologies (NAAT) are currently available to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) directly in GA. Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra is currently one of the most widely utilized diagnostic tests, with sensitivity ranging from 52.20% to 87.50% in bacteriologically confirmed TB, and from 6.10% to 44.40% in clinically diagnosed or suspected TB [6–8]. Additionally, significantly higher sensitivity was demonstrated in children aged < 4 years [8]. Similarly, comparable sensitivity has been reported using cross-priming amplification technology and PCR targeting the IS6110 region in smear-negative paediatric TB patients [2, 9]. An alternative diagnostic method is the Anyplex MTB/NTM real-time PCR, which can detect both Mtb and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM). This assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 81.80% and a specificity of 94.40% for the detection of Mtb from extrapulmonary samples, including GA. For NTM detection, the sensitivity and specificity were 56.50% and 99.00%, respectively. The sensitivity of Anyplex MTB/NTM was higher in smear-positive specimens, reaching 97.70% for Mtb and 80% for NTM detection [10]. Future directions could include the application of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, which have demonstrated superior diagnostic performance on gastric aspirate compared to Xpert MTB/RIF [7]. However, further studies are needed to validate and optimise these methods.

This study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance of Anyplex MTB/NTM in detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) and NTM compared to Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, culture, and smear microscopy in the diagnosis of tuberculosis (TB) among patients under 18 years of age. We also hypothesize that the sensitivity of each method might vary depending on the patient’s age.

Materials and methods

Patient data collection

A retrospective study was performed on data obtained from electronic medical records of patients hospitalized at the National Institute of Children Tuberculosis and Respiratory Diseases in Slovakia and the Department of Paediatrics of the 1st Faculty of Medicine, Charles University, Thomayer University Hospital, Prague, Czech Republic from January 2020 to June 2024.

Evaluation of possible TB was typically initiated through routine contact tracing. The clinical diagnosis of TB required both immunological evidence of infection and supportive radiological finding. Immunological evidence included either: (i) a positive tuberculin skin test (TST), performed by intradermal injection of 0.1 mL PPD-S 2 T.U. (AJ Vaccines A/S, Denmark) with induration read after 48–72 h and considered positive ≥ 5 mm in BCG-unvaccinated children or ≥ 10 mm in BCG-vaccinated; or (ii) a positive interferon--γ release assay (IGRA) using QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus; Qiagen, Germany) performed according to the manufacturers’s instructions. All participants underwent microbiological evaluation (smear microscopy, culture, and PCR-based testing: Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra in the Czech Republic; Anyplex MTB/NTM in Slovakia) and imaging studies (chest X-ray - CXR; chest CT was performed when findings were equivocal on CXR). Imaging features suggestive of TB were required to support the diagnosis. Bacteriologically confirmed TB cases were defined as those with positive microscopy in combination with culture or RT-PCR, or with a positive culture or RT-PCR result alone. Although Anyplex™ MTB/NTM and Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra were not performed on identical samples/patients, we report their observed diagnostic yields/sensitivities side-by-side as part of routine practice in two centres. Both centres follow comparable clinical protocols for GA collection, sample processing, and diagnostic evaluation under similar public health and laboratory standards; however, the results should be interpreted descriptively as real-world performance rather than as a head-to-head comparison, given potential differences in patient cohort, disease stage/bacillary load, and other centre-specific factors.

Children with ICD-10 codes A15 (bacteriologically confirmed TB) or A16 (clinically diagnosed TB without bacteriological confirmation) were eligible for inclusion.

The data used in this article was fully anonymized in accordance with ethical guidelines and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of all individuals involved.

Gastric aspirate collection and processing

GA was performed as the primary alternative for all pediatric patients unable to expoctorate. In our clinical settings, GA was also performed children aged above 5 years and adolescents, as patient cooperation and the ability to provide a sputum sample were often limited, particularly in patients from socially disadvantaged communities. Induced sputum collection was considered when feasible; however, this procedure requires trained physiotherapy staff and a cooperative patient, which cannot always be ensured in routine clinical practice. Therefore, GA represented the most practical and reliable method for obtaining diagnostic material in this population.

An appropriately sized nasogastric (NG) tube was transesophageally inserted into the stomach of a fasting patient early in the morning. The stomach was lavaged with 50 mL of normal saline administered via the NG tube, followed by the aspiration of 35 mL of gastric fluid, which was collected in a sterile falcon tube. During sample processing, the GA was first concentrated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting sediment was then resuspended and decontaminated using N-acetyl-L-cysteine-sodium hydroxide. Following decontamination, the sample was further concentrated, neutralized with NaOH, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline before transportation or storage. After decontamination and concentration, the final sediment volume was approximately 3 mL. From this sediment, 200 µL was used for direct smear microscopy, 500 µL was inoculated onto each of three Löwenstein–Jensen media, and 1,000 µL was used for nucleic acid extraction (for Anyplex MTB/NTM, n = 421) or added to Xpert MTB/RIF cartridge (n = 62). The nucleic acid extraction procedure yielded a final eluate of 100 µL, of which 5 µL was used in the reaction.

Statistical analyses

The sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values (PPV, NPV) of each microbiological test were assessed along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated against the Composite Reference Standard (CRS). In this study, the CRS was defined as a clinical diagnosis of active TB, integrating clinical symptoms, immunological evidence (positive TST or IGRA), and suggestive radiological findings. This approach was chosen because all microbiologically positive cases (culture or RT-PCR) were already captured within the clinically confirmed group, and the CRS better accounts for the paucibacillary nature of pediatric TB compared to culture alone. The Fisher’s exact test was used to compare sensitivities across various diagnostic methods and Chi-square test in various age groups (GraphPad Prism 8.0.1, San Diego, CA, United States). To control for type I error inflation due to multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction (0.05/4), thus p < 0.0125 was considered statistically significant. As this study aimed to explore potential age-related differences across several diagnostic approaches rather than test a single predefined hypothesis, the analyses were considered exploratory in nature.

Results

Patients characteristics

Data were obtained from 483 patients under 18 years of age with suspected TB who underwent GA collection. Of these, GA collection was successful in all patients, allowing for microbiological testing. The cohort comprised 249 (51.13%) boys and 234 (48.44%) girls, with a mean age of 7.2 years. The laboratory workflow and a summary of the defined diagnoses are presented in Fig. 1. A total of 265 patients were diagnosed with the active TB-disease, comprising 246 cases of pulmonary TB, 11 cases of extrapulmonary TB, and 8 cases of acute miliary TB. Among these, 123 were bacteriologically confirmed as TB-positive by at least one diagnostic method (culture, microscopy, Anyplex™ MTB/NTM, or Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra).

Anyplex™ MTB/NTM detected NTM DNA in 7 GA samples. In all NTM-positive cases, smear microscopy and culture were negative. No patient fulfilled clinical criteria consistent with NTM disease based on available clinical data; therefore, these findings were interpreted as possible transient presence/colonization or environmental contamination rather than definitive NTM disease. TB was clinically confirmed in 4 of these patients.

Comparison of sensitivity of RT-PCR, culture and smear-microscopy

A total of 123 GA samples were positive by at least one diagnostic method performed within the respective centre-specific workflow (RT-PCR, culture, or smear microscopy): 98/421 in the Slovakia (tested by Anyplex™ MTB/NTM) and 25/62 in the Czech Republic (tested by Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra cohort). Overall, 32/123 (26.0%) were positive by more than one microbiological method; 10/123 were positive by all three microbiological tests (RT-PCR, culture, and smear microscopy; Fig. 2A–B). Among pulmonary TB cases, Mtb was detected by Anyplex™ MTB/NTM in 38.20% of GA samples and by Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra in 16.00%; culture was positive in 21.50% of GA samples. Detection rates were lower in extrapulmonary TB (27.27%, 3/11; tested only by Anyplex™ MTB/NTM, as no extrapulmonary TB cases were tested by Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra in the Czech centre) but higher in miliary TB (75.0%, 6/8), consistent with the higher bacillary burden in disseminated disease. The overall cumulative sensitivity of all diagnostic tests was 41.04% (95%CI 35.32–47.02) (Table 1).

Influence of age on Mtb positivity in GA

Age is a well-recognized factor influencing the diagnostic yield of tuberculosis testing in children, as both disease presentation and bacillary load vary substantially across age groups. The overall positivity rate for all diagnostic methods combined was higher in children under five years (46.70%) and in adolescents aged 16–18 years (54.20%) compared with patients aged 6–15 years (31.20%). A comparison of the sensitivity of individual diagnostic methods across the three age groups showed a similar pattern, with the lowest sensitivities consistently observed in the 6–15-year group (Tables 1, 2, 4 and 5, and 6).

Discussion

The confirmation of TB in paediatric cases patients a diagnostic challenge. Children usually have a paucibacillary form of the disease, with more than 70% of culture samples being negative [11, 12]. Bacteriological confirmation of Mtb remains crucial, given also the increasing incidence of resistant forms. Although both in Slovakia and in the Czech Republic prevails drug-sensitive TB, due to high migration from Ukraine, drug-resistant TB is also beginning to emerge as an issue in paediatric population [13–15]. Furthermore, it is imperative to differentiate between NTM and TB, given that the clinical manifestations of these diseases can be strikingly similar, yet their therapeutic approaches differ significantly [16].

The role of GA in TB diagnosis has been debated for years, with some studies dismissing its effectiveness, while others support its utility, particularly in children unable to expectorate sputum [17, 18]. The WHO has recommended GA as a gold standard recently [19]. In comparison with BALF, the procedure is less complicated and does not require the use of anaesthetics. Furthermore, the procedure can be performed by a member of the nursing staff. Our data showed that the Anyplex MTB/NTM method exhibited the highest sensitivity (37.81%), which increased to 80% in smear-positive samples. In contrast, the sensitivity of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay in our cohort was lower than expected (16.67% when compared with the clinical diagnosis and 40.00% when compared to culture), as the previous studies showed the sensitivity of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra ranging from 52.50 to 66.30% compared to culture [20, 21]. This finding, although unexpected, can be explained by both methodological and specimen-related factors. It was showed that the sensitivity of these methods is lower in non-respiratory samples [22]. Also, the variability in bacterial load among GA samples likely reflects differences in the quality and volume of the primary specimens rather than inconsistencies in laboratory processing. GA collection is not a fully standardized procedure and depends on several patient-related factors, including fasting duration, technique, and individual gastric content. Consequently, the total amount of mycobacteria recovered from each sample may vary, which can influence the diagnostic yield of both molecular and culture-based methods, especially the amplification efficiency in cartridge-based systems such as Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra that rely on a fixed and limited input volume. This inherent variability represents one of the main limitations in the interpretation and comparison of microbiological results obtained from GA in pediatric TB diagnostics. A key limitation is that the Anyplex MTB/NTM and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra were used at different centres and not on identical samples/patients; thus, side-by-side sensitivities reflect real-world yields and should not be interpreted as proof of assay superiority.

Results of our study suggest that age has a significant influence on GA positivity across all methods, except for Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra. The higher sensitivity observed in children under five compared to those aged 6–15 may be due to differences in the manifestation of the disease. Younger children may have higher bacterial loads or increased mycobacterial concentrations in their GA and stool, possibly because they swallow a greater portion of respiratory secretions rather than expectorating them [23]. School-aged children (approximately 6–15 years) represent the so-called “safe age group,” in which the immune response is relatively effective in containing Mtb. In this group, infection usually manifests as primary intrathoracic TB, with well-contained granulomatous lesions and minimal bacillary dissemination. Consequently, bacterial load in respiratory specimens, including GA, is lowest in this age range, leading to markedly reduced test sensitivity. In adolescents (≥ 15 years), the clinical presentation begins to resemble adult-type pulmonary TB, often with cavitary disease and higher bacterial burden, resulting in improved microbiological yield [24, 25]. The severity of TB disease also influences test performance across age groups. Children with a more severe or disseminated form of disease typically have higher bacterial loads, leading to an increased likelihood of detection in GA samples, regardless of age. Conversely, children with mild or early stages of the disease may have lower bacterial concentrations, making detection more challenging.

One of the key advantages of the Anyplex assay is its capability to detect NTM; however, only seven patients tested positive for Mycobacterium species other than Mtb. In all seven cases, culture and smear microscopy yielded negative results. The detection of NTM DNA in GA from four patients with clinically confirmed TB does not necessarily indicate assay contamination or poor test specificity. Species-level identification of NTM could not be performed because culture isolates were unavailable, ,as line-probe assays used for confirmatory speciation at the National Reference Centre for Mycobacteria require cultured material. NTM are ubiquitous environmental organisms and their DNA can be detected in non-sterile clinical specimens, particularly in samples such as GA that are prone to environmental exposure. In addition, transient colonization or the presence of NTM DNA may account for these positive results. Co-detection of Mtb and NTM has also been described in pediatric patients, especially in those with a low bacillary load [26]. Therefore, positive NTM findings should be interpreted cautiously and always in correlation with the clinical and bacteriological context of the patient. On the other hand, the primary benefit of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra lies in its ability to determine genotypic resistance to rifampicin (RIF). Nevertheless, given the low prevalence of RIF-resistant TB in pediatric patients in the Czech Republic, no such cases were identified using this test between 2018 and 2024.

The challenges associated with collecting GA have led to the exploration of alternative approaches, including the use of stool samples, which may contain swallowed Mtb from the respiratory tract. This approach is non-invasive and readily achievable in most clinical settings. A study across three African countries found a 97.00% success rate in stool collection for pediatric TB patients [27]. Also, it was found that GA had a diagnostic yield of 60.00%, similar to the 64.00% yield from stool samples. Notably, in an older study by Bonnave et al. no patients were diagnosed solely via GA, raising doubts about the need for this invasive procedure in diagnostics of TB [28].

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that Anyplex MTB/NTM (V2.0) shows the highest sensitivity among tested methods, particularly in younger children and adolescents. Despite clear trends showing age-related differences in diagnostic sensitivity, interpretation should be made with caution. The subgroup of adolescents aged 16–18 years represented the smallest cohort in our study (n = 43), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of findings for this age group. WHO recommends Xpert Ultra as an initial test in children with presumptive pulmonary TB and allows its use on gastric aspirate or stool specimens; therefore, less invasive stool-based testing may reduce the need for gastric aspiration in selected settings. On the other side, culture remains essential for drug susceptibility testing. Also, the emergence of drug-resistant TB, driven primarily by increased migrant influx from Ukraine, highlights the need for continued surveillance and molecular epidemiological studies. Future research should focus on optimizing non-invasive diagnostic strategies and evaluating age-related differences in test performance.
