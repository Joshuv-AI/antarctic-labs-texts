Surveillance biopsy detects nearly half of early rejections in living donor kidney transplants: an observational study from Nepal
Authors: Mukunda Prasad Kafle, Sushma Thapa, Reshika Shrestha, Vijay Shrees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProtocol biopsies (PBs) are done at different time points after organ transplantation to explore any signs of damage to the allograft without manifesting clinically. There are no consensus guidelines on use of protocol biopsies or specific time points to perform a PBs after kidney transplantation but literature suggests that findings of PBs may be a guide to long term graft outcomes. It is specially important to know whether such interventions are worth undertaking in the developing world where patients pay for healthcare. We studied the results of PBs done in living donor kidney transplant (LDKT) in our centre, to explore the frequency of various clinical conditions that could be detected by PBs.MethodsWe collected the reports of PBs done after completion of 6 months of kidney transplant surgery. Among the 203 patients who had LDKT done between 27 Oct 2021 and 29 May 2024, 119 underwent PB. Nineteen others had indication biopsy and 65 didn’t have biopsy at all. We collected and entered relevant information to Microsoft Excel and performed descriptive analysis.ResultsRejection rates in our transplant population in the first year after transplant was 5.41%. Of these rejections, 45.45% were detected by PBs. Subclinical rejection (SCR) was seen in 2.46% of all transplants and 4.20% of all PBs. Glomerulonephritis and BK polyomavirus associated nephropathy were detected in 15.96% and 5.04% of all PBs. However, renal tissue injury was the most common finding detected in 21.01% of all PBs.ConclusionSurveillance biopsies have important implications for detection of subtle allograft damage which would otherwise unknowingly damage the allograft and lead to irreversible graft loss.Keywords: Biopsy, BK virus, Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, Graft survival, Kidney transplantation, Living donors, Subclinical rejection

Introduction

Background

A surveillance allograft biopsy or the ‘protocol biopsy’ (PB) is a biopsy obtained from the allograft at predefined intervals after transplantation, regardless of the organ function. As yet, there is no consensus or guideline on performing a PB for routine surveillance of renal allograft [1].

Some transplant units pursue the practice of doing PB because these biopsies are helpful in detection of potentially correctable problems such as rejections, viral and other infective allograft injuries, recurrences of native diseases, toxicity of drugs or interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) which can occur silently, without any clinical or biochemical manifestations but potentially reversible if treated early [1, 2]. Moreover, steroid avoidance programs and calcineurine inhibitor (CNI) withdrawal programs largely depend upon PB to detect allograft rejections [2]. Therefore, PBs are important in individualization of therapy [2].

Other transplant centers deliberately discontinued the practice of PBs [3]. The reasons for not taking up PB as an institutional protocol have been cited as patient inconveniences, adverse events, increased expenses, uncertainty of getting useful clinical information, and other logistic issues [3]. Procedure related complications like hematuria, hematomas, arteriovenous fistulas and vasovagal reactions have been reported with PB. Major complications like bleeding, macroscopic hematuria with ureteric obstruction, peritonitis, or graft loss have been reported in approximately 1% of biopsies. [1] Yet, PB has been considered as a relatively safe procedure [1].

Even when taken as protocol, appropriate time of performing surveillance biopsies lacks consensus. Lee et al. from Korea saw that patients undergoing PB at 2 weeks and 12 months had advantages in graft survival, progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and new-onset CKD as compared to no PB group. They also concluded that PB could have a protective role in the management of renal allograft even though it did not significantly improve the graft or overall patient survival. [4] After a review of 25 studies, Shukla and colleagues concluded that doing PBs at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years post-transplant can be beneficial for determining long term allograft survival by revealing subclinical rejection (SCR) [5]. Whenever utilized, biopsies at 3 and 12 months were more common than at other time points, in the US [6].

Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) recommends allograft biopsy only when clinically indicated for proteinuria or a change of serum creatinine [1].

Once a clinical indication for allograft biopsy is met, organ damage might have already been set, putting the transplant kidney at risk. If PBs can detect potential threats to organ function which can be managed early on, it could be considered as general approach to save the allograft and the resources spent in treating an advanced organ dysfunction specially in the developing world where patients pay the cost of therapy from their pockets. We looked into the results of the PB done in our living donor kidney transplant (LDKT) recipients to spot the potential benefits of this elective intervention to our transplant population.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional design with both qualitative and quantitative methods to investigate the findings of PB done after 6 months of transplant in recipients who had undergone LDKT at Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital (TUTH), Kathmandu, Nepal.

Inclusion

Patients who had received LDKT at TUTH were included for this research. Data from the patients who had undergone a PB as part of routine surveillance was used for analysis. Information from patients who didn’t undergo PB were used to describe the data of PB patients in relation to universal set of transplant population. PB was not performed in patients who had already undergone an indication biopsy before scheduled PB.

Sampling

Patients were selected using purposive sampling, with the inclusion criteria being those who had a LDKT at TUTH.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of Institute of Medicine (IOM), Tribhuvan University on June 20, 2024 Ref No. 01/081/082 (6–11) E2, and patient confidentiality was maintained by de-identifying all records.

Methodology

We looked into the case records of patients undergoing PB. Case records of patients who underwent LDKT till the end of May 2024 were taken for the study. As policy of PB was interrupted during COVID, only the cases after resumption of PB following COVID were included. This included patients who underwent LDKT between 27 Oct 2021 and 29 May 2024.

Institutional protocols followed in the care of patients with living donor kidney transplantation

Pretransplant protocols

Clinical protocol

LDKT in Nepal is regulated by the Human Body Organ Transplantation (Regulation and Prohibition) Act, 1998. Donors and recipients are evaluated by Nephrology specialists. The evaluation process includes detailed history taking and clinical examination of the donor and the recipient. Clinical fitness for organ transplant surgery is obtained from cardiologists, psychiatrist, gynecologist and other clinical specialists as may be applicable. All recipients undergo dental consultation and treatment if required. Transplant surgery is done by kidney transplant surgeons who specialise in urology.

Pretransplant investigations

Pre-transplant laboratory investigations include blood grouping and Rh typing and routine hematological, biochemical and serological bloodworks including screening for malignancies when appropriate, urinalysis, ultrasonogram (USG), chest x-ray, electrocardiogram, and echocardiography for all. We screen all for HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C and TORCH IgM & IgG panel tests. Pap smear, mammogram, colonoscopy are done as per clinical guidelines.

Immunological investigations

All donors and recipients are subjected to HLA typing by PCR; and CDC crossmatch is done at least twice, once at the beginning of workup and later just before admission for surgery.

Post transplant protocols

Immunosuppression protocol

We start immunosuppression 2 days before the planned surgery with tacrolimus (Tac) 0.1 mg/kg/day in two divided doses, and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 500 mg twice daily. On Day 0 of transplantation, the morning doses of Tac and MMF are skipped.

All patients get rabbit antithymocyte globulin (Thymoglobulin™) 3 mg/kg in two divided doses, one in the theatre just before releasing the vascular clamp and next on Day 1 in the post-transplant ward.

Intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) 500 mg is given to all patients on the day of surgery, just before shifting to the theatre. Then, all get oral prednisolone at 20 mg per day from Day 1 for 4 weeks. Later, it is reduced by 2.5 mg every week to maintain at 5 mg daily forever. Tacrolimus levels are initially measured on Day 0 then every alternate day for the first week. Afterwards, it is measured on as and when required basis. Target tacrolimus levels were 10−15 ng/mL in the first 4 weeks, 8−10 ng/mL in the first 6 months, and 5−6 ng/mL thereafter. MMF is prescribed at a dose of 1 g twice daily for the first 2 weeks and then maintained at 500 mg three times daily. We maintain immunosuppressive drugs standard triple immunosuppression with Tac, MMF and prednisolone.

Infection prevention protocol

All recipients are vaccinated against hepatitis B, influenza, pneumococcus, meningococcus, and MMR at least 1 month before the scheduled transplantation date. Intravenous cefazolin 1 g and ceftriaxone 1 g stat doses are given just prior to sending the patient to the operating theatre on the day of surgery. After surgery, all recipients are given clotrimazole oral solution four times daily for 3 months. When serum creatinine is normalized, valganciclovir 450 mg and cotrimoxazole 480 mg are administered daily for 100 days and 6 months respectively.

Surveillance allograft biopsy protocol

PB is performed in all consenting LDKT patients between 6 months to 1 year post-transplant. Prior to the procedure, baseline tests including hemogram, viral serology, coagulation screening, urinalysis and USG of the allograft are done. Blood sugar and blood pressure control is achieved.

All allograft biopsies are done at the radiology suite under real time USG guidance by a radiology fellow. The samples are collected in 10% Formalin and Mitchel’s medium for light microscopy and immunofluorescence respectively. The samples are then transported to the pathology laboratory. Biopsy specimens were processed and reported at Dr Lal’s Pathlab, New Delhi, India by renal pathologists.

Operational definitions

Rejection

The Revised Banff 2017 classification criteria was followed in the reporting of histological features of rejection. It classified histological findings of allograft biopsy into normal, antibody mediated changes, borderline changes and T cell mediated rejection respectively based on various histopathological characteristics described by Haas et al [7].

BK virus-associated nephropathy

Defined as positive staining for SV40 antigen in IHC.

IgA nephropathy

Defined as diffuse mesangial deposition of IgA in immunofluorescence staining of the biopsy.

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis

Defined as segmental sclerosis seen in some glomeruli

Membranous glomerulonephritis

Defined as diffuse thickening of glomerular capillary walls, granular deposition of IgG and C3, and subepithelial electron-dense deposits.

Clinical management after PB

The information obtained from the PBs were interpreted by the treating clinician. Management of the conditions revealed by the PB were at the clinicians discretion.

Data collection

Data, including demographic details (age, sex, comorbidities), donor information, causes of pre-transplant renal failure, immunological data, and immunosuppression regimens were collected from patient records. Information on biopsy findings, Banff scores, and post-biopsy interventions (e.g., steroid use or immunosuppressive changes) were also recorded in Excel sheet. Post transplant complications (rejection, infection, and acute tubular necrosis [ATN], recurrences of diseases) were noted. When complete data was recorded, patient identification was anonymized and analysis was done.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics to summarize the collected data. The study took approximately six months for data collection and analysis.

Acute tubular injury

Renal tubular injury was the most common finding (21.0%; N = 25) in our PBs. Similarly, more than half of the for cause indication biopsies (52.63%; N = 10) showed tubular injury (Table 4).

Rejection

Rejection rates of our transplant population was 5.41%, demonstrated in 11 kidney biopsies among 203 total transplants. Among them, rejections in 5 patients were detected by PB and others by indication biopsies. Among the rejections detected by PB, 4 had antibody mediated rejection (ABMR) pattern and 1 had acute cellular rejection (ACR) while in the cohort undergoing indication biopsy, 4 had ABMR, 1 had ACR and 1 had a mixed pattern of injury. The ABMR rejections in PBs consisted of, g2ptc2t0c4d1 lesion in 1, g2t0ptc0c4d0 lesion in 1, g1v1ptc1c4d0 lesion in 1, g1ptc0t0c4d0 lesion in 1 case. The case with ACR was reported to have g0i1t1ptc0c4d0 lesion. Surveillance biopsy detected rejection in 2.46% of total transplants done, or in 4.2% of patients undergoing PBs and contributed to detection of 45.45% of all rejections.

Glomerulonephritis

Glomerulonephritis (GN) was seen in 19 PBs representing 9.35% of our total transplant population. The majority of GN were caught on PB contributing to recognition of 90.48% of all GN detected (Table 4).

Viral Infections

Of the total 7 BK polyomavirus associated nephropathy (BKPyV-nephropathy) cases, 85.71% were detected on PB.

Others

We didn’t find any lab reports suggestive of drug toxicities or other abnormal findings in the PBs done after 6 months of LDKT. There was one report suggesting pyelonephritis and another one suggesting thrombotic microangiopathy.

Patient & allograft survival

There were no deaths or initiation of maintenance dialysis in the transplant patients within the first 12 months after transplantation.

Discussion

As the quality of grafts obtained from dying donors is inferior to those obtained from thoroughly investigated living donors, deceased donor kidney transplant recipients (DDKTs) would require more intense surveillance of function. Therefore, surveillance allograft biopsy is expected to be of higher importance in DDKT than in LDKT. Our study adds evidence to the need for PB after LDKT.

Our results show that close to half (47.89%) of the PB specimens demonstrated some abnormalities. We noted BKPyV-nephropathy and GN after transplantation as two important elements in the PB samples. These conditions can unknowingly damage the allograft until irreversible damage sets in and could easily be overlooked if PBs are not utilized. Furthermore, almost half (45.45%) of the rejections in our population were detected through PB. Some of the salient findings of this study are discussed below.

Acute tissue injury is common in protocol biopsy after 6 months

Our cohort showed renal tubular and interstitial tissue injury in 21% of all PBs, and in 17.24% of the total transplant biopsies. While the majority of tissue injury detected by PB, 76% had acute tissue injury (ATI) and 24% of these patients had evidence of chronic fibrosis as well. Gwinner and colleagues calculated the prevalence of ATI to be about 40% in indication biopsies timed around 6 months post transplantation [8]. Various factors including the donor and recipient characteristics, immunosuppressive drug regimens, comorbidities, coprescription, individual patient characteristics etc. could be responsible for different results in a different setting. The prevalence rates of ATI in our patients translate that it is a major clinical issue in LDKT patients undergoing PB after 6 months of transplantation. It is understandable to encounter ATI in biopsies done in the peri-operative period and in indication biopsies, but persistence of this entity even after 6 months of immunologically sound post transplant course could reflect an ongoing insult to the renal tissue despite clinical recovery from peri-operative trauma. It is an important finding in itself because Nankivell and colleagues have shown that tubulointerstitial injury as early as 3 months after transplant can profoundly influence chronic fibrosis later [9]. Therefore, ATI after 6 months could also potentially indicate further graft damage in the future [3].

Detection of subclinical rejection by surveillance biopsy

Immunological rejection of one or the other type is the most common cause of allograft failure. Nankivell and team further showed that SCR, a finding of PB, would correlate with chronic allograft nephropathy, the main culprit behind long term allograft failure. [10] As SCR is a measure of organ damage before it is clinically manifested, it is detected by PB only before clinician is aware enough to subject the patient to an indication biopsy. SCR has been defined by Nankivell as a “histologically identified acute rejection without concurrent functional deterioration and has only been recognized with PB” [10]. Earlier, Rush and team first coined the term “subclinical rejection” as “a picture of rejection on histological examination of graft biopsies and classified based upon the Banff schema, in the absence of a clinical syndrome to suggest rejection.” [11].

The number of patients showing SCR in our cohort was 5, comprising 2.46% of all transplant population (N = 203) and 45.45% of all diagnosed to have rejection (N = 11). It is not surprising to see more rejections in the indication (for cause) biopsy group because the primary indication of such biopsy is the clinician’s suspicion of rejection. When Moein and colleagues compared the number of rejections between the PB done within 12 months of transplant and indication biopsy group, 12.5% of total rejections were detected by PB [12]. Subclinical inflammation was detected in 31% of surveillance biopsies at 6 months in a report by Seifert and team, from Alabama, USA [13]. Rea and colleagues from Rochester, found SCR in 3%, 1%, and 6% of PBs done at 4, 12 and 24 months respectively [14].

As compared to published literature, our patient group has shown lower SCR rates which could reflect the better quality of donors, optimal immunosuppressive drug therapy, robustness in the selection of donor and recipient, patients’ commitment to therapy, and timely indication biopsy by the treating doctor, and other factors. Previously, we showed SCR in 8.5% of our recipients undergoing PBs [15].

Detection of glomerular diseases by surveillance biopsy

Glomerular diseases are significant contributors to allograft loss. Primary, recurrent and donor derived glomerular diseases after transplantation are important risks to the allograft [16, 17]. In the post transplant setting, differentiation between donor transmitted, de novo, secondary, and recurrent disease might be difficult. Rates of recurrence and potential risk factors for recurrence of glomerulonephritis after transplant differ by GN type. [17] IgA nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), idiopathic membranous GN, and membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) are among the most notorious to reappear in the transplanted kidney [18]. According to Cosio and colleagues, GN contributes to 18% to 22% of allograft loss [18]. Earlier, El-Zoghby and colleagues reported the highest contribution of graft loss by glomerular disease in 36.6% of allograft failure representing 4.2% of all kidney transplants in a follow up of 8 years [16]. PB is an important tool for the surveillance of recurrence of disease [19].

Most of the IgA nephropathy after kidney transplantation is found on protocol biopsy

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) was seen in 10.08% of total PBs in our cohort. Earlier, we had found IgAN in 12.8% of protocol biopsies at our center [15]. Of the total IgAN detected in this study, 92.31% were detected on PB. In a total of 19 patients diagnosed as ESRD due to IgAN in our transplant population, 3 (15.78%) showed IgAN in PB. These numbers are consistent with recurrence in 15.63% of IgAN cohort reported by Schmidt & colleagues from Germany [20]. Moroni & colleagues have shown significantly lower rates of actuarial death-censored graft survival at 15 years in IgAN patients when compared to controls [21]. Our findings suggest that PB is the most important tool to detect IgAN after transplant. This could be more significant when considering long term graft outcomes [19]. Unfortunately, rates of graft loss due to recurrence of IgAN are high despite the institution of therapy [21].

Majority of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis is detected by protocol biopsies

High likelihood of post transplant recurrence and subsequent graft loss have been reported in patients losing their native kidneys to FSGS [1]. According to El-Zoghby & colleagues, FSGS accounted for the majority of recurrent glomerular diseases contributing to graft loss [16].

Seven patients had FSGS in our transplant population. Of the 5 patients whose ESRD cause was recorded as FSGS, none showed FSGS on post transplant biopsy. While the recurrence rate of FSGS in our study population was nil, prevalence of FSGS in our PB cohort was 5.04%. PB contributed to 85.71% of total FSGS cases detected in our population. This finding again supports the role of PB in detection of GN after transplantation. Lack of adequate pre-transplant kidney biopsy data was a challenge for us to precisely know whether the disease is recurrent or de novo disease.

Although donor and recipient size mismatch can’t be changed, interventions to manage hypertension, obesity and CNI effect, and use of therapeutic interventions like plasmapharesis can help in improving long-term outcomes in patients getting kidney transplant for ESRD due to FSGS [1, 19].

Detection of viral infection by surveillance biopsy

BK polyomavirus virus associated nephropathy

The reported incidence of BK polyomavirus virus associated nephropathy (BKPyV-nephropathy) is around 8% [22]. BKPyV-nephropathy is a very important cause of progressive allograft dysfunction in up to 50% of the organs affected by the virus [23]. Early detection and prompt intervention can prevent graft loss. Therefore, recently published international consensus guidelines emphasize on serial screening for BK polyomavirus (BKPyV) either in blood or urine until the first 9 months after transplantation [22]. As viremia precedes nephropathy, detection rates of viremia would be higher than that of proven nephropathy.

In our population, 7 patients (3.45% of all transplants) demonstrated BKPyV-nephropathy. Among them, 85.71% of BKPyV-nephropathy were detected by PB. Early biopsy could be the cause of lower rates of BKPyV-nephropathy. If we continued to follow our cohort for a longer duration, more BKPyV-nephropathy could be detected. For example, Rea and colleagues from Rochester found BKPyV-nephropathy in 3%, 6% and 10% of PBs done at 4, 12 and 24 months respectively [14]. Similarly, histologic analysis of the biopsies from 17 patients from Poland showed that incidence of BKPyV-nephropathy was 11.8% at 1 year and half of that in the PB at 3 months [24]. Giron-Luque & colleagues from Columbia observed the incidence of BKPyV-nephropathy to be 3.7% but with average time of diagnosis at around 18 months after transplantation [25].

Clinical management

Treating physicians have followed the results of PB in the individualized management of the LDKT recipients. In general, ACRs were treated with IVMP 250 mg daily for 3–5 days; ABMRs were managed with IVMP 250 mg daily for 3–5 days followed by plasma exchange if donor specific antibodies (DSA) were high– further targeting Tac levels in the higher normal range. ATI was managed conservatively. IgAN & FSGS were managed with angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. BKPyV-nephropathy was managed with temporary discontinuation of MMF followed by half the previous maintenance dose after BK viremia was undetectable.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of our work is that we have reported the observation of protocol biopsies from more than a hundred patients. It is the first of its kind from our country, where LDKT is the main kidney transplant policy. Here, we have shown that 45.45% of all rejections in a set of over 200 LDKT patients were detected by PBs. Ninety two percent of post-transplant IgAN, 85.7% of post-transplant FSGS and 85.7% of all BKPyV-nephropathy were detected by PBs. If there was no PB policy in our institute, we could have missed the early diagnosis potentially endangering graft and patient survival. Moreover, our setting is representative of many transplant centers in developing countries and the region. These findings will help in deciding whether to adopt surveillance biopsy policy in similar settings as ours. Therefore, results of our data add evidence in support of PB policy in undecided and novel settings. Identifying a future threat in early phase and tactful management initiated promptly can save allografts, patients’ money and lives in the long run.

Our study has many limitations. The main limitation of our work is that it is a retrospective chart review. Its take up as a routine procedural guideline may not be statistically as powerful as a prospectively designed randomized research. Additionally, due to the study design, we don’t have data on procedure related complications. Thirdly, if we had follow up data, we could have reported detailed outcome data and contributed better evidence for others to follow. Lack of a comparator group has limited the calculation of outcomes and hence the validity of our observations. Furthermore, our biopsies are reported out of the country limiting the possibility of clinicopathological discussion meetings. If this was possible, some diagnoses might be altered. Despite including more than a hundred transplant patients, comparison to data from highly established transplant setting might undersize our study population. Finally, we saw significant contribution of PB in detection of subclinical diseases but couldn’t do a biopsy in many of our patients due to many limiting factors including coagulation related contraindications and previously biopsied for indication.

Our follow up was short. Though we looked into well-known early markers of long term graft outcomes, we missed many factors that impact long term outcomes. For example, transplant glomerulopathy (TG) is an important element adversely impacting transplant outcomes. We couldn’t capture TG because our biopsies were done early on and TG is a disease detected later in the course of transplant. We need to do more biopsies later in the course of transplant to catch other conditions of graft damage like TG and diabetic nephropathy. Longer term prospective comparative study of PB and non PB groups in terms of graft and patient survivals may give more meaningful results to make stronger recommendations.

Conclusions

Findings of this study suggest that surveillance allograft biopsies contribute significantly to the total rejections detected early after transplantation. Additionally, protocol biopsy also helps in detection of glomerulonephritis and BK virus nephropathy. Protocol biopsies at various time points may be more helpful in delineating the natural history of the allograft.
