Comparative transcriptomic points of departure analysis of pesticides in human Caco-2 and Hep G2 cells
Authors: Ke Xu, Krittika Mittal, Niladri Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlight•Transcriptomic points of departure derived for 19 pesticides.•Human liver cell results showed concordance with chronic animal data.•Short-term in vitro studies produced conservative regulatory estimates.•In vitro transcriptomics may support chemical screening and prioritization.Keywords: Pesticides, Transcriptomic point of departure (tPOD), In vitro toxicology, RNA sequencing

Introduction

Pesticides are essential in modern agriculture as they help control disease vectors and ensure crop productivity (Cooper and Dobson, 2007; Oerke and Dehne, 2004). However, their widespread use raises concerns about unintended effects on non-target organisms, including humans, through dietary exposure or environmental contamination (Damalas and Eleftherohorinos, 2011; Kim et al., 2017). These concerns have led to regulatory evaluations and risk assessments including derivation of maximum residue limits and acceptable intakes (FAO/WHO, n.d.a). Traditionally, pesticide risk assessment has relied on chronic and sub-chronic mammalian animal studies to identify apical endpoints such as carcinogenicity (OECD, 2018) and reproductive toxicity (OECD, 2025), which are then used to derive no-observed-adverse-effect levels (NOAELs). However, such experiments are time-consuming, resource-intensive, and ethically questionable (Basu et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2022). For example, a study from the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) Office of Pesticide Programs estimates that toxicological evaluations of a single pesticide may cost $8 to $16 million USD and use 5000 to 7000 animals (Craig et al., 2019). Furthermore, extrapolating results from animals to humans introduces uncertainty, particularly for pesticides with species-specific modes of action or metabolism (Krewski et al., 2010). These limitations have prompted growing interest in new approach methodologies (NAMs) that offer faster, more cost-effective and ethical, and human-relevant toxicity information (Schmeisser et al., 2023; van der Zalm et al., 2022).

One notable new approach, the US EPA Transcriptomic Assessment Product (ETAP), sets a foundation to derive transcriptomic-based reference values (Brennan et al., 2024a). Transcriptomic data capture early molecular perturbations, offer mechanistic insights into toxicity, and can also be modeled to derive transcriptomic points of departure (tPODs) (Harrill et al., 2019; Harrill et al., 2021a; Head et al., 2025; Reardon et al., 2023). The underlying premise is that tPODs obtained from short-term animal studies will yield data that may be more conservative than, or comparable to, apical points of departure (POD) derived from chronic animal studies. This hypothesis also extends to in vitro studies (Emberley-Korkmaz et al., 2025; Harrill et al., 2021a; Jackson et al., 2025; Reardon et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025), though the direct application of in vitro tPODs in regulatory decision-making remains challenging because of the lack of a human exposure context. To address this limitation, physiologically based toxicokinetic (PBTK) modeling (Pearce et al., 2017) can be used for in vitro-in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE). This approach translates in vitro concentrations into administered equivalent doses (AEDs), enabling direct comparison with animal-derived PODs used to establish regulatory thresholds for chronic and acute exposures (OECD, 2021a). Beyond providing quantitative PODs, the tPOD approach also yields mechanistic information by identifying enriched biological pathways, thereby linking molecular perturbations to potential modes of action. As such, tPODs are increasingly recognized as both informative and protective in regulatory applications (Harrill et al., 2021b; Harrill et al., 2019). Collectively, this approach highlights the potential of transcriptomic technologies in providing quantitative and mechanistic information in risk assessment of chemicals, potentially enabling faster, mechanistically informed, and more ethically sustainable evaluations of human health risk.

In this study, we applied the TPD-seq workflow (Mittal et al., 2025) to derive tPODs for 19 pesticides prioritized for further assessment by Fisheries and Oceans Canada based on their occurrence and potential risks in Canadian aquatic environments (Anderson et al., 2021), using two established human cell lines relevant to oral exposure, Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. Caco-2 cells are widely used as an in vitro model of the intestinal epithelium and are commonly applied to investigate oral exposure, intestinal transport, and absorption (Sambuy et al., 2005). HepG2 cells are frequently used as a hepatic model to evaluate xenobiotic-induced responses (Wilkening et al., 2003). The TPD-seq workflow integrates in vitro cytotoxicity assays (based on principles and steps from OECD testing guideline 249) (OECD, 2021b) with a commercially available high-throughput RNA-sequencing technique and an open-source, cloud-based software tool (Liu et al., 2023) designed for transcriptomics data analysis. The calculated tPODs were converted to transcriptomic administered equivalent doses (t-AEDs) and compared with in vivo PODs from animal studies. Following the ETAP approach (Brennan et al., 2024), uncertainty factors were subsequently applied to derive transcriptomic reference values (TRVs) and were then compared with available regulatory human health thresholds, including acceptable daily intakes (ADIs) (FAO/WHO, n.d.b.), reference doses (RfDs) (EPA, n.d.), human health benchmarks for pesticides (HHBPs) (EPA, 2021), and minimal risk levels (MRLs) (ATSDR, n.d.). In addition, pathway analysis of the gene expression data enabled the identification of significantly enriched biological pathways, thereby providing mechanistic insights into potential modes of action. The overarching hypothesis of this study was that this in vitro transcriptomic workflow would yield tPOD data that are comparable with existing human health thresholds for the pesticides evaluated.

Materials and methods

Materials

The pesticides studied include: Aminomethyl-phosphonic acid (AMPA), atrazine (ATA), carbaryl (CAR), chlorothalonil (COL), chlorantraniliprole (CAT), chlorpyrifos (CLP), clothianidin (CLN), dicamba (DIC), dimethenamid-P (DIP), diquat dibromide monohydrate (DIQ), diazinon (DIZ), glyphosate (GLY), imidacloprid (IMI), imazethapyr (IMZ), metolachlor (MET), metribuzin (MBZ), permethrin (PER), S-metolachlor (SMET), and thiamethoxam (THX). Pesticide selection was detailed previously (Emberley-Korkmaz et al., 2023), and additional information on each chemical is in the supplementary information (Supplementary Table ST1), including vendor, lot numbers, chemical classifications, and physicochemical properties.

Cell culture

The Caco-2 cell line (HTB-37) and HepG2 cell line (HB-8065) were originally obtained from ATCC and cultured according to their protocols. Caco-2 cells (passage number 30–35) and HepG2 cells (passage number 9–14) were seeded in 100 mm Petri dishes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and supplemented with 10 mL of complete culture medium containing Gibco Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (WISENT, Quebec, Canada), and 1% Penicillin-Streptavidin. Both cell lines were maintained in the Caron Oasis™ Benchtop IR CO2 Incubator (VWR, Canada) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with 95% humidity. Media changes were performed every two days until the cells reached at least 90% confluency for sub-culturing or experiments using 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA (WISENT).

Chemical exposure

The experimental protocol was based on details published elsewhere (Mittal et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025), and these were largely based on principles documented in OECD Test No 249. An initial study was performed to characterize the cytotoxic effects of each pesticide, and this was followed by a subsequent exposure study that was designed to derive tPODs data. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 35,000 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 hrs. The next day, stock solutions of 200 mM were prepared for 17 pesticides. Due to solubility limitations, GLY and COL were prepared at 20 mM. All stock solutions were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) except for DIQ, AMPA, and GLY, which were prepared in Milli-Q water. For cytotoxicity testing, the stock solutions were further diluted to 1000 µM as the highest exposure concentration to make sure that the DMSO concentration is non-cytotoxic to cells (<0.5% v/v). For COL, the 20 mM stock resulted in a higher final DMSO concentration at 1000 µM. This concentration was retained based on prior in-house testing showing no cytotoxicity at 10% DMSO under the same assay conditions (Mittal et al., 2025). Each pesticide was tested at eight concentrations (1000 µM, 500 µM, 125 µM, 62.5 µM, 31.3 µM, 15.6 µM, 7.8 µM, 3.9 µM) plus a 0 µM concentration (0.5% DMSO) as the negative control, following a two-fold serial dilution in serum-free DMEM medium. Hydrogen peroxide was used as a positive control due to its known cytotoxicity, well-characterized effects on cells and cost-effectiveness, making it suitable for validating assay performance and enabling comparison with previous studies. Each sample was tested in triplicate, representing biological replicates obtained from three independent cell passages (one well per passage, three wells per concentration).

For the subsequent transcriptomics study, the highest concentration of each chemical was the effective concentration that reduced cell viability by 20% (EC20) of controls, as determined by the cytotoxicity assay. If a pesticide did not induce cytotoxicity, 1000 µM was used as the highest exposure concentration. At these exposure levels, final DMSO concentrations were ≤0.5% (v/v) for all pesticides. Each pesticide was tested at eight concentrations (eight wells) that followed a two-fold serial dilution in serum-free culture media. Three replicates were tested for each sample, with one replicate from each independent cell passage (three wells per concentration).

Library preparation and sequencing

The library preparations for sequencing followed the TPD-seq method, as detailed previously (Mittal et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). We used the QIAseq UPXome RNA library kit (QIAGEN) (QIAGEN, 2024) for cell lysis, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) removal, reverse transcription, cDNA pooling, library indexing, and amplification. A quality check was performed using the Tapestation 4150 (Agilent). All prepared libraries were stored at −20 °C before shipping to the Genome Quebec Innovation Center where they were quantified with the PicoGreen assay. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 PE100 flowcell to generate 150 bp paired-end reads, and the raw data files are publicly available at NCBI GEO under the accession number GSE328863.

Data analysis

The complete workflow for data analysis followed the TPD-seq (Fig. 1) (Mittal et al., 2025). Raw RNA-sequencing fastq files were first filtered based on read depth (greater than 10%, ∼434,000) and percentage of mapping (greater than 35%). All files passing the filters were then demultiplexed by Deplexer v.0.01 (Liu, 2025) to remove barcodes and indexes to yield individual sample read files. The demultiplexed files were then uploaded to ExpressAnalyst (https://www.expressanalyst.ca/) and mapped to the human genome (hg38.p14 Ensembl v113 GCA_000001405.29, April 2025) using the Kallisto pseudoalignment algorithm to obtain count tables (Ewald et al., 2023). Quality control metrics of the sequencing data were assessed using CLC Genomics Workbench (v25.0.1, QIAGEN), with sequencing reads mapped to the human reference genome (hg38.p14; Ensembl v113, GCA_000001405.29, April 2025) using STAR alignment (Dobin et al., 2013).

Workflow illustrating data analysis steps from in vitro transcriptomic data.

Subsequent analyses were performed using ExpressAnalyst as detailed in Supplementary Figure SF1 (Ewald et al., 2023). Briefly, raw count tables were normalized using the DESeq2 median-of-ratios method and subsequently log2-transformed. Genes were filtered using average abundance and variance thresholds of 5 and 15%, respectively. Identification of concentration-responsive genes (CRGs) was based on an analysis of protein-coding genes using the Williams trend test (WTT) (Williams, 1971). Benchmark dose-response analysis was performed on the identified CRGs using eight regression models with a lack-of-fit p-value of 0.1. To ensure sufficient data for dose-response modeling, a threshold of 200 gene benchmark doses (BMDs) was used to determine the filtering stringency under the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. An FDR-adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05 was applied when the total number of genes BMDs exceeded 200. When a pesticide resulted in fewer than 200 gene BMD with FDR correction, the "FDR off" dataset was utilized instead for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Figure SF1). Based on our previous studies, the parameters for gene BMD calculations can cause significant differences in tPODs (Xu et al., 2025). In this study, we used an absolute fold change of 1.5 or 2 and a BMR factor of 1 or 1.35 to derive a range of gene BMDs (Brennan et al., 2024). Subsequently, tPOD values were calculated as: (1) the BMD of the 20th most sensitive gene (tPOD20th); (2) the maximum of the first peak of the gene-level BMD distribution (tPODmode) (calculated if there are at least 5% of the gene-BMDs contained within the first peak); (3) the 10th percentile of all gene-level BMDs (tPOD10th perc) and the lower concentration-responsive gene-BMD (tPODLCRD). Finally, pathway-level BMDs were derived using the KEGG pathways database, with adjusted p < 0.05 and a minimum number of hits of three per pathway. Analyses were repeated across combinations of parameters, and thus the results are presented as ranges throughout the manuscript. All figures were generated using RStudio (version 4.3.2).

Mass balance modeling and administered equivalent dose calculation (AED)

The experimental concentrations of the pesticides in the microplate wells were predicted based on the nominal concentrations using the IV-MBM EQP v2.1 mass balance model (Armitage et al., 2021). This was to determine the biologically effective dose in the well based on each chemical’s physicochemical properties. The parameters used for modeling were obtained from the developer’s website (https://beta.eas-e-suite.com/). The Excel/VBA spreadsheet version of the model was used in this study and is available for download at https://arnotresearch.com/models/.

Next, the tPOD values derived from both cell lines were used as inputs for an in vitro-in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) model to estimate human-relevant doses. In this study, we applied the R package “httk” (v. 2.6.1; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=httk), developed by the US EPA, as a reverse dosimetry tool to calculate the t-AEDs. The compartmental PBTK model for oral exposure was used to generate two sets of t-AEDs based on in vitro results from Caco-2 cells (gut compartment) and HepG2 cells (liver compartment). In total, t-AEDs could be estimated for 14 of the 19 chemicals; SMET, DIQ, CAT, AMPA, and GLY were excluded due to the lack of available physicochemical properties (e.g., logP, molecular weight, pKa) and toxicokinetic parameters (e.g., intrinsic clearance, fraction unbound in plasma, and blood-to-plasma partitioning) in the database. The predicted human t-AED values for each pesticide were compared with the corresponding in vivo PODs from animal studies used to derive the acute and chronic reference doses. These values were obtained from the World Health Organization Joint Meeting on Pesticide Residues (WHO JMPR) database (FAO/WHO, n.d.b), US EPA (EPA, 2003; EPA, n.d.; ATSDR, n.d.; EPA, 2021), and EFSA journal (EFSA, 2023).

The AEDs can be further transformed into transcriptomic-based reference values (TRV) for the corresponding chemicals by dividing an equivalent oral dose by uncertainty factors (UF) (Equation 1) (Brennan et al., 2024). The UFs are intended to account for: 1) intraspecies variability due to variability among the exposed population (UFH); 2) interspecies variability due to animal-human data extrapolation (UFA); 3) uncertainties from sub-chronic to chronic extrapolation (UFS); 4) uncertainties from extrapolating from the lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) instead of the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) (UFL); and 5) database uncertainty (UFD). Based on the design of this study, a composite UF of 1000 was calculated as the product of UFH (10), UFS (10), and UFD (10) to account for the intraspecies variability, exposure duration uncertainties, and database uncertainty. The resulting TRVs were then compared with chronic and acute human health regulatory thresholds. In this study, the chronic values include the ADIs from WHO (FAO/WHO, n.d.b) and EFSA (EFSA, 2023), chronic RfDs (cRfDs) from the U.S. EPA Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) (EPA, 2003), human health benchmarks for pesticides (HHBP) from the U.S. EPA (2021), and minimal risk levels (MRLs) from the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) (n.d.). The acute thresholds include acute reference doses (ARfDs) from JMPR (FAO/WHO, n.d.b) and EFSA (2023), acute RfDs (aRfDs) from HHBP from the U.S. EPA (2021), and minimal risk levels (MRLs) from the ATSDR (n.d.). All comparison figures were generated using R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Cytotoxicity

For Caco-2 cells, the cell viability in DMSO solvent controls relative to the untreated controls was 97.26% ± 4.00%, with variability among the no-cell control wells being <20% for all experiments. Similarly, in HepG2 cells, viability in DMSO solvent controls compared to the untreated controls was 101.96% ± 2.38%, with variability in no-cell controls also being <20%. As for the positive control H2O2, the EC50 was 6.27 mM in Caco-2 cells and 5.60 mM in HepG2 cells, respectively. The full compilation of cytotoxicity results is available in Supplemental Table ST2.

For pesticides, the EC50 values in Caco-2 cells ranged from 12.66 µM to 406.59 µM, and in HepG2 cells ranged from 120.72 µM to 870.7 µM. EC50 values could not be determined for nine pesticides in Caco-2 cells and fourteen pesticides in HepG2 cells. EC20 values were also calculated, ranging from 3.42 µM to 266.30 µM in Caco-2 cells and from 46.10 µM to 812.30 µM in HepG2 cells. The EC50 and EC20 data are compiled visually in Fig. 2. In Caco-2 cells, the top three cytotoxic pesticides were IMZ, COL, and CAR, and in HepG2 cells, these were DIQ, DIP, and MET (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table ST2).

In the Caco-2 cell line, 15 of the 19 pesticides did not meet the >200 gene BMD threshold under FDR correction and were therefore analyzed using the less stringent filtering approach. Only CAR, CAT, DIP, and IMI satisfied the threshold with FDR correction. However, even with relaxed filtering, a few pesticides (e.g. AMPA and MBZ) still yielded relatively low numbers of gene BMDs (78 and 46, respectively), depending on the statistical method applied. Overall, tPOD values in Caco-2 spanned nearly four orders of magnitude. Out of the eight cytotoxic pesticides (i.e., those with EC50 values), CLN (tPOD: 0.032–1.1 µM) and MET (tPOD: 0.022–1.1 µM) showed the lowest tPODs, with mean values between 0.1 and 1 µM. The other cytotoxic pesticides with an EC20 lower than 100 µM, namely COL, PER, CAR, DIC, SMET, IMZ, and DIQ, yielded average tPODs in the range of 1 µM to 10 µM. The only remaining cytotoxic pesticide, DIP, had an EC20 greater than 100 µM, and yielded a tPOD value in the range of 10 µM to 100 µM. Approximately half of the pesticides yielded tPODs above 10 µM, among which AMPA, ATA, CAT, GLY, IMI, MBZ, and THX resulted in average tPODs exceeding 100 µM.

The HepG2 cell line showed a different pattern, with 12 out of 19 pesticides (63%) in HepG2 yielding >200 gene BMDs with FDR correction. The number of CRGs ranged from 284 to 2022, while gene BMDs were between 119 and 1269. Seven pesticides, including ATA, CLN, CLP, GLY, IMI, IMZ, and THX, were analyzed without FDR correction and produced a CRG range between 295–2118 and gene BMDs with values as low as 51 and as high as 652. Overall, tPOD values in HepG2 spanned three orders of magnitude of concentrations. The lowest tPOD values were observed for COL (0.3–4.4 µM), while CLN, IMI, and IMZ had the highest tPOD values, with ranges extending up to 410 µM. None of the pesticides yielded average tPOD values in HepG2 cells below 1 µM. About 52% of the pesticides, namely COL, SMET, PER, DIQ, CAR, DIC, DIP, CAT, AMPA, and MBZ, resulted in average tPODs ranging between 1 µM and 30 µM. The other half (48%) of the pesticides, including MET, CLN, IMZ, GLY, CLP, DIZ, ATA, IMI, and THX, yielded average tPODs close to and above 100 µM.

The tPOD values indicated similar sensitivity between Caco-2 and HepG2 cells for most pesticides tested, with approximately 70% (12 of 17) showing average tPODs within one order of magnitude (Fig. 2). For the remaining five pesticides, the average tPOD values for AMPA and MBZ in Caco-2 cells were two orders of magnitude higher than those in HepG2 cells. In contrast, Caco-2 cells showed greater sensitivity to CLN, IMZ, and MET with the average tPODs below 5 µM, whereas the corresponding HepG2 values exceeded 50 µM.

Compared to Caco-2 cells, a greater number of pesticides induced significant pathway-level changes in HepG2 cells (16 of 19 pesticides, Table 3). ‘Metabolism’ and ‘human diseases’ were the most frequently represented categories with 15 pathways identified, followed by ‘organismal systems’ (8 pathways), ‘environmental information processing’ (7 pathways), and ‘cellular processes’ (6 pathways) (Fig. 3). Several compounds were associated with extensive pathway enrichment, including MET (9–13 pathways), CAT (4–11 pathways), DIP (3–-17 pathways), DIQ (3–9 pathways), and CAR (2–8 pathways). In contrast, CLP, ATA, PER, and CLN resulted in fewer than three enriched pathways across different statistical conditions (Table 3).

For HepG2 cells, pathways within ‘metabolism’ and ‘human diseases’ displayed some of the lowest minimum BMDs (below 5 µM) (Fig. 3). Higher minimum BMDs exceeding 300 µM were identified in ‘human diseases’, ‘metabolism’ and ‘Environmental Information Processing’ categories. At the pesticide level, PER, ATA, SMET, AMPA, DIC, and CAR were predominantly associated with low pathway BMDs (<10 µM), while DIZ, CLP, CLN, and THX exhibited consistently high BMDs (>300 µM). Benchmarking tPODs with regulatory threshold and reference in vivo values

The derived nominal tPOD values were converted to predicted in vitro concentrations and subsequently translated into t-AEDs (Supplementary Table ST8) before comparing to acute and chronic in vivo PODs (FAO/WHO, n.d.b; EPA, 2003; EFSA, 2023) values from literature (Supplementary Table ST9). For this analysis, t-AEDs derived from the tPOD10th percentile and tPODmode were selected due to their consistency and robustness. The mean t-AEDs ranged from 0.0026 to 81.46 mg/kg bw-day in Caco-2 cells and from 0.0013 to 69.18 mg/kg bw-day in HepG2 cells (Supplementary Table ST10).

Overall, among the 12 pesticides for which both t-AEDs and in vivo PODs were available in Caco-2 cells, six (THX, MBZ, IMI, DIP, CAR, and ATA) yielded mean t-AEDs within approximately one order of magnitude of the corresponding in vivo PODs (Fig. 4A). In HepG2 cells, 10 of the 14 pesticides with available data (THX, MET, MBZ, IMZ, IMI, DIP, CLP, CLN, CAR, and ATA) produced t-AEDs within one order of magnitude of their respective in vivo PODs (Fig. 4B). Among these, five pesticides (THX, MET, IMI, DIP, and CLP) differed by less than three-fold from their corresponding in vivo PODs (Supplementary Table ST10).

A similar comparison was conducted for the t-AEDs and in vivo acute PODs, which ranged from 2 to 300 mg/kg bw-day, with most values (7 of 13) between 10 and 100 mg/kg bw-day. In Caco-2 cells, five pesticides (THX, MBZ, IMI, DIP, and ATA) yielded t-AEDs within approximately one order of magnitude of the corresponding in vivo acute PODs (Supplementary Figure SF4A). In HepG2 cells, six t-AEDs (CAR, CLN, DIZ, IMI, MBZ, and THX) differed by less than one order of magnitude from the acute PODs (Supplementary Figure 4B).

As a next step, and following the ETAP approach from US EPA, t-AEDs were converted to TRVs and compared with corresponding regulatory human health thresholds (Brennan et al., 2024; FAO/WHO, n.d.b.; EPA, n.d.; EPA, 2021, ATSDR, n.d.). In Caco-2 cells, mean TRVs (UF = 1000) spanned approximately four orders of magnitude, ranging from 2.63 × 10–6 mg/kg bw-day (PER) to 0.08 mg/kg bw-day (IMI) (Supplementary Table ST10). In HepG2 cells, TRVs similarly covered approximately four orders of magnitude, from 1.36 × 10–6 mg/kg bw-day (PER) to 0.07 mg/kg bw-day (CLN). Most TRVs (11 of 14) were distributed between 10–4 and 10–2 mg/kg bw-day, with three (CLP, COL, and PER) below 10–4 mg/kg bw-day (Supplementary Table ST10). In comparison, most chronic thresholds (11 of 14) ranged between 0.01 and 0.2 mg/kg bw-day, with two values (CLP and DIZ) below 0.01 mg/kg bw-day and one (IMZ) exceeding 1 mg/kg bw-day (Supplementary Table ST9, ST10).

Overall, 10 of 12 pesticides in Caco-2 cells yielded TRVs lower than the mean corresponding chronic thresholds. Although no significant correlation was observed between TRVs and chronic thresholds (Supplementary Figure SF5), TRVs for MBZ and IMI were within one order of magnitude of the corresponding thresholds (Supplementary Table ST10). In HepG2 cells, TRVs for 13 of 14 pesticides remained below chronic thresholds, out of which four TRVs (THX, IMI, DIZ, and CLN) differed by less than one order of magnitude from their respective thresholds (Supplementary Table ST10). A moderate positive correlation with weak statistical significance (ρ = 0.5, p = 0.07) was observed between TRVs and chronic thresholds in HepG2 cells (Supplementary Figure SF5B).

The acute thresholds were compared similarly to the derived TRVs. The available threshold values ranged from 0.002 to 3 mg/kg bw-day, with most values (8 of 13) between 0.1 and 1 mg/kg bw-day (Supplementary Table ST10). In Caco-2 cells, 9 of 11 pesticides yielded TRVs that were at least two orders of magnitude lower than the corresponding acute thresholds (Supplementary Table ST10). In HepG2 cells, all TRVs were lower than the acute thresholds, with three pesticides (CLP, DIZ, and MBZ) differing by less than one order of magnitude. No significant correlation was observed between TRVs and acute thresholds in either cell line (Supplementary Figure SF6).

Discussion

With the growing need for more rapid, cost-efficient, and ethical methods for chemical risk assessment, the tPOD approach can provide quantitative benchmark dose data with mechanistic information (Harrill et al., 2019; Head et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2022) and are gaining attention from regulatory agencies (Brennan et al., 2024; NTP, 2018; Reardon et al., 2023). In previous in vitro studies, we have demonstrated the use of the TPD-seq workflow in deriving tPODs and providing mechanistic insights for common solvents and positive controls (Mittal et al., 2025), chemical mixtures (Mittal et al., 2024), and emerging chemicals (Xu et al., 2025). However, the possibility that the method could yield POD values comparable to those of sub-chronic and chronic animal studies remained uncertain. In this study, we selected 19 pesticides from a priority list compiled by Fisheries and Oceans Canada, which ranked compounds based on sales volume, detection frequency, measured concentrations in Canadian surface waters, inherent toxicity relative to guideline values, and regulatory status in other countries (Anderson et al., 2021). We yield cytotoxicity data, tPOD values, and information on enriched biological pathways for two cell lines relevant to human oral exposure. Our study shows several situations in which the tPOD values yielded information comparable to the corresponding PODs from mammalian studies that were used to establish acute and chronic human health thresholds (FAO/WHO, n.d.b.; EPA, n.d.; ATSDR.n.d.; EPA, 2021). In doing so, we add to a growing number of studies that demonstrate that the tPODs approach can generate information meaningful for scientific and regulatory situations without the use of animals and within a few short weeks for only a few thousand dollars. Contrast this with typical toxicity tests of pesticides that cost several millions of dollars and use thousands of animals (Craig et al., 2019).

In general, the cell culture studies yielded high quality data. The cytotoxicity results (Supplementary Table ST2) passed multiple quality control checks in accordance with OECD Guideline 249 (OECD, 2021b). The media control and solvent control (exposed to DMSO) samples showed no evidence of cytotoxicity (viability between 93.19%−102.80%). The EC50 of the positive control (H2O2) for Caco-2 cells and for HepG2 cells were 6.27 mM and 5.59 mM, respectively. These values were consistent with cytotoxicity results previously reported for H₂O₂ in an earlier study using the same cell lines (6.07 mM for Caco-2 and 4.37 mM for HepG2) (Mittal et al., 2025).

The quality metrics for sequencing results also aligned with previous studies, where the mapping percentages for HepG2 cells ranged from approximately 70% to 90% in most cases (Mittal et al., 2025). For Caco-2 cells, the mapping was also comparable to a past study (70–93%) (Mittal et al., 2025). Although 23 of 449 Caco-2 samples showed lower mapping percentages (<50%), these were generally accompanied by high total read counts, suggesting sufficient sequencing depth despite reduced mapping efficiency (Supplementary Table ST3). Overall, the workflow could still capture approximately 7500 to 9000 unique genes with counts over 5 per sample for downstream analysis, which is higher than the number in the TPD-seq protocol (∼6500) (Mittal et al., 2025). Two pesticides, DIZ and CLP, were excluded from downstream analyses in Caco-2 cells due to low total read counts. However, it is noteworthy that CLP produced the highest number of CRGs (>3900) despite the limited sequencing depth, with >1000 gene-level BMDs available that could potentially support tPOD derivation (Supplementary Table ST5). This indicates that the workflow may still yield biologically meaningful information even with limited read counts.

For Caco-2 cells, more pesticides produced measurable EC50 values than in HepG2 cells (8 pesticides vs. 5 pesticides), while fewer pesticides induced >200 gene BMDs (4 pesticides vs. 15 pesticides). In addition, more pesticides in Caco-2 cells had tPOD lower bounds below 1 µM than HepG2 cells (8 pesticides vs. 1 pesticide), and fewer pesticides were identified with enriched pathways (8 pesticides vs. 16 pesticides). These differences likely arise from intrinsic biological properties of the two cell models, particularly their metabolic capacities (Sambuy et al., 2005; Uchida et al., 2024). Caco-2 cells share morphological and functional features with mature intestinal enterocytes (Sambuy et al., 2005), with known expression of receptors and transporters (Hilgendorf et al., 2007), and therefore are often used as a model to study transportation and absorption. For example, organic anion transporters can alter the intracellular dose and allow higher accumulation of some weak acids in Caco-2 cells (Keiser et al., 2017). Consistent with this, the weak acid pesticides IMZ and DIC induced cytotoxicity in Caco-2 cells, whereas no EC50/EC20 values or significantly enriched pathways were observed in HepG2 cells (Supplementary Tables ST2 and ST7). Moreover, Caco-2 cells are known to express low levels of some metabolizing enzymes, such as cytochrome P450 enzymes (Verhoeckx et al., 2015). In this study, metabolic pathways were not commonly identified among the enriched pathways in Caco-2 cells. Instead, the ‘organismal systems’ and ‘human disease’ categories contained both the largest number of enriched pathways and the majority of low-BMD pathways, including pathways related to immune, endocrine, and nervous system functions, as well as molecular pathways perturbed in disease states (Fig. 3). These categories primarily reflect systemic and disease-associated processes, whereas comparatively fewer pathways were related to metabolic functions (Kanehisa et al., 2023). Together, these observations suggest that higher intracellular exposure, combined with limited metabolic capacity in Caco-2 cells, may contribute to the activation of disease-associated pathways and cytotoxic responses at lower concentrations.

In HepG2 cells, despite the absence of measurable cytotoxicity for many pesticides, tPODs were derived for all 19 compounds, and more pesticides induced significantly enriched pathways (16 out of 19) than in Caco-2 cells. This demonstrates the applicability of the workflow for quantitatively characterizing dose-response perturbations at the molecular level, even in the absence of observable apical toxicity. HepG2 cells retain some phase I and phase II metabolic activities and generally exhibit greater metabolic competence than Caco-2 cells (Guo et al., 2011). The relatively higher glutathione (GSH) and antioxidant capacity of HepG2 cells may also mitigate oxidative stress and contribute to the higher tPODs and pathway BMDs observed in this study (Uchida et al., 2024). Based on the pathway analysis results in this study for CLN (Supplementary Table ST7), the enriched oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic pathways suggest that HepG2 cells can metabolize CLN via oxidative metabolism, resulting in an average tPOD of >100 µM and an EC50 exceeding 1000 µM. This finding is also supported by another study, in which gene sets associated with ‘xenobiotic metabolism’ were differentially regulated in the liver of rats exposed to CLN (Alarcan et al., 2021). In contrast, the EC50 for CLN in Caco-2 cells was calculated as 39.84 µM, with tPOD values more than two orders of magnitude lower (<1 µM), and no significantly enriched pathways were detected. Another example is the redox-cycling herbicide DIQ, which activated ‘systemic lupus erythematosus’ and ‘complement and coagulation cascades pathways’ at concentrations below 1 µM in Caco-2 cells, with no metabolic pathways identified. In contrast, DIQ resulted in five metabolic pathways in HepG2 cells, all induced at concentrations greater than 1 µM (Fig. 3). These findings align with a previous study on 6PPD-quinone, an ozonated transformation product of 6PPD, which induced cytotoxicity in Caco-2 cells but not in HepG2 cells (Xu et al., 2025). In that study, Caco-2 cells exhibited lower tPODs and enrichment of stress- and disease-related pathways, whereas HepG2 cells showed higher tPODs and enrichment of metabolic pathways (Xu et al., 2025). Collectively, these results suggest that differences in relative metabolic competence and cellular antioxidant capacity between the two models may contribute to the observed toxicity outcomes and highlight the importance of cell model selection for chemical hazard characterization.

The increasing interest in the tPODs approach originates from the hypothesis that in vitro short-term molecular responses may provide early indications of biological perturbations that are relevant to longer-term toxicity outcomes in whole animals (Harrill et al., 2021a; Jackson et al., 2025; Reardon et al., 2023). Supporting this hypothesis, a previous study using the same set of pesticides studied here showed that in vitro tPODs derived from rainbow trout gill cells were significantly correlated with fish chronic lethal and sublethal effect concentrations (Emberley-Korkmaz et al., 2025). In the current study, the in vitro tPODs were converted to t-AEDs and subsequently to TRVs using the ETAP approach, which estimates a daily oral dose unlikely to cause adverse non-cancer health effects over a lifetime (Brennan et al., 2024). Following the ETAP method, the concordance between the t-AEDs and in vivo PODs was evaluated using an approach similar to that illustrated in the ETAP supporting documentation (Chang et al., 2024) for comparing transcriptomic BMDs and chronic apical BMDs. TRV-to-RfD ratios were used to compare transcriptomic-derived thresholds with human health benchmarks following the standard ETAP method (Brennan et al., 2024). Based on the results of this study, approximately 40% of t-AEDs were within one order of magnitude of the in vivo acute PODs (Supplementary Figure SF4), and approximately 55% were within one order of magnitude of the in vivo chronic PODs (Fig. 4), indicating some degree of concordance across both cell lines. The moderate concordance observed in this study likely reflects the limited quantitative predictive ability of a generalized, single –cell line transcriptomic workflow that does not explicitly account for chemical-specific modes of action or target-tissue responses, among other considerations, that are meaningful to in vivo responses. Consistent with this, several pesticides exhibited deviations greater than one order of magnitude between t-AEDs and corresponding in vivo PODs, likely reflecting chemical-specific differences in mechanisms of toxicity, tissue susceptibility, and toxicokinetic processes that are not fully represented in the current workflow. Nevertheless, most t-AEDs were lower than the corresponding in vivo PODs, suggesting that the transcriptomic-derived estimates are generally protective of (i.e., lower than) effect levels identified in animal studies. TRVs were generally two to three orders of magnitude lower than the corresponding acute and chronic thresholds, indicating a consistently more conservative estimate relative to regulatory benchmarks (Supplementary Table ST10). This pattern is consistent with the objective of the ETAP framework to derive transcriptomic reference values as protective screening-level estimates. This, and our results, suggest that transcriptomic-derived TRVs, at this moment, may best be used for screening and prioritization rather than as direct substitutes for regulatory thresholds.When comparing the results between the two cell lines, the derived t-AEDs and TRVs for HepG2 cells were more closely aligned with chronic in vivo PODs and chronic human health thresholds. The mean t-AEDs fell within approximately one order of magnitude of the corresponding values for in vivo PODs of approximately 71% of the tested pesticides in the study for HepG2 cells. TRVs derived from HepG2 cells exhibited moderate correlations with the mean chronic in vivo thresholds from multiple literature sources (Supplementary Figures SF4-SF6). This pattern is consistent with the lower tPODs and enrichment of stress- and disease-related pathways observed in Caco-2 cells, which may limit their concordance with chronic toxicity thresholds. In contrast, the metabolic functions of HepG2 cells may better capture biological processes relevant to longer-term exposure, potentially making them a closer in vitro proxy for chronic toxicity scenarios. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of selecting appropriate cell models for in vitro transcriptomic testing. The differences between the two cell lines likely reflect the fundamental differences in cellular function and xenobiotic metabolism between the two models, as Caco-2 cells primarily represent intestinal barrier and stress-response processes, while HepG2 cells are more involved in chemical biotransformation and detoxification. From a risk-assessment perspective, Caco-2 cells may be particularly useful for evaluating early intestinal perturbations associated with oral exposure and identifying chemicals that elicit stress-related responses at low concentrations, whereas HepG2 cells may provide complementary information for screening chemicals for longer-term hepatic responses and deriving transcriptomic thresholds that are more closely aligned with chronic toxicity benchmarks. Consequently, these datasets are likely most useful as screening-level evidence for prioritization rather than as stand-alone evidence for regulatory decision-making. Nonetheless, as our understanding of tPODs improves, the data presented here can be re-analyzed in the future.

Despite our results suggesting that tPOD-derived data are associated with chronic animal outcomes for certain chemicals, there are some study limitations to acknowledge. First, we noted that the tPODs were derived from acute exposures, which may not fully capture the adaptive responses or cumulative effects that occur under repeated or chronic exposure scenarios. Second, different FDR thresholds were applied during tPOD derivation depending on the number of gene BMDs identified. A data-adaptive FDR strategy was used to avoid eliminating responsive genes and becoming overly conservative in low-signal datasets, which could otherwise yield non-informative tPOD estimates. Nevertheless, the use of different FDR settings may introduce variability and limit direct comparability across chemicals or cell models. Third, the use of single cell lines such as Caco-2 and HepG2 offers reproducibility and convenience but do not reflect the full complexity of in vivo tissues, including interactions with immune, endocrine, or other organ systems. Although HepG2 cells retain some metabolic activity, their phase I and phase II metabolic capacities in conventional 2D culture conditions are substantially lower than those of primary human hepatocytes (Wilkening et al., 2003). Consequently, transcriptomic responses observed in this study may not fully capture metabolite-mediated effects of the pesticides. Given that some pesticides are neurotoxic (e.g., PER, DIZ, IMI, CLN, etc.), neuronal models such as neuroblastoma cells SH-SY5Y (Kovalevich and Langford, 2013) or nAChR-expressing HEK293 cells (Buisson et al., 1996) could provide more relevant insights into neurotoxic effects. Fourth, extrapolation to human health risk assessment from in vitro data remains challenging, as the t-AEDs and tPODs depend on extensive modeling assumptions and limited pharmacokinetic information, which introduces uncertainty into the predictions and reduces the overall sample size (number of chemicals included in downstream analysis). It is also important to acknowledge that both the in vivo acute and chronic thresholds (Supplementary Table ST9) and the derived in vitro metrics (t-AEDs and TRVs) spanned several orders of magnitude across pesticides, and that testing of a single chemical using typical animal-based approaches also yields variable outcomes. As a result, the concordance and correlations observed in this study are based on averaged values compiled from multiple sources and should be interpreted cautiously as approximate. Differences in study design, regulatory derivation methods, and uncertainty factors may further contribute to variability in the reported thresholds. Nevertheless, the observed trends provide valuable insight into the relative alignment of tPODs from acute exposures with chronic in vivo PODs and regulatory toxicity benchmarks and support their potential utility for screening and prioritization purposes. This is further supported by the relatively fewer resources needed to conduct these types of in vitro tPOD studies versus animal studies.

Conclusion

This study applied the TPD-seq workflow to derive tPODs for 19 pesticides of concern and evaluated their relevance to regulatory toxicity thresholds. The streamlined library preparation enabled rapid processing of large sample numbers while substantially reducing cost and time. Despite limited cytotoxic responses, tPODs were successfully derived for all pesticides. Caco-2 cells exhibited greater sensitivity to stress-related responses, whereas HepG2 cells reflected mitigated responses likely related to metabolic activity. Comparisons with in vivo PODs demonstrated that t-AEDs and TRVs from Caco-2 and HepG2 cells were generally protective of, or moderately correlated to, chronic animal study results and the corresponding thresholds, respectively. Although correlations were generally weak to moderate and not statistically significant, the overall patterns suggest that transcriptomic-based AEDs and TRVs may still provide useful preliminary insights for chemicals lacking extensive in vivo data. These results are consistent with prior studies showing that short-term in vitro-derived tPODs are protective of, or associated with, apical PODs and regulatory thresholds (Emberley-Korkmaz et al., 2025; Harrill et al., 2021a; Reardon et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Collectively, these results support the utility of transcriptomic endpoints for detecting early molecular perturbations and highlight their potential for chemical screening in risk assessment.

In conclusion, in vitro transcriptomic data derived from acute exposure may provide meaningful information that supports risk assessments and chemical prioritization in an animal-free, resource efficient manner. Further testing of the workflow with more physiologically relevant cell models and validation of the modeling parameters will be necessary for future application of this method towards regulatory decision-making and other applications.
