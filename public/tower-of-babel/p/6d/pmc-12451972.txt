Manganese (III) tetrakis (4-benzoic acid) porphyrin (MnTBAP) represses sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase expression and targets the sulfido-redox system in glioblastoma models
Authors: Elise Malard, Benoît Bernay, Jérôme Toutain, Samantha Ballesta, Marie Lévêque, Julien Pontin, Samuel Valable, Myriam Bernaudin, Laurent Chatre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The adaptation of the redox system and bioenergetics is a major factor contributing to cancer metabolism. Redox therapy is promising but still requires molecular studies that consider the reactive species interactome (RSI) concept, which integrates reactive oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, carbonyl species, and redox enzymes. Our aim was to decipher the role of the RSI in glioblastoma (GBM), including by challenging the RSI with the MnTBAP redox agent.Methods:The effects of MnTBAP on the redox system and bioenergetics were investigated on several GBM models, namely in vitro 2D culture, in vitro 3D culture with two human GBM tumoroids, and in vivo preclinical model, which included male and female comparisons.Results:We show – for the first time – that MnTBAP represses the sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR) involved in the sulfur metabolism and bioenergetics, and targets the RSI through the sulfido-redox system. Through in vitro silencing and overexpression approaches, we also demonstrate that SQOR contributed to GBM cell growth and that its decrease is involved in the molecular effect of MnTBAP. Consequently, MnTBAP induces a switch between apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis depending on the glioblastoma models.Conclusion:Our findings represent the next step in establishing a better understanding of redox biology in the context of GBM.KEYWORDS: Glioblastoma, MnTBAP, SQOR, reactive species, mitochondria

Introduction

Bioenergetics and redox alterations are shared hallmarks of aging, inflammation, and many diseases, including metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancers [1,2]. Cancer metabolism is characterized by both bioenergetics and redox reprogramming to meet oxidative dysregulation and energy demand during cancer survival, progression, and resistance to therapies [3]. Additionally, hypoxia – another major hallmark of solid cancer – contributes to reprogramming bioenergetics and epigenetics to maintain cancer stem-like cells, and to trigger resistance to conventional cancer therapies and immunotherapy – including through mitochondria homeostasis, apoptosis inactivation, and ferroptosis dysfunction [4,5]. The dysregulation of both bioenergetics and the redox system is a primary concern in the fast-growing and aggressive brain cancer glioblastoma (GBM), which requires further investigation for improved cancer targeting [6].

While reactive oxygen species (ROS)-targeting antioxidant cancer therapies have failed, ROS-targeting prooxidant cancer therapy is suggested to improve cancer patient outcomes [7]. However, the cancer context could be much more complicated than targeting ROS alone since the reactive species interactome (RSI) is a contemporary biological stress signaling concept that unifies ROS, reactive nitrogen species (RNS), reactive sulfur species (RSS), reactive carbonyl species (RCS) including malondialdehyde (MDA), and redox enzymes that generate or metabolize the reactive species and downstream biological targets [8,9]. Representative examples include the effects of manganese Mn (III) porphyrins (MnPs) on the RSI (also in cancer), which need further study since their superoxide dismutase (SOD) mimetic activity induces the accumulation of ROS H2O2 and because MnPs can react with RNS ONOO–, the most abundant antioxidant, reduced glutathione (GSH), and the mitochondrial antioxidant ascorbic acid [10,11]. This includes MnTnBuOE-2-PyP5+, which is involved in four Phase II clinical trials on glioma and brain metastases [11]. Additionally, MnPs efficiently attenuate hypoxia-induced O2– and inflammation, which positions this class of agents as particularly interesting for cancer therapy; however, this requires deeper investigations regarding all molecular effects [12].

One promising MnP is Mn (III) tetrakis (4-benzoic acid) porphyrin (MnTBAP), which controls colon cancer progression [13]. MnTBAP is not a SOD mimic per se given that the SOD mimetic effect is debated regarding traces of manganese in this class of agents; instead, it is a scavenger of ONOO– over O2.– in cell models [14,15]. In the present study, we focused on the molecular effects of MnTBAP on GBM, which is the most common primary brain cancer in adults and one of the most deadly conventional therapy-resistant cancers [5]. First, we compared the effects of MnTBAP on an in vitro 2D culture model with two well-known human GBM cell lines, which recapitulate different aspects of GBM heterogeneity [6,16]. In detail, the GBM cell line U251-MG, which includes wild-type isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), mutated phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), tumor protein 53 (P53), and methylated O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter, was compared to the GBM cell line U87-MG, which includes wild-type IDH1 and P53, mutated PTEN, and methylated MGMT promoter [17]. The GBM cell line U251-MG is more invasive than U87-MG [16]. For simplification, the two GBM cell lines were named U251 and U87 in this study. We also included the human GBM LN18 cell line, which is mutated for P53, wild-type for PTEN, IDH1, and non-methylated MGMT promoter [18], as well as the mouse GBM GL261 cell line, which is mutated for P53 and PTEN, and wild-type for IDH1 and non-methylated MGMT promoter in the in vitro study. Then, an in vitro 3D culture model was used with two human GBM tumoroids (2019GBM01-TO with wild-type IDH1 and PTEN), high nuclear localization for P53 (80% of the cells) and non-methylated MGMT promoter, 2021GBM01-TO with wild-type IDH1 and low nuclear localization for P53 (2% of the cells), and mutated PTEN and methylated MGMT promoter. Finally, an in vivo preclinical model of GBM (U251) in immunodeficient mice was used, which included male and female comparisons.

GBM cell lines 2D cell cultures

The U251-MG, U87-MG GBM, LN18 and GL261 cell lines were used in the in vitro GBM 2D study. The human glioblastoma cell lines U251-MG (NCI, Bethesda, MD, U.S.A.), U87-MG (American Type Culture Collection ATCC, Manassas, VA, U.S.A.) and LN18 (a generous gift from Dr. S. Etienne-Manneville, Institut Pasteur, Paris, France) were derived from biopsies of GBM patients. The mouse GBM cell line GL261 (American Type Culture Collection ATCC, Manassas, VA, U.S.A.) was derived from GBM mouse. GBM cell lines were maintained in culture in DMEM medium 1 g/L of glucose (D6046, Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium, Sigma-Aldrich), supplemented with 10% decomplemented fetal calf serum (dFBS) (CVFSVF00, Eurobio, France), 2 mM glutamine (G7513, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 µg/mL of penicillin–streptomycin (P4333, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in a humid atmosphere composed of 21% O2 and 5% CO2. Once at confluence, the cell layer was washed with a phosphate buffer saline (PBS) solution (CS1PBS00, Eurobio, France). The cells were detached by adding 0.25% trypsin – EDTA (T4049, Sigma-Aldrich, France) for 5 min at 37°C. The cells were then counted and seeded at a density of 50,000 cells/mL in T25 flasks.

GBM cell lines treatments: MnTBAP, radiotherapy (RT), SQOR overexpression and silencing

For MnTBAP, cells were treated with MnTBAP 50 or 100 µM (10-1386, TEBU-BIO), and treatment was renewed 3 days later in fresh medium. Cell number was assessed using both Malassez cells and Scepter cell counter (Merck). For RT, cells were irradiated with an X-ray cellular irradiator (Cell-Rad, Faxitron). During 2-Gy irradiation, the energy delivered by the X-ray beam was 130 keV with an intensity of 5 mA and a dose rate of 1.9 Gy/min. After irradiation, the cells were returned to culture without changing the medium in order to preserve the radiation-induced products. For RT + MnTBAP condition, cells were treated with MnTBAP 100 µM directly after irradiation, and MnTBAP was renewed 3 days later in fresh medium. For SQOR overexpression in U251 cells, 10 μg of SQOR (NM_001271213) human untagged clone plasmid (SC330932, Origene, Clinisciences) were transfected in U251 cells using JetPRIME Transfection Reagent (POL114-01, Polypus Transfection). After 24 h, the culture medium was removed and replaced by fresh culture medium ± MnTBAP 100 μM for 6 days, including fresh medium after 3 days. For SQOR silencing in U87 cells, 100 pmol of three different unique SQOR 27mer siRNA duplexes or trilencer-27 universal scrambled negative control siRNA duplex (SQOR Human siRNA Oligo Duplex (Locus ID 58472), Origene, Clinisciences) were transfected in U87 cells using JetPRIME Transfection Reagent (POL114-01, Polypus Transfection). After 24 h, the culture medium was removed and replaced by fresh culture medium ± MnTBAP 100 μM for 6 days, including fresh medium after 3 days.

Processing of patient GBM samples and tumoroid generation

During surgery, fresh tissue was collected and placed in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS, TMS-012, Sigma-Aldrich) and taken immediately to the Department of Pathology (Hospices Civils de Lyon, Centre de Biologie et de Pathologie EST, Lyon) to confirm a preliminary diagnosis of GBM by the neuropathologist. After diagnosis was confirmed, the tissue was distributed and placed in MACS® Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotech) kept at 4°C and sent immediately to the 3D-ONCO platform for reliable tumoroid generation. Upon receipt, the tissue was transferred to a sterile glass dish with sterile PBS and minced into approximately 0.5 to 1 mm diameter pieces using dissection scissors and washed with cold PBS to remove cellular debris. Small explants of tumor were distributed in ultra-low attachment 6-well culture plates (Corning) to inhibit cell adhesion, with 4 mL of medium containing 50% DMEM/F12 (11320033, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50% Neurobasal (21103049, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X GlutaMax (35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X NEAAs (11140050, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X PenStrep (15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X N2 supplement (17502001,Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X B27 w/o vitamin A supplement (12587010, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X 2-mercaptoethanol (31350010, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2.5 mg/ml human insulin (PHR8925, Sigma-Aldrich) per well and placed on an orbital shaker rotating at 90 rpm within a 37°C and 5% CO2 sterile incubator. Tumor pieces generally formed rounded tumoroids within 1–2 weeks, depending on tissue quality and patient-specific tumor growth characteristics. Then, tumoroids were routinely cutted to 200-500 mm diameter pieces using dissection scissors to prevent necrosis and amplify them.

MnTBAP treatment in GBM tumoroid models

Multiple tumoroids were dissociated using 1X Accumax™ (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were strained through a 70 µm filter (Miltenyi Biotech), analyzed for viability by AO/PI staining, and counted using an automatic cell counter (LUNA-FL™, Logos Biosystems). About 5.103 cells per well were seeded in 96-well U-shape microplates Ultra Low Attachment (Corning) in triplicates to form single tumoroid per well. After overnight incubation, tumoroids were treated with MnTBAP 0, 50, 100 or 200 μM for 72 h. Then, the cell cytotoxicity was followed by imaging using Opera Phoenix® Plus Hight-Content Screening System, and assessed according to manufacturer’s instructions by CellTiter-Glo3D® luminescent cell viability assay (G9681, Promega), which assesses ATP content, and quantified with Spark® microplate to determine cell viability.

In vivo GBM model

The U251-MG cells were detached using trypsin (T4049, Sigma-Aldrich, France) and resuspended in 1X PBS (CS1PBS00, Eurobio, France) / 2 mM Glutamin (G7513, Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 1.67.107/mL for inoculation in the right striatum of 8-weeks old both male and female NMRI-nu immunodeficient mice (Janvier Labs). Mice were placed in specific pathogen-free accommodation at the ONCOModels platform at CYCERON (Caen, France). Twenty minutes before the start of the surgery, an injection of Buprécare (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) was given to reduce and limit the animal's pain. Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for induction and maintained at 2.5% during surgery, in a 02/NO2 30/70% mixture allowing the analgesic effect of nitrous oxide. The depth of anesthesia was assessed by weight reflex. Mice were placed and maintained in a stereotaxic frame after application of xylocaine gel to the ear bars. A craniotomy was then performed using a dental drill at the following coordinates: 2 mm lateral to the interhemispheric fissure and 0 mm antero-posterior to bregma. A dental needle (30 G) connected to a Hamilton syringe was then inserted 3.5 mm deep into the craniotomy hole. The solution in which the cells were resuspended was then inoculated at a flow rate of 0.6 µL/min for 5 min to avoid any intracranial excess pressure. A delay of 5 min was then applied before removing the needle to avoid any reflux of the cell suspension. After removal of the needle, the skin of the mice was sutured and the animals returned to their housing cage and were monitored. Body weight was managed during the entire protocol. The probability of survival of the mice was evaluated by a Log-Rank test.

MnTBAP treatment in in vivo GBM model

MnTBAP at 10 mg/kg was widely used in vivo, including in lung injury [19,20], ischemia/reperfusion injury [21] and multi-organ failure [22] studies. In addition, given that in colon cancer intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) of MnTBAP 10 mg/kg three times weekly for two weeks and more decreased the tumor volume [13], we used the same mode of administration and regimen in the present in vivo study. MnTBAP solution was prepared with sodium chloride injection at a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL administered to animals intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg 3 times a week for more than 2 weeks. Control mice receive an identical volume of sodium chloride injection.

Monitoring of tumor growth by MRI

Mice were anesthetized during the MRI acquisition using the same procedure as described previously. The animal was placed in a prone position in a cradle heated to 37°C and its breathing was monitored using a sensor placed under its abdomen. Images were acquired on the CYCERON imaging platform with a high magnetic field Pharmascan (7 Tesla, Bruker) specially designed for small animals. Image acquisition was carried out using Paravision software (Bruker). A FLASH (fast low angle shot) sequence was first carried out for the required pre-sets. This sequence also makes it possible to have the positioning of the animal and thus to delimit the acquisition zone located between the olfactory bulbs and the cerebellum. Anatomical imaging was then performed with a T2w RARE (rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement) sequence. The tumor volume was then delineated manually using Image J software. Tumor delineations were performed manually on all adjacent T2w sections. The tumor volume was obtained by multiplying the sum of the contiguous tumor surfaces by the thickness of the section (0.5 mm). Tumor volume analysis was performed using a multiple Wilcoxon test, and by One-way ANOVA. The MRI allowed to define the starting point of MnTBAP treatment (1 mm3) and the endpoint limit of 40–60 mm3 for biochemical studies.

Left hemisphere and GBM-bearing right hemisphere harvesting

The animals received, 20 min before sacrifice, an injection of Buprecare (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) to alleviate and limit the animal's pain. The animals were then sacrificed by deep sublethal isoflurane (469860, Isovet) anesthesia, followed by thoracotomy and intracardiac perfusion of cold heparinized saline. The brain, then left hemisphere and GBM-bearing right hemisphere were harvested from the cranium, and finally briefly immersed in n-pentane before freezing at – 80°C. For the in vivo biochemical analyses, for two weeks of treatment and considering the endpoint limit of 40–60 mm3 corresponding to the window D38±3 days, used animals were the following: for in vivo GBM with MnTBAP 10 mg/kg, vehicle male N = 3 (1 D36, 1 D38, 1 D41), vehicle female N = 3 (2 D36, 1 D38), MnTBAP male N = 3 (2 D36, 1 D44), and MnTBAP female N = 3 (1 D36, 1 D38, 1 D44).

Proteomic analyses

Sample preparation and analysis

Five µg (proteome) of each protein extract were prepared using a modified Gel-aided Sample Preparation protocol (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4409837/). Samples were digested with trypsin/Lys-C overnight at 37°C. For nano-LC fragmentation, protein or peptide samples were first desalted and concentrated onto a µC18 Omix (Agilent) before analysis. The chromatography step was performed on a NanoElute (Bruker Daltonics) ultra-high-pressure nano flow chromatography system. Approximatively 200 ng of each peptide sample were concentrated onto a C18 pepmap 100 (5 mm x 300 µm i.d.) precolumn (Thermo Scientific) and separated at 50°C onto a reversed phase Reprosil column (25 cm x 75 μm i.d.) packed with 1.6 μm C18 coated porous silica beads (Ionopticks). Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid, 99.9% water (v/v) (A) and 0.1% formic acid in 99.9% ACN (v/v) (B). The nanoflow rate was set at 250 nl/min, and the gradient profile was as follows: from 2 to 30% B within 70 min, followed by an increase to 37% B within 5 min and further to 85% within 5 min and reequilibration. MS experiments were carried out on a TIMS-TOF pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) with a modified nano electrospray ion source (CaptiveSpray, Bruker Daltonics). A 1400 spray voltage with a capillary temperature of 180°C was typically employed for ionizing. MS spectra were acquired in the positive mode in the mass range from 100 to 1700m/z and 0.60 to 1.60 1/k0 window. In the experiments described here, the mass spectrometer was operated in PASEF DIA mode with exclusion of single charged peptides. The DIA acquisition scheme consisted of 16 variable windows ranging from 400 to 1200 m/z.

Protein identification

Database searching and LFQ quantification (using XIC) was performed using DIA-NN (version 1.6.0). An updated UniProt Homo sapiens database was used for library-free search / library generation. For RT prediction and extraction mass accuracy, we used the default parameter 0.0, which means DIA-NN performed automatic mass and RT correction. Top six fragments (ranked by their library intensities) were used for peptide identification and quantification. The FDR was set to 1% at the peptide precursor level. The variable modifications allowed were as follows: Nterm-acetylation and Carbamylation (RK). In addition, C-Propionoamide was set as fix modification. ‘Trypsin/P’ was selected. Data were filtering according to a FDR of 1%. Cross-run normalization was performed using RT-dependent.

Identification of differentially expressed proteins

To quantify the relative levels of protein abundance between different groups, datas from DIA-NN were then analyzed using DEP package from R. Briefly, proteins that are identified in 2 out of 3 replicates of at least one condition were filtered, data were normalized using a variance stabilizing transformation, missing data were imputed using random draws from a manually defined left-shifted Gaussian distribution and differential enrichment analysis was based on linear models and empherical Bayes statistic. A 1.5-fold increase in relative abundance and a 0.05 FDR were used to determine enriched proteins.

Enrichment analysis

Enrichment in pathways was performed using Reactome pathway with ClueGo App from Cytoscape software. Network specificity was set to medium; GO tree interval was set between 2 and 4. Cluster were performed using a selection set to 3 min genes. Enrichments were performed using a Bonferroni step dowm method.

Proteomic repository data available at iProX https://www.iprox.cn/ with the official number IPX0007516001.

Results

MnTBAP targets bioenergetics and the redox system at the proteome level in GBM cells

First, we investigated the effects of MnTBAP in an in vitro 2D culture study using MnTBAP at 100 μM for 6 days in the U251 and U87 GBM cell lines (Figure 1(A)). The cell number decreased in U251 (Figure 1(B)). We also show that MnTBAP decreased the cell number in two other GBM cell lines at 50 and 100 μM for 6 days, namely the human cell line LN18 and the mouse cell line GL261 (Fig. S1).

MnTBAP affects mitochondria and the redox system at the proteome level in GBM cells. (A) Experimental procedure for the in vitro 2D culture of two GBM cell models. (B) Cell number expressed as percentage relative to respective control (%), N = 5 per condition, error bars indicate SD, statistical test: One-way ANOVA, p values < 0.05. (C) Proteome clustering heatmap in U251 with Reactome Pathway enrichment analysis for cluster 1, pathways with significance p < 0.05, including with listed key mitochondrial proteins. (D) Proteome clustering heatmap in U87 with Reactome Pathway enrichment analysis for U87 cluster 2, pathways with significance p < 0.05, including with listed key mitochondrial proteins. N = 5 per condition for the proteome analysis.

Furthermore, we used a proteome approach with nano-scale liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (nLC-ESI-MS/MS), which detected 7,085 significantly differently expressed proteins out of 8,173 proteins from all GBM cell conditions. The difference in cell number was associated with a global proteome difference validated by heatmap visualization comparing both U251 and U87 GBM cells (Fig. S2A). At the basal level, the proteome of U251 displayed differences in cancer-associated, bioenergetics, and redox proteins when compared to that of U87 (Fig. S2B; Supplementary Table 1). Among the top 10 most abundant proteins in U251, we identified the most abundant as follows: glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which is a promising therapeutic target in GBM [23]; creatine kinase B-type (CKB) [24]; anterior gradient protein 2 (AG-2) [25]; tubulin beta-2B chain (TUBB2B) [26]; and secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1) [27]. In U87, the most abundant protein was macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), which is an immunoregulatory tautomerase involved in neuroinflammation, cancer (including GBM), vascular diseases, and Alzheimer’s disease [28]. We also identified proteins known to be involved in cancer therapy resistance when accumulated, namely lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [29], PDZ and LIM domain protein 1 (CLP-36) [30], carbonic anhydrase 12 (CA12) [31], proteolipid protein (PLP2) [32], dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) [33], and SLC43A3 [34]. Apart from the two most abundant proteins, mitochondrial proteins were identified, such as OXPHOS ATP synthase-coupling factor 6 (ATP5PF) [35], sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR; mainly located at mitochondria and involved in both bioenergetics and RSS H2S metabolism [36]), ATP-binding cassette sub-family member 6 (ABCB6; a heme and porphyrin transporter implicated in drug response and ROS generation [37]), adenine nucleotide translocase 3 (ANT3; involved in mitochondrial permeability transition, apoptosis, and mitochondrial ADP-ATP export [38]), D-β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (BDH; involved in glucose metabolism, ketone bodies and tumor growth [39]), SOD2, and glycolytic hexokinases [40] (Fig. S2B; Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, comparing the control of both GBM cells using reactome pathway enrichment analysis [41], among the significant pathways, U251 displayed upregulated pathways involved in redox anion homeostasis, as well as carbon and energy sources pathways, while U87 displayed upregulated pathways in bioenergetic metabolite pyruvate and acetyl-CoA [42], glucose homeostasis, redox, and inflammation with prostaglandin [43] (Fig. S2C).

Then, we checked whether MnTBAP exacerbated bioenergetics and redox differences between U251 and U87 at the proteome level. We performed a clustering heatmap focused on the effects of MnTBAP on each GBM cell. For U251, we identified four clusters, including cluster 1 (upregulated by MnTBAP) and cluster 4 (downregulated by MnTBAP) (Figure 1(C); Supplementary Table 2). Focusing on cluster 1 using the reactome pathway enrichment analysis, from 937 proteins, cellular response to chemical stress and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA)-mitochondria pathway were identified among the major upregulated pathways (Figure 1(C)). This last pathway, based on 69 proteins, included proteins involved in the TCA cycle, namely isocitrate dehydrogenase 3 (IDH3) [44], malate dehydrogenase (MDHM) [45] and succinate-CoA ligase subunit beta (SUCB2) [46], and many oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complex subunits. Focusing on cluster 4, from 1,181 proteins, most of the identified pathways were related to RHO GTPase (dysregulated in cancer [47]) and RNA metabolism (Fig. S2D). Then, for U87, we also identified four clusters (Figure 1(D); Supplementary Table 3). Cluster 1, which was upregulated by MnTBAP, was mainly involved in RHO GTPase and RNA metabolism (Fig. S2E). This cluster was similar to cluster 4 from U251 (Fig. S2D). Interestingly, in U87, cluster 2, which was unaffected by MnTBAP, was listed as the most important set of proteins involved in the tumor protein P53 pathway and bioenergetic metabolism, with 368 mitochondrial proteins (Figure 1(D)). The U87 cluster 2 shared cluster 1 mitochondrial proteins involved in the TCA cycle and OXPHOS complexes that were already upregulated in U87 with U251, while MnTBAP upregulated these proteins in U251.

Overall, the proteomic study suggests that the U251 and U87 GBM cell lines were different in bioenergetics and redox metabolism and that MnTBAP affected the global proteome differently in both GBM cells. Focusing on the MnTBAP-induced decrease in cell number in U251, we identified SQOR as a potentially good candidate in this mechanism since SQOR, which is involved in RSS metabolism, bioenergetics, and cancer [48], was more abundant in U87 when compared with U251, and targeted by MnTBAP at the proteome level.

MnTBAP represses SQOR protein expression level in GBM cells

Next, we aimed to validate the potential role of SQOR in the MnTBAP-induced decrease in cell number in U251. By western blotting, we confirmed a very low SQOR protein expression level in U251 and a higher protein expression level in U87 (Figure 2(A–B)). MnTBAP decreased SQOR levels in both GBM cells. The link between the MnTBAP effect on GBM cell survival and SQOR expression was reinforced by the results showing that, for 6 days, SQOR overexpression in U251 blocked the effect of MnTBAP on the cell number (Figure 2(C–E)) and that the silencing of SQOR in U87 decreased the cell number (Figure 2(F–H)).

Focusing on a potential bioenergetics effect induced by MnTBAP in GBM cells, we checked whether acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) – a major bioenergetic metabolite in cancer metabolism [42] – was altered. MnTBAP did not alter acetyl-CoA in both GBM cells (Fig. S3A). Among all the different sources of acetyl-CoA, the most important are the glycolytic pyruvate pathway (which also generates lactate through LDH activity) and the mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids (mitochondrial FAO) [42]. Pyruvate, lactate, and intracellular glycolytic LDH activity were unaffected by MnTBAP in both GBM cells (Fig. S3B–D). The major glycolysis marker GAPDH [49] was decreased by MnTBAP in both GBM cells (Figure 2(I–J)). Additionally, in both GBM cell lines, MnTBAP inhibited the mitochondrial FAO activity (Figure 2(K)). Alteration of the bioenergetic metabolism at this initial step of RSS metabolism contributes to modulating the bioenergetic metabolite glutamate, which is also associated with cell death apoptosis and necrosis through H2S [50]. MnTBAP increased glutamate in both GBM cells (Figure 2(L)).

Then, acetyl-CoA is used by citrate synthase (CS), the first and rate-limiting enzyme of the TCA cycle [42]. In both GBM cell lines, CS activity was unaffected by MnTBAP (Fig. S3E). The TCA cycle generates many metabolites, including NADH from NAD+, which are delivered to OXPHOS for mitochondrial ATP generation [42]. Again, in both GBM cell lines, NAD+, NADH, NAD+:NADH ratio, and total ATP were unaffected by MnTBAP (Fig. S3F–I). Finally, although GAPDH was downregulated by MnTBAP in both GBM cells, the glycolytic reserve remained stable upon MnTBAP treatment (Fig. S3J).

Overall, by combining the bioenergetic and proteomic data at a basal level, we confirmed that U87 and U251 displayed different bioenergetic metabolisms. Additionally, our results show that MnTBAP induced a decrease in the abundance of glycolytic GAPDH, the mitochondrial FAO activity associated with an increase in glutamate, and in the abundance of SQOR in both GBM cells; however, SQOR remained higher in U87 when compared to U251 cells (Figure 2(M)).

MnTBAP targets the sulfido-redox system in GBM cells

Then, comparing U251 and U87, we focused on the redox system with different major reactive species, notable redox enzymes that generate or metabolize these reactive species, and major antioxidants that contribute to the dynamics of the RSI [8]. For the ROS metabolism, MnTBAP increased H2O2 in U87 (Figure 3(A,B)). For the RNS metabolism, MnTBAP did not alter RNS in both GBM cells (Fig. S4A–B). Finally, for the RSS metabolism, MnTBAP decreased H2S in U251 and decreased H2Sn in U87 (Figure 3(C,D)). Thus, MnTBAP targeted RSS in U251, and both ROS and RSS in U87, which together indicate that MnTBAP targets the sulfido(RSS)-redox(ROS) system in GBM cells.

The most abundant antioxidant thiol in animal cells is reduced glutathione (GSH), which is metabolized by SQOR to initiate the RSS metabolism [48,51]. MnTBAP specifically decreased GSH in both GBM cells (Figure 3(E)). MnTBAP also increased oxidized GSSG in both GBM cells (Figure 3(F)). The GSH:GSSG ratio was unaffected in both GBM cells (Fig. S4C). Additionally, MnTBAP did not affect the major mitochondrial antioxidant ascorbic acid (AA), which is coupled to the GSH system [52], although U87 contained more AA than U251 (Fig. S4D). Altogether, MnTBAP induced a GSH system response in both GBM cells that was not in accordance with the decrease in cell number in U251.

Then, given that MnTBAP targeted RSS in U251, as well as both ROS and RSS in U87, we focused on notable redox enzymes involved in both ROS and RSS dynamics, i.e. SOD, catalase, and GPX activities [8]. MnTBAP inhibited the SOD activity in both GBM cells (Figure 3(G)). Although catalase activity was unaffected by MnTBAP, U87 displayed reduced catalase activity when compared to U251 (Fig. S4E). Additionally, MnTBAP specifically inhibited GPX activity in U251 (Figure 3(H)). Finally, the dysregulation of the RSI affects downstream targets, including protein carbonylation with protein carbonyls as biomarkers [53]. Protein carbonyls were unaffected by MnTBAP in both GBM cells (Fig. S4F). Thus, combining the redox analyses with the decrease in cell number induced by MnTBAP in U251 cells, the determination of whether GBM cells survived or died under MnTBAP treatment did not appear clear; however, H2O2, H2S, H2Sn, and GPX activity levels might be interesting factors to consider (Figure 3(I)).

Next, regarding the types of cell death induced by MnTBAP, in U251, MnTBAP specifically inhibited caspase-dependent apoptosis according to caspase 3/7 activation as a biomarker and activated the uncontrolled necrosis pathway according to the extracellular LDH activity release in line with the observed decrease in cell number (Figure 4(A–B)). The ferroptosis pathway, which is a caspase-independent form of regulated necrosis with iron Fe2+ and RCS malondialdehyde (MDA) as biomarkers, was unaffected in both GBM cells, although MnTBAP increased the iron Fe2+ level in U87 (Figure 4(C–D)). Therefore, we checked whether the effect of MnTBAP on Fe2+ in U87 reflected a larger effect on the major divalent ions Ca2+, Cu2+, Mg2+, and Zn2+, which are involved in redox enzyme activities, certain cell death types, mitochondrial function, and cancer [54–56]. MnTBAP increased Ca2+ and Zn2+ levels in U251, and Cu2 in U87, while MnTBAP did not affect Mg2+ (Fig. S5A–D). This result suggests a larger effect of MnTBAP on major divalent ions, particularly iron, calcium, zinc, and copper in GBM cells, and that MnTBAP might kill U251 through a specific activation of uncontrolled necrosis (Figure 4(E)).

Finally, we challenged the effects of MnTBAP on cell number, SQOR expression level, the redox system, bioenergetics, apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis on both U251 and U87 GBM cells in the context of radiotherapy (RT) at 2 Gy – a dose that decreases U251 cell viability [57] (Fig. S6A). In both GBM cells, the cell number decreased at 6 days post-RT, and MnTBAP exacerbated the RT effect in U251 (Fig. S6B). Surprisingly, RT (2 Gy) induced SQOR accumulation in both GBM cells 6 days post-RT; however, this increase was counteracted by MnTBAP – fully in U251 and partially in U87 (Fig. S6C–D). Post-RT, MnTBAP recapitulated most of its effects observed in U251 without RT, including decreased SQOR expression level, H2S, GSH, SOD activity, caspase-dependent apoptosis, and cell number, with an increase in GSSG and uncontrolled necrosis (Fig. S6E–Q). This was not the case for GPX activity, which remained similar to the control (Fig. S6K). In U87, RT decreased GSH, increased GSSG, and decreased GPX activity at 6 days post-RT (Fig. S6E–Q). MnTBAP recapitulated most of its effects observed in U87 without RT (i.e. a decrease in H2Sn, GSH, and SOD activity, and an increase in GSSG and H2O2); however, this was not the case for SQOR expression level, GPX activity, and cell number (Fig. S6B, C–D, K).

MnTBAP targets the sulfido-redox system and bioenergetics, thereby decreasing viability in GBM tumoroid models

Next, we investigated the activity of MnTBAP on an in vitro 3D culture study from two different male patient-derived GBM tumoroid models (2019GBM01-TO and 2021GBM01) cultured at 21% of O2. Given that patient-derived GBM tumoroid models showed promising results starting 3 days post-treatment for different drug screenings [58], in our study, we compared the gradual concentration of MnTBAP from 50 to 200 μM for 3 days (Figure 5(A,B)). Due to the limited amount of samples, the study focused on GBM viability with the bioenergetic metabolite ATP and the sulfido-redox system.

First, GBM tumoroid viability was evaluated using the CellTiter-Glo 3D viability assay, which assesses the level of total ATP and thus also reflects the bioenergetic metabolism [59]. In both GBM tumoroid models, MnTBAP decreased the viability and targeted the bioenergetic metabolism at its highest concentration, as evidenced by the decrease in total ATP (Figure 5(C)). Then, in a concentration-dependent manner, MnTBAP specifically increased ROS O2.– and RNS ONOO– in the 2021GBM01-TO tumoroid model, increased H2O2 in both GBM tumoroid models, and specifically decreased RSS H2Sn and RNS NO in the 2019GBM01-TO tumoroid model (Figure 5(D–I)). Finally, SOD activity and the GSH system were unaffected by MnTBAP in both GBM tumoroid models (Figure 5(J–L)). Overall, our results indicate that MnTBAP targeted both the sulfido-redox system and RNS metabolism in GBM tumoroid models (Figure 5(M)). Additionally, MnTBAP affected both bioenergetics and GBM tumoroid viability.

MnTBAP represses SQOR and targets the sulfido-redox system in vivo, with sex differences

Then, we investigated the effect of MnTBAP on an in vivo orthotopic model of GBM based on the implantation of U251 GBM cells in the right striatum of the brains of 8-week-old male and female NMRI-nu immunodeficient mice. This study aimed to decipher the effect of MnTBAP on SQOR, bioenergetics, and the RSI, including the sulfido-redox system and the RNS metabolism in this in vivo model of GBM. Considering the rapid growth and aggressiveness of GBM, as well as the rapid decline in animal survival a few weeks post-implantation [60], the administration of MnTBAP started at a tumor volume of 1 mm3 for 2 weeks, which reached the endpoint limit of 40 mm3 (Figure 6(A)). We included a T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (T2w-MRI) approach to determine when the tumor volume reached 1 mm3 [61] (Fig. S7A). With an endpoint limit of a 40 mm3 tumor volume starting at day 34, MnTBAP did not significantly affect survival probability, body weight, or tumor volume (Fig. S7B–D). Notably, starting on day 34, which corresponded to approximately 2 weeks of treatment, there might be a trend toward a decrease in the tumor volume induced by MnTBAP; however, there was no significance in the pool or considering the sex of the animals (Fig. S7E). Thus, to determine the effects of MnTBAP at the molecular level, we compared the left hemisphere with the GBM-bearing right hemisphere of both male and female animals (N = 3 mice per group) after 2 weeks of treatment, considering both pool and sex-separated groups.

First, similarly to the in vitro results for GBM cells, MnTBAP downregulated SQOR protein expression levels in the GBM-bearing hemisphere (i.e. right hemisphere) in both male and female mice (Figure 6(B–C)). Notably, SQOR was more abundant in males than in females in both hemispheres. Then, concerning the ROS metabolism, MnTBAP specifically increased O2.– in the female GBM-bearing hemisphere and decreased H2O2 in the male GBM-bearing hemisphere (Figure 6(D–E)). In both hemispheres, H2O2 was more abundant in males than in females. Concerning the RNS metabolism, MnTBAP specifically decreased ONOO– in the female left hemisphere (Fig. S7F–G). Notably, ONOO– was more abundant in the male than in the female left hemisphere. Concerning the RSS metabolism, MnTBAP specifically increased H2S in the male left hemisphere and both H2S and H2Sn in the female GBM-bearing hemisphere (Figure 6(F–G)). In the left hemisphere, H2S was more abundant in females than in males. Thus, MnTBAP exhibited a sex-specific effect on the sulfido-redox system in the in vivo model of GBM, with overstimulation of both ROS O2.– and RSS metabolism in the female GBM-bearing hemisphere, and a decrease in ROS H2O2 in the male GBM-bearing hemisphere. Additionally, MnTBAP specifically inhibited total SOD activity in the female GBM-bearing hemisphere (Fig. S7H). Moreover, for the GSH system, in the left hemisphere, MnTBAP decreased GSH in males and increased GSH in females (Fig. S7I). Notably, in the left hemisphere, GSH was more abundant in males than in females, while GSH was more abundant in the GBM-bearing right hemisphere in both sexes when compared to the left hemisphere. MnTBAP also decreased GSSG in the female left hemisphere and increased GSSG in the male GBM-bearing hemisphere (Fig. S7J). In both hemispheres, GSSG was more abundant in females than in males. Consequently, the GSH:GSSG ratio was higher in males than in females in both hemispheres, as well as in the GBM-bearing hemisphere when compared to the left hemisphere in both sexes. MnTBAP increased this ratio in the female left hemisphere (Fig. S7K). Together, our results indicate that MnTBAP exhibited a sex-dependent effect on total SOD activity and the GSH system. We also confirmed the sex-dependent effect of MnTBAP on the redox system with the essential redox-active transition metal copper Cu2+ involved in redox reactions, redox enzyme activities, inflammation, and cancer [56]. In detail, in the left hemisphere, the Cu2+ level was higher in males than in females, and MnTBAP decreased Cu2+ in males and increased Cu2+ in females (Fig. S8A). In the GBM-bearing hemisphere, MnTBAP decreased Cu2+ in females, while no effect was observed in males.

Then, we showed that the sex-dependent effect of MnTBAP on the RSI (including the sulfido-redox system) was not accompanied by an effect on the major bioenergetic metabolite ATP in all conditions (Fig. S8B). Since bioenergetic metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer [4], we focused on two more analyses for glycolysis on the GBM-bearing hemisphere. In males, MnTBAP increased LDH activity and GAPDH protein levels, yet decreased LDH activity in females (Fig. S8C–E). Notably, females relied more on glycolysis than males in the GBM-bearing hemisphere. The results suggest that MnTBAP upregulated glycolysis to maintain ATP in males. In this context, MnTBAP also increased catalase activity in males, while MnTBAP decreased its activity in females, and MnTBAP decreased GPX activity in both males and females (Fig. S8F–G).

Finally, in the GBM-bearing hemisphere, specifically in females, MnTBAP decreased caspase 3/7 activation and activated ferroptosis with MDA accumulation in the absence of Fe2+ alteration (Figure 6(H–I); Fig. S8H). In the male left hemisphere, MnTBAP increased both caspase 3/7 activation and ferroptosis. Notably, caspase 3/7 activation was higher in both hemispheres in females than in males, and higher in the GBM-bearing hemisphere when compared to the left hemisphere in both sexes (Figure 6(H)). Also, in both hemispheres, the Fe2+ level was lower in females than in males, while MnTBAP increased Fe2+ in the female left hemisphere (Fig. S8H).

In conclusion, the in vivo study indicates that MnTBAP decreased SQOR protein expression level in a sex-independent manner in the GBM-bearing hemisphere (Figure 6(J); Fig. S9A–B). MnTBAP also decreased GPX activity. Additionally, in the GBM-bearing right hemisphere in females, MnTBAP exhibited strong, sex-dependent activity on the sulfido-redox system, with ROS O2.– and the RSS metabolism, both SOD and catalase inactivation, copper depletion, the inactivation of caspase-dependent apoptosis, and the activation of ferroptosis. In males, MnTBAP decreased ROS H2O2 and increased GSSG, Fe2+, and catalase activity, which are jointly associated with glycolytic activity to maintain stable ATP content. Last, both the presence of GBM and the sex of the animal influenced SQOR protein expression level, the sulfido-redox system, apoptosis, and ferroptosis, which were the identified targets of MnTBAP in our study (Figure 6(J); Fig. S9A–B).

Discussion

Comparison between the in vitro 2D culture of GBM cells, the 3D culture of GBM tumoroids, and in vivo GBM models revealed that MnTBAP is a promising redox molecule that represses SQOR and targets both the sulfido-redox system and bioenergetics associated with proteome reprogramming.

MnTBAP represses SQOR in GBM

The SQOR enzyme is mainly located between OXPHOS complex II and III and catalyzes the first step in H2S oxidation by using the most abundant antioxidant thiol (GSH) to generate glutathione persulfide (GSSH) [48]. Deficiency in or inhibition of SQOR decreases angiogenesis and neovascularization by limiting endothelial cell proliferation [62], induces mitochondrial dysfunction [63], and activates ferroptosis [64]. In our study, many mitochondrial proteins were identified at the proteome level in GBM cells, including SQOR. We show that MnTBAP downregulated SQOR expression in both in vitro and in vivo GBM models. We also show – for the first time – that SQOR abundance expression in GBM cells modulated cell survival. Indeed, after observing that SQOR is more abundant in U87 when compared to U251, we showed that SQOR silencing in U87 decreased GBM cell survival, and, conversely, SQOR overexpression in U251 protected GBM cells. MnTBAP is still able to limit the SQOR response, thereby inducing GBM cell death. These results underscore that a MnTBAP-induced decrease in SQOR expression and its final level of expression afterward might serve an important role in its level of effect on GBM cell survival. Additionally, as shown in the in vitro study, we show that MnTBAP can counteract the RT (2 Gy)-induced SQOR accumulation in both GBM cells. These results open new perspectives on the interest of MnTBAP and other strategies to reduce the abundance of SQOR in GBM cells, including after RT. In addition, beyond SQOR, our findings open new perspectives on investigating whether MnTBAP can also affect other enzymes involved in the RSS metabolism, including in cancer.

MnTBAP targets the sulfido-redox system in GBM

MnTBAP displays different properties, namely ROS H2O2 formation from O2.–, the reduction of H2O2 to H2O and O2 through catalase-like and glutathione peroxidase (GPX)-like activities, the scavenging of RNS ONOO–, and the generation of RSS polysulfides (H2Sn) from H2S through a possible sulfide oxidase activity [10,15,65,66]. In all the studies performed using different in vitro and in vivo GBM models herein, we showed that MnTBAP targeted the sulfido(RSS)-redox(ROS) system. As shown in the in vivo results in the U251-GBM model, sex influenced a switching of effects in the GBM-bearing hemisphere: namely H2O2 downregulation in males and RSS upregulation in females. Therefore, this highlights a possible association with the endocrine status. Moreover, the modulation of H2O2 metabolism in cancer enhances therapeutic efficacy [67], while the modulation of H2S metabolism is a promising anti-cancer therapy involving the regulation of glycolysis, mitochondrial function, O2 homeostasis, ferroptosis, and pH balance [10,68]. Into details, the specific increase in H2O2 in U87 cells compared with U251 cells, could be explained by the SOD mimetic property of MnTBAP or by an opposite effect in U87 compared with U251 cells on a redox enzyme involved in H2O2 generation or H2O2 degradation, yet to be determined as perspective. In addition, the increase in H2O2 in U87 cells was not associated with a significant decrease in cell number, which suggested either that H2O2 is not a major factor involved in the decrease in cell number in U251, or that the accumulation of H2O2 contributed to protect U87 cells. In vivo, the decrease in H2O2 induced by MnTBAP in the GBM-bearing hemisphere in male mice might be attributed to the increase in catalase activity. Then, for the RSS, MnTBAP decreased H2S in U251 cells, and increased H2S in the left non GBM-bearing hemipshere in male, and in the GBM-bearing hemisphere in female mice. In addition, MnTBAP decreased H2Sn in U87 cells, and increased H2Sn in GBM-bearing female mice. Altogether, our results underlined that comparing the effects of MnTBAP between in vitro models and in vivo models is challenging given that (1) the in vivo model also included the GBM microenvironment, and (2) the endocrine/sex status might be a key factor in the mechanisms of MnTBAP effects on GBM. Thus, depending of the models, MnTBAP could act from SOD mimetic agent to RSS regulator. Finally, as shown in the in vivo study, it is important to consider that MnTBAP also targeted the redox system in the non-bearing GBM hemisphere differently from the GBM-bearing hemisphere. Given that the effects of MnTBAP depend on the GBM models, our results invite future studies to be very careful in the interpretation of redox analyses in cancer, including endocrine/sex status.

MnTBAP also targets GPX, SOD, and catalase activities in GBM

Our results uncovered that MnTBAP specifically decreased redox GPX activity in U251 GBM cells and in the in vivo U251-GBM model in both male and female mice. Additionally, for other redox enzymes, MnTBAP decreased SOD activity in vitro in GBM cells and in vivo in the GBM-bearing hemisphere in female mice. MnTBAP also increased catalase activity in the GBM-bearing hemisphere in males and decreased it in females. The SOD inhibition by MnTBAP encourages further development of this finding since SOD inactivation is a promising target to kill cancer cells mediated by ROS generation, and possibly by regulating RSS metabolism [69]. The regulation of catalase contributes to the control of mitochondrial function through H2O2 [70], which is also metabolized by GPX and involved in redox signaling and ferroptosis [71]. Finally, the inhibition of GPX activity by MnTBAP in vivo in the GBM-bearing hemisphere is also promising since GPX inhibition mediates ferroptosis and increases cancer sensitivity to therapy, including chemotherapy [71]. Nevertheless, the increase in GPX activity induced by MnTBAP in U87 GBM cells treated with RT encourages further investigations of the relationship between MnTBAP and GPX in a very cautious manner.

MnTBAP induces a bioenergetic response in GBM

Associated with the redox system and as represented in a schema that summarizes the in vitro findings (Figure 7), MnTBAP also induced a bioenergetic response. However, when compared to U251, U87 contained more acetyl-CoA, pyruvate, NAD+, a higher NAD+:NADH ratio, similar amounts of lactate, LDH activity, mitochondrial FAO activity, and citrate synthase activity, and less NADH, GAPDH, glycolytic reserve, ATP, and glutamate. In GBM cells, MnTBAP decreased glycolysis, and many mitochondrial proteins were regulated by MnTBAP at the proteome level. In tumoroid GBM models, MnTBAP depleted ATP. Moreover, in the in vivo GBM model, in GBM-bearing hemisphere, still with stable total ATP levels, MnTBAP increased glycolysis in male and decreased glycolysis in female. Targeting glycolysis reverses cancer resistance to conventional therapies [72].

Schema that summarizes the effects of MnTBAP on U251 and U87 GBM cells.

Sulfido-redox and bioenergetics targeting by MnTBAP influences apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis in GBM

Additionally, MnTBAP is a regulator of apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis, as evidenced in vitro in U251 by the inhibition of apoptosis and the activation of uncontrolled necrosis, and in vivo in the GBM-bearing hemisphere in female mice via apoptosis inhibition and ferroptosis activation. Exceeding the resistance of cancer to apoptosis through the activation of ferroptosis is a promising anti-cancer therapy [71]. This finding opens new perspectives on improving our understanding of the regulation of apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis by MnTBAP, including in cancer.

MnTBAP and SQOR-targeted therapies in GBM and other cancers

Interestingly, through the knockout (KO) of SQOR in human male HCT116 colon cancer cell line, tumor xenograft in immunodeficient female mice, as well as the use of a SQOR inhibitor (FC9402), a recent study raised the interest in the development of SQOR-targeted therapies for colorectal cancer [73]. Into details, transcription of SQOR was high in clinical colorectal cancer samples. The KO of SQOR in HCT116 cells increased H2O2, H2S, catalase and total SOD activities, apoptosis and ferroptosis with lipid peroxidation, while it decreased total polysulfides content (thiosulfate, GSSH, supersulfides), aldolase A, total ATP and impaired mitochondrial respiration [73]. In addition, SQOR knockdown led to a metabolic reprogramming of glycolysis at the fructose-1,6-bisphosphate degradation step, catalyzed by aldolase A (ALDOA).

Thus, our results showing that SQOR expression contributed to GBM cell growth, are in line with this recent published study on colorectal cancer. We also further introduced MnTBAP as an interesting agent to repress SQOR. In details, in line with the colorectal study on SQOR-KO HCT116 cells [73], an increase of H2O2 and a decrease of H2Sn or H2S is observed in U87, U251 cells and GBM tumoroid models treated by MnTBAP. However, different results are observed on GSSG and SOD activity with a decrease (U251, U87) or no effect (GBM tumoroid) on SOD activity and an increase (U251, U87) or no effect (GBM tumoroid) on GSSG content. Moreover, MnTBAP did not alter total ATP levels in in vitro GBM models, except for GBM tumoroid models, but decreased glycolysis in GBM cells. Therefore, in line with the colorectal study, we also observed in GBM cells treated with MnTBAP a metabolic reprogramming. In addition, in our proteomic study, ALDOA was identified by the clustering heatmap analysis for U251 cells, in cluster 2, which corresponded to significantly increased proteins due to MnTBAP (Table S2). This is a difference between our study and the colorectal cancer study, linking SQOR to ALDOA in different types of cancers.

Also, in line with the colorectal study for apoptosis and ferroptosis, MnTBAP is a regulator of apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis depending on the GBM models. As HCT116, U87 and U251 cells are all derived from male patients and regarding the results obtained in vivo showing sex differences, such comparison encourages to investigate in the future the effect of MnTBAP or other SQOR repressors on the sulfido-redox system, bioenergetics, and cell death in different cancer types with including sex difference. This perspective is supported by another study, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, showing that SQOR promoted the resistance of cancer cells to ferroptosis and, the progression of this cancer in hypoxic environment [74].

MnTBAP and transition metal ions

Our study showed promising results regarding the effects of MnTBAP on transition metal ions, which illustrates the interplay between the redox system and bioenergetics. In GBM cells, MnTBAP increased Ca2+ and Zn2+ in U251, and increased both Cu2+ and Fe2+ in U87. Magnesium was unaffected by MnTBAP. Then, at the in vivo level, in the left hemisphere, MnTBAP decreased Cu2+ in males and increased both Cu2+ and Fe2+ in females. In the GBM-bearing hemisphere, MnTBAP increased Fe2+ in males and decreased Cu2+ in females. This complex effect of MnTBAP on major divalent ions might suggest a deeper effect on metallostasis, which considers all cellular processes from their sensing and trafficking to the metalloproteome [75–78]. Transition metal ions, including calcium, zinc, copper, iron and magnesium are essential cofactors of many metalloproteins, including redox enzymes such as SOD, bioenergetic proteins involved in OXPHOS and mitochondrial iron-sulfur clusters, and cell death [55,76,77]. Calcium plays important roles in the cancer progression, in the switch between necrosis, apoptosis and ferroptosis, in the mitochondrial function, in zinc dynamics, and in both the central nervous system and the cardiovascular system [55,77]. Zinc contributes to shape intracellular calcium dynamics, and is essential for the immune system [55,77]. Copper is also essential for the immune system, redox activities, bioenergetics and the cancer progression [55,56,77]. Iron is essential for many processes, including ferroptosis, bioenergetics with mitochondrial iron-sulfur clusters, and the cancer progression [55,76,77,79]. Magnesium contributes to shape calcium dynamics, and plays roles in both the immune system, and bioenergetics [55,77]. Last, metalloporphyrins can exhibit either a central iron for Fe porphyrins, or a central manganese for Mn porphyrins such as MnTBAP [80]. Altogether, our findings open new perspectives on investigating (1) the suggested link between MnTBAP and metallostasis, including in cancer, and (2) whether other metalloporphyrins, Mn and Fe porphyrins, exhibit the same effects of MnTBAP on transition metal ions, the redox system, bioenergetics, and SQOR, including in cancer.

Effect of sex differences on MnTBAP in GBM

Compared to male mice, female mice exhibit a more powerful antioxidant defense, which includes higher redox signaling and response linked to sex hormones, sex chromosomes, mitochondrial inheritance, and greater GPX and SOD expression, which ultimately confer resistance to inflammation and chronic diseases [81]. In cancer, sex differences also imply epigenetics with more DNA methylation in females. In male bioenergetics, sex differences result in more pyruvate and glucose uptake, more glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, and less oxidative balance with high mitochondrial respiratory capacity and ROS level, while females exhibit high mitochondrial respiration and low ROS levels [81]. Females also exhibit significant differences in angiogenesis and immune system response [81]. Sex differences in redox, bioenergetics, angiogenesis, immunity, and inflammation are crucial for cancer growth, progression, and therapy [82]. In the present study, the in vitro study was based on male (U251, U87, and LN18) and female (GL261) GBM cells, GBM tumoroids derived from male patients, and on an in vivo GBM model using male GBM U251 cells implanted in both male and female mouse brains. In the in vivo study, sex-based differences existed in the non-bearing GBM hemisphere for the sulfido-redox system, RNS peroxynitrite, SQOR, the glutathione system, copper, iron, and apoptosis, and in the GBM-bearing hemisphere for ROS H2O2, SQOR, GSSG, iron and apoptosis (Fig. S9A–B). Then, when including MnTBAP, the in vivo results suggest that the effects of MnTBAP on bioenergetics, redox metabolism, and cell death types differ between the male and female host microenvironments, including the alteration of glycolysis and OXPHOS complexes, as well as the downregulation of ROS (H2O2), in males, and the upregulation of both ROS (O2.–) and RSS (H2S, H2Sn), inhibition of apoptosis and activation of ferroptosis pathways in females.

Therefore, future cancer research should include both male and female animals. Finally, given that studying the effect of MnTBAP on in vivo parameters (including the tumor volume) would require a larger cohort of animals, this will be necessary to achieve useful results.

Notably, the in vivo GBM model used in the present study was based on athymic nude mice with a deficiency in peripheral T lymphocytes [83]. These mice still introduced a major player in cancer metabolism and therapy, namely the tumor microenvironment (TME). TME hosts the extracellular matrix and multiple noncancerous cell types, which contribute to cancer progression and therapy efficiency through tumor stem cell homeostasis, heterogeneous immune microenvironment, oxidative distress, and bioenergetics [84]. The GBM microenvironment includes blood vessels, axonal tracts, astrocytes, extracellular matrix, and an immune microenvironment with monocyte-derived tumor macrophages, tumor microglia, tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells, and regulatory T cells, which are lacking in the nude mouse model [84].

In conclusion, this study on the effects of MnTBAP on different types of GBM models uncovered that this agent targets the sulfido(RSS)-redox(ROS) system and represses SQOR, thereby interfering with cancer bioenergetics. These results also reveal that the abundance of SQOR is essential to help protect or kill GBM cells. Linked to these redox-bioenergetics effects, MnTBAP contributes to regulating the apoptosis, uncontrolled necrosis, and ferroptosis pathways. These findings represent the next step in improving our understanding of redox biology in the context of GBM and highlight the promising redox agent MnTBAP.
