Decoding personality functioning: the impact of DSM-5 pathological traits mediated by emotion dysregulation and metacognition
Authors: Amin Nazari, Nina Mafakheri, Roonak Shafiei, Farideh Nargesi, Carla Sharp, Saeid Komasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersonality disorders (PDs) have long been studied in adulthood, with relatively limited attention to their developmental course during adolescence. Recent research, however, underscores the importance of early identification and intervention in youth, as personality pathology can be reliably detected in this period and is associated with significant functional impairments. Grounded in the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD), the present study explores potential intrapsychic mechanisms linking maladaptive trait domains to global personality functioning in adolescents. Drawing on developmental theories that emphasize the role of self-regulatory and reflective capacities in personality development, we examined whether emotion dysregulation and metacognitive difficulties help explain the relationship between pathological traits and functioning.MethodsA sample of 470 Iranian adolescents aged 14–17 years completed validated measures of personality traits, emotion regulation, metacognition, and personality functioning. Data were analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficient and mediation analysis.ResultsAll trait domains except antagonism were significantly associated with poorer functioning. Mediation analyses suggested that both emotion dysregulation and metacognition partially accounted for these associations, particularly for negative affectivity, detachment, disinhibition, and psychoticism.ConclusionsThese findings support developmental models of personality by highlighting the interplay between emotion regulation and metacognition in shaping adolescent personality functioning. While the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-report measures limit causal interpretations, the study emphasizes the importance of incorporating emotion regulation and metacognitive processes into early assessments and interventions. Future research should employ longitudinal and multi-method approaches to further clarify the developmental pathways underlying personality pathology in adolescence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40479-025-00315-1.

Introduction

Personality disorders (PDs) have traditionally been considered conditions that emerge and stabilize in adulthood [1, 2]. Historically perceived as resistant to change and even incurable [3, 4], this view contributed to longstanding diagnostic hesitancy in adolescents due to stigma and concerns over developmental instability [5, 6]. However, recent empirical evidence suggests that personality pathology can be reliably identified in adolescence and displays relative stability comparable to that observed in adults [7–10]. Untreated PDs during this stage are associated with significant psychological, social, and academic impairments, including heightened risk for psychopathology, suicidality, substance use, and reduced quality of life [11–13], underscoring the urgency of improved early identification frameworks and intervention strategies [14, 15].

The DSM-5’s Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) [16] offers a dimensional framework for understanding PDs that emphasizes impairments in personality functioning (Criterion A) and pathological personality traits (Criterion B). While these components are conceptually distinct, they are jointly essential for characterizing personality pathology. Criterion A, in particular, is seen as a robust marker of severity and therapeutic need [17]. Although research supports the validity and utility of both criteria [18], emerging research has raised questions about the consistency and interrelation between Criterion A and B, particularly in adolescent samples [19], highlighting the need for further empirical examination.

In addition to Criterion A, which captures impairments in self and interpersonal functioning, Criterion B of the AMPD provides a complementary dimensional framework by assessing maladaptive personality traits across five broad domains. While A and B are conceptually distinct, contemporary scholarship highlights their close interdependence. Recent theoretical debates have emphasized that pathological traits (Criterion B) not only predispose individuals to difficulties in functioning but also interact dynamically with global personality dysfunction (Criterion A) in shaping problems in living [20, 21]. For instance, traits such as negative affectivity or disinhibition may increase vulnerability to impairments in identity or interpersonal functioning, while dysfunction in personality functioning can, in turn, exacerbate maladaptive trait expression [20, 21]. Incorporating these perspectives underscores the importance of examining the mechanisms through which dispositional traits exert their impact on personality functioning. By focusing on intrapsychic processes such as emotion regulation and metacognition, the present study aims to illuminate one pathway through which Criterion B contributes to Criterion A, thereby situating our investigation within ongoing theoretical debates and advancing dimensional models of personality pathology in adolescence.

In efforts to understand the psychological mechanisms through which maladaptive traits influence personality functioning, emotion regulation has gained special attention. Emotion dysregulation is widely recognized as a central transdiagnostic factor implicated in PD development, particularly during adolescence—a period marked by ongoing affective maturation [22–24]. It is associated with interpersonal dysfunction, symptom severity, and poor treatment response, and is considered a critical target for intervention [25, 26]. Similarly, deficits in metacognition—the capacity to reflect upon and understand mental states—have been linked to identity confusion, poor self-other understanding, and interpersonal conflict in individuals with personality dysfunction [27, 28].

Although both constructs have been individually linked to maladaptive traits and impaired functioning, little is known about their combined, mediating roles in adolescence. Prior empirical research [29] has shown that emotion dysregulation mediates the link between mentalizing deficits (a form of metacognition) and borderline traits, offering indirect support for such pathways. However, no study to date has directly tested whether both emotion dysregulation and metacognitive difficulties mediate the association between DSM-5 trait domains and global personality functioning in adolescents.

While our study is cross-sectional, developmental models of personality [30] and personality pathology [31, 32] suggest that dispositional traits constitute the most basic layer of personality, shaping individuals’ emotional and cognitive styles from early adolescence. These traits interact with environmental demands to influence goals, values, and coping strategies (i.e., characteristic adaptations), which in turn shape the development of identity and interpersonal functioning. Crucially, intrapsychic capacities such as emotion regulation and metacognition are theorized to serve as mechanisms that translate dispositional traits into functional outcomes by either mitigating or exacerbating maladaptive tendencies. In line with this view, we conceptualize maladaptive traits as relatively stable predispositions that increase the likelihood of difficulties in self- and emotion regulation, as well as in reflective functioning, which then undermine global personality functioning. Alternative pathways are plausible—for instance, functional impairment itself may worsen emotion regulation, or emotion dysregulation may precede metacognitive deficits. However, based on existing developmental theory and prior empirical evidence linking traits to deficits in regulatory and reflective capacities [22–32], we hypothesize that emotion dysregulation and metacognition are more likely to operate as mediating processes through which pathological traits exert their impact on personality functioning. Our study therefore tests this theoretically grounded model as a first empirical step.

Study objectives

The present study aims to examine two specific research questions. First, it investigates whether emotion dysregulation mediates the relationship between maladaptive personality traits—specifically, the five AMPD trait domains of negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism—and global personality functioning. Second, it evaluates whether metacognitive deficits serve as a mediating mechanism in the same association. By exploring these intrapsychic pathways in an adolescent sample, the study seeks to clarify the psychological processes linking pathological traits to personality dysfunction and to inform the refinement of dimensional models for early identification and targeted intervention.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This cross-sectional study recruited adolescents aged 14–17 years from public high schools in northwestern Iran (Kermanshah and Sanandaj). A total of 610 students were invited, and 470 adolescents (73% female; mean age = 16.2 years, SD = 0.8) voluntarily agreed to participate. An active informed consent was obtained from all students. In addition, passive consent was obtained from parents, whereby parents were informed about the study via school communication channels (letters and school websites) and given the option to decline their child’s participation.

The participants were selected using a stratified cluster sampling method, ensuring representation across school types and educational levels. Inclusion criteria were: (a) age between 14 and 17 years, (b) regular school attendance, and (c) ability to understand the questionnaire language. Exclusion criteria included: (a) a self-reported diagnosis of a developmental (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, intellectual disability) or neurological disorder (e.g., epilepsy, traumatic brain injury), applied to avoid potential confounds associated with these conditions that may independently affect cognitive functioning and emotion regulation; and (b) incomplete response to more than 10% of the questionnaire items. However, because the online survey required responses to all items, no cases of missing data occurred in practice.

Data collection took place during school hours in classrooms, following formal approval from school administrators. Questionnaires were administered digitally via a secure online platform (Google Forms), accessible through participants’ personal smartphones. If a student did not have a device, a shared tablet provided by the school was used. Each participant completed the assessment independently. Responses were automatically recorded through the online system, minimizing researcher influence. Three graduate students in clinical psychology were present to provide clarification, ensure compliance with ethical standards, and assist with technical issues. No monetary or material incentives were offered. The study protocol received ethical approval from the local ethics committee (IR.IAU.SDJ.REC.1403.074).

Measures

The Personality Inventory for DSM-5 Short Form (PID-5-SF): The Personality Inventory for DSM-5 Short Form (PID-5-SF) is a 100-item abbreviated version of the original PID-5 that comprises of five higher-order domains (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism) and 25 lower-order trait facets [33]. Its internal structure replicated the structure of the original PID-5 in community and clinical samples [34–36]. Adolescents rated to what extent each item described themselves on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (very false or often false) to 3 (very true or often true). A recent review [37] has addressed the reliability (α between 0.74 and 0.86) and convergent validity of the Persian version of this tool in the Iranian population. This short form has been applied and structurally validated in an adolescent community sample (N = 1,940), supporting its use in youth populations [38]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the five trait domains were as follows: negative affectivity (0.79), detachment (0.68), antagonism (0.55), disinhibition (0.78), and psychoticism (0.57).

The Level of Personality Functioning Scale-Self-Report (LPFS-SR): The LPFS-SR is an 80-question survey that evaluates disruptions in both self and interpersonal functioning on a global severity scale, which aligns with Criterion A of the AMPD in the DSM-5 Section results [39]. This questionnaire is divided into four separate personality components: identity (21 questions) and self-direction (16 questions) for self-functioning, empathy (23 questions) and intimacy (20 questions) for interpersonal functioning. Participants use a four-point response scale (ranging from “Totally False, not at all True” to “Very True”) when answering. The item scores are weighted based on an algorithm provided by Morey (2017), with weights ranging from −0.5 to + 3.5. Morey (2017) confirmed that there is support for a single-factor structure of personality functioning [39]. Hemmati et al. [40] found that the Persian translation of LPFS-SR shows high internal consistency and can differentiate between nonclinical and clinical samples. The self-report LPFS-SR has shown strong construct validity in young adult and late adolescent samples, such as in Iranian studies where it showed robust correlations with the STIPO-R [41, 42]. The alpha coefficient of the full scale was 0.94 for our sample.

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS): The scale is a self-report measure developed to assess difficulties in emotion regulation. It has 36 items that are rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Eleven items (1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 10, 17, 20, 22, 24, 34) are rated inversely. DERS items are recoded so that higher scores in every case indicate greater difficulties in emotion regulation (i.e., greater emotion dysregulation). The scale is composed of 6 factors: Non-acceptance of emotional responses (Non-Acceptance); difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior (Goal); impulse control difficulties (Impulse); lack of emotional awareness (Awareness); limited access to emotion regulation strategies (Strategy); lack of emotional clarity (Clarity). The alpha coefficient for the DERS is 0.93 whereas test-retest reliability is 0.87, ranging from 0.69 to 0.89 for all subscales [43]. The research supports the validity and reliability of the Persian version of this questionnaire [44, 45]. The adolescent version DERS‑16 demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and discriminated between clinical and community adolescents [46]. The alpha coefficient of the full scale was 0.92 in our study.

Metacognition Questionnaire (MCQ-30): This questionnaire consists of 30 items designed to assess individuals’ metacognitive beliefs. It evaluates five subscales of metacognitive beliefs: Cognitive confidence (items 1, 6, 11, 16, 21, and 26), positive beliefs about worry (items 2, 7, 12, 17, 22, and 27), cognitive self-consciousness (items 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, and 28), uncontrollability and danger (items 4, 9, 14, 19, 24, and 29), and the need to control thoughts (items 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30). Each item is scored on a scale from 1 to 4 (strongly agree, agree, no comment, and strongly disagree). The MCQ-30 demonstrates good internal consistency, convergent validity, and acceptable test-retest reliability [47]. Alpha coefficient for the total scale of the Persian version was 0.92, ranging from 0.73 to 0.90 to the subscales [48]. The MCQ‑30 has demonstrated suitability and validation among adolescents, showing reliability in measuring metacognitive beliefs [49]. The alpha coefficient of the full scale was 0.89 for our sample.

Analytic plan

We did not encounter any missing data as answers to all the questions in the online form were required. Although incomplete responses were theoretically an exclusion criterion, the forced-response design of the online survey ensured that this did not occur. We reported descriptive data for all included variables. Before running the main analyses, we evaluated the assumptions of parametric statistics such as data normality (i.e., skewness and kurtosis between − 1 and + 1). Pearson coefficients were used to calculate the correlation between associations between all continuous variables. To ensure the accuracy of the mediation analysis models, we also reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all predictors, mediators, and outcome.

Since main purpose was to examine the significance of mediators (i.e., emotion dysregulation and metacognition) in the relationship between maladaptive personality domains and global personality functioning, we focused on direct effects, indirect effects, and total effects for each predictor. Therefore, we reported the estimates (i.e., beta standardized coefficients) and standard errors for all predictors. We performed two independent mediation analysis models, in the first one, emotion dysregulation was mediator, and in the second one, the total score of metacognition was mediator. In both mediation analysis models, five domain scales of maladaptive personality (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism) were predictors, while global personality functioning was outcome. Finally, we conducted an additional analysis to assess direct-, indirect-, and total-effects models for associations between five maladaptive domain scales and global functioning mediated by both emotion dysregulation and metacognition, spontaneously. On all models we reported R2 for both the outcome and mediators. We also reported the estimates for the path coefficients. The estimates were significant if p-value was ≤ 0.05. The free version of JASP 0.19.0.0 software and GraphPad PRISM 10.2.3 software were used to perform all the analyses.

Discussion

Our study investigated the relationship between maladaptive personality traits, as defined by the American Psychiatric Association’s DSM-5, and personality functioning in a sample of 470 Iranian adolescents aged 14 to 17. We specifically examined the mediating roles of emotion dysregulation and metacognition. Overall, the results support our hypothesis that both intrapsychic capacities act as mechanisms through which pathological traits exert their influence on personality functioning. This study provides novel evidence on these processes in adolescence, a developmental stage in which personality pathology begins to consolidate and early intervention may be particularly impactful.

Consistent with previous research, we found that all five pathological trait domains—negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism—were significantly related to difficulties in both emotion regulation and metacognitive functioning. This aligns with prior findings demonstrating that maladaptive traits are closely intertwined with deficits in affective and reflective capacities. For example, antagonism and detachment have been linked to impulse control problems and reactivity to interpersonal stressors [50], while specific styles of emotion regulation, such as undercontrol and overcontrol, predict various maladaptive personality dimensions [51]. Other studies have shown that impulsivity, lack of perseverance, and suspiciousness are associated with both emotional dysregulation and symptom severity in clinical disorders such as gambling [52]. Similarly, Katar et al. [53] found that emotion regulation difficulties partially mediated the association between antagonism and personality functioning impairments in depressed and anxious patients. Longitudinal findings also suggest that pathological traits and emotion regulation difficulties co-develop in adolescence, jointly shaping impairment in functioning [23]. Metacognitive impairments have likewise been established as central features of personality pathology, with deficits in mastery undermining the ability to manage distress and resolve interpersonal conflicts [54]. Taken together, our findings and prior work reinforce the idea that maladaptive traits do not operate in isolation but exert their pathological influence largely through disruptions in regulation and reflection.

When considering the mediating mechanisms separately, our findings indicate that emotion dysregulation plays a particularly robust role in linking pathological traits to personality dysfunction. Adolescents with higher levels of negative affectivity, for instance, tend to experience more frequent and intense negative emotions, which makes it harder to manage distress and increases reliance on maladaptive coping strategies [43]. Detachment, defined by interpersonal withdrawal and emotional avoidance, is also associated with dysregulation, as adolescents high on this trait often lack social support and accumulate negative affect [50, 55]. Although antagonism is theoretically characterized by hostility and aggression, which are often linked to difficulties in regulating anger and frustration [56, 57], in our analyses no significant direct, indirect, or total effects were observed through either emotion dysregulation or metacognition. This lack of mediation may be explained by developmental factors, as antagonistic tendencies in early to mid-adolescence might be more situational and less consolidated as stable traits. Furthermore, the relatively low internal consistency for this domain in our sample may have reduced statistical power to detect effects. Previous adolescent studies have similarly reported weaker reliability and limited differentiation of antagonism in this age group [58]. Similarly, disinhibition reflects poor impulse control and difficulty delaying gratification, contributing to dysregulated responses under stress [59]. Finally, psychoticism—marked by unusual beliefs and behaviors—is associated with instability in affect and difficulty distinguishing reality from fantasy, further exacerbating emotional dysregulation [60–62]. These findings are consistent with prior work showing that difficulties in emotion regulation independently predict personality disorder symptoms across diagnoses [63–66].

Metacognition also emerged as a significant mediator in the relationship between pathological traits and personality functioning. Adolescents scoring high on these traits tended to hold maladaptive beliefs such as overgeneralization, catastrophizing, or negative self-appraisals, which may reinforce emotional difficulties and impair reflective thinking [28]. Negative affectivity, for instance, is strongly tied to rumination and negative self-talk [67, 68], while detachment is linked to difficulties in recognizing one’s thoughts and feelings. Antagonism, by contrast, is associated with distorted views of others, limiting accurate self-reflection and empathy [69]. Disinhibition involves reduced awareness of emotional responses and impaired self-monitoring [70], whereas psychoticism is linked to confusion between reality and fantasy, producing distortions in thought and reflection [71]. These results are consistent with prior research suggesting that metacognitive deficits contribute to identity confusion, interpersonal dysfunction, and overall impairment [27, 54]. Importantly, our findings also resonate with theoretical work indicating that metacognitive capacities serve as mechanisms of therapeutic change in personality pathology [72, 73].

Beyond their individual effects, our findings suggest that emotion dysregulation and metacognition are closely interconnected processes that jointly mediate the influence of traits on personality functioning. Adolescents who struggle to regulate their emotions often find it difficult to reflect accurately on their mental states, which in turn exacerbates distress and maladaptive coping [74, 75]. For example, heightened negative affectivity may increase sensitivity to stressors, leading to avoidance or suppression as coping strategies; these responses limit self-reflection and further reinforce maladaptive beliefs. Such patterns illustrate the intertwined nature of emotional and cognitive vulnerabilities and highlight their combined impact on personality functioning [76]. These findings indicate that, although emotion dysregulation and metacognition show conceptual and empirical overlap, each mediator also accounted for unique variance in personality functioning when modeled simultaneously. This underscores the importance of considering both domains as complementary yet distinct mechanisms. From a clinical perspective, it highlights the need for interventions that target both affective regulation and metacognitive capacities, as strengthening one domain alone may not be sufficient to buffer against personality dysfunction in adolescence.

From a developmental perspective, our results support models that view intrapsychic capacities such as emotion regulation and metacognition as key mechanisms through which traits shape the emerging sense of self and interpersonal functioning in adolescence [30–32]. Maladaptive traits may set the stage for regulatory and reflective difficulties, which, if left unaddressed, undermine the development of coherent identity and adaptive interpersonal functioning. Conversely, strengthening these capacities may buffer against long-term dysfunction. Clinically, this highlights the importance of incorporating both affect-regulation and metacognitive interventions into adolescent treatments. By addressing these mechanisms, clinicians can more effectively target the pathways through which maladaptive traits compromise personality functioning.

It is important to note that alternative pathways are also plausible. For instance, impaired functioning itself may exacerbate emotion regulation difficulties, which in turn could undermine reflective capacities, or emotion dysregulation may precede metacognitive deficits. Given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, we cannot establish causal directions between these processes. Nevertheless, our findings are consistent with developmental theories suggesting that maladaptive traits contribute to personality dysfunction primarily through their impact on regulatory and reflective capacities. Future longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these reciprocal pathways.

In addition to these broader implications, certain measurement issues should also be acknowledged. Specifically, the internal consistency values for Antagonism and Psychoticism were lower than the conventional thresholds for reliability. Similar findings have been reported in previous adolescent studies, where some PID-5 domains have shown weaker reliability compared to adult samples. For example, De Clercq et al. [58] observed that several PID-5 facets yielded relatively modest alpha coefficients in youth, reflecting developmental factors that may limit trait differentiation at this age. Psychoticism-related features, such as eccentricity and perceptual dysregulation, are particularly likely to overlap with more general maladaptive tendencies in early adolescence rather than forming a distinct construct. Other studies have also noted that personality trait expression in adolescents can be less stable and more intertwined with normative developmental processes, which may account for reduced reliability in certain domains [77, 78]. Accordingly, findings related to Antagonism and Psychoticism in this study should be interpreted with caution, and future research would benefit from longitudinal and multi-method designs to better capture the developmental unfolding of these traits.

Taken together, these findings emphasize that adolescent personality pathology should be conceptualized not merely in terms of trait constellations, but as emerging from the dynamic interplay between traits, regulatory capacities, and reflective functions.

Limitations

This study, while offering valuable insights, is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents us from establishing causal relationships between the variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore the dynamic interplay between these constructs over time. Second, the study relied on self-report measures, which are susceptible to biases such as social desirability and recall bias. In addition, the relatively high intercorrelations observed between maladaptive traits, emotion regulation, metacognition, and personality functioning raise the possibility of multicollinearity or redundancy among constructs. These associations may partly reflect a general psychopathology factor (p-factor), limiting the ability to disentangle unique pathways. Future research employing longitudinal and multimethod designs, as well as advanced statistical approaches such as bifactor or hierarchical models, will be important to clarify the extent to which these constructs exert distinct versus overlapping influences on adolescent personality functioning. Fourth, the study utilized a nonclinical sample, which may not fully reflect the characteristics of adolescents experiencing more severe personality problems. Moreover, by excluding adolescents with developmental or neurological conditions such as ADHD or ASD, our findings may have limited generalizability. Given the heightened risk of personality pathology in these groups, future research should examine whether similar mechanisms operate in such populations. Additionally, the relatively low internal consistency values observed for the Antagonism and Psychoticism domains should be acknowledged as a limitation. These lower reliability coefficients may have reduced the robustness of associations involving these domains and therefore require cautious interpretation of findings related to them. Future research employing longitudinal designs and multi-method assessments could help clarify the role of these traits in adolescent samples. Finally, future research should also investigate these relationships in clinical samples to assess the generalizability of the findings to individuals with diagnosed PDs.

Despite these limitations, the study offers a significant contribution to the field by underscoring the crucial roles of emotion regulation and metacognition in the development and management of adolescent personality pathology. Future research should address the limitations of the study by incorporating longitudinal designs, multiple data sources, and diverse samples. Exploring the potential moderating effects of factors such as family support, social networks, and cultural influences on the relationship between pathological traits, emotion regulation, and metacognition is also essential.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a complicated interaction between pathological personality traits, emotion regulation, and metacognition, suggesting that these components significantly influence adolescent personality functioning. Understanding these interconnected pathways is crucial for early identification, effective assessment, and targeted treatment interventions. The study’s findings emphasize the importance of recognizing and addressing both emotional dysregulation and metacognitive difficulties when working with adolescents exhibiting signs of personality pathology.
