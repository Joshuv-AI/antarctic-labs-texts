Triple burden of malnutrition in children and mothers from Ceará, brazil: a cross sectional study
Authors: Sabrina G.M.O. Rocha, Luciano L. Correia, Márcia M.T. Machado, Álvaro J.M. Leite, Anamaria C. e Silva, Jocileide S. Campos, Sâmia G.M.O. Giacomini, Christopher R. Sudfeld, Hermano A.L. Rocha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study evaluated the prevalence and associated factors of the triple burden of malnutrition (TBM)—defined as the coexistence of child undernutrition (low growth indices), anemia in children, and maternal overweight/obesity—in 3,200 mother-child pairs in Ceará, Brazil, in 2023. Standardized anthropometric measurements and hemoglobin levels were obtained. The prevalence of TBM was 3.9% (95% CI: 3.2–4.6), with 14.6% (95% CI: 13.3–16.0) of children presenting any form of undernutrition, 36.6% (95% CI: 34.5–38.8) with anemia, and 65.5% (95% CI: 63.7–67.2) of mothers with overweight/obesity. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that age was a significant predictor: children under 6 months had an odds ratio (OR) of 3.8 (95% CI: 1.8–8.3, p = 0.001) and those aged 6 to 11 months had an OR of 5.1 (95% CI: 2.6–10.1, p < 0.001) compared to children aged 24 to 71 months. The lack of cow’s milk consumption was associated with a 2.1-fold increased risk (adjusted OR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.1–3.8, p = 0.017), while non-use of anti-helminthics raised the risk by 2.4 times (adjusted OR = 2.4; 95% CI: 1.5–3.7, p < 0.001). Environmental factors also played a key role: households with more than three children (adjusted OR = 2.2; 95% CI: 1.4–3.6, p = 0.002) and the absence of daycare attendance (adjusted OR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.8–5.7, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with TBM. Maternal factors such as possessing a pregnancy card (adjusted OR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.5–6.7, p = 0.002) and smoking (adjusted OR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.1–4.0, p = 0.024) also increased the risk of TBM. These findings underscore the need for early nutritional interventions and integrated public policies addressing dietary habits, healthcare access, and living conditions in vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01110-y.

Background

The concept of the triple burden of malnutrition (TBM) encompasses the coexistence of undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight/obesity within a population, particularly affecting maternal and child health, that should be understood as a distinct public health condition, rather than a mere aggregation of individual nutritional disorders. This perspective is critical because the components of TBM—child undernutrition, child anemia, and maternal overweight/obesity—often share common underlying socioeconomic, environmental, and dietary determinants. Interventions targeting one form of malnutrition in isolation may inadvertently worsen another, highlighting the need for a holistic approach. This phenomenon is a recognized significant public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), where the nutritional landscape is rapidly evolving due to globalization, urbanization, and changing dietary patterns [1, 2]. The implications of the TBM on maternal and child health are profound, as it affects the immediate nutritional status of mothers and children as well as has long-term consequences on their health, risk for non-communicable diseases, cognitive development, and economic productivity [3].

The rapid nutritional transition observed in many LMICs is characterized by increased consumption of ultra-processed foods high in sugar, salt, and unhealthy fats [1, 2, 4]. This transition is often accompanied by a decline in traditional diets rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, further contributing to micronutrient deficiencies and obesity [2–4]. Urbanization also plays a role, as it often leads to lifestyle changes that promote sedentary behavior and reliance on convenience foods, which can negatively impact nutritional status [2, 4, 5].

Several risk factors contribute to the existence of TBM within families. Socioeconomic status plays a crucial role, as families with limited financial resources often resort to cheaper, energy-dense, but nutrient-poor foods, leading to micronutrient deficiencies and obesity [4–6]. Additionally, education, particularly maternal education, has been recognized determinant of child nutritional outcomes [7]. Higher levels of maternal education are associated with better child dietary practices and health-seeking behaviors, which can mitigate the adverse effects of poverty [1, 8]. Furthermore, cultural beliefs and practices surrounding food can influence dietary choices, leading to inadequate nutrient intake or the overconsumption of unhealthy foods [1, 4, 5].

Despite the growing recognition of TBM, there are significant gaps in knowledge regarding the factors associated with its existence in families worldwide. Research on the TBM is still emerging, and many studies focus primarily on either undernutrition or obesity rather than examining the interplay between these conditions [1, 2, 4]. Additionally, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the prevalence of TBM across different regions and populations, which hinders the development of targeted interventions [1, 2, 4]. There are no studies evaluating the TBM condition in Brazil. While the global concept of TBM is emerging, context-specific data from large low- and middle-income countries like Brazil are important for developing targeted and effective public health policies.

Furthermore, the complexity of the TBM requires interdisciplinary approaches that integrate nutrition, health, agriculture, and social sciences, yet such collaborative efforts are often lacking [1, 2, 4]. Moreover, there is a need for more inclusive definitions and frameworks to understand the TBM. Current terminologies and classifications may not adequately capture the nuances of malnutrition, particularly in diverse cultural contexts [1, 2, 9]. This lack of clarity can lead to confusion in policy-making and program implementation, ultimately affecting the effectiveness of interventions addressing malnutrition [1, 2, 9].

In order to address this research gap, our study aimed to assess the prevalence of TBM and evaluate the factors associated with the triple burden of malnutrition in families with children up to six years of age in a large state in Brazil and establish a baseline for Brazil and identifies key areas for intervention, in a previously unexamined, yet highly relevant, population.

Methods

This was an analytical cross-sectional study, with data collected from the population, with a randomly selected sample, in 2023, from the study PESMIC. The population of this study was mother-child dyads, with children up to 71 months of age throughout the state of Ceará. Ceará is the state with the sixth lowest per capita income in Brazil. It is located in a region with an untamed climate, with more than 80% of its territory under the influence of the semi-arid climate. This state in northeast Brazil has 8.795 million inhabitants, of which just over 20% are children since its birth rate has been falling in recent decades.

Sampling

The sample for this study consisted of 3,200 mother-child pairs, selected through a multi-stage, systematic draw and by clusters. The data for this study come from the Maternal and Child Health Survey – Pesmic, seventh edition, 2023. Pesmic is a series of cross-sectional studies that have been taking place since 1987 and follow the same population sample selection methodology [10].

In short, the Pesmic sampling process involves the systematic random selection of municipalities based on the population weight of each city. Census sectors are randomly selected from each municipality, according to the delimitation of the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics, the central institute that provides population information in Brazil. Each census sector selects a group of 20 houses to be visited randomly. Thus, 3,200 homes were drawn in 2023, and only homes with children up to 71 months old eligible for this study were selected.

Institutionalized children, or whose mothers could not be assessed, and moving families were excluded from the sample and replaced by eligible families in the neighborhood until the sample of 20 families per sector was reached.

Data collection

Data was collected through interviews, using a structured questionnaire, with families in their homes and anthropometric and hemoglobin measurements of children and their mothers by professionals previously trained in interviews and measurements.

All anthropometric measurements were performed by trained health professionals following standardized World Health Organization (WHO) protocols to ensure accuracy and comparability [11]. For children aged 0 to 23 months, recumbent length was measured using a calibrated infantometer (SECA 416®), and weight was measured using a calibrated electronic scale (SECA 803®) while the child was held by the mother, with the mother’s weight subsequently subtracted. For children aged 24 to 71 months, standing height was measured using a calibrated stadiometer (SECA 213®), and weight was measured using a calibrated electronic scale (SECA 803®). All equipment was regularly calibrated according to manufacturer guidelines. Field teams underwent rigorous training and standardization exercises prior to data collection to minimize measurement error and ensure inter-observer reliability.

Hemoglobin levels were measured by extracting a capillary blood sample from the individual’s finger, which was then immediately analyzed using a portable hemoglobinometer (HemoCue Hb 201 + Analyzer, HemoCue AB, Ängelholm, Sweden). This method provides rapid and accurate on-site assessment of hemoglobin concentration.

Rigorous quality control measures were implemented throughout the data collection and management process. Prior to fieldwork, all interviewers and measurers underwent intensive training sessions covering interview techniques, standardized anthropometric measurement protocols, and ethical considerations. Standardization exercises were conducted to ensure inter-observer reliability for anthropometric and hemoglobin measurements. During data collection, field supervisors conducted regular spot checks and re-measurements to ensure adherence to protocols. Data entry involved double independent entry into EpiInfo™, followed by consistency checks to identify and correct discrepancies. Any inconsistencies were resolved by referring back to the original questionnaires.

Variables

Many studies have evaluated criteria for the double burden of malnutrition, such as the association of some undernutrition with some micronutrient deficiency, such as anemia. However, the chosen outcome variable for this study was the triple burden of malnutrition (TBM), a broader and more complex concept for the current context of nutritional transition that many populations face. To exemplify the possible differences among the different indicators of malnutrition, we also calculated other kinds of calculations for presentation.

Outcome variable

In this study the triple burden of malnutrition (TBM) was defined by [1] child having underweight or wasting or stunting [2], child anaemia and [3] maternal overweight or obesity, resulting in a binary response variable, as found in the literature [12, 13].

Child anthropometric measures were used to calculate height-for-age z-scores (HAZ), weight-for-height z-scores (WHZ), and weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ) were using the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standard classification, to classify stunting, wasting, and underweight, respectively, when Z score was below − 2 standard deviations below the reference median [11]. Anaemia in children was defined with a hemoglobin level of less than 11 g/deciliter, as recommended by the WHO [14].

The nutritional status of mothers was classified as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 to < 24.99 kg/m2), or overweight/obese (>25.0 kg/m2) [15].

Independent variables

This study’s potential confounding variables were based on previous studies on socioeconomics, environment, health services access, and the biological status of children and women.

Child variables: gender (male, female)/age groups (0 to 5.9, 6 to 23.9, 24 to 71.9 months) according to breastfeeding ages/gestational age (premature = 25 to 36 weeks, at term = 37 to 42 weeks), extremes were excluded because were few/birth weight (low weight < 2500 g, normal = 2500 to 3999 g), extremes were excluded because were also few/Apgar score (excellent = 8 to 10; no)/food history, food groups that child eat currently (cow milk, grains, fruits, biscuits, bean, embedded foods, sugary beverages, meat, egg)/ferrous sulfate supplementation/exclusive breastfed/received vitamin A in last 6 months/recent illnesses (diarrhea, fever infection)/Antibiotic use/received deworming medication in that previous 6 month/gestational factors (gestation card, mother smoked during pregnancy, delivery type, placental abruption)/go to daycare.

Maternal variables: age; race; marital status; occupational status; comorbidities (smoking, exercises, mental disorder, anemia history, ferrous and folic acid use); gynecological factors (menstruates, puberty, hormonal contraceptive use).

Environmental factors: family income; family chief and mother education; zone (urban or rural); drinking water source; receiving support of a governmental cash transfer program; count of people living in the house; number of kids in the house; health service access; food security. The variable ‘Number of people living in the house’ refers to the total count of all individuals residing in the household, including adults and children. The variable ‘Number of children in the house’ is a distinct subset of this total, specifically counting children aged 0 to 71 months within the household.

Statistical analysis

Two independent typists entered the data into spreadsheets to check consistency. The data were processed using Stata software and analyzed using SPSS. The svy command was used to correct cluster sampling. Numerical data were analyzed using measures of central tendency and normality assessment. Categorical data were analyzed using frequencies. Logistic regression was used to evaluate risk factors. To this end, we constructed a regression model with factors that presented statistical significance below 0.20 in the bivariate regression. While statistical assumptions for logistic regression were assessed, no specific transformations were applied to continuous variables as it was not deemed as necessary to meet model requirements. The statistical significance for the association between factors and the outcome after regression was 5%.

Ethical aspects

This study followed the ethical criteria for research involving human beings according to current Brazilian legislation. The participating mothers signed an informed consent form. The research project was submitted to the ethics committee through Plataforma Brazil and approved under protocol number 5.614.102. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All data collectors were trained health professionals. In instances where acute or severe health issues were observed during the household visits, families were advised to seek immediate medical attention at their local basic health units provided for free by the government, consistent with standard ethical practice in public health surveys in Brazil. The Pesmic study, from which this data was derived, operates in collaboration with local health authorities, facilitating general awareness and access to existing health services for the population studied.

The bivariate analysis, presented in the supplementary table, revealed an association between TBM and childhood factors, such as the child’s age, perinatal factors such as birth weight and gestational age, and nutritional factors, such as not consuming some food groups, not having taken a dose of vitamin A, having had exclusive breastfeeding, and not having used antiparasitic medication. The maternal and environmental factors associated with TBM were the mother having a pregnancy card, having had placental abruption during pregnancy, the mother smoking, having more than three people and more than three children in the household, and the child not attending daycare (Supplementary Table).

Many of these factors were considered risk factors for TBM after multivariable regressions. Table 2 shows the factors associated with TBM with their respective odds ratios and confidence intervals. House TBM was more present among younger children, with almost 5 times more chances among children under 1 year old than children over 2 years old. We found that TBM was 2.1 times higher odds among children who do not drink cow’s milk (ORa = 2.1; 95% CI 1.1–3.8; p = 0.017) compared to children who drink cow’s milk, and this food remained statistically significant after confounding analysis. Another factor that showed a clear protective association was the use of antiparasitic or anthelmintic drugs. Children who never took anthelmintic were 2.4-fold higher odds with TBM (ORa = 2.4; CI 95% 1.5–3.7; p < 0.001) than those who took the medication (Table 2).

TBM was associated with environmental factors such as having more individuals in the same household and attending daycare. Families with more than three people living in the household and those with more than three children increased the odds of having TBM by 60% (ORa = 1.6; CI95% 1.0–2.6.0.6; p = 0.044) and by 120% (ORa = 2.2; CI95% 1.4–3.6; p = 0.002), respectively. Moreover, families whose children do not go to daycare and/or school, regardless of age, were more than 3 times more common with TBM (ORa = 3.2; CI 95% 1.8–5.7; p < 0.001) than those who do (Table 2).

Furthermore, a factor that can be considered as better access to health services or that may be associated with the preexistence of some clinical condition, whether of the mother or the child, the mother who had the pregnancy card was 3.2 times higher odds to have TBM (ORa = 3.2; CI95% 1.5–6.7; p = 0.002) than those who did not have one (Table 2). In addition, smoking was a risk factor for TBM. Mothers who smoked were twice as likely to have TBM (ORa = 2.1; CI 95% 1.1–4.0.1.0; p = 0.024) than those who did not smoke (Table 2).

Many of these factors were considered in the multivariable regression analysis. Table 3 shows the child characteristics and environmental factors associated with TBM. Children who did not consume cow’s milk had more than twice the odds of TBM compared to those who did (ORa = 2.1; CI 95% 1.2–3.6; p = 0.012). Although exclusive breastfeeding, lack of grain or bean consumption, and absence of vitamin A supplementation were evaluated, none of these reached statistical significance (exclusive breastfeeding: ORa = 1.3; CI 95% 0.8–1.9; p = 0.274; grains: ORa = 1.3; CI 95% 0.7–2.4; p = 0.450; beans: ORa = 1.4; CI 95% 0.9–2.2; p = 0.125; vitamin A: ORa = 0.9; CI 95% 0.6–1.6; p = 0.994). In contrast, children who had never received anthelmintic treatment were more than twice more higher odds to develop TBM (ORa = 2.2; CI 95% 1.4–3.4; p < 0.001) compared with those who had been treated. (Table 3)

Environmental exposures also played a role. Children who did not attend daycare exhibited nearly double the odds of TBM versus those who did (ORa = 1.9; CI 95% 1.1–3.3; p = 0.023). Households with four or more residents showed a trend toward increased risk (ORa = 1.6; CI 95% 0.9–2.6; p = 0.053), although this did not reach significance. Notably, having four or more children in the home more than doubled the odds of TBM (ORa = 2.1; CI 95% 1.3–3.5; p = 0.004) compared with homes with three or fewer children. (Table 3)

Discussion

In this study, using a representative sample of the population, carried out in a Brazilian state with almost 9 million inhabitants, we identified that 3.9% of the families studied presented criteria for the triple burden of malnutrition, ranging from 0.5% (Stunting + Anemic child + Obese mother) to 4.6% (Wasting + Anemic child + Overweight/Obese mother), depending on the criterion used. We also observed that children under 2 years of age, without access to cow’s milk and antiparasitic drugs, with mothers who smoked, without access to daycare, and living in overcrowded households, were more likely to be affected by the triple nutritional burden, regardless of other factors.

The prevalence of TBM found in our study is in line with that found in other places worldwide. In Nepal, the TBM was found in 7% of mother-child pairs, highlighting the simultaneous presence of maternal obesity and child undernutrition [13]. Similarly, research in South Africa identified a dual burden of overweight in women alongside stunting in children, underscoring the complex interplay of nutritional issues within households [16, 17]. These examples illustrate the global nature of the TBM and the urgent need for comprehensive strategies to address its multifaceted causes.

As we demonstrate in the results, different definitions or interpretations of nutritional load can lead to different conclusions about the phenomenon. In fact, a study in Malaysia reported that stunting was notably high among younger children, with 16% of infants under one year affected. At the same time, overweight was also prevalent, indicating a dual nutritional challenge, but this study did not evaluate the coexistence of the phenomenon in the same family unit [18]. The operationalization of these definitions can greatly influence public health policies. When interventions are framed around the triple burden, there is a shift from isolated nutritional programs to integrated strategies that address the nutritional needs of different social groups, including mothers and children [19]. This holistic approach is necessary to tackle the complicated interrelations among the different forms of malnutrition. For instance, focusing solely on one aspect, such as undernutrition, could exacerbate obesity concerns.

We identified that families with younger children were more likely to suffer from the triple nutritional burden. Families with younger children are particularly vulnerable to the triple nutritional burden (TBM) due to several interrelated factors. Young children have heightened dietary needs for growth and development, making them susceptible to undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies when dietary diversity is lacking [1, 20]. Concurrently, the increasing availability of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods can lead to overweight and obesity, especially in households where parents may lack nutritional knowledge or resources to provide balanced diets [3, 21]. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions addressing the unique nutritional challenges families with young children face.

We found that lack of access to nutritious foods, especially cow’s milk, which is rich in calcium and protein, for children older than 2 years old, and difficulty accessing antiparasitic drugs were related to a higher chance of TBM. Lack of foods rich in essential nutrients, such as protein and calcium, compromises children’s growth and development, increasing their vulnerability to malnutrition [22]. Furthermore, the absence of antiparasitic drugs can result in parasitic infections that aggravate malnutrition since these parasites compete for nutrients and can cause diarrhea and malabsorption [23, 24]. For example, a study in Egypt showed that children in rural areas with limited access to antiparasitic drugs had higher rates of malnutrition and anemia [25]. Similarly, in communities in sub-Saharan Africa, the combination of inadequate diets and parasitic infections was associated with high rates of stunting and obesity, reflecting the complexity of the nutritional burden [23, 24]. These examples highlight the need for interventions that address food security and treat parasitic infections to mitigate the triple nutritional burden.

In addition to the above mentioned factors, lack of access to childcare and living in overcrowded households were positively associated with TBM in the families studied. Lack of childcare limits employment opportunities for parents, especially mothers, resulting in lower household income and, consequently, restricted access to nutritious food [26]. Furthermore, overcrowding at home can lead to unhealthy living conditions, increasing vulnerability to infectious diseases, which can exacerbate malnutrition and obesity. For example, a study in India found that families living in overcrowded conditions had a significantly higher prevalence of childhood malnutrition and maternal obesity, reflecting the complexity of the nutritional burden [12, 27]. In another case, in Nigeria, lack of access to childcare was associated with poor feeding practices, resulting in high rates of anemia and overweight among young children [28]. Lastly, we argue that there is the possibility of competition for access to food when the child lives with elderly people who are the mainstay of the family. These examples highlight the need for policies that address early childhood education support and home safety to mitigate the effects of the triple nutritional burden.

The finding that mothers possessing a pregnancy card had higher odds of TBM warrants careful interpretation. In the Brazilian public health system, pregnancy cards are generally issued upon a pregnant woman’s first prenatal visit, regardless of her initial risk status [29]. Therefore, the pregnancy card itself may not be a direct risk factor but rather a marker of engagement with the public health system. It is plausible that women who consistently attend prenatal care (and thus possess a pregnancy card) are those who are already aware of, or are being screened for, existing health issues, or are from socioeconomically vulnerable groups that rely more heavily on public health services where such cards are routinely issued and where health conditions, including those contributing to TBM, may be more prevalent. This suggests that the association observed may reflect the identification of pre-existing vulnerabilities or health conditions within a population actively engaged with healthcare services, rather than the card itself conferring risk. Future research could explore the specific characteristics of women who do and do not obtain pregnancy cards, and how this correlates with their health status and access to comprehensive care.

Limitations.

This study has some limitations that are worth mentioning. First, the study’s cross-sectional design may lead to reverse causality bias, and is a primary limitation. In terms of generalizability, although Ceará has the fourth largest capital in Brazil and one of the largest populations in the country, the other regions of the country are not perfectly represented by Ceará, despite the state being at the national average. Furthermore, the nutritional assessment was performed using a 24-hour dietary recall, which may lead to biases in the evaluation of household food availability. However, other studies have shown that this approximation is usually sufficient for general food availability. Moreover, while the study evaluated the association of certain child nutritional habits with TBM, it did not analyze detailed, age-stratified information on comprehensive child feeding practices, such as the introduction of complementary foods, dietary diversity scores, or meal frequency by specific age groups, due to limited sample power for this specific variable. This limits the ability to fully explain age-specific undernutrition and micronutrient deficiency prevalences through granular feeding practices and represents a limitation for a deeper mechanistic understanding of age-related nutritional burdens. Future longitudinal studies incorporating detailed dietary assessments by age group would provide valuable insights into the dynamic interplay between feeding practices and the development of TBM components Finally, there is no data on how the family decides to prioritize food purchases.

Conclusions

We conclude that the malnutrition (Children with any Undernutrition, Children with Anaemia and Overweight/Obese mother) has a high prevalence in Ceará, one of the largest states in Brazil. In addition, triple nutritional burden affects nearly one in each 25 households, and is associated with intrinsic factors, such as the child’s age, dietary factors, such as availability of foods with high nutritional value, and social factors, such as access to daycare centers and overcrowding in the home. We also identified that the indicator is affected by the criterion used. These findings suggest that public policies aimed at remedying the occurrence of TBM can focus on specific populations and that it is relevant to establish a single and official criterion for TBM to standardize assessments on the subject.
