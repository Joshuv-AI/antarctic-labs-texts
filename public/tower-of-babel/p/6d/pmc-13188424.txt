Clinicopathological and molecular characterization of tumor-associated macrophages in sporadic and Xeroderma Pigmentosum-related cutaneous melanoma
Authors: Asma Chikhaoui, Haifa Tounsi, Imen Nabouli, Mariem Jones, Rim Jenni, Malika Ben Ahmed, Chokri Naouali, Mohammed Zghal, Hamida Turki, Houda Yacoub-Youssef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXeroderma pigmentosum (XP) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder, resulting from genetic defects in the nucleotide excision repair (NER) pathway. It predisposes individuals to skin cancers, including melanoma. The melanoma microenvironment is enriched with inflammatory mediators, particularly macrophages, which may serve as prognostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets. While inflammation is linked to melanoma outcomes, its role in carcinogenesis among XP patients remains poorly understood.MethodsWe assessed the cytokine profiles in the sera of XP-melanoma patients, as well as the expression level of a pan-macrophage marker (CD68), M2-markers CD163, and M1-associated nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression.ResultsXP patients exhibited significantly reduced levels of iNOS, MIP-1β, MCP-1, and IL-33, indicating an impaired inflammatory and macrophage-mediated immune response. We also found that IL-33 and iNOS levels were positively correlated with the density of inflammatory infiltrates. Moreover, in vitro assays using G361 melanoma cells demonstrated that both pro-inflammatory macrophage-conditioned media and exogenous IL-33 suppressed tumor cell proliferation.ConclusionThis study emphasizes the need for specific macrophage biomarkers and proposes local interleukin-33 delivery as a potential therapy for poorly infiltrated melanoma. This also suggests that NER-deficient patients may exhibit unique molecular profiles, warranting personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15966-8.

Background

The global incidence of skin cancers has risen markedly over the last decades [1]. Although, cutaneous melanoma remains relatively uncommon in Tunisia, with fewer than 20 new cases annually, it represents the most lethal form of skin cancer with an average metastasis-free survival of only 19 months [2]. Among the genetic risk factors, Xeroderma pigmentosum is a rare genetic disease arising from defects in the nucleotide excision repair (NER) pathway. This autosomal recessive genetic defect arises from mutations in genes responsible for repairing UV-induced DNA damage. This autosomal recessive condition arises from mutations in genes responsible for repairing UV-induced DNA damage.

This repair system is responsible for the excision and resynthesis of nucleotides following DNA damage [3]. XP patients are hypersensitive to UV radiation, which leads to persistent erythema and, consequently, skin cancer. In Tunisia, this genetic disorder is frequent due to the high rate of consanguinity [4]. Recent studies have revealed that XP genes display distinct expression patterns in melanoma, which are differentially correlated with immune microenvironment markers and may serve as potential predictors of immunotherapy response [5].

Macrophage polarization is relevant for tumor biology. Two main subsets have been described: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages involved in antigen presentation and tumor cell elimination and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, which promote extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis and other functionalities. Many attempts have been tested in order to discriminate between these two main population using markers such as CD163, CD206 for M2 and HLA-DR, CD86 and iNOS for M1, have faced significant challenges due to methodological variability and limitations in detection and quantification [6].

The role of both macrophages subsets in tumor progression remains an active research subject. In tumors, infiltrating macrophages, known as tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), are the most frequent immune cell population in melanoma [7]. Assessing the functions of pro- and anti-tumor macrophage subsets remains challenging due to their plasticity and the inconsistent performance of commonly used biomarkers [8].

Monocytic cell lines such as THP-1 are frequently used to study macrophage function. However, they display substantial differences compared to primary macrophages [9, 10]. Macrophages generated from peripheral blood monocytes are in the field of active area of research as they could be exploited for the development of new treatments for cancers [11].

In vitro, monocytes can be polarized into classically activated M1 macrophages (following stimulation by LPS and IFN-γ) or alternatively activated M2 macrophages (stimulated by glucocorticoids/ IL10 or stimulated with IL-4 and IL-13 ) [12, 13]. The use of conditioned media harvested from these conditions has been employed frequently as drug delivery material and for understanding different disease physiopathological process as for infertility [14], wound healing [15] and cancerogenesis [16].

Previous reports from our team and others have highlighted distinct transcriptional and immunological features in patients with Xeroderma pigmentosum developing melanoma [17, 18]. However, due to the rarity of the disease, macrophage phenotypes and their associated cytokine profiles, remain poorly characterized. Given the potential relevance of melanoma-macrophage cross-talk for personalized therapies, further investigation is warranted.

Cytokines and chemokines are secreted mediators that modulate tumor microenvironment dynamics, influencing cancer progression or regression. Their roles are pleiotropic and context-dependent, varying with origin (endogenous vs. exogenous) and concentration, which complicates precise assessment in melanoma [19]. One major example of this controversial effect may be attributed to IL-33, a member of the IL-1 family, whose role varies depending on both, source and dose [20].

Here, we assessed tumor-associated macrophages in Formalin-Fixed, Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) primary melanoma samples from XP and non-XP patients, along with the expression of key cytokines. Based on these findings, we further investigated in vitro the effects of macrophage-conditioned media on tumor cell viability.

Methods

Ethical statement

All patients have given written informed consent to participate in this study, approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute Pasteur of Tunis Tunisia, Registration No.( IPT/LR05/Project PCI/22/2012/v2), and the study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Study design and case selection for IHC study

This retrospective study was conducted in the laboratory of Human and Experimental Pathology at Institute Pasteur of Tunis, between January 2015 and June 2025 and included 31 formalin fixed paraffin embedded blocs of cutaneous melanoma diagnosed from 2 regional centers (the Hedi Chaker Hospital in Sfax and Charles Nicolle Hospital in Tunis). The primary cohort included 23 sporadic cases (SP-Mel) and 8 XP patients who developed Melanoma (XP-Mel).

The 31 samples were further sorted to select biopsies with moderate to high inflammatory index after HE staining between sporadic and XP groups. The selection included also only non-ulcerated biopsies and the same histological type group to ensure the maximum level of homogeneity between the compared groups. The process of case selection is illustrated in Fig. 1, and a list of the selected samples is detailed in supplementary Table 1.

Fig. 1Flow diagram of patient selection for the study

Flow diagram of patient selection for the study

For each case, five slides of 4 μm thickness were cut, one slide was stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE), to assess the histological type, the inflammation level, the depth of invasion and the number of mitosis. The 4 other sections were used for Immunohistochemistry staining with three antibodies (anti-CD68, anti-CD163 and iNOS) to assess the presence of TAMs in cutaneous melanoma, and the CD34 to evaluate the microvessel density (MVD).

Immunobiological staining and scoring

A deparaffinization first step was performed using Toluene solution followed by the rehydration of the sections using ethanol solution with serial lower concentrations. Antigen retrieval was performed by heat treatment at 95° C in citric acid for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol followed by incubation with protein block for30 min. The sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies for 30 min : anti-CD163 (ref ab64693), anti-CD68 (clone 512H12 ref NCL-L-CD68) and anti CD34 (ref NCL-L-END). For anti-iNOS (ref ab3523) the incubation was overnight. The following steps were done using MACH 4 Universal HRP-Polymer Detection System Polymer Detection as instructed by the manufacturer recommendation.

Skin tissue from department of Human and Experimental Pathology of Institute Pasteur de Tunis obtained from benign nevi was used as a positive control for primary antibody.

Macrophage stained with CD68 were used to evaluate the intensity of monocyte inflammation. The proportion of each type of macrophage labeled with CD163 and iNOS was then estimated by the proportion of positive stained cells compared with negative ones. This was graded as previous work [21]: less than 10% for week staining cells to more than 50% for strong staining.

Microvessel density (MVD) was determined in hotspot fields by counting the number of positive CD34 vessels and calculating the average as detailed previously [22].

Cytokine and chemokine serum measurement by multiplex

Serum samples were obtained from 11 patients with melanoma: 7 sporadic, 4 XPC, and 6 healthy controls. The human inflammatory cytokine and chemokine 18-plex array for AimPlex technology was purchased from CliniSciences (Nanterre - France). The amount of each produced cytokine was quantified relatively to standard curves. Standard curves for each cytokine ⁄chemokine was generated by using the reference concentration supplied in the kit. Serum obtained from whole blood was diluted in the appropriate assay diluent and measured as recommended using all the assay components provided in the kit. All detected inflammatory markers detected bellow standard curves were excluded.

Differentiation of monocytes into macrophages from peripheral blood

Peripheral blood monocular cells PBMCs isolated by Ficoll are diluted to a concentration of 10^6 cells per mL in a culture flask and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using RPMI 1640 medium containing 15% FBS supplemented with 1% sodium pyruvate, 10 mM Hepes, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% nonessential amino acids, and 100 × 1% vitamins (gibco). During this step, macrophages adhere to the plastic surface. Subsequently, the supernatant and non-adherent cells are removed to increase the purity of the adherent monocytes, which are then cultured in an appropriate medium for macrophage culture with the same composition as above replacing FBS with 15% human AB serum (hAB) (Invitrogen) according to the protocol described by Arnold et al. [23]. On day 7, a new medium using advanced RPMI at low concentration of FBS with polarization activators is prepared and added to the culture medium in contact with the cells: M1 (LPS [1 µg/mL] and IFN-γ [10 ng/mL]), M2 (80 ng/ml Dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 ng/ml IL-10). The medium mixed with stimuli is removed after 48 h of activation, and the cells are cultured in a fresh medium for 24 h, M1 and M2 macrophage conditioned medium was collected and used for further experiments and stored at − 80 °C. This procedure was repeated for three different healthy blood donors.

MTT assay

G361 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C 5% CO2 in a medium composed of RPMI1640 Glutamax with a low concentration of FBS 0,05%. They were treated with Conditioned media harvested from macrophages or IL-33 at various concentrations for 24 h. Each concentration was tested in triplicate and two plate were prepared separately with the same condition, at different times. The medium in the different conditions was replaced after 24 h with fresh medium, and some wells were photographed under a microscope. Consequently 500 µg/ml of thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT solution) was added for 4 h. The assessment of formed formazan crystals that reflect cell viability was measured by ELISA reader at 570 nm excitatory emission wavelength were dissolved in DMSO.

DCF-DA staining

Cellular ROS levels was assessed by DC-FHDA (Sigma-Aldrich) in G361. Cells images were captured using Leica DM200 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) for stimulated and control condition. Quantitative measurements were performed with FIJI software and at least 50 cells were selected.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and real-time polymerase reaction (RT-PCR)

RNA extraction was performed using Rneasy (Qiagen) kit for macrophages derived from monocyte differentiation as well as for melanoma biopsies (N = 3). The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA was verified using Nanodrop (Novix DS-11). cDNA synthesis was done according to the manufacturer’s recommendation using SuperscriptIII (Invitrogen) and real time PCR was performed using specific primers selected from primer bank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/) and according to the protocol of SyBR green Roche on lightcycler480. Relative quantification was the used method for quantification.

Acquisition of gene expression profiling data

Gene expression profiling data with the series number GSE178744 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. GSE178744 was provided by the institute of biomedicine Finland. The data included in triplicate melanoma cell line MV3 that was stimulated in a similar condition to conditioned THP1 of M0, M1 and M2 macrophages.

Statistical analysis

The data was represented using GraphPad prism software (SAS). Results with p- values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical comparisons between the percentage intensity of staining were made by Fisher exact test, while for the rest it was done using the Mann–Whitney U test or T student, as appropriate.

Results

General clinical characteristics of melanoma patients

The initial cohort in this study englobes thirty-one patients (10 males; and 21 females); the mean age for the XP-Mel group was 26 years (range 14–41) while it was 62 years for the SP-Mel (range 90 − 51). All XP-Mel patients developed non-ulcered melanoma in sun-exposed areas. Histologically, most of the cases in SP-Mel were nodular NMM (N = 9/23) whereas in XP-Mel the lentigo malign LMM histological type was the predominant type. According to the Clark classification, most SP-Mel cases were at level IV, while the detection of melanoma in the XP-Mel group occurs more precociously at level II with less appearing mitosis index (Table 1).

All markers showed strong cytoplasmic expression in cells with macrophage morphology (Fig. 2).

Macrophage-conditioned media modulate melanoma viability

Control monocyte-derived macrophages were first evaluated morphologically for their polarization into M1 M2 phenotype. The morphology of macrophages is illustrated under phase contrast microscopy and shown in Fig. 4A. M0 (naive non-activated) and M2 macrophages have a similar morphology; a rounded/oval shape maintained over 72 h. M1 cells form clusters with rounded cells, and the shape of some cells elongates after 48 h of stimulation. After 72 h, some cells resemble spindles, but we still see clusters of polarized M1 macrophages adopting a “dendritic”-like morphology with large filopodia. The polarization into M1 and M2 was further confirmed in the mRNA levels by RT-PCR measuring the expression of specific CD86, iNOS and CD206. We found that M1 macrophages over-expressed CD86 (fold change = 2.8), while M2c macrophages over-expressed CD206 (fold change = 6.23) (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

Melanoma development in XP patients

Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) is a rare genetic DNA repair disorder caused by an inherited genetic mutation in 8 complementation group (XP-A to XP-G and XP-V), implicated in nucleotide excision repair. This disorder is characterized by photosensitivity, keratitis, and an increased risk of skin cancer tumorigenesis [24]. It is more frequent in country with high rate of consanguinity [25]. In our study the mean age of cancer development in XP patients was 26, similar to other previous reports in other countries, highlighting the increased risk of tumorigenesis [26]. Cutaneous melanoma accounts for 75% of skin cancer-related deaths [2].

However, its incidence in Tunisia is relatively low, even with the sunny climate [27]. According to AJCC Melanoma Staging, the presence of ulceration in melanoma is a predictor of survival, and it is associated with poor clinical outcomes. This criterion is usually associated with altered activation of inflammatory pathways [28]. However, in the current study, all XP patients had non-ulcered tumors in sun-exposed area, nonetheless, their life expectancy don’t exceed 37 year-old [29]. In addition, most of XP-Mel patients have lentigo malignant histological subtype. Patients with similar phenotype in the general population were older and more likely to have thinner and non-ulcerated tumors at initial diagnosis than patients with superficial or nodular melanoma [30].

Previous report described cutaneous melanoma in XP patients with similar low Clark and Breslow index [31]. This may be due to the regular clinical follow-up, performed in these patients that could lead to an early clinical detection.

Assessment of tumor associated macrophages phenotype in cancer-prone XP patients

Tumor associated macrophages TAMs are a subset of innate immunity cells that gain great interest in the study tumor microenvironment. They constitute more than 50% of the infiltrating immune cells within the tumor microenvironment and are classified into two main functional phenotypes pro-inflammatory phenotype M1 or immunosuppressive M2 subtypes [32]. Previous studies have explored the potential prognostic relevance of macrophage subtypes in melanoma by assessing the expression levels of key surface markers, including CD68, CD163 as well as iNOS [33–36]. However, these investigations have given inconsistent results, with no clear consensus regarding their prognostic significance.

The cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68) is a glyprotein expressed in several cell types including monocytes, macrophages, microglia, and osteoclasts [37]. Recent evidence suggests that CD68 expression is inversely correlated with DNA mismatch repair (MMR) proteins in various tumor types, highlighting its role as a possible novel immune checkpoint and therapeutic target in cancer treatment [38]. It is considered as a pan-macrophage marker, reflecting the global macrophage population within the tumor microenvironment [39]. In melanoma, CD68 macrophages, particularly at the invasive front of the tumor, has been correlated with reduced overall survival and unfavorable clinical outcomes [33–36]. In this study no significant difference in CD68⁺ macrophage density was observed between melanomas arising in sporadic cases and those associated with Xeroderma Pigmentosum (XP) suggesting that CD68 expression, and by extension the general macrophage infiltration, may not be influenced by defects in the nucleotide excision repair pathway.

The CD163 receptor is a well-established surface marker of human monocytes and macrophages highly expressed in pro-tumoral TAMs [40, 41]. It is associated with poor patient outcome across a variety of solid tumors [42]. In melanoma, CD163 + macrophage density increased with Breslow depth, indicating their accumulation in advanced tumors [43]. However, since the selected groups for the current study predominantly included primary melanomas of early stages (mostly with clark level < III), this aspect was not observed.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a free radical molecule generated by 3 enzymes from the nitric oxide synthase (NOS) family, among which the inducible form (iNOS) is of particular relevance [44]. In immune cells, iNOS is expressed by M1-type macrophages during inflammatory responses, catalyzing synthesis of nitric oxide, which is an important signaling and effector molecule involved in many physiological and pathological processes. Aberrant iNOS expression has been implicated in different skin diseases such as (atopic dermatitis, lupus, psoriasis, etc.) and in several types of cancer (breast, cervical, etc.) [45]. Most studies that explored the tumoral microenvironment using standard technique such as real-time PCR, immunoblot analysis and immunohistochemistry suggested that high level of iNOS is associated with poor cancer survival rate [46]. However iNOS-derived NO effect remains context-dependent, as it can exert can tumorigenic or tumoricidal activities, leading to conflicting findings across studies [47–49].

Regarding the association between iNOS expression and macrophage polarization in melanoma, the literature remains inconsistent. Some studies have reported that a high rate of M1 macrophages is associated to poor survival [35], whereases others, observed a positive correlation was noted between prolonged survival time and iNOS+ cell infiltration [50]. These discrepancies may be explained by the dual role of iNOS in tumor biology and by the fact that tumor cells themselves can express iNOS, rendering it a nonspecific marker for M1 macrophages when assessed by immunohistochemistry [51].

Interestingly, the marked under-expression of iNOS in XP-Mel group is noteworthy, particularly in light of previous reports describing spontaneous melanoma regression in XP patients [52–54] including Tunisian patients sharing similar genetic etiology [55]. Beside this hypothesis, we may also suggest that the defective NER pathway in XP patients could exert local immunomodulatory effects in the skin driven by the extreme UV-B hypersensitivity characteristic of these patients. Indeed, reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α have been reported in cultured XP keratinocytes Following UV-B stimulation [56].

Finally, because of the limited number of homogeneous cases, robust correlations between macrophage markers and clinicopathological parameters, including microvessel density, could not be established in either group. We therefore present only our observations in this limited cohort, pending confirmation in larger multicenter studies.

Assessment of cytokine expression in cancer-prone XP patients

To further investigate the immunological abnormalities previously suggested by our team [17] and others [57], we analyzed the expression levels of several cytokines in patients who developed melanoma. While previous studies on other skin malignancies in XP patients reported no significant differences in cytokine profiles between controls and sporadic cancer cases [58], our data revealed a possible significant down-expression of MCP1, MIP1B and IL33 in all XP-Mel patients compared with both healthy controls and to SP-Mel group. It is well established that immune competence declines with aging, leading to deregulated immune responses that increase susceptibility to autoimmunity, infection disease and cancer in elderly population. While XP is considered as premature ageing disorder, little is still known regarding immune dysfunctions associated with this syndrome [59]. Thus, the differences in cytokine expression observed in comparison with aged sporadic melanoma patients may also reflect differences in age.

The first factor MCP1, also known as CCL2, belongs to the family of inflammatory CC chemokines that recruits monocytes, memory T lymphocytes and dendritic cells to inflammatory sites through interaction with its receptor CCR2 [60, 61]. In melanoma, MCP1 promotes TCD8 + lymphocytes and NK cell infiltration [62]. At high doses, MCP1 contributes to tumor rejection, while at low concentrations it supports tumor growth [63]. Reduced MCP1 expression has also been linked to M2 macrophage polarization [64]. In our study, MCP1 levels were decreased in XP-Mel patients compared with both SP-Mel and healthy controls, suggesting a possible impaired immune surveillance contributing to tumorigenesis.

MIP1-b, is another chemokine involved in NK cell, monocyte recruitment and Th1 activation [65, 66]. Its reduced serum concentration may reduce anti-tumor immunity [67]. These findings support the hypothesis of either an underlying immunodeficiency in XP patients or the development of immunosenescence linked to accelerated aging and cumulative DNA repair defects.

The last cytokine is the Interleukin-33 (IL-33), that belongs to the IL-1 family, abundantly expressed by endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts [68]. It has a crucial modulatory function, with pleiotropic activities affecting type 2, type 1 and regulatory immune responses. Depending on the tumor type and microenvironment, IL-33 can exert either pro- or anti-tumoral effects [69, 70].

An anti-tumor protective role for IL-33 has been reported in previous studies, given that its low expression level in the plasma of patient with lung cancer compared to healthy controls [71]. In cutaneous melanoma, this interleukin has not been quantified in the serum of melanoma patients.

Interplay between IL-33 and iNOS in melanoma tumorigenesis

The role of IL-33 in melanoma remains controversial, as it can induce both pro- and anti-tumor immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. Some studies have shown that IL-33 promotes metastasis by driving macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype [72]; whereas others, demonstrated that IL-33 overexpression in cutaneous melanoma mouse model inhibits tumor progression by increasing the frequency of CD8 + and NK T cell infiltration in tumor tissue [73]. IL-33 can activate other immune cells including eosinophils, capable of killing target melanoma cells [74]. More evidently, understanding IL-33 anti-tumoral effect seems to be more context-dependent by the cellular sources of IL-33 and the status of the melanoma tumor [75].

Our findings revealed a positive correlation between IL33 and iNOS expression in melanoma biopsies on the transcriptomic level, along with increased inflammatory infiltrates evaluated through immunohistochemistry. These findings may suggest that, in these cases, iNOS and IL-33 are expressed primarily by inflammatory cells rather than by the tumor cells themselves.

In skin immunity, in the context of antibacterial defenses on the skin, it has been shown that IL33 induces iNOS expression in dermal macrophages [76], and similar co-upregulation has been observed in ovarian tumor [77]. These effects may involve the MAPK pathway and NFκB which regulate iNOS expression in melanoma cells [78]. Indeed, IL-33 can enhance macrophage pyroptosis by activating the NF-kB/p38MAPK signal pathway [79]. Further studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm these mechanistic links.

IL-33 directly decreases melanoma cell line viability

Our findings demonstrate an inhibition of tumor proliferation following the exogenic IL-33 supplementation in melanoma cell line. This aligns with its reported pro-apoptotic effects in pancreatic (MIA PaCa-2) [80] and colon (HCT-116) cancer cell lines by downregulating pro-tumor proliferative molecules such as cyclin B, cyclin D and cyclin dependent kinase 2 [81]. In our work, we have shown that this effect is dose-dependent, which could explain the conflicting reports on IL-33’s role across tumor types [82]. Given that melanoma often exhibits a “cold” immune phenotype poorly responsive to therapy, local IL-33 delivery within the tumor microenvironment could represent a promising therapeutic approach to enhance immune activation.

Macrophage-secreted factors affect melanoma viability and NER related gene expression

Human monocyte-derived macrophages generated from peripheral blood monocytes were polarized in vitro into M1 and M2c phenotypes, validated by surface markers CD86 and CD206, respectively, consistent with previous studies [83, 84].The low iNOS expression observed in these conditions may be related to inter-individual differences among healthy donors, as previously suggested in the literature [85].

Conditioned media (CM) from M1 macrophages significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation. Although the limitations of the MTT assay in assessing cell number and viability are well recognized [86], similar observations have been reported in This has already been demonstrated on renal cell carcinoma [87], breast cancer [88] colon cancer [89], and more recently, in the cutaneous melanoma cell line, MV3, which is consistent with our results obtained in G361 cell line [90]. Furthermore, consistent with our findings, other studies have reported that the M1 macrophage secretome reduces melanoma cell viability, particularly through modulation of the H19/iNOS/PD-L1 immune-regulatory axis [91]. Although assessing metabolites produced by M1 macrophages, especially nitric oxide (NO), would have strengthened this analysis, NO is widely recognized as a regulator of numerous proteins and signaling pathways [92], including those associated with DNA damages, such as 8-nitroguanine and 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG) [93].

Interestingly, previous studies have suggested that NER pathway deficiency in XP may modify the mutational burden inside the melanoma niche, potentially enhancing tumor immunogenicity [94]. Conversely, mutations in NER genes (ERCC2 and ERCC3) can increase tumor sensitivity to specific antitumor agents such as irofulven [95]. Transcriptomic analysis of melanoma cells exposed to M1 macrophage CM, revealed enrichment in NER related genes (ERCC1, ERCC2, ERCC3 and XPC), suggesting a selective activation of DNA repair pathways to ensure anti-tumoral effect. XPC expression may contribute to the inhibition of tumorigenesis, since the opposite effect has been described in in vitro melanoma models via CDKN2A interaction independently of NER [96]. In addition, increased ERCC3 expression may be linked to a better melanoma prognosis, as indicated by previous bioinformatic studies [5]. Conversely, lower expression of XPA, ERCC1, and ERCC2 may also be favorable, as high expression of these genes has been associated with treatment resistance, poor survival in other cancer models [97–99] and increased inflammatory signaling in knock out melanocytes models [100] .

Combining NER-targeted modulation with immune-based therapies could therefore enhance anti-tumor efficiency, particularly in XP-related melanomas, which often exhibit better immunotherapy responses due to their high mutational load [101]. Previous studies of melanomas in 10 XP patients with defective DNA repair reported fewer BRAF, NRAS, and KIT mutations than in sporadic melanomas [102]. This difference may be relevant to our findings, as the unique immune characteristics of XP-derived melanoma cells may result not only from a protective adaptation to oxidative DNA damage in NER-deficient cells, but also from differences in somatic mutational burden. Further work is needed to better define these respective contributions.

However, this study’s conclusions remain limited by the small XP melanoma cohort and potential biases related to sample representativeness. Technical constraints of immunohistochemistry on serial tumor sections may also affect precise immune infiltration assessment in low-density tumors [51]. Moreover, macrophage diversity and plasticity are driven by complex epigenetic programs not fully captured by classical surface markers [36]. Another limitation in the immunobiological analysis of this study giving the rarity of samples is the merge between skin samples from different anatomological of sun-exposed and non-exposed area in the comparison as immune system modulation differs through skin exposure to UVs [102, 103]. Furthermore, future investigations incorporating site-matched benign nevi and employing standardized quantitative assessment protocols would help strengthen and validate our findings which were constrained by budgetary limitations [104] and the scarcity of samples. As reported in other rare diseases, these challenges emphasize the importance of greater stakeholder awareness and support.

Conclusion

In summary, deciphering macrophage heterogeneity and function in cutaneous melanoma may uncover novel therapeutic targets. Gaining insight into the mechanisms underlying macrophage polarization could lead to the development of macrophage-directed therapies. These strategies could complement immunotherapy of cancer and preliminary proofs suggest that rare DNA repair disorders such as XP represent interesting study models for a better understanding of these concepts and for advancing precision oncology approaches.
