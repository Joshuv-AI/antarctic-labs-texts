Staged Care Needs of Home Enteral Nutrition Patients: A Longitudinal Qualitative Study Based on Maslow’s Hierarchy
Authors: Ru-Ting Shen, Ye-Hua Xie, Yang Yang, Li-Fang Xie, Li-Li Gu, Wei Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the staged characteristics and variations in care needs among patients receiving home enteral nutrition (HEN) during the transition from healthcare institutions to home settings, using Maslow’s hierarchy of needs as a theoretical framework. The study aimed to inform the development of a needs-based nursing system specific to HEN.MethodsFor purposive sampling, 15 patients meeting clinical indications for HEN were recruited. Following timing theory, each patient participated in three semi-structured interviews: 1–3 days prior to discharge, 7–14 days following discharge, and approximately 90 days post-discharge (±3 days). Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method to synthesize findings, interpret experiences, and identify key themes.ResultsThree primary themes, seven secondary themes, and thirteen tertiary themes were identified. During the discharge preparation phase, care needs were primarily focused on physiological stability and safety. In the home-transition phase, continued attention to these needs was observed. In the home-adaptation phase, care needs evolved toward psychological well-being and self-actualization.ConclusionCare needs among patients receiving HEN demonstrated distinct stage-specific and multidimensional changes during the transition from institutional to home care. This progression followed a trajectory from physical survival to emotional support to the reconstruction of personal value. These findings emphasize the importance of holistic nursing approaches and support the development of a structured, needs-based HEN care model.Keywords: home enteral nutrition, Maslow’s theory, needs, nursing, qualitative study

Introduction

Home enteral nutrition (HEN) is a modality through which nutritional support and treatment are administered in home and community settings via enteral tubes placed in the gastrointestinal tract, under the supervision of a specialized nutrition support team.1 HEN has garnered substantial international research interest due to its cost-effectiveness and its role in enhancing quality of life.2 In the United States, approximately 1,385 patients per million residents receive HEN therapy.3 Although the development of HEN in China commenced relatively later, it has experienced rapid advancement. In 2013, West China Hospital introduced the first continuous and individualized nutritional management framework in China: the “H2H” (hospital to home) model, which represented a significant advancement in HEN research. This model facilitates the extension of nutritional therapy and nursing care from hospital-based to out-of-hospital environments and expands treatment modalities from single to multiple approaches.4 Longitudinal qualitative research, which emphasizes temporality and centers on the concept of change, aligns closely with the evolving nature of personal experiences.5

Currently, significant attention has been directed by domestic researchers toward the continuity of care in the management of patients receiving HEN. However, there remains a gap in research concerning the multidimensional, self-care-oriented needs of this population. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory posits that human needs are organized into five hierarchical levels: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization needs.6 Consequently, this study integrates both the perspective of patients receiving HEN and the theoretical framework of Maslow to conduct a longitudinal qualitative investigation. The aim is to examine the changes in care needs throughout the treatment trajectory and to identify practical strategies for ensuring continuity of care. The study seeks to enhance the congruence between nursing services and patient expectations, reduce systemic response delays and mismatches between care supply and demand, and provide a theoretical foundation for developing a localized HEN nursing care system.

Participants and Methods

Participants of This Study

A purposive sampling method was employed to recruit patients scheduled to begin HEN in the Department of General Surgery at a tertiary hospital in Nanjing between May and December 2024. Patients discharged from our department who required HEN were followed up regularly by the department’s Home Nutrition Support Team. One of the authors was a member of this team and had established good trust relationships with the patients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① an anticipated HEN duration of ≥ 3 months to allow for sufficient observation of the clinical course; ② age between 18 and 65 years with preserved independent decision-making capacity; ③ first-time initiation of HEN therapy; ④ administration of enteral nutritional support through tube feeding; ⑤ intact abilities in comprehension, expression, and communication to ensure the reliability of in-depth interviews; ⑥ voluntary participation confirmed by signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: ① a clinical diagnosis of anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress reactions; ② presence of major organ failure or other severe conditions (eg., end-stage malignancies). The elimination criterion was: ① inability to continue participation due to unforeseen or specific circumstances during the study period or voluntary withdrawal. The sampling strategy was guided by information power and maximum variation strategy.7 Patients were recruited sequentially according to discharge order. Two patients who met the inclusion criteria were subsequently excluded because they did not complete 3 months of enteral nutrition (switching from tube feeding to oral intake during the HEN period). A total of 15 patients were included in the interview phase, at which point recruitment ceased as data saturation was achieved. To ensure anonymity, participants were coded numerically from N1 to N15, and all interview data were collected without identifiers.

Methods

Research Team Composition

The research team was composed of six members: one chief physician, who contributed medical expertise and clinical condition analysis; one nursing management expert, who oversaw research guidance and quality control; one nurse affiliated with the psychology group; two nurses specializing in nutrition; and one undergraduate nursing student. All members of the team had received training in qualitative research methodologies and possessed substantial experience in conducting qualitative research.

Study Design

In accordance with timing theory and following internal discussions within the research team, three key time points were identified to represent the transitional process from institutional care to home adaptation. Semi-structured interviews were employed at all three time points: T1, 1–3 days prior to discharge (discharge-preparation phase); T2, 7–14 days post-discharge (home-transition phase); and T3, 90 ± 3 days post-discharge (home-adaptation phase).8 T1 interviews were conducted in the patient education room within the inpatient ward. During the follow-up period (T2–T3), face-to-face interviews were conducted during scheduled return visits. When in-person interviews were not feasible, telephone interviews were conducted instead.

Development of the Interview Guide

The final interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive review of relevant literature and the outcomes of pilot interviews. Prior to formal data collection, two pilot interviews were conducted to refine the interview guide. These pilot participants met the same inclusion criteria as the study participants but were subsequently excluded from the main study to avoid data contamination. The final interview guide was established following these pilot interviews.

Data Collection methods

Two nurses conducted interviews. One nurse was responsible for leading the interview, managing audio recordings, posing follow-up questions, and adjusting the questioning strategy as needed. The second nurse supported the process by observing and documenting nonverbal behaviors, including micro-expressions and body language. Prior to each interview, the study purpose, methodology, and significance were explained to participants, and informed consent was obtained. Privacy was maintained throughout the interview process. When ambiguity in participant responses was encountered, the interviewer restated and confirmed the information with the participant to ensure accuracy. Each interview continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes were identified. Audio recordings and observational notes were transcribed into text within 24 hours following each interview. After transcription, members of the research team reviewed the audio recordings and verified the transcripts for accuracy and completeness through repeated review.9

Data Analysis Methods

Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method, combined with both diachronic and synchronic analytical strategies.10,11 Following each round of interviews, manual coding and theme extraction were conducted independently by two nurses, using a line-by-line and paragraph-by-paragraph approach to the same set of transcripts to ensure data completeness and accuracy. Upon completion of all three interview rounds, cross-time comparative analyses were performed for each participant’s data to further refine and validate the emerging themes. The extracted themes were subsequently reviewed through re-examination of the original transcripts or by seeking confirmation from the interviewees, in order to ensure accurate interpretation of both linguistic content and behavioral expressions within the population of patients receiving HEN. Finally, based on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the identified care needs were stratified, and their dynamic developmental trajectories were analyzed to provide deeper insight into the underlying meanings of the themes.

Rigour and Quality Control

The rigour of this qualitative study was established through several strategies aligned with established criteria for trustworthiness. First, the study design was underpinned by clear theoretical frameworks—Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and Timing Theory—which guided the development of the interview guide and the longitudinal data collection across three distinct phases of the patient journey. Second, methodological rigour was ensured through strict adherence to inclusion and exclusion criteria and the use of purposive sampling to select information-rich participants.7 Data were collected longitudinally via semi-structured interviews at three time points (1–3 days pre-discharge, 7–14 days post-discharge, and 90±3 days post-discharge), allowing for the capture of dynamic changes in patient needs. Third, data analysis followed the systematic and well-established Colaizzi’s seven-step method, with independent coding by two researchers to enhance analytical rigour. Finally, the entire research process was guided by a standardized quality control protocol, including peer debriefing within the research team and member checks, to minimize bias and ensure the credibility and authenticity of the findings.

To enhance the reliability of the study findings, several quality control measures were implemented: (1) Participant selection was not restricted by sex. Educational backgrounds ranged from illiteracy to postgraduate education, and places of residence included provincial capitals, prefecture-level cities, and township areas. This diversity contributed to the credibility of the sample and the potential generalizability of the findings. (2) To reduce the risk of sample attrition influencing the study outcomes, contact information was collected during the initial interview, and a WeChat follow-up group was established to facilitate ongoing communication. (3) The final version of the interview guide was developed to reflect real-life scenarios encountered by patients during the implementation of HEN. It incorporated physiological, psychological, and social dimensions of patient needs, thereby supporting the comprehensiveness and richness of the data collected.12

Results

Discharge-Preparation Phase: Emphasis on Physiological and Safety Needs

During the discharge-preparation phase, primary attention among patients receiving HEN was directed toward meeting physiological and safety needs. This pattern aligns with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory, which posits that the fulfillment of physiological and safety needs is foundational to the satisfaction of higher-level needs.13

Specific concerns during this phase included access to HEN-related supplies, preparation of theoretical knowledge and nursing skills, and psychological support to promote a sense of safety. Needs associated with emotional connection, social interaction, and self-actualization were not prominently expressed (Figure 1).

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs pyramid model.

Physiological Needs

Accessibility of Resources

At the initiation of HEN, Almost all patients expressed considerable concern regarding the availability of enteral nutrition-related consumables and medications, such as feeding tubes and nutrient solutions.

N2:

Other patients mentioned that only a small amount could be taken at discharge, which lasts for about a week. In the hospital, they do not give extra each day. We hoped to take more home to maintain it longer before returning to the hospital. I also consulted a local hospital, but they didn’t have the nutrient solution.

N5:

I ideally hope that no one needs to come over; the nutrient solution could just be delivered directly to my home.

N9:

The feeding tube is used for no more than a week. I wash it every day, so technically it should be replaced daily. But the small hospital cannot supply that many, so I wash it daily and will replace it immediately if it gets blocked.

Operational Certainty

The physiological safety of patients receiving HEN is primarily dependent on accurate execution of the full procedural workflow. This includes pre-infusion preparation (eg., tube flushing, patient positioning, device connection), infusion management (eg., adjustment of flow rate, alarm response), and post-infusion care (eg., tube maintenance, documentation of abnormal events). Despite receiving hands-on training from nursing staff during hospitalization, in this study, 40% of the patients still frequently encountered difficulties, including forgetting procedural steps, mechanical repetition without understanding, or difficulty in translating visual knowledge into practical execution.

N1:

The nurse explained the steps to me, but when I get home, I can’t remember them. It would be best to give me a booklet or an instructional video that I can watch repeatedly. Also, I can’t apply the nasal adhesive well. At the hospital, a nurse helped me, but at home, when I try to apply it myself, it feels awkward, crooked, and not secure.

N5:

I still find it inconvenient to replace the nasal adhesive by myself. Because of the nasogastric tube, it tends to sag, and if I’m not careful, it can be pulled out. So, I need to fix it first, then apply the nasal adhesive. When doing it myself, I sometimes get confused about which side of the tape should face the tube.

Safety Needs

Accessibility of Data

In the early phase of HEN implementation, 60% patients primarily focused on acquiring reliable information regarding formal, stable, affordable, and convenient supply channels for specialized consumables. Additionally, there was a strong demand for clear guidance on theoretical knowledge and operational procedures related to HEN.

N1:

After using the feeding tube, I wanted to buy one online, but I didn’t know if it was compatible or which was the better quality brand. I had to spend a lot of time searching through different websites each time.

N7:

Online shopping is indeed convenient, but I wouldn’t buy the nutrient solution online for fear of getting a fake product. I saw a wide range of prices online; some were too cheap, and there was no way to verify the quality.

Psychological Safety

During the interviews, a range of emotional and behavioral responses to HEN were identified, with patients exhibiting contrasting attitudes. Approximately 20% of patients demonstrated low self-efficacy, accompanied by anxiety and feelings of helplessness. These responses were manifested through symptoms such as insomnia, often related to repeated mental rehearsal of HEN procedures, and through avoidance behaviors, including delays in discharge or reinforcement of the sick-role identity. In contrast, approximately 60% of patients expressed confidence in their ability to manage HEN independently.

N1:

I was really scared when they told me I could go home. I was extremely worried. The attendant asked why I had not been discharged if I was stable. I said I was afraid there would be no one to help me at home, and I didn’t know what to do.

N8:

After the first teaching session, I learned it. If a problem occurs at home, I will adapt as needed. But this depends on the individual; some people may not master nasogastric HEN techniques.

Home-Transition Phase: Continued Focus on Physiological and Safety Needs

Physiological Needs

Physical Comfort

During the home-transition phase, 67% patients receiving HEN experienced complications and physiological maladaptation. Commonly reported issues included nutrient solution intolerance resulting in diarrhea, as well as sleep disturbances caused by the need to change feeding bags during nighttime infusions. These factors contributed to a decline in overall physical comfort.

N1:

When I got home, I had a new tube (nasogastric tube), and my nose always felt foreign and uncomfortable It was even worse when I sneezed.

N4:

It’s inconvenient to sleep at night; most of the time I can only lie flat because I’m afraid the tube (PEJ tube) might come out. In summer, there’s growth of granulation tissue, and the skin around the wound becomes red and itchy.

N7:

I use about 5–6 bottles of nutrient solution each day. The amount is quite substantial, but my stomach gurgles at various times throughout the day, which is a bit uncomfortable.

N11:

When I first started HEN at home, I was always worried during the night that changing positions might bend the feeding tube. I had to stay in one position, which often resulted in insomnia.

Safety Needs

In-Depth Understanding of Specialized Knowledge

Approximately 67% of participants expressed the need for systematic education on topics such as nutrient solution composition and physiological effects, selection of appropriate enteral nutrition methods, calculation of individualized physiological requirements, and adjustment of infusion parameters. In contrast, 33% of participants focused more on the accessibility of professional support services, including contact with medical personnel, availability of home-visiting services, interpretation of diagnostic test results, and information related to follow-up appointments.

N1:

I’m still confused about how to assess the daily requirement of nutrient solution. The doctor told me to gradually combine the nutrient solution with regular food at home to replace it over time. How should I calculate calories to meet my body’s needs? Also, are kilocalories and calories the same?

N8:

I think the concentration of the solution is closely related to diarrhea, and these details need to be clearly explained to the patient.

Controllability of Emergencies

After acquiring proficiency in basic operational skills, patients receiving HEN began to shift their attention from immediate survival to risk prevention, a shift often prompted by the occurrence of complications.

N1:

Once I bought a nasal adhesive online, but the material was poor, the glue was very sticky, and it damaged my face. I had to go to the hospital for some ointment and also bought the proper tape.

N5:

When changing the nasal adhesive, I accidentally cut the nasogastric tube. I had to go to the hospital immediately to have the tube reinserted. It was really troublesome and time-consuming, but there was no other way since I couldn’t insert it myself.

N8:

One time, the nutrition pump at home kept ringing the alarm. I tried for a long time but couldn’t figure out the problem. The nutrient solution couldn’t be used that day, and I had to buy a new one the next day.

Home-Adaptation Phase: Pursuit of Psychological Fulfillment and Self-Actualization Needs

Love and Belonging Needs

Community Attachment

In this study, 60% of the patients were accompanied by negative emotions and behaviors, while 40% of the patients actively self-adjusted. Although some patients exhibited negative emotional states and disengaged behaviors, others adopted active coping and self-regulatory strategies.

N1:

I get very bored at home every day, but when I think of my family returning each night, I try to pass the time quickly. I started learning courses online, such as Baduanjin and Qigong. I later got to know that Baduanjin is very beneficial for intestinal conditions. Before, I could only have one bowel movement using Western medicine, but after practicing Baduanjin, it has become smoother. Exercising not only passed my time, but it also aided my digestive system and improved my mood.

N7:

You ask what I can do at home every day. Going out is troublesome, and I can’t go to work, so I just play games.

N13:

My family members have to work, so most of the time I am alone at home. I feel very bored, scroll through my phone, zone out, and there’s nothing to make me happy.

Family Role Reconstruction

The implementation of HEN influenced the distribution of responsibilities and roles in the division of labor among family members, 60% of the interviewees had changes in family roles and division of labor.

N4:

This treatment is very expensive, but in my family only my father works to earn money, so the financial burden is heavy. My mother also works hard to take care of me every day.

N13:

My spouse and I don’t know how to use smartphones. If we need something, I call my son and ask him to purchase it online. If you have anything, you can contact him; we don’t really understand how to do it ourselves.

Esteem Needs

Accompanied by Illness-Related Stigma

Continuous reminders of their clinical status, such as specialized care from family members and the visible presence of the nasogastric tube, reinforced their perception of the “patient identity.” As a result,74% of the respondents believed that their family members’ special care and the existence of nasogastric feeding tube always reminded them of their “patient identity”, social anxiety was frequently reported, along with efforts to conceal their medical condition or a desire to transition from nasogastric to oral intake for improved social acceptability.

N1:

I don’t dare to go out during the day, afraid of being laughed at and feeling different or special. Sometimes in the morning, before anyone is awake, I quickly go out for a walk, or I go out wearing a mask, afraid that people will see me and think it looks bad.

N7:

I still have to go to work. Sitting in the office like this, what will people think? We also have clients; when they come, how will I appear to them?

N5:

At work or school, there are situations where nasogastric feeding is inconvenient or inappropriate. If I could take the nutrient solution orally, I wouldn’t need to insert a tube.

Desire for Social Acceptance

Throughout the process of societal reintegration, nearly all patients receiving HEN expressed a pronounced desire for social acceptance.

N4:

I was in my final year of high school with a tight schedule, spending most of my time in class. Wearing this tube was very inconvenient and restricted my activities. My teachers and classmates didn’t ask much about my situation and treated me as usual, although some classmates were quite considerate.

N7:

I will continue working later. If I wear the nasogastric tube, my workplace might worry about whether I can handle my current position and also fear that I might have an accident at work, which could even lead to dismissal.

Self-Actualization Needs

Buffering Economic Pressure

Sixty percent of patients receiving HEN often face high medical expenses, contributing to feelings of helplessness, guilt, and despair.

N4:

This treatment is very expensive, but only my father works to earn money for the family, so the financial burden is heavy.

N8:

As for the price itself, there’s a huge difference between reimbursed and non-reimbursed costs, which is unacceptable. Many patients cannot afford it; even the nutrient solution alone is a large expense, not to mention other consumables. The costs are substantial.

Restoration of Social Value

Upon entering the home-adaptation phase, 67% patients receiving HEN demonstrated increased self-efficacy and a strong desire to restore recognition of their social roles.

N4:

I don’t want to continue like this. Although my general daily life is not affected, there are many activities I cannot do such as running, swimming, or vigorous exercise. I want to recover quickly and be like an ordinary person.

N7:

I will continue working later. I don’t want people to question my work ability just because I am wearing a nasogastric tube.

Discussion

Staged, Dynamic, and Multidimensional Characteristics of HEN Patients’ Care Needs

The care needs of patients receiving HEN demonstrate stage-specific variations over time. Distinct differences are observed between the discharge-preparation and home-adaptation phases: the former is primarily concerned with survival needs, while the latter is centered on developmental needs. Survival-related needs persist throughout the entire care process; in contrast, developmental needs arise at different time points, typically after the fulfillment of basic survival requirements. This progression is consistent with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, whereby care needs transition from lower-level domains (eg., resources, operational skills, and safety) to higher-level domains (eg., social engagement and self-actualization). As stable access to consumables, information, and operational skills was established, gradual improvement in both physical and psychological functioning was observed among patients receiving HEN. Initial challenges associated with HEN were progressively overcome, leading to enhanced self-efficacy and a shift from passive dependence to autonomous adaptation. This transition was associated with an increased expression of needs related to emotional support, social integration, and self-actualization.

The dynamic nature of care needs among this population is reflected in three primary dimensions: (1) From dependence to autonomy: During the early phase, patients rely heavily on guidance from healthcare professionals; in later stages, greater independence and decision-making capacity are exhibited. (2) From physiological to psychological: Once foundational care needs are met, psychological and social dimensions become increasingly salient. (3) From individual to systemic: After achieving individual-level adaptation, patients express the need for a broader support system that integrates family, community, and healthcare institutions. In response to the evolving care needs of patients receiving HEN, it is recommended that current HEN management frameworks incorporate structured monitoring mechanisms and enhance connectivity across hospital, community, and family settings. Greater refinement in coordination and integration across care stages is also advised.

Ensuring Physiological and Safety Needs During the Discharge-Preparation Phase

A stable supply of HEN consumables is essential for the effective initiation of home-based nutritional support. In this study, two primary channels for obtaining consumables were identified among patients in China: discharge medication provided by the hospital and self-procurement. Supplies obtained through hospital channels are generally reliable but are subject to institutional regulations and inventory limitations, which restrict their adequacy for long-term or high-volume use. In contrast, self-procurement mainly via online platforms, raises concerns regarding product authenticity, contributing to reduced patient confidence in supply quality.

Among the two sources, hospital-distributed HEN supplies were perceived as more trustworthy. However, economic burden significantly influenced procurement behavior. The substantial disparity between reimbursed and non-reimbursed costs prompted some patients to reuse consumables or seek repeated hospital admissions for resupply, both of which negatively impact the continuity and stability of HEN care. To address these challenges, the development of a coordinated resource support network is recommended, integrating hospitals, pharmacies, and community clinics to establish a unified, one-stop supply platform for HEN kits. Simplifying reimbursement procedures is also advised. In addition, promotion of a “home resource checklist” including emergency contact cards and equipment repair pathways, may further enhance preparedness. Strengthening the roles of general practitioners, community nurses, and community dietitians is recommended to optimize the contributions of community health services in both primary care and extended support.4

During the in-hospital preparation phase, anxiety and psychological insecurity are frequently reported by patients receiving HEN, primarily due to insufficient mastery of HEN-related skills. Difficulties such as forgetfulness, mechanical repetition, or the inability to translate visual understanding into manual execution are common. During home care, additional risks arise from discrepancies between consumables used in clinical settings and those available for home use. Analysis indicates that these self-care challenges are closely associated with inadequate discharge preparation. Therefore, enhancing skill-based training during the discharge-preparation phase is essential for both healthcare professionals and caregivers, in order to reduce the incidence of adverse events during HEN implementation.

Training protocols should incorporate competency assessments and ensure that HEN-related information is provided in multiple formats including verbal, written, and pictorial presentations to address varying individual needs.14 Additionally, new demands are placed on professional staff, including the requirement for timely updates to HEN clinical guidelines and strengthened personnel training. These align with established recommendations that all healthcare personnel directly involved in patient care receive education and training relevant to their responsibilities, encompassing all aspects of safe HEN delivery.15

Strengthening Continuity of Care During the Home-Transition Phase: Early Identification of Physical and Psychological Changes

In this study, reduced comfort among patients receiving HEN was primarily attributed to gastrointestinal and tube-related complications, such as diarrhea and nasopharyngeal foreign-body sensation, as well as difficulty adapting to the home care environment. Persistent adverse experiences of this nature not only diminish the effectiveness of nutritional therapy but also negatively impact treatment adherence, thereby increasing the risk of interruptions in nutritional support.16 As a result, monitoring of home visits should extend beyond objective clinical indicators, such as body weight, body composition, and laboratory test results. Incorporating the subjective experiences of patients is essential. When inconsistencies between objective measures and subjective reports are identified, early recognition of potential psychological concerns is critical. These should be addressed through timely professional interpretation and supportive guidance.17

Promoting Patient Integration into Family and Society and Achieving Self-Actualization During the Home-Adaptation Phase

Findings from this study indicate that the implementation of HEN led to changes in family role dynamics and the division of responsibilities. These changes primarily manifested in several ways: the addition of caregiving tasks contributed to increased burden among primary caregivers, often resulting in role overload; where the patient served as the primary income earner, a financial crisis could be precipitated; and partial or complete substitution of the familial role of the patient diminished autonomy and increased reliance on specific family members. These outcomes appeared to be influenced by factors such as age, personality, occupation, and pre-illness lifestyle. It is therefore recommended that an assessment of the patient’s family care index be conducted during the discharge process and throughout the HEN period. Targeted interventions should be employed to support family engagement and strengthen emotional bonds, thereby promoting reintegration into the family environment.

In addition, in this study, the author found that 74% of patients receiving HEN did not experience a sense of freedom upon discharge. Some patients in this study demonstrated social anxiety, concealment of their illness, or a preference for transitioning from nasogastric to oral nutrition. These behaviors are reflective of a psychological adjustment lag relative to physiological adaptation. Difficulties in accepting the illness identity were observed to contribute to anxiety regarding social reintegration. The role of broader society is also significant in supporting the reintegration of individuals receiving HEN. Despite recognizing the therapeutic effectiveness of HEN, many patients reported that the visibility of a nasogastric tube continued to negatively affect self-image, particularly during reentry into educational or occupational environments.

From these findings, it is recommended that healthcare professionals maintain patient dignity through the use of empowerment-based communication. Language that may be perceived as overly protective should be avoided, and patient autonomy should be respected, including in areas such as the adjustment of nutrition plans. Implementation of individualized empowerment strategies is advised to enhance the sense of control and accomplishment of patients. Additionally, public awareness efforts, including media advocacy, may contribute to reducing societal stigma toward individuals receiving enteral nutrition.

Conclusion

From the patient perspective and longitudinal tracking, we identified distinct stage-specific and multidimensional dynamic changes in the care needs of patients receiving HEN, with each phase encompassing multiple domains. These findings are consistent with the core principle of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, which highlights the progression from lower-level survival needs to higher-level developmental needs, and provide a foundation for healthcare professionals to develop a demand-oriented, stepwise model of care. Although the sample size (n = 15) and the recruitment period are appropriate for a longitudinal qualitative design based on data saturation, the transferability of the findings may be limited by the single-center nature of the study and the age restriction (18–65 years), which reduces representativeness relative to the typical HEN population.
