Mitigating rice chalkiness while ensuring grain quality and seed vigor
Authors: Dongsheng Zhao, Nese Sreenivasulu, Qiaoquan Liu
Published: 2026

Main text

As the third-most-produced crop globally, rice (Oryza sativa) is predominantly cultivated and consumed in Asia. Rice chalkiness typically arises from incomplete endosperm development, in which loosely packed starch and protein granules form air spaces that scatter light, thereby producing a white, opaque region (Figure 1). Based on its position within the grain, chalkiness is classified as white core, white belly, or white back. Endosperm development in rice is described as grain filling, a process involving the accumulation of starch, proteins, and other nutrients, with starch constituting the major component. The physiological basis of chalkiness is often linked to insufficient nutrient accumulation, reflecting an imbalance in the source–sink relationship (Liu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025). Chalkiness is a complex trait primarily governed by genetic factors. Several genes with natural allelic variation, such as Chalk9, Chalk5, and WCR1, have been cloned to date (Zhao et al., 2022). Rice chalkiness influences grain quality

Rice varieties are developed for distinct purposes in modern breeding, including eating and cooking rice, industrial processing, and animal feed. Among these, eating rice accounts for the largest share of rice demand. The grain quality of eating rice is evaluated based on four aspects: appearance, taste, milling quality, and nutritional quality. Chalkiness has long been regarded as a defect in grain appearance and is also associated with reduced milling quality and taste (Figure 1). Therefore, chalkiness is often used as an indicator of grain quality, and lower chalkiness generally increases the commercial value of rice (Zhao et al., 2022). Although the phenotypic correlation between chalkiness and grain quality traits is well documented, a clear genetic link between them has not yet been identified. Head rice yield is an important indicator of milling quality. Recently, a transcriptional activator, OsMYB99, was found to exert dual beneficial effects by simultaneously increasing head rice yield and reducing chalkiness. This finding provides a potential target for the coordinated improvement of milling and appearance quality (Yu et al., 2025).

Rice chalkiness in industrial processing

The grain quality requirements for rice used in industrial processing and animal feed differ markedly depending on their specific end uses. Industrial processing quality refers to the suitability of rice for products such as flour, noodles, brewing, and other food products. In most cases, chalkiness reduces the economic value for manufacturers and is therefore considered undesirable (Figure 1). However, in certain situations, chalky grains are preferred. For example, sake is a traditional Japanese rice wine. Rice varieties suitable for sake brewing are characterized by unique grain features, including a high proportion of white-core grains. Several genomic regions exhibiting differentiation between eating rice and sake-brewing rice have been identified, suggesting targeted selection during breeding. The OsMnS gene increases the proportion of white-core grains, a desirable trait for sake brewing despite its negative impact on grain appearance quality. In addition, OsWOX9D increases grain width, another morphological trait favored in sake brewing (Yoshida et al., 2023). Breeding of sake rice aims to produce larger grains with white cores by altering the route of grain filling, rather than its rate, thereby minimizing negative impacts on grain yield. In line with this, grain shape and chalkiness are tightly linked: larger grains tend to exhibit higher levels of chalkiness. It is generally believed that grain shape influences chalkiness by altering nutrient transport routes within the grain. Therefore, modifying grain shape has become one of the most effective approaches for improving chalkiness (Zhao et al., 2022).

Rice chalkiness in seed quality

Rice grains serve not only as a staple food but also as seeds for crop production. High-quality seeds are essential for maximizing crop productivity. Seed quality is mainly characterized by varietal purity, seed lot quality, and seed vigor. Among these factors, seed vigor is a key determinant of seed germination rate, seedling growth, and stress resistance. High seed vigor enables rapid and uniform germination and seedling establishment in the field, which plays a decisive role in modern direct-seeded rice cultivation (Zhao et al., 2021).

Seed vigor is influenced by a range of genetic, physical, and environmental factors. Numerous quantitative trait loci and genes associated with rice seed vigor have been identified across different developmental stages, including seed development, storage, and germination. Specifically, these genetic loci are associated with nutrient reserves, seed size, and seed dormancy during development, lipid peroxidation during storage, and reserve utilization, signaling pathways, stress tolerance, and seed treatments during germination (Zhao et al., 2021). Currently, research on factors influencing rice seed vigor mainly focuses on seed dormancy and germination, whereas other contributing factors remain less studied.

During development, rice seeds accumulate large amounts of nutrients. As noted above, most chalkiness results from abnormal nutrient accumulation; notably, premature degradation of storage reserves has not been reported (Figure 1). Interestingly, japonica rice typically exhibits lower levels of chalkiness but poorer seed vigor, whereas indica rice generally shows higher chalkiness but stronger seed vigor. However, the genetic link between chalkiness and seed vigor remains unclear in seed biology.

Recently, a molecular module regulating α-amylase levels in the endosperm was reported to influence starch deposition, increasing chalkiness and seed vigor. Specifically, OsTPS8, which encodes a trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS), was cloned with naturally occurring variation. OsTPS8 interacts with OsTPS1 and inhibits its enzymatic activity, thereby reducing the synthesis of trehalose-6-phosphate (Tre6P) (Chen et al., 2025). Tre6P acts as a sugar signaling molecule that is synthesized by TPS and hydrolyzed by trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase. Most identified TPS and trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase genes regulate seed germination and seedling stress resistance, whereas their roles in grain development remain less well understood. Tre6P interacts with and inhibits the central energy regulator SnRK1, forming a signaling loop that promotes sugar allocation to sink organs and thereby enhances starch synthesis during endosperm development (Figure 1) (Li et al., 2022). Tre6P signaling can be modulated through both genetic and chemical manipulations to improve crop yield under different environmental conditions (Li et al., 2022; Griffiths et al., 2025).

Conversely, reduced Tre6P levels relieve the repression of OsMYBS1 expression, a downstream positive regulator of α-amylase genes, thereby increasing α-amylase accumulation and accelerating starch degradation in the endosperm (Chen et al., 2025). Similarly, seed invigoration treatments are employed in agricultural production to induce the accumulation of hydrolases (e.g., amylases) and antioxidants (Farooq et al., 2009). Consequently, OsTPS8 regulates both chalkiness and seed germination by modulating α-amylase activity.

Moreover, the team identified a SNP that affects the binding of OsbHLH001 to the OsTPS8 promoter, resulting in differential OsTPS8 expression between indica and japonica rice. The high-expression OsTPS8IR24 haplotype, which is associated with increased chalkiness and rapid seed germination, was strongly selected during indica domestication. In contrast, japonica retains the low-expression OsTPS8ASO haplotype, which is also present in wild rice. Importantly, the selection of OsTPS8 in indica was likely driven not by human preference for high chalkiness but by its association with enhanced seed vigor, which enables indica rice to better adapt to the hot and humid conditions of low-latitude regions and to meet the requirements of modern direct-seeded rice cultivation. Although the accompanying high chalkiness is undesirable for the grain quality of eating rice, it may have been tolerated as a secondary trait during domestication or breeding (Chen et al., 2025).

Concluding remarks

Rice grain chalkiness now extends far beyond a simple appearance trait and is linked to multiple aspects of grain and seed quality. OsTPS8 regulates the trade-off between grain chalkiness and seed vigor in rice. Notably, the observed chalkiness results from increased starch degradation mediated by elevated amylase activity rather than from insufficient nutrient accumulation (Chen et al., 2025). Chalkiness itself does not improve seed vigor; on the contrary, it generally correlates with poor seed vigor. However, moderate mobilization of seed reserves can promote embryo development and thereby benefit crop production. Consequently, the breeding target for chalkiness is shifting from simply achieving “the lowest possible level” toward defining an “end-use-specific optimum.” Rational utilization of key regulators to obtain primary benefits while accepting manageable trade-offs represents a viable strategy in modern breeding. Alternatively, overcoming such trade-offs could enable coordinated trait improvement. For example, pyramiding genes that confer slender grain shape is expected to mitigate grain chalkiness while maintaining high seed vigor.
