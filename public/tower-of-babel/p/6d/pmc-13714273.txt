Explainable foundation model for dementia screening and risk stratification using retinal fundus images
Authors: Changho Han, Jaewon Kim, Hyeokjong Lee, Hyunwoo Joo, Jooyoung Chang, Sang Min Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia is a growing global health challenge, and early identification is essential for timely intervention. We evaluated whether foundation model–based deep learning of retinal fundus photographs can detect dementia and predict future dementia incidence. Using health checkup data from over 36,000 Korean individuals, dementia was defined by diagnosis with relevant medication. Five vision foundation models were assessed with multiple fine-tuning strategies. The best-performing model, RETFound-MAE with partial fine-tuning, achieved an AUROC of 0.750 for dementia detection and a C-index of 0.812 for future incidence prediction. Model outputs remained independent risk factors after adjustment (adjusted odds ratio, 1.155; adjusted hazard ratio, 1.045). Quantitative saliency analyses highlighted biologically plausible regions, primarily the optic disc and adjacent peripapillary areas. These findings suggest that foundation model–based analysis of routinely acquired retinal fundus images may provide a scalable and interpretable approach for opportunistic dementia risk stratification, warranting validation in ethnically diverse populations.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Risk factors

Introduction

Dementia is a major global health challenge with profound social and economic consequences. As populations age, its prevalence is projected to rise from 57 million in 2019 to 152 million by 20501. Nearly half of cases are considered preventable or postponable through modification of lifestyle and health-related risk factors2. For Alzheimer’s disease (AD), several symptomatic medications are available, and recently approved disease-modifying therapies show promise in slowing progression3–5. Thus, early identification of dementia or individuals at high risk is crucial for timely intervention and risk reduction.

However, dementia screening remains complex and costly, as no single definitive diagnostic test exists. Current approaches typically combine clinical assessments, cognitive testing, and biomarker evaluation through modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis3,6. Thus, simpler, more accessible, and lower-cost screening strategies are highly desirable.

Retinal fundus imaging is a widely available, low-cost modality in primary care and community settings, making it a practical tool for population-level screening. The retina, an accessible extension of the central nervous system, shares its developmental origin, anatomy, and physiology with the brain7. Its microvasculature and neural components mirror those of the brain: retinal vessels reflect cerebral small vessels, while retinal neurons, glial cells, and optic nerve axons connect directly to central structures7–10. These parallels suggest that brain neurodegeneration may manifest as retinal structural or functional changes, positioning the retina as a promising, noninvasive window for dementia screening.

Deep learning algorithms have achieved remarkable success in detecting both ocular diseases (e.g., diabetic retinopathy11, glaucoma12) and systemic conditions (e.g., systemic biomarkers13, cardiovascular disease14) from retinal images. However, their development has traditionally been constrained by the need for large annotated datasets and substantial computational resources to train models from scratch. The emergence of large-scale foundation models—pre-trained on massive, diverse uncurated datasets—is transforming the field15–17. These models can be efficiently fine-tuned for specific clinical tasks using smaller datasets, thereby expediting the creation of robust, clinically meaningful artificial intelligence (AI) applications18,19.

This study aimed to evaluate whether foundation model–based deep learning applied to retinal fundus photographs can detect dementia in terms of screening utility. We further examined whether the model’s outputs serve as independent risk factors after adjustment for established clinical variables, and whether they also provide prognostic value in terms of risk stratification for predicting future dementia incidence. Finally, saliency maps were used to identify and visualize the retinal regions most influential to model predictions, alongside anatomical segmentation for quantitative assessment.

Results

Study population and characteristics

This retrospective cohort included individuals who underwent routine health examinations, including nonmydriatic retinal fundus photography, at the Health Promotion Center of Seoul National University Hospital (HPC-SNUH) between November 2004 and December 2016. Dementia status was ascertained using National Health Insurance Service claims data and was defined by relevant International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes combined with anticholinesterase prescription records. Among 54,129 health checkup visits (108,008 images; 36,322 individuals), 1442 visits (2868 images; 1001 individuals) had a dementia diagnosis before or within two years of the index fundus examination and were defined as dementia-positive cases for the detection task (Fig. 1A). This case definition was intended to reflect a screening-oriented dementia detection setting that captures both clinically recognized dementia and near-term diagnoses likely representing underlying but clinically unrecognized disease at the time of imaging. These positive-case images were randomly matched with controls at a 1:4 ratio to construct the development dataset (14,340 images; 12,247 visits; 10,448 individuals), which was divided into training (8591 images; 7342 visits; 6268 individuals), validation (2892 images; 2462 visits; 2090 individuals), and test (2857 images; 2443 visits; 2090 individuals) sets using stratified random sampling without patient overlap. The future dementia incidence cohort included 32,352 visits (64,874 images; 25,874 individuals), with an incidence rate of 6.4 per 1000 person-years. The median follow-up duration was 5.51 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 2.79–9.00). Of the 64,874 included images, 2435 (3.8%) reached the endpoint of dementia diagnosis, 50,809 (78.3%) were censored at the study end (December 31, 2018), and 11,630 (17.9%) were censored at 10 years after the index examination.

In the development dataset, the median age was 54 years (IQR 46–60) in dementia-negative and 66 years (IQR 57–73) in dementia participants (P < 0.001; Table 1). The proportion of males was lower in the dementia group than in controls (41.8% vs 52.7%, P < 0.001). The CAIDE (Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Dementia) risk score distribution also differed: dementia participants more often had higher scores, with 37.6% scoring 6–7 and 9.7% scoring ≥8, versus 27.9% and 7.6% among dementia-negative participants (P < 0.001). In the future-incidence cohort, the median age was 53 years (IQR 45–60), 51.6% were male, and CAIDE scores were 59.3% for 0–5, 24.6% for 6–7, and 6.8% for ≥8.

Performance and clinical utility

We evaluated five vision foundation models under three fine-tuning strategies. The performance of each model and corresponding logistic regression results for dementia detection are shown in Table 2. Across all combinations of foundation models and fine-tuning methods, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) ranged from 0.695 to 0.750. The RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning achieved the highest performance (AUROC = 0.750; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.726–0.774). In comparison, the CAIDE score, calculated without individual-level education data, yielded an AUROC of 0.624 (95% CI, 0.602–0.648). Within the test set, 2572 images (2210 visits; 1914 individuals) with complete data were included in the logistic analyses. For interpretability, model outputs (originally 0–1) were rescaled to 0–10 by multiplying by ten, allowing adjusted odds ratios (ORs) in Table 2 to represent the odds change per 10% absolute increase in output. In the classifier-only fine-tuning setting, none of the models showed significant associations with dementia (all P > 0.05). In contrast, several partially fine-tuned or Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)-tuned models demonstrated significant associations. Among these, the Masked Autoencoder (MAE) model with LoRA tuning had the highest adjusted OR (1.161; 95% CI 1.027–1.313; P = 0.017), followed by the RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning (1.155; 95% CI 1.077–1.240; P < 0.001). Age and sex-adjusted results are shown in Supplementary Table S2, exhibiting similar trends.

Table 3 presents the predictive performance of the developed models for future dementia incidence. Across all combinations, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) ranged from 0.761 to 0.812. The RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning achieved the highest performance (C-index = 0.812; 95% CI 0.803–0.820). In comparison, the CAIDE score, calculated without individual-level education data, yielded a C-index of 0.689 (95% CI, 0.680–0.697). Because individual-level education data were unavailable, the education component of the CAIDE score was assigned 0 points in the primary analysis. Pairwise bootstrap comparisons with the CAIDE score are presented in Supplementary Table S3, although these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the education component of the CAIDE score was unavailable in the primary analysis.

Within the future incidence evaluation cohort, 58,869 images (29,522 visits; 23,817 individuals) with complete data were included in Cox proportional hazards analyses. As in the logistic regression analysis, model outputs were rescaled to 0–10. Among all models, only the RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning showed a statistically significant association, serving as an independent risk factor for future dementia incidence (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 1.045; 95% CI 1.015–1.076; P = 0.003). Age and sex-adjusted results (Supplementary Table S2) showed similar trends.

Collectively, these findings indicate that the RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning was the best-performing model for dementia detection and provided the strongest prognostic value for future incidence. Specifically, it achieved an AUROC of 0.750 (95% CI 0.726–0.774) for dementia detection with an adjusted OR of 1.155 (95% CI 1.077–1.240; P < 0.001). For dementia incidence prediction, it achieved a C-index of 0.812 (95% CI 0.803–0.820) and an adjusted HR of 1.045 (95% CI 1.015–1.076; P = 0.003).

In the sensitivity analysis using CAIDE scores with imputed education components, the overall findings remained consistent with those of the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). The best-performing RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning remained significantly associated with both dementia detection and future dementia incidence, with an adjusted OR of 1.153 (95% CI, 1.074–1.238; P < 0.001) and an adjusted HR of 1.057 (95% CI, 1.026–1.088; P = 0.003), respectively. In the sensitivity analysis excluding prevalent dementia cases and retaining only short-term incident cases diagnosed within two years after the index fundus examination among dementia-positive cases, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S6). The best-performing RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning showed robust performance and a significant association with dementia detection (AUROC, 0.768 [95% CI, 0.731–0.805]; adjusted OR, 1.154 [95% CI, 1.030–1.293; P = 0.013]). Additional robustness analyses using the best-performing RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning further supported the primary findings (Supplementary Table S7). These analyses included retraining under a prevalent-case-only definition, retraining using the full unmatched dataset, temporal validation with later-year testing, and individual-level evaluation using averaged bilateral-eye scores. Across these analyses, the model consistently demonstrated robust discrimination and significant associations with dementia detection, and analyses that included future incidence evaluation also showed consistent prognostic performance.

Decision curve analysis provided an additional assessment of threshold-dependent clinical utility for dementia detection (Supplementary Figure S1). The RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning showed greater net benefit than the CAIDE score, screen-all strategy, and screen-none strategy across all threshold probabilities. Among test set participants with complete CAIDE component data, the combined logistic regression model integrating the best-performing model’s output with individual CAIDE component variables demonstrated a significantly higher AUROC than the RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning alone (0.774 [95% CI: 0.748–0.797] vs. 0.749 [95% CI: 0.723–0.773]; ΔAUROC 0.025 [95% CI: 0.013–0.037], P = 0.001).

Model explainability

Explainability was assessed using vision transformer (ViT) attention rollout saliency maps20. Saliency analyses were conducted among true-positive (TP) cases, defined using model-specific validation-set thresholds based on the maximum Youden J index. Saliency was then quantified across segmented retinal anatomical regions, including arteries, veins, vessels, and the optic disc21. Table 4 and Supplementary Table S8 present the relative saliency intensity ratios among anatomical regions for dementia detection across models. Classifier-only fine-tuning models, which showed inferior performance and clinical utility, were excluded from the explainability evaluation. Compared with the reference region (non-vessel, non-optic disc [OD]), all models showed significantly higher saliency in the OD region, while saliency patterns in arteries, veins, and combined vessels varied across models. Overall, the OD exhibited the highest relative saliency ratio among anatomical regions in most models. For the best-performing model, RETFound-MAE with partial fine-tuning, the relative saliency ratios were 1.22 (95% CI 1.20–1.24) for arteries, 1.34 (95% CI 1.31–1.36) for veins, 1.28 (95% CI 1.26–1.30) for vessels (artery ∪ vein), and 5.72 (95% CI 5.35–6.11) for the OD.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that a foundation model-based deep learning approach using retinal fundus photographs can effectively detect dementia and, moreover, that the same model provides prognostic value for predicting future dementia incidence—highlighting its dual potential for dementia screening and risk stratification. Among various foundation models and fine-tuning strategies, the RETFound-MAE model with partial fine-tuning achieved the highest performance (AUROC = 0.750 for dementia detection; C-index = 0.812 for future dementia prediction), consistently outperforming the CAIDE score in both tasks. The model output also served as an independent risk factor: for dementia detection, the adjusted OR was 1.155 (95% CI 1.077–1.240; P < 0.001) per 10% increase in model score, while for dementia prediction, the adjusted HR was 1.045 (95% CI 1.015–1.076; P = 0.003). These findings were consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, including education-imputed CAIDE adjustment, alternative dementia case definitions, full unmatched dataset training, temporal validation, and individual-level bilateral-eye score averaging. Furthermore, saliency mapping with anatomical segmentation revealed biologically plausible regions of focus—particularly around the OD—suggesting that the model captures subtle structural signals associated with dementia pathology.

Our study underscores the transformative potential of foundation models in medical imaging. Foundation models, pre-trained on large-scale general or domain-specific datasets, provide rich feature representations adaptable to new tasks with relatively small datasets15–17. Recent studies have shown that foundation-model-based pipelines can be optimized in other clinical domains—for example, work on osteoporosis prediction from chest radiographs19 and on carotid atherosclerosis prediction from fundus photographs18 has demonstrated that multidimensional evaluation of performance, clinical utility, and explainability can effectively guide the adaptation of foundation models in clinical applications. Building on these advances, our framework extends this line of work by systematically comparing multiple foundation models and fine-tuning strategies to identify the most effective approach for dementia-related tasks. The superior performance of RETFound-MAE with partial fine-tuning demonstrates that leveraging domain-specific representations from retinal pretraining, while selectively updating higher-level layers, enables effective transfer to downstream tasks even with limited labeled data. This paradigm may accelerate the development of AI tools for a wide range of clinical applications.

Previous AI-based retinal imaging studies have examined dementia-related outcomes using fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT)/OCT angiography (OCTA), or multimodal retinal imaging (Supplementary Table S10). Prior fundus photography studies have mainly addressed AD classification, CAIDE-risk estimation, or cognitive impairment screening, whereas OCT/OCTA and multimodal studies have used retinal layer or microvascular information to classify AD or mild cognitive impairment (MCI)22–28. However, many of these studies were case-control or cross-sectional analyses, focused on symptomatic AD/MCI or risk-score estimation rather than future incident dementia, or required specialized imaging beyond standard fundus photography. In contrast, the present study used routine fundus photographs from a large health-checkup cohort, thereby reflecting an opportunistic screening context, and systematically optimized foundation model adaptation across multiple models and fine-tuning strategies while evaluating both dementia detection and longitudinal risk stratification.

The application of AI to retinal fundus imaging shows potential as an accessible approach for dementia-related risk assessment. Unlike neuroimaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography, as well as cerebrospinal fluid analysis—which remain diagnostic standards but are costly, invasive, and require specialized expertise3,6—retinal fundus imaging is widely available in routine health checkups and ophthalmology clinics. Moreover, with the growing availability of smartphone-based and portable fundus cameras29,30, integrating these modalities with AI could further expand opportunities for risk assessment in community and primary care settings. In this study, AI-applied fundus imaging showed meaningful performance for both cross-sectional dementia detection and longitudinal risk prediction and served as an independent risk factor after adjustment for established clinical variables. These findings suggest that routinely collected retinal fundus images may provide accessible information relevant to dementia risk.

The most clinically relevant use of the present model may be as an opportunistic risk-stratification signal within routine fundus imaging workflows. The best-performing model, RETFound-MAE with partial fine-tuning, achieved moderate discrimination for dementia detection; at the selected threshold, its sensitivity of 62.5% and positive predictive value of 36.3% indicate that positive predictions should be interpreted as prompts for further evaluation rather than diagnostic conclusions. Thus, when applied to retinal fundus images already obtained during routine health checkups or ophthalmology visits, a high-risk model output could prompt cognitive assessment, clinical follow-up, or further confirmatory evaluation. This opportunistic use may help identify individuals warranting further evaluation before substantial cognitive decline becomes clinically evident. Furthermore, the model may complement existing clinical risk stratification tools, as our analysis showed that combining the best-performing model output with CAIDE component variables demonstrated incremental discriminative value beyond the model alone. Clinical acceptability will depend on the target population, dementia prevalence, acceptable trade-offs between missed cases and false-positive referrals, downstream confirmatory testing pathways, patient burden, and cost-effectiveness. Although decision curve analysis showed greater net benefit of the best-performing model than the CAIDE score, screen-all strategy, and screen-none strategy across all threshold probabilities, formal cost-effectiveness evaluation would require additional health economic data and was beyond the scope of this study. Therefore, further external validation and health economic evaluation are needed to determine real-world clinical utility before clinical implementation.

The biological plausibility of using retinal fundus images for dementia screening and risk stratification is grounded in shared developmental and structural features of the retina and brain7. Both originate from the neural tube neuroectoderm and share vascular and neuronal architectures, making the retina a potential window into cerebral pathology7,31. Previous OCT and postmortem histopathologic studies have consistently shown peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning, including the temporal and inferior quadrants, as well as the macular RNFL, in patients with AD and MCI, reflecting axonal loss and trans-synaptic degeneration associated with cerebral neurodegeneration32–38. Longitudinal studies have further demonstrated that reduced RNFL thickness predicts future dementia onset or cognitive decline, indicating that retinal thinning is not only reflective of concurrent brain pathology but may also serve as a preclinical biomarker of future dementia development39,40. OD pallor—papillary paleness secondary to axonal loss and perfusion changes—has also been reported in AD41.

Our saliency analysis mirrored these pathophysiological insights: the model consistently highlighted the OD, especially the temporal rim and papillomacular bundle, with additional saliency in the inferior peripapillary region. These saliency distributions were largely consistent between the dementia detection (cross-sectional) and prediction (longitudinal) tasks, aligning with prior evidence that retinal structural alterations in these regions are not only indicative of concurrent brain pathology but may also serve as preclinical biomarkers of dementia. These regions encompass RNFL pathways connecting the macula and OD, as well as inferior arcuate fibers projecting toward the disc. Because RNFL axons converge at the optic nerve head to form the optic nerve, dementia-related axonal loss may manifest as structural changes in the OD, particularly along the temporal rim where the papillomacular bundle inserts. While such alterations can be characterized using OCT, retinal fundus photography offers a more cost-effective, rapid, and widely accessible modality for large-scale screening. Such subtle alterations, largely imperceptible to the human eye during retinal examination, may nonetheless be captured from retinal fundus images by deep learning–based models. Thus, fundus-based models emphasizing the OD and adjacent peripapillary areas may plausibly capture signals of dementia-related neuroaxonal degeneration, consistent with prior structural and histopathologic evidence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population comprised individuals undergoing voluntary health checkups at a single tertiary hospital in Korea and was expected to be almost entirely Korean, which may limit generalizability to populations with different ethnic, socioeconomic, healthcare, or clinical characteristics. Health checkup participants may differ from general population-based screening cohorts in health awareness, healthcare access, socioeconomic and educational status, comorbidity profiles, and baseline dementia prevalence. In addition, retinal anatomy, OD appearance, and disease patterns may vary across ethnic groups, and all fundus photographs were obtained within a single-center imaging environment; therefore, performance may vary in ethnically diverse populations or when applied to different fundus cameras, imaging protocols, or clinical settings. Although temporal validation partially assessed robustness across examination periods, external validation in independent institutions, ethnically diverse populations, and different imaging systems is required before broad clinical deployment. Second, dementia diagnosis was based on administrative claims data using ICD-10 codes and medication records, which may be prone to misclassification or delayed diagnosis. Because our case definition required anticholinesterase prescriptions, milder untreated cases may have been excluded, potentially introducing selection bias. To approximately characterize dementia subtypes, we examined ICD-10 codes among individuals diagnosed with dementia and found that 55.3% were classified as having AD (F00 or G30), while the remaining cases were classified as other dementia subtypes. However, claims data alone cannot reliably distinguish dementia etiologies without detailed neuroimaging, biomarker assessment, or clinical adjudication. Mixed dementia subtypes may have affected model performance and interpretation, and the biological plausibility of OD and RNFL–related findings, supported mainly by prior AD literature, may not apply uniformly to all subtypes. Future studies with clinically adjudicated dementia subtypes are needed to evaluate subtype-specific retinal imaging signals. Third, education level—an important determinant of dementia risk and a component of the CAIDE score—was unavailable. Because lower educational attainment contributes additional CAIDE points, omitting this component may have underestimated CAIDE performance and limited the fairness of direct comparisons with the retinal AI model. Although we performed a sensitivity analysis using age- and sex-stratified Korean Population Census data to impute the education component and found consistent results, population-level imputation cannot replace individual-level education data. Future studies with directly measured education levels are needed for more rigorous comparison with CAIDE. Fourth, information on major ocular comorbidities, such as glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, and other retinal or optic nerve diseases, was unavailable. Therefore, we could neither exclude individuals with severe ocular conditions nor adjust for these comorbidities. Because such diseases may alter retinal and optic nerve morphology and are particularly prevalent in older, dementia-prone populations, residual confounding and potential bias cannot be ruled out. Future studies incorporating detailed ophthalmologic diagnoses and clinical data are needed to determine whether the observed associations remain robust after excluding or adjusting for major ocular comorbidities. Fifth, the saliency analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal evidence of dementia-specific retinal pathology. Saliency maps indicate image regions contributing to prediction, but do not prove that the model directly captured pathological changes rather than correlated features such as age or other clinical factors. Although AutoMorph is a previously validated retinal segmentation pipeline, separate manual validation of OD or vessel segmentation was not performed in our dataset, and segmentation errors may have affected quantitative saliency estimates. In addition, OCT-derived RNFL thickness, manual OD pallor grading, and quantitative vessel measurements were unavailable, precluding direct validation against structural retinal phenotypes. Future studies with multimodal retinal imaging and clinically validated retinal measurements are needed to confirm the biological interpretation of these findings.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that foundation model–based deep learning applied to retinal fundus images can detect dementia and predict its future incidence with performance exceeding established risk scores. Our study provides a framework for adapting foundation models to specific medical challenges, showing how domain-specific pretraining and efficient fine-tuning can be practically leveraged. Given that retinal fundus imaging is a rapid, noninvasive, cost-effective, and widely available modality, these findings suggest that AI-based retinal imaging could enable scalable, opportunistic screening and risk stratification for dementia in routine health checkups, facilitating earlier detection and preventive intervention. As the global dementia burden continues to rise, AI-driven approaches using accessible imaging modalities may play a pivotal role in advancing precision prevention, although validation in ethnically diverse populations is needed before broad deployment.

Methods

Ethics approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB approval number: H-1703-044-837). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. Data usage was limited to the approved study period and followed institutional anonymization protocols. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study subjects and data collection

This study included individuals who underwent comprehensive health examinations at the HPC-SNUH between November 2004 and December 2016 (Fig. 1A). HPC-SNUH offers elective checkups comprising questionnaires, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging modalities, with retinal fundus imaging included in all packages. Fundus photographs were acquired using a CR-2 digital nonmydriatic camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan)42 without pupil dilation, capturing one 45° color image per eye encompassing the OD and macula. Retinal images from both eyes and additional health checkup data—demographics (age, sex), blood tests (lipid profile), physical measures (weight, height, blood pressure), and survey responses (physical activity)—were extracted.

Dementia diagnoses were obtained from the National Health Insurance Service, which provides universal healthcare coverage to nearly the entire Korean population. Dementia was defined as the presence of ICD-10 codes F00–F03 or G30, with at least three outpatient visits or three or more days of hospitalization, and prescription of an anticholinesterase (tacrine, donepezil, rivastigmine, or galantamine). Subtypes of dementia could not be distinguished due to the lack of imaging data.

For the dementia detection task, cases were defined as those with a recorded diagnosis either before or within two years after the index fundus examination (Fig. 1A). This two-year window was prespecified as an operational short-term ascertainment window. This rationale was based on prior evidence that dementia-related pathophysiological and biomarker changes may begin years before clinical diagnosis, as well as reports that dementia diagnosis is often delayed after symptom onset43–46. Accordingly, this case definition was intended to reflect a screening-oriented detection setting that captures both clinically recognized dementia and near-term diagnoses likely representing underlying but clinically unrecognized disease at the time of imaging. Each image was treated as an independent observation, and images from dementia cases were randomly matched, at a 1:4 ratio, with control images from individuals who were free of dementia for at least 2 years after the index fundus examination. Controls were selected by random undersampling without matching on specific covariates. The 1:4 ratio was chosen to mitigate the extreme class imbalance caused by the much larger number of dementia-negative images while maintaining a feasible development dataset size47. The resulting model development dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets (6:2:2) via stratified random sampling without patient overlap; all reported performance metrics for dementia detection were calculated in the held-out test set.

To further assess whether the trained model could predict future dementia incidence, it was applied to a separate future incidence evaluation cohort comprising individuals who were free of dementia for at least 2 years after the index fundus examination and had not been used for model development, including training, validation, or test set construction. This design ensured that no individuals used for model development were included in the future incidence evaluation cohort, thereby extending the evaluation from a cross-sectional detection setting to a longitudinal prediction framework.

Foundation model-based development framework

Vision foundation models, typically based on ViT architectures48, are pre-trained on large-scale image datasets to learn generalizable visual representations, facilitating both broad and domain-specific applications in fields such as medical imaging. We employed five models as follows (Fig. 1B).

OpenCLIP49 is a dual-encoder image–text contrastive learning model trained on billions of image–caption pairs (e.g., LAION-2B) with a ViT image encoder, demonstrating strong zero-shot and transfer learning performance.

MAE50 is a ViT-based model trained using a self-supervised learning strategy that reconstructs randomly masked image patches via an asymmetric encoder–decoder architecture, enabling the learning of dense visual representations from large unlabeled datasets (e.g., ImageNet).

DINOv351 is a self-supervised vision foundation model (successor to DINOv217), trained on approximately 1.7 billion unlabeled images using a knowledge-distillation (student-teacher) framework with a ViT backbone, designed to learn high-quality global and local representations for a wide range of downstream transfer tasks.

RETFound-MAE16 is a retina-specific foundation model pre-trained using the MAE framework on 904,170 color fundus photographs and 736,443 OCT scans, with a ViT encoder, enabling efficient downstream adaptation for both ophthalmic and systemic disease prediction tasks.

RETFound-DINOv252 is a variant of the RETFound family that adopts the DINOv2 self-supervised learning paradigm (ViT backbone) within the retinal-image domain, aiming to achieve more discriminative feature representations, improved data and adaptation efficiency.

We evaluated different levels of fine-tuning by applying and comparing multiple strategies across the foundation models. Unlike the original RETFound study16, which used full fine-tuning of the pretrained encoder with a task-specific classification head for downstream adaptation, we adopted representative parameter-efficient or selective fine-tuning strategies—classifier-only training, partial fine-tuning, and low-rank adaptation53. This approach was intended to reduce the risk of catastrophic forgetting, in which pretrained representations are overwritten during downstream adaptation, while enabling efficient task-specific learning54. Our foundation model–based development framework aimed to identify the most effective combination of model and fine-tuning method by conducting assessments across three key dimensions—performance, clinical utility, and explainability (Fig. 1C). Three fine-tuning approaches were applied as follows.

Classifier-only training, also referred to as linear evaluation, trains only the final classification layer while keeping all backbone parameters frozen. This approach assesses the intrinsic quality and transferability of the pre-trained representations without altering the learned visual features.

Partial fine-tuning55,56 updates the upper transformer blocks and the classification head while keeping the remaining backbone layers frozen. This strategy leverages the hierarchical nature of visual feature learning, in which lower layers capture general features such as edges and textures, whereas later layers learn more task-specific representations.

LoRA57 inserts lightweight trainable rank-decomposition matrices into the multi-head attention blocks, enabling efficient parameter adaptation with minimal additional computational cost. Rather than updating the full model weights, LoRA optimizes a small number of additional parameters, facilitating flexible fine-tuning while maintaining the stability and efficiency of the pre-trained model.

Implementation details

Model training was performed using PyTorch Distributed Data Parallel58 across eight NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. For comparability across foundation models, we used Large-scale ViT backbone variants for all models while preserving each model’s original pretrained architecture and weights. All fundus images were resized to 448×448 pixels, and the binary cross-entropy loss was used for optimization. To improve generalization and reduce overfitting, data augmentation was applied only to the training set and included random resized cropping, resizing, AutoAugment59, and horizontal flipping. No augmentation was applied to the validation set, test set, or future evaluation cohort to ensure consistent evaluation. For classifier-only fine-tuning, training used a learning rate of 1 × 10−3, weight decay 1 × 10−6, batch size 32, and the AdamW optimizer. For partial fine-tuning and LoRA, the learning rate was 1 × 10−4, with the same weight decay, batch size 8, and AdamW. Partial fine-tuning was applied to the top four transformer blocks, while LoRA was performed across all transformer blocks with a rank of 4. The best model checkpoint was selected based on the highest validation AUROC; the learning rate was halved after five consecutive epochs without improvement in validation AUROC, and early stopping was applied after ten consecutive epochs without improvement. A reproducibility checklist summarizing these implementation details, including model architectures, preprocessing, augmentation, hyperparameters, model selection criteria, data splitting, and availability of code, is provided in Supplementary Table S11.

Performance and clinical utility evaluation

For the dementia detection task, model performance was evaluated using AUROC in the test set. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score were calculated at the threshold maximizing the Youden J index in the validation set. Performance was compared with the CAIDE risk score, a validated dementia prediction tool60. The CAIDE score includes age, sex, education, systolic blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), total cholesterol, and physical activity. Because individual-level education data were unavailable in our dataset, the education component of the CAIDE score could not be directly assigned and was therefore omitted by assigning 0 points for this component.

To examine whether the model output served as an independent risk factor for dementia presence, we performed multiple logistic regression analyses in the test set. Dementia diagnosis recorded before or within two years of the index fundus examination was defined as the outcome, and adjusted ORs for the model output were calculated after controlling for the variables included in the CAIDE score, thereby adjusting for established dementia risk factors. Unlike the CAIDE score calculation, age, systolic blood pressure, BMI, and total cholesterol were modeled as continuous variables. Individuals with missing values for any covariates were excluded from this analysis.

In the future incidence evaluation cohort, the trained model’s prognostic value for predicting dementia incidence was assessed using the C-index and compared with that of the CAIDE score. Adjusted HRs for incident dementia were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, controlling for CAIDE score variables. Individuals with missing covariate values were excluded. Follow-up began two years after the index fundus examination, and participants were observed until the earliest of dementia diagnosis, December 31, 2018, or 10 years after the index examination, whichever occurred first.

As a sensitivity analysis, we recalculated the CAIDE score after imputing the education component using age- and sex-stratified educational attainment distributions from the Korean Population Census provided by the Ministry of Data and Statistics, Republic of Korea61, and repeated the CAIDE comparison and covariate-adjusted regression analyses using this imputed score. To additionally evaluate short-term screening utility independent of prevalent dementia cases, we conducted a sensitivity analysis limited to model evaluation in the test set without retraining. In this analysis, prevalent dementia cases diagnosed on or before the index fundus examination date were excluded, and only short-term incident cases diagnosed within two years after the index examination were retained among dementia-positive cases. To further evaluate the robustness of the findings, we performed four additional sensitivity analyses using the best-performing model. First, the model was retrained and evaluated after restricting dementia cases to individuals diagnosed on or before the index fundus examination date. Second, the model was retrained and evaluated using the full unmatched dataset instead of the 1:4 matched development dataset. Third, temporal validation was performed by using data from 2004 to 2012 for model development, with individuals randomly split into training and validation sets at a 3:1 ratio, and data from 2013 to 2016 for testing. Individuals included in model development were excluded from the temporal test set to prevent data leakage. Fourth, to account for potential within-subject correlation arising from bilateral fundus images, we performed a sensitivity analysis at the individual level by averaging model scores across both eyes when bilateral images were available and repeating the performance and regression analyses using the averaged score.

Decision curve analysis was performed for the best-performing model in the dementia detection task to evaluate threshold-dependent clinical utility. Net benefit was calculated across a range of threshold probabilities and compared with the CAIDE score, screen-all strategy, and screen-none strategy. To enable direct comparison with the model output, the CAIDE score was min–max normalized to a 0–1 scale before decision curve analysis. To assess the incremental predictive value of combining the best-performing model with conventional risk factors, a logistic regression model incorporating the best-performing model’s output alongside individual CAIDE component variables was trained on the validation set, applied to the test set, and compared with the best-performing model alone.

Explainability evaluation

To assess and compare explainability across architectures and fine-tuning strategies, we generated class-specific saliency maps using a ViT attention rollout approach20. For each model, attention matrices from all transformer blocks were extracted and aggregated through gradient-weighted attention rollout, producing patch-level saliency maps resized to the original image resolution (448 × 448). For interpretability, saliency was summarized over TP cases, defined using model-specific thresholds that maximized the Youden J index on the validation split and were then applied to the test and future-incidence cohorts.

To quantify the spatial distribution of saliency, retinal structures—including arteries, veins, and the OD—were anatomically segmented using an external segmentation pipeline (AutoMorph)21 (Supplementary Fig. S4). A circular field-of-view (FOV) mask centered at the image midpoint—radius equal to half of the shorter image dimension—was applied to exclude peripheral background pixels outside the visible retinal field. Saliency values within the FOV were normalized to sum to 100%.

We first computed the centroid (cx,cy) of the OD mask by averaging the horizontal and vertical coordinates of all pixels within the mask. The mask was then partitioned by two diagonal lines intersecting at this centroid— y−cy=±(x−cx) —thereby dividing the OD region into four wedges corresponding to the four quadrants. Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and as all variables were non-normally distributed, group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, with results presented as median [IQR]. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. For model performance and explainability evaluations, 95% CIs were determined using 2000 bootstrap iterations (resampling with replacement).
