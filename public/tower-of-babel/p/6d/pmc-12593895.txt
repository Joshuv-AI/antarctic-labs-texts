Lower FT3 levels are associated with a poor prognosis in patients with acute type A aortic dissection: a retrospective study
Authors: Yuanyuan Tao, Yanchun Peng, Yaqin Chen, Hong Ni, Liangwan Chen, Yanjuan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid hormones are closely associated with cardiovascular disease and the use of thyroid hormones as prognostic indicators in cardiovascular disease patients has recently renewed interest. The purpose of this study was to investigate the prognostic value of free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels in patients with acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients who underwent AAAD surgery in Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between January 2020 and June 2024. The patients were divided into three groups (Tertile 1, Tertile 2, and Tertile 3) based on the tertiles of preoperative FT3 levels. The primary outcomes were in-hospital mortality and prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV). Logistic regression model was used to explore the effect of FT3 concentration on in-hospital mortality and PMV. Further subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of risk associations across different patient subgroups.ResultsThis study included a total of 511 patients, categorized into three FT3 tertiles. Compared to Tertile 3, patients in Tertile 1 and Tertile 2 were older, had a higher incidence of hypertension, elevated troponin levels, body mass index (BMI), a higher proportion of smokers, and lower levels of albumin, platelets, and hemoglobin (all P < 0.05). Compared to non-death and non-PMV patients, death and PMV patients had significantly lower FT3 levels. After multivariable adjustment, FT3 remained an independent predictor of both in-hospital mortality and PMV. For every 1 pmol/L increase in FT3, the in-hospital mortality rate decreased by 33.0% (OR = 0.670, P = 0.039), and the risk of PMV decreased by 33.3% (OR = 0.667, P = 0.008). Compared to Tertile 1, the adjusted OR for in-hospital mortality were 0.652 for Tertile 2 (P = 0.220) and 0.413 for Tertile 3 (P = 0.030). For PMV, the adjusted OR were 0.563 for Tertile 2 (P = 0.024) and 0.4502 for Tertile 3 (P = 0.011). Stratified analysis demonstrated that lower FT3 levels were consistently associated with higher in-hospital mortality across all subgroups.ConclusionLower FT3 levels are independently associated with in-hospital mortality and PMV in AAAD patients. These findings suggest that FT3 may serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker in AAAD patients, warranting further prospective validation studies.Keywords: Free triiodothyronine, Aortic dissection, Poor prognosis, In-hospital mortality, Prolonged mechanical ventilation

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD) is a common clinical emergency in the cardiovascular field, which is characterized by acute onset, rapid progress, and high mortality. If left untreated, the mortality rate for AAAD increases by 1% to 2% per hour [1]. Despite the continuous development of surgical technology, the mortality and postoperative complications of surgery are still high, and the postoperative mortality of patients is still 10–24% [2, 3]. Therefore, paying attention to screening risk factors for poor prognosis of AAAD has important clinical significance for its prevention and management.

The cardiovascular system is one of the target organs of thyroid hormones. Excessively high or low levels of thyroid hormones may cause cardiovascular disease or lead to poor prognosis in patients with cardiovascular disease [4]. The role of thyroid hormones as modifiable contributors to cardiovascular disease has been extensively studied. Recent findings suggest that low triiodothyronine (FT3) levels predict higher mortality in cardiovascular disease patients and may directly contribute to worse clinical outcomes [5–7]. In a 52-month follow-up investigation of 782 patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, He et al. [8] found that reduced FT3 levels were prevalent in this population and independently predicted sudden cardiac death (Hazard Ratio [HR] = 1.53, 95%CI 1.13–2.24, P < 0.01) and heart failure (HF) (HR = 3.87, 95%CI 2.91–4.98, P < 0.001). A study by Side Gao et al. [9] in acute myocardial infarction, patients also showed that FT3 was an independent predictor of death in myocardial infarction (MI) patients and was negatively associated with major adverse cardiovascular events.

Existing research provides limited evidence on the relationship between thyroid hormone levels and AAAD prognosis. Whether FT3 can effectively predict poor outcomes in AAAD patients remains uncertain. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the association between FT3 levels and clinical outcomes in AAAD patients, providing a promising predictor for assessing disease severity and prognostic risk in AAAD patients.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively reviewed medical records of AAAD cases treated at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital during the period from January 2020 to June 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows:(1) aortic dissection was diagnosed for the first time by CT angiography (CTA) or Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (2) age ≥ 18 years; and (3) onset time ≤ 2 weeks. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnancy; (2) history of thyroid or pituitary disease; (3) no thyroid hormone testing was conducted within 24 h of admission; (4) died within 48 h postoperatively; and (5) with chronic renal dysfunction, chronic liver dysfunction, or chronic HF. Chronic renal dysfunction is defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 for at least three months [10], or a diagnosis of chronic renal insufficiency according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Chronic liver dysfunction is defined as a Child–Pugh score of Class B or C [11]. The ethical committee of local ethics committee granted approval (approval number: 2019KY019) and this study followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Information on all patients was collected retrospectively by searching our clinical electronic database. The following data were collected: demographic data, laboratory data, and clinical data. Demographic data included age, gender, body mass index (BMI). Laboratory data including preoperative white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), platelets, hemoglobin (Hb), FT3, free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels. Clinical data included smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, Marfan’s syndrome, and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time. The definition of smoking is at least one cigarette per day, lasting for more than 6 months [12]. The primary outcomes of this study were in-hospital mortality and prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV). In this study, in-hospital mortality was defined as all-cause mortality during hospitalization and PMV was defined as a mechanical ventilation length of more than 48 h. In addition, acute kidney injury (AKI), postoperative stroke, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and ICU readmission were collected.

Thyroid hormone levels in this study were measured within 24 h of admission. Serum TSH, FT3, and FT4 were measured by chemiluminescence using DXI 800 automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The normal reference range was as follows: FT3 (3.53–7.37 pmol/L), FT4 (7.86–21.1 pmol/L), and TSH (0.56–5.91 mIU/L). In this study, FT3 levels were divided into three equal groups (tertiles) from highest to lowest, and patients were categorized accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v26.0; IBM Corp) and the R statistical package (http://www.R-project.org). Participants were stratified by FT3 tertiles for baseline characterization. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, non-normal variables as median (IQR). Inter-group comparisons employed ANOVA (normal distribution) or Kruskal–Wallis test (non-normal distribution). Categorical variables are expressed as counts (%) and were analyzed using χ2 tests. Logistic regression analysis was used to explore the relationship between FT3 levels and primary outcomes, and Cochran–Armitage trend tests were performed to explore the stability of this relationship. Furthermore, stratified analyses and interaction tests were performed to evaluate potential modifications in the associations between FT3 and mortality, as well as PMV, using multivariate logistic regression models.

Results

This retrospective study analyzed 528 consecutive AAAD surgical cases from our institutional database. After excluding 17 patients (2 pregnant, 4 with pre-existing thyroid disorders, 7 lacking thyroid hormone data, and 4 postoperative deaths within 48 h), the final cohort comprised 511 patients (Fig. 1).

When FT3 was considered as a continuous variable, in Model I, FT3 levels were independently associated with both mortality and PMV. For each 1 pmol/L increase in FT3, the risk of mortality decreased by 47.8% (OR = 0.522, 95%CI 0.375–0.728, P < 0.001), and the risk of PMV decreased by 33.3% (OR = 0.667, 95%CI 0.527–0.844, P < 0.001). In Model II, FT3 levels remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality and PMV. For every 1 pmol/L increase in FT3, the in-hospital mortality rate decreased by 34.7% (OR = 0.653, 95%CI:0.441–0.965, P = 0.033), and the risk of PMV decreased by 29.1% (OR = 0.709, 95%CI 0.534–0.941, P = 0.017). In Model III, FT3 levels remained independently associated with mortality and PMV. For every 1 pmol/L increase in FT3, the mortality rate decreased by 33.0% (OR = 0.670, 95%CI 0.452–0.993, P = 0.039), and the risk of PMV decreased by 33.4% (OR = 0.666, 95%CI 0.493–0.899, P = 0.008).

After categorizing patients into three groups based on FT3 levels. In Model I, compared to Tertile 1 group, the risk of in-hospital mortality was reduced by 47.2% (OR = 0.528, 95%CI 0.294–0.950, P = 0.033) and 69.4% (OR = 0.306, 95%CI 0.156–0.601, P = 0.001) in Tertile 2 and Tertile 3 groups, and the risk of PMV was reduced by 45.5% (OR = 0.545, 95%CI 0.354–0.939, P = 0.006) and 53.8% (OR = 0.462, 95%CI:0.300–0.712, P < 0.001), respectively. In Model II, the risk of mortality was reduced by 31.1% in Tertile 2 group compared to Tertile 1 group, although this reduction was not statistically significant (OR = 0.689, 95%CI:0.349–1.610, P = 0.284). The risk of in-hospital mortality was significantly decreased by 62.8% in Tertile 3 group (OR = 0.372, 95%CI 0.164–0.842, P = 0.018). Additionally, the risk of PMV was reduced by 42.8% (OR = 0.572, 95%CI 0.350–0.936, P = 0.026) and 48.4% (OR = 0.516, 95%CI 0.303–0.881, P = 0.015) in Tertile 2 and Tertile 3 groups, respectively. In Model III, the risk of mortality in Tertile 2 group was 34.8% lower compared to Tertile 1 group, although this difference was not statistically significant (OR = 0.652, P = 0.220). The risk of in-hospital mortality in Tertile 3 group was 58.7% lower (OR = 0.413, 95%CI 0.186–0.930, P = 0.030), and the risks of PMV were reduced by 43.7% (OR = 0.563, 95%CI 0.342–0.926, P = 0.024) and 49.8% (OR = 0.502, 95%CI 0.295–0.854, P = 0.011), respectively. Across all models, FT3 levels were independently associated with mortality and PMV, and trend analyses demonstrated that as FT3 levels increased, the risks of both mortality and PMV progressively decreased (P < 0.05).

We conducted a stratified analysis of the relationship between FT3 levels and mortality and PMV in AAAD patients based on potential moderating factors (Fig. 3). The results indicated that FT3 levels were negatively correlated with mortality across all subgroups. Stratified analysis of the association between FT3 levels and PMV revealed that in female patients and those age ≥ 60 years, FT3 levels were positively correlated with PMV, albeit this association was not statistically significant. In other subgroups, FT3 levels were negatively correlated with PMV. Furthermore, stratified analysis by age and BMI revealed significant interactions.

Discussion

Our study results show that among 511 patients with AAAD, there were 70 cases of in-hospital mortality (13.7%) and 242 cases of PMV (47.4%). Logistic regression analyses, incorporating FT3 as either a continuous or categorical variable, consistently support that FT3 levels are independent predictors of both in-hospital mortality and PMV in AAAD patients, with lower FT3 levels associated with higher risks of these outcomes. In addition, stratified analysis of FT3 levels and PMV in AAAD patients showed an interactive effect (Pfor interaction < 0.05) in age and BMI groups. Notably, this investigation represents the inaugural examination of the correlation between FT3 concentrations and PMV risk in AAAD patients. Furthermore, in this study, we excluded patients with chronic kidney disease, liver disease, or heart failure. Previous studies have demonstrated that these chronic conditions are often associated with thyroid hormone metabolism disorders, manifesting as decreased FT3 or fluctuations in thyroxine-binding globulin levels with abnormal thyroid hormone binding rates [13–15]. These pathological states can inherently cause systematic deviations in thyroid hormone profiles, thereby masking or amplifying the true effect of thyroid hormones on the prognosis of AAAD patients. Additionally, these chronic conditions are characterized by a persistent systemic inflammatory state (elevated IL-6 and TNF-α), which may introduce unmeasurable confounding variables.

Thyroid hormones play an important role in maintaining the homeostasis of cardiovascular. Cardiac muscle and vascular endothelium express thyroid hormone receptors, rendering the cardiovascular system highly responsive to fluctuations in thyroid hormone levels. Even minor alterations in these hormone concentrations can potentially exert adverse effects on cardiovascular function [7]. FT3 is closely related to the progression and outcome of cardiovascular disease, and patients with low FT3 tend to have a worse prognosis [16, 17]. Studies have confirmed that FT3 is involved in the expression of various structural and functional proteins in cardiomyocytes, and FT3 plays an important role in regulating heart rate, cardiac contractility, and peripheral arterial resistance [4, 18]. In contrast, emerging evidence suggests FT3 may exert unique protective effects in AAAD through direct modulation of vascular smooth muscle cell function, inflammatory cascades, and extracellular matrix metabolism [19–21]. FT3 has been shown to upregulate nitric oxide synthesis while suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, thereby mitigating oxidative injury to aortic endothelial and smooth muscle cells––a process central to ROS-induced membrane damage, inflammatory amplification, and matrix degradation [19, 20]. Furthermore, thyroid hormones exhibit immunomodulatory properties by differentially regulating macrophage activation and neutrophil function which may stabilize vascular immune homeostasis during acute aortic injury [21]. Conversely, low T3 states, often secondary to systemic inflammation, may exacerbate aortic wall damage via cytokine-mediated endothelial dysfunction [19, 22].

Hypothyroidism has been shown to be a known cause of ventilator-dependent respiratory failure [23, 24]. A study of 264 critically ill patients showed that FT3 was closely associated with mortality and was an independent risk factor for PMV [25], which is consistent with the findings of this study. Yasar et al.’s research [26] on COPD patients demonstrated that low FT3 levels could predicted the occurrence of PMV. Similarly, in cardiovascular disease, Shen et al.’s study of 118 cardiac surgery patients indicated that preoperative reductions in FT3 could serve as an effective predictor of PMV post-surgery [27]. Experiments have shown that thyroid hormone improves alveolar fluid clearance and reduces fibrin deposition by increasing the amount of Na+-K+-ATPase in alveolar type II cells [28, 29], and it demonstrates that thyroid hormone can prevent lung damage and promote the repair of lung damage, thus improving lung oxygenation in patients and reducing the duration of mechanical ventilation. In the age subgroup analysis results, the higher the FT3 level, the lower the risk of PMV in patients < 60 years. There was no statistical association between FT3 and PMV in patients > 60 years. The interaction test showed significant interaction (Pfor interaction = 0.006). There is heterogeneity in the role of FT3 among different age groups. This result suggests that FT3 levels have a significant protective effect on PMV risk in young patients, while this association is weak or even disappears in elderly patients. The specific reason may be related to age-related regulatory differences in the hypothalamic pituitary thyroid axis (HPT): the HPT axis of young patients remains highly sensitive to thyroid hormone feedback signals, allowing subtle changes in FT3 levels to directly regulate metabolic status and organ function, thereby reducing PMV risk [30]. However, elderly patients experience compensatory adaptive changes in the HPT axis, including an increase in TSH levels with age, a reset of thyroid hormone set points, and a decrease in pituitary responsiveness to thyroid hormone feedback [31, 32]. This physiological buffering mechanism significantly weakens the impact of FT3 level fluctuations on metabolism and organ function.

Although patients with AAAD as a whole are considered a high-risk population, there is significant heterogeneity in their short-term outcomes. Some patients recover well after surgery, while others face a higher risk of mortality or complications. Therefore, early risk assessment using objective and easily accessible biomarkers such as FT3 can help identify the truly high-risk subgroups of patients upon admission. It can also be combined with other biomarkers to better predict high-risk populations, thereby optimizing resource allocation and guiding individualized treatment decisions, such as more aggressive hemodynamic support and intensified monitoring. Considering the cardiovascular risks linked to low FT3 levels, FT3 supplementation has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy. For instance, animal studies have demonstrated that administering low-dose FT3 orally within 2 months following an acute myocardial infarction can enhance cardiac structure and function, reduce the incidence of tachyarrhythmias, and mitigate adverse left ventricular remodeling [33]. Pantos et al. [34] demonstrated that acute treatment with low-dose levothyroxine can enhance cardiac function in patients with MI. In a similar vein, Kumar et al. [35] reported that intravenous levothyroxine administration successfully reversed weaning difficulties in critically ill patients with unexplained hypotension. However, some research showed different results. A randomized placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that while FT3 supplementation in dilated cardiomyopathy patients with low FT3 levels led to improvements in left ventricular diastolic volume and stroke volume, it did not significantly alter external and intracardiac workload [36]. Similarly, Holmager et al.’s randomized controlled study [37] revealed that three months of oral FT3 treatment in HF patients failed to produce any discernible changes in cardiac function, end-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume, or cardiac output. Consequently, the therapeutic effectiveness, applicability across diverse patient populations, and safety profile of FT3 supplementation warrant further investigation through large-scale clinical trials in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center investigation, our findings require validation through multicenter studies. Second, thyroid hormone measurements were obtained only at admission without subsequent monitoring. Third, despite adjusting for a broad array of confounding factors, other unmeasured variables may still influence our results. Additionally, the influence of inflammatory status and nutritional factors on FT3 levels was not assessed, warranting future investigation into these complex relationships.

Conclusion

Lower FT3 levels are independently associated with mortality and PMV time in AAAD patients. Future research should confirm this association and consider clinical trials to test whether thyroid hormone supplementation can reduce poor outcomes in patients with AAAD.
