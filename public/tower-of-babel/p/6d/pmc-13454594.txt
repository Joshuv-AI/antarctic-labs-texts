A nucleus accumbens-projecting prefrontal cortex circuit underlies chronic social stress-induced depression-like behaviors
Authors: Xiaokuang Ma, Heedae Kim, Le Zhang, Peng Chen, Jing Wei, Yuehua Cui, Lishu Chen, Yuan Xu, John Zhang, Zhiyu Dai, Deveroux Ferguson, Shenfeng Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and debilitating psychiatric illness with limited treatment success. While chronic social stress is a well-established risk factor, the neural circuits that determine individual susceptibility or resilience to MDD remain poorly defined. Here, we identify a distinct subpopulation of layer 5 neurons in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) that project to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) that are selectively activated by chronic social defeat stress (cSDS) in male mice. Using activity-dependent genetic tagging (TRAP), retrograde tracing, and electrophysiology recordings, we demonstrate that these stress-responsive mPFC–NAc neurons exhibit circuit-specific synaptic dysfunction—characterized by reduced excitatory postsynaptic currents, impaired local excitatory connectivity, and dendritic spine loss—in mice susceptible to stress, but not in resilient individuals. Optogenetic modulation further reveals a causal role for this pathway: activation of the mPFC-NAc circuit during cSDS promotes resilience, whereas inhibition increases susceptibility to stress. Together, these findings define a functionally distinct corticolimbic circuit that regulates depression-like behaviors and highlight a potential target for circuit-based interventions in MDD.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent and debilitating psychiatric condition that poses a significant global public health burden. Despite extensive research and a range of pharmacological and psychotherapeutic options, treatment efficacy remains limited; more than half of patients fail to achieve full remission with currently available therapies [1–3]. This limited efficacy underscores the need to better understand the neural mechanisms underlying MDD, particularly those contributing to individual variability in vulnerability and response to environmental stressors.

Chronic social stress, particularly paradigms like chronic social defeat stress (cSDS), stands out as one of the most potent environmental risk factors precipitating depressive symptoms, as demonstrated in both human epidemiological data and well - established animal models of depression [4–6]. Stress-related mood disorders -including MDD - are among the most common psychiatric diagnoses and are often marked by persistent negative affect, social withdrawal, and impaired reward processing. Animal models have played a pivotal role in elucidating the biological underpinnings of stress-induced mood disorders. In particular, the rodent model of cSDS has emerged as one of the most robust and translationally relevant paradigms for modeling key features of human depression, such as anhedonia, social avoidance, and cognitive inflexibility [7–9].

Importantly, not all animals exposed to cSDS develop maladaptive behaviors. Similar to variability observed in human populations, a subset of mice subjected to cSDS exhibit depression-like behaviors (‘susceptible’), while others maintain normal behavior and are considered ‘resilient’ [8, 10]. These divergent outcomes suggest the presence of distinct neurobiological mechanisms underlying susceptibility versus resilience to stress. However, the circuit-level substrates that mediate this behavioral heterogeneity remain incompletely understood.

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is a key hub for emotional regulation and stress adaptation [9, 11]. It provides top-down control over subcortical structures, including the nucleus accumbens (NAc), a critical node in the mesolimbic reward system. Dysregulation of mPFC activity—particularly within layer 5 (L5) projection neurons—has been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression through both clinical imaging studies and preclinical models [12–14]. mPFC L5 neurons are heterogenous populations, in terms of their molecular marker, synaptic properties, projection targets, local circuit integration, and functional specialization [15–18]. Despite these insights, little is known about how specific populations within the mPFC adapt to stress exposure, and how these adaptations may differ across behavioral phenotypes.

In the present study, we investigate the circuit-level basis of stress susceptibility by examining how distinct social stress paradigms differentially engage mPFC projection neurons. We show that acute versus chronic social defeat stress recruits separable ensembles of mPFC neurons, suggesting a temporal and circuit-specific encoding of this stress modality. Using the TRAP activity-dependent labeling system [19], we found that cSDS preferentially activates mPFC neurons projecting to the NAc (mPFC→NAc), whereas acute social defeat stress (aSDS) more strongly engages projections to the dorsal striatum. This differential recruitment points to discrete circuit mechanisms underlying the brain’s response to acute versus prolonged stress exposure.

Further analyses revealed that in stress-susceptible mice, cSDS-activated (cSDS⁺) mPFC→NAc neurons exhibit marked disruptions in synaptic homeostasis. These include reductions in miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC) amplitude and frequency, as well as diminished dendritic spine density. In contrast, these alterations were absent in resilient mice and in neighboring, non-activated L5 neurons, indicating that the synaptic deficits are both circuit-specific and phenotype-dependent. Moreover, laser scanning photostimulation (LSPS) mapping revealed reduced intracortical excitatory input and an altered excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance in cSDS⁺ mPFC→NAc neurons from susceptible mice, further supporting the notion that chronic social stress disrupts local microcircuit homeostasis within behaviorally relevant ensembles.

Together, our findings define a functionally distinct corticolimbic circuit that mediates vulnerability to chronic social stress. By linking projection-specific neuronal ensembles in the mPFC to stress-induced behavioral outcomes, this work provides new insight into the cellular and synaptic mechanisms of susceptibility and resilience. Our results highlight the mPFC→NAc pathway as a promising target for developing circuit-based interventions for stress-related mood disorders, including MDD.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experiments were performed using male mice, including TRAP2 (JAX Stock #030323) and Ai14 (Rosa-CAG-LSL-tdTomato-WPRE, JAX Stock #007914) transgenic lines. Mice were 10 to 12 weeks old at the onset of behavioral testing. Animals were group-housed (3–5 per cage) under a standard 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00), with food and water provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Arizona and carried out in compliance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Acute and chronic social defeat stress

Protocols for acute and chronic social defeat stress (SDS) were adapted from established procedures [20]. Male double heterozygote TRAP2:Ai14 mice were used for both paradigms.

Acute SDS (aSDS)

For acute stress induction, mice received a single oral gavage of tamoxifen (TAM; 50 mg/kg) to enable activity-dependent labeling. One hour post-TAM administration, each mouse was placed into a large cage containing a pre-screened aggressive retired CD1 breeder for 5 min, during which it was subjected to repeated attacks. Following this physical interaction, the experimental mouse was separated from the aggressor by a perforated divider for 10 min of sensory contact. This cycle was repeated five times within the same day, with either the same or different aggressors, to model an acute social defeat experience.

Chronic SDS (cSDS)

For the chronic paradigm, experimental mice were exposed daily to a novel aggressive CD1 resident for 10 min of direct physical interaction, followed by 24 h of sensory contact in the same cage separated by a perforated Plexiglas divider. This procedure was repeated for 10 consecutive days, with a different aggressor used each day to prevent habituation. After the 10-d defeat protocol, mice were singly housed and assessed for stress response using a social interaction (SI) test, or forced swim test and tail suspension test.

Social interaction (SI) assay and classification

The SI test was used to classify mice as susceptible or resilient to cSDS. Each mouse was placed in a square open-field arena containing a perforated wire-mesh enclosure (interaction zone) for 2.5 min (no target phase), and time spent in the interaction zone was recorded using EthoVision tracking software. In the second phase, a novel C57BL/6 male mouse was placed inside the enclosure, and the test mouse was reintroduced to the arena for an additional 2.5 min (target phase). The average interaction time with the social target was calculated. The average interaction time with the social target was calculated. The classification of mice as susceptible or resilient is determined by the social interaction (SI) ratio [20], which is defined by the ratio of time spent in the interaction zone with social target over time in the interaction zone with no target. Susceptible was define as SI ratio < = 1.0, which resilient was defined as SI ratio > 1.0.

Forced swim test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST)

FST and TST were used to assess despair-like behavior, were performed as previously described. For FST, mice were individually placed in a 4-L beaker containing 3 L of water maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. Behavior was recorded and tracked using EthoVision for 5 min under red light illumination. Animals were analyzed for the duration of mobility and immobility during the testing period. Mice were tested at 1 week prior to the first session of cSDS as baseline, and also tested at 1 day after the last session of cSDS.

TST was performed as previously described [20, 21]. Each mouse was suspended by the tail using adhesive tape placed approximately 2 cm from the tip of the tail, with the animal positioned ~20 cm above the surface of a shelf. To prevent climbing behavior, the tail was passed through a small paper cylinder (1.5 cm diameter, 2 cm length) prior to suspension. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using EthoVision for a 5-min testing period under white light conditions. The duration of mobility and immobility was quantified during the test.

TRAP labeling of activated neurons

To label neurons activated during social defeat stress, TRAP2:Ai14 double heterozygote mice were used. In these mice, behaviorally activated neurons express tdTomato (tdT + ) following TAM-induced Cre-mediated recombination. For aSDS, mice were administered TAM (50 mg/kg, oral gavage) 1 h prior to the first defeat session. For cSDS, TAM was given 1 h before the final defeat session on day 10, to capture neuronal ensembles engaged during the terminal phase of repeated stress exposure, when susceptibility or resilience has already emerged. Our intent was to label neurons actively recruited by chronic stress rather than during early acquisition or single-episode stress.

Following behavioral testing, mice were returned to their home cages and allowed to recover for 7 to 14 d to permit robust tdT expression. Brains were subsequently collected for histological imaging and electrophysiological recordings.

Retrograde labeling

To identify projection-specific subpopulations of mPFC neurons, fluorescent retrobeads (‘beads’, Lumafluor) were stereotaxically injected into downstream target regions, including the NAc, ventral tegmental area (VTA), and dorsal striatum (dSTR). Injections were performed using a pulled glass micropipette with a beveled tip, connected to a micropressure injection system. The stereotaxic coordinates used relative to bregma were as follows: NAc (AP, +1.5 mm; ML, ±1.0 mm; DV, –4.6 mm), VTA (AP –3.30 mm; ML ± 0.50 mm; DV –4.6 mm), and dSTR (AP + 0.50 mm; ML ± 2.00 mm; DV –3.00 mm). Each site received a 50 nL injection of retrobeads. Following the procedure, mice were allowed to recover in their home cages, and recordings or histological analyses were performed at least 48 h later to allow for adequate retrograde transport and labeling of mPFC projection neurons. In a subset of experiments, we used GABA antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A2052, RRID:AB_477652) to co-label the tdT+ neurons to investigate whether inhibitory neurons can be TRAP’ed.

Whole-cell patch clamp recording

Whole-cell patch clamp recordings were performed on acute coronal brain slices containing the mPFC. Slices were sectioned in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) continuously bubbled with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂, containing (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO₃, 2 CaCl₂, 1 MgCl₂, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, and 10 glucose. The mPFC was visualized under a 4 × objective for gross anatomical localization, and L5 neurons were targeted for recording under a 60 × water-immersion objective (numerical aperture = 0.9). tdT+, retrobead-labeled L5 neurons located at least 50 μm below the slice surface were identified under epifluorescence and selected for recording to minimize artifacts due to surface truncation.

Spontaneous miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) were recorded in the presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM; Tocris-Cookson) and bicuculline (20 μM; Tocris-Cookson) to block action potentials and GABA_A receptor-mediated currents, respectively. The internal solution used for mEPSC recordings contained (in mM): 130 K-gluconate, 4 KCl, 2 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, 1 EGTA, and 14 phosphocreatine (pH 7.2, 295 mOsm). For miniature inhibitory postsynaptic current (mIPSC) recordings, CNQX (10 μM) and TTX were added to the ACSF to block AMPAR and sodium channel activity, and the internal pipette solution consisted of (in mM): 125 KCl, 2.8 NaCl, 2 MgCl₂, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na₃GTP, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, and 10 phosphocreatine (pH 7.25, ~300 mOsm). Electrophysiological signals were acquired using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Foster City, CA), low-pass filtered at 10 kHz for current-clamp and 1 kHz for voltage-clamp recordings, and digitized at 20 kHz using a Digidata 1440 A interface. Basic membrane properties, including resting membrane potential, input resistance, and action potential threshold, were also recorded. Neuronal morphology was visualized by including 0.25% biocytin in the internal solution, followed by post hoc staining with avidin-Alexa Fluor 488 and reconstruction of dendritic architecture and spine morphology using Neurolucida and Imaris software.

Laser scanning photostimulation (LSPS) for cortical circuit mapping

To assess local intracortical connectivity, LSPS with glutamate uncaging was performed in acute parasagittal brain slices containing the mPFC from stress-exposed mice. Following classification as susceptible or resilient after the cSDS paradigm, mice were sacrificed and slices were prepared and perfused in modified ACSF containing 4 mM Ca²⁺, 4 mM Mg²⁺, 0.2 mM MNI-caged glutamate, and 5 μM R-CPP to suppress NMDA receptor activity and minimize short-term synaptic plasticity. L5 pyramidal neurons—identified by their soma shape and position—were selected for whole-cell patch clamp recordings and voltage clamped at –70 mV. Only neurons located at least 50 μm below the slice surface were recorded to minimize artifacts from truncated dendrites and disrupted connectivity.

LSPS mapping was carried out using a 4 × objective lens (NA 0.16, Olympus) with ~80% UV transmission, and 1-ms laser pulses (355 nm, 20 mW; DPSS Lasers) were delivered to a 16 × 16 grid (100 μm spacing) superimposed on the cortical slice. Laser timing and intensity were controlled using an optical shutter (Conoptics Model 3050) and mechanical shutter (Uniblitz VCM-D1). Digital images were registered using a CCD camera (Retiga 2000DC, QImaging), and recordings were performed in a custom chamber mounted on a motorized stage (MPC-78, Sutter Instruments). Neuronal signals were amplified using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier, digitized at 10 kHz, and acquired with two BNC-6259 data acquisition boards (National Instruments). Data collection and laser positioning were controlled by Ephus software [22].

Once whole-cell configuration was achieved, voltage steps were applied to characterize basic membrane properties, followed by a series of current steps (−100 to 500 pA in 50 pA increments) to assess intrinsic excitability. LSPS mapping was then used to quantify local synaptic input to L5 mPFC neurons, specifically targeting tdT⁺ beads+ mPFC→NAc projection neurons. The UV laser was directed across a 16 × 16 stimulation grid spanning cortical layers 2–6, and evoked excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents were recorded at holding potentials of –70 and 0 mV, respectively, to isolate AMPA-mediated excitatory and GABA-mediated inhibitory inputs.

Circuit-specific optogenetic excitation and inhibition

To manipulate activity in the mPFC→NAc projection pathway in a circuit-specific manner, male TRAP2 mice (10–12 weeks old) were stereotaxically injected bilaterally into the NAc shell with either AAVrg-DIO-hChR2-EYFP (1.5 × 10⁹ genome copies in 0.5 μL; Addgene #20298-AAVrg) for optogenetic excitation or AAVrg-FLEX-Jaws-GFP (1.5 × 10⁹ genome copies in 0.5 μL; Addgene #84445-AAVrg) for inhibition. Following virus injection, optic fibers (200 μm core diameter) were implanted above the injection site to allow light delivery. After a two-week recovery period to allow for viral expression, mice underwent the cSDS protocol and were subsequently phenotyped as either susceptible or resilient based on SI testing.

To assess the behavioral impact of projection-specific excitation or inhibition during social interaction, mice were subjected to a 6-min SI test with optogenetic stimulation. For hChR2-mediated excitation, blue light (470 nm) was delivered using an LED system (Thorlabs) at 20 Hz with 5-ms pulses (5 mW output), in alternating 30-s ON and OFF blocks. For Jaws-mediated inhibition, continuous orange-red light (595 nm, 2.5 mW) was delivered in the same 30-s interleaved ON/OFF pattern. Light pulses were programmed in Ethovision using a USB-IO box, synchronized with behavioral tracking. This approach enabled real-time modulation of mPFC→NAc neuronal ensembles during social behavior in stress-exposed mice.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). Statistical analyses were conducted using ImageJ, MATLAB, Microsoft Excel, Adobe Creative Cloud, and GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Data distributions were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the F-test. When assumptions of normality and equal variance were met, parametric tests—including unpaired two-tailed or paired t-tests, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA—were applied as appropriate. For data that do not meet normal distribution or equal variance, nonparametric tests such as the Mann–Whitney U test were used for two-group comparisons. Cumulative distribution comparisons, including mEPSC, mIPSC amplitude and frequency, were analyzed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test. A threshold of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

Results

Acute social defeat stress activates selective mPFC neurons

To identify neurons activated by social stress, we utilized the TRAP2 mouse line [19], in which a tamoxifen-inducible CreERT2 recombinase is expressed under the control of the immediate early gene c-fos promoter. Neuronal activation during a defined TAM time window drives cre-mediated recombination, enabling permanent genetic labeling of recently active neurons (Fig. 1A). When TRAP2 mice are crossed with the Ai14 Cre-reporter line, behaviorally activated neurons are marked with tdT⁺ fluorescence, providing temporally restricted labeling for subsequent anatomical, physiological, and connectivity analyses [23].

Zoomed region bounded by blue box show laminar distribution of TRAP’ed PFC neurons. We adopted established protocols for acute (aSDS) and chronic (cSDS) social defeat stress in male mice [7, 10, 20, 24] (Fig. 1B, C; see Methods). Following the cSDS paradigm, animals were categorized as either resilient or susceptible based on SI scores, in accordance with our prior work [24]. To minimize confounding effects from acute stress or sensory stimulation associated with intraperitoneal TAM delivery [23], we administered TAM by oral gavage 1 h prior to aSDS or before the final cSDS session in TRAP2:Ai14 heterozygous male mice. Handled home-cage controls received the same TAM gavage but were not exposed to defeat stress.

Through dose titration studies (10–150 mg/kg TAM; data not shown), we found that 50 mg/kg TAM reliably induced robust tdT⁺ labeling in aSDS-exposed mice, particularly within cortical regions, with the most prominent activation observed in the mPFC (Fig. 1D). tdT⁺ neurons in the mPFC were distributed across both superficial (L2/3) and deep (L5) layers, spanning prelimbic (PrL) and infralimbic (IL) subregions. A smaller number of tdT⁺ neurons was also observed in handled control mice, likely reflecting physiological neuronal activation in the home cage environment. As an additional negative control, no tdT⁺ expression was observed in mice that did not receive TAM (Fig. 1E, F), confirming the inducibility and specificity of the TRAP system. Based on these results, we adopted the 50 mg/kg oral gavage protocol for all subsequent experiments involving activity-dependent labeling.

cSDS TRAPs a distinct mPFC ensemble compared to aSDS

To determine whether aSDS and cSDS recruit different projection-defined subpopulations of mPFC neurons, we combined activity-dependent TRAP labeling with retrograde tracing. One week after TRAP activation (TAM gavage and aSDS or cSDS behavioral stress) in TRAP2:Ai14 male mice, we stereotaxically injected 50 nL of green fluorescent retrobeads [25] into one of three known mPFC projection targets [11]: the NAc, dorsal striatum (dSTR), or ventral tegmental area (VTA) (Fig. 2A). This allowed us to identify the projection identity of tdT⁺ L5 neurons activated by each stress paradigm (Fig. 2B, C). Through immunohistochemical staining, we confirmed that TRAP-labeled neurons in aSDS mice were likely predominantly excitatory, as tdT⁺ neurons did not co-localize with GABA immunostaining (Fig. 2B).

Immunohistochemistry labeling of GABA revealed no colocalization with TRAP’ed neurons under both aSDS and cSDS conditions. C Quantification of the percentage of tdT⁺ neurons retrogradely labeled from each target region. aSDS preferentially activates neurons projecting to the dorsal striatum (dSTR), whereas cSDS predominantly activates neurons projecting to the NAc.

In aSDS-exposed animals, dSTR beads injection retrogradely labeled a substantial proportion of L5 tdT⁺ neurons in the mPFC, suggesting that corticostriatal neurons are preferentially engaged by acute stress. In contrast, in cSDS-exposed mice, only a small fraction of tdT⁺ mPFC neurons projected to the dSTR (aSDS: 35.2 ± 3.2%; cSDS: 7.1 ± 1.8%; ***p < 0.001, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test), while a significantly larger proportion projected to the NAc (aSDS: 8.3 ± 1.79%; cSDS: 44.7 ± 3.4%; ***p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). The percentage of VTA-projecting tdT⁺ neurons was comparable between groups (aSDS: 11.5 ± 1.59%; cSDS: 14.5 ± 1.11%; n.s., p = 0.79), indicating that VTA-directed projections are not differentially recruited by aSDS vs cSDS. These findings reveal that chronic social stress selectively engages a distinct population of L5 excitatory NAc-projecting mPFC neurons (mPFCNAc), in contrast to the corticostriatal population activated by acute stress.

To further validate that aSDS and cSDS recruit non-overlapping mPFC ensembles, we cross-referenced TRAP-labeled cells with c-fos immunostaining. In one experimental cohort, TRAP2:Ai14 male mice underwent TAM-aSDS labeling, followed by a 10-day cSDS paradigm, and were sacrificed 2 h after the final defeat session for c-fos immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 3A). In a separate group, mice underwent TAM-cSDS labeling (TAM administered on day 10), followed by an additional single session of social defeat 7 d later to induce c-fos expression. In TAM-aSDS mice, only a small proportion of tdT⁺ L5 neurons in the mPFC co-expressed c-fos following cSDS (10.2 ± 3.2%, n = 5). By contrast, in TAM-cSDS mice, a large majority of tdT⁺ neurons were c-fos⁺ following the additional defeat session (71.0 ± 4.9%, n = 5) (Fig. 3B, C, p < 0.0001). These results confirm that aSDS and cSDS selectively engage distinct, minimally overlapping prefrontal ensembles, highlighting the specificity of the TRAP strategy for temporally resolved labeling of stress-responsive neuronal populations.

NAc-projecting, cSDS+ neurons show reduced spontaneous excitatory synaptic activity selectively in susceptible mice

Mice were classified as resilient or susceptible based on established social interaction (SI) criteria [7, 24, 26]. We have shown that this single criteria is consistent with canonical measures depressive-like behaviors, such as behavioral despair (Supplementary Fig. 1), in that susceptible mice demonstrate significantly increased immobility time during a forced swim test (FST, Supplementary Fig. 1A), and significantly increased immobility time during a tail suspension test (TST, Supplementary Fig. 1B). To determine whether mPFCNAc neurons exhibit altered synaptic function in stress-susceptible vs resilient animals, we performed patch clamp recordings in TRAP2:AI14 male mice subjected to cSDS. To retrogradely label NAc-projecting neurons, 50 nL of green retrobeads were injected into the NAc shell (Fig. 4A). As a critical control, we first assessed whether the number of activated mPFC neurons (tdT⁺) and the proportion of NAc-projecting cells were equivalent between groups. Quantification revealed comparable densities of TRAP-labeled L5 neurons in the mPFC of resilient and susceptible mice (neurons/mm²: resilient: 150.6 ± 7.9; susceptible: 149.0 ± 5.7; Fig. 4B. p = 0.82), with a similar proportion projecting to the NAc (resilient: 41.6 ± 3.4%; susceptible: 40.6 ± 2.6%; Fig. 4C. p = 0.88).

We next performed whole-cell patch clamp recordings of spontaneous miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) from beads⁺ L5 mPFCNAc neurons that were either TRAP-labeled (tdT⁺) or unlabeled (tdT⁻). These neurons were further classified by behavioral phenotype (resilient vs susceptible; Fig. 4D). In susceptible mice, tdT⁺/beads⁺ neurons displayed significantly reduced mEPSC amplitude (p < 0.05, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test on cumulative histogram curve) and frequency (resilient: 3.17 ± 0.21 Hz; susceptible: 2.08 ± 0.29 Hz; t₁₇ = 3.38, p = 0.004), compared to their counterparts from resilient mice (Fig. 4E). In contrast, neighboring tdT⁻/beads⁺ neurons from the same animals showed no significant differences in mEPSC amplitude (p = 0.82, K–S test) or frequency (resilient: 2.69 ± 0.24 Hz; susceptible: 2.62 ± 0.27 Hz; t₁₇ = 0.38, p = 0.81), suggesting that synaptic alterations were specific to stress-activated projection neurons. From the same animal cohort, we also recorded miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) (Fig. 4F, G). Neither mIPSC amplitude distribution of frequency changes were observed for both tdT+ and tdT- NAc-projecting mPFC neurons.

To examine whether these functional changes were accompanied by structural remodeling, we included biocytin in the recording pipette and reconstructed dendritic and spine morphology using Neurolucida and Imaris (Fig. 4H–M). Sholl analysis revealed no significant difference in dendritic arborization between groups for either tdT⁺ or tdT⁻ NAc-projecting neurons (tdT⁺: p = 0.38; tdT⁻: p = 0.87; two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s post hoc test; Fig. 4H, K). However, tdT⁺ neurons from susceptible mice exhibited reduced spine density (resilient: 12.3 ± 0.53 spines/10 μm; susceptible: 10.4 ± 0.42/10 μm; p = 0.018. Figure 4J) and smaller spine head width (p = 0.002, Mann–Whitney test; Fig. 4J), consistent with diminished excitatory synaptic inputs. These differences were not observed in tdT⁻/beads⁺ neurons (Fig. 4L, M. spine density: resilient: 11.7 ± 0.45; susceptible: 11.42 ± 0.56; t₁₉ = 0.38, p = 0.71; test on spine head width: p = 0.32, Mann–Whitney test).

Together, these results suggest that repetitive activation of cSDS-responsive mPFC→NAc neurons in susceptible mice leads to synaptic dysregulation characterized by decreased excitatory synaptic drive and reduced spine integrity, without altering inhibitory synaptic connectivity. This circuit- and phenotype-specific synaptic disruption may contribute to the expression of social avoidance and depressive-like behaviors observed in susceptible individuals.

LSPS reveals that NAc-projecting, cSDS+ neurons show reduced intracortical excitatory but not inhibitory connectivity

To examine how cSDS alters local synaptic connectivity in activated mPFCNAc neurons, we employed laser scanning photostimulation (LSPS) with glutamate uncaging [22, 25, 27]. This technique was used to map functional synaptic inputs onto L5 tdT⁺, retrobead-labeled pyramidal neurons from cSDS-exposed TRAP2:Ai14 mice. Parasagittal slices containing the mPFC were prepared from both resilient and susceptible mice following behavioral phenotyping. LSPS mapping were performed to generate excitatory and inhibitory synaptic input maps onto cSDS-activated NAc-projecting neurons (Fig. 5A, B).

Neurons from susceptible mice exhibit reduced EPSC amplitudes compared to those from resilient mice. E Quantification of layer 2/3 excitatory input strength onto L5 tdT⁺ neurons. For excitatory input mapping, neurons were voltage-clamped at –70 mV while spatially patterned laser stimulation uncaged glutamate across a defined cortical grid. A representative example of excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) recorded across an 8 × 8 stimulation area is shown in Fig. 5B. Population data from 17 resilient and 14 susceptible neurons revealed that the majority of excitatory inputs to L5 tdT⁺ mPFCNAc neurons originated from superficial layers, particularly L2/3 (Fig. 5C). To quantify laminar input strength for neurons in both resilient and susceptible populations, responses were binned by cortical depth (16 bins; Fig. 5D), and input strength from L2/3 was specifically analyzed. We found that tdT⁺ neurons from susceptible mice exhibited significantly reduced excitatory inputs from L2/3 compared to those from resilient animals (Fig. 5E. resilient: 39.4 ± 3.89 pA; susceptible: 24.3 ± 2.26 pA; t₂₈ = 3.1, p = 0.0024; unpaired t-test), suggesting a reduction in intracortical excitatory drive following chronic social stress in susceptible mice.

After completion of excitatory mapping, inhibitory synaptic inputs were assessed by holding neurons at 0 mV, the reversal potential for AMPA-mediated currents. Population heatmaps of averaged 16 × 16 responses are presented in Fig. 5F (resilient: n = 12 cells; susceptible: n = 15 cells). Both L2/3 and L5 were found to contribute to inhibitory input onto L5 tdT⁺ neurons (Fig. 5F, G). We then quantified total inhibitory inputs from both layers combined. No significant difference in inhibitory synaptic input was observed between groups (resilient: 59.9 ± 3.8 pA; susceptible: 65.8 ± 2.7 pA; t₂₅ = 1.3, p = 0.20; Fig. 5H), indicating that intracortical inhibition is preserved following cSDS. To further ascertain if there is layer-specific changes, we also quantified inhibitory inputs from L2/3 and L5 separately, and did not observe significantly changes on layer-specific inhibitory inputs (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Together, these data indicate that chronic social stress selectively impairs excitatory but not inhibitory local synaptic connectivity onto behaviorally relevant NAc-projecting mPFC neurons. The reduction in layer 2/3 excitatory input observed in susceptible mice aligns with prior findings of diminished spontaneous mEPSCs, further supporting a model in which stress-induced disruption of excitatory intracortical signaling contributes to circuit dysfunction and depressive-like behavior.

Activating NAc-projecting, cSDS+ L5 mPFC neurons optogenetically improves depression-like behavioral outcomes

Our electrophysiology and LSPS mapping results suggest that cSDS leads to reduced excitatory synaptic drive onto L5 mPFCNAc neurons, in part due to diminished L2/3 intracortical inputs. This reduction in excitatory drive may impair the output of these projection neurons, contributing to the development of depressive-like behaviors in susceptible mice. If this is the case, direct manipulation of cSDS-activated mPFC→NAc neuron activity should modify behavioral outcomes.

To test this hypothesis, we used a circuit-specific optogenetic approach to either activate or inhibit cSDS⁺ mPFC→NAc neurons. For excitation experiments, TRAP2 mice received bilateral injections of a retrograde AAV enabling cre-dependent expression of the excitatory opsin channelrhodopsin-2 (AAVrg-DIO-hChR2-EYFP) into the NAc, followed by bilateral optic fiber implantation above the mPFC. Mice underwent the cSDS/TAM administration to TRAP label stress-activated neurons, followed by behavioral screening to classify animals as susceptible or resilient. Optogenetic excitation was tested in the susceptible group during a 6-min social interaction (SI) test using 30-s interleaved ON/OFF blue light stimulation. Blue light activation of cSDS-TRAP⁺ neurons significantly increased the time susceptible mice spent in the SI zone (OFF: 79.1 ± 10.0 s; ON: 147.8 ± 13.5 s; t₇ = 3.57, p = 0.009; paired t-test; Fig. 6A, B), indicating a reversal of social avoidance.

Bilateral optic fibers were implanted above the mPFC, and susceptible mice were selected for optogenetic activation. We next examined whether inhibiting cSDS⁺ mPFC→NAc neurons would have the opposite effects. TRAP mice were bilaterally injected in the NAc shell with AAVrg-FLEX-JAWS-GFP (see Methods) and implanted with bilateral optic fibers targeting the mPFC. Following the cSDS/TAM protocol and behavioral classification, optogenetic inhibition was tested in the resilient group during the SI test using interleaved ON/OFF deep red light stimulation. Inhibition of cSDS-TRAP neurons significantly reduced SI time (OFF: 135.3 ± 11.9 s; ON: 34.1 ± 8.6 s; t₇ = 7.59, p < 0.0001; paired t-test; Fig. 6C, D), effectively converting a resilient phenotype to a susceptible-like behavior.

These results demonstrate that the activity of cSDS-activated mPFC→NAc L5 neurons is both necessary and sufficient to determine resilience versus susceptibility following chronic social stress. Enhancing the activity of this pathway in susceptible mice improves social interaction, while suppressing it in resilient mice induces social avoidance, highlighting this corticolimbic projection as a potential therapeutic target for stress-related mood disorders.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that chronic social defeat stress recruits a specific subset of medial prefrontal cortex L5 neurons projecting to the nucleus accumbens, and that this circuit plays a critical role in mediating depressive like behavioral responses to stress. Our findings complement recent work examining layer- and circuit-specific mechanisms of stress susceptibility [14, 28]. These studies demonstrate layer-specific or region-specific excitation–inhibition imbalance in stress-related circuits; however, they do not identify behaviorally defined, activity-tagged projection ensembles or link chronic stress–activated neuronal identity to synaptic, morphological, and causal behavioral function within a single circuit. Our study combines activity-dependent TRAP labeling with retrograde tracing, electrophysiology, LSPS, and optogenetics to define a temporally tagged, projection-specific mPFC→NAc ensemble that is selectively engaged by chronic (but not acute) stress and whose dysfunction is restricted to stress-susceptible animals. This clarification highlights how our work complements and extends these recent studies by resolving stress-responsive circuit identity at the ensemble level. Our findings reveal that this cSDS-activated mPFC→NAc ensemble exhibits circuit-specific changes in synaptic physiology and connectivity that are selectively observed in stress-susceptible animals, but not in resilient counterparts. Furthermore, we show that manipulation of this circuit via optogenetics can bidirectionally influence stress-induced behavioral outcomes, establishing a causal link between cSDS-induced neural activity in this pathway and the expression of depression-like behaviors.

Our results also expand on other previous work implicating mPFC dysfunction in depression [7, 9, 29, 30] by identifying a projection-specific population of mPFC neurons that responds uniquely to chronic social stress. Using activity-dependent TRAP labeling combined with retrograde tracing, we show that aSDS and cSDS engage distinct mPFC projection populations: while aSDS-activated neurons primarily project to the dorsal striatum, cSDS predominantly recruits a larger population of neurons projecting to the NAc. This dissociation supports the idea that repeated, chronic stress may engage a maladaptive corticolimbic circuit that underlies the transition from adaptive coping to pathological behavioral states.

At the synaptic level, we found that cSDS+ mPFC→NAc tdT+ neurons from susceptible mice exhibited reduced mEPSC frequency and amplitude compared to resilient mice and neighboring tdT- neurons. These deficits were accompanied by a significant reduction in dendritic spine density and spine head diameter, suggesting compromised excitatory input and synaptic integration in the stress-activated ensemble. These alterations were not observed in resilient animals, indicating that synaptic dysfunction in this circuit is tightly linked to susceptibility. Moreover, LSPS revealed disrupted excitatory synaptic input maps in cSDS-tagged neurons from susceptible animals, without significant alteration in local inhibitory connectivity, highlighting broader impairments in local intracortical connectivity and excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance within this projection-defined circuit. These findings align with previous reports indicating altered connectivity [13] and disruptions in E/I balance contribute to the core pathophysiology of major depressive disorder [28, 31, 32].

Importantly, these structural and physiological deficits were not simply correlational but played a functional role in shaping behavior. Optogenetic activation of cSDS+ mPFCNAc neurons during social exposure reduced the likelihood of developing depression-like behaviors, measured here as social avoidance, while optogenetic inhibition of the same neurons during a social interaction test was sufficient to promote social avoidance. These findings suggest that repeated activation of this circuit may represent a key driver of vulnerability, potentially through activity-dependent synaptic weakening and loss of functional integration with local microcircuits. This has been observed under aging and other neuropsychiatric conditions [33–35].

In rodent models of depression and depressive-like behaviors, multiple behavioral assays in addition to social interaction test, including sucrose preference, tail suspension, and forced swim tests are often used [7, 20, 29]. These tests probe different behavioral domains relevant to depression, such as anhedonia, behavioral despair, social withdrawal, and anxiety-related behavior. The susceptible vs resilient population selected using social interaction score are consistent with these depressive-like behavior measures. As such, we focused on correlation of neuronal activity with social interaction/withdraw behavior, without going through extensive measures of anhedonia-related tests, behavioral despair/passive coping, anxiety-related behavior, or motivation and effort related behaviors. Our findings build upon prior studies showing that non-selective activation of the mPFC can reverse behavioral deficits in depression models [36–39], by demonstrating that specific ensembles within the mPFC may encode negative valence and promote maladaptive outcomes when persistently activated, which are causatively linked to their altered activity and synaptic connectivity. This projection-specific approach underscores the importance of dissecting circuit heterogeneity within the mPFC and highlights the need to move beyond global manipulations in favor of targeting precise ensembles defined by their input/output architecture and activity history.

Another key insight from this study is the discovery that circuit engagement differs not only between acute and chronic stress, but also between susceptible and resilient individuals exposed to the same chronic stress paradigm. This suggests that resilience may be associated with the ability to buffer or contain stress-related activity within the mPFC without allowing it to propagate maladaptive changes beyond projection-specific circuits. Future work may explore whether these protective effects involve local inhibitory control, neuromodulatory gating, or differential afferent inputs onto the mPFC→NAc pathway.

In summary, this work identifies a specific mPFC→NAc projection that is preferentially recruited by chronic, but not acute, social defeat stress and demonstrates that dysfunction within this circuit contributes to behavioral susceptibility. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the circuit-level basis of stress-induced mood disorders and lay the foundation for future studies aimed at targeting defined neural ensembles for therapeutic intervention in depression.
