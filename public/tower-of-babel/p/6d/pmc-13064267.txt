Pitfalls in the management of undiagnosed secondary adrenal insufficiency: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Koharu Sakai, Tomohiko Yoshida, Takuyo Chiba, Masaya Yamaga, Minoru Takemoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged cortisol deficiency in undiagnosed central adrenal insufficiency can lead to severe hypotonic hyponatremia due to inappropriate vasopressin secretion and malnutrition caused by inhibition of orexigenic signals. Notably, although hydrocortisone-induced recovery can trigger osmotic demyelination and refeeding syndromes, no previous report has simultaneously described these complications and documented significant decreases in vasopressin levels, along with changes in urine osmolality and volume before and after hydrocortisone administration.Case presentationA 48-year-old Japanese man presented with fever, severe nausea, and oliguria and was brought to our hospital by ambulance due to impaired consciousness. Physical examination and laboratory analysis showed severe euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia and low-normal glucose value. Low adrenocorticotrophic hormone and cortisol levels, undetectable 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and minimal response to corticotropin-releasing hormone indicated secondary adrenal insufficiency. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed slight pituitary swelling, suggesting hypophysitis. Treatment started with a 200 mg hydrocortisone infusion over 24 hours, and 6 hours later, the patient experienced a marked decrease in vasopressin levels, accompanied by significant dilute urine excretion and an excessively rapid increase in blood sodium levels, which posed a risk of osmotic demyelination. Rehydration with 5% dextrose and desmopressin was used to prevent this risk. Carefully adjusting plasma osmolality successfully prevented osmotic demyelination syndrome. Hydrocortisone replacement significantly increased the patient’s appetite, leading to refeeding hypophosphatemia and disorientation; however, these resolved with intravenous sodium phosphate replacement. The patient developed a fever on day 12 and was confirmed to have coronavirus disease 2019. The fever subsided by day 16 with molnupiravir treatment and hydrocortisone dose adjustment, and he was discharged on day 23 with a maintenance dose of hydrocortisone.ConclusionCareful management is required while administering hydrocortisone in patients with undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency, as it may cause osmotic demyelination syndrome or refeeding syndrome due to sudden changes in blood electrolytes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-05924-0.Keywords: Undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency, Hyponatremia, Osmotic demyelination syndrome, Refeeding syndrome, Case report

Background

Adrenal insufficiency (AI) is a severe medical condition caused by inadequate cortisol levels, which can be life-threatening if not diagnosed and treated promptly. However, diagnosing AI can be challenging, as its symptoms can often be mistaken for psychiatric disorders [1]. Therefore, patients with a suspected adrenal crisis or severe and acute form of AI should undergo intravenous hydrocortisone and immediate fluid resuscitation even when the confirmatory test has not been performed [2]. Moreover, it is crucial to manage the condition carefully, as clinicians may overlook close monitoring of patients after diagnosing AI and initiating hydrocortisone. Since there are at least two potential pitfalls after starting hydrocortisone, including osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) and refeeding syndrome (RFS), particularly in patients without a prior diagnosis of AI, prudent risk management remains vital even after intravenous hydrocortisone initiation. Nevertheless, no reports have addressed these two pitfalls concurrently in managing undiagnosed AI.

The brain cells require more than 48 hours to adapt to sudden changes in plasma osmolality [3]. A gradual decrease in osmolality allows brain cells to adjust, preventing brain edema and herniation. In contrast, overly rapid correction of chronic hypotonic hyponatremia is also harmful to the brain, causing ODS [4]. In rodent experiments, adaptive reductions in taurine, other amino acids, creatinine, and electrolytes in the brain prevented cerebral edema caused by chronic hyponatremia, whereas rapid correction (experimental ODS) led to brain dehydration and shrinkage [5]. Administration of myoinositol, a major organic osmolyte, has been reported to prevent the onset of ODS [6]. Symptoms of ODS are dysarthria, dysphagia, paraparesis or quadriparesis, seizures, disorientation, obtundation, and coma. Therefore, severe and chronic hyponatremia should be treated gradually, with the increase in serum sodium limited to 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L for the subsequent 24 hours until the sodium level exceeds 130 mmol/L, according to the European experts [3]. Patients with AI often present with hypotonic hyponatremia and oliguria due to improper arginine vasopressin (AVP) secretion, inducing the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD)-like condition [7, 8]. Generally, AVP secretion is suppressed under hypoosmotic conditions, but low cortisol levels can stimulate AVP release via increased corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) production [7, 8]. As cortisol deficiency causes hypotonic hyponatremia and primarily triggers AVP release in AI, inhibiting AVP secretion by hydrocortisone may induce a vigorous increase in hypoosmotic urine excretion and plasma osmolality in patients with AI. This phenomenon, termed “autocorrection” of hyponatremia, heightens the risk of ODS [8]. It has been reported that even when severe and chronic hyponatremia is corrected abruptly, ODS can be prevented by promptly re-lowering the blood sodium concentration [9].

Anorexia, abdominal pain, and weight loss are common in AI [10]. Regarding the mechanisms underlying anorexia in AI, cortisol deficiency results in elevated CRH, which suppresses appetite. Morley and Levine reported that intracerebroventricular administration of CRH in rats reduced appetite following fasting [11]. Kuperman et al. demonstrated that Agouti-related peptide (AgRP)/neuropeptide Y (NPY) neurons in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, which promote appetite, are innervated by CRH neurons and express CRH receptor type 1 [12]. Administration of CRH decreases the excitability of AgRP/NPY neurons. Consequently, glucocorticoid deficiency may reduce negative feedback to the hypothalamus, increase CRH secretion, inhibit AgRP/NPY neurons, and suppress appetite. Although hydrocortisone therapy generally restores appetite, a sudden increase in food intake may lead to RFS, characterized by thiamine and essential mineral deficiencies. Specifically, refeeding hypophosphatemia can be fatal because phosphate is a critical source of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) [13]. ATP is an essential compound for living cells to provide energy, and 2,3-BPG is crucial in decreasing hemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen, shifting the hemoglobin–oxygen dissociation curve to the right and making it easier to provide oxygen to the peripheral tissues. Therefore, refeeding hypophosphatemia may lead to peripheral tissue energy depletion and hypoxia [13]. Prophylactic essential mineral supplementation, including phosphate, may prevent RFS [14].

Here, we report a patient with secondary AI who showed a significant rise in blood osmolality from excessive excretion of diluted urine and experienced RFS due to a sudden improvement in appetite following hydrocortisone treatment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to simultaneously describe these pitfalls in the management of undiagnosed secondary AI and to demonstrate a significant change in AVP levels and urine osmolality before and after hydrocortisone administration.

Case report

A 48-year-old Japanese man presented with impaired consciousness and severe nausea; 10 years earlier, he had been diagnosed with depression and was prescribed duloxetine (60 mg/day). The patient underwent a cholecystectomy for cholecystitis caused by gallstones 9 years ago. Due to agoraphobia, the patient could not see his doctor and asked his mother to obtain his medication. Consequently, the patient had been taking the same dosage of duloxetine and had not been regularly taking any other medication apart from it. Six days before admission, the patient experienced fever, malaise, severe nausea, and noticed oliguria, prompting consultation with a local physician who prescribed acetaminophen and domperidone. However, the patient developed dysarthria and decreased consciousness, necessitating emergency transport to the hospital.

On physical examination, the patient’s Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 8 out of 15 (he responded to voice for eye-opening, was unable to speak, and showed normal flexion in response to pain). He did not have a fever (his temperature was 36.2 °C), likely due to taking acetaminophen, and his blood pressure was 110/76 mmHg (Fig. 1A), indicating that he was hemodynamically stable. No obvious signs of volume overload or depletion were observed, such as pulmonary crackles, pitting edema, dry mouth and skin, or the chest X-ray showing any abnormalities, with a cardiothoracic ratio of 43%. The patient weighed 58 kg, and his body mass index was 20.1 kg/m2. Laboratory analysis revealed severe hyponatremia (plasma sodium level: 108 mmol/L) along with plasma hypotonicity (plasma osmolality: 229 mOsm/kg), inappropriately high urine osmolality (534 mOsm/kg), and elevated urine sodium (125 mmol/L), requiring differentiation from the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) (Table 1). Since the patient had low-normal glucose value (glucose level on admission: 73 mg/dL), 20 mL of 50% dextrose was given intravenously at 2:20 PM and 5:20 PM (Fig. 1B). Although the patient had hypoosmotic hyponatremia, normal and half-normal saline were administered at rates of 20 mL per hour and 40 mL per hour, respectively, which are relatively hypotonic for this condition (Fig. 1B). We used commercially available half-normal saline, which contained Na+ 90 mmol/L, Cl− 70 mmol/L, lactate 20 mmol/L, and 2.6% dextrose. This decision was made due to concerns about a potential AI-induced SIAD-like condition, as hydrocortisone replacement may cause a sudden increase in plasma osmolality. Subsequent test results suggested secondary AI, indicated by a serum cortisol level below 0.05 µg/dL (1.38 nmol/L) (reference range: 6.24–18.0 µg/dL [172–497 nmol/L]) and an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) level of 2.0 pg/mL (reference range: 7.2–63.3) (Table 1).

Nevertheless, within 6 hours (12:00 AM), the patient experienced a sudden increase in urine output (> 100 mL per hour) despite not being given any diuretics (Fig. 1B). After 10 hours (4:00 AM, day 2), the urine osmolality was measured at 81 mOsm/kg (Fig. 1C). In addition, our patient’s AVP level decreased from 1.3 to 0.4 pg/mL after hydrocortisone administration. The patient was promptly administered large doses of 5% dextrose (500 mL was rapidly injected at 4:00 AM, followed by continuous infusion adjusted within the range of 300 mL per hour–20 mL per hour [600 mL every 2 hours–40 mL every 2 hours] while monitoring the urine volume), with normal and half-normal saline discontinued. In addition, intranasal desmopressin, a V2 vasopressin receptor agonist, was applied (Fig. 1B). Four hours later (8:00 AM, day 2), a noticeable decrease in urine excretion was observed, and the patient’s blood sodium level had dropped to 114 mmol/L (Fig. 1C). The patient regained normal consciousness on the second day, and the serum sodium levels gradually increased at a rate of 3 mmol/L per day.

The patient’s appetite improved on day 3, and the hydrocortisone dose was gradually tapered from an initial 200 mg/day (intravenous) to 20 mg/day (oral) from day 6 onward. Although the patient’s condition seemed to have improved, his level of consciousness suddenly deteriorated on the night of day 6, with a GCS score of 12 out of 15 (eye-opening to sound, confused conversation, and normal flexion to pain). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings were unremarkable, except for slight swelling and enhancement of the pituitary infundibulum (Fig. 2), suggesting hypophysitis-induced secondary AI without findings compatible with ODS. However, the patient’s lactate level was elevated (Fig. 3). Despite the detection of hypophosphatemia with a blood phosphorus level of 1.2 mg/dL (Fig. 3) noted on the morning of day 6 and phosphate supplementation planned, it was inadvertently not administered. Therefore, we immediately initiated treatment with 30 mmol/day of sodium phosphate over 6 hours for 2 days. On day 7, the patient experienced difficulty eating, and his speech was slurred. However, by day 8, his consciousness returned to normal (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Diagnosis and treatment of AI

Upon admission, the patient exhibited typical symptoms of adrenal crisis, including nausea, low-normal glucose value, and hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately high urine osmolality. When evaluating hypotonic hyponatremia, it is essential to determine the volume status through detailed history taking, physical examination, and laboratory tests, although this can be challenging [17]. In this case, the patient’s hypotonic hyponatremia was suspected to be euvolemic hyponatremia because we observed no evident signs or symptoms of heart or kidney failure, no diuretic use, and no clear signs of hypovolemia (such as hypotension) on the basis of radiographs, physical examination, medication history, and laboratory test results. In cases of euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia, assessing the urinary sodium concentration and osmolality is essential. In this case, the urinary osmolality was inappropriately high, despite the low blood osmolality (Table 1). Therefore, it is crucial to distinguish between true SIADH, AI, and severe hypothyroidism [17]. Subsequent blood tests confirmed hypocortisolemia and a low ACTH level (Table 1), indicating that the patient’s hyponatremia was most likely due to secondary AI, prompting us to initiate hydrocortisone therapy immediately. Immediate intravenous administration of 100 mg hydrocortisone, followed by 200 mg hydrocortisone intravenous infusion for 24 hours continuously or every 6 hours, is recommended for adults at risk of adrenal crisis [2]. However, we decided against administering the initial 100 mg bolus of hydrocortisone, as it could have led to excessive dilute urine output. Additionally, given the patient’s relatively stable hemodynamic condition, we determined that an initial bolus of hydrocortisone was unnecessary. Once stabilized, the hydrocortisone dose was gradually reduced to a maintenance dose of 15–25 mg daily. In secondary AI, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system remains intact; therefore, the risk of salt wasting and hypotension is lower than in primary AI. The Endocrine Society recommends using the lowest tolerable hydrocortisone dose replacement, with a usual maintenance dose of 15–20 mg [18]. Given this patient’s low body mass index, we considered a total daily dose of 15 mg appropriate.

Since hydrocortisone is chemically identical to cortisol, evaluating endogenous adrenal function without discontinuing hydrocortisone is challenging; therefore, it may be advantageous to use dexamethasone in the initial treatment of secondary AI because it has limited cross-reactivity with cortisol [18]. However, interpretation is difficult, considering its potential inhibitory action on the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. Furthermore, its longer half-life presents some limitations, including difficulty in dose titration to prevent adrenal crisis while reducing the risk of developing conditions such as osteoporosis and diabetes, particularly when used for an extended period. In addition, since dexamethasone has no mineralocorticoid activity, concomitant use of fludrocortisone may be necessary when treating “primary” AI with dexamethasone [18, 19].

Endocrinology experts advocate that a morning cortisol level of < 3 µg/dL (83 nmol/L) [18] and a peak cortisol below 13.7 µg/dL (378 nmol/L) after the CRH stimulation test indicates AI [16]. However, using the latest immunoassays, 30–35% lower cortisol cutoff values seem appropriate [20]. Moreover, the corticotropin (ACTH) stimulation test is generally recommended for the diagnosis of central AI [18], primarily due to safety concerns associated with insulin tolerance testing, including the risk of hypoglycemia-related seizures, brain damage, increased cardiovascular events, and the limited global availability of CRH. However, when using the corticotropin stimulation test to diagnose central AI, there remains a risk of misdiagnosing it as “normal” during the acute phase, approximately 4 weeks after onset, before complete adrenal atrophy has occurred [21]. In this case, the patient showed minimal response to ACTH or cortisol after CRH stimulation (Table 2), and the 24-hour urinary-free cortisol level was below the detection limit. Hypophysitis often causes a decrease in other pituitary hormones. On day 5 of illness, results showed no noticeable reduction in gonadal function or prolactin (Additional file 1). Concerning the growth hormone (GH)-insulin-like growth factor (IGF)−1 axis, the IGF-1 level was initially low; however, the growth hormone-releasing peptide-2 (GHRP-2) test performed on day 23 revealed no decrease in GH response (Additional file 2) [22]. A follow-up IGF-1 measurement 16 months later was within normal limits at 131 ng/mL (Additional file 3). Malnutrition has been reported to cause false-negative results in patients with acromegaly [23]. Therefore, the initial decrease in IGF-1 may be due to malnutrition. Additionally, the elevated prolactin levels may indicate pituitary stalk interruption syndrome due to hypophysitis.

Brain contrast MRI revealed swelling and enhancement of the pituitary infundibulum (Fig. 2), while follow-up MRI 16 months after admission showed resolution of the pituitary stalk enlargement and no noticeable tumorous lesions, suggesting that the patient likely experienced hypophysitis (Additional file 4). Histologically, several types of hypophysitis have been identified: lymphocytic hypophysitis, granulomatous hypophysitis, xanthomatous hypophysitis, IgG4-related hypophysitis, necrotizing hypophysitis, and mixed forms [24, 25]. Lymphocytic hypophysitis is the most common variant and is initially considered to occur exclusively in women. However, the recent report suggests that approximately 20% of lymphocytic hypophysitis occurs in men [24]. The second most frequent variant, granulomatous hypophysitis, featured multinucleated giant cells and granulomas with epithelioid histiocytes, more frequently associated with severe clinical symptoms, such as chiasmal syndrome, than lymphocytic hypophysitis. Xanthomatous hypophysitis is associated with foamy histiocytes without granulomas, and the radiographic findings of xanthomatous hypophysitis resemble those of a pituitary adenoma [26]. IgG4-related hypophysitis is known as one of the spectrum of IgG4-related diseases characterized by infiltration of IgG4-positive plasma cells into the relevant tissues [27].

This patient had anemia and thrombocytopenia upon admission (Additional file 1). These findings are commonly seen in hypophysitis associated with connective tissue diseases such as Sjögren’s syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus [25]. However, no arthritis or characteristic skin changes were observed, and the follow-up complete blood counts had returned to normal within 16 months after admission (Additional file 3). Histologically confirmed hypophysitis is often accompanied by IgG4 elevation [27, 28] and other autoimmune diseases; however, in this patient, IgG4, thyroid peroxidase antibody, and anti-thyroglobulin antibody levels were all within the normal range (Table 1). Rabphilin-3A has been reported as a crucial autoantigen in the pathophysiology of lymphocytic infundibulo-neurohypophysitis [29]. However, we were unable to measure anti-rabphilin-3A antibodies in this case. No chiasmal syndrome was present, pituitary stalk swelling had normalized 16 months after admission, and no pituitary hormone deficiencies other than ACTH was observed. Given its frequency of occurrence, lymphocytic hypophysitis is the most likely diagnosis; however, this remains speculative because pathological evaluation of the pituitary tissue was not possible. Several reports have described the association between COVID-19 and hypophysitis [30], but we believe there is no direct link because the secondary AI in this patient developed before the onset of COVID-19, not after. Consequently, we will continue monitoring the patient closely to distinguish it from other pituitary diseases, including pituitary tumors.

In healthy individuals, endogenous cortisol levels increase in response to stressful events such as infections. Therefore, increasing hydrocortisone doses in patients with AI under stress is necessary to prevent adrenal crises [2]. Generally, hydrocortisone doses should be 2–3 times higher than regular doses for minor illnesses. Our patient developed COVID-19 with a fever of 38 °C and diarrhea while on a maintenance dose of hydrocortisone (15 mg/day). Increasing the dose to 30 mg/day helped prevent an adrenal crisis.

Hypotonic hyponatremia associated with AI and “autocorrection” of hyponatremia

AI often presents with euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia, making it a critical differential diagnosis for SIAD. On admission, our patient’s urine osmolality and AVP levels were inappropriately high relative to plasma osmolality (Table 1). While the antidepressant duloxetine and vomiting could have some role in stimulating AVP secretion, AI might have been the main cause of the SIAD-like pathology in our patient, given that he had a large amount of diluted urine after hydrocortisone administration (even without an initial bolus hydrocortisone treatment). For patients with hyponatremia accompanied by severe symptoms, such as unconsciousness and cardiorespiratory distress, current guidelines recommend withholding hypotonic fluids [3, 17]. However, because this patient had euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia accompanied by a low-normal glucose value (Table 1), we considered the possibility of AI and were concerned that hydrocortisone replacement would result in the excretion of large amounts of dilute urine, posing the risk of developing ODS. Therefore, we decided to administer relatively hypotonic fluids (normal and half-normal saline) (Fig. 1B). Notably, the patient’s urine output did not change significantly until 10:00 PM on day 1, despite the continuous administration of normal saline and half-normal saline (Fig. 1B). In contrast, urine output suddenly increased around midnight on day 1 when the slowly administered hydrocortisone reached the recommended dose for minor illness [2] (approximately 50 mg of hydrocortisone was administered at midnight on day 1) (Fig. 1B). Therefore, AI was likely the primary cause of the SIAD-like pathology in this patient. Cortisol suppresses AVP, and in AI, reduced negative feedback inhibition by cortisol elevates CRH, which stimulates AVP production [8]. As the principal stimulus for AVP release is plasma osmolality, hydrocortisone treatment in patients with AI exhibiting hypotonic hyponatremia can cause a sudden reduction in AVP. Accordingly, our patient’s AVP level decreased from 1.3 to 0.4 pg/mL after hydrocortisone administration, along with a significant quantity of diluted urine.

Chronic hyponatremia lasting more than 48 hours causes brain cells to adapt to a decreased plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) osmolality by reducing intracellular osmolytes, thereby minimizing the risk of brain herniation. However, rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia can cause ODS because brain cells cannot adapt to sudden changes in serum and CSF osmolality, resulting in myelinolysis in the pons, basal ganglia, and thalamus. This can cause symptoms such as dysarthria, dysphagia, and coma, and patients with blood sodium levels below 113 mmol/L are at a high risk of ODS [8]. US experts recommend that the correction rate for chronic hyponatremia should not exceed 4–6 mmol/L over 24 hours to prevent ODS [31]. It is advised to limit the increase in blood sodium to 10 mmol/L during the first 24 hours and then by an additional 8 mmol/L every 24 hours until it exceeds 130 mmol/L in Europe [3]. The sodium concentration in our patient increased faster than the recommended limit during the first 2 days (10 mmol/L per 24 hours). However, immediate treatment to lower the patient’s blood sodium concentration eventually changed it to a more favorable level (4 mmol/L per 24 hours). Although the patient’s level of consciousness suddenly decreased on the sixth night and clinical symptoms of ODS typically appear 2–6 days after a sudden increase in blood osmolality [32], we believe that the primary cause of the loss of consciousness from day 6 onward was RFS, because the patient’s consciousness improved immediately following phosphate supplementation. Additionally, the MRI showed no remarkable findings compatible with ODS (Fig. 2), nor were any abnormal findings seen in the MRI 16 months later (Additional file 4). According to the European Union guidelines, a rapid rise in urine output exceeding 100 mL per hour during the treatment of hyponatremia suggests an increased risk of an excessively rapid rise in plasma sodium levels [3], suggesting that desmopressin should have been administered earlier in our patient. In addition, it is generally recommended to avoid the use of hypotonic fluids in cases of hyponatremia that present with sudden and severe clinical symptoms [3, 17]; however, in the treatment of hypotonic hyponatremia associated with AI, it may be advisable to prepare hypotonic fluids such as 5% dextrose and desmopressin in case of a sudden increase in blood osmolality after administration of hydrocortisone.

RFS

RFS is a metabolic disorder or electrolyte imbalance triggered by a sudden increase in caloric intake in patients with chronic undernutrition; however, there is no internationally accepted definition [13]. The intracellular demand for thiamine, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus increases during RFS, and hypophosphatemia is a hallmark of the condition. This results in decreased energy and oxygen deficiencies in the peripheral tissues. This can lead to organ damage and abnormal blood cell function, which can be fatal. Patients with an intake of < 50% of the estimated energy requirements for more than 5 days during an acute illness or injury are at high risk for RFS [13]. Our patient had a history of reduced appetite due to severe nausea and vomiting caused by AI for more than 6 days, placing him at high risk for RFS. Hydrocortisone treatment in patients with AI typically results in a sudden improvement in appetite. Our patient resumed eating on day 4 of treatment, with an oral calorie intake ranging from 700 to 1300 kcal/day; however, by day 6, his blood phosphorus concentration dropped to 1.2 mg/dL, followed by disturbed consciousness (Fig. 3). Blood phosphorus levels below 1.0 mg/dL (0.32 mmol/L) are considered to be at high risk for RFS [14, 33], and in such high-risk patients, prophylactic administration of 0.5–0.8 mmol/kg per day of phosphate, along with thiamine, potassium, and magnesium, is recommended [14]. However, treatment for hypophosphatemia was inadvertently delayed; therefore, the patient’s level of consciousness deteriorated. Blood lactate levels increased (Fig. 3) while the patient was unconscious, suggesting tissue hypoxia and ATP depletion. We immediately administered 30 mmol/day of phosphate intravenously over 6 hours after the patient became unconscious. We repeated this treatment the following day to raise the patient’s phosphate level to be within the normal range (Fig. 3). The patient’s consciousness recovered on day 8, indicating that refeeding hypophosphatemia, and not ODS, contributed to the deterioration of his condition.

Summary of key arguments

AI causes cortisol deficiency, which increases AVP secretion and produces a syndrome resembling SIADH. Appetite suppression occurs due to reduced activity of orexigenic AgRP/NPY neurons, driven by excessive CRH secretion. Hydrocortisone supplementation can rapidly modify these conditions, potentially precipitating ODS by shifting to an AVP-deficiency-like state, and RFS by abruptly increasing appetite (Additional file 5). Therefore, careful clinical monitoring is essential.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, as this is a single case report of a patient with undiagnosed secondary AI, it is unclear whether these findings are similar to those of patients with primary AI or apply to all cases of undiagnosed secondary AI. Second, the mechanisms described in this report regarding the excretion of a large amount of hypotonic urine after hydrocortisone treatment and the development of impaired consciousness with hypophosphatemia after resuming meals are speculative. Finally, the diagnosis of hypophysitis was not pathologically confirmed, and the cause remains unknown. These limitations need to be confirmed in future similar studies.

Conclusion

When treating patients with undiagnosed AI, physicians must be cautious after the initiation of hydrocortisone therapy to avoid potential complications such as “autocorrection” of hyponatremia-induced ODS and RFS caused by robust improvement in appetite.
