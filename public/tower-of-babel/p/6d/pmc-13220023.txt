Sensoriality of emollient creams: can sensory experience compromise blinding in trials?
Authors: Jean-Matthieu L’Orphelin, Cécile Morice, Johan Gardères, Sylvie Chopin, Céline Lucas, Clément Bouchet, Barbara Bailly-Caillé, Victoria Da Silva-Dias, Nadim Mourad, Marie Jouandet, Julia Akroun, Aurélie Acher, Anne Dompmartin, Jean-Jacques Parienti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegular application of emollients, particularly those that enhance the skin’s acid mantle and promote ceramide production, can help restore skin barrier integrity, reduce inflammation and prevent disease flares. These emollients not only protect the skin, but also modulate on the skin microbiome, which has become a cornerstone in disease management. Sensory properties, such as texture, appearance and the sensation of freshness, are critical factors influencing patient perception, treatment adherence and product efficacy.ObjectivesTo test, a controlled study involving 120 participants, the ability of consumers to recognize emollient creams based solely on sensory characteristics to ensure that sensory stimuli from emollients cannot be a source of unblinding in a clinical trial involving topical corticosteroids.MethodsWe conducted a double-blind quantitative assessment in a controlled environment involving 120 healthy volunteers who were regular users of moisturizing creams, of whom 50% were classified as ‘naïve’ users and 50% as ‘connoisseurs’. The creams under investigation (n = 4) were placed in identical bottles and evaluated in monadic sequence according to a Latin square experimental design.ResultsThere were no significant statistical differences in overall appearance, shine or transparency between the four emollients. Only 6% of consumers recognized Neutraderm, 8% La Roche-Posay, 6% Avene and 4% Uriage (i.e. figures below any recognition by chance). Overall, we report a satisfaction rate of almost 60% for the topicals used (from 45% up to 64%).ConclusionsThis finding supports the feasibility of conducting blinded clinical trials on the emollients used in this study as sensory cues are unlikely to compromise the blinding.

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease of increasing prevalence.1 Pruritus is its most common and distressing symptom,2,3 sometimes requiring systemic treatments.4 The relationship between itch and pain pathways may provide novel therapeutic insights.2

The stratum corneum ensures the skin’s barrier function through intercellular lipid cement, limiting water loss and allergen/microbe infiltration.5 The disease is often of genetic origin, involving mutations in filaggrin or other barrier proteins, as well as immune receptors for pro inflammatory molecules,6 thereby contributing to skin dysbiosis.7,8 Maintaining an acidic skin pH may mitigate disease severity.9

Appropriate emollients help restore barrier integrity by reinforcing the acid mantle, stimulating ceramide synthesis, enhancing tight junction function and modulating local immunity.10 The pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis involves complex interactions between the immune, epidermal and nervous systems. Regular hydration with emollients can modulate these interactions.11 Next-generation emollients specifically target the skin microbiome,7,12 are essential for barrier maintenance and flare prevention,13 and are now central to current management guidelines.14 Thermal waters,15,16 rich in minerals, are often used as a base for these formulations. Dermocosmetics play a significant role in daily skincare routines across genders.17 They are designed to maintain skin hydration18 and support barrier function.19 However, not all topical products offer tangible skin benefits: some prioritize sensory appeal.20 Emollients are characterized by four main sensory parameters: sensory perception (sensations such as softness or freshness, which activate sensory neurons);21 friction and texture influenced by the formulation;22 hydration-related sensations23 associated with skin softness and suppleness; and application and absorption properties24 describing how lipids penetrate the stratum corneum and impact post use skin feel.

These perceptions are shaped by skin mechanoreceptors responsive to pressure or stretch25 stimuli. Skincare products, unlike typical tactile stimuli, alter the skin’s physical properties (e.g. hydration levels),26 which in turn modify how mechanical forces are experienced and how subsequent tactile stimuli are processed.27,28 Understanding these sensory effects requires identifying key product attributes, which are often selected intuitively. Evaluated parameters include smoothness,20,24,29 softness24 and gloss.30 However, the relative importance of other sensory traits (such as ease of pickup, spreadability and afterfeel) remains insufficiently understood, despite their influence on mechanical properties and sensorial perception.24,31

The choice of emollient and thickeners significantly affects the rheological and sensory profiles of surfactant-based creams, linking physical and sensory characteristics32 to sensory experiences. These properties enable differentiation of creams based on emollient composition.

Dermocosmetic evaluation demands a rigorous methodological framework to assess product efficacy, safety and user acceptability,33,34 necessitating robust scientific evidence,35 as in randomized trials. The primary objective of our study was to determine whether consumers can identify emollients based solely on their sensory characteristics. In other words, could sensory perception compromise the blinding in double-blind clinical trials? Secondary objectives included assessing differences in consumer appreciation across the tested emollient creams.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient recruitment

We conducted a quantitative study carried out in a controlled environment (sensory booths, Caen, France) on 120 volunteer participants who were all regular users of body lotion/moisturizing cream (minimum once daily), of whom 50% were classed as ‘connoisseurs’ (buyers of body lotion/moisturizing cream for dry and/or atopic skin purchased from a parapharmacy or drugstore) and 50% as ‘naïve’ (buyers of body lotion/moisturizing cream from supermarkets or other retail channels, regardless of skin type); the separate analysis of the two subgroups was in order to assess:

Whether knowledgeable users of this category of products (i.e. those who use them regularly) would be able to recognize their own product, thereby potentially biasing the results of the future clinical study. This subgroup was included to simulate the most critical scenario in the clinical trial design, aimed at evaluating the risk of unblinding.

Whether knowledgeable users of this category of products (i.e. those who use them regularly) would be able to recognize their own product, thereby potentially biasing the results of the future clinical study. Whether non knowledgeable users of this type of product (i.e. the vast majority of individuals seen in hospital consultations for atopic dermatitis, who are typically prescribed products for atopic skin and are thus still naïve to such products) would be able to recognize them. While this seemed unlikely due to their lack of prior use, it was still important to investigate the possibility that certain moisturizing brands might have distinctive sensory signatures across their product lines that could allow users to identify them and potentially bias the study results.

Gender was not a criterion of recruitment, and we only enrolled volunteers aged ≥ 18 years (no quotas).

We performed a cross-sectional analysis on healthy volunteers from a large panel. Our aim was to conduct a sensory study on 120 consumers (60 naïve and 60 connoisseurs) to perform a blind cream recognition test to ensure that blindness was maintained during the whole course of the study. The conditions in which this study was carried out are detailed in Appendix S1 (see Supporting Information), and show that there is a structure in place that maintains blindness.

Test procedure

The products to be tested were four moisturizers evaluated in monadic sequence (i.e. one after the other) according to a Latin square experimental design. Two products were evaluated per session, then users came 2 days in a row to evaluate the four products. Laboratoires Gilbert repackaged the four creams in identical white pump bottles. The one-pump pressure delivered very similar quantities for the four products: Lipikar AP+ M[La Roche-Posay Laboratoire Dermatologique (L’Oréal), La Roche-Posay, France], 1.05 g; Xemose (Laboratoires Dermatologiques d’Uriage, Échirolles, France), 1.08 g; Neutraderm Relipid (Laboratoires Gilbert, Hérouville-Saint-Clair, France), 1.15 g; and XeraCalm AD [Laboratoires Dermatologiques Avène (Pierre Fabre Dermo-Cosmétique), Lavaur, France], 1.10 g. Volunteers participated in two sessions due to the evaluation protocol: they could only test two body lotions per session, as the products had to be applied on the forearms (one product per forearm). The sessions were held on two consecutive days to help participants remember the products tested the day before.

The four tested products were fully randomized to avoid any order bias.

Participants evaluated the products immediately after application: firstly, they assessed the appearance and aroma (before application), then they applied the products (in a fixed quantity) and responded immediately to questions about the sensations during and after application.

Considering cream application, consumers applied one pumpful of cream to their forearms. They repeated this operation on their second arm for the second cream.

Blind testing took place at the test site (Actalia Sensoriel premises – Rue Villons les Buissons Caen, France) in dedicated rooms, enabling blindness to be maintained (Appendix S2; see Supporting Information).

Statistics

We used a convenient sample and no a priori sample size was computed.

The first question was a closed question to blindfolded patients: ‘Did you recognize the cream you tested?’Permitted answers were ‘Yes or No’. For this, we use a binomial test alpha risk 5% comparison of Yes/No proportions for each product.

Sensory perception studies were done of the four tested moisturizers and investigated shiny appearance, thickness, odour intensity in the bottle and odour intensity after application, thick appearance, ease of spreading on the skin, speed of penetration, freshness sensation, shiny skin, oily skin, sticky skin and soft skin. For ratings of appreciation and of sensory perceptions of products, comparison of means between products was done with Anova: Anova on the attribute factor with product factor and judge (consumers), with a fixed model, no interaction term and an alpha risk 5%.

If post hoc analyses were performed, the Newman–Keuls test was used in the following way: multiple comparisons, starting with the farthest starting with the pairs furthest apart, then moving successively closer successively to the closest pairs, with correction for the inflation of alpha risk according to the number of comparisons performed.

Results

Characterization of the consumer panel

We included 120 consumers from the Caen area, 87.5% (n = 105/120) of whom were women and with an age distribution as follows: 40.8% (n = 49/120) 50–65 years old; 25.8% (n = 31/120) 35–49 years old; 20.8% (n = 25/120) 18–34 years old; and 12.5% (n = 15/120) over 66 years of age. Consumers were representative of all socioprofessional categories, with 41.7% (n = 50/120) employees, 25.0% (n = 30/120) retired, 15.0% (n = 18/120) in middle management, 5.8% (n = 7/120) ‘other’, 5.0% (n = 6/120) stay-at-home parents, 2.5% (n = 3/120) students, 2.5% (n = 3/120) senior executives, 1.6% (n = 2/120) blue-collar workers and 0.8% (n = 1/120) craftsmen.

Fifty-one per cent (n = 61/120) reported that their skin was dry, 22.5% (n = 27/120) reported very dry skin with an atopic tendency, 20.0% (n = 24/120) reported mixed skin and 6.7% (n = 8/120) normal skin. In this panel, the emollient creams used on a daily basis were Exomega control (A-derma, Pierre Fabre Dermo-Cosmétique) (n = 1), Adopt (Adopt Parfums, Cestas, France) (n = 1), Bepanthen (Bayer Consumer Health, Leverkusen, Germany) (n = 1), L’Occitane (L’Occitane en Provence, Manosque, France) (n = 1), Love & Green (Love & Green, Boulogne-Billancourt, France) (n = 1), Lovea (Laboratoires Biocos, Clermont-Ferrand, France) (n = 1), Roger Cavailles (Pierre Fabre Dermo-Cosmétique) (n = 1), Clarins (Clarins Groupe, Paris, France) (n = 2), Ducray (Pierre Fabre Dermo-Cosmétique) (n = 2), Laino (Laboratoire Gilbert, Hérouville-Saint-Clair, France) (n = 2), Nuxe (Laboratoire Nuxe, Paris, France) (n = 2), SVR (Laboratoire SVR, Boulogne-Billancourt, France) (n = 2), Rituals (Rituals Cosmetics, Amsterdam, Netherlands) (n = 4), Avene [Laboratoires Dermatologiques Avène (Pierre Fabre Dermo-Cosmétique)] (n = 5), Bioderma [Laboratoire Dermatologique Bioderma (NAOS), Aix-en-Provence, France) (n = 5)], Dexeryl (Pierre Fabre Healthcare, Castres, France) (n = 5), CeraVe [CeraVe (L’Oréal Group), Asnières-sur-Seine, France] (n = 6), Xémose (Laboratoires Dermatologiques d’Uriage) (n = 7), Neutraderm Relipid+ (Laboratoires Gilbert) (n = 8), Yves Rocher [Yves Rocher (Groupe Rocher), La Gacilly, France] (n = 8), Le Petit Marseillais (Johnson & Johnson Santé Beauté France, Issy-les-Moulineaux, France) (n = 9), Nivea (Beiersdorf, Hambourg, Germany) (n = 9), Mixa (L’Oréal France (Division Cosmétique Active), Clichy, France) (n = 15) and Lipikar baume AP + M [La Roche-Posay Laboratoire Dermatologique (L’Oréal Groupe), La Roche-Posay, France] (n = 22). Daily emollients were purchased by 50%, 32% and 17% in parapharmacies, supermarkets and specialist retailers respectively, and 1% in places other than those mentioned above.

Participants reported that they would expect an emollient cream to be moisturizing (99.2%, n = 119/120), nourishing (98.3%, n = 118/120), repairing (86.7%, n = 104/120), soothing (75.0%, n = 90/120), anti-itching (65.0%, n = 78/120), antiscratching (64.2%, n = 77/120) and antiredness (59.2%, n = 71/120).

There were no significant differences in overall appearance, surface shine or transparency among the four emollients. However, Neutraderm cream was significantly perceived as the most fluid (3.9/7) while La Roche-Posay was perceived as the thickest (4.9/7) based on visual assessment. The sensation of freshness after application was significantly more intense with Neutraderm cream (5.2/7).

Global appreciation of creams

Overall, the Neutraderm, La Roche-Posay and Uriage creams were significantly more appreciated than Avène cream, with respective satisfaction rates of 58%, 64% and 61% compared with 45% for Avène. This was reflected in global appreciation scores of 6.6/10, 6.8/10 and 6.8/10, respectively, vs. 5.9/10 for Avène (Figure 3).

Overall appreciation in the naïve and connoisseurs subgroups; blue: connoisseurs; yellow: naïve.

When subdividing participants into the ‘naïve’ or ‘connoisseurs’ subgroups, no significant differences were observed in the evaluation of the Neutraderm, La Roche-Posay, Avène and Uriage creams between these two consumer segments. However, a trend towards a more favourable overall assessment of La Roche-Posay cream by connoisseurs was noted (P = 0.06). Global appreciation scores for all creams were slightly higher among connoisseurs compared with naïve users, as illustrated in Figure 3.

In terms of texture and preapplication odour, no significant differences were observed in the assessments of the Neutraderm, La Roche-Posay, Avène and Uriage creams between naïve and connoisseur consumer subgroups. Regarding postapplication application odour, the Neutraderm, La Roche-Posay and Uriage creams exhibited no significant differences in evaluation between the two consumer subgroups. However, the postapplication scent of the Avène cream was significantly more appreciated by the connoisseur subgroup. Overall, odour ratings after application were consistently more favourable among the connoisseur compared with naïve users across all creams.

Discussion

Blinding is essential to ensure the validity and reliability of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in cosmetology. Although unique challenges arise due to the distinct sensory characteristics of cosmetic products, strategies such as the use of placebos, identical packaging, double-blinding procedures, investigator training and objective assessment methods can be employed to mitigate these obstacles. By rigorously applying these strategies, researchers can minimize bias and generate results that are more credible and acceptable to both the scientific community and regulatory authorities. Nevertheless, maintaining blinding in cosmetology remains a substantial and unique challenge. Cosmetic products often exhibit distinct sensory properties (e.g. scent, texture and colour) that may reveal product identity, particularly through immediate perceptible effects (i.e. visible or tactile sensation upon application), making it difficult to maintain blinding. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dermocosmetic study to specifically address this issue. In fact, we deliberately adopted the methodological rigour of RCTs within the field of cosmetology and designed a preliminary sensory study to ensure that blinding could be preserved.

Among the four commercially available creams tested, participants were unable to recognize the product based solely on its sensory characteristics. The sensory signatures of the creams were not distinct enough to enable identification, as only 6% correctly identified Neutraderm, 8% La Roche-Posay, 6% Avene and 4% Uriage.

Overall, we observed a satisfaction rate of approximately 60% for the tested topicals. While acceptable, this rate remains modest. It is important to note that these satisfaction ratings were based solely on sensory perception and did not assess the functional efficacy of the emollients. This is a critical consideration, as emollients for atopic dermatitis are primarily designed as therapeutic dermocosmetics rather than products intended for sensory pleasure. This point is further emphasized by the fact that skin finish and fragrance received the lowest scores in the global assessment. In the context of atopic dermatitis, where the skin is highly reactive, the formulation of fragranced products poses a significant challenge as fragrances may trigger atopic reactions. Individuals with atopic dermatitis frequently exhibit heightened sensitivity to fragrances, which can exacerbate their skin conditions. Fragrance allergies are common in this population, and exposure to scented products often leads to dermatitis or irritant reactions. This heightened susceptibility is due to the compromised barrier function in atopic skin, which increases vulnerability to allergens and irritants, leading to flare-ups and discomfort upon fragrance exposure. To mitigate these risks, dermatologists generally recommend the use of fragrance-free products.36

Appreciation ratings followed the same trend across connoisseurs and naïve participants, although connoisseurs tended to provide slightly higher evaluations overall.

One of the limitations of this study when extrapolating to future clinical trials is that each participant was required to choose among nine product options available in this specific market, making recognition more challenging compared with typical double-blind trial designs that involve only two products. A potential criticism is that our research focused exclusively on immediate sensory perceptions. However, the study was designed to assess purely cosmetic and sensory parameters, despite the fact that some sensations may persist over time. Longer-term sensory effects were not evaluated. Similarly, participants’ preferences for specific sensory attributes do no guarantee any clinical benefit or therapeutic efficacy of the emollients tested. Nonetheless, the primary objective of our study was to ascertain whether sensory perception could compromise the blinding process, rather than to evaluate product efficacy. Another potential concern relates to our participant demographics, with 88% of the sample consisting of middle-aged women. However, this reflects the natural demographic distribution of our recruitment panel (comprising 4500 individuals of whom 70% are women). Given that our study’s objective was not to assess therapeutic outcomes in patients with atopic dermatitis, but rather to evaluate product recognition through sensory stimuli, the lack of representativeness does not undermine the study’s aims.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that even among participants experienced in the use of emollients and topical products, sensory perceptions of the creams (whether in a container, during dispensing or upon skin application) were not sufficiently distinctive to allow for product identification. This finding is crucial in confirming that double-blind clinical trials involving these emollients can be conducted without the risk of blinding being compromised by sensory cues.

The daily routine of patients with atopic dermatitis typically involves the application of emollients once or multiple times per day. Nevertheless, the majority of clinical trials in atopic dermatitis focus primarily on evaluating the efficacy of systemic therapies, while the role of dermocosmetic products is often underexplored in controlled clinical settings.

Our study demonstrates that patients are unable to distinguish between different emollients based solely on sensory attributes. This preliminary study suggests feasibility in healthy volunteers under controlled short-term conditions, but further validation is needed in patients to ensure that blinding can be effectively maintained in clinical trials involving dermocosmetic products.
