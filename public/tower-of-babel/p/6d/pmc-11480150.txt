Progression from Non-alcoholic Steatohepatitis to Advanced Liver Diseases and Mortality Among Medicare Patients
Authors: Yestle Kim, Joe Medicis, Matthew Davis, Dominic Nunag, Robert Gish
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNon-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) may progress to more advanced liver disease. This study aimed to characterize NASH progression and mortality in the Medicare population.MethodsPatients with NASH in 100% Medicare fee-for-service claims accrued from 2015–2021 who were ≥ 66 years old at index diagnosis, continuously enrolled for ≥ 12 months prior to and ≥ 6 months following index (unless death), and had no evidence of other causes of liver disease were included. Diagnosis codes defined severity states: non-cirrhotic NASH, compensated cirrhosis (CC), decompensated cirrhosis (DCC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver transplant (LT). Survival analyses of disease progression and mortality were conducted for each state and by year of progression (Y1–5). Cox proportional hazards models assessed risk factors of worsening disease.ResultsMean age and follow-up were 72.2 and 2.8 years in 14,806 unique patients (n = 12,990 NASH; 1899 CC; 997 DCC; 209 HCC; 140 LT). Progression rates were highest for patients with CC (11–37% for Y1–5), followed by DCC (3–18%), NASH (3–12%), and HCC (2–4%). Mortality rates were highest for patients with HCC (41–85% for Y1–5), followed by DCC (41–76%), LT (7–33%), CC (6–26%), and NASH (2–12%). Patients with any disease progression had a 5-year mortality rate more than double that of patients without progression (41% vs. 16%).Delayed progression from NASH was associated with lower mortality risk; the 5-year mortality rate was 26% lower for patients with progression in Y2 vs. Y1 (32% vs. 43%) and further decreased for progression in Y3-Y5. Risk factors included age, nursing home use, congestive heart failure, coagulopathy, fluid/electrolyte disorders, and unexplained weight loss.ConclusionMedicare patients ≥ 66 years with NASH experience high risk of disease progression associated with increased mortality rates. Slower disease progression is associated with lower mortality rates, suggesting that therapies that can delay or prevent NASH progression may reduce morbidity and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-024-02979-7.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, is a severe form of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [1]. NASH is distinguished by the presence of hepatic inflammation [2]. As patients with early-stage NASH often remain asymptomatic, the condition is significantly underdiagnosed and prevalence estimates vary widely [3]. Most prevalence estimates for NASH range from 1 to 6% of US adults [4–6].

Patients with NASH may progress to more advanced liver disease states, including compensated cirrhosis (CC), decompensated cirrhosis (DCC), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and may eventually require a liver transplant (LT) [2]. Prior studies have found that 1 in 5 patients with NASH progress within 2 years to a subsequent severity state [7]. More advanced severity states have been associated with an increased risk of mortality [5]. In addition, research in other liver diseases, including chronic viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver diseases, and autoimmune liver diseases, has shown cirrhosis to be a key link between liver inflammation and HCC onset, which further increases the risk of death [8–10]. There is a lack of recent literature that addresses the relationship between the timing of NASH progression and the risk of mortality, though there is evidence that older patients are more likely to experience NASH progression than younger patients [11, 12]. Thus, Medicare-aged patients are a relevant population to analyze when aiming to understand NASH progression.

Until recently, treatments were not available that had demonstrated efficacy in addressing liver damage associated with NASH; however, resmetirom, a liver-directed thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist designed to target key underlying causes of NASH, was approved under priority review by the FDA in March 2024 for the treatment of adults with non-cirrhotic NASH with moderate to advanced fibrosis in conjunction with diet and exercise [13]. The ongoing pivotal Phase 3 trial (MAESTRO-NASH; NCT03900429) demonstrated that resmetirom stabilized or halted progression of fibrosis in approximately 90% of patients with pre-cirrhotic NASH [14]. With a treatment for NASH now available, a better understanding of the consequences of NASH disease progression and progression timing is needed to inform treatment decisions.

The aim of our study was to characterize NASH progression among the Medicare population and to examine the relationship between progression and mortality risk. We hypothesized that progression of NASH would be associated with an increased risk of mortality in Medicare patients.

Methods

Data Source

This observational study used 100% of Medicare fee-for-service insurance claims accrued from October 1, 2015 through December 31, 2021. The data included hospital (Part A), physician (Part B), and pharmacy (Part D) claims.

An Institutional Review Board exemption was not required for this study as the data were de-identified and compliant with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations. This study was conducted according to best practices recommended by the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Data for Pharmacoepidemiology guidelines [15]. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent updates. Database permission was provided by CMS.

Study Design

This was a retrospective, non-interventional study of administrative claims data conducted to estimate progression and mortality rates among patients with NASH or advanced liver disease that progressed from NASH.

The index date was defined as the date associated with each eligible disease severity state: non-cirrhotic NASH, CC, DCC, HCC, and LT (Supplementary Material Fig. 1). Consistent with previous literature, for patients with observed disease progression during the study period (e.g., transition from non-cirrhotic NASH to CC), the diagnosis date for the new disease state was considered a new index date [5]. Study Population

Patients with NASH were identified based on having at least one claim with International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) code K75.81 as the primary or secondary diagnosis on an inpatient claim, or diagnoses on two separate outpatient claims. Patients were required to be aged ≥ 66 years on index date (date of diagnosis), have had continuous enrollment in Medicare Parts A, B, and D for at least 12 months prior to index and 6 months following index (unless death), and have had no enrollment in Medicare Part C. An age of ≥ 66 years as of the index date was required to allow for 12 months of baseline observation following the automatic Medicare enrollment age of 65 years. Disease state cohorts were constructed by assigning patients to each disease state for which they had a qualifying index date. A 90-day grace period was used to account for the time between biopsy/imaging procedures and a follow-up visit confirming diagnosis of advanced liver disease. Thus, patients with a diagnosis for a more advanced liver disease state prior to or within 90 days following an index date were considered to have the more advanced disease as of the earlier index date. In subgroup analyses, patients were stratified based on whether progression to a more advanced disease state was observed following study entry. Patients in the non-cirrhotic NASH cohort were further stratified based on the year they progressed to a more advanced disease state post-index [year 1 (Y1), year 2 (Y2), year 3 (Y3), year 4 (Y4), year 5+ (Y5+)].

Outcomes

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race, census region, coverage type, nursing home use, and comorbidities, were assessed in the 12 months prior to index date. In the follow-up period, outcome measures of disease progression and mortality were assessed. Disease progression was identified based on claims with a diagnosis/procedure code for more advanced liver disease states.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics of baseline covariates were assessed by cohort. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses for time to disease progression and time to mortality were conducted for each state (non-cirrhotic NASH, CC, DCC, HC, LT). Time to disease progression analyses treated end of follow-up and death as censoring events, and time to mortality analyses treated end of follow-up and disease progression as censoring events. Subgroup analyses stratified time to mortality by year of progression from NASH (Y1–5). Cox proportional hazards models assessed baseline risk factors associated with worsening disease (progression or mortality).

Consistent with Medicare data use agreements and to prevent patient re-identification, any outputs representing N < 11 were redacted, as were medians for any populations where N < 50 [17].

Data analysis was performed using SAS software v.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 14,806 unique patients met the study eligibility criteria and were included in the overall sample (Table 1). Demographic and clinical characteristics are reported in Table 2. Of the study sample, 12,990 patients were initially identified with non-cirrhotic NASH, 1899 were diagnosed with CC, 997 were diagnosed with DCC, 209 were diagnosed with HCC, and 140 had LT at index. The mean (SD) [median] patient age was 72.2 (5.5) [71.0] years. The mean [median] ages of patients with NASH and LT, at 72.1 [71] and 70.5 [70] respectively, were younger compared to patients with CC (72.9 [72]), DCC (74.7 [74]), and HCC (74.3 [74]). The mean (SD) [median] length of follow up was 2.8 (1.5) [2.8] years. Patients in the sample were predominantly white (84%) and female (63%).

Discussion

In this retrospective, observational study of Medicare patients with NASH, we found that patients with non-cirrhotic NASH (n = 12,990) and patients with CC (n = 1899) experienced high rates of disease progression (12% and 37%, respectively, at 5 years) and that patients with non-cirrhotic NASH experienced the lowest rate of mortality compared to patients with more advanced liver disease states. Delayed disease progression and absence of progression were both associated with lower mortality. Older patients, patients with dual enrollment in Medicaid, patients in nursing homes, patients living in the Midwest or South, and patients with specific comorbidities faced increased risk of death or disease progression. Prior studies have shown that patients experiencing disease progression also incur significantly increased healthcare costs [18]. Thus, it may be particularly important to ensure these groups have access to NASH treatments that may slow progression to advanced liver diseases, especially those who may not be eligible for liver transplants.

Our estimates are aligned with the existing literature’s consensus that NASH progression timing is heterogeneous, with most patients’ disease progressing slowly and a subset experiencing accelerated progression. Loomba et al. (2019) found that approximately 20% of patients with baseline F3 fibrosis progressed to cirrhosis and approximately 20% of patients with cirrhosis progressed to decompensation within 2 years [7]. We similarly found that 19% of patients with cirrhosis experienced disease progression within 2 years and 12% of patients with NASH did so within 5 years. The Loomba study’s greater NASH progression estimate may in part be explained by its focus on patients with F3 fibrosis staging. Our research also builds on previous research that used 20% Medicare data from 2007 to 2015, providing more recent and representative progression estimates for NASH, which is pertinent as practice patterns and clinical guidelines have changed since 2015 [19]. Additionally, Allen et al. (2022) used a multistate model with a sample of patients from Olmsted, Minnesota, USA, to estimate that 33% of patients diagnosed with CC progressed to decompensation/HCC/LT in 4 years, which closely compares to our estimate of 31% of patients with CC experiencing progression within 4 years [20].

To contextualize our mortality rate estimates, previous literature from Loomba et al. (2020) found in a Medicare 20% sample that patients with NAFLD/NASH alone, CC, DCC, HCC, and LT had 5-year mortality rates of 8%, 20%, 39%, 72%, and 40%, respectively [5]. Compared to Loomba’s estimates, we found that patients with NASH had a 12% mortality rate in 5 years. Our NASH cohort did not include patients with NAFLD, thus we would expect a mortality rate higher than Loomba’s NAFLD/NASH alone cohort. Our estimates of mortality rates for patients with CC, DCC, and HCC exceeded Loomba’s, at 26%, 76%, and 85%, respectively, while we estimated 33% of patients with LT experienced death in 5-years, slightly under Loomba’s estimate. The large discrepancy in DCC mortality is likely due to methodological differences in identifying patients with DCC, as we used a stricter algorithm-based approach to classify patients into the DCC cohort.

Strengths of this study include the usage of 100% Medicare data. The large database allowed for a sample of nearly 15,000 patients with NASH from across the US and access to patient demographics, diagnosis, and mortality data. Additionally, use of Medicare data allows for longer follow-up observation than in commercial claims databases.

There are numerous limitations to this analysis. There may be inaccuracies stemming from the intrinsic nature of administrative claims, including lack of clinical fidelity and lack of information on patients prior to their entry into the database. Also, there is limited generalizability of results outside the Medicare fee-for-service population, to patients with NASH younger than 66 years old, and to populations outside the study period. The results are from descriptive analyses and thus cannot infer causality. The mortality by progression year subgroup analyses may be subject to immortal time bias, as patients who experienced disease progression in Y1–5 were required to survive until Y1–5 to be classified into those cohorts. Thus, time to mortality results by progression year should be interpreted solely as a subgroup comparison. Also, outputs representing n < 11 and medians for populations where n < 50 are redacted. Disease progression that occurs post-liver transplant is not included in the analyses. Lastly, there is an inability to identify fibrosis staging or confirm disease states due to lack of laboratory/biopsy results, and the data do not allow for identification of cause of death which may be unrelated to NASH or more advanced liver disease.

Conclusion

NASH progression among Medicare patients ≥ 66 years of age is associated with higher risk of mortality, and slower progression is associated with lower risk of mortality. This analysis suggests that there is potential benefit to delaying and/or preventing disease progression at any stage of liver disease.
