Comprehensive identification of immune-related biomarkers and therapeutic targets in preeclampsia: integrative bioinformatics and experimental validation
Authors: Xiuyan Wu, Xuemei Li, Yaoru Wu, Huijuan Yang, Jianbo Wu, Lidan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) is a serious hypertensive complication during pregnancy characterized by immune dysregulation and vascular dysfunction, however, the precise molecular mechanisms and effective therapeutic strategies remain unclear. This study focused on identifying immune-related differentially expressed genes (IRDEGs) in PE, investigate their biological significance and regulatory networks, and establish robust diagnostic models through integrated bioinformatics and experimental analyses.MethodsGene expression data from the GSE75010 dataset were analyzed utilizing the R-based "limma" package to determine differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were intersected with immune-related genes (IRGs) to obtain IRDEGs. Functional enrichment was assessed using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Disease Ontology (DO) analyses. Hub genes were identified via Random Forest (RF) and LASSO regression algorithms, and their diagnostic performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation in both training (GSE75010) and validation (GSE44711) cohorts. Immune cell composition and its association with hub genes were explored using CIBERSORT. Regulatory networks, including protein–protein interaction (PPI), mRNA-miRNA and mRNA-TF interactions, were constructed using ENCORI and CHIPBase databases. Analysis of potential pharmaceutical-gene interactions was performed via DGIdb platform interrogation, followed by experimental validation in placental tissue and trophoblast cells.ResultsWe identified 354 DEGs, including 49 IRDEGs (25 upregulated and 24 downregulated). Enrichment evaluation demonstrated that IRDEGs were associated with PI3K-AKT signaling, chemokine signaling, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. DO analysis linked IRDEGs to PE, cardiovascular diseases, and reproductive disorders. Four hub genes (FLT1, PIK3CB, KLRD1, and APLN) were identified as PE biomarkers based on their connectivity in the PPI network and performance in machine learning models. The RF-based diagnostic model demonstrated excellent discrimination ability with AUCs of 0.9468 (training cohort) and 0.9844 (validation cohort). Immune infiltration analysis revealed higher levels of eosinophils, plasma cells, and CD8 + T cells in PE, while monocytes and M2 macrophages were reduced. Notably, hub genes showed distinct correlations with immune cell subtypes, such as the positive association observed between FLT1 and plasma cells, contrasting with the inverse relationship documented between APLN and CD8 + T cells. Network analysis identified 128 mRNA-miRNA and 31 mRNA-TF interaction pairs. Drug-gene interaction analysis showed cyclooxygenase inhibitors, such as aspirin, targeted APLN, while TNF-α inhibitors, such as etanercept, targeted KLRD1. Experimental validation confirmed consistent expression trends across clinical specimens and in vitro models: FLT1 and PIK3CB were significantly upregulated while KLRD1 and APLN were significantly downregulated in both preeclamptic placental tissues and hypoxia-exposed trophoblast cells.ConclusionsOur study identified four hub IRDEGs that may serve as potential diagnostic indicators and therapeutic targets for PE. These findings suggest an important role of immune dysregulation in PE pathogenesis and offer new perspectives for treatment strategies. By integrating computational predictions with experimental evidence, our work contributes to the foundation for future clinical applications, though further research including early-stage PE is needed to validate these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08169-9.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Immune-related genes, Bioinformatics, Diagnostic biomarkers, Experimental validation, Therapeutic targets

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a severe pregnancy-specific hypertensive disorder, affecting roughly 2–8% of gestations globally and contributing substantially to maternal–fetal morbidity and mortality [1]. This multisystem disorder manifests primarily beyond the 20th gestational week and presents clinically with novo hypertension accompanied by multi-organ dysfunction, including but not limited to proteinuria and hematological abnormalities [2]. Despite considerable progress in obstetric care over recent decades, our understanding of PE's etiology and pathogenesis remains incomplete, hampering efforts toward effective prevention and intervention. Current clinical approaches focus predominantly on symptom management through antihypertensive therapy and vigilant monitoring, with delivery remaining the only definitive resolution regardless of gestational age [2, 3]. This therapeutic limitation underscores the urgent necessity for early identification, enhanced diagnostic capabilities, and novel treatment approaches.

The pathophysiology of PE exhibits considerable complexity, involving abnormal placentation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and endothelial dysfunction [3–5]. Notably, immune dysregulation has emerged as a critical factor in PE development. Disrupted immune homeostasis, characterized by altered immune cell infiltration and cytokine signaling, contributes significantly to the disease pathogenesis [6, 7]. Immune-related genes (IRGs) potentially play determinative roles in PE pathogenesis. though their specific mechanisms of action and regulatory networks warrant remain unclear.

Cutting-edge developments in next-generation sequencing technologies and computational biological analysis have enabled the systematic identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and their functional significance in various diseases [8–10]. Machine learning approaches now enable detection of disease-specific molecular signatures and construction of predictive diagnostic frameworks with enhanced accuracy [11–13]. While several studies have explored the role of IRGs in PE [14], a comprehensive analysis integrating immune-related differentially expressed genes (IRDEGs), functional enrichment, immune infiltration, and diagnostic model construction remains lacking.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of IRDEGs in PE using a multi-faceted bioinformatics strategy based on placental transcriptomic data from public repositories. Specifically, we sought to: identify and functionally characterize the IRDEGs through enrichment and network analyses; screen for hub genes with diagnostic potential using advanced feature selection algorithms; characterize immune microenvironment alterations in PE placenta; establish regulatory networks involving microRNAs and transcription factors; and predict candidate therapeutic compounds targeting the hub genes. This comprehensive approach is expected to provide novel insights into immune-mediated mechanisms underlying PE pathogenesis and to identify potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for clinical application.

Materials and methods

Obtainment and preprocessing of datasets

The transcriptomic datasets GSE75010 [15] and GSE44711 [16] were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) utilizing the "GEOquery" R package [17]. The primary dataset (GSE75010) was generated on the GPL6244 platform ([HuGene-1_0-st] Affymetrix Human Gene 1.0 ST Array, transcript version), comprising placental tissue samples from 80 preeclamptic pregnancies and 77 normotensive controls. For validation purposes, we incorporated the GSE44711 dataset, generated on the Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0 expression beadchip platform (GPL10558), which contains an equal distribution of preeclamptic and normotensive placental samples (8 each). The GSE75010 was employed as the training dataset, while GSE44711 served as an external validation cohort, with comprehensive dataset characteristics detailed in Supplementary Table S1. To investigate immune-related mechanisms, we obtained a curated inventory of 1,793 immune-related genes (IRGs) from the Immunology Database and Analysis Portal (ImmPort, https://www.immport.org/shared/), a specialized repository for immunological research data.

Analysis of immune-related differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis was performed between preeclamptic and normotensive placental tissues using the "limma" package in R (v 3.6.3) [18]. Genes were classified differentially expressed when they satisfied dual statistical thresholds: |log fold change (FC)|> 0.05 and adjusted p-value (adj.p) < 0.05. Within these parameters, genes exhibiting logFC > 0.05 were designated as upregulated, while those with logFC < −0.05 were classified as downregulated. To specifically identify IRDEGs with altered expression in PE, we intersected the DEGs with the previously compiled IRGs. The resultant IRDEGs were visualized via volcano plots, Venn diagrams, and heatmaps and created with the "ggplot2" and "heatmap" packages in R.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) [19] categorized candidates genes across three complementary territories: Biological Processes (BP), Molecular Functions (MF), and Cellular Components (CC), providing a structured interpretation of gene roles within established biological systems. Concurrently, we identify biochemical and signaling pathways among these genes using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database [20]. To connect our findings with clinical relevance, we employed Disease Ontology (DO) [21] analysis, which maps genes to a comprehensive catalog of human disorders using standardized nomenclature.

Functional enrichment analyses were conducted within the R environment using specialized bioinformatics packages. GO and KEGG analyses were executed via the "clusterProfiler" package [22], while DO term enrichment utilized the "DOSE" package. Enriched terms were considered statistically significant when meeting dual criteria: adj.p < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, with multiple testing correction applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method.

Protein–protein interaction network construction

We examined the functional relationships among proteins encoded by the identified IRDEGs by developing an interaction network. Network construction utilized the STRING database (v11.0, https://cn.string-db.org/) [23], which integrates protein associations derived from experimental determinations, computational predictions, and literature Mining. To ensure biological relevance, we applied a stringent confidence threshold of 0.700 for interaction inclusion. The constructed network was visualized and topological analyzed with Cytoscape software (v3.9.1, https://cytoscape.org/) [24], enabling the identification of highly connected nodes and interaction patterns within the IRDEG-derived proteome.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To evaluate systematic expression shifts in functionally related gene clusters across the compared biological conditions, we employed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [25]. This approach examines whether members of predefined gene sets show consistent differences between biological conditions. We performed the analysis through the "clusterProfiler" R package, employing 1, 000 permutations cycles to establish statistical reliability. Computational parameters were standardized with a fixed random seed (2020) and gene set size filters (Minimum: 10 genes; maximum: 500 genes). Statistical significance was controlled using the BH procedure for multiple comparison adjustment. For pathway definition, we selected the comprehensive canonical pathway collection from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB v2022.1, "c2.cp.all.v2022.1.hs.symbols.gmt"), which comprises 3,050 curated gene sets representing established biological processes [26]. Significantly enriched pathways were identified based on combined criteria of FDR below 0.25 and adj.p less than 0.05.

Hub genes identification and diagnostic model construction

We employed the Random Forest (RF) algorithm [27] to construct a classification model using the expression profiles of the 49 identified IRDEGs. Random Forest represents an ensemble learning methodology that aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees, each trained on bootstrap samples of the original data. The analysis was implemented using the "randomForest" R package [28] with computational standardized parameters (seed = 500, ntree = 500). Feature importance was evaluated through two metrics: MeanDecreaseGini, which quantifies node impurity reduction, and MeanDecreaseAccuracy, which measures prediction accuracy decline when specific variables are permuted. Higher values in both metrics indicated greater diagnostic relevance for PE, facilitating prioritization of potential biomarkers.

Following initial feature selection by RF, we implemented Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) [29] regression via the "glmnet" R package [30] to optimize our predictive model. This technique incorporates regularization through a penalty coefficient (λ) applied to absolute parameter values, simultaneously reducing overfitting risk and enhancing model performance.The regularization process systematically excluded less contributory variables while preserving features with strong predictive capability. The coefficient trajectories were visualized against the logarithm of λ values to determine optimal regularization parameters. The top four ranked IRDEGs from this analysis were designated as hub genes for the final diagnostic model.

Hub gene diagnostic efficacy was evaluated through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the "pROC" R package [31]. These curves illustrate classification accuracy by mapping sensitivity against 1-specificity across decision thresholds. Discriminatory capacity was quantified by under the curve (AUC) calculation, where values nearing 1.0 reflect exceptional diagnostic precision. Model robustness was confirmed by testing our four-gene signature in an independent validation dataset (GSE44711), confirming the generalizability of our findings across different patient cohorts.

Immunoinfiltration analysis

To characterize the immune microenvironment in disease pathogenesis, we employed computational deconvolution of bulk transcriptomic data using the CIBERSORT algorithm [32]. This method applies machine learning-based linear support vector regression to estimate quantify immune cell compositions within tissue samples. Analysis was conducted using the LM22 signature matrix. Deconvolution was performed with default parameters and 1000 permutations to enhance statistical rigor. We implemented quality control by filtering for samples with CIBERSORT p < 0.05 and positive enrichment scores, thereby reducing false positives. Immune cell distribution visualization employed hierarchical clustering heatmaps via pheatmap. Relationships between candidate genes and immune infiltration were assessed through Spearman's rank correlation and rendered graphically using ggplot2.

Interaction network construction of hub genes

MicroRNA (miRNA) interactions were identified using the ENCORI platform [33] (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/), which integrates data from CLIP-seq and degradome sequencing experiments. mRNA-miRNA interaction pairs were filtered using a stringent threshold (pancancerNum > 6) to prioritize well-supported regulatory relationships. For transcriptional regulation analysis, we utilized CHIPBase v3.0 [34] (https://rna.sysu.edu.cn/chipbase/), which aggregates ChIP-seq derived TF binding data. Significant mRNA-TF interaction pairs were selected when the combined frequency of detection in upstream and downstream regions exceeded five samples, ensuring robust regulatory evidence. Both regulatory networks were visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1 to illustrate the multi-level control mechanisms of hub gene expression.

Potential therapeutic drug prediction

To translate our genomic findings into potential clinical applications, we leveraged the Drug Gene Interaction Database[35] (DGIdb v5.0, https://dgidb.org/) to identify candidate therapeutic compounds targeting our hub genes. This curated resource integrates pharmacological data from multiple authoritative sources including DrugBank, PharmGKB, and ChEMBL. Query parameters were configured to prioritize clinically relevant interactions with experimental or approved drug compounds. The gene-drug interaction network was visualized through Cytoscape 3.9.1.

Clinical data source

Our research cohort comprised 20 PE cases paired with 20 normal pregnancy controls, all selected from patients attending the Obstetrics Department at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University in China (participant details appear in Supplementary Table S2). Research protocols received ethical clearance from the hospital's Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024.076), and documented consent was secured from every participant. PE was identified using diagnostic parameters established by the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) [36].

Immunohistochemical analysis

Placental samples were processed as 4 μm sections from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks. Following deparaffinization and rehydration followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Background staining was reduced with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking prior to primary antibody application. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies targeting FLT1 (Proteintech, 13,687–1-AP, 1:100), PIK3CB (Proteintech, 20,584–1-AP, 1:100), KLRD1 (Affinity, DF6773, 1:100), and APLN (Affinity, DF13350, 1:100). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibodies (ZB-2301, ZSGB-BIO, 1:100) for 1 h at ambient temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized with DAB chromogen followed by hematoxylin nuclear counterstaining. Visual documentation was performed using a digitally-enabled Olympus BX43 microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

The HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cell line (CL-0765, ProCell Life Science & Technology) served as our experimental model. Cells were maintained in DMEM medium (KGL1219-500, Keji Biology) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin antibiotic solution. Cell populations were maintained at 37℃ in a moisture-saturated atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For experimental interventions, these cells were divided into a standard normoxic control group and a hypoxia-exposed experimental group. Hypoxic conditions were generated using a controlled tri-gas incubator system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) programmed to maintain an atmospheric composition of 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2. Cells underwent hypoxic exposure for a 48-h duration, while corresponding control populations were simultaneously maintained under standard oxygen conditions. Upon completion of the treatment period, cellular material was collected for downstream analytical procedures.

Western blot analysis

Cellular protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein concentration were assessed through bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification method (Pierce). Following denaturation, proteins were resolved on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide electrophoresis gels for 90 min, then electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore) at 300 mA for 60 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim Milk solution before overnight exposure to primary antibodies at 4℃, followed by 1-h room temperature incubation with HRP-linked secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using ECL chemiluminescent reagent (Thermo Fisher) and captured using a sensitive digital imaging system (Bio-Rad). Complete antibody information including source and working dilutions is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total cellular RNA was isolated via TRIzol extraction (Invitrogen) and further purified with an RNA purification kit (CWBIO). RNA quality and concentration was assessed by OD260/OD280 ratio (acceptable range: 1.8–2.0) using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (NanoPhotometer NP80). Reverse transcription to cDNA utilized the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme) following manufacturer guidelines, including a genomic DNA removal step at 42 °C for 2 min.

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Each 20 μL reaction Mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CWBIO), 0.5 μL of each forward and reverse primer (0.5 μM final concentration), 2 μL of cDNA template, and 7 μL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling parameters included initial denaturation (95 °C, 10 min), followed by 40 amplification cycles comprising denaturation (95 °C, 10 s), primer annealing (58–60 °C depending on the target gene, 30 s), and polymerase extension (72 °C, 30 s).

The specificity of amplification was verified by melting curve analysis performed immediately after the amplification cycles by Heating from 65 °C to 95 °C with 0.5 °C increments. Each amplification product exhibited a single, sharp melting peak, confirming target-specific amplification. The absence of primer dimers was confirmed by both melting curve analysis and 2% agarose gel electrophoresis of PCR products, which showed single bands of expected sizes without additional low molecular weight bands. All primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and synthesized by General Biosystems (Anhui, China). Transcript levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Complete primer sequences, product sizes, and optimal annealing temperatures are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Statistical analysis

Computational processes were implemented in R (version 4.2.0) using specialized bioinformatics packages. Experimental data analysis employed GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results represent means ± standard deviations from triplicate independent experiments. Multi-group comparisons utilized one-way ANOVA with subsequent post hoc testing. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05.

Identification of hub genes and construction of diagnosis model

We employed dual machine learning approaches (RF and LASSO regression) to extract pivotal genes from the 49 IRDEGs. Initially, the RF algorithm analyzed the expression profiles of 49 IRDEGs between PE and normotensive pregnancy samples. The decision tree error curve analysis indicated an optimal performance at approximately 100–150 decision trees, where error metrics stabilized and reached minimum values (Fig. 5A). Variable importance was quantified through Mean Decrease Gini and Mean Decrease Accuracy parameters, with results visualized via scatter plot representation (Fig. 5B). Ubsequently, LASSO regression was implemented for feature dimension reduction and to mitigate potential model overfitting (Fig. 5C). Model calibration assessment revealed appropriate probability estimation characteristics (Fig. 5D). This dual-algorithm approach identified 12 significant IRDEGs: KLRD1, FABP4, CXCR6, PIK3CB, SEMA3D, SEMA4C, SLIT2, APLN, NTS, FLT1, IL1R2, and OPRK1. Based on statistical significance rankings and biological relevance, four genes (FLT1, PIK3CB, KLRD1, and APLN) were designated as hub genes for diagnostic model development.

To assess the diagnostic potential of these identified hub genes, we constructed a RF-based diagnostic model. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative capability in the primary dataset (GSE75010), with ROC analysis yielding an AUC of 0.9468 (Fig. 5E). Importantly, when applied to an independent validation cohort (GSE44711), the model maintained robust performance with an improved AUC of 0.9844 (Fig. 5F), confirming their reliability as potential diagnostic biomarkers for PE.

Immunoinfiltration analysis

Computational deconvolution of cellular composition within placental tissues using CIBERSORT methodology revealed distinct immunological signatures between preeclamptic and normotensive specimens from the GSE75010 cohort. Quantitative evaluation identified statistically significant enrichment of eosinophils, CD8 + T cell, and plasma cells within preeclamptic samples. Notably, these samples simultaneously displayed marked reductions in monocyte and M2 macrophage proportions (Fig. 6A, B).

To explore potential functional connections between placental immune infiltration patterns and our candidate genes, we performed comprehensive correlation analyses. This analysis revealed that FLT1 expression positively associated with plasma cell, CD8 + T cell, naïve B cell, eosinophil, and regulatory T cell (Tregs), while demonstrating inverse relationships with neutrophil, monocyte, and M2 macrophage populations (Fig. 6C). APLN displayed contrasting immunological associations, negatively correlating with naive B cells, CD8 + T cells, and plasma cells but positive relationships with M2 macrophages and monocytes (Fig. 6D). PIK3CB negatively correlated with neutrophils, monocytes, and M2 macrophages, but positively associating with plasma cells, eosinophils, naive B cells, Tregs, and CD8 + T cells, (Fig. 6E). KLRD1 exhibited negative associations with plasma cells, Tregs, eosinophils, CD8 + T cells, activated NK cells, and naive B cells, while positively associating with neutrophils and M2 macrophages (Fig. 6F).

Interaction network analysis of hub genes

To explore potential transcriptional regulatory mechanisms governing the four identified hub genes, we conducted in silico prediction of miRNA interactions using ENCORI database. Stringent selection criteria were applied, specifically including only interactions documented across multiple cancer types (pancancerNum > 6) to ensure biological relevance. Cytoscape visualization generated a comprehensive mRNA-miRNA interaction landscape (Fig. 7A). The network incorporated our four pivotal immune transcripts (FLT1, APLN, PIK3CB, and KLRD1) and their 124 regulatory MiRNAs, forming 128 unique regulatory connections. Potential pharmacological target analysis

GIdb interrogation revealed 217 distinct compounds interacting with four hub genes. FLT1 interacted with 84 compounds (Fig. 7C), while PIK3CB demonstrated the highest druggability with 126 potential modulating compounds (Fig. 7E). KLRD1 and APLN showed more limited druggability, with five and two interacting compounds respectively (Fig. 7D). Notably, cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors displayed particularly strong interaction characteristics, specifically targeting APLN with inhibitory effects and demonstrating the highest interaction scores in the analysis. Differential expression of four hub IRDEGs in PE placentas

A comparative analysis of clinical characteristics showed that preeclamptic women exhibited significantly higher blood pressure and 24-h urine protein levels compared to normotensive controls, while neonatal birth weight and 1-min Apgar scores were lower in the PE group, suggesting adverse perinatal outcomes. Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed that the expression of FLT1 and PIK3CB was significantly upregulated, and KLRD1 and APLN was markedly downregulated, in placentas from preeclamptic patients relative to controls (p < 0.05, Fig. 8A). Importantly, correlation analysis demonstrated that FLT1 and PIK3CB expression levels were positively associated with systolic/diastolic blood pressure and urine protein, and negatively correlated with gestational age at delivery and neonatal birth weight, while KLRD1 and APLN showed the opposite pattern. These findings suggest that altered expression of these hub IRDEGs is closely related to disease severity and adverse pregnancy outcomes in PE (Fig. 8B).

mRNA and protein expression of hub IRDEGs in HTR-8/SVneo cells under hypoxic conditions

HTR-8/SVneo trophoblasts were subjected to low-oxygen conditions (94% N2, 5% CO2, 1% O2) for 48 h, followed by comprehensive transcriptional and translational profiling. Quantitative PCR assessment revealed distinct transcriptional responses among our four target genes. Specifically, hypoxic treatment triggered significant mRNA increase for FLT1 and PIK3CB (p < 0.05), while simultaneously inducing marked reduction in KLRD1 and APLN transcript abundance relative to cells maintained under standard oxygen tension (Fig. 8C). To determine whether these transcriptional alterations translated to corresponding protein-level changes, we conducted immunoblot analysis of cellular lysates. This investigation confirmed that oxygen deprivation produced parallel modifications in protein expression patterns, with FLT1 and PIK3CB showing enhanced abundance, whereas KLRD1 and APLN proteins exhibited diminished expression compared with normoxic control specimens (p < 0.05, Fig. 8D).

Discussion

Preeclampsia represents a major contributor to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide. However, the underlying pathogenesis remain incompletely understood, and effective therapeutic interventions continue to be limited [1]. At present, the only effective intervention is prompt termination of pregnancy, which can lead to preterm labor and worsen neonatal complications. This clinical dilemma underscores the urgent need to elucidate preeclampsia's molecular pathogenesis, identify reliable diagnostic biomarkers, and develop targeted therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we identified immune key genes, shedding Light on its role in PE. Our analysis revealed 354 DEGs, including 167 upregulated and 187 downregulated genes. Enrichment analysis revealed associations with cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways, chemokine signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and pre-eclampsia ontology, suggesting its significant role in PE pathogenesis. We also identified 49 IRDEGs, comprising 25 upregulated and 24 downregulated genes. Using RF and LASSO algorithms, we pinpointed four hub genes (FLT1, PIK3CB, KLRD1, and APLN) relevant to PE diagnosis, supported by ROC curve analysis. These hub genes were linked to distinct immune cell infiltration Microenvironments and formed a complex regulatory network with 24 transcription factors and 124 MicroRNAs. Additionally, we predicted 217 drug-gene interactions targeting FLT1, APLN, PIK3CB, and KLRD1. Finally, We confirmed hub gene expression patterns through dual verification approaches in clinical placental specimens and in vitro trophoblast cells.

FLT1 (Fms-like tyrosine kinase 1), located on chromosome 13q12, encodes VEGFR1, a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase. FLT1 regulates angiogenesis, vascular permeability, inflammation, and placental development through interactions with VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF. Its soluble form (sFLT1) functions as an angiogenesis antagonist by sequestering VEGF and PlGF, limiting their bioavailability. Elevated sFLT1 levels are a hallmark of PE [37, 38] impairing angiogenesis and inducing endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, and proteinuria. Prolonged sFLT1 elevation disrupts placental angiogenesis and trophoblast proliferation, contributing to placental dysfunction [39, 40]. Thus, FLT1 demonstrates dual clinical utility in preeclampsia as a diagnostic indicator and potential intervention site. APLN gene encodes a bioactive peptide Apelin, which functions as the natural binding partner for the APJ receptor (APLNR). This signaling system contributes to vascular homeostasis, blood vessel formation, cardiac contractile function, and placental vasculature establishment [41, 42]. In PE, particularly severe cases, APLN is significantly downregulated in the placenta, correlating with vascular insufficiency [43]. APLN protects trophoblasts from hypoxia-induced oxidative stress via the PI3K-Akt pathway and promotes cell invasion and migration during early placental formation. Its reduced expression in PE impairs vascular function and placental development [44], and diminished serum Apelin levels have diagnostic potential. FLT1 and APLN have opposing roles in angiogenesis, highlighting the importance of balance between angiogenesis promoters and inhibitors in preeclampsia pathophysiology. Elevated sFLT1 and reduced APLN disrupt placental vascular function, worsening the condition. Targeting this imbalance offers new opportunities for PE treatment, providing insight into its molecular mechanisms and potential therapies.

PIK3CB (Phosphoinositide-3-kinase catalytic subunit beta) is a key catalytic subunit of the PI3K family, involved in cell proliferation, survival, adhesion, and signaling. The PI3K pathway regulates cellular growth, metabolism, and angiogenesis, and is crucial in both physiological and pathological conditions. PIK3CB has been implicated in cancer metastasis and angiogenesis, such as promoting pancreatic cancer metastasis via cell adhesion to collagen I [45] and influencing the tumor microenvironment in gastric cancer [46]. Although its direct link to PE remains unclear, the PI3K-Akt pathway is critical for placental angiogenesis and trophoblast invasion. Dysregulation of this pathway leads to placental maldevelopment and vascular dysfunction, key features of PE [47]. As a core PI3K member, PIK3CB likely contributes substantially to preeclampsia pathophysiology through its influence on trophoblast activity and vascular development. KLRD1 (Killer cell lectin-like receptor D1, or CD94) functions as a cell surface receptor present on NK cells and some T cells, forming heterodimers with NKG2 family members to regulate NK cell activity. NK cells are essential for maternal immune tolerance and placental vascular remodeling, with uterine NK (uNK) cells driving spiral artery remodeling at the maternal–fetal interface [48]. In PE, altered uNK cell numbers and reduced secretion of pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF and PlGF contribute to poor placental angiogenesis. KLRD1's regulation of NK function suggests its involvement in PE-related immune dysregulation [49]. Polymorphisms in KLRD1 gene are linked to samll for gestational age (SGA) births in African-American [50], further supporting its role in pregnancy-related immunity.

The identified hub genes showed predominant association with cytokine-receptor interaction, chemokine signaling pathway, PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, and RAP1 signaling pathway, suggesting that immune dysregulation and vascular dysfunction play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of PE. Cytokines and chemokines are key mediators of immune responses and inflammation, and their dysregulation contributes to the abnormal immune environment in PE. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6 and TNF-α, promote systemic inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular injury in PE [51]. Similarly, aberrant chemokine signaling exacerbates immune cell recruitment and activation, disrupting maternal–fetal immune tolerance and impairing placental development [52, 53]. While our analysis identified the PI3K-AKT pathway as significantly associated with PE, it remains unclear whether its dysregulation represents a primary pathogenic mechanism or occurs secondary to immune dysfunction. Several studies suggest bidirectional interactions between immune signaling and PI3K-AKT activation, where inflammatory cytokines can modulate PI3K-AKT activity, which in turn regulates immune cell function and inflammatory responses [54]. Recent research has demonstrated that inflammation-induced activation of PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling contributes to metabolic alterations in pregnancy-related tissues, highlighting this complex interplay [55]. The RAP1 signaling pathway serves as a pivotal regulator of endothelial function, placental angiogenesis, cell adhesion, and inflammatory responses [56]. Dysregulation of this pathway has been linked to poor placental development, vascular dysfunction, and impaired spiral artery remodeling, leading to reduced placental perfusion [57]. GSEA further highlighted significant enrichment of the HIF1 transcription factor pathway, endothelin signaling cascade in PE. The HIF1 pathway, critical for cellular adaptation to hypoxia, correlates with impaired trophoblast invasion and placental dysfunction [58]. Disruptions in the endothelin signaling pathway, which coordinate vascular contractility and endothelial homeostasis, potentially contribute to blood pressure elevation and endothelial impairment PE [59].

Our analysis reveals complex regulatory networks among the four hub genes. FLT1 appears to function as an upstream regulator, with its increased expression influencing the other hub genes. Elevated sFLT1 inhibits VEGF signaling, which would normally activate the PI3K-AKT pathway involving PIK3CB, creating a negative feedback loop where reduced PI3K-AKT signaling further impairs placental development [39]. This interaction exemplifies how altered angiogenic factors may influence PI3K-AKT signaling, potentially downstream of initial immune dysregulation. Indeed, the temporal sequence of pathway dysregulation in PE remains incompletely understood, with evidence suggesting that immune aberrations may precede and potentially trigger alterations in angiogenic factors and signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT [60, 61]. Multiple studies have established that immune dysregulation, particularly involving NK cells and other immune cell populations, contributes to the early pathogenic events in PE that subsequently lead to the release of anti-angiogenic factors and downstream signaling disruptions [5, 62, 63]. Additionally, hypoxia-induced FLT1 upregulation can suppress APLN expression through HIF-1α-dependent mechanisms, establishing another regulatory connection [64]. The relationship between FLT1 and KLRD1 is mediated through immune microenvironment alterations, where elevated sFLT1 promotes a pro-inflammatory state that affects NK cell function and KLRD1 expression [65]. These interactions form complex feedback loops, with APLN potentially counteracting FLT1's anti-angiogenic effects by promoting VEGF expression and enhancing PI3K-AKT signaling [66].

Immune cells, predominantly T cell subsets, NK cells, macrophages and neutrophils, provide essential functions for embryo attachment and development of the mother-fetus interface. During physiological gestation, these immune cells populations undergo precise control mechanisms to preserve conceptus acceptance. However, in PE, this regulation becomes disrupted, leading to inflammation and oxidative stress abnormalities. Preeclampsia features irregular macrophage distributions that hinder spiral arterial restructuring via disrupted production of vasculogenic mediators [67]. Additionally, Th1 and Th2 cells influence hypertension through their roles in angiotensin release and NK cell activation [68]. Physiological gestations demonstrate Th2 lymphocyte predominance, while preeclamptic conditions exhibit diminished Th2 populations alongside expanded CD4 + cell numbers, inflammatory mediator elevation, and oxidative imbalance indicators [69, 70]. Studies have demonstrated that preeclamptic patients exhibit significantly elevated IL-17A production by CD3(+)CD4(+) T lymphocytes in peripheral blood, accompanied by a marked reduction in immunosuppressive CD25(+)FoxP3(+) Treg cells, suggesting that enhanced Th17 immune responses may contribute to preeclampsia pathogenesis through modulation of the Th1/Th2 immune balance [71]. Recent evidence indicates that gut microbiota dysbiosis in preeclampsia disrupts immune homeostasis by impairing macrophage M2 polarization and promoting inflammatory responses in the placental bed [72]. Our data on immune cell infiltration trends in PE align with these findings. Importantly, these altered immune cells correlated with all four hub genes. Correlation analysis of IRGs found that FLT1 and PIK3CB associated positively with plasma cells, CD8 + T cells, naive B cells, and Tregs, while demonstrating inverse relationships with neutrophils, monocytes, and M2 macrophages. Conversely, KLRD1, and APLN showed positive correlations with neutrophils, M2 macrophages, and monocytes, but showed negative relationships with Tregs, plasma cells, naive B cells and CD8 + T cells. These findings highlight the intricate interactions between immune-associated genes and immune cell population in PE, enhancing our comprehension of disease mechanisms.

Drug-gene interaction screening via the DGIdb repository identified 217 candidate therapeutic agents targeting the four immune-related differentially expressed genes, with PIK3CB emerging as the predominant molecular target. Notably, cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors were found to strongly target APLN. COX inhibitors function by suppressing COX activity. Aspirin, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), is a widely used classical non-selective COX inhibitor for the prevention of PE. Bioinformatics analysis indicated that APLN expression is significantly downregulated in PE patients, and COX inhibitors exert an inhibitory effect on APLN, forming an intriguing contrast with the established clinical use of low-dose aspirin (75–150 mg/day) to prevent PE [73–75] This apparent contradiction reflects the complexity of PE pathogenesis and may be explained by several factors. First, low-dose aspirin selectively targets platelet-derived COX-1 with minimal impact on endothelial cell-derived COX, suggesting that dose-dependent effects might lead to divergent biological outcomes [76]. At low doses (75–150 mg/day), aspirin primarily acetylates COX-1 in platelets, reducing thromboxane A2 production while preserving prostacyclin synthesis in endothelial cells, which may indirectly modulate APLN expression through altered vascular homeostasis [77]. In contrast, higher doses may non-selectively inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2 across multiple cell types, potentially resulting in more pronounced APLN suppression with different physiological consequences [78]. Second, the timing of aspirin administration plays a critical role-prophylactic use before 16 weeks of gestation might activate compensatory mechanisms that counterbalance APLN downregulation or alter downstream signaling pathways, whereas ntervention during later gestational stages may occur after irreversible pathophysiological changes have been established [42]. The gestational time-dependent effects of aspirin on APLN expression likely vary according to the dynamic placental microenvironment and changing maternal–fetal interface throughout pregnancy. Finally, the COX-APLN pathway is likely just one of the many signaling pathways involved in PE, and aspirin may exert protective effects through alternative molecular mechanisms including modulation of inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress reduction, and improved uteroplacental blood flow that collectively outweigh potential negative effects on APLN [66]. These findings provide novel insights into APLN regulation and highlight the need for further research to verify the effects of COX inhibitors on APLN expression through in vitro experiments. Future studies should also explore the time-dependent and dose–response relationships of aspirin on APLN expression and its downstream signaling pathways in different trophoblast cell types and gestational stages to optimize its clinical application in PE management and potentially develop more targeted approaches that preserve beneficial APLN functions while mitigating pathological processes in PE.

In addition to COX inhibitors, TNF-α inhibitors represent another promising therapeutic avenue for PE. Etanercept, a recombinant TNF receptor-Fc fusion protein, inhibites TNF- α activity and specifically targets the KLRD1 gene. Elevated TNF- α levels are a hallmark of PE, resulting in excessive inflammation, a key feature of the disease. Animal studies have demonstrated that etanercept can attenuate PE-associated inflammatory responses, protect placental function, and potentially improve endothelial function [79]. However, it is important to note that despite the encouraging results from preclinical models, large clinical studies have revealed a significant translational gap. These studies demonstrate that TNF-α inhibitors, including etanercept, do not effectively reduce the risk of preeclampsia in real clinical practice. This discrepancy highlights the challenges in translating findings from animal models to human pathology, possibly due to the complex, multifactorial nature of PE, differences in drug metabolism between species, or the timing of intervention in the disease process. Current findings are predominantly limited to preclinical studies, and there is a lack of human clinical trial data and comprehensive safety evaluations for its use during pregnancy. Despite these limitations, etanercept offers a novel therapeutic perspective and warrants further exploration to assess its translational and clinical application in PE treatment.

Our investigation offers novel perspectives on immune-related pathways in PE; however, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite employing sophisticated bioinformatics analysis and preliminary experimental validation, the absence of in-depth mechanistic studies warrants cautious interpretation of the results. Second, while our diagnostic model demonstrated promising performance in independent validation testing, further validation in larger, more diverse populations is essential to ensure its stability and clinical applicability. Third, the precise functional significance of our candidate genes and their immunological interactions remains to be elucidated and require comprehensive investigations through cellular and animal model experiments. Finally, the therapeutic potential of the identified drugs should be rigorously evaluated in preclinical and clinical settings to determine their viability for treating PE. A significant limitation of our study is the exclusive use of placental transcriptomic data at a single time point after disease manifestation. This approach precludes analysis of dynamic changes during early PE development and does not include other tissues involved in PE pathogenesis, such as maternal vascular endothelium or circulating immune cells. This limits our understanding of cause-effect mechanisms, as placental changes may be consequences rather than causes of immune dysfunction. The observed gene expression patterns represent end-stage disease rather than initiating events, potentially obscuring the true sequence of molecular dysregulation. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal sampling of multiple tissue types to better elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune dysregulation in PE and distinguish primary causative factors from secondary adaptive responses.

Conclusions

Our investigation employed comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to identified four immune-related hub genes (FLT1, PIK3CB, KLRD1, and APLN) as potential diagnostic biomarkers for PE. These hub genes exhibited excellent diagnostic performance in both training and validation datasets, supported by the construction of a reliable RF-based diagnostic model. Pathway Enrichment revealed these genes' participation in immune dysfunction and key signaling pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and PI3K-AKT signaling, which are closely associated with PE pathogenesis. Immune infiltration analysis revealed notable alterations in immune cell and complex correlations with the hub genes, further emphasizing the role of immune dysregulation in disease development. Interaction network analysis uncovered significant mRNA-miRNA and mRNA-TF regulatory interactions, while drug-gene interaction analysis identified cyclooxygenase inhibitors and TNF-α inhibitors as potential therapeutic agents. Experimental validation using placental tissue and trophoblast cell models confirmed the differential expression of the hub genes, providing additional evidence of their biological relevance. These insights advance our knowledge of immune-centered mechanisms in preeclampsia pathology while suggesting promising avenues for diagnostic tool development and therapeutic intervention design.
