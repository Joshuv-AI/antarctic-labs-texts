A novel approach to Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fracture: Percutaneous lumbopelvic fixation following manual hyperextension-assisted closed reduction
Authors: Ali Güleç, Abdullah Balcı, Sadettin Çiftci, Ebubekir Eravsar, Selim Safalı, Bahattin Kerem Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransverse sacral fractures with spinopelvic dissociation, commonly referred to as “suicidal jumper's fractures” -particularly Roy-Camille type 2 injuries- represent a rare and unstable injury pattern frequently associated with neurological deficits. Surgical management typically requires complex reduction maneuvers and rigid fixation to restore spinopelvic continuity and optimize functional outcomes.Case presentationWe report the case of an 18-year-old male who sustained a Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fracture following a high-energy motorcycle accident. Neurological examination revealed hypoesthesia in the S3–S5 dermatomes, absent bulbocavernosus reflex and loss of anal sphincter tone. A novel closed reduction technique was performed prior to incision, involving simultaneous manual hyperextension at the lumbosacral junction and counter-traction through the extremities. This maneuver achieved satisfactory realignment without requiring joystick manipulation or external fixation. Percutaneous lumbopelvic fixation was subsequently applied using bilateral L5 pedicle and iliac screws. The patient experienced progressive neurological recovery without implant-related complications.ConclusionThis case illustrates that manual hyperextension-assisted closed reduction -when combined with percutaneous lumbopelvic fixation- may serve as a safe, reproducible, and resource-efficient strategy for Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fractures. This technique eliminates the need for external fixation or open reduction, and may be particularly beneficial in urgent trauma scenarios. Introduction

Transverse sacral fractures with spinopelvic dissociation, commonly referred to as “suicidal jumper's fractures,” represent a rare but devastating injury pattern, typically resulting from high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle collisions or falls from height [1], [2]. These injuries are defined by a transverse fracture line through the sacrum, often accompanied by varying degrees of posterior displacement of the cephalad segment, leading to disconnection between the axial skeleton and the pelvis [3]. Roy-Camille et al. were the first to classify such fractures, with type 2 injuries characterized by posterior translation of the superior sacral fragment relative to the distal segment [1]. This specific pattern carries a high risk of neurological compromise due to compression or avulsion of the sacral nerve roots within the canal, especially those involved in sphincter control and perineal sensation [2], [3]. In such cases with neurological impairments, timely and effective reduction and stabilization of the fracture are important to prevent progression of nerve injury and to facilitate potential recovery [2], [4].

Surgical intervention is considered the standard of care for these injuries, with goals including anatomical realignment, decompression when indicated, and restoration of spinopelvic stability [4]. Various indirect reduction strategies have been proposed to avoid the morbidity associated with open surgical approaches [5], [6]. However, these methods may prolong operative time, require additional instrumentation, and in some cases, fail to adequately reduce posteriorly displaced fragments [6], [7]. In more unstable patterns, triangular osteosynthesis or lumbopelvic fixation constructs are often necessary to provide sufficient mechanical stability and allow early mobilization [4], [8], [9].

In this report, we present a previously undescribed technique for indirect closed reduction of a Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fracture. The reduction was achieved through manual hyperextension-assisted closed reduction -herein referred to as the “GÜLEÇ Technique”- involving posterior-to-anterior pressure at the lumbosacral junction by hands combined with simultaneous counter-traction and extension applied to both upper and lower extremities. This maneuver enabled restoration of sagittal alignment without the need for joystick instrumentation or external fixation and was followed by minimally invasive percutaneous lumbopelvic fixation. To our knowledge, this approach has not been previously reported and offers a simplified, atraumatic alternative for managing this complex injury pattern.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old male presented to the emergency department following a high-energy motorcycle collision, resulting in a transverse sacral fracture consistent with the so-called “suicidal jumper's fracture” pattern, characterized by posterior displacement at the S2 level. The patient was hemodynamically stable but reported severe lumbosacral pain and numbness in the perineal region. Neurological examination revealed hypoesthesia in the S3–S5 dermatomes, absent bulbocavernosus reflex, and loss of anal sphincter tone, suggestive of sacral nerve root involvement. Motor function in the lower extremities was preserved. Digital rectal examination confirmed decreased resting tone, while perianal reflexes were absent [2]. Pelvic computed tomography (CT) revealed a complete transverse fracture line at the S2 level with posterior displacement of the superior sacral segment, consistent with a Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fracture [1] (Fig. 1). The fracture line traversed the sacral canal, with sagittal plane disruption of spinopelvic continuity, indicating spinopelvic dissociation [3], [4] (Fig. 1). Given the instability and neurological findings, urgent surgical reduction and fixation were indicated [2].

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone on a radiolucent operating table. The reduction maneuver began with the application of firm posterior-to-anterior pressure at the lumbosacral junction (Fig. 2). We hypothesize that this action may contribute to relaxation of the anterior longitudinal ligament, potentially increasing lumbosacral lordosis while possibly inducing kyphosis in the proximal sacral segment. It is thought that this controlled compression could facilitate anterior decompression at the fracture site, potentially disimpacting the locked fragments while preserving the fracture hematoma. The reduction was then completed through coordinated bilateral hip hyperextension with axial traction (Fig. 2). Based on current biomechanical understanding, this maneuver may generate tension in the sacroiliac and sacrotuberous ligaments, theoretically producing complementary vector forces that could contribute to anterior translation of the cephalad fragment and posterior reduction of the distal segment. The proposed mechanism suggests that these integrated forces might enable anatomical reduction through ligamentotaxis while minimizing soft tissue disruption and preserving the biological environment essential for healing. Fluoroscopic imaging confirmed satisfactory reduction before proceeding to percutaneous fixation (Fig. 3). The stabilization phase employed bilateral L5 pedicle screws and iliac screws inserted through separate stab incisions using a guidewire-assisted technique. Precontoured rods were tunneled subfascially to complete the construct, providing rigid spinopelvic fixation while maintaining a minimally invasive approach [4], [6]. The entire procedure was completed without complications and with minimal blood loss (approximately 50 mL), supporting the safety and feasibility of this technique. Postoperative computed tomography confirmed satisfactory reduction of the sacral fracture and appropriate screw placement, restoring spinopelvic continuity (Fig. 4).

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the neurosurgical unit. Non-weight-bearing mobilization was maintained for six weeks. Sensory improvement in the S3–S5 dermatomes began at postoperative week 2, with gradual return of perianal sensation by week 6. At the 3-month follow-up, partial recovery of anal tone was noted. At 6 months, the patient remained continent, ambulatory without assistance, and reported no implant-related complaints. Follow-up pelvic imaging demonstrated stable alignment and maintained fixation without evidence of hardware loosening or displacement (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Spinopelvic dissociation due to transverse sacral fractures represents a rare yet clinically devastating injury pattern, particularly when associated with posterior displacement as seen in Roy-Camille type 2 lesions [1]. These fractures disrupt the continuity between the spine and pelvis, frequently resulting in neurological deficits including bowel and bladder dysfunction [1]. As emphasized by Roy-Camille et al., understanding fracture morphology and displacement vector is crucial as these factors directly guide both reduction strategy and fixation technique [1].

The management of these injuries remains particularly challenging due to their inherent instability and potential for long-term disability. Current literature widely accepts that early reduction and stabilization serve as critical factors influencing neurological recovery [2]. Among existing techniques, joystick-assisted reduction using percutaneously placed Schanz screws has demonstrated effectiveness while maintaining minimal invasiveness [5]. However, this method's requirement for hardware manipulation through fracture sites carries potential risks of iatrogenic neural injury. Alternative approaches such as ligamentotaxis via external fixation utilize skeletal traction to achieve indirect reduction through soft tissue tensioning [6]. While avoiding direct exposure, this technique presents limitations including prolonged construct time, pin site infection risk, and potential inadequacy for severely displaced fractures [6], [7]. More invasive options like open reduction with triangular osteosynthesis provide rigid three-point fixation but are associated with increased soft tissue morbidity, blood loss, and extended hospitalization [8], [9].

In this context, we present a novel closed reduction maneuver combining simultaneous manual hyperextension at the lumbosacral junction with counter-traction applied to the extremities. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported use of such an isolated maneuver for Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fractures. The technique, which we term the “GÜLEÇ Technique,” operates on biomechanical principles of anterior column tension restoration while utilizing residual ligamentous attachments to guide reduction [4], [5]. Distinct from joystick or ligamentotaxis approaches, it achieves closed realignment without hardware manipulation or external fixation, potentially reducing both soft tissue disruption and operative complexity in selected cases.

Following successful reduction, we implemented percutaneous lumbopelvic fixation with bilateral L5 pedicle screws and iliac fixation, consistent with established minimally invasive protocols for spinopelvic instability [4]. This construct provided adequate mechanical stability while avoiding extensive dissection. The observed early neurological improvement in our patient supports existing evidence that atraumatic, timely reduction and stabilization can optimize nerve recovery in sacral root injuries [2], [4].

Our findings contribute to the growing body of literature supporting individualized, minimally invasive strategies for Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fractures. While the “GÜLEÇ Technique” shows promise, further biomechanical validation and larger clinical series are needed to confirm its reproducibility and efficacy. Future research should focus on establishing clear indications and refining technical aspects of this manual reduction approach.

Conclusion

The “GÜLEÇ Technique” represents a novel, minimally invasive approach for Roy-Camille type 2 sacral fractures, combining closed reduction via lumbosacral hyperextension with extremity counter-traction followed by percutaneous fixation. This method successfully addresses both biomechanical and biological considerations through controlled ligamentotaxis and preservation of the fracture environment, offering a safe and effective alternative to conventional techniques while minimizing soft tissue disruption. Our initial experience demonstrates its potential as a practical solution for these challenging injuries. Further clinical validation and biomechanical studies are warranted to assess its broader applicability and long-term outcomes.
