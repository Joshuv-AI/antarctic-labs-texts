Cross-sectional association of food insecurity with loneliness in older adults: The role of sex, age, and psychosomatic factors
Authors: Razak M Gyasi, Emelia Aikins, André Hajek, Jones Opoku-Ware, Benjamin Appiah Osei, Joana Kwabena-Adade, Louis Jacob, Masoud Rahmati, George Dakurah, Karl Peltzer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFood insecurity (FI) is a critical social determinant of poor psychosocial health. While data on the specific roles of sex and age in the FI-loneliness link among older adults are limited, the underlying mechanisms are largely unknown. This study examines the age-sex-specific associations of FI with loneliness among older adults in Ghana and quantifies the extent to which psychosomatic factors mediate the association.MethodsWe analyzed cross-sectional data from the Aging, Health, Psychological, and Health-seeking Behavior Study in Ghana. The past 30-day FI was assessed using items on hunger and breakfast skipping frequency due to a lack of resources. We assessed loneliness severity with the University of California, Los Angeles 3-item Loneliness Scale. Multivariable OLS regressions and bootstrapping mediation analysis using the Hayes PROCESS macro plug-in were used to evaluate the associations.ResultsWe included 1,201 individuals aged ≥50 years (mean = 62.9 [SD = 11.9]; women = 63.3%). The prevalence of loneliness was 17.7%. The prevalence of moderate and severe FI was 44.0% and 8.5%, respectively. In the adjusted model, greater FI was significantly associated with loneliness severity (B = .22, SE = .029, p < .001). We found significant interactive effects of FI × age (B = −.17, SE = .023, p < .01) and FI × sex (B = −.28, SE = .036, p < .001) on loneliness. Thus, the FI-loneliness link was respectively more marked among women (B = .25, SE = .035, p < .001) and ≥65 age groups (B = .34, SE = .041, p < .001) than men (B = .16, SE = .051, p < .01) and those aged 50−64 (B = .22; SE = .040, p < .001). Finally, comorbid depression/anxiety (41.07%), hopelessness (48.6%), worthlessness (42.1%), functional limitations (8.2%), and pain severity (6.4%) mediated the FI-loneliness association.ConclusionsAge- and sex-specific associations between FI and loneliness exist among older Ghanaians. Addressing FI in concert with psychosomatic problems in older adults may contribute meaningfully to reducing loneliness in later life.Keywords: Food insecurity, Hunger, Perceived isolation, Social determinant of health, Older adults

Introduction

Up to a third of older adults feel lonely globally [1]. Loneliness is a distressing subjective feeling due to the discrepancy between an individual’s desire for connections and the extent of social relationships [2]. Loneliness in older adults is a critical psychosocial determinant of health [2,3] as old age is beset with widespread personal losses, including bereavement, declining functionality, and loss of mobility. Indeed, emotional strains, including loneliness, deteriorate health and well-being in old age [1]. Thus, older adults who feel lonely are at an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [4], functional impairment [5], hypervigilance [6], cognitive and neurological conditions such as dementia [7], and premature mortality [8]. With increasing global and regional aging populations, loneliness will place an increasing burden on older adults, their families, and the already strained health systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Therefore. efforts to identify the social determinants of health and the risk factors for loneliness, such as food insecurity (FI), may be crucial for healthy aging agendas and health-related sustainable development goals [1], particularly in LMICs and the sub-Saharan African contexts.

FI has been strongly related to loneliness in a few extant studies, particularly those emanating from high-income countries (HICs). For example, longitudinal data from U.S. adults found that experiencing FI was associated with an increased likelihood of loneliness [9]. Gonyea et al. [10] found among low-income U.S. older population that FI and loneliness are strongly related. A study of 1,482 older adults found that a higher prevalence of FI was associated with increased loneliness in rural Indiana [11]. FI may increase loneliness levels in old age via several mechanistic and theretical pathways. For example, the intake of diets with poor nutrition components due to FI may induce abnormal levels of cytokines, neurotropic factors, and poor inflammation responses [12,13]. Moreover, FI may invoke psychological pain and emotional frailty, such as shame, anxiety, hopelessness, and helplessness, which in turn may lead to loneliness [3,14]. Finally, FI has been linked with dynapenia and pathophysiological problems, which may increase physical inactivity and metabolic dysfunction via low handgrip strength, which may subsequently lead to loneliness [15].

Research on FI and loneliness is highly limited in LMICs and the psychosomatic mechanisms underlying the FI-loneliness association are largely poorly understood. Understanding these dynamics may aid interventions for addressing FI and loneliness syndromes in older populations not only in LMICs but globally. Moreover, the differential vulnerability thesis indicates that exposure to social determinants of health is diverse and that age- and sex-specific effects of FI on loneliness may be essential [16]. This study investigated the association between FI and loneliness among older adults in Ghana and explored the potential mechanism explaining this association. We hypothesized that FI would be positively related to loneliness. We further hypothesized that the FI-loneliness link would be modified by age and sex.

Methods

The analyses were based on data from the Ghana Aging, Health, and Health-seeking Behavior study [17], in which participants completed a baseline questionnaire from 2016 to 2018. The survey involves 1247 men and women aged 50 years or older. Details of the survey methodology and architecture, including sampling procedures, have been published elsewhere [17]. A multistage clustered sampling design method was employed to obtain representative samples. Participants were randomly sampled from six randomly selected districts in Ghana. Trained interviewers with backgrounds in public health conducted face-to-face interviews using a standard questionnaire to collect information using a paper and pencil interview approach. The survey response rate was 96%, with an analytic sample size of 1201. The study’s protocol received ethics approval from the Committee on Human Research Publications Ethics, School of Medical Sciences, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital, Kumasi, Ghana (Reference number: CHRPE/AP/507/16). Written informed consent was obtained from all eligible individual participants.

Variables and measurements

FI (exposure)

We assessed FI with dietary inadequacy-related questions, including hunger and breakfast skipping. “During the past 30 days, how often did you go hungry because there was not enough food in your home?” and “During the past 30 days, how often did you skip breakfast because there was not enough food and you had no resources to obtain one?” Each item had a 5-point response option: 1 (never), 2 (rarely), 3 (sometimes), 4 (most of the time), and 5 (always). The items were adapted from the US Household Food Security Survey Module and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Food Security module [3,18] and used in WHO SAGE studies [19]. An overall score (range: 2–10) was created: higher scores depicting greater FI. We used the following algorithm to categorize the FI variable: those who answered 3 through 5 to both questions or answered 5 to either item were classified as severely food insecure. Those who did not fulfill the criteria for severe FI but answered 3 through 4 for either question were coded as moderately food insecure. Those who answered 1 through 2 for both items were categorized as no food secure.

Loneliness (outcome)

Loneliness was assessed with the University of California, Los Angeles 3-item loneliness scale: The questions include: “How often do you (a) lack companionship?” (b) “Feel left out?” and (c) “Feel isolated?” with a 3-point scale: hardly ever/never, some of the time/sometimes, and often/always [20,21]. The overall score ranged 3–9, with a higher score suggesting a higher severity of loneliness (Cronbach alpha was 0.81 in the current study). This tool has been validated and widely used in African populations [5].

Potential mediators

Depressive and anxiety symptoms

Anxiety was assessed using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale [22]. Each of the seven items had four options: 0 = never, 1 = for several days, 2 = more than half of the days, and 3 = almost every day (scores range: 0–21), with higher scores suggesting more severe anxiety symptoms (Cronbach’s alpha = 897). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the CES-D-10 [23] scored on a four-level scale: 0 = rarely, 1 = some days (1–2 days), 2 = occasionally (3–4 days), and 3 = most of the time (5–7 days). The total score ranged 0–30; higher scores reflected more depressive symptoms (Cronbach’s alpha = .806). We then created a 4-level variable: 1 = no anxiety, no depression; 2 = depression, no anxiety; 3 = anxiety, no depression; 4 = anxiety and depression.

Hopelessness and worthlessness

Hopelessness and worthlessness were assessed using a single item with four-level responses, including 1 = never/none of the time, 2 = little of the time, 3 = most of the time, and 4 = always/all of the time with the question “Over the past 30 days, would you say you are 1) hopeless and 2) worthless” [24]. Such a single-item measure is sensitive and provides a very accurate measure of hopelessness and worthlessness at the population level. We used a continuous measure of each of hopelessness and worthlessness (range: 1–4), with higher scores indicating higher levels of hopelessness or worthlessness.

Sleep problems

Sleep problems were measured with two items: “Overall, in the last 30 days, (1) how much of a problem did you have with sleeping (e.g., falling asleep, waking up frequently during the night, or waking up too early in the morning)? and (2) how much of a problem did you have due to not feeling rested and refreshed during the day (for example, feeling tired, not having energy)?” [25]. Each item had five-point options from 1 = none to 5 = extreme. A total score ranged 2–10, with increasing scores indicating more sleep problems (Cronbach’s alpha = .830).

Functional limitations

Functional limitations were assessed with seven functional impairments and mobility-related deficiencies of older persons: (1) Vigorous activities such as weeding, running, lifting heavy objects, etc., (2) Moderate activities, such as moving a table/chair, washing, etc., (3) Lifting or carrying groceries, (4) Climbing about several flights of stairs, (5) Bending, kneeling, or stooping, (6) Walking more than one kilometer, and (7) Bathing and dressing yourself up [24] with response options: from not limited at all (1) to much limited (4). A score ranging from 7 to 28, was created. Higher scores represented higher levels of physical function impairment (Cronbach’s alpha = .830 in this study).

Bodily pain

We assessed pain with a cross-culturally validated single-item question, “How much bodily pain have you had during the past 4 weeks?” Response options ranged from none = 1 to very severe = 5 [24]. We generated a continuous score ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating higher levels of pain severity.

Control variables

In our analysis, regression models were controlled for a wide range of covariates, selected in agreement with prior research [26]. Demographic factors were age, sex (men/women), rural/urban locations, education level (up to primary/higher), and personal income (Ghana Cedis). Lifestyle, social determinants of health, and health variables included alcohol use (no/yes), physical activity, financial inclusion, and chronic conditions.

Statistical analysis

Sample characteristics are stratified by the loneliness status (not lonely, moderately lonely, or severely lonely) status. We also calculated One-way ANOVA (F Statistic) for numerical variables or χ2 for categorical variables to compare characteristics across the loneliness categories. Multiple linear regressions examined the association between FI (exposure) and loneliness (outcome). Model 1 calculated the age-adjusted regression estimates by regressing the outcome measure on FI. Model 2 adjusted for all included demographic and socioeconomic covariates (sex, residential status, level of education, and income level). In Model 3, we additionally incorporated covariates related to lifestyle and psychosocial factors (physical activity, sleep quality, and financial inclusion), and in Model (Full), 4 added health factors (pain, functional limitations, and comorbidities). Additional OLS regression analysis considered age- and sex-specific estimates of the effects of FI on loneliness. Thus, we explored within age and sex heterogeneity in the FI and outcome variable associations. In another sensitivity analysis, we estimated FI components (i.e., hunger and breakfast skipping) effects on loneliness.

To quantify the extent to which psychosocial and physical factors explain the association between FI and loneliness, we performed parallel mediation analysis in the overall sample. We used the Hayes PROCESS macro [27] to decompose the total effect of the association into direct and indirect effects. As we included each potential mediator in the model separately, we calculated the percentage of the association explained by the mediator in each case. Regression and mediation models were fully adjusted for potential confounding variables. Results from the regression analyses are presented as unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with robust standard errors and 95%CIs. The VIF scores ranged ≤1.12, suggesting no multicollinearity issues in this analysis. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A p < .05 was selected for assessments of statistical significance.

Results

The final analytic sample included 1,201 individuals aged 50 years or over. The characteristics of these participants are presented in Table 1, including an evaluation of differences among the three loneliness statuses. The average age was 66.16 (SD = 11.9; range = 50–111) years, and 63.3% were women. For example, most of the sample resided in urban areas (55.0%) and with none or lower education levels (86.2%). Income levels were generally low, with an average monthly income of 308.0 (338.8) Cedis ∼ $63. Overall, the prevalence of moderate and severe FI was 44.1% and 8.5%, respectively. The prevalence of moderate and severe loneliness was 37.5% and 17.7%, respectively. Crucially, the prevalence of FI increased with loneliness (4.5% vs. 7.8% vs. 20.2%, p < .001) (Table 1).

Discussion

We found that increasing FI levels were significantly associated with higher levels of loneliness among older adults in Ghana with sex- and age-specific differences. Comorbid depressive and anxiety symptoms, hopelessness, worthlessness, functional limitations, and pain mediated the FI-loneliness association. This is the first study to quantify the potential psychosomatic mediators in this association. This research is particularly interesting as very little is known about the association between FI and later-life loneliness in Ghana. The focus of policy interventions on screening/addressing FI as a way to attenuate loneliness should be prioritized, particularly in LMICs. Our finding that FI is significantly associated with increases in loneliness supports a few investigations among Western samples, suggesting that FI is a consistent and strong risk factor for loneliness among young and older people [10,11,28]. The current study's findings are unique in many ways. They extend the existing scanty literature by showing that the FI and loneliness association also exists among older adults in the LMICs and SSA contexts.

Several mechanisms may theoretically explain the association between FI and loneliness. First, our analysis identified comorbid anxiety and depressive symptoms to mediate approximately 41% of the association. FI may increase levels of perceived stress by creating heightened uncertainty over the ability to sustain or access food over time. For example, research has shown that individuals who are food insecure are more likely to express sadness and often have poor concentration and may not be able to involve themselves in intellectually stimulating activities [29]. A meta-analysis found that FI was associated with an increased risk of testing positive for depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders [30]. Mental health problems such as depression and anxiety have been strongly related to the onset and progression of loneliness, particularly in later life [31]. Second, feeling hopeless and worthless emerged as significant mediators, explaining 48.6% and 42.1%, respectively, of the association. FI may cause shifts in dietary quantity and quality for less nutritious food, such as those with higher levels of carbohydrates and fats/oils with low minerals, proteins, vitamins, and micronutrients [32]. This FI-induced malnutrition may lead to shame and disquiet, angst, and emptiness [14]. Indeed, persons who are food-insecure increasingly tend to have low expectations about the good in what lies ahead, including low self-esteem, low self-efficacy, and increased psychological distress [33,34]. Harrison et al. [35] applied network analysis to psychopathology and concluded that worthlessness co-occurred more strongly with self-blaming attributions and hopelessness. Such feelings render individuals socially disengaged and may deepen the severity of loneliness.

Third, functional limitations explained approximately 8.2% of the FI-loneliness link. FI may lead to functional limitations due to the lack of required nutrients to boost immunity and keep the body in shape, which may eventually reduce physical function, particularly in later life [3]. Functional limitations may, in turn, lead to loneliness, more importantly, among older adults due to the risk of social isolation, compromised social contacts, and difficulty in mobility. To a lesser extent, bodily pain explains the FI-loneliness link. FI and hunger-induced poor nutrition may increase pain inhibition and pain-related disabilities [36]. Thus, pain may lead to the avoidance of daily activities and reduce physical activity, which may, in turn, lead to loneliness [16]. FI may lead to loneliness via abnormal levels of cytokines [12]. Inadequate cytokines has also been linked to poor inflammation and may disrupt the immune system and blood cells, leading to sedentary behavior, which are established risk factors for loneliness [37]. In turn, these chronic conditions may increase the risk of loneliness via related anguish, pain catastrophizing, and low muscle strength.

Our analysis found important sex- and age-wise differences in the association between FI and loneliness. Among women and those aged ≥65 years, FI exposure was more highly related to loneliness than men and those in the 50–64 age cohort. Women and very old people are less likely than men and younger cohorts to meet dietary requirements due to general socio-economic deprivations among women and older people, particularly in Ghana [19]. Just as women and oldest old people are at a greater risk of FI [38], they are proportionately more likely to experience loneliness. Again, the male-female health-survival paradox suggests that women generally present higher lifetime health problems [39].

Our findings have implications for policy and public health practice, particularly in LMICs where loneliness and FI are common in later life [38,40]. Interventions that address FI and its underlying psychosomatic mechanisms while improving loneliness among older adults are critical. Programs addressing FI may include implementing amiable social policy and support strategies, including expanding Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP), which has been somewhat successful in Ghana and many sub-Saharan African countries in fighting poverty among vulnerable population groups, including older adults [41].

Our study has several strengths based on the robust sample of older adults which allowed sufficient power to estimate this vital association and the inclusion of various mediators for more policy and practical purposes. However, our findings should be considered in the context of some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design did not guarantee a directional relationship between FI and loneliness. Loneliness may likely predict FI in older age. Second, the retrospective self-reported data on FI and subjective measurement of loneliness may be imbued with recall and social desirability biases, which could undermine the accuracy of the findings.

In summary, our analysis found that FI was associated with increasing odds of loneliness with some age- and sex-specific differences. Moreover, psychological factors largely explained this association. These data provide evidence that loneliness in later life may be a consequence of FI. Interventions to reduce loneliness among older adults should include strategies to address FI and FI-related psychological factors concurrently. Future longitudinal analysis is needed to confirm these findings.
