Prevalence of multidrug resistant and ESBL producing Enterobacteriaceae among patients with urinary tract infections in Southwest Shewa, Ethiopia
Authors: Dirriba Diyana, Bizuayehu Ketema Tafes, Degefu Beyene, Dejenie Shiferaw Teklu, Chenglong Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUropathogenic Enterobacteriaceae with multidrug resistance (MDR) have become a significant threat to global healthcare systems in the treatment of urinary tract infections (UTIs). This present study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of MDR and Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamases (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae among patients suspected of having UTIs in the Southwest Shewa Zone of Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January to March 2025. Midstream urine specimens were collected from 278 consecutive patients with urinary tract complaints, along with gathering comprehensive sociodemographic data. Enterobacteriaceae were isolated and identified using Gram staining and biochemical characteristics. Antibiotic susceptibility was determined using the Kirby-Bauer method on Mueller-Hinton agar. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify factors linked to MDR-Enterobacteriaceae-related UTIs, considering a statistically significant P value of < 0.05.ResultAmong the 278 enrolled patients, Enterobacteriaceae were recovered from 16.9% (47/278) of urine samples. Of these, 51.1% (24/47) were multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (MDRE) and 44.7% (21/47) were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBLE). Escherichia coli predominated among MDRE isolates (38.3%, 18/47), while Klebsiella spp. demonstrated the highest species-level MDR rate (62.5%, 5/8). The isolates exhibited high-level resistance to ampicillin (63.8%, 30/47). Notably, all isolates remained susceptible to piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenems. ESBL production, a history of UTI, and prior antibiotic use were identified as statistically significant factors associated with MDRE-related UTIs. Among these, prior antibiotic use emerged as the most statistically significant independent predictor of MDRE isolation in patients suspected of having UTIs (AOR 9.38, 95% CI: 1.30–67.65; p = 0.026).ConclusionEnterobacteriaceae isolates from UTI-suspected patients in the study area exhibit a significant and escalating incidence of multidrug resistance. Therefore, the treatment of UTI should be informed by antimicrobial susceptibility patterns to ensure the judicious prescription of antibiotics.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12982-8.

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a microbe-induced infectious disease characterized by significant bacteriuria within the urinary tract system [1]. It is anatomically classified as involving either the lower tract (urethra and bladder) or upper tract (kidneys). Clinical manifestations include dysuria, urinary frequency, hematuria, flank pain, and pyrexia, which impair quality of life and often induce anxiety or depression in more than half of cases [2]. Over 50% of women experience at least one UTI in their lifetime [3]. Most critically, the emergence of antibiotic resistance, especially multidrug resistance (MDR), has become a key factor undermining treatment efficacy and worsening clinical outcomes in UTI patients [4, 5]. Even with antibiotic therapy, 30–50% of patients will still experience recurrent episodes [6–8]. Severe complications, including sepsis and renal failure, may ensue in progressive cases [9].

The global burden of UTIs is significant, with an estimated 405 million infections and 236,786 deaths attributed to UTIs in 2019 [10]. Recent projections suggest that by 2050, there will be 652,793,817 (~ 652 million) incident cases worldwide [11]. The economic impact in the United States alone exceeds $6 billion annually [12]. While prevalence rates vary considerably across different regions and countries, the burden is disproportionately heavy in some developing nations—Ethiopia, particularly, faces a severe and escalating UTI crisis [13]. As a developing country in East Africa, Ethiopia reports an age-standardized disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rate of 222.24 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 170.92-295.63) for UTIs, which significantly surpasses the global average of 83.74 per 100,000 [10, 11]. Hospital-based studies indicate prevalence rates among symptomatic patients in Ethiopia ranging from 15.8% to 32.1% across various regions [14–16]. However, existing research on UTIs in Ethiopia remains fragmented, especially regarding data on multi-drug resistant uropathogen, a key driver of severe UTI outcomes, creating critical gaps in understanding the local epidemiological landscape.

Enterobacteriaceae, a family of aerobic Gram-negative bacilli, are the primary etiological agents of bacterial UTIs globally, accounting for 80–90% of positive urine cultures [17, 18]. Among these, uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) stands out as the most frequent pathogen, responsible for approximately 75% to 80% of UTIs [18–20]. Pathologically, Enterobacteriaceae induce UTIs by invading urinary tract tissues through multiple virulence factors, including adhesins and virulence antigens such as fimbriae, O antigens, K capsular antigens, serum resistance determinants, and hemolysin-producing proteins [1]. Antibiotics are the first-line treatment for UTIs, but rising antibiotic resistance in Enterobacteriaceae has challenged empirical antimicrobial regimen selection, especially for multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (MDRE)-associated UTIs [4].

Globally, the emergence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae has been widely reported over the past decade, including in Ethiopia [21, 22]. Furthermore, the emergence of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases (KPC)-producing strains, has rendered carbapenems clinically ineffective [23], and New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM)-1-producing strains have likewise been increasingly identified [24]. In certain regions of Ethiopia, up to 87% of Enterobacteriaceae isolates implicated in UTIs are MDR, with 2.7–7.4% of these isolates additionally producing carbapenemases [25–27]. Consequently, patients with MDRE-associated UTIs require timely, targeted antibiotic therapy to achieve the best possible clinical outcomes.

However, bacterial pathogens responsible for UTIs exhibit considerable geographical and temporal variations in both etiology and resistance profiles [10, 11, 13]. Antibiotic therapy for UTIs should be based on local resistance data, drug availability, and antibiotic allergy history of treated patients [28]. Currently, supplementary empirical evidence that comprehensively maps the prevalence and risk factors of MDRE among Ethiopian UTI patients would be greatly beneficial for informing locally targeted UTI management strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate these characteristics of Enterobacteriaceae in patients with suspected UTIs in Southwest Shewa, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study setting

A cross-sectional prospective study was conducted from January to March 2025 at Kersa Malima Woreda Health Centre and Leman Hospital, located approximately 60 km from Addis Ababa in Southwest Shewa, Ethiopia. Both facilities are classified as primary healthcare facilities offering a comprehensive range of essential health services to residents in Southwest Shewa including emergency care, outpatient and inpatient services, maternal and child health, delivery, imaging, laboratory services, physiotherapy, pharmacy, psychiatry, and chronic disease management. Together, these two health facilities serve over 1 million residents of Southwest Shewa. While their primary function of the health facilities is to provide medical care, they also engage in medical education and research. The main objectives of these institutes include addressing priority areas related to health and nutrition issues, as well as delivering services for both communicable and non-communicable diseases across various wards.

Population and recruitment

The study population comprised all patients suspected of having community- and/or hospital-acquired UTIs at Kersa Malima Woreda Health Centre and Leman Hospital during the study period, provided they expressed a willingness to participate. Socio-demographic characteristics, including gender, age, residential area, educational attainment, and marital status etc., were gathered from eligible patients. Patients who had received antibiotic treatment within two weeks prior to enrollment or those unwilling to participate were excluded from the study. Additionally, after receiving instructions on urine specimen collection, midstream urine specimens were obtained from patients suspected of having UTIs using sterile, screw-capped, wide-mouth containers. Each specimen was labeled with a unique sample number, along with the date and time of collection.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion calculation, n1 = Z2 p (1-p)/E2 [29]. This was based on the prevalence of symptomatic UTI of 42.7% among patients at the Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Debre Berhan Ethiopia which is located approximately 200 km from the study site [30]. The sample size was calculated by assuming a 95% confidence interval (Z = Za/2 = 95% = 1.96), and a margin of error (E = 5% = 0.05). After applying a finite population correction, n2 = n1/ (1 + (n1/N)), where N was the total number of patients suspected with UTI at study site for a three months period (N = 1070), we obtained a final sample size of 278 subjects with suspected UTI was estimated.

Isolation and identification of Enterobacteriaceae

All midstream urine samples collected from patients presenting with UTIs were transported to the laboratory within 2 h of collection and were immediately processed for microbial culture. A 1 µl well-mixed urine sample was inoculated onto 5% sheep blood agar (Catalog No.: 48794, Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India), MacConkey agar (Catalog No.: CM0007B, Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK), and chocolate agar (Catalog No.: CM0367B, Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) individually, followed by initial incubation at 37 °C. The culture procedure and interpretation of anticipated results were performed in according to the guideline method described previously [31]. For 5% sheep blood agar, the inoculated plate was incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24–48 h to observe the growth of bacteria and the presence of hemolysis. For MacConkey agar, it was incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h to screen for Gram-negative bacteria (lactose-fermenting bacteria form pink colonies, while non-lactose-fermenting bacteria form colorless colonies). For chocolate agar, it was incubated in a 5%-10% CO₂ environment at 37 °C for 24–48 h to support the growth of fastidious pathogenic bacteria (such as Haemophilus influenzae, Neisseria gonorrhoeae). Notably, no fastidious bacteria were isolated and recovered from the chocolate agar plates in this study.

A culture was considered positive when the blood agar plate yielded a bacterial concentration of ≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL. Enterobacteriaceae from positive urine cultures were identified based on colony morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical profiles, employing standard procedures. Biochemical tests, including indole production, sugar fermentation, H2S and gas production, citrate utilization, motility, urease, and oxidase tests, were conducted to identify the Enterobacteriaceae isolates. The target species for isolation and identification in this study included Escherichia coli (indole⁺, urease⁻, gas⁺), Klebsiella pneumoniae (indole⁻, urease⁺, mucoid colonies), Klebsiella oxytoca (indole⁺, otherwise similar to K. pneumoniae), Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae (indole⁻, urease⁺, non-lactose-fermenting, characteristic musty odour), Enterobacter spp. (indole⁻, citrate⁺, gas⁺), among other clinically relevant species.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

Antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST) was performed on all 47 Enterobacteriaceae isolates obtained from positive urine cultures following the 2022 guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), utilizing the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar [32]. A pure colony was first suspended in sterile normal saline, and the suspension was then adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard. The adjusted suspension was inoculated onto a Mueller-Hinton agar plate (Catalog No.: CM0337B, Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) with a sterile cotton swab, after which antibiotic disks were placed accordingly.

The antibiotics tested, along with their drug classes, concentrations and catalog numbers, were as follows: third-generation cephalosporins (Ceftazidime, CAZ, 30 µg, CT0412B; Ceftriaxone, CTR, 30 µg, CT0417B; Cefotaxime, CTX, 30 µg, CT0166B); first-generation cephalosporin (Cefazolin, CZ, 30 µg); second-generation cephalosporin (Cefuroxime, CXM, 30 µg, CT0127B); carbapenems (Ertapenem, ERP, 10 µg, CT1761B; Meropenem, MEM, 10 µg, CT0774B); penicillin-β-lactamase inhibitor (Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, AMC, 30 µg, CT0223B; Piperacillin/tazobactam, TZP, 100/10 µg, CT0725B); penicillin (Ampicillin, AMP, 10 µg, CT0003B); aminoglycoside (Gentamicin, GEN, 10 µg, CT0024B); nitrofurans (Nitrofurantoin, NIT, 100 µg, CT0034B); sulfonamides (Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, SXT, 25 µg, CT0052B). All antibiotics were obtained from Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK. MDR was defined as resistance to ≥ 3 antimicrobial classes, with intrinsic resistance excluded to ensure accurate estimation of acquired resistance prevalence.

Screening for ESBL-producing isolates

ESBL screening was performed using the standard disk diffusion method in accordance with CLSI guidelines [32]. CAZ (30 µg) and CTX (30 µg) discs were utilized. Freshly grown bacterial colonies were suspended in normal saline to achieve a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. The suspension was inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar with a sterile cotton swab, and the two antibiotic discs were positioned 20 mm apart. After incubation at 37 °C for 16–18 h, isolates exhibiting inhibition zone sizes of ≤ 22 mm for ceftazidime or ≤ 27 mm for cefotaxime were classified as potential ESBL producers.

To confirm the phenotypic presence of ESBL producers, the combination disk test (CDT) was employed in accordance with CLSI guidelines. A pure bacterial suspension, standardized to 0.5 McFarland, was inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar. Subsequently, discs containing AMC (30 µg), CTX (30 µg), and CAZ (30 µg) were placed on the agar. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, ESBL production was confirmed if the inhibition zone diameter for the oxyimino-β-lactam discs (CTX/CAZ) increased by more than 5 mm compared to the same disc tested alone, indicating clavulanate synergy from the AMC disc.

Quality control

To guarantee quality from isolate collection to the ultimate bacterial identification and data management, we strictly adhered to standard operating procedures (SOPs). Specimens were gathered following SOPs and promptly conveyed to the clinical and microbiology laboratory for culturing. The expiration dates of media, antibiotic disks, and reagents were validated, and daily quality control was executed utilizing reference strains such as Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923. Quality control was performed using reference strains (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603) in each batch. Clinical isolates were tested once, with repeat testing performed only for unexpected phenotypes (e.g., unexplained carbapenem resistance) to confirm results.

Data analysis and interpretation

The data were collected, summarized, and analyzed using SPSS v27. The frequency, proportion, and 95% confidence interval (CI) of MDR Enterobacteriaceae were calculated. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the independent determinants of MDR Enterobacteriaceae-related UTIs. Variables including patient gender, age, educational level, ESBL production, history of UTI, antibiotic use within 6 months, and residential area were included in the analysis. Candidate variables with a P-value < 0.05 on univariate analysis were included in multivariable model, followed by backward elimination where variables with a P-value > 0.05 were sequentially excluded. Model fit was assessed via Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with a P-value > 0.05 indicating acceptable fit. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs, P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Department of Medical Laboratory Science at the College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University, and a collaboration letter was dispatched to the study site. All specimens and isolates were managed and disposed of in accordance with established biosafety guidelines. Laboratory results were communicated to clinicians in a timely manner to enhance patient management.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 278 individuals suspected of UTIs participated in the study, with the majority being female, constituting 89.9% (250/278), significantly higher than males at 10.1% (28/278). The participants, with a mean age of 37 ± 14 years, were predominantly in the 25–34 age group, making up 30.6% (85/278) of the cohort. Furthermore, 61.5% (171/278) of the participants resided in rural areas, and 59.0% (164/278) had attained elementary school education. Moreover, 70.9% (197/278) of the participants were married. Prevalence and antimicrobial sensitivity patterns of Enterobacteriaceae isolates from UTI suspected participants

Among the study participants, 16.9% (47/278) of patients exhibited positive urine cultures with a single non-duplicate isolate of Enterobacteriaceae. Non-Enterobacteriaceae uropathogens were also isolated, including Enterococcus faecalis (12 isolates), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (8 isolates), and Staphylococcus aureus (3 isolates), but were excluded as the study focused on MDR/ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae. All isolates were obtained from individuals attending the outpatient department. Of the culture-positive cases, 8.5% (4/47) were male, while 91.5% (43/47) were female. Escherichia coli was the predominant isolate (78.7%, 37/47), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (8.5%, 4/47), Klebsiella oxytoca (6.4%, 3/47), Enterobacter spp. (4.3%, 2/47), and Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae (2.1%, 1/47) (Fig. 1).

Multidrug resistance and ESBL producing Enterobacteriaceae

Among the 47 Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 51.1% (24/47) were classified as MDRE, with E. coli representing the predominant isolate (18/47, 38.3%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (5/47, 10.6%). Only 27.7% (13/47) remained susceptible to all tested antibiotics (Table 3).​ MDR rates varied considerably among Enterobacteriaceae species. Klebsiella spp. exhibited the highest rate at 62.5% (5/8), comprising K. pneumoniae (2/4, 50.0%) and K. oxytoca (3/3, 100%), with no resistance detected in K. pneumoniae subsp. Ozaenae (0/1). Enterobacter spp. demonstrated 50.0% (1/2), and E. coli showed 48.6% (18/37). Further, among the 47 Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 44.7% (21/47) were identified as ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, with E. coli accounting for 85.7% (18/21), K. pneumoniae 9.5% (2/21), and K. oxytoca 4.8% (1/21). No ESBL-producing isolates were detected in Enterobacter spp. or K. pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae. The breakdown of MDR and ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae by species was shown in Table S1. Notably, among MDR Enterobacteriaceae isolates, ESBL producers exhibited significantly higher resistance rates to most antibiotics than non-ESBL producers (Fig. 2). For MDR strains, TZP, ERP, and MEM exhibited the highest efficacy (100% susceptibility), with NIT (87.5%) and GEN (79.9%) showing intermediate activity, the detailed resistance profiles are provided in Table S2.

Risk factors associated with MDR Enterobacteriaceae among UTI suspected patients

Logistic regression analysis identified prior antibiotic use as the most statistically significant independent predictor of MDRE isolation among patients suspected of UTIs. Specifically, patients with a history of any antibiotic exposure within the preceding six months exhibited a 9.4-fold increase in the odds of carrying MDRE compared to those without such exposure (AOR 9.38, 95% CI: 1.30–67.65; p = 0.026; Table 4). Although bivariate analysis indicated strong associations with ESBL production (COR 4.72, 95% CI 1.36–16.39; p = 0.015) and a history of prior UTI (COR 5.04, 95% CI 1.40–18.14; p = 0.013), these associations were significantly attenuated and became non-significant after adjusting for confounders (ESBLs: AOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.02–10.02; p = 0.577; prior UTI: AOR 2.80, 95% CI 0.15–53.71; p = 0.495). No evidence of independent influence was found for gender, age, educational level, or rural residence (p > 0.05).

Discussion

MDR among Enterobacteriaceae has emerged as a significant public health concern, closely linked to increased mortality rates [33]. In the present study, among Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 51.1% (24/47) were classified as MDR, with E. coli predominating (38.3%, 18/47), followed by Klebsiella spp. (10.6%, 5/47). This prevalence aligns with Eastern Ethiopia (51.6%) [34] and Western Ethiopia (56.4%) [16], yet exhibits marked divergence from other regions, substantially lower than Gondar (87.4%) [25] and Dessie (81.3%) [35], but higher than Uganda (25.05%) [36] and Indonesia (35%) [37]. These variations cannot be attributed solely to geographical location, rather, they likely reflect temporal trends in resistance evolution, differing antibiotic stewardship policies, and methodological heterogeneity. For instance, the higher rates in Gondar and Dessie may stem from these being tertiary referral centers receiving complex cases with extensive antibiotic exposure histories, whereas our primary care inclusion criteria select for treatment-naïve or minimally exposed populations. Conversely, the lower rates in Uganda and Indonesia might reflect restricted fluoroquinolone availability delaying resistance selection, or differences in phenotypic testing methodologies.

At the species level, Klebsiella spp. exhibited the highest MDR rate (62.5%, 5/8), followed by Enterobacter spp. (50.0%, 1/2) and E. coli (48.6%, 18/37). This hierarchical pattern, consistent with reports from Ethiopia (82.9% for K. pneumoniae) [38], Nepal (100%) [39], and Sierra Leone (90.8%) [40], underscores the differential propensity of these pathogens to acquire and maintain resistance determinants. This trend may be linked to the increased propensity of Klebsiella spp. to harbor resistance plasmids and the derepressed high-level cephalosporinase (AmpC enzyme) mechanism observed in Enterobacter spp [41, 42]. The elevated resistance rates of these species necessitate clinical vigilance to prevent treatment failures stemming from inadequate empirical therapy.

In the present study, however, Enterobacteriaceae were isolated from 16.9% (47/278) of urine samples in the Southwest Shewa Zone of Ethiopia. Its relatively low compared with other Ethiopian hospital-based studies [25, 26], which aligns with observations of geographical and setting-based variability in uropathogen isolation across Ethiopia [14, 43–45]. The discrepancy may be attributed to prior antibiotic exposure (11.5% of participants in the study), a known modifier of Enterobacteriaceae detection in urinary samples [46, 47]. Additionally, it is important to note that non-Enterobacteriaceae uropathogens, including Enterococcus faecalis (12 isolates), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (8 isolates), and Staphylococcus aureus (3 isolates), were also isolated during the study. However, as the primary focus of our investigation was on Enterobacteriaceae, particularly MDR and ESBL-producing strains which represent a critical public health threat due to their high levels of antimicrobial resistance, these non-Enterobacteriaceae isolates were excluded from the main analysis. Furthermore, the inclusion of primary care facilities where non-bacterial etiologies (e.g., sterile pyuria) may mimic UTI symptoms [48], and intrinsic geographical heterogeneity specific to the Southwest Shewa Zone, where local epidemiology, patient demographics, and healthcare-seeking behaviors may differ substantially from other regions [49, 50], and minor variations in laboratory protocols.

ESBL production was identified in 44.7% (21/47) of isolates, a prevalence exceeding that reported in Addis Ababa (38.8%) [38], Arba Minch (33.25%) [51], Bahir Dar (26.3%) [52] and Jimma (38.4%) [53], yet comparable to Hawassa (41.7%) [26] in Ethiopia. This geographic gradient likely correlates with antibiotic consumption intensity [54], as Southwest Shewa Zone’s agricultural profile may drive extensive veterinary antibiotic use [55, 56], facilitating horizontal gene transfer between environmental and clinical reservoirs [57]. Notably, in this study, ESBL-producers exhibited significantly higher resistance rates to most antimicrobials compared with non-producers, particularly against ampicillin (62.2–100% resistance across species) and amoxicillin-clavulanate (100% in K. oxytoca and Enterobacter spp.). The complete resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanate, despite its β-lactamase inhibitor component, suggests inhibitor-resistant TEM/SHV variants or co-production of multiple β-lactamases, a phenomenon requiring molecular confirmation but clinically manifesting as therapeutic failure [58, 59]. This renders traditional first-line agents (ampicillin, early-generation cephalosporins) obsolete for empirical therapy in this setting, necessitating protocol revision to prioritize piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenems for severe infections [60].

Full susceptibility to piperacillin-tazobactam, ertapenem, and meropenem (100%) was observed, with no carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) detected. This preservation of carbapenem efficacy, contrasting with escalating CRE reports in South Africa and ub-Saharan African [61], likely reflects conservative carbapenem prescribing practices in Ethiopian public health facilities, where economic constraints and prescription regulations limit indiscriminate use [62]. However, this “last line of defense” remains precarious, the documented 9.38-fold increased risk of MDRE associated with prior antimicrobial use (AOR: 9.38; 95% CI: 1.30–67.65) aligns with studies linking antecedent antibiotic exposure to multidrug resistance in Enterobacteriaceae infections [25, 63, 64], suggesting that selection pressure from empirical broad-spectrum therapy rapidly depletes susceptibility options. The lack of association between MDRE and sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education, residence) indicates that antibiotic exposure, rather than host characteristics, drives resistance epidemiology, a pattern consistent with global analyses of antimicrobial resistance determinants [65]. This finding carries immediate policy implications, specifically, infection control should prioritize antimicrobial stewardship (restricting third-generation cephalosporins, implementing sequential therapy, and enhancing diagnostic stewardship to distinguish bacterial from non-bacterial UTI) over population-targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The high prevalence of MDR (51.1%) and ESBL-producing (44.7%) Enterobacteriaceae in Southwest Shewa Zone, dominated by E. coli and Klebsiella spp., constitutes a critical public health threat. The significant association between prior antibiotic exposure and MDRE underscores the urgent need for stringent antimicrobial stewardship, emphasizing that empirical antibiotic therapy should be strictly guided by local AST data rather than clinical experience alone. While carbapenems retain full efficacy, their preservation necessitates immediate implementation of hierarchical antibiotic protocols and routine ESBL surveillance to prevent the emergence of pan-resistant pathogens.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study merit recognition. First, the sample size of culture-positive Enterobacteriaceae isolates was small (n = 47), which may reduce statistical power, particularly for analyzing risk factors of MDR, and restrict the generalizability of results to broader populations. Second, the study exclusively employed phenotypic methods to detect MDR and ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, without molecular characterization (e.g., PCR, sequencing) to identify specific resistance genes. This limits mechanistic insights into resistance (e.g., resistance gene types, distribution, and resistant bacterial transmission routes) and complicates comparisons with studies using molecular detection. Thus, future research should recruit larger samples to enhance result reliability and integrate phenotypic and molecular approaches to comprehensively elucidate Enterobacteriaceae resistance mechanisms in UTI patients.
