Cell-cycle-related transcriptional factor DLX4: a novel prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jingsong Cheng, Chengxi Zhang, Yiman Luo, Yihan Chen, Nanting Chen, Yuanyuan Wan, Xinyu Huang, Ziheng Zheng, Qingyao Yin, Xue Chen, Jing Hua, Yang Li, Rongzhong Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Distal - less homeobox 4 (DLX4) is a transcription factor vital for embryonic development and shows pro - oncogenic properties in some hematological and solid tumors. In colorectal cancer (CRC), its molecular function and contributions to tumorigenesis remain to be explored. Using bulk, single - cell and spatial transcriptomics, we assessed DLX4’s clinicopathological significance and its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Our results show DLX4 is highly expressed in CRC, correlating with poor prognosis in multi - center cohorts and is also related to a cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) dysfunction - related immunosuppressive microenvironment. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses identified DLX4 - associated cell signaling. In vitro, we confirmed DLX4 promotes cell proliferation, cell - cycle transitions and suppresses apoptosis. Furthermore, we found its key transcriptional target ZC3HC1 via ChIP – seq analysis, ChIP - qPCR and luciferase assay. Notably, DLX4 has strong potential for liquid - liquid phase separation and likely forms condensates when binding to ZC3HC1’s promoter, a crucial cell - cycle regulator. Overall, DLX4 is a novel prognostic biomarker and suggests potential CRC therapeutic strategies, making significant contributions to the understanding and potential treatment of CRC at the molecular level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03998-2.

Introduction

CRC is the third most common cancer (9.6%) and the second mostly fatal cancer (9.3%) in the world, causing nearly 900,000 deaths annually [1]. CRC is widely believed to arise from colonic stem cells at the bottom of intestinal crypts, advancing as a result of cumulative genetic and epigenetic changes [2]. Typically, CRC develops hiddenly, following the “adenoma-carcinoma” sequence, with the transition from a precancerous lesion to cancer usually takes 5 to 10 years [3]. A landmark consensus by a CRC subtyping consortium in 2015 identified four consensus molecular subtypes of CRC, a discovery that holds significant promise for future clinical classification and targeted interventions [4]. Hence, identifying biomarkers for early diagnosis, molecular typing, and prognosis assessment in CRC through RNA-seq cohorts is crucial for improving personalized precision diagnostics and targeted therapies.

The current multidisciplinary approach to managing CRC includes surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, which can sometimes lead to drug resistance and side effects [5]. Immunotherapy is a promising anticancer treatment. By targeting negative immune regulators (such as PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) activate anti-tumor immunity and reverse the immunosuppressive state of TIME to enhance T cell-mediated tumor killing [6]. PD-1 inhibitors demonstrate enhanced effectiveness in metastatic CRC (mCRC) patients with dMMR and MSI-H status, also known as dMMR-MSI-H [7, 8]. The combination of a CTLA-4 inhibitor with nivolumab is effective for dMMR-MSI-H CRC patients’ post-chemotherapy [9]. In contrast, mCRC patients with pMMR, MSS, or MSI-L status (~ 95%) show minimal response to ICI monotherapies, described as “cold tumors” [8, 10]. One of the crucial challenges in CRC immunotherapy today is the transformation of MSS-type tumors into highly immunogenic tumors. Consequently, identifying crucial biomarkers that target colorectal tumor immune checkpoints holds great importance for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.

The Distal-Less Homeobox (DLX) gene family, comprising six members (DLX1-DLX6), serves as essential transcription factors (TFs) [11]. These genes have indispensable roles in embryogenesis, engage in interactions with other signaling pathways during postnatal homeostasis, and their dysregulation is linked to cell proliferation across various cancers [12, 13]. Specifically, the DLX4 gene has been identified as frequently expressed and prognostically significant in several cancers, including breast cancer, choriocarcinoma and renal clear cell carcinoma [14–16]. Studies have uncovered how DLX4 drive cancer development as TFs. By directly binding to the TWIST gene promoter, DLX4 enhanced TWIST expression, leading to increased invasion and migration of breast cancer cells [17]. These results suggest that DLX4 promotes various tumorigenesis by regulating different downstream targets. However, DLX4’s role in colorectal cancer remains largely unexplored. This study is the first to systematically dissect DLX4’s oncogenic and immunomodulatory roles in CRC using multi-omics data.

In eukaryotic cells, biological macromolecules undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) to form liquid, membrane-less condensates. This process enables the specific and efficient facilitation of various biochemical reactions within the cells [18]. Many LLPS proteins contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), crucial for this process [19]. According to recent research, abnormal phase separation of biomolecules is linked to the development of neurodegenerative disorders and cancer progression [20]. Given the diverse transcription-related proteins, including RNA polymerases, TFs, co-activators, and transcription elongation factors, the formation of transcriptional condensates via LLPS may contribute to the dysregulation of oncogenic transcription [21]. LLPS condensates have been shown to facilitate oncogene activation by enhancing the function of super-enhancers (SEs) [22]. Recent studies have demonstrated that transcription factors (TFs) play critical roles in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression and prognosis. For instance, Elevated MYC expression, stabilized by MAPK signaling, has been associated with poor PFS and DFS in CRC patients [23]. FOXM1 promotes glycolysis, lipid biosynthesis, and tumor growth in CRC, with high expression predicting worse survival outcomes [24]. Furthermore, systematic analyses have identified E2F3 and E2F4 as independent prognostic indicators, with their overexpression linked to shorter overall survival [25]. Against this backdrop, our study introduces DLX4 as a novel TF in CRC, linking immune dysfunction, cell cycle control, and potential phase separation.

In this study, we discovered an elevation in DLX4 expression within CRC, which correlates with poor prognosis. Further investigations revealed a connection between DLX4 and a suppressive microenvironment TIME, along with an attenuated response to immunotherapy. This suggested that DLX4 could serve as a potential biomarker for immunotherapeutic response. Moreover, a significant association of DLX4 with crucial biological processes, such as the cell cycle, was observed. As a transcription factor, DLX4 performs its biological roles by regulating transcription. The functional enrichment of its transcriptional targets revealed patterns similar to those seen in genes co-expressed with DLX4, involving pathways associated with RNA modification, the mitotic cell cycle, and spliceosome activities. Notably, the transcriptional target ZC3HC1 showed the strongest association with DLX4. Comprehensive bioinformatics analyses further indicated that transcriptional regulation of DLX4 may be LLPS-dependent. Ultimately, our findings have demonstrated that DLX4 inhibited apoptosis and fostered cell cycle progression. These insights indicated that DLX4 facilitates transcriptional responses contributing to the progression and adverse prognosis of CRC by utilizing its phase-separation capabilities to regulate the cell cycle protein ZC3HC1. This unveils a new strategy for therapeutic intervention, targeting the phase-separation properties of DLX4 in the development of anticancer drugs.

Method

Bulk RNA-seq data acquisition and normalization

Utilizing UCSC XENA [26], clinical and RNA-seq data from pan-cancer, together with corresponding normal samples, were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) (https://gtexportal.org/home/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [27]. Batch-effect correction was performed using the ComBat function from the sva package. We extracted transcripts per million (TPM) format data and further transformed to log2 (TPM + 1) values. Data analysis was performed with the R package “plyr” (version 3.7.8), and visualization was done using “ggplot2” (version 3.3.6). Statistical significance was assessed with Wilcoxon’s rank sum test, considering p < 0.05 as significant. R code used for TCGA transcriptomics analyses can be found at our GitHub project (https://github.com/m78yh/DLX4_code).

Survival and clinical-pathological parameters analysis

For TCGA cohort, survival probability was determined through the utilization of Kaplan–Meier survival curves. The Kaplan-Meier plotter was used to visualize the prognostic value of DLX4 in CRC [28]. Samples were divided into DLX4 high and low based on expression level of DLX4. Survivals were compared by the logrank test. GEO dataset was downloaded from GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The detailed method was provided in the Supplementary material. The clinicopathologic data of GEO cohort were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed by using a DLX4 specific antibody (Rabbit Anti-BP1 Polyclonal Antibody, Novus Biologicals, Inc., Littleton, CO). A given cell was considered positive for DLX4 expression if distinct chromogen coloration was consistently seen in its cytoplasm or nucleus in at least two duplicates of the same IHC analysis. A case was considered positive if more than 5% of its entire cell population showed distinct DLX4 immunoreactivity. The specific patient information were shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Genetic alteration analysis

cBioPortal (http://cbioportal.org) provides the alteration frequency, mutation type, CNA (copy number alteration) and mutation site information of candidate proteins in all TCGA tumors, specifically CRC. We used scatterplot analysis combined with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to explore the correlation between copy number variation (CNV) scores from GISTIC2.0 and DLX4 mRNA expression. Data on somatic non-silencing mutations in CRC cells (gene level) were obtained from UCSC Xena. We used GISTIC 2.0 pipeline to analyze the copy number variation (CNV) characteristics in DLX4 high and low groups. In addition, the R package “maftools” (version 2.12.0) was used to present and compare the tumor somatic mutation landscapes in DLX4 high and low groups as well as the mutated genes that differed significantly between the two groups.

In the methylation analysis, we included TSS1500 (from − 200 to −1500 bp upstream of TSS), TSS200 (from − 200 bp upstream of TSS), 1stExone, (the first exon), 5’UTR (5’ untranslated region [UTR]), and median values were calculated to characterize the methylation level of each sample. We further used the SMART (Shiny Methylation Analysis Resource Tool) App (http://www.bioinfo-zs.com/smartapp/) to demonstrate differential expression of CpG sites in normal and tumor samples of TCGA [29, 30].

Comprehensive analysis of immune characteristics

We performed the immunogenicity analysis using the IMPACT (http://www.brimpact.cn/) [31]. The ImmuCellAI algorithm (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/ImmuCellAI/) was used to reveal the association between DLX4 expression and immune cell infiltration in CRC. In addition, immuno-infiltration data of all samples of TCGA were quantified by R package “IOBR”. Spatial Transcriptomics data was obtained from CRC patients in 10xgenomics database (https://www.10xgenomics.com/), Spatial transcriptomic registration was achieved using histological alignment to H&E images, and deconvolution of cell types was conducted via the SPOTlight algorithm. Single-cell RNA-seq dataset E-MTAB-8107 was obtained from EMBL-EBI database, which are listed in Supplementary Table S3. For single-cell RNA-seq analyzing process, we downloaded the corresponding single-cell dataset E-MTAB-8107 in.h5 format and annotation results from TISCH (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/). The R package “Seurat” (version 4.1.4) were applied for quality control. Dimensionality reduction using PCA. The “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions in Seurat were applied with a resolution of 0.8. Re-cluster the cells with tSNE functions. Analysis of cell-cell communication was conducted with the R package “CellChat”. The quantification and visualization of the specific interaction between malignant cells with or without DLX4 expression and other identified cell types in various receptor-ligand signaling pathways were performed. For spatial transcriptomics analyzing process, we downloaded the CRC_WholeTranscriptomeAnalysis_10x in.h5 format and annotation results from 10xGenomics. Enrichment score matrix was generated with R package “Cottrazm”. Wilcoxon Rank Sum Tests evaluate the significance of statistics. The TIDE algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) was used to quantify the T cell dysfunction and exclusion in different immunotherapy cohorts. More detailed method of the process of spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA-seq was provided in the Supplementary material.

Integrative multi-omics and enrichment analysis

The LinkedOmics database includes multi-omics and clinical data for 32 cancer types from the TCGA [32], along with mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics data from select TCGA tumor samples, generated by the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC)(https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-gdc/contributed-genomic-data-cancer-research/clinical-proteomic-tumor-analysis-consortium-cptac).

This study investigated genes and proteins co-expressed with the transcription factor DLX4 using transcriptomics and proteomics data from LinkedOmics. Metascape (https://metascape.org/) conducted Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genome (KEGG) enrichment analyses to these co-expressed genes and revealed some of the gene interactions. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was utilized to investigate the link between DLX4 co-expressed genes and KEGG pathways. The R package “clusterProfiler” facilitated the enrichment analysis, with the “msigdbr” package serving as the reference gene set repository. Top 10 NES pathways were selected for visualization. P-value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Significance of DLX4 in drug sensitivity

To facilitate personalized therapy, we utilized the R package “pRRophetic” to estimate chemosensitivity in colorectal carcinoma patients based on varying DLX4 expressions, derived from databases GSDC, CTRP (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2.1/) and PRISM three databases. We examined the differences in drug IC50(half maximal inhibitory concentration) and AUC (area under concentration-time curve) for the high versus low DLX4 expression cohorts using the Wilcoxon test, where p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Additionally, we computed the CMap score employing the XSum algorithm. CMap scores between − 1 and 0 suggest a drug’s potential as an anti-carcinogenic for CRC [33–35].

Transcriptional regulation analyses and prediction of phase separation potential of DLX4

ChIP-seq dataset was analyzed by CistromeDB [36, 37]. FuzDrop was used to predict the probability of LLPS and the likelihood of aggregates within liquid droplets [38]. PONDR was utilized to predict intrinsically disordered region (IDR) of DLX4 protein from amino acid sequence [39]. PhaSePred (http://predict.phasep.pro/) was a comprehensive repository for predicting self-assembling and partner-dependent phase-separating proteins, consolidating scores from various phase separation prediction tools, which were used to analyze the phase separation propensity of the DLX4 protein [40]. The detailed method was partly provided in the supplementary material [40].

Then, the protein’s UniProt accession number was submitted to the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (DeepMind’s AI tool predicting protein 3D structures from their amino acid sequences), initiating structure prediction [41]. We also used AlphaFold3 to predict the binding structure of DLX4 protein and ZC3HC1 promoter [42].

Cell culture, lentiviral infection, and SiRNA transfection

Human CRC cell lines HCT116, SW480 and normal colon mucosal cells NCM460 wereobtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, America). They were maintainedin DMEM (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) with 10% FBS (PAN-Biotech, Adenbach, Germany) and penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2. When cells grew to ∼60% confluence, transfection was performed in 6-cm tissue culture dishes or 24-well plates (BD company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were purchased from Tsingke (Beijing, China). All cell lines were tested free of mycoplasma contamination and authenticated by the short tandem repeat (STR)-based method. A set of two siRNAs with different sequences for DLX4 were used to knock down DLX4 expression in cells. Sequences for siRNAs were listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Western blotting

Proteins (30 µg) were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the protein concentrations were measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Meilunbio, Dalian, China). The proteins were then separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were subsequently blocked and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After that, the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Protein bands were then detected using chemiluminescence with ECL (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and quantified using ImageJ software. The antibodies used for western blot were listed in Supplementary Table S5.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The primers of GADPH and DLX4 were designed by the primer 5.0. Sequences for primers for qPCR were listed in Supplementary Table S4. Total RNAs were extracted and reversely transcribed into cDNA by HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR. GADPH and DLX4 expression levels were conducted using 2-ΔΔCT.

Cell counting Kit-8 assay and colony formation assay

Following siRNA transfection, HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2000 cells/well). On days 1–6 after transfection, cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) (TargetMol, USA) solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for one hour, followed by the measurement of absorbance values at 450 nm.

After siRNA transfection, HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 6-well plates (3000 cells/well) and cultured for 10 days to form colonies. The cells were then washed with PBS (phosphate buffer saline) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After washing the cells three times, they were stained with 1% crystal violet for 20 min. After washing the cells three times, stained cell colonies were observed and counted under a microscope. The ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to quantify colony formation.

ChIP-seq analysis and ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets of Lovo cells were obtained from CistromeDB, stored in GSE49402.For ChIP-qPCR, cells were cross-linked by 1% formaldehyde for 10 min. Chromosome DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided by SimpleChIP ® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell signaling, Danvers, MA) and precipitated by using specific anti-DLX4 rabbit antibody. Rabbit IgG was used as negative control. The PCR conditions were 15 min at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles of 15 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 55 °C and 30 s at 72 °C, and then 10 min at 72 °C. The ChIP-PCR primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Luciferase assay

For dual-luciferase reporter assay, the cells were seeded in 24-well plates. After 24 h, an expression construct containing the firefly luciferase ORF and the ZC3HC1 promoter (pL-ZC3HC1), or firefly luciferase control plasmid (pL-con) was co-transfected with the Renilla luciferase plasmid (pRL-TK). Cells were replated in 96-well plates the next day at 2 × 104 cells/well density and transfected with the siNC or siDLX4-1 using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After 20 h the activities of firefly and Renilla luciferases were measured sequentially with the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), firefly luciferase activity was expressed as relative luciferase units (RLU) after correction for Renilla luciferase activity.

Flow cytometry

For Edu assay, Edu was added to HCT116 and SW480 cells to a final concentration with 10 µM. After incubation for 2 h, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions using Click-iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (#C10634, Invitrogen, America). For Ki67 assay, HCT116 and SW480 cells were stained with anti-ki67-FITC antibody (#343510, Biolegend, America) for 2 h at 4 °C, before being stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (8 µg/mL) overnight. For detecting apoptosis, HCT116 and SW480 cells were stained with anti-annexinV-APC and DAPI in a binding buffer with Ca2+.

Flow cytometric analysis was done by gating on single cells and dead cells were excluded by DAPI staining. Data was analyzed using FlowJo Version 10 software (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, America).

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the correlations between continuous variables, either Spearman or Pearson correlation analysis was utilized. Two-tailed t-tests or one-way ANOVA were used for variables with a normal distribution to assess significant quantitative differences between and among groups. For nonnormally distributed variables, significant quantitative differences between and among groups were determined by a Wilcoxon test or a Kruskal-Wallis test, respectively. Differential expression thresholds were set at |log2FC| >1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control for multiple testing. R 4.1.2 and Graphpad Prism 9 were utilized to conduct all statistical procedures. P-values of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all tests were two-sided. Supplementary methods, tables, details for web tools and R packages can be found in supplementary material. Specific version, functions, access route, parameters and doi number are provided in Supplementary Table S6. The details of main R code used in this article can be found following: https://github.com/m78yh/DLX4_code.

Result

DLX4 expression is elevated in CRC

We explored DLX4 mRNA expression levels in diverse types of human cancer from TCGA datasets. These datasets were then compared to normal tissue using the GTEx datasets. Our analysis revealed a significant upregulation of DLX4 mRNA in 23 of the 33 cancer types studied, including but not limited to COAD, READ, and 21 other cancers. DLX4 was overexpressed in BRCA, BLCA, CESC, CHOL, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PAAD, PAAD, PCBG, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THYM, UCS, and UCEC (Fig. S1A). Bodymap depicted that DLX4 is highly elevated in patients with gastrointestinal tumors compared with normal (Fig. 1A). In paired analyses of carcinoma tissues and adjacent normal tissues from TCGA, DLX4 showed elevated expression specifically in CRC, with the AUC value of 0.924 (95% confidence interval: 0.902–0.945). (Fig. 1B and E). Consistent with the above results, the TNMplot.com platform revealed a notable elevation in DLX4 mRNA expressions in CRC (Fig. 1C). We also examined that DLX4 was overexpressed in CRC by GPL-570 from GEO datasets (Fig. 1D). Then, IHC staining of tumor tissues showed increased DLX4 protein expression levels (Fig. 1F) (Fig. S2 A-I). DLX4 staining showed in the nuclei, with different staining intensity in different specimens. (Fig.S2). The results showed that DLX4 expression was upregulated in CRC compared with normal tissue. Next, to investigate the association between DLX4 expression and clinical outcomes in CRC, we categorized CRC cases into high and low DLX4 groups based on DLX4 mRNA levels. RNA-seq and microarray datasets from TCGA and KM-plotter were used to examine the prognostic implications of these two groups. The results from the former suggested that high expression of DLX4 corresponds with poor prognosis in TCGA-COADREAD cohorts. (Fig. 1, PPS, log-rank p = 0.015; RFS, log-rank p = 0.0027; OS, log-rank p = 0.0034) (Fig. 1G, PFS, log-rank p = 0.043; DSS, log-rank p = 0.015; OS, log-ranl p = 0.020). Moreover, we confirmed this association in independent cohorts GSE72970 and GSE41258 (Fig. 1I and J).

High DLX4 expression is associated with malignant clinicopathologic features in CRC

High expression of DLX4 in CRC suggested poor prognosis. As shown in Fig. S1B and C, poor overall survival (OS) was significantly correlated with high DLX4 expression in elderly patients (aged > 65) and male patients. In terms of pathological parameters, associations were found between TNM stages and DLX4 expression. High DLX4 expression indicated poor prognosis in patients at T stages 3&4(p = 0.007), N stages 1&2(p = 0.008), and pathological stages III & IV (p = 0.02) (Fig. S1D-G). To verify DLX4’s potential utility as a prognostic marker in CRC, we utilized Cox proportional-hazards models on TCGA cohorts. Univariate cox regression (high vs. low, HR = 1.482, 95% CI: 1.169–1.877, p = 0.001) and multivariate cox regression (HR = 1.331, 95% CI: 1.023–1.733, P = 0.034) both suggested that DLX4 could predict poor survival in CRC. (Fig. S2A and B). We developed a nomogram for predicting 1, 3, and 5-year overall survival rates in CRC patients (Fig. S2C). The time-dependent ROC curves with AUC values of 0.763, 0.792, and 0.762 at these time points confirmed the model’s reliable predictive ability in the TCGA CRC datasets (Fig. S2E). In addition, the calibration curves showed that the observed outcomes were highly consistent with the predictions of the nomogram. (Fig. S2D) Decision curve analysis (DCA) validated the nomogram as an effective prognostic tool for DLX4-associated CRC (Fig. S2F). In summary, elevated DLX4 expression levels indicated aggressive clinicopathological features and poor prognosis in CRC patients. DLX4 could serve as a independent robust prognostic marker for CRC.

Association of genomics alterations and immunogenicity with DLX4 in CRC

To comprehensively elaborate the mutation features and biological functions for DLX4 in CRC development, we utilized the cBioPortal platform based on the TCGA database to conducted comprehensive analyses of copy number variations (CNVs) and single nucleotide variations (SNVs) of DLX4. The overview picture illustrated that the primary mutation type of DLX4 in CRC was no alternation, with a minor portion depicting mutation, amplification, and deletion (Fig. 2A, Fig. S3C). These findings aligned with the results from the TIMER2, demonstrating the relative proportions of the sCNA (Somatic Copy Number Alternations) of DLX4 in CRC and high gene mutation frequency (0.017%) (Fig. S3A and B). In CRC, DLX4 mutations mostly presented as diploid and gains. Amplifications were rare but associated with high DLX4 expression (Fig. S3E). Moreover, DLX4 CNV in COAD positively correlated with mRNA normalized RESM, showing a Spearman correlation of 0.26. In READ, the correlation increased to 0.36 (Fig. 3C). We detected DLX4 mutation sites in multiple CRC cohorts, including the S180Y alteration (Fig. 2B). The CNV pie chart distribution represented the percentage of heterozygous and homozygous CNV in Fig. S3D, indicated that the main CNV types of DLX4 in CRC were heterozygous deletion and amplification.

Next, we examined the genomic profiles linked to DLX4 expression in TCGA to investigate DLX4’s potential influence on other genes. The high-DLX4 group in CRC patients showed more clustered amplification peaks at 20q11-13, and their deletion peaks had higher G scores (Fig. 2E). Analysis of CNV amplification and deletion revealed significant differences in the amplification at 11p15.5, 17q12, and 17q24.1, and deletions at 1p36.11, 1p33, 1p13.2, 15q22.33, 17q24.3, 20p12.1, and 21q21.1 among various DLX4 expression groups (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, mutations in APC (77%), TP53 (61%) and TTN (47%) were frequent in the high-DLX4 group. In contrast, KRAS (45%), PIK3CA (29%) and SYNE1 (28%) were common in the low-DLX4 group (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, genes such as TGFBR3, HIF3A, ZMYM3 and TRPM7 were significantly upregulated in the high-DLX4 group (Fig. S2F).

We investigated the methylation level of DLX4 and its relationship with mRNA in CRC. The β-value and expression of DLX4 in CRC were significantly negatively correlated (Fig. 2D). We found a negative correlation between DLX4 mRNA expression and methylation levels of cg01713701, cg11769332, cg18877667, cg01713701, cg13206920, and cg08039704 probes (Fig. S4A).

Genomic instability could lead to the production of mutant proteins with high immunogenicity in tumors, potentially affecting the response to ICIs. To investigate the immunogenic effects of varying mutational profiles linked to DLX4 expression levels, we explored the relationship between immunogenicity indexes and DLX4 expression in TCGA. We found that patients in the high-DLX4 group having positive correlations with tumor-purity, aneuploidy scores, wGII, HRD, LST and LOH than those in low-DLX4 group (Fig. S4B).

In sum up, DLX4 expression reflected varying mutation profiles in CRC patients, and low expression of DLX4 mRNA might be associated with its aberrant DNA methylation status. Moreover, the high-DLX4 group was significantly positively correlated with several indicators of immunogenicity, revealing that expression of DLX4 might be associated with different immunogenicity.

We used multiple algorithms to analyze differences in immune cell and stromal cell abundance as well as immune scores between high and low DLX4 groups in the TIME. Considering that the immune microenvironments of colon and rectal cancer are slightly different, we analyzed them separately [43]. In contrast, the xCell and Quantiseq algorithm showed significant CD8 T cell expression only in RC patients, suggesting potential immune microenvironmental differences between CC and RC (Fig. 3A). DLX4 is significantly positively correlated with tumor cells and negatively corelated with B cells (Fig. 3D). Wilcoxon rank sum tests verified the mean expression of DLX4 in malignant tissue regions is significantly higher than that in normal regions of CRC tissue (Fig. 3F). Meanwhile, it was discovered that DLX4 was strongly associated with immune checkpoint related molecules, including CD276, VEGFA and VEGFB (Fig. 3E). To further discover the role of DLX4 in TIME and cellular communication, single-cell RNA-seq dataset EMTAB-8107 was utilized and indicating that DLX4 mainly expressed in malignant tumor cells (Fig. 4A-B), and up to 53.5% cells with DLX4 + were malignant tumor cells (Fig. 4C), which suggests that DLX4 may involve in the crosstalk between tumor cells and other immune cells. Then we carried out the cellular communication analysis to investigate the interaction between DLX4 + tumor cells and other cells. Utilizing the “CellChat” R package, tumors with DLX4 + or DLX4- formed a complex communication network with other cell groups (Fig. 4D). DLX4 + malignant was identified as the most active cell group of outgoing signaling patterns, exhibiting a high possibility that DLX4 + CRC cells can disturb other cell groups through various pathways (Fig. 4E). The above results demonstrated that DLX4 mainly expressed in CRC tumor cells and contribute to an immunosuppression TIME.

This led us to hypothesize that CRC patients with low DLX4 expression might benefit more from ICB therapy. In an independent ICB treatment cohort (E-MATB-6270), the high-DLX4 group exhibited a reduced rate of responder (CR and PR) (Fig. 4H). While proportion of responder is higher in high-DLX4 group than the low-DLX4 group, but not significantly, which may be attributable to the limited sample size. ROC curve analysis affirmed DLX4’s predictive accuracy for ICB therapy response (Fig. 4I). These findings implied a correlation between DLX4 expression levels and response to ICB therapy, indicating its potential utility as a clinical indicator in clinical. Further use of the TIDE algorithm identified a correlation between high DLX4 expression and adverse treatment outcomes in an anti-PD-1 cohort (Fig. 4J-K). To further delineate the role of DLX4 on CD8 T cell exhaustion, Within the four CRC dataset from TCGA and CRC datasets from GEO (GSE71187, GSE28722, GSE14333), elevated levels of CD8 T cells were linked to improved survival, and this This benefit to overall survival was only contingent upon a low DLX4 level (Fig. 4L-M). Our findings highlighted DLX4’s significant role in modulating the effectiveness of CD8 T cells on patient survival, where high DLX4 expression totally reversed the protective effect of CD8 T cells on prognosis of CRC patients, turning it into a risk factor. The GDSC database analysis showed that high DLX4 expression might indicate resistance to various drugs, such as Dabrafenib and Trametinib, while being sensitive to other drugs including AZD5582, PFI-3, CI-1033, SB505124, PF- 00299804, PI3Ka, AZD8835, Afatinib and AST-1306 (Fig. S6A). We evaluated both mRNA and protein level of DLX4 in normal colon mucosal cell line NCM460 and CRC cell lines (HCT116 and SW480). To delve into the mechanisms of oncogenic transcriptional regulation mediated by the homeobox protein DLX-4, we explored whether DLX-4 possesses intrinsic features of IDRs using computational models. Discussion

Through our comprehensive multi-omics analyses and in vitro experiments, we illuminated the intricate biological function of DLX4 in CRC across various cohorts. DLX4, a significant member of the DLX homeobox gene family, plays a crucial role in jaw development and the transformation process leading to oncogenicity. However, key aspects of DLX4’s involvement in CRC—including its expression patterns, prognostic implications, mutational status, immunotherapy responses, and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms—demand comprehensive investigation. Our research substantiated that DLX4 directly bound to the promoter of ZC3HC1, undertaking a critical role in transcriptional regulation. This interaction notably inhibited cell apoptosis while promoting cell cycle progression in CRC cell lines, suggesting DLX4 promotes cell proliferation. Moreover, potential properties of DLX4 to form spontaneous condensates might drive oncogenic transcriptional responses. We revealed the overexpression of DLX4 in CRC. Through univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis, we established DLX4 as an independent prognostic factor for CRC. Further, we crafted a nomogram designed to effectively predict the correlation between DLX4 expression and patient survival times, offering a novel tool for clinical assessment in CRC management.

Furthermore, the role of gene alterations of DLX4 has been well-established. We observed that most DLX4 CNVs involved either heterozygous amplification or deletion in CRC. Notably, high expression of DLX4 was significantly linked to amplification of chromosome band 11p15.5, which has been previously identified in liver metastases originating from primary CRC, primarily driven by ASCL2 and IGF2 [47]. The loss of 20p12.1 was validated in both CRC cell lines and primary samples [48]. Our data suggested that the loss of 20p12.1 might be associated with high DLX4 expression. Deletions in the 1p locus were frequently observed in CRC, with previous studies suggesting that loss of 1p36 could serve as an indicator for metastatic recurrence in CRC patients at stages II and III, especially in microsatellite-stable CRCs [49]. We identified that increased DLX4 expression in patients correlated with deletions at 1p36, 1p33, and 1p13, suggesting that high DLX4 expression could be indicative of worse survival outcomes. Previous research has established the poor prognosis of MSI-L patients compared to MSI-H patients [50]. We discovered that DLX4 expression was dramatically increased in MSI-L patients. We also found a substantial negative link between high DLX4 expression and methylation levels in CRC patients and investigated the CpG sites that were highly correlated with DLX4 expression, although more study is needed to identify specific methylation biomarkers. In sum up, these findings suggested that upregulated expression of DLX4 was related to aggressive phenotypes in CRC.

MSI CRC cases with a hereditary background exhibit a more robust immune response and induce immune evasion [51]. Recent pan-cancer studies have shed light on how transcriptional and post-transcriptional networks regulate tumor immune microenvironments (TIME) and immune evasion mechanisms. For example, Zhou et al. developed the IOBR framework, which integrates multi-omics data to dissect TME composition, immune ligand-receptor interactions, and immunotherapy responses across multiple cancers, providing a robust computational reference for immune remodeling analysis [52]. In thyroid cancer, Chen et al. applied dynamic network biomarkers to identify molecular subtypes with distinct immune infiltration and disease trajectories, highlighting how transcriptional regulators such as ASPH mediate immune escape [53]. In lung adenocarcinoma, Yu et al. uncovered the immunosuppressive role of the post-translational regulator B4GALT2, which depleted CD8 + T cells and impaired their functionality, ultimately reducing immunotherapy efficacy [54]. Contrasting these mechanisms, our study suggests that DLX4 reprograms the immune landscape in colorectal cancer (CRC) through a unique transcriptional axis. DLX4 upregulation correlates with elevated immune checkpoint expression, reduced NK/NKT cell activity, and exhausted CD8 + T cells, potentially mediated through ZC3HC1 activation and transcriptional condensate formation. These insights underscore DLX4’s specific role in orchestrating CRC immunosuppression and provide a complementary perspective to existing immune remodeling frameworks in other cancers. Although DLX4 has been relatively poorly explored in immunology, a previous study suggested that in autoimmune conditions like Alopecia Areata, DLX4 was a key regulator of induced immune infiltration [55]. Specifically, High levels of DLX4 expression may induce the differentiation of Treg cells and exhausted T cells and suppress proliferation of NK cells and NKT cells. This dynamic might contribute to an immunosuppressive TIME. Prior research has noted a significant overexpressed in Treg cells within CRC-infiltrated tissues, a positive correlation with the tumor’s TNM stage [56]. CTL is the most potent subset of lysogenic cells and decrease with increasing TNM staging in CRC [57]. Multiple studies have revealed that high infiltration of CTL is associated with improved probability of recurrence-free and overall survival in CRC patients [58]. In our study, various algorithms including Xcell, EPIC, Quantiseq and MCPcounter showed positive correlation between CD8 T cells and high DLX4-group. Previous studies found that CD3 and CD8 T lymphocytes were enriched in CC patients and significantly impacting overall survival, but not in rectal cancer [59]. Our findings showed higher T lymphoid infiltration in the high DLX4 group compared to the low DLX4 group, with RC patients in the high DLX4 group having higher T infiltrates than CC patients. Thus, DLX4 may contribute to T-cell immune infiltration in RC patients, shaping different TIME in RC and CC. Additionally, analysis of single cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptome independently proved that DLX4 was highly expressed in malignant tumor cells from different dimensions, which also contribute to the form of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Cellular communication analysis shows that DLX4 + tumor cells have active outgoing signaling patterns, which may affect the depletion of other immune cells, including CD8 T cells, by activating carcinogenic pathways and ligands. Results of immunotherapy response and TIDE score revealed a significant positive correlation between high DLX4 expression and CD8 T cells exhaustion. These results suggested that CD8 T cells in the DLX4 high-expression group might infiltrate into tumor tissues but did not fully exert their immune function under the influence of CD4 tumor cells. However, the specific mechanisms need to be further studied. Meanwhile, we discovered that DLX4 is strongly associated with immune checkpoint related molecules, especially those inhibiting CD8 T cell anti-tumor activity (CD276 [60], VEGFA, VEGFB [61]), was strongly evident. Thus, DLX4 might promote CRC development and poor prognosis through T-cell exhaustion and could serve as a potential target for CRC immunotherapy.

Fluorouracil-based combination therapies such as oxaliplatin or irinotecan (FOLFOX, FOLFIRI or XALIX) are fundamental in the first-line treatment of advanced CRC [62]. However, multiple drug resistance to 5-FU and platinum-based drugs is a major factor in treatment failure [63]. Our study proposes that W.13, STOCK1N.35,874 and imatinib can be potentially used as DLX4-related small molecule drugs to prevent CRC progression, further experimental validation is needed to confirm DLX4-dependence.

Recent studies have illuminated the role of LLPS in assembling multiple biomolecules at specific gene loci, which collaboratively regulate gene expression [21, 64]. This process has been shown to facilitate the formation of oncogenic transcription factor condensates, driving tumor mutations. In CRC, SENP1 enhances DNA damage repair and drug resistance by preventing the LLPS of transcription factor RNF168 [65]. Additionally, the RNA-binding protein DDX21 augments CRC metastasis through an MCM5-dependent epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway via LLPS [66]. Due to the abundance of IDRs within their structure, more than 80% eukaryotic TFs exhibit at least one IDR [67]. In our research, we concentrated on investigating the transcription factor DLX4, which was predominantly localized within the nuclear matrix, exhibiting a punctate or droplet-like distribution. Further analysis of the DLX4 amino acid sequence uncovered a notable abundance of IDRs, suggesting a propensity for phase separation. Most previous research has detailed how transcription factors and coactivators collaborate to initiate gene expression. In embryonic stem cells, the interaction between the transcription factor OCT4 and the mediator complex subunit MED1 leads to the formation of phase-separated condensates, which actively drive gene expression [68]. This process is dependent on OCT4’s presence. Some investigations have demonstrated the intrinsic capacity for LLPS within the transcription factor itself. Specifically, LLPS of specific structural domains of the transcription factor Runx 2 in the nucleus enhances EGFR transcription in cardiomyocytes [69]. Moreover, LLPS of transcription factors have been reported to be frequently involved in the regulation of a variety of biological functions, including chromosome organization, mitochondrial autophagy, and nucleosomes organization [21, 70, 71]. Furthermore, recent high-resolution studies have shown that oncogenic TFs like BRD4, MYC, and TP63 form LLPS condensates at super-enhancers to modulate transcription and drive tumor progression [72]. In gastrointestinal cancers, including CRC, LLPS regulators such as BRD4 and TP53 are frequently altered and correlate with immune infiltration and poor prognosis [73].

These comparisons underscore the biological plausibility of DLX4 participating in similar LLPS-mediated regulatory pathways in CRC. Our data further revealed that DLX4 transcriptionally regulates ZC3HC1, a downstream effector involved in cell cycle control. Transcriptomic and proteomic integration, along with DLX4 knockdown assays, support the role of ZC3HC1 as a functional target mediating DLX4’s oncogenic activity in CRC. Together, these findings suggest that DLX4 may drive tumor progression through LLPS-dependent transcriptional regulation, a mechanism warranting further biochemical validation.

The regulation of cell cycle and proliferation is a hallmark of malignant tumors, and our study elucidates the interplay between the transcription factor DLX4 and ZC3HC1 in this context. NIPA (nuclear interaction partner of ALK), encoded by ZC3HC1, is a critical F-box protein that plays an essential role in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis inhibition as part of the SCF complex (an SCF-type E3 ligase) [44, 74]. Research has demonstrated that cell cycle-dependent phosphorylation of NIPA controls the assembly of the SCFNIPA complex, specifically targeting nuclear Cyclin B1 for ubiquitination, thus maintaining its levels low within the nucleus [44]. Studies have also shown that NIPA exerts an anti-apoptotic effect in NPM-ALK-mediated signal transduction and impacts on ALK-positive malignancy, but the exact mechanisms remain unclear. These effects may be related to NIPA’s regulation of the transcription of the anti-apoptotic protein FKHR and NIPA’s effect on the cell cycle [75]. Despite the established role of NIPA in apoptosis and the cell cycle, the regulation of ZC3HC1 expression by transcription factors like DLX4 has not been previously explored.Our enrichment analysis of predicted transcriptional targets of DLX4 revealed significant enrichment in pathways associated with the cell cycle, particularly in mitotic cell cycle gene ontology pathways, with the strongest correlation observed between DLX4 and ZC3HC1. This suggests a potential mechanism by which DLX4 may promote CRC development, indirectly influencing both apoptosis and the cell cycle through ZC3HC1.

Several studies have elucidated the role of DLX4 in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. Previous study demonstrated that DLX4 knockdown induced cell cycle arrest in NSCLC cells, aligning with our results [76]. In endometrial cancer cells, overexpressed DLX4 led to a reduced proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and an increase in the S phase [77]. Our study found that knockdown of DLX4 inhibited cell viability and colony formation in HCT116 and SW480 cells compared to siRNA control group, suggesting that DLX4 enhances CRC cell proliferation. Flow cytometry verified a significant increase in the apoptotic rate after DLX4 knockdown in CRC cell lines. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of DLX4 on apoptosis by examining the expression of the Bax/Bcl-2/Caspase3 proteins in CRC. In DLX4-downregulated CRC cell lines, we observed elevated levels of Bax and Caspase3, along with decreased levels of Bcl-2 compared to control groups. These findings suggest that overexpression of DLX4 may inhibit apoptosis. Another study showed that DLX4 inhibited apoptosis in choriocarcinoma cells by regulating caspase-3 and activating caspase-8 expression, which further supports our findings [15]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these processes warrant further investigation.

In this study, we explored the molecular mechanisms behind the stated phenotypes and revealed several signaling pathways that are closely related to these phenotypes. Our findings revealed that DLX4 knockdown resulted in downregulated expression of CyclinD1 and CDK4, thereby blocking cell cycle progression. Additionally, after DLX4 knockdown, CyclinE1 and CDK2 expressions were reduced. Flow cytometry results showed DLX4 promoted an increase percentage of cells in G1 and S phases. We suggested that DLX4 might promote cell cycle entry by affecting the expression of CyclinD1 and CDK4. Furthermore, our analysis indicates a significant correlation between DLX4 and ZC3HC1, suggesting that the DLX4/ZC3HC1 axis may play a pivotal role in regulating these pathways. As ZC3HC1 is implicated in the modulation of cell cycle and apoptosis through its role as an F-box protein, the interplay between DLX4 and ZC3HC1 could provide additional insights into the complex regulatory network governing CRC progression. By influencing both pro-apoptotic and cell cycle-related pathways, DLX4 may have a multifaceted impact on CRC tumorigenesis, warranting further investigations into the mechanisms involved.

Furthermore, our integrative findings support a causal hierarchy linking DLX4 overexpression to oncogenic immune remodeling in colorectal cancer. First, DLX4 is strongly associated with the enrichment of immunosuppressive cells, including M2-like TAMs, MDSCs, and Tregs, which are well-known to suppress CTL responses. Second, although technically challenging, the rescue of this immune phenotype—via either ectopic ZC3HC1 expression or modulation of DLX4 transcriptional condensates—would provide direct evidence for mechanistic causality.

Based on these data, we propose two testable hypotheses: (1) inhibiting DLX4’s liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) ability may reduce its transcriptional control over ZC3HC1 and downstream immunosuppressive programs; and (2) blocking the DLX4–ZC3HC1 promoter interaction could restore CTL functionality and mitigate tumor immune evasion. These hypotheses lay the groundwork for future therapeutic strategies targeting DLX4’s structural and regulatory functions in CRC.

This study presents several limitations that merit consideration. Firstly, while we investigated the phase-separated phenotype of the transcription factor DLX4, although computational analyses suggest DLX4 may form condensates via its low-complexity domain (LCD), experimental validation of LLPS—including FRAP to measure condensate turnover or chemical disruption with 1,6-hexanediol—was beyond the scope of this study due to technical constraints. These experiments represent a critical direction for future research to determine whether DLX4’s transcriptional activity relies on phase-separated states. Secondly, although our findings indicate that DLX4 regulates ZC3HC1 expression and that both genes impact similar aspects of cell cycle progression, we acknowledge that functional rescue experiments are needed to establish a direct mechanistic link. Additionally, while the nuclear localization and expression patterns of DLX4 suggest possible involvement of LLPS, we did not directly assess whether DLX4’s transcriptional activity depends on its phase-separated state. These limitations highlight the need for further studies to validate the DLX4–ZC3HC1 regulatory axis and to explore the potential role of LLPS in mediating its function. Moreover, the prognostic significance of DLX4 has yet to be validated in real-world cohorts, prospective validation in independent clinical cohorts is needed to confirm DLX4’s robustness as a biomarker, and another limitation of this study is the absence of in vivo validation, such as xenograft or organoid-based models, which are critical to evaluating therapeutic relevance in a physiological setting. Future studies are warranted to test DLX4 function in these systems. Furthermore, DLX4’s specific involvement in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) requires further investigation. Our conclusions regarding DLX4’s role in immune suppression are based on integrative analysis using computational methods. However, direct functional validation, such as immune cell profiling or co-culture assays, is lacking and should be prioritized in future work.

In summary, our study provides the first comprehensive delineation of DLX4’s role within the CRC TIME, utilizing bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomics to elucidate its interaction with the immune microenvironment. We also establish DLX4’s predictive capability regarding immunotherapy response. Importantly, DLX4 knockdown significantly impaired cell proliferation in vitro, as flow cytometry indicated a reduction in G1 and S phases, alongside an increase in G0 phase cells, suggesting cell cycle arrest. Our findings establish that DLX4 promotes cell cycle entry and inhibits apoptosis. The transcriptional target ZC3HC1, linked to cell cycle regulation and anti-apoptotic properties, may work in concert with DLX4. We hypothesize that the DLX4/ZC3HC1 axis regulates cell cycle progression, facilitating CRC development by enhancing cell cycle entry and promoting resistance to apoptosis, potentially through LLPS-dependent mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, we establish that DLX4 is significantly associated with prognosis, clinicopathological features, and the immune microenvironment in CRC. Utilizing bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomics, we demonstrate that DLX4 is predominantly expressed in tumor cells and plays a critical role in shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment through the exhaustion of CD8 T cells, which correlates with poor responses to immunotherapy. In CRC tumor cells, DLX4 appears to exert its function in a phase separation-dependent manner by binding to the promoter of the cell cycle-related gene ZC3HC1, thereby influencing cell cycle progression and apoptosis. These findings position DLX4 as a potential personalized prognostic biomarker and a novel therapeutic target for CRC patients, underscoring the necessity for further investigations to elucidate its mechanisms and therapeutic implications.
