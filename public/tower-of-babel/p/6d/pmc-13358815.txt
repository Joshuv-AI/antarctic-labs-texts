A guiding beacon for surgeon-scientists: book review of Surgeons’ Guide to Medical Writing
Authors: Jinlin Wu
Published: 2026

With years of experience in surgical clinical practice and academic research, I have published roughly one hundred papers and authored Interpretation of the STROBE Statement for Observational Research. I frequently discuss paper writing and manuscript revision with young surgical colleagues. When I first picked up Surgeons’ Guide to Medical Writing (1), published by AME Publishing Company, an immediate sense of kindred spirit washed over me, accompanied by profound regret for not having access to such a resource earlier in my career.

In my early days learning medical writing, no systematic textbooks tailored specifically to surgical researchers were available. I could only piece together fragmented guidance from generic academic articles and journal author guidelines, which led to countless detours. By chance, I came across Dr. Elefteriades’ article “Twelve tips on writing a good scientific paper” (2). This concise piece struck me instantly. Free from rigid, hollow theoretical jargon, it unpacked the core logic of manuscript writing from a practicing clinical researcher’s perspective, marking a turning point in my understanding of academic communication. It dawned on me that papers teaching scholars how to craft scientific manuscripts carry immense scholarly value, and its insights have shaped every piece of research and writing work I have produced ever since. Serendipitously, during my subsequent academic visit to Yale University, Dr. Elefteriades turned out to be my supervisor.

Inspired by his rigorous writing philosophy, I later compiled Interpretation of the STROBE Statement for Observational Research to address the prevalent obstacles faced by domestic surgical investigators. Yet even after finishing this book, I remained convinced that the market lacked a comprehensive guide fully aligned with surgical practice that covers the entire lifecycle of clinical research—until I read this volume.

Edited by three veteran journal editors, Abe Fingerhut, Des Winter, and Steven Wexner, this book gathers contributions from leading surgical specialists worldwide (1). Comprising 21 chapters, it covers every stage of surgical research from formulating clinical research questions to navigating the ethical pitfalls of artificial intelligence (AI). Unlike generic medical writing handbooks, it is custom-built for surgeons and packed with discipline-specific practical advice. Several sections stand out as particularly relevant to the recurring struggles I witness among early-career surgeons, and I would like to share their unique strengths.

Tables and figures form the face of any surgical paper. Surgical research heavily relies on intraoperative images, survival curves, baseline patient data, and multivariate analytical graphs. Many investigators generate solid clinical data yet compromise the quality of their manuscripts through disorganized, illogical visual exhibits. This book devotes an entire chapter to systematic instructions on designing tables and figures, offering concrete, actionable guidance that fills a long-standing gap in domestic surgical writing resources.

Most medical writing manuals only focus on drafting manuscripts, with few resources unpacking the full publication journey from the standpoint of journal editors and peer reviewers. This book fills this critical gap, featuring dedicated chapters on journal selection, responding to manuscript rejection, open-access journals vs. predatory publishers, and the peer review system, forming a complete, practical roadmap for submission.

AI-assisted writing and figure generation have become ubiquitous in modern medical research, yet most scholars lack clarity on its legitimate boundaries. Many over-rely on AI to generate full manuscripts, while others overlook risks including AI hallucinations, patient privacy breaches, copyright violations, and data distortion. A full chapter in this book addresses research misconduct and standardized AI application with balanced, rigorous arguments. It acknowledges AI’s auxiliary merits in literature sorting, grammar polishing and statistical coding, yet explicitly prohibits generating complete manuscripts via AI, and highlights major hazards such as fabricated citations, confidential data leakage and textual plagiarism.

Beyond these highlights, the book boasts a complete, well-structured framework spanning the full spectrum of academic work: framing research questions via the FINER and PICO(T) frameworks; reporting standards for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case series and systematic reviews with corresponding EQUATOR guidelines (CONSORT, STROBE, SUPER); the IMRAD manuscript structure; linguistic norms for scientific writing; guidance for non-native English authors; basic biostatistics; reference management; and step-by-step grant writing tutorials. The SUPER guideline for surgical technique reporting and the PROCESS guideline for surgical case series are exclusive, invaluable resources rarely found elsewhere.

Dr. Elefteriades’ 12 writing tips served as a tiny flame that lit my path in academic research (2), whereas Surgeons’ Guide to Medical Writing functions as a full-fledged lighthouse illuminating the entire journey of surgical scholarly communication. This volume deserves a permanent spot on every clinical surgeon’s, surgical resident’s, and early-career researcher’s desk. Far more than a textbook on academic writing, it is an indispensable tool for surgeons to translate clinical observations into impactful, globally recognized research.
