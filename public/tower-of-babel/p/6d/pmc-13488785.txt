Cytokine-induced memory-like natural killer cells in systemic lupus erythematosus patients
Authors: Chien-Ya Hsu, Yen-Chang Lee, Hsiu-Shan Hsiao, Pei-Tzu Lee, Ji-Yih Chen, Syh-Jae Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNatural killer (NK) cell abnormalities may contribute to the pathogenesis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Interleukin (IL)-15, an immunoregulatory cytokine crucial for NK survival, is overexpressed in SLE patients. Cytokine-induced memory-like (CIML) NK cells, which are NK cells pre-activated with short-term IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 followed by incubation with low-dose IL-15 for 1-2 weeks, exhibit enhanced NK effector function upon re-stimulation.MethodsWe collected blood samples from 54 patients with SLE and 38 healthy volunteers. Extended IL-15 stimulation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was performed. Surface marker analysis, IFN-γ, TNF-α production, and cytotoxicity evaluation were conducted. Next, we generated CIML NK cells by culturing PBMCs with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18. The influence of short-term and long-term IL-15 treatment on CIML NK cells was assessed. Finally, we examined the effect of re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 on CIML NK cells from SLE patients and healthy controls. Cytotoxicity, CD107a degranulation, and surface marker expression were analyzed.ResultsWe found that 1. The percentage of CD3-CD56+ NK cells was initially decreased in SLE patients compared with controls, but was enhanced after long-term IL-15 incubation; 2. Long-term IL-15 stimulation facilitated the conversion from CD3-CD56dim to CD3-CD56bright NK cells; 3. CIML NK cells were successfully generated in SLE patients, showing increased percentages of CD3-CD56+ and CD3- CD56bright NK cells and a concomitant decrease in CD3-CD56dim NK cells; 4. SLE CIML NK cells showed decreased IFN-γ, CD69, CD94, NKG2A, NKG2C, NKP30, NKP44, NKP46, and granzyme B expression compared with healthy controls; 5. On day 8, re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 further enhanced the expression of IFN-γ, CD69, CD94, NKG2A, perforin, and granzyme B expression on SLE CIML NK cells; 6. Granzyme B expression in SLE CIML NK cells was higher on day 8 than on day 15.ConclusionOur findings revealed phenotypic differences and functional deficiencies in SLE CIML NK cells. Further investigation into the role of SLE CIML NK cells in providing antiviral and anti-tumor responses is warranted.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Cytokine-induced memory-like natural killer cells, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Natural killer cell, NK cell memory, Interleukin, Autoimmune disease

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease with multiorgan involvement, characterized by many immunologic abnormalities [1]. Natural killer (NK) cells are a distinct lineage of lymphoid cells characterized by the absence of membrane CD3, and the presence of CD16 and CD56, which are responsible for the first-line defense against tumor and virus-infected cells. The role of NK cells in the pathogenesis of SLE remains poorly understood. Previous studies demonstrated the numerical and functional deficiencies in NK cells among SLE patients, making them more susceptible to infections [2, 3]. Chronic inflammation may increase the cancer risk in autoimmune diseases, as evidenced by the substantial increase in the risk of hematologic malignancies observed in SLE patients [4].

Interleukin (IL)-15, a member of γ-chain cytokine, is a pro-inflammatory cytokine essential for NK cell proliferation and differentiation [5, 6]. Serum IL-15 levels are elevated in SLE patients compared to healthy individuals [7, 8]. Our previous studies have detailed the short-term (18 hours) effect of IL-15 on activation of NK cells from SLE patients [9–11].

NK cells, when pre-activated with a combination of IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 followed by a resting period cultured with low-dose IL-15, exhibit enhanced effector function when re-stimulated. They were called the cytokine-induced memory-like (CIML) NK cells [12, 13]. CIML NK cells have been demonstrated to provide superior anti-tumor efficiency compared to conventional NK cells [14, 15]. We have previously examined how IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity were impaired in CIML NK cells from patients with rheumatoid arthritis [16]. However, CIML NK cells have not yet been investigated in SLE patients.

In the present study, we examined the effect of long-term IL-15 incubation on NK activation in SLE patients and sought to generate CIML NK cells in SLE patients and examine their phenotypic and functional characteristics, in comparison with healthy controls.

Methods

Study subject and preparation of mononuclear cells

The clinical characteristics of the study subjects are shown in Table 1. We recruited 54 SLE patients and 38 healthy volunteers from the Outpatient Department, Division of Rheumatology, Department of Internal Medicine, and Division of Asthma, Allergy, and Rheumatology, Department of Pediatrics, Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou, Taiwan. We diagnosed all SLE patients abided by the new 2019 SLE EULAR/ACR Classification Criteria and evaluated the disease activity of these patients according to the SLEDAI score, a global index validated to be reliable and sensitive to disease activity change. The participants were randomly allocated to one of the experiments described below. Peripheral blood samples were collected into sterile tubes containing heparin (20 units/ml) and processed within 24 hours of collection. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated from heparinized blood using Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation.

We confirmed that the above protocols and methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB: 201801498A3). Peripheral blood samples were obtained from volunteers with the pre-approval of Medical Ethics and Human Clinical Trial Committee of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. Additionally, written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers.

Extended IL-15 stimulation of mononuclear cells

After preparation of MNCs, PBMCs (peripheral blood mononuclear cells) from healthy volunteers and SLE patients were incubated for 10 days in complete RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal calf serum, with or without recombinant human IL-15 (rhIL-15)(10 ng/ml, Preprotech, Rocky Hill, USA). Cultured cells were then harvested and prepared for analysis of surface markers and cytotoxicity evaluation.

CIML generation

For CIML generation, MNCs were incubated with rhIL-15 (1 ng/ml) for 16 hours as controls. The MNCs (3 × 106/ml) were incubated with rhIL-12 (10 ng/ml, Preprotech, Rocky Hill, USA), rhIL-15 (1 ng/ml), and IL-18 (50 ng/ml, R&D Systems, USA) for 16 hours as the pre-activated group. Control and pre-activated cells were washed by PBS solution for 3 times, and cultured in RPMI-1640 with rhIL-15 (1 ng/ml) and 10% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich) for various time points. Every 2 to 3 days, half of the medium was discarded and replenished by fresh medium with IL-15 (1 ng/ml). Cultured cells were then harvested and re-stimulated with IL-12 (10 ng/ml), IL-18 (50 ng/ml) plus IL-15 (100 ng/ml) for 6 hours. Cell viability was assessed before and after (Day 8 and 15) CIML NK cell generation by staining with propidium iodide (PI, 1 mg/ml, Sigma) and analyzing the percentage of PI-negative CD3-CD56+ NK cells by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometric analysis of NK cell markers

Cells were washed in cold PBS with 0.1% sodium azide and 2% FCS and subsequently stained with anti-human monoclonal antibodies, including Becton-Dickinson anti-CD3-APC/CD16-APC-Cyaninie7+ CD56-FITC, CD69-PE, CD94-PE, NKG2A-PE. The fluorescent staining was analyzed using a flow cytometer FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Electronic gating was set based on FSC/SSC histograms following staining with anti-CD3-APC/CD16-APC-Cyaninie7+ CD56-FITC antibodies.

Intracellular stain assay

For intracellular stain, cells were stained with anti-CD3-APC mAbs (BD Bioscience) and anti-CD56-FITC for 20 minutes. Subsequently, they were washed with PBS and resuspended in Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Bioscience). After incubation at 4 °C for 20 minutes with one wash using Perm/wash buffer, cells were further stained with anti-human IFN-γ-PE and TNF-α-PE (BD Bioscience) for 20 minutes at room temperature. The expression of intracellular perforin and granzyme B was analyzed after permeabilization of the cell membrane using a Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) with anti-perforin- PerCP or anti-granzyme B-PE (BD Pharmingen). The samples were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cytotoxicity assay

Flow cytometric NK cytotoxicity assays were performed as previously described. [17] Control and CIML NK cells were harvested after a resting period of 8 or 15 days. Cells (Effectors) were re-stimulated with IL-12, IL-15 and IL-18, and then co-cultured with K562 cells (Targets) at various Effector-to-Target (E:T) ratios for 4 hours. After incubation, cells were stained with CD45-FITC (BD Bioscience) and propidium iodide (PI, 1 mg/ml, Sigma). Percentages of dead K562 cells were calculated from histograms showing the fluorescent intensity of PI uptake by K562 cells after subtracting background cell death.

Degranulation assay

For the degranulation assay, cells were stained with anti-CD3-APC/CD56-FITC mouse anti-human monoclonal antibodies (BD Pharmingen) and anti-CD107a-PE and then incubated at 37 °C for 25 minutes in the dark. Cells were cultured with K562 cells at ratios of 5:1 for 4 hours. CD107a production on CD3-CD56+ NK cells was further analyzed by flow cytometer.

Statistical analysis

We used the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U-test for analyzing the data groups of SLE patients and healthy donors. The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used for determining the responses of IL-15. If the p value was less than 0.05, the comparison between the data groups was considered significantly distinct. The data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Data availability

The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Characteristics of SLE patients and healthy volunteers

The demographic features, laboratory data, and medication of SLE patients are shown in Table 1. SLE patients were predominantly female (M:F = 7:47), with ages ranging from 22 to 64 years. Patients had lower CD3-CD56+ NK cell percentages in the peripheral blood compared with healthy controls (2.2%±0.2% vs. 5.4%±1.1%, p = 0.003). As shown in Fig. 2B, CD69 expression on both resting SLE NK cells and control NK cells were minimal (2.2%±0.7% in SLE vs. 3.1%±1.1% in controls, p = 0.267). Extended IL-15 stimulation enhanced CD69 expression on SLE NK cells to a greater extent compared to corresponding controls (14.9%±1.7% vs. 9.1%±1.8%, p = 0.050).

The expression level of IFN-γ (Fig. 2C) on resting SLE NK cells was comparable to that of controls (48.2%±5.0% vs. 39.9%±6.0%, p = 0.572). In contrast, resting NK cells expressed more TNF-α than controls (40.5%±4.7% vs. 24.1%±3.1%, p = 0.03). Extended IL-15 stimulation enhanced both IFN-γ and TNF-α expression in NK cells from both SLE patients and controls, respectively (IFN-γ: SLE 74.7%±3.7% vs. 48.2%±5.0%, p=0.003; Controls 75.1%±5.0% vs. 39.9%±6.1%, p = 0.008; TNF-α: SLE 68.2%±3.5% vs. 40.5%±4.7%, p < 0.001; Controls 67.9%±5.1% vs. 24.1%±3.1%, p = 0.008).

As depicted in Figs. 2E and 2F, the production of granzyme B and perforin in SLE NK cells were similar to those in controls (Granzyme B: MFI 918.0 ± 134.7 vs. 831.6 ± 99.1, p = 0.526, percentage 77.3%±4.2% vs. 80.0%±4.4%, p=0.942; Perforin: MFI 357.2 ± 26.7 vs. 386.9 ± 50.9, p = 0.526, percentage 76.2%±2.6% vs. 80.2%±1.9%, p=0.294). Extended IL-15 stimulation enhanced granzyme B and perforin production in NK cells from both SLE patients and controls alike (Granzyme B: SLE MFI 1712.3 ± 224.0 vs. 918.0 ± 134.7, p = 0.002, percentage 98.3%±0.8% vs. 77.3%±4.2%, p<0.001; Controls MFI 1994.8 ± 453.4 vs. 831.6 ± 99.1, p = 0.036, percentage 98.2%±1.5% vs. 80.0%±4.4%, p=0.012; Perforin: SLE MFI 579.7 ± 38.9 vs. 357.2 ± 26.7, p < 0.001, percentage 89.4%±2.6% vs. 76.2%±2.6%, p<0.001; Controls MFI 652.5 ± 95.0 vs. 386.9 ± 50.9, p = 0.025, percentage 93.0%±3.2% vs. 80.2%±1.9%, p=0.025).

We also examined the cytotoxicity of NK cells against K562 cells at E:T ratios of 12.5:1 and 25:1 in SLE patients and controls (Figs. 2G and 2H). The cytotoxicity of resting SLE NK cells exhibited no difference against K562 cells compared to controls, regardless of the E:T ratio being 12.5:1 or 25:1 (E:T = 12.5:1: 12.8%±1.3% vs. 16.2%±1.7%, p = 0.142; E:T = 25:1: 17.7%±2.1% vs. 21.9%±2.8%, p = 0.208). IL-15 stimulation enhanced NK cytotoxicity in both SLE patients and controls. However, a decreased cytotoxicity of SLE NK cells compared to controls was observed specifically with an E:T ratio of 12.5:1 (25.1%±1.7% vs. 36.0%±4.0%, p = 0.012).

Increased percentage of CD3-CD56bright NK cells after IL-15 stimulation in SLE patients

Next, we examined the differential expression of CD69, IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin levels between two major subsets, CD3-CD56dim NK cells and CD3-CD56bright NK cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, without IL-15, the percentage of CD3-CD56bright NK cells was lower than CD3-CD56dim counterparts in SLE patients (23.6%±4.1% vs. 76.4%±4.1%, p < 0.001) and healthy controls (21.4%±2.2% vs. 78.6%±2.2%, p = 0.008). IL-15 treatment led to the conversion of CD56dim NK subsets into CD56bright subsets. Extended IL-15 stimulation enhanced the percentage of CD56bright NK cells in SLE patients (83.7%±1.8% vs. 23.6%±4.1%, p < 0.001) and healthy donors (80.4%±4.2% vs. 21.4%±2.2%, p = 0.008), with concomitant decrease of CD56dim NK subsets.

No major difference could be found in CD56dim NK cells between SLE patients and healthy controls with regards to their CD69, IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin expression no matter how the PBMCs were treated (Fig. 3B–F). However, in SLE patients, CD56bright NK cells demonstrated higher CD69 expression compared to CD56dim NK cells (7.5%±3.2% vs. 2.3%±1.0%, p = 0.024).

After IL-15 treatment, the expression of IFN-γ in CD56dim and CD56bright NK cells was augmented in both SLE patients and healthy controls. Particularly in SLE patients, there was a notable increase in IFN-γ expression within the CD56bright subset compared to the CD56dim subset following IL-15 stimulation (Fig. 3C).

For both SLE patients and healthy donors alike, IL-15 enhanced TNF-α, granzyme B and perforin expression of CD56bright NK cells to a greater extent compared to CD56dim counterparts (Fig. 3D–F).

Subsequently, both the control group and the pre-activated group were cultured with low-dose IL-15 for either 16 hours or 8 days to determine the influence of short-term and long-term IL-15 treatment.

As shown in Fig. 4A, the percentage of CD56+ NK cells was higher in the pre-activated group compared to controls in healthy donors after culture with IL-15 for 8 days (41.0%±4.6% vs. 28.8%±1.9%, p = 0.008) and similarly in SLE patients (19.6%±3.7% vs. 11.7%±2.8%, p = 0.006). However, the percentages of CD56+ NK cells in either the control or pre-activate group were markedly higher in healthy donors compared to SLE patients on day 8, respectively.

On day 8, the percentage of CD56dim NK cells in both the control group (49.3%±8.2% vs. 85.2%±1.8%, p = 0.001) and the pre-activated group (55.2%±6.5% vs. 75.9%±2.9%, p = 0.015) was decreased after long-term cultured with IL-15 in SLE patients compared to the percentage after short-term IL-15 treatment. In contrast, there was no difference in the percentage of CD56dim NK cells after short-term and long-term IL-15 treatment in healthy donors (Fig. 4B).

After long-term incubation with IL-15, the percentage of CD56bright NK cells in the control group increased in healthy donors compared to short-term IL-15 treatment (16.0%±3.4% vs. 3.0%±0.4%, p < 0.001), but not in SLE patients (7.0%±2.3% vs. 4.5%±1.1%, p = 0.419). Pre-activation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 restored the expression of CD56bright NK cells in SLE patients (18.8%±7.2% vs. 7.0%±2.3%, p = 0.002) (Fig. 4C).

Deficient cytotoxic function of SLE CIML NK cells

We next compared the CD107a degranulation and K562 cytotoxicity of CIML NK cells between SLE patients and healthy controls. On day 8, the CD107a expression level (Fig. 7A) was lower in SLE patients compared to healthy donors (17.7 ± 3.1% vs. 42.6 ± 7.2%, p = 0.008). However, re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 resulted in enhanced CD107a expression on NK cells from both SLE patients and controls (SLE 31.8 ± 3.6% vs.17.7 ± 3.1%, p = 0.017; Controls 50.6 ± 6.8% vs. 42.6 ± 7.2%, p = 0.043). Nonetheless, CD107a expression remained lower in SLE patients (31.8 ± 3.6% vs. 50.6 ± 6.8%, p = 0.049). On day 15, the CD107a expression was markedly lower compared to day 8. Re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 enhanced CD107a expression on CIML NK cells from healthy donors (23.6 ± 4.1% vs. 19.1 ± 3.4%, p = 0.012) but not in SLE patients (21.1 ± 5.6% vs. 15.7 ± 3.5%, p = 0.416) on day 15.

Decreased K562 cytotoxicity at an E:T ratio 5:1 of resting SLE CIML NK cells was noted on day 8 compared to healthy controls (16.7 ± 2.5% vs. 51.7 ± 5.0%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 7B). Re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 resulted in increased NK cytotoxicity in controls (61.1 ± 6.1% vs. 51.7 ± 5.0%, p = 0.011), but elicited a minimal response in SLE patients (18.0 ± 2.6% vs. 16.7 ± 2.5%, p = 0.241).

Cell viability during CIML NK cell generation

We analyzed the viability of CIML NK cells before and after (Day 8 and 15) their generation from both SLE patients and healthy controls. Viability was assessed based on the percentage of PI-negative cells within the CD3− CD56+ NK cell population. Across all groups and time points, cell viability remained consistently high ( > 85%), indicating that the CIML induction process was well-tolerated in both SLE-derived and healthy NK cells.

Discussion

Our previous studies have characterized various aspects of NK phenotypes and their short-term (18 hours) response to IL-15 in SLE patients [9–11]. In the present study, we laid our emphasis on the long-term (10 days) effect of IL-15 as it better reflects the clinical scenario that circulating IL-15 is persistently high in SLE patients [18]. The impact of IL-15 would be increasingly noticeable with extended incubation periods.

The 10-day IL-15 stimulation protocol was chosen to reflect the chronic elevation of IL-15 observed in SLE patients [7, 18], allowing us to assess its long-term impact on NK cell survival and function, in contrast to the 8- and 15-day time points used to evaluate the memory-like properties of CIML NK cells.

SLE NK cells, similar to those from healthy donors, increased after 10-day culture with IL-15, highlighting its crucial role in the survival and expansion of NK cells [5]. When CD56+ NK cells were further divided into CD56dim and CD56bright subsets, we found that IL-15 increased the percentage of CD56bright NK cells with concomitant decrease of CD56dim NK cells in SLE patients.

CD56bright NK cells demonstrate heightened proliferation capacity and predominantly engage in cytokine secretion, often associated with immunoregulation and immaturity. Conversely, CD56dim NK cells, characterized by elevated levels of perforin, granzyme, and CD16 expression, are recognized as cytotoxic NK cells due to their enhanced cytotoxicity [19].

Previous study found that increased IFN-γ expression of CD56bright NK cells in SLE patients [3] and that CD56bright NK cells were expanded in SLE patients and correlated with disease activity [20]. Our study also revealed elevated IFN-γ expression in CD56bright NK cells among SLE patients and IFN-γ production increased dramatically after extended IL-15 stimulation providing insight into the correlation between disease activity in SLE patients, circulating IL-15 levels, and the expansion of CD56bright NK cells.

While CD56bright NK cells primarily participate in cytokine secretion and immunoregulation, they have the capacity to transition into cytotoxicity under suitable activation conditions. [21] This phenomenon is evident during prolonged IL-15 stimulation of NK cells. Following prolonged exposure to IL-15, CD56bright NK cells exhibited heightened expression of perforin and granzyme B, thereby enhancing their cytotoxic potential.

The cytotoxicity of NK cells from SLE patients against K562 cells, derived from myelogenous leukemia, was compromised. Following IL-15 stimulation, NK cells from SLE patients exhibited reduced killing ability at an E:T ratio of 12.5:1, which could be restored by increasing the E:T ratio to 25:1. This observation suggests a potential role for immunotherapy in cancer treatment for SLE patients, as their impaired NK cell cytotoxicity may necessitate increasing the number of NK cells.

We further investigated the phenotypic variations and effector functions of IL-15-stimulated CIML NK cells in individuals with SLE. The CIML NK cell generation protocol employed varying IL-15 concentrations to optimize NK cell priming, maintenance, and re-activation, as established in prior studies [12, 13]. Low-dose IL-15 (1 ng/ml) was used during pre-activation and maintenance to support NK cell survival and memory-like properties, while high-dose IL-15 (100 ng/ml) during re-stimulation was designed to robustly enhance effector functions, reflecting physiological cytokine surges during immune responses. We illustrated a notable and more pronounced defect in CIML NK cells specific to SLE patients, surpassing the responsiveness of conventional SLE NK cells upon stimulation by IL-15. Impaired IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin responses upon re-stimulation were evident in CIML NK cells in SLE patients. This suggests impairment not only in cytotoxicity but also in immunoregulatory functions.

CD69, a triggering C-type lectin receptor, plays a crucial role in modulating NK cell cytokine release patterns and serves as a dependable activation marker for NK cells [22, 23]. We found that long-term culture with IL-15 induced CD69 expression in SLE NK cells comparable to healthy donors regardless of CD56dim and CD56bright subsets. CD56bright NK cells derived from SLE patients exhibited heightened expression of CD69 after IL-15 stimulation. Nevertheless, a noteworthy deficiency in CD69 expression was observed in SLE CIML NK cells following re-stimulation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18, in contrast to healthy donors.

During CIML NK cell generation, resting CD56+ NK cell percentages increased on day 8 in healthy donors but not SLE patients. Pre-activation with IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 improved the CD56+ NK cell yield in SLE CIML NK cells on day 8, but still lower than corresponding healthy donors.

NKG2A, which contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motif, is a lectin-like NK cell receptor that forms heterodimers with the CD94 molecule, exerting an inhibitory function on NK effector function. As opposed to NKG2A, NKG2C transmits activating signals to NK cells. Upon re-stimulation, CIML NK cells exhibited an increase in the expression level of NKG2A, while the expression level of NKG2C remained unchanged. We observed a deficiency in NKG2A expression on SLE CIML NK cells even after re-stimulation. Additionally, CIML NK cells from SLE patients displayed a reduced level of NKG2C, and re-stimulation had no effect on NKG2C expression. In contrast, re-stimulation of CIML NK cells from normal controls resulted in heightened NKG2A expression, with the expression level of NKG2A surpassing that of NKG2C.

Given that CIML NK cells showed elevated cytotoxicity after re-stimulation, it is plausible that more inhibitory signals from NKG2A than activating signals from NKG2C may contribute to an immunoregulatory effect, leading to the down-regulation of NK cell activity after activation. This behavior may act as a brake mechanism to prevent excessive cytotoxicity, thereby reducing the risk of autoimmunity.

The natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs), such as NKp44, NKp30, and NKp46, are type I trans-membrane receptors involved in regulating NK cell cytotoxicity and immunoregulation. Previous studies have shown that NKp46 and NKp30 are expressed by NK cells freshly isolated from peripheral blood, while NKp44 is predominantly found on activated NK cells [24]. The NCR receptors primarily play a role in activating NK cells. Our research revealed a decrease in the expression of NKp30 and NKp46 receptors in CIML NK cells of SLE patients. Tumor tissues can secrete cytokines and chemokines that facilitate the recruitment of specific subsets of NK cells and trigger the expression of NKp44. NKp44 receptors, expressed by activated NK cells, interact with their ligands, resulting in NK-mediated target cell killing. [25] The decreased expression of activating receptors on CIML NK cells in SLE patients may lead to impaired activation upon encountering tumor cells or virus-infected cells.

Granzyme B is an unique serine protease responsible for NK-mediated apoptosis of tumor cells or virally infected cells [26]. Perforin is a 60–70 kDa glycoprotein essential for pore formation on the target cell membrane induced by NK cells [27]. We observed that long-term incubation with IL-15 enhanced perforin and granzyme B expression of SLE NK cells, especially the CD56bright subsets. SLE CIML NK cells readily responded to IL-12+IL-15+IL-18 re-stimulation, demonstrating a memory-like property. However, the expression levels of granzyme B and perforin were significantly lower compared to those in healthy donors.

Defective NK cytolytic capacities have been described in SLE patients [28]. NK cells release lytic granules to eliminate tumor cells. When these granules fuse with the cell membrane, CD107a is exposed on the cell surface. Therefore, CD107a expression serves as a marker indicating NK cell cytolytic activity [29]. We previously reported that CD107a degranulation in SLE NK cells was comparable to that in healthy donors [9]. Hence, the reduced NK cytotoxicity observed in SLE patients may not necessarily correlate with the ability to release lytic granules, as indicated by CD107a expression [30]. However, in our present study, we are the first to demonstrate a defect in CD107a degranulation in SLE CIML NK cells, although this defect could be partially compensated for with re-stimulation. Additionally, the K562 NK cytotoxicity assay unveiled a significant impairment in killing capacity among SLE CIML NK cells, which remained unimproved even after re-stimulation. Therefore, the decrease in cytolytic granule release, as well as reduced production of granzyme B and perforin altogether, contributed to impaired cytotoxicity toward K562 cells even after re-stimulation.

To investigate the clinical relevance of CIML NK cell deficiencies, we analyzed correlations between CIML NK cell parameters and SLEDAI scores in SLE patients. No significant correlations were observed between the percentage of CD3-CD56+ CIML NK cells, IFN-γ expression, granzyme B expression, CD107a degranulation, or K562 cytotoxicity and SLEDAI scores (p > 0.05). Future studies should incorporate these metrics to comprehensively assess the role of CIML NK cells in SLE pathogenesis and their potential as biomarkers for disease progression and therapeutic efficacy.

Due to their potent anti-tumor effect, CIML NK cells have increasingly been utilized in clinical trials aimed at combating certain hematological malignancies [31]. Defective CIML NK cell function may predispose SLE patients to malignancy. Several reports have shown increased risk of leukemia and lymphoma in SLE patients [32, 33]. The role of CIML NK cells in the pathogenesis of SLE remains to be determined.

Taken together, our findings suggest that IL-15 can effectively maintain cytotoxic function and cytokine production of NK cells in SLE patients. Although CIML NK cells from SLE patients still demonstrated memory-like properties, they exhibited deficiencies compared to those from healthy individuals. The dysregulated response of CIML NK cells to cytokines stimulation may indeed contribute to the pathogenesis of SLE, as well as impair the killing of tumor and virus-infected cells.

While our ex vivo findings demonstrate that IL-15 can partially restore the quantity and function of CIML NK cells in SLE patients, the clinical impact of these changes on disease outcomes remains to be explored. Future in vivo studies, such as animal models of SLE or clinical trials involving IL-15-based therapies, are warranted to determine whether enhancing CIML NK cell function can be beneficial in SLE patients.

Conclusions

Our findings underscore the pivotal role of SLE CIML NK cells in immune regulation. We discovered that IL-15 effectively preserves the cytotoxic function and cytokine production of NK cells in SLE patients. However, our investigation unveiled phenotypic disparities and functional impairments within SLE CIML NK cells. Despite retaining memory-like properties, CIML NK cells from SLE patients exhibited deficiencies compared to those from healthy individuals. These insights highlight the critical need for further exploration into the unique contributions of SLE CIML NK cells to antiviral and antitumor responses.
