Barriers and facilitators to practitioner prescribing and patient adoption of anti-inflammatory maintenance and reliever therapy (MART) for adult asthma: a qualitative study
Authors: Judith Dyson, Helena Cummings, Gemma Williams, Vieshal RajaGopal, Michael Watt, Tamsin Morris, Michael G. Crooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma was traditionally treated with separate corticosteroid (ICS) containing (‘preventer’) and short-acting beta-agonist (SABA; ‘reliever’) inhalers. But overuse of SABA-reliever inhalers is common and associated with poor outcomes. Clinical guidelines increasingly recommend using combined ICS and formoterol (ICS-formoterol) as an anti-inflammatory reliever. While ICS-formoterol use as a reliever only, without maintenance doses, has only recently been licenced in the United Kingdom; use as both preventer and reliever (Maintenance and Reliever Therapy; MART) has been recommended for many years. Despite the evidenced benefits of this approach, MART uptake in the UK has been low.MethodsWe studied the barriers and facilitators to primary care practitioners’ prescribing and adult asthma patients’ adoption of MART. We recruited practitioners and patients from a network of general practices that had participated in the SENTINEL programme to address SABA over-use and support MART adoption. Semi-structured, theoretically underpinned interviews were conducted with both groups. Data analysis involved deductive coding to the theoretical domains framework (a comprehensive framework including all potential determinants of practitioner and health behaviours) followed by inductive coding within domains.ResultsFourteen themes were identified, with novel findings. Facilitators were practitioner knowledge and skills, and practice prescribing and appointment systems. Practitioner barriers mostly related to available resources, changes to treatment following emergency hospital presentations, and managing patient barriers to MART adoption and maintenance. Patient barriers included: psychological dependence on SABA-relievers and associated fear of discontinuation lack of understanding of the rationale for change to MART; and reluctance to change caused by concerns about difficulty getting an appointment if their asthma control deteriorated. Some practitioner barriers reported by previous studies (e.g., guideline knowledge and asthma management skills) were identified as facilitators in our study, potentially due to SENTINEL Programme participation.ConclusionsThe Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) endorse ICS-formoterol as the preferred reliever in asthma, making it vital to understand barriers and facilitators to clinician and patient adoption in practice. We identified unique and previously unreported barriers and facilitators for both groups with potential to inform strategies to support practitioner and patient concordance with anti-inflammatory reliever-based asthma treatment and implementation of evidence-based, guideline-recommended care.

Background

Asthma is an inflammatory condition affecting the lung airways and is characterised by variable symptoms of breathlessness, cough, wheeze, chest tightness, and associated airflow limitation [1]. Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) containing inhalers, sometimes referred to as ‘preventers’, are the cornerstone of asthma treatment [2]. However, short-acting beta-agonist (SABA) inhalers, traditionally given to provide short-term symptom relief (known as ‘relievers’), are often favoured by patients despite having no anti-inflammatory properties. SABA over-use, defined as using 3 or more SABA canisters per year, is common in the UK [3] and is associated with an increased risk of asthma exacerbations [4] and asthma-related deaths [5]. Additionally, uncontrolled asthma has significantly higher greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions than controlled asthma, with the majority of GHG emissions directly attributed to SABA [6]. Therefore, identifying and addressing SABA over-use in asthma offers benefits for both patient outcomes and environmental sustainability.

In 2019, the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) [1] recommended a new treatment paradigm in asthma, naming anti-inflammatory, ICS-formoterol combination inhalers as the preferred relievers in asthma. Formoterol is a long-acting beta-agonist (LABA) with an onset of action comparable to SABA, providing rapid and effective symptom relief. The ICS component targets inflammation to achieve asthma control. When used as a reliever, additional ICS doses effectively address heightened inflammation during symptomatic episodes [1].

In mild asthma, ICS-formoterol use only as-needed significantly reduces the risk of asthma exacerbations compared with SABA-only treatment. In moderate-severe asthma, regular maintenance ICS-formoterol with additional anti-inflammatory reliever-use (known as maintenance and reliever therapy; MART) is associated with reduced exacerbation risk compared with ICS-LABA preventer with separate SABA-reliever regimens [7]. While the use of ICS-formoterol only as-needed in mild asthma has only been licenced in the UK since 2023, its use as MART has been available since 2007. Despite guideline endorsement, uptake has historically been poor [8] with complacency and lack of recognition of the dangers associated with SABA over-use likely contributing to its low uptake [9].

In 2020, a health authority in the North of England recognised high levels of SABA over-use among their local population and associated adverse clinical outcomes, leading them to roll out a co-designed implementation intervention, “SABA rEductioN Through ImplemeNting asthma guidELines” (SENTINEL). [10] This programme supported implementation of a local guideline that endorsed MART as the preferred approach for treating adult asthma patients uncontrolled on ICS monotherapy.

SENTINEL supported implementation of a prescribing guideline that recommended low-dose ICS plus SABA reliever in mild asthma, escalating to MART for those uncontrolled on this regime. This was because as-needed ICS-formoterol was not licensed in the UK for use in mild asthma at the time. The guideline offered clear advice about when to escalate (or deescalate) treatment, including when to consider specialist referral, and the importance of assessing and supporting inhaler technique and adherence. Implementation was supported across 6 Primary Care Networks (PCNs; groups of general practices including multidisciplinary clinicians) using a stepped-wedge design, with all PCN’s having commenced implementation by November 2021. The co-designed SENTINEL intervention included (full description of the development and content [10] and the early evaluation of this implementation intervention are published elsewhere [8]):i)Healthcare Professional Education (topics covered included: the nature of asthma and its burden, diagnosis, the dangers of SABA over-use, treatment guidelines and how to implement them, how to implement prescribing gold standards, communication with asthma patients, when to refer and Personalised Asthma Action Plans);ii)implementation of ‘gold standard’ prescribing practices (5 key principles that were defined through co-design with the aim of supporting good quality asthma care and addressing SABA over-use);iii)Targeted Asthma Reviews for patients, prioritised based on SABA over-use;iv)Patient Support and Education (topics included: what is asthma and how is it treated, SABA inhalers and the dangers of over-use, MART, and ‘frequently asked questions); andv)Data monitoring and feedback (to practices).

Data from the pilot PCN revealed a marked reduction in SABA prescribing in the year following implementation, with fewer asthma patients experiencing exacerbations [8]. While the SENTINEL intervention was co-designed to address known barriers to implementation of guideline recommended asthma care, little is known about specific barriers and facilitators to MART adoption among high-SABA users, MART persistence after initial adoption, and how programmes such as SENTINEL can help to address these. In line with guidelines for the development of complex interventions (defined as an intervention with several interacting components, such as the SENTINEL intervention components) [10, 11], these learnings could facilitate timely modification of SENTINEL and enhance intervention impact as it is scaled. They also have significant potential to inform implementation strategies to broaden anti-inflammatory reliever use in asthma, as recommended by GINA.

Design

Interventions to support optimal behaviours are more effective when they are theoretically underpinned [12, 13] and tailored [14] according to assessed barriers [15] to practice (in this case MART prescribing and persistence) or health (in this case MART use and persistence) behaviours. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [15] is a synthesis of all published models of behaviour or behaviour change and as such offers a comprehensive set of potential determinants (environmental, social, cultural, institutional and individual) to understand and change both health [16] and practitioner [17] behaviours. Once determinants to adoption are identified and categorised to the 11 TDF domains (knowledge, skills, social/professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, beliefs about consequences, motivation and goals, memory attention and decision processes, environmental context and resources, social influences, emotion and action planning), it offers a pragmatic way to select the behaviour change techniques (BCTs) likeliest to be effective in underpinning interventions to positively modify the specified behaviours [18]. Theoretically informed interviews are likely to mitigate the biases associated with how people explain their behaviour [17].

A qualitative exploratory approach using semi-structured interviews used two interview schedules (patients and practitioners) underpinned by the TDF, the available literature and data from SENTINEL co-design workshops.

Participants

Participants were recruited from PCNs that had adopted the SENTINEL intervention within two sub-integrated care board (ICB) health regions. Patient participants were adults with asthma and efforts were made to identify both those that i) transitioned to MART and continued to use it, or ii) initially transitioned to MART before returning to use a SABA containing regime. Beyond these variables, we sought to recruit a maximum variation group according to gender, age, asthma symptom severity, education, and deprivation status (Index of Multiple Deprivation [IMD]). It was expected that patients would have a range of co-morbidities and health behaviours (e.g., smoking). Health care practitioner participants were a maximum variation (according to role and experience) group who were responsible for the review of patients with Asthma. We expected maximum sample sizes of 15 patients and 15 practitioners would be sufficient to achieve data saturation given the theoretical nature of the interviews [19] to support transferability of our findings.

Procedure

Clinical teams identified patient participants according to inclusion criteria, provided them with verbal information and invited participation. Those interested were asked permission for a member of the research team to contact them, whereupon further verbal and written information was given, consent taken, and an appointment made for interview. Potential practitioner participants were contacted by email and asked to contact a member of the research team if interested, whereupon they were given additional information and consent was taken. Participants were offered interviews via Microsoft Teams (MST) or telephone. Consent was confirmed at the beginning of interviews which were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviews were conducted by JD (PhD, female, senior researcher and chartered psychologist, non-respiratory clinician with no prior knowledge of participants).

Analysis

Data were professionally transcribed and analysed within NVivo v.12 and analysed according to practitioner and patient barriers and facilitators, first deductively to the domains of the TDF and subsequently inductively within domains. Data were coded by one main coder (JD) but, to increase reliability of analysis, independent coding of six transcripts (GW) took place and a convergent coding framework agreed. Analysis was conducted concurrent to interviews and data saturation defined by three interviews in each group whereby no new themes were identified. No new ideas were identified in practitioner data after participant 8 and in patient data after participant 10. Patient and practitioner data were combined as the synthesised whole offered more insights than each part alone.

Practitioner knowledge

Practitioner asthma care knowledge was good with all able to outline the guidelines for asthma care, both generally and in relation to prescribing. Many acknowledged SENTINEL for improving knowledge in addition to other sources such as guidelines and informal talks. Some attended formal education (e.g., asthma diploma, spirometry, supplementary prescribing) but there was little consistency in what formal training outside SENTINEL was accessible: “sometimes the learning can be, you know, very much on the job and, and learning from peers, which isn’t always ideal” (Lucy, ANP). Some participants acknowledged that those less frequently involved in asthma care were subject to knowledge and skills decay, particularly GPs who often saw patients with acute asthma needs but rarely engaged in routine reviews: “we just don’t see those patients as much unless it’s the acute exacerbations” (Jo, GP).

Practitioner physical care skills

Practitioners were confident in their physical care skills and referred to wide ranging support they offered to patients including demonstrating inhaler technique and conducting peak flow tests. About half considered themselves competent with spirometry and FeNO (Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide) testing. Physical care skills challenges included GPs becoming deskilled through lack of exposure to routine asthma care and familiarity with the range of inhaler devices: “None of us are familiar with [all] inhaler devices and telling them [patients] how to use them... we all struggle” (Jo, GP).

Patients’ engagement and concordance

Although most patient participants in our study reported attending routine appointments and adopted treatment plans, non-attendance was one of the most frequently cited barriers for practitioners. This was managed through insisting on appointments before renewing a prescription. Most patients attended when asked and adopted practitioners’ treatment recommendations, but some only attended reviews to ensure they received their medication and others avoided appointments completely: “I don’t go. I get text messages... I’m like no” (Max, patient). When pushed for a rationale for not attending, patients said they did not think practitioners listened to them. Practitioners said patients expressed reluctance when they initially suggested MART: “I’d say about seventy percent are (sigh) their reluctant at first” (Anna, ANP) but most went on to switch. Most practitioners had experienced an encounter with someone who became angry when a switch to MART was suggested, particularly when denied a SABA-reliever inhaler: “Some of them shout at me ‘You can’t take my blue inhaler away’ and they’re not willing to listen” (Anita, PN). One patient participant reported feeling angry toward practitioners. Practitioners said if they did not give a blue inhaler, patients would sometimes get them elsewhere. Sources included other practitioners: “so they’ll know, ‘oh don’t go to Dr X, I know she’s not gonna entertain me, but if I go to so-and-so..’” (Nic, GP); other countries: “they’ll go on holiday and buy loads of it; maybe eight, nine, ten Salbutamol inhalers in Spain” (Carol, PN); or relatives: “I care for my mum... COPD... I just go and get hers” (Jill, patient).

Practitioner diagnosis, decision making and habit

Some practitioner participants found ambiguous or unconfirmed asthma diagnoses a barrier to appropriate care. Participants talked about patients smoking and possibly having Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), having post Covid-19 symptoms, or being misdiagnosed in childhood: “end up on the asthma register that haven’t had any objective testing... overusing blue inhalers” (Lucy, ANP). Practitioners suggested tests such as FeNO and spirometry to confirm or dismiss asthma, but these were not always available. Most practitioner’s described challenges in breaking prescribing habits, many of whom had succeeded with a minority continuing to struggle: “old habits die hard, you carry on doing what works” (Pat, GP).

Prescribing systems and routine appointments

Electronic systems were a facilitator, prompting appointments when prescription ordering suggested patients’ asthma was uncontrolled. If a patient ordered more prescriptions than expected, they were called in for a check-up. Some practices did not include SABA inhalers on repeat prescription. Some practices were firmer than others with appointments before prescriptions, the firmest approach being: “stop their inhalers or kick them off your books” (Dora, PN). Practitioners recognised that these systems could be distressing for patients, but most patients accepted them. The problem they reported was getting non-routine appointments in an emergency: “they chase me up, for an asthma review, but, if I was to try and get an appointment... that would be impossible” (Charlie, patient).

Practitioner resource needs

During Covid-19, respiratory tests ceased, causing a backlog. There were also problems with equipment availability and use, for example, peak flow meters, and placebo devices that are used by practitioners to support patients with their inhaler technique. Where equipment was available, costs of consumables sometimes led to use being rationed: “the consumables for FeNO; so we are being very, very kinda tight on how we and when we use FeNO” (Sam, GP). Some practitioners perceived a reluctance to engage with spirometry as this was not a service GPs were funded to provide.

Staffing levels, time and priorities

Having sufficient, qualified and experienced nursing staff was sometimes a problem; where there were sufficient nursing staff, not all were qualified to deliver the full spectrum of asthma care: “we only have two nurses that are qualified to do spirometry to a thirty-six thousand patient population” (Jo, GP). Staff turnover led to untrained and less experienced staff. GPs in particular said having enough time to provide sufficient information/education to support a change in treatment was challenging: “Switching from a preventor [and] reliever to MART therapy is time consuming” (Nic, GP). Patients recognised practitioners were busy, and although this limited access to acute appointments, they generally did not consider that their routine appointments were compromised.

Professional roles, the influence of others and teamwork

Most participants agreed that asthma care and prescribing was usually consistent within their practice. Nurses, who were responsible for routine asthma appointments, were not always prescribers which sometimes caused tension, but GPs usually adopted the nurse’s advice: “when they need a prescription, the asthma nurses are very up to date with the current guidance” (Nic, GP). The more frequently reported barrier related to patients being seen as emergencies in secondary care: “somebody goes to A&E without a diagnosis of asthma, and they can come back without any test being done and... they’re on a [inhaler]” (Lucy, ANP). This was not only the case with the emergency department but also reported patients admitted to acute medical assessment units.

Practitioners valued a receptionist who knew when to deny a repeat prescription and offer an appointment, but recognised support was needed. Patients were less positive and did not think the receptionist had the clinical or communication skills needed to triage care. Bill said: “She said, ‘only if you’re at high risk’. I says, ‘well I am classed as high risk”... could I, hellfire, get an appointment... the doctor says, ‘no, she shouldn’t have said that’”. When GP practices had a pharmacist, the role was highly valued and included identifying patients needing additional care, telephoning and advising patients who did not attend, and conducting consultations face to face: “the person [patient] who gains the most is the person who sits with the [name] pharmacist” (David, GP).

SENTINEL related

Most practitioners said they knew about SENTINEL, were familiar with the gold standard prescribing principles, and had accessed the training resources and found them useful. The specialist knowledge gained during the project was sometimes held by specific people within the practice: “I’ve only really recently looked, ‘cause I think the ACPs took the lead on the SENTINEL” (Carol, PN). This was a problem when staff left: “how many of the newer staff know about SENTINEL? I’m not confident that they do” (Nic, GP). Patients did not mention the SENTINEL project, but most recalled being involved in conversations about switching their current treatment to MART therapy. Practitioners tended to be less aware of SENTINEL resources for patients (e.g., information leaflets and videos).

Patient’s low health expectations

When asked to reflect on the reasons patients did not want to engage in routine appointments or MART therapy, some practitioners said they had low asthma health expectations: “Patients tell me they get short of breath... and they say, you know ‘well I’ve got asthma, that’s what happens with asthma’ and [I] have to say ‘well actually, you know, the aim is for you to have NO symptoms’” (Sereena, PN). Although patients did not explicitly express low health expectations as a barrier, it was clear they believed some degree of breathlessness or wheeze normal: “it’s not debilitating but I do wheeze during the week” (Jenny, patient).

Habit or psychological dependence

The most frequently cited barrier for patients was a perceived dependence or concern about omitting their SABA-reliever inhaler: “I was petrified, because my blue one, I was told that that prescription had stopped. So, all my life I’ve just used the blue one as like my reliever to help my symptoms” (Karen, patient). Many said that this anxiety was also related to being unable to get a doctors’ appointment if things went wrong: “I was really concerned, what if I can’t get an appointment and I need a blue inhaler... I was really nervous” (Karen, patient). Nearly all practitioners perceived patient dependence on the reliever inhaler: “patients are very worried about you taking away their blue inhaler... their crutch” (Alex, pharmacist) and “comfort blanket of a blue inhaler” (Sereena, PN).

MART as a reliever

Some patients perceived that MART took longer to relieve their symptoms: “if I just had a couple of puffs of [blue reliever-inhaler] it would have gone. It’s not instant the [MART]” (Jenny, patient). Sometimes this was attributed to differences in inhalation technique required when using a dry powder MART inhaler (DPI) compared with a pressurised, metered dose, SABA-reliever inhaler (pMDI): “if I am actually having an asthma attack, I can’t inhale it properly... you’ve got to really like suck it in” (Jill, patient). Practitioners recognised perceived differences in relief onset as a problem in some patients, for example, “they feel that the combined inhaler is not as effective at relieving the symptoms that they do ask to have the blue inhaler back” (Sereena, PN). Some practitioners also believed MART took longer to relieve, for example, “what they’re used to, especially when it comes to using short-acting agents, it provides instant relief” (Pat, GP) and “if it takes that long for MART to kick in as a reliever... I can see why people do panic” (Carol, PN). Others cited evidence to the contrary, recognising that this was a misheld belief: “research has been done and it’s been shown to you know, open the airways and be as effective as a reliever” (Sereena, PN).

Rationale for switch to MART unknown

Many patients did not know why they had been changed to MART. The minority who did, explained it using phrases such as: “I wasn’t blowing the number what she was wanting” (Bill, patient). Others were not able to say why, or they offered guesses for reasons, such as: “I assume because it was new. I don’t know... or maybe a supply issue or something” (Karen, patient) and “no real explanation of why they changed it” (Jim, patient).

Addressing patient barriers

Information given

The main strategy practitioners used to improve adoption of their treatment plan, was to provide information: “the key is the patient education” (Sam, GP). According to practitioners, information included how the lungs work and the impact of medicines on asthma. When asked how they explained the shift to MART, the answers were consistently about benefits versus risks: “Discuss the benefits … the risks of not changing [to MART]” (Sereena, PN). This was sometimes phrased as a warning: “I often say ‘I don’t want to be responsible for you…..” (Nic, GP). Practitioners expressed surprise or frustration when information alone was ineffective in supporting patients to adopt or maintain MART. When information was ineffective, practitioners either continued to give further information or they gave up: “it’s a case of choosing yer battles, it’s, I tend to just say ‘fine... put ‘em back on [it]” (Liz, PN).

Information heard

Patient participants remembered receiving information about health behaviours such as smoking, alcohol consumption, weight and exercise: “they ask you ‘how much do you drink? How much do you smoke’” (Karen, patient). When prompted, some remembered having received information about conducting peak flow tests or using an inhaler device. Patients often sought information from the internet, family and friends: “I know the websites that are legitimate” (Louise, patient). Patient participants were able to speak confidently about their own asthma, what triggered it and when and how to take their medicines.

Partnership working, trust and communication

Some practitioners said partnership working with patients would support medicines concordance and they used terms such as “patient empowerment” (Nic, GP) or “build that relationship and trust” (Alex, pharmacist). When asked about this process, practitioners reiterated the need to give or repeat information: “don’t treat it as a one-off exercise... consistently feeding that message” (Nic, GP). Patient participants talked about trust and sometimes this was enough for them to accept a change in treatment without fully understanding the rationale: “I just trusted them, you know” (Jack, patient). Some patients did not believe medication change was in their best interest: “I honestly thought they’d changed me... purely on a cost basis’’ (Jim, patient). Practitioners realised patients sometimes felt this way. Most patients were happy with their consultations: “lovely” (Rose, patient), but a minority found communication a problem and this impacted trust. Georgina (patient) said: “what upset me … I got a letter, oh we’re changing you to this... just changed it and then when I went for it, I couldn’t use it”. Others felt patronised: “why do you feel you know my body better than I do … they’re condescending” (Max, patient).

A short-term SABA to support change

Both practitioners and patients found having a SABA-reliever inhaler during the transition to MART was often enough to give the patient confidence to transition to an anti-inflammatory reliever and prepare to be SABA-free. One practitioner said, “we agree that they can have a blue inhaler as a backup … comfort blanket …” (Dora, PN). Patients had the same idea: “make sure you’ve got a back-up of the Salbutamol, just in case” (Karen, patient). There was some evidence that this approach worked, and patients went on to embrace MART and stop their SABA-reliever inhaler: “I’d ordered them just in-case I needed them, which I didn’t” (Rose, Patient). Rose went on to say, her problem was confidence in MART as a reliever, once she tried, it was “a life changer”.

Acute and “in case” appointments

A common experience among patient participants was difficulty securing GP appointments when they had a problem: “you are 76 in the queue... it stresses me out so much” (Max, patient). This had led to them being reluctant to change medicines, in case they did not work, and no appointment was available. Several patients understood that chest problems did not always require antibiotics but did not understand why they were not given appointments for other forms of treatment. Some suggested a rapid, pre-booked follow up appointment after a switch to MART, as a safety net: “I would say to them, try it for maybe a month... but it would be a better thing if they had a follow up.”

Discussion

Interviews with practitioners and patients revealed that barriers and facilitators to MART adoption in the context of SABA over-use were interwoven and largely inseparable from those of SABA discontinuation. Practitioner facilitators were knowledge, clinical skills, clarity of professional roles, and embedding changes within practice systems. The SENTINEL intervention was identified as a facilitator in its own right and, through provision of healthcare professional education about asthma care and system change, positively leveraged other identified facilitators. Identified barriers included staffing levels, time, competing priorities, and managing patient engagement and treatment concordance.

While some identified patient barriers have previously been described (e.g., poor asthma health expectations) [21], we identified numerous novel patient barriers (e.g., lack of clarity on rationale for MART, a misperception that MART takes longer than SABA to relieve symptoms, and worry about obtaining timely access for review in the event that treatment change had adverse effects). Some patient barriers appeared to contribute to practitioner barriers; for example, despite most practitioners’ being aware that evidence shows that ICS-formoterol provides comparable symptom relief to SABA [22, 23], practitioner beliefs were susceptible to being informed by some patients’ perception that the onset of relief was slower, creating a barrier to prescribing. Cognitive biases are well reported in health decision making, where people are more convinced by what they experience (their subjective social reality) or recent encounters rather than what research evidence presents [24]. Recognising this and helping both clinicians and patients to understand their own cognitive biases and adopt strategies to overcome this may address this barrier.

Few previous studies have considered barriers and facilitators for both practitioner prescribing and patient adoption of MART. But, where individually studied, reported barriers generally concur with our findings, (e.g., lack of time [25] and challenges with patient engagement [21]). Some previously reported practitioner barriers were identified as facilitators by our participants, e.g., guideline knowledge [26, 27] and asthma management skills [28]. This may be attributed to the SENTINEL intervention, which includes clinician education relating to asthma guideline implementation, the dangers of SABA over-use, and rationale for MART [10] Although some interviewed staff had not been directly involved in SENTINEL or interacted with the intervention, most had.

Despite the identified barriers, most patient participants successfully transitioned to MART and appropriately discontinued SABA-reliever use, with the support of their clinicians. A good therapeutic relationship between patient and empowered practitioners was key in facilitating both MART adoption and persistence. Sustainability strategies included within SENTINEL, such as monitoring and feedback of prescribing practices, and practice system changes to enable SABA over-use to be identified and addressed [10] also contributed to successful MART adoption and persistence.

Most practitioners were able to talk articulately about patient barriers to treatment adoption and had deployed strategies to address these. This was particularly important for those that had developed a psychological dependence on their SABA-reliever and expressed fear relating to its discontinuation. In such circumstances, a therapeutic partnership formed between practitioner and patient through effective trust building, and use of practical solutions in some cases, helped to overcome barriers. One practical solution identified as helpful was short-term co-prescription of a SABA-reliever (e.g., up to 3-months) when initially switching to MART. This appeared to provide a degree of reassurance to those fearful of SABA discontinuation, facilitating successful MART adoption and subsequent SABA discontinuation. Use of the reliever reliance test [29] may offer patients further insight into their reliance, and is included in the SENTINEL educational materials, however, our data suggest patients generally had good insight into their dependence and no participants reported use of this test.

Recognising and adequately addressing patient barriers was important and where barriers were not adequately addressed, some patients reported accessing SABA inhalers through other means (e.g., purchasing SABA over the counter when on holiday in other countries or obtaining unused SABA from relatives who were prescribed SABA for their own use). Such hidden SABA-use risks persistent, undetected over-use and associated risks.

Practitioners sometimes believed that information alone would address patient barriers, irrespective of what these were. However, repeated provision of information without practical support contributed to some patient’s perception of being patronised, negatively impacting the therapeutic relationship and shared trust. The SENTINEL intervention includes patient facing resources and offers practical solutions to facilitate MART adoption and persistence, but awareness of this component of the intervention was lower among practitioners than other intervention components. Greater awareness and utilisation of patient facing resources and practical solutions to identified barriers could help support successful MART adoption and persistence.

SENTINEL recognises a role for all clinical and non-clinical members of the primary care team to identify and address SABA over-use in asthma and therefore advocates for all team members to access the healthcare professional education resources. However, practitioner participants identified that unexplained medication changes following hospital attendances during asthma emergencies were a barrier to MART persistence. A system-wide approach that includes other healthcare providers, including emergency and unscheduled care services, community pharmacies and other specialist services is therefore important.

We observed an apparent disconnect between the information practitioners believed that they offered their patients and what patients believed that they had received. The former reported information about the lungs, effects of medicines and the need for adherence with anti-inflammatory therapies; the latter heard information about health behaviours relating to alcohol, smoking and diet. These differences did not appear to impact treatment adherence, but a lack of understanding of the rationale for treatment change was identified as a barrier to MART adoption and persistence. Most patients did not know the reason for the change in their treatment and very few practitioners explicitly reported explaining this to their patients. Once again, this may reflect underutilisation of patient facing resources developed to support clinicians to communicate and enable understanding of the dangers of SABA over-use and the rationale for change to an anti-inflammatory reliever strategy (ICS-formoterol MART).

The SENTINEL intervention promotes the use of electronic systems to identify SABA over-reliance, education, skills training, prescribing principles, and monitoring of prescribing practice. From our data, this positively impacted practitioner knowledge and skills barriers and went some way to address patient engagement with appointments. Practitioner challenges not directly addressed relate to lack of time and resources, for example, FeNO and spirometry testing (impacting the reliability of practice asthma registers). Addressing more complex resource challenges such as unrestricted access to FeNO and spirometry requires recognition of the value of these tools for diagnosis and management to patient and clinicians by healthcare commissioners/funders. Alternative approaches such as the use of serial peak flow readings may be an option where spirometry is not available [30].

Practitioners may also benefit from an understanding of the range of determinants to common asthma related health behaviours and strategies to address these. For example, mis-held beliefs may be more effectively addressed through self-monitoring (e.g., symptom frequency, time from symptom onset to relief after inhaler use) and feedback (e.g., changes in peak expiratory flow rate or FeNO) than information alone [18]. Similarly, persuasive communication (use of testimonials, personal stories, social proof, visual data) may impact patient’s appointment and medication concordance rather than reiteration of information [18]. Implementation of these strategies should be considered as an addition to SENTINEL and other programmes aiming to support anti-inflammatory reliever approaches in asthma.

This study is the first to explore both practitioner and patient barriers and facilitators to MART adoption and persistence. By recruiting participants from a region taking part in the SENTINEL Project, we gained valuable insights into: i) barriers and facilitators to successful MART adoption among asthma patients with a history of SABA over-use; ii) how SENTINEL intervention components supported successful transition; and iii) remaining barriers and how these may be overcome. The robust study conduct and inclusion of both patients and practitioners, including patients who generally did not attend routine asthma appointments, are strengths of this study. Recruitment from a single health authority region within a nationalised health system that is free at the point of care may limit transferability to other health systems. However, we believed that our findings have important implications for those aiming to implement anti-inflammatory reliever approaches to asthma care, as endorsed by GINA. Adopting a whole system approach, ensuring utilisation of both practitioner and patient facing resources, facilitating positive practitioner-patient relationships, and providing timely access to advice and support following treatment changes has potential to enhance the impact of SENTINEL and similar interventions.

We recommend future research focuses on designing and testing implementation interventions that are theoretically underpinned [10, 15] and tailored [14] to address barriers and enhance facilitators. We support a co-design approach [31] involving all stakeholders (including service providers and users) [32] to ensure implementation interventions are acceptable, practicable, equitable (accessible by all), affordable, safe and effective (APEASE) [33].

Conclusion

Interviews with practitioners and patients about barriers and facilitators to both practitioner prescription and patient adoption of MART identified unique and previously unreported barriers for both groups. Barriers reported elsewhere (e.g., knowledge and skills related) were identified as facilitators in our study, possibly due to participation in SENTINEL. Our findings have potential to inform and maximise the impact of strategies to support practitioner and patient concordance with anti-inflammatory reliever-based asthma treatment and improve delivery and receipt of evidence-based, guideline-recommended care.
