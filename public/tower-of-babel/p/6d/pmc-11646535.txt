Guideline Updates Needed: Closure Devices to Prevent Recurrent Stroke in Patients With Patent Foramen Ovale
Authors: Jeffrey L. Saver
Published: 2024

Updating clinical practice guidelines when new evidence emerges is essential to best patient care. Guidelines regarding guideline generation generally recommend that comprehensive guidelines for a disease state be updated every 3 to 5 years and focused interim updates be issued more rapidly when important new data has emerged since the writing of the most recent full guideline document. 1 , 2 , 3 An international consensus group has identified 5 general criteria that indicate a guideline needs urgent, high‐priority updating: (1) the outdated recommendation is likely to result in harm to patients; (2) authoritative new evidence has emerged; (3) the clinical question is still relevant to current practice; (4) the clinical question can still be soundly addressed methodologically; and (5) there is evidence of continued interest in the management issue by patients, providers, and other stakeholders. 4 Based on meeting each of these 5 criteria, the leading US guidelines on prevention of recurrent patent foramen ovale (PFO)‐associated stroke all need urgent updating, including those of the Society of Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI), 5 the American Academy of Neurology (AAN), 6 and the Stroke Council of the American Heart Association (SC AHA). 7

The need for guideline revision was precipitated by the publication of the primary results of the pooled, individual participant–level data (IPD) meta‐analysis of all 6 completed randomized trials of PFO closure versus medical management for secondary stroke prevention. 8 The pooled analysis was published in December 2021, after the literature search cutoff dates for each of the current guidelines: AAN (August 2019), SC AHA (June 2020), and SCAI (May 2021). While multiple study‐level meta‐analyses of these trials had previously been published, IPD meta‐analyses can provide unique insights distinguishing treatment responders and nonresponders not achievable with study‐level investigations. 9 , 10 , 11 For any particular single patient feature, IPD meta‐analysis can characterize the magnitude of effect modification more accurately because it enables time‐to‐event assessments. Study‐level meta‐analyses' use of average values or aggregate proportions of patient characteristics can commonly lead to the ecological bias and underestimation of the influence of single patient features on outcomes. Even more critically, IPD meta‐analyses can characterize patient responses on the basis of clusters of multiple variables while study‐level meta‐analyses can only explore subgroups defined by 1 variable at a time. Considering the combined effect of multiple individual patient features identifies patients with particularly distinctive treatment responses and enables individualization of treatment plans. To implement patient‐centered, evidence‐based medicine, it is essential that guidelines draw upon the results of well‐performed and adequately powered IPD meta‐analyses when they become available. 11 , 12

The IPD meta‐analysis of PFO closure devices was undertaken collaboratively by the leaders of the six completed randomized trials and encompassed 3740 patients, predominantly between ages 18 to 60, with PFO and no other major defined cause of their index ischemic stroke. To identify within this population patients with potential differential response to treatment, the Systematic, Collaborative, PFO Closure Evaluation Consortium assessed 2 prespecified patient classification systems, both derived in data sets preceding and external to trial data: (1) the Risk of Paradoxical Embolism Score 13 ; and (2) the PFO‐Associated Stroke Causal Likelihood (PASCAL) Classification System. 14 The Risk of Paradoxical Embolism Score is a 0‐to‐10 scale that quantifies the absence of features that increase likelihood of an occult, non‐PFO stroke mechanism: older age; nonembolic stroke topography; and vascular risk factors of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and tobacco use. The PASCAL Classification System incorporates the Risk of Paradoxical Embolism Score and adds information regarding the presence of cardiac features that increase likelihood of PFO stroke mechanism: large shunt size and presence of an atrial septal aneurysm. When assessed in the pooled trial data set, both classification systems identified differential responses to closure therapy, with the PASCAL system showing greater differentiating power of particular clinical consequence.

The PASCAL Classification System sorts patients into three categories. Patients with both high‐risk PFO features and a low burden of risk factors for a non‐PFO stroke mechanism are classified as having Probable PFO‐associated strokes. Patients with either, but not both, high‐risk PFO features and a low burden of risk factors for a non‐PFO stroke mechanism are classified as having Possible PFO‐associated strokes. Patients with neither high‐risk PFO features nor a low burden of risk factors for a non‐PFO stroke mechanism are classified as having Unlikely PFO‐associated strokes. Among patients enrolled in the randomized clinical trials of PFO closure, the frequencies of each PASCAL subtype were: Probable—37%; Possible—48%; Unlikely—15%.

When prospectively evaluated in the randomized trial data, the PASCAL patient groups showed strong modification of PFO closure treatment efficacy. 8 The more strongly PFO causal likelihood was assessed as present, the greater the efficacy of active intervention in reducing recurrent stroke over 5 years (Figure). Among PASCAL Probable patients, device placement reduced recurrent stroke over 5 years dramatically (relative risk reduction, 90%; absolute risk reduction, 2.6%). Among PASCAL Possible patients, device placement reduced recurrent stroke substantially (relative risk reduction, 62%; absolute risk reduction, 2.8%). In contrast, among PASCAL Unlikely patients, no statistical differences were noted and the point estimate for recurrent stroke was actually higher with device placement (relative risk increase, 14%; absolute risk increase, 0.5%).

A contrasting pattern was seen for the adverse effect of long‐term atrial fibrillation (AF) over 5 years of follow‐up (Figure 1). Early periprocedural AF is often self‐limited and not of major clinical concern. However, new onset of AF beyond the periprocedural period (≥45 days after the procedure) is likely clinically consequential. Such late onset of AF beyond the periprocedural period showed graded increases as the likelihood of PFO stroke causality decreased. 8 Among PASCAL Probable patients, no statistical differences were noted though the point estimate for new‐onset late AF was slightly higher with device placement (absolute increase, 0.8%). Among PASCAL Possible patients, device placement increased new‐onset late AF moderately (absolute increase, 1.5%). In contrast, among PASCAL Unlikely patients, device placement increased new‐onset late AF substantially (absolute increase, 3.6%).

This pattern of escalating benefit and decreasing harm as likelihood of PFO pathogenesis increases accords with pathophysiologic expectations. The more likely the index stroke is due to the PFO, the greater the expected reduction in recurrent stroke by PFO closure. The less likely the index stroke is due to the PFO, the more likely it was due to undiagnosed occult AF that could be exacerbated by device placement.

These findings have important implications for clinical practice. Both PASCAL Probable and PASCAL Possible patients experience net benefit from device placement, with magnitude of recurrent stroke reduction substantially larger than magnitude of long‐term AF increase. However, PASCAL Unlikely patients will experience net harm from device placement: no reduction in recurrent stroke and a substantial increase in long‐term AF.

Accordingly, randomized trial data have validated the PASCAL Classification as a clinical prediction rule that provides guidance on the most appropriate treatment strategy for patients aged 18 to 60 years with a PFO and otherwise cryptogenic stroke. The PASCAL Probable and Possible categories identify the 6 of every 7 patients likely to experience net benefit from PFO closure and the PASCAL Unlikely category identifies the 1 of every 7 patients likely to be experience net harm from PFO closure.

Though this clinical prediction rule is of clear importance for best treatment selection, the PASCAL Classification System is not incorporated in any of the current US clinical practice guidelines. As a result, though appropriate at the time of their composition, the guidelines are now outdated and suboptimal. Interestingly, the guidelines depart from current best evidence in distinct ways that reflect the predispositions of the specialty groups formulating their content 15 (Table). The SCAI interventional cardiology society guidelines are overly aggressive, recommending closure in more patients than now supported by the evidence, including some patients who will experience net harm. 5 The AAN guidelines are overly cautious, recommending closure in fewer patients and with less certainty than now supported by the evidence. 6 The multispecialty SC AHA guidelines fall in between. 7 Where they provide a clear recommendation, they are overly cautious, suggesting closure in fewer patients than now supported by the evidence. In addition, they suggest that weak support exists for closure in additional patients, among whom evidence now shows closure is indicated in some but contraindicated in others. (Many current international guidelines share these liabilities, including the European Stroke Organization guidelines written before the publication of the Systematic, Collaborative, PFO Closure Evaluation IPD meta‐analysis and the Australian guidelines, which reference the meta‐analysis but do not incorporate the PASCAL Classification System in decision‐making recommendations.)

It is universally recognized that clinical practice guidelines should be updated expeditiously when new evidence arises indicating that applying their recommendations would cause patient harm, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 including by the guideline generation procedure manuals of the SCAI, 16 AAN, 17 and SC AHA. 18 However, there is a need for these manuals (and indeed all recently reviewed guideline manuals 3 ) to better specify mechanisms to ensure speedy uptake in guidelines of new authoritative evidence. As a result, all 3 of the major US PFO treatment guidelines now suffer from the liability of containing recommendations that could cause patient harm. Following the SCAI guidelines will lead to a harm of commission, recommending closure in patients in whom treatment is now relatively contraindicated. Following the AAN guidelines will lead to a harm of omission, failing to recommend closure in patients in whom treatment is now indicated. And following the SC AHA guidelines will lead to harms of both commission and omission. Prompt updating of all 3 guidelines is warranted to ensure that patients with ischemic stroke and PFO nationwide receive optimal care.
