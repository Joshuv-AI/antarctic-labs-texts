Patient‐Surgeon Sex Concordance and Clinical Outcomes After Adult Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Lamia Harik, Alexander C. Gregg, Yiyuan Wu, Bjorn Redfors, Sigrid Sandner, Leonard N. Girardi, Mario Gaudino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient–surgeon sex concordance influences postoperative outcomes in general surgery, but its relevance in cardiac surgery is unknown.MethodsThis was a cohort study of 223 065 Medicare beneficiaries undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, surgical aortic valve replacement, and/or proximal aortic surgery from 2010 to 2021, stratified by surgeon and patient sex. The primary and secondary outcomes were composites of mortality and morbidity (myocardial infarction, stroke, and all‐cause readmission) at 30 days and 5 years postoperatively, respectively. Multivariable regression analysis identified independent associations.ResultsThe male surgeon–male patient group comprised 59.9% (133 533/223 065), the female surgeon–male patient group comprised 8.3% (18 623/223 065), the female surgeon–female patient group comprised 3.8% (8466/223 065), and the male surgeon–female patient group comprised 28.0% (62 443/223 065) of the cohort. There was no difference in the incidence of the 30‐day composite outcome between groups and no independent association of sex concordance with the 30‐day composite outcome (odds ratio sex discordance, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.96–1.04]; P=0.84); the interaction between patient sex and sex discordance was not significant (P=0.75). The incidence of the 5‐year composite outcome was higher in the male surgeon–male patient than the female surgeon–male patient group (67.2% [89 795/133 533] versus 65.3% [12 160/18 623]; P<0.0001), and in the male surgeon–female patient than the female surgeon–female patient group (75.7% [47 262/62 443] versus 74.0% [6264/8466]; P=0.0006). There was no association of sex concordance with the 5‐year composite outcome (hazard ratio sex discordance, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.98–1.02]; P=0.69); the interaction between patient sex and sex discordance was not significant (P=0.89).ConclusionsThere was no independent association of patient–surgeon sex concordance with 30‐day or 5‐year outcomes after cardiac surgery.

Patient–surgeon sex concordance has been associated with postoperative outcomes, although available data are sparse 1 , 2 and limited to the general surgery field. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5

In cardiac surgery, there are well‐described sex disparities in postoperative clinical outcomes, 6 which have persisted for decades with minimal change. 6 , 7 , 8 The cause of the worse cardiac surgery outcomes of women compared with men is likely multifactorial, including sex differences in anatomic and biological characteristics, 6 , 8 , 9 intraoperative techniques, 7 , 10 social and economic factors, timing of presentation, severity and acuity of coronary disease, 11 and access to surgical care. It is conceivable that, as in other surgical fields, patient–surgeon sex concordance may also play a role (especially as cardiac surgery is a male‐dominated field), 12 , 13 but this has not been investigated to date. 1

This study represents the first investigation to date of sex concordance in cardiac surgery. The aim of this analysis is to investigate the relationship between patient–surgeon sex concordance and postoperative cardiac surgery outcomes in the United States from 2010 to 2021. We hypothesized that patient–surgeon sex concordance would be associated with better outcomes than sex discordance, and that this would be particularly evident in female patients.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Collection

The present retrospective observational cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Weill Cornell Medicine (Institutional Review Board number 24‐07027675), and the need for individual patient consent was waived. This cohort study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines, 14 and the checklist is provided in Table S1. One author (Y.W.) had complete access to all study data and assumes responsibility for the integrity of the data and the accuracy of the analyses. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Deidentified patient data were obtained through the 20% Medicare fee‐for‐service claims database. The Medicare database is a prospectively maintained national database that includes data from all Medicare payor hospitalizations across the United States.

Population

The Medicare database was queried to identify all patients undergoing isolated coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), isolated surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR), isolated proximal aortic surgery, and any combination of these procedures, from 2010 to 2021. Patients were included if they underwent any of these procedures during the study period. Patients were excluded if they underwent surgery combined with an interventional cardiology procedure (ie, hybrid surgery or revascularization), or if data on provider sex, admission, or discharge were not available. A flowchart of included patients is provided in Figure S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the composite of mortality and morbidity (defined as the composite of all‐cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or all‐cause hospital readmission) within 30 days of surgery. The secondary outcome was the same composite outcome 5 years after surgery. Mortality at 30 days and 5 years was also reported.

Mortality and readmission were identified by querying the Medicare database; myocardial infarction and stroke were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes and/or International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth (ICD‐9; ICD‐10) codes within the Medicare database (Table S1), as in previous publications. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Statistical Analysis

As in previous works on patient–physician sex concordance, 3 , 20 the cohort was stratified by patient–surgeon sex concordance and sex discordance. The sex concordant group included male surgeon–male patient and female surgeon–female patient pairs, whereas the sex discordant group included male surgeon–female patient and female surgeon–male patient pairs.

Baseline demographic continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Anderson‐Darling test. Given the absence of normally distributed variables, continuous variables were presented using medians and interquartile ranges. Standardized mean differences were used to assess the descriptive data, with standardized mean differences <10% considered as reflective of well‐balanced groups. Percentage and frequency counts were used to report categorical variables, and the χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables across groups.

Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate postoperative survival, and the log‐rank test was used to compare survival of different groups. Pairwise log‐rank tests were conducted with Benjamini‐Hochberg adjustment for multiple comparisons.

A logistic regression model, including sex concordance, race, patient age, hypertension, prior myocardial infarction, myocardial infarction at time of admission, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and home oxygen use, diabetes, end‐stage renal disease, prior stroke, connective tissue disease, nonelective surgery (defined as surgery occurring within 72 hours of a nonelective admission), year of surgery (2010–2015 and 2016–2021), patient sex, Medicaid insurance status (Medicare or Medicaid eligible), type of surgery, surgeon age, surgeon volume, and the interaction term of patient sex and sex discordance as covariates, was used to identify variables associated with the 30‐day composite outcome. These estimates were presented as odds ratios (ORs) and their respective 95% CIs.

A Cox regression model including the same covariates was used to identify those variables associated with the 5‐year composite outcome. These estimates were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and their respective 95% CIs.

Similar logistic and Cox regression models with the addition of surgeon sex as a covariate and without sex concordance as a covariate were used to assess the association between surgeon sex and the 30‐day and 5‐year composite outcomes.

The covariates for all the regression models were prespecified and selected on the basis of their clinical relevance.

Missing data were only present for 2 covariates: provider age and Medicaid eligibility. For provider age, indicator variable encoding was used. For Medicaid eligibility, only 9 patients had missing data, and mode imputation was used.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analysis was performed on the basis of procedure type (CABG, SAVR, proximal aortic surgery, and any combined surgery) and surgeon volume.

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding those patients who underwent combined surgery (CABG and SAVR, CABG and proximal aortic surgery, and SAVR and proximal aortic surgery).

All analyses were prespecified. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant without multiplicity adjustment (with the exception of the aforementioned Benjamini‐Hochberg adjustment for the pairwise log‐rank tests). All the analyses were performed using the SAS 9.4 platform and R version 4.4.0.

RESULTS

Preoperative Data

Between January 2010 and December 2021, 223 065 patients underwent CABG, SAVR, proximal aortic surgery, or a combination of these procedures.

Male patients comprised 68.2% (152 156/223 065) of the cohort (male surgeon–male patient: 133 533/223 065 [59.9%] and female surgeon–male patient: 18 623/223 065 [8.3%]), and female patients comprised the remaining 31.8% (female surgeon–female patient: 8466/223 065 [3.8%] and male surgeon–female patient: 62 443/223 065 [28.0%]). Patients operated on by male surgeons comprised 87.9% (195 976/223 065) of the cohort.

Overall, sex concordance was present between 141 999/223 065 (63.7%) patients and their surgeon.

When stratified by sex concordance, there was a higher incidence of the 30‐day composite outcome in the sex discordant compared with the sex concordant group (33.6% [27 253/81 066] versus 28.0% [39 816/141 999]; P<0.001). Operative mortality was also higher in the sex discordant compared with the sex concordant group (4.8% [3913/81 066] versus 3.6% [5136/141 999]; P<0.001) (Table S4).

There was no independent association of sex discordance with the primary outcome (OR for sex discordance, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.96–1.04]; P=0.84), and the interaction term between patient sex and sex discordance was not statistically significant (P=0.75) (Table S5).

Surgeon sex was not associated with the 30‐day composite outcome (OR for female surgeon, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.97–1.04]; P=0.84) (Table S6).

The incidence of the 5‐year composite outcome was 69.7% (155 481/223 065), and the incidence of 5‐year mortality was 10.0% (22 265/223 065).

The incidence of the secondary outcome was significantly higher in the male surgeon–male patient group compared with the female surgeon–male patient group (67.2% [89 795/133 533] versus 65.3% [12 160/18 623]; P<0.0001) and in the male surgeon–female patient group compared with the female surgeon–female patient group (75.7% [47 262/62 443] versus 74.0% [6264/8466]; P = 0.0006) (Table 3).

Consistent with the main analysis, there was no independent association of sex discordance with the primary outcome when excluding patients who underwent combined surgery (OR for sex discordance, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.97–1.05]; P=0.76). DISCUSSION

In this analysis of 223 065 Medicare beneficiaries undergoing cardiac surgery procedures, we found that there was no difference in outcomes in male or female patients who were sex concordant or sex discordant with their surgeons. There was also no association of surgeon sex with 30‐day or 5‐year outcomes.

This is the first study to examine the relationship between sex concordance and clinical outcomes in cardiac surgery. The little existing literature on patient–surgeon sex concordance and clinical outcomes has only been in the field of general surgery, and results have been mixed. 1 , 3 , 5 , 20 , 21 , 22 In the first large‐scale study of the role of sex concordance in surgery, Wallis et al 3 found that in 1.33 million patients in the Ontario database who underwent 1 of 21 common surgeries from 2007 to 2019, patient–surgeon sex discordance was associated with a higher risk of the 30‐day composite outcome of operative mortality, readmission, or procedure‐specific complication (adjusted OR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.04–1.09]). In addition, patient sex was an effect modifier (interaction P=0.004): the male surgeon–female patient group had worse outcomes than the female surgeon–female patient group (adjusted OR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.10–1.20]), but there was no outcome difference between the female surgeon–male patient group and the male surgeon–male patient group (adjusted OR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.95–1.03]). Possible underlying mechanisms for these findings include better outcomes among female physicians and surgeons compared with their male counterparts, 4 , 23 , 24 , 25 female physicians' superior adherence to preventive and guideline‐directed care recommendations, particularly for female patients, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 female physicians' patient‐centered communication, 28 or male physicians' underestimation of female patients' symptoms and risk factors, 1 , 2 , 24 , 29 , 30 among others. On prespecified subgroup analysis by surgical subspecialty, patient–surgeon sex discordance in cardiothoracic surgery (n=98 112; female surgeon–female patient n=1854) was associated with a higher risk of the composite of death, readmission, and complications (adjusted OR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.06–1.13])3; and female patient–surgeon sex concordance in cardiothoracic surgery was associated with a trend toward better outcomes (adjusted OR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.74–0.80]; P=0.08).3 However, the female surgeon–female patient cohort was small, and the reported results may have been attributable to a type 1 error.

A 2023 study from the same authors using the 100% Medicare fee‐for‐service claims data and including >2.9 million patients undergoing 1 of 14 common surgeries from 2016 to 2019 had mixed results. 20 Patient–surgeon sex concordance was associated with a slightly lower operative (30‐day) mortality for female patients (adjusted risk difference, −0.2 percentage points [95% CI, –0.03 to −0.01]; P=0.001), but was associated with a slightly higher operative mortality for male patients (adjusted risk difference, 0.3 [95% CI, 0.2–0.5]; P<0.001) in elective procedures. 20 In nonelective procedures, there was no association of sex concordance with operative mortality. 20 And, in 1 of the first studies of sex concordance and longer‐term outcomes (90‐day and 1‐year outcomes) in 2.2 million patients undergoing 1 of 14 common surgeries from the 100% Medicare fee‐for‐service claims database, 21 patient–surgeon sex concordance was associated with lower incidence of 90‐day and 1‐year readmission and procedure‐specific morbidity in female patients, but there was no association of sex concordance with mortality or morbidity in male patients. 21 Smaller studies of sex concordance and clinical outcomes in surgery also had mixed results. 5 , 22

The present study did not find an association of sex concordance with early or late postoperative outcomes after cardiac surgery in male or female patients. Several factors distinguish cardiac surgery from general surgery that might explain the observed results. When compared with general surgery, cardiac surgery is a more team‐oriented subspecialty. 31 The cardiac anesthesiologist, cardiologist, interventional cardiologist, and perfusionists all play a key role in getting patients to, and through, a cardiac surgery. 31 The surgeon is only 1 operator who is part of a larger team; thus, the effect of the individual surgeon on outcomes may be somewhat mitigated. In fact, the impact of implicit bias and sex discordance between the cardiac anesthesiologist and surgeon on patient outcomes has recently come into question. 32 , 33 Cardiac surgery is a more standardized field than general surgery. This may again mitigate the effect of the individual surgeon on outcomes. Last, the relatively longer training pathway of cardiac surgery may minimize technical skill disparities among surgeons, such that there is less intraoperator variability in cardiac surgery by the time attendinghood is reached. Last, in the prior studies, general surgery morbidity outcomes were disparate for each type of surgery, making it difficult to compare surgical subspecialties to one another and to cardiac surgery, where major adverse cardiovascular events are still widely used to report outcomes across the specialty. 34 , 35

Limitations

The study is retrospective and observational, and therefore is subject to biases and hidden confounders. The cohort is drawn from the Medicare database, which may produce a selection bias. Medicare patients may represent high health care utilization patients, and so the outcome of readmission in particular may be higher in this cohort. Last, the Medicare database does lack depth and granularity in claims coding, which may be a source of error.

The results of the study may be limited by the relatively lower power of this study compared with those in general surgery, 3 , 21 especially as the percentage of female providers in cardiothoracic surgery is low. 1 The small number of female providers in cardiothoracic surgery in this study naturally limits the number of female surgeon–female patient pairs. It was not possible to determine if the primary surgeon was the resident, fellow, or the attending of record, which may have confounded the designation of the patient–surgeon sex concordant pairs and influence patient outcomes. 36

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective cohort study of >200 000 patients based on the Medicare claims data, there was no association of sex concordance with operative (30‐day) or 5‐year mortality and morbidity after cardiac surgery.
