Dysregulation of mineral metabolism: association of albumin-corrected calcium and vitamin D with diabetic kidney disease in type 2 diabetes—a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qiong Ma, Xue Li, Lin Liu, Fuqiang Liu, Ying Lv, Junkui Wang, Ting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisturbances in calcium (Ca), vitamin D, and electrolyte balance have been implicated in diabetic kidney disease (DKD), yet existing evidence remains inconsistent. Moreover, the role of albumin-corrected Ca—a more accurate measure of biologically active Ca in patients with frequent hypoalbuminemia—remains poorly defined in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). We aimed to investigate the association between albumin-corrected Ca, vitamin D, electrolytes and DKD in a large T2DM cohort.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 5550 T2DM patients were enrolled, including 1574 participants with DKD. Multivariable regression analyses were used to assess the association between albumin-corrected Ca, vitamin D and DKD. Spearman’s correlation analyses were conducted to examine the correlation between electrolyte levels, vitamin D and indicators of renal impairment.ResultsPatients with DKD in T2DM were older, predominantly male, with longer diabetes duration, higher blood pressure, poorly glycemic control and more diabetic complications compared to those without DKD. Notably, patients with DKD also showed significant electrolyte imbalances, suggesting dysregulated mineral metabolism. Multivariate logistic regression revealed albumin-corrected Ca (odds ratio [OR]: 4.032, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.017–8.062, P < 0.001), 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 (OR: 0.967, 95% CI: 0.960–0.974, P < 0.001) were independently associated with DKD after accounting for confounding factors.ConclusionsWe demonstrated that elevated albumin-corrected Ca and decreased 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 were significantly associated with DKD in T2DM patients. These findings suggest the potential of mineral metabolism parameters, particularly albumin-corrected Ca, to enhance early detection and risk stratification. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has become a pressing global health concern, largely attributable to the increasing prevalence of obesity rates, unhealthy dietary patterns, environmental and occupational hazards, and persistent tobacco use [1]. The chronic nature of T2DM results in severe complications, contributing to significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide. Among these complications, microvascular and macrovascular diseases—such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular disease—are the primary contributors to adverse clinical outcomes in affected individuals [2, 3].

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the most common and severe microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus, particularly T2DM [4]. DKD is characterized by progressive proteinuria and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline, evolving through stages of hyperfiltration, microalbuminuria (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio [UACR] 30–300 mg/g), and macroalbuminuria (UACR > 300 mg/g) with irreversible renal damage [5]. DKD occurs in approximately 20–40% of patients with T2DM and serves as the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide [5]. Despite therapeutic advances, including sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonists [6, 7], the escalating global prevalence of T2DM has exacerbated the burden of DKD, which independently amplifies cardiovascular risk and healthcare costs [5, 8]. Early intervention in DKD can effectively delay progression to kidney failure, but current biomarkers (e.g., albuminuria and eGFR) often identify the disease only after significant kidney damage has occurred due to its asymptomatic onset. Consequently, early biomarkers are urgently needed to enable timely clinical intervention.

The pathophysiology of DKD is complex and involves multiple factors, including a combination of metabolic, hemodynamic, inflammatory, and fibrotic factors. These factors contribute to glomerular damage, fibrosis, and eventual renal failure [9, 10]. Notably, emerging evidences suggest that electrolyte imbalances—particularly in calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg) and phosphate (P) homeostasis—may play a critical yet understudied role in DKD progression [11–14]. The balance of Ca, P, and Mg is disrupted in DKD through renal reabsorption channels and hormonal networks, including the parathyroid hormone–fibroblast growth factor 23–Klotho (PTH–FGF23–Klotho) axis [12]. These electrolyte disturbances activate profibrotic (e.g., TGF-β/Smad3), proinflammatory (e.g., NLRP3) pathways, accelerating renal fibrosis, vascular calcification, oxidative stress, and podocyte injury [13–15]. Clinically, hypomagnesemia is a novel predictor of ESRD and hyperphosphatemia correlates with heightened cardiovascular mortality in T2DM patients [11, 16].

Notably, Hypercalcemia and hypocalcemia have been associated with adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes. Some studies have indicated a significant positive association between serum Ca levels and DKD in T2DM patients [17], but their primary analysis was unable to account for PTH and vitamin D levels, which are known to influence serum Ca levels, particularly in patients with DKD. Besides, others suggest that low serum Ca levels contribute to kidney damage through mechanisms, such as vascular calcification and increased renal fibrosis [14, 18, 19]. Some reports suggest that hypercalcemia contributes to renal dysfunction by promoting vascular stiffness and calcification [10], while others indicate that hypocalcemia may worsen kidney injury through mechanisms, such as increased oxidative stress and impaired endothelial function [20].

Accurate assessment of Ca metabolism in DKD is challenging due to the high prevalence of hypoalbuminemia, as nearly 40% of circulating Ca is albumin-bound. This issue is particularly relevant in T2DM, where albumin levels frequently fluctuate due to renal impairment and comorbidities [21, 22]. Although albumin-corrected Ca offers a more accurate reflection of bioavailable Ca, its relationship with DKD severity remains poorly defined, with limited and conflicting data regarding its utility as a predictive biomarker. Given these conflicting findings and limitations, we conducted what is, to the best of our knowledge, the largest cross-sectional study to date investigating the association between serum albumin-corrected Ca levels and DKD in a T2DM population. In addition, this study simultaneously evaluated bone mineral metabolism parameters and blood electrolyte indices. By elucidating these associations, this study enhances the understanding of Ca homeostasis in DKD pathogenesis and may provide novel insights into the role of mineral metabolism in DKD.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a single-center, cross-sectional study in the Endocrinology Department at Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2015 to April 2021 in Xi’an, China. In this study, we consecutively enrolled in patients aged 18–75 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic criteria [2]. Diagnosis required meeting at least one of the following: (1) fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) 2-h plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); (3) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%; (4) random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L in patients with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis; and (5) receiving treatment with oral antidiabetics and/or insulin. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) types of diabetes other than T2DM or recent acute complications of diabetes and (2) the existence of major diseases or related diseases, including inflammatory disease, rheumatologic disease, adrenal disease, malignancy, cirrhosis, non-diabetic CKD, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. Recent acute diabetic complications were defined as diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state, or severe hypoglycemic episodes occurring within the past 6 months.

The clinical diagnosis of DKD was established following the ADA guidelines [5], meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) consistent UACRs in two of three samples within a 3–6-month period, with both being ≥ 30 mg/g and (2) sustained GFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. In addition, for the diagnosis to be valid, a definite history of diabetes mellitus was required, along with the exclusion of primary or secondary glomerulopathies (such as IgA nephropathy and lupus nephritis) and systemic disorders that could affect renal function. Finally, 5550 patients were included in this study, including 1574 patients with DKD. All the participants signed an informed consent form with full knowledge of the study protocol. The Medical Ethics Committee of the Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (SPPH–LLBG-17–3.2) approved this study.

Data collection

Basic information and anthropometric measurements were obtained from hospitalized patients using an electronic medical record system. Basic information included age, sex, duration of T2DM, diabetic complications, and hypoglycemic therapy. Anthropometric measurements included body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Trained technicians measured body weight and height according to standard operating procedures. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Blood pressure was measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer on the right arm, positioned 2–3 cm above the elbow, with the patient seated after a minimum of 5 min of rest. SBP and DBP were recorded as the average of three consecutive measurements, with at least a 1-min interval between each reading. The sample size was calculated for the planned multivariable logistic regression analysis. Based on the events per variable criterion, a minimum of 15 events per independent variable was sought [23]. With an anticipated number of 14 variables to be included in the final model and the estimated DKD prevalence of 28% in our T2DM cohort, the minimum total sample size was calculated to be 750 participants. To account for potential missing data and exclusions, we increased this number by 15%, yielding a final target sample size of 862.

Biochemical blood analyses

After an 8 h fast in the morning, we collected venous blood samples. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated albumin, total protein, albumin, globulin, uric acid, cystatin C, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), potassium (K), sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl), Mg, P, and Ca levels were measured using the LABOSPECT 008AS automatic biochemical analyzer (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). HbA1c was measured to the nearest 0.1% using an HLC-723G8 automatic analyzer (TOSOH BIOSCIENCE, Tokyo, Japan).

The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation was used to calculate the eGFR [24]: eGFR=141×minScrκ,1α×maxScrκ,1-1.209×0.993age×1.018 (if female). In this equation, κ is 0.7 if male and 0.9 if female, α is − 0.411 if male and − 0.329 if female, and min and max mean the minimum and maximum values, respectively.

Urine samples were collected in two forms: fasting morning urine and 24 h total urine samples. These samples were obtained in the early morning and were centrifuged within 30 min of collection at 2000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. Urine creatinine, microalbumin, 24-h microalbumin, and 24-h urine protein (24hU-TP) levels were measured using a LABOSPECT 008AS automatic biochemical analyzer (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). UACR was calculated as the ratio of urine albumin to urine creatinine. Albumin-corrected Ca was calculated as: Ca (mmol/L) + 0.02 ×(40 – albumin [g/L]).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t test was used to assess the differences between the groups. Variables with a skewed distribution were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges, and comparisons between the groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and comparisons between the groups were conducted using the chi-squared test.

Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to explore the correlation between serum electrolyte levels and related indicators of renal impairment. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate factors influencing DKD in patients with T2DM. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and existing scientific literature. Basic demographics (age, sex, BMI) and diabetes-specific markers (duration of T2DM, HbA1c) were included as fundamental confounders affecting diabetes outcomes. Established renal risk factors (SBP, DBP, eGFR) were adjusted for given their strong association with DKD progression [25]. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to show statistical significance. SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study participants

In this study, we enrolled 5550 patients with T2DM, including 1574 patients with DKD and 3976 without DKD (Fig. 1). Among 1574 DKD patients, the vast majority (91.9%) exhibited active albuminuria, primarily microalbuminuria (61.7%), while most patients (71.5%) maintained normal renal function. Notably, 16.6% of patients showed a discordant phenotype of macroalbuminuria despite preserved eGFR (G1A3), indicating active nephropathy independent of glomerular filtration rate (Supplementary Table 1).

Patients with DKD were significantly older than those without DKD (59.44 ± 12.46 vs. 54.70 ± 14.43 years, P < 0.001) and had a higher proportion of males (67.8% vs. 64.2%, P = 0.012). Furthermore, renal function was markedly impaired in patients with T2DM and DKD, as evidenced by elevated levels of uric acid, cystatin C, serum creatinine, BUN, microalbumin, UACR, 24-h microalbumin, 24hU-TP, alongside reduced eGFR and urine creatinine levels (all P < 0.001). In comparison with patients without DKD, patients with DKD showed lower serum total protein and albumin levels but higher serum globulin levels. This led to a decreased albumin-to-globulin ratio. Notably, patients with DKD also showed significant electrolyte imbalances, including higher serum K, P, and albumin-corrected Ca levels but lower Na, Cl, and 1,25-dihydroxy-vitamin-D3 (1,25-(OH)2-VitD3) levels. These findings show a dysregulated mineral metabolism.

Serum electrolyte indices on the occurrence of DKD

Univariate logistic regression analysis showed significant associations between serum electrolyte levels and DKD occurrence (Fig. 2). Higher serum K (odds ratio [OR]: 1.223, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.092–1.371), P (OR: 1.479, 95% CI: 1.132–1.931), and albumin-corrected Ca (OR: 2.143, 95% CI: 1.462–3.139) levels were positively associated with DKD occurrence. Conversely, elevated Na (OR: 0.977, 95% CI: 0.965–0.985), Cl (OR: 0.986, 95% CI: 0.974–0.997), and 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 (OR: 0.951, 95% CI: 0.938–0.963) levels showed inverse association with DKD risk. All P values were < 0.05. However, in the univariate analysis, we did not observe a significant association between serum Mg, PTH levels, and DKD.

Correlation of serum electrolyte levels with indicators of renal impairment

We conducted Spearman’s correlation analyses (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2) to further explore the associations between the above significant serum electrolyte indices, 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3, and DKD-related parameters. The results revealed that albumin-corrected Ca level was positively correlated with FBG, HbA1c, glycated albumin, cystain C, blood creatinine, BUN, 24-h microalbumin, and UACR, but negatively correlated with eGFR. Conversely, 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 exhibited significant negative correlations with glycemic control markers (FBG, HbA1c, glycated albumin) and albuminuria markers (UACR, microalbumin, 24-h microalbumin). In addition, serum electrolytes such as K, Na, Cl and P were positively associated with uric acid, cystain C, and BUN, but negatively associated with eGFR. However, their relationship with blood glycemic control indicators was not consistent.

Multivariate analysis of the independent effect of albumin-corrected Ca on the risk of DKD

To account for confounding variables, we created three multivariate logistic regression models using the forward (conditional) stepwise regression method to examine the associations between serum electrolyte levels (albumin-corrected Ca, K, Na, Cl, P), 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 and DKD risk in T2DM population (Table 2). The models incorporated progressively increasing adjustment levels: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, duration of T2DM, and HbA1c levels; and Model 3 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, duration of T2DM, HbA1c, SBP, DBP, and eGFR levels. Our findings show that higher albumin-corrected Ca, P levels and lower Na, Cl, 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 levels were associated with an increased DKD risk in partially adjusted models 1 and 2. Most importantly, in comprehensive Model 3, incorporating full clinical adjustment, albumin-corrected Ca level maintained a robust positive association with the risk of DKD (OR: 4.032, 95% CI: 2.017–8.062, P < 0.001). Conversely, 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 showed negative association with DKD (OR: 0.947, 95% CI: 0.960–0.974, P < 0.001).

To further evaluate the clinical utility of albumin-corrected Ca, we performed additional receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Figure S1). Despite the area under the curve (AUC) for albumin-corrected Ca alone (0.563; 95% CI: 0.546–0.580) was lower than that of microalbumin (0.708; 95% CI: 0.691–0.725), a composite model integrating both biomarkers yielded an increased AUC of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.712–0.745), indicating superior diagnostic performance.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study revealed a significant positive association between elevated albumin-corrected Ca levels and increased DKD risk, which persisted despite comprehensive confounder adjustment. Besides, our ROC analysis further revealed that albumin-corrected Ca provided incremental value to microalbuminuria, as their combination improved the discriminatory power for DKD compared to microalbuminuria alone. These findings suggest that albumin-corrected Ca can be a potential biomarker for DKD risk, independent of key renal and metabolic parameters, including eGFR and blood pressure. Total Ca measurements can be a misleading indicator as nearly 40% of circulating Ca is albumin-bound, especially in T2DM patients, where hypoalbuminemia is common [21, 22]. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) position states that the measurement of ionized Ca is preferred and that if total Ca concentration is used instead, then it should be adjusted in the setting of hypoalbuminemia [26]. Although ionized Ca is the physiologically active form, its measurement is not routinely recommended due to poorer reproducibility as well as greater cost. Therefore, the use of albumin-corrected Ca in this study aligns with pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical guidelines.

Recent studies showed the significant role of Ca metabolism in the pathogenesis of diabetes-related complications. As an essential mineral, Ca is crucial for biological functions, including intracellular signaling, muscular activity, and skeletal health. Nevertheless, disturbances in Ca homeostasis have been implicated in the progression of CKD [27]. Our findings further support the idea that even within the normal range, higher serum Ca levels may contribute to DKD progression. Interestingly, our findings align with that of previous studies that have highlighted the role of Ca in diabetic complications. For instance, high serum Ca levels have been associated with an increased risk of vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy in patients with T2DM [28]. Moreover, Ca supplementation has been linked to adverse outcomes in patients with diabetes, including an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease [29]. These findings show that Ca metabolism may be an important factor in the development of diabetic complications and that monitoring serum Ca levels could be an important aspect of managing patients with T2DM.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms can explain this association. Elevated serum Ca levels may lead to vascular calcification [30, 31], which promotes inflammatory responses [32] that contribute to glomerulosclerosis. Vascular calcification increases arterial stiffness and exacerbates pressure in the renal glomeruli, potentially accelerating kidney damage. In addition, Ca plays a significant role in oxidative stress, which can directly damage renal tissues [33]. Disrupted Ca signaling has also been shown to affect podocyte structure and function, leading to glomerular injury and proteinuria, which are both hallmark features of DKD [34]. The precise mechanisms by which Ca signaling influences podocyte function remain incompletely understood. However, emerging evidence suggests that Piezo1 and its downstream signaling pathways may play a critical role. Proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), as well as soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) and autoactivation, have been shown to upregulate Piezo1 expression [35]. Activated Piezo1 triggers a signaling loop involving nuclear factor of activated T cells c1 (NFATc1) and/or transient receptor potential canonical 6 (TRPC6), leading to increased Ca influx and subsequent sustained podocyte injury [36]. Consistent with these findings, renal biopsy samples from diabetic patients demonstrate that overexpression of TRPC6 in podocytes is associated with reduced calpastatin expression, impaired autophagy, and podocyte damage [37]. Further investigation is expected to yield more evidence clarifying the complexity of Ca signaling pathways and their multifaceted roles in podocyte injury under diabetic conditions. Notably, the relationship between serum Ca and DKD may be bidirectional. Elevated serum Ca levels may contribute to renal damage; however, DKD progression can also lead to disturbances in Ca metabolism. Several pathophysiological mechanisms associated with DKD may contribute to elevated serum Ca levels. One plausible explanation is the progressive decline in renal function, which impairs the ability of the kidney to excrete excess Ca [38, 39]. In advanced DKD, reduced GFR and tubular dysfunction may promote Ca mobilization from bone by increasing osteoclastic activity and reducing renal tubular Ca reabsorption, thereby contributing to hypercalcemia [40].

In addition, we observed a significant positive association between elevated serum P and lower 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 levels and increased DKD risk after adjustment for basic clinical and glycemic control indicators. However, the association between serum P and DKD became non-significant after further adjustment for eGFR, SBP, and DBP. Deficient 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 in DKD disrupts Ca homeostasis, reducing intestinal absorption of both Ca and Mg, promoting hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia. Besides, Mg deficiency can impair both vitamin D metabolism and function [41]. This triggers secondary hyperparathyroidism, elevating PTH levels, compounding dysregulation of Ca–P metabolism. Sustained high PTH and low Ca directly damage renal tissues by promoting podocyte apoptosis, activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and stimulating profibrotic signaling. Importantly, Mg deficiency may also directly influence insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction, further worsening diabetic renal injury [42]. Furthermore, vitamin D deficiency itself exacerbates renal inflammation and fibrosis [43]. Thus, the interplay between vitamin D, PTH, Ca, P, and Mg creates a vicious cycle that accelerates DKD progression by driving tubular injury, glomerulosclerosis, and interstitial fibrosis.

This study has several strengths, including large sample size and multivariate logistic regression utilization to explore the association between albumin-corrected Ca levels and DKD. This study identified distinct disturbances in Ca metabolism among DKD patients, offering new insights for improved clinical management. It highlights the potential benefits of routine albumin-corrected calcium screening in high-risk T2DM individuals, and the added value of albumin-corrected Ca in conjunction with microalbuminuria, as demonstrated by ROC analysis. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design of this study and the lack of data regarding the duration of DKD precluded us from establishing a causal link between serum albumin-corrected Ca levels and DKD. Future prospective studies are necessary to confirm our findings and determine whether the progression of DKD can be influenced by interventions aimed at modulating serum albumin-corrected Ca levels. Second, despite comprehensive multivariable adjustment for known clinical confounding factors, the potential for residual confounding (such as by unmeasured genetic, environmental, or lifestyle factors) still persists. Third, we lacked data on dietary Ca intake and the use of Ca supplements, which may influence serum Ca levels and potentially confound the observed associations. Finally, the absence of patients with clinically abnormal serum Ca levels may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader T2DM population, particularly those with overt Ca metabolism disorders.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that elevated albumin-corrected Ca and decreased 1,25-(OH)2-VitD3 are significantly associated with DKD in T2DM patients. These findings suggest the potential of mineral metabolism parameters, particularly albumin-corrected Ca, to enhance early detection and risk stratification. However, due to the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional design, this observed link should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future prospective cohort studies are essential to establish a causal relationship and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
