COX-2/PGE₂ signaling drives Toxocara canis-induced lung pathology by enhancing plasminogen activator activity and collagen VI degradation
Authors: Shih-Chan Lai, Cheng-You Lu, Ching-Yu Chuang, Hsiu-Hsiung Lee, Meng-Chia Lee, Ke-Min Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractToxocara canis infection induces severe pulmonary pathology, including hemorrhage, inflammation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study explores the role of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) in lung injury during T. canis infection, focusing on its regulation of plasminogen activator (PA) activity and collagen type VI (COL6) degradation. A murine model was established by orogastric inoculation of 2000 embryonated T. canis eggs. Lung tissues were analyzed via histopathology, ELISA, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry. COX-2 inhibition with celecoxib was used to assess its effects on COX-2/PGE₂ signaling, PA activity, PI3K/AKT pathway activation, and COL6 degradation. Infection significantly upregulated COX-2, peaking at day 7, correlating with increased PA activity and COL6 cleavage (70/50 kDa fragments). Celecoxib suppressed COX-2/PGE₂, downregulated PI3K/AKT, reduced PA activity, and preserved COL6 integrity, mitigating lung injury. Mechanistically, COX-2/PGE₂ enhances PA activation via PI3K/AKT, exacerbating COL6 degradation. In conclusion, COX-2 drives lung pathology in toxocariasis by modulating PA activity and COL6 degradation, suggesting that targeting COX-2/PGE₂ signaling could ameliorate infection-induced pulmonary damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22711-x.Keywords: Toxocara canis, COX-2, Plasminogen activators, Collagen type VI (COL6), Pulmonary pathologySubject terms: Diseases, Infectious diseases, Respiratory tract diseases

Introduction

Toxocara canis and Toxocara cati are significant concerns in public health and hygiene. Adult T. canis worms reside in the small intestine of dogs, releasing eggs through faeces, leading to environmental contamination1. Upon ingestion by intermediate or paratenic hosts (e.g. humans, mice), larvae penetrate the intestinal wall and migrate to various organs,, including the liver, lungs, muscles, eye, and central nervous system, causing toxocariasis, a disease with severe pathological consequences2. Toxocariasis is characterized by hemorrhage, immune cell infiltration around larvae, and the formation of eosinophilic granulomas3. Pulmonary infection with T. canis larvae can induce asthma-like symptoms in children4. During the lung migratory stage, a fine balance between inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses is essential for host protection5.

The inflammatory response is a defense mechanism triggered by tissue injury from infections and is mediated by cytokines, prostaglandins, cyclooxygenase (COX) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), all of which contribute to disease progression6,7. The COX-2/prostaglandin E2 (COX-2/PGE2) axis plays a central role in driving inflammation and is involved in various infectious diseases8. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β can activate immune responses and upregulate COX-2 via the NF-κB pathway, enhancing PGE₂ production9. These cytokines also induce nitric oxide (NO), further promoting COX-2 activation and PGE₂ synthesis10,11. The PI3K/AKT pathway is positively correlated with COX-2 expression by activating AKT and downstream NF-κB signaling. Moreover, PGE₂ can feedback to activate PI3K/AKT signaling through its receptors, amplifying inflammation12–14.

During the fibrinolytic process, plasmin, the key serine protease of the fibrinolysis cascade, is generated from the zymogen plasminogen, either by tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA) or urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA). Although tPA is crucial for intravascular fibrinolysis, uPA facilitates cell migration, invasion, and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) during tissue remodeling15. Plasminogen activators are involved in the pathogenesis of numerous infectious diseases caused by viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites16–19. Collagen type VI (COL6), a fundamental component of the ECM in the basement membrane in the lung parenchyma, airways, and vasculature, plays a crucial role in airway remodeling and vascular permeability20. Alterations in the composition of the ECM and the expression of the basement membrane have been shown in many pulmonary disorders, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD), and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis21.

The regulatory mechanisms of the host immune response to T. canis infection remain unclear. Inflammatory responses and ECM remodeling play a critical role in parasitic infections, with the COX-2/PGE2 axis and the PA system requiring further investigation. As a key component of the ECM, COL6 can contribute to parasite-induced lung pathology. This study aims to clarify the interaction between the COX-2/PGE2 axis, the PA system, and COL6 in lung toxocariasis, providing information on the mechanisms underlying parasite-associated lung disease.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

A total of 81 five-week-old male BALB/c mice were purchased from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Taipei, Taiwan. All mice were used at 5 weeks of age when they weighed 20–22 g. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Euthanize experimental animals using carbon dioxide in accordance with approved protocol. The animal care and handling protocols were approved by the Animal Use Protocol Chung-Shan Medical University Animal Center Affidavit (IACUC Approval No: 2572). All animal experiments were carried out under institutional policies and guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals, and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. The procedures fully complied with the approved guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Toxocara canis larval preparation

Toxocara canis was prepared via the Lu method with slight modifications22. The eggs of T. canis were obtained from the uterus of adult female parasites from infected dogs. The embryonated eggs were incubated in 2% formalin at room temperature with gentle weekly agitation for 3–5 weeks to obtain the infective eggs. The eggs were washed three times with deionized water to remove the formalin. The mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 1400 × g. For eggshell lysis, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was agitated in 10 ml of 3% sodium hypochlorite for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 1400 × g. The eggs were washed three times with deionized water and collected for experimental studies.

Animal infection

For the time course experiment, 54 BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to 6 groups, each consisting of 9 mice. The mice in the T. canis infection group were sacrificed on days 4, 7, 14, 28, and 56 after infection. In the infection groups, each mouse received 2000 embryonated T. canis eggs through a stomach tube. The noninfected control group received an equivalent volume of deionized water on day 0, as did the infected groups, and was sacrificed on day 56. For the COX-2 inhibition experiment, 27 BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to 3 groups, each consisting of 9 mice. These groups included the uninfected control group, the T. canis infection, and the T. canis infection group treated with pharmacological inhibition of COX-2 (Celecoxib, Viatris Healthcare Hong Kong). In the groups infected with T. canis and treated with celecoxib, each mouse received 2000 embryonated T. canis eggs through a stomach tube. In the celecoxib treatment group, celecoxib was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 5 mg/kg/0.5 ml/day from day 0 of T. canis infection to day 7, with euthanasia of the mice on day 7 after infection.

Recovery of larvae from the lungs of T. canis-infected mice

The lung tissues were homogenized at a 1:30 ratio (tissue: artificial pepsin digestion solution containing pepsin from Sigma, USA) via a digestive juice grinder. The mixture was then evenly stirred and digested in a 37 °C incubator for 1 h. The lower sediment layer was carefully collected via a dropper and transferred to a glass plate, and the number of T. canis larvae was counted with an inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The concentrations of PGE2, INF-γ, TNF-α, IL1-β, IL-6 and NO in the homogeneous solution of the lungs were determined via commercially available enzyme immunoassay kits (Cayman Chemical, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Casein/plasminogen zymography

PA activity was determined by casein/plasminogen zymography according to the Hou method with slight modifications23. In a typical procedure, lung proteins were subjected to 7.5% (mass/volume) SDS‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis after copolymerization with 0.1% casein (Sigma, USA) and plasminogen (13 mg/mL; American Diagnostica). The stacking gels were composed of 4% polyacrylamide (mass/volume). Electrophoresis was performed in running buffer (25 mM Tris, 250 mM glycine, and 1% SDS) at room temperature and 110 V for 1 h. The gel was washed twice at room temperature for 30 min each in 2.5% Triton X-100 and then twice with double distilled H2O for 10 min each. The gel was incubated in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, containing 10 mM CaCl2 and 0.01% NaN3) at 37 °C for 18 h. The gel was stained with 0.25% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (Sigma, USA) for 1 h and destained in 15% methanol/7.5% acetic acid. Quantitative analysis of these caseinolytic enzymes was performed via an iBright CL750 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Western blot analysis

The proteins were extracted from lung tissues via radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease inhibitors (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), resolved via SDS‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The membranes were incubated for 1 h in blocking buffer (3% BSA in PBS) at room temperature. The membrane was washed three times with PBST (0.1% Tween-20 in phosphate-buffered saline). The following primary antibodies were used: tPA (1:500, American Diagnostica, USA), uPA (1:500, American Diagnostica, USA), COX-2 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, USA), COL6 (1:1000, Abcam, USA), PI3K (1:1000, Abcam, USA), p-PI3K (1:500, Abcam, USA), AKT (1:1000, Abcam, USA) and p-AKT (1:1000, Abcam, USA). β-Actin monoclonal antibody (1:5000, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used as a loading control. The membrane was then incubated with a primary antibody and an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) diluted 1:5000 at 37 °C for 1 h. Protein bands were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized with an iBright CL750 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Mouse lung slides were sequentially immersed in xylene, 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 75% ethanol, 50% ethanol, 25% ethanol, and deionized water for deparaffinization and rehydration. The nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, washed with deionized water, and then immersed in acid alcohol for differentiation. After another wash with deionized water, an eosin solution was used to stain the cytoplasm. Finally, the slides were washed with deionized water and air-dried at room temperature prior to mounting. The mounted slides were examined under a microscope (ZEISS, Axio Imager A2, Heidelberg, Germany). The pulmonary bleeding intensity was quantified via ImageJ software (version 1.54 g).

Pathological analysis

A semi-quantitative assessment of lung tissue inflammation was performed using the Szapiel scoring system, which grades alveolitis severity on a scale of 0–3: score 0: absence of alveolitis. Alveolitis. score 1: mild alveolitis, mononuclear cell infiltration was seen locally and in the near thorax, with an area of less than 20% of the whole lung, and the alveolar structure was more or less normal. Score 2: moderate alveolitis, with the lesion area accounting for 20–50% of the whole lung. Score 3: severe alveolitis and pulmonary fibrosis, with the area of the lesion being greater than 50% of the whole lung, with occasional mononuclear cells and/or hemorrhage in the alveolar lumen causing solid lesions.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed to detect COX-2, tPA, uPA and COL6 proteins in lung sections from BALB/c mice infected with T. canis at 7 days post infection (dpi). In a typical procedure, paraffin-embedded section (10 μm) were deparaffinized, treated with 3% H2O2 in methanol for 10 min to inactivate any endogenous peroxidase, and washed with PBST. The sections were blocked for 1 h in blocking buffer (3% BSA in PBST) and incubated for 1 h with primary antibodies (COX-2, Santa Cruz, USA), tPA (American Diagnostica, USA), uPA (American Diagnostica, USA) and COL6 (Abcam, USA) diluted 1:50 in 1% BSA. Sections incubated with normal serum served as negative controls. The sections were washed three times for 10 min each in PBST and incubated for 1 h with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) diluted 1:1000 in 1% BSA at 37 °C. The sections were finally incubated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) at room temperature for 3 min for color development. The sections were counterstained with diluted hematoxylin. Mounted slides were examined under a microscope (ZEISS, Axio Imager A2, Heidelberg, Germany).

Statistical analysis

The results from different groups of mice were compared via the nonparametric Kruskal‒Wallis test followed by post hoc testing via Dunn’s multiple comparison of means. All the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The correlation test used Spearman’s ranking correlation test. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Results

Lung pathology and larval recovery in T. canis-infected mice

This study investigated lung pathology and larval recovery in mice infected with T. canis to elucidate the inflammatory response and its association with larval migration. Pulmonary hemorrhage and lung inflammation scores peaked between 4 and 7 dpi, followed by a gradual reduction at 14, 28, and 56 dpi (p < 0.05; Fig. 1). In high-magnification H&E-stained lung sections, highlighting leukocyte infiltration, particularly eosinophils. Although eosinophils were the predominant cell type, other leukocyte populations were also evident. These findings indicate a sustained pathological response to T. canis infection, characterized by persistent hemorrhage and inflammation. Larvae were detected in the lungs early after infection, with the highest recovery occurring between 4 and 7 dpi, followed by a marked decline later (p < 0.05; Fig. 3). In particular, the peak in larval recovery coincided with maximum pulmonary hemorrhage and inflammatory cell infiltration, suggesting that larval migration and/or remnants of dead larvae contribute to lung pathology. The strong correlation between larval presence, severe hemorrhage, and immune cell infiltration underscores the role of T. canis larvae in causing lung damage. These findings highlight the complex interplay between parasitic migration and the host’s inflammatory response, providing information on the pathogenesis of toxocariasis-associated lung injury.

The COX-2/PGE2 pathway mediates the activation of the plasminogen activators in mice infected with T. canis

This study investigated the role of inflammatory mediators in modulating lung tissue responses in mice infected with T. canis. Our findings revealed significant alterations in COX-2 expression and PGE₂ levels in infected lung tissue. Western blot analysis demonstrated marked upregulation of COX-2 in all the infected groups compared with the control groups. Although COX-2 expression began to decrease at 14 dpi, it remained significantly elevated at 56 dpi (p < 0.05; Fig. 4a). Similarly, PGE₂ concentrations were minimal in control lungs but significantly increased in the infected groups, reaching a maximum at 4 and 7 dpi (p < 0.05; Fig. 4b). The COX-2 protein levels were correlated significantly with PGE2 concentration (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 4c). Heatmap analysis revealed strong correlations among bleeding intensity, number of recovered larvae, COX-2 expression, and PGE₂ levels in the lungs of T. canis-infected mice (Fig. 4d). To investigate whether the COX-2/PGE₂ pathway regulates PA activity, we measured tPA and uPA protein levels and enzyme activity via Western blotting and casein zymography, respectively. Western blot analysis revealed a significant upregulation of both tPA and uPA in the infected groups at all time points, reaching a plateau between 4 and 14 dpi. Although a slight decline was observed at 28 and 56 dpi, expression levels remained elevated compared with those in the control group (p < 0.05; Fig. 5a). Casein zymography confirmed these findings, revealing increased enzymatic activity of both tPA and uPA in infected mice, particularly at 4, 7, 14, 28, and 56 dpi (p < 0.05; Fig. 5b). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between the number of recovered lung larvae and COX-2 expression (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 6a) and PGE₂ concentrations (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 6b), as well as COX-2 levels were significantly correlated with the activity of tPA (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 6c) and uPA (p = 0.00021; Fig. 6d). Similarly, PGE₂ levels were significantly correlated with tPA (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 6e) and uPA (p = 0.0000102; Fig. 6f) activity. These data support a tightly regulated pathway in which the COX-2/PGE2 pathway plays a crucial role in the regulation of PA proteolytic responses in T. canis-induced lung injury, contributing to the inflammatory and fibrotic response.

Degradation of COL6 in the lung of T. canis-infected mice and its correlation with plasminogen activators

Plasminogen activators play a critical role in the degradation of the ECM. To investigate their involvement in T. canis-induced lung pathology, we analyzed the degradation of COL6 in infected mice. Western blot analysis revealed intact COL6 (100 kDa) and its cleavage fragment products (70 and 50 kDa), with significantly higher levels detected at 4, 7, 14, and 28 dpi than in the controls. At 56 dpi, the degradation products of COL6 were markedly reduced (p < 0.05; Fig. 7). A significant positive correlation was observed between COX-2 protein levels and 70 kDa (p = 0.00577; Fig. 8a) and 50 kDa COL6 fragments (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 8b). Further analysis revealed that the 70 kDa fragment of COL6 was strongly correlated with tPA (p = 0.000121; Fig. 8c) and uPA (p = 0.0000002; Fig. 8e). Similarly, the 50 kDa COL6 fragment was significantly correlated with tPA (p = 0.000244; Fig. 8d) and uPA (p = 0.00305; Fig. 8f), determined by Spearman’s rank correlation test. These findings suggest that COL6 degradation is regulated through the COX-2/PGE₂ pathway, which activates PA and contributes to the remodeling of the ECM during T. canis infection.

Modulation of inflammatory responses and hemorrhagic changes in mice iInfected with T. canis following celecoxib treatment

At 7 dpi, T. canis larvae were recovered from the lungs of infected mice, with no significant differences in larval burden between the untreated and celecoxib-treated groups (Fig. 9a). ELISA results demonstrated a significant increase in PGE₂, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NO levels in infected mice compared to controls. Celecoxib treatment effectively suppressed PGE₂ production. Likewise, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NO levels were markedly reduced in celecoxib-treated mice relative to untreated infected mice, although they remained elevated compared to uninfected controls. In contrast, IFN-γ expression was significantly upregulated in infected mice and further increased following celecoxib treatment (Fig. 9b, c). Histopathological analysis revealed pronounced pulmonary hemorrhage in infected mice at 7 dpi, with a significantly increased hemorrhagic intensity compared with that in controls. Quantitative assessment via ImageJ software confirmed this increase. In particular, compared with infection, celecoxib treatment markedly reduced hemorrhagic intensity, with significantly lower levels (p < 0.05; Fig. 10). These findings suggest that celecoxib effectively modulates inflammatory responses and mitigates tissue damage in lung pathology induced by T. canis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention for parasitic infections.

Celecoxib attenuates COX-2 expression, plasminogen activators activity, COL6 deposition, and PI3K/AKT signaling in mice infected with T. canis

Western blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of the COX-2, tPA, uPA, and COL6 proteins (including intact 100 kDa protein and fragments cleaved at 100, 70 and 50 kDa) in the lungs of mice infected with T. canis (7 dpi) (Fig. 11a). Celecoxib treatment markedly attenuated the expression of these inflammatory and fibrotic markers, suggesting a potent modulatory effect on the infection-induced response. Enzyme activity assays via casein/plasminogen zymography revealed a significant increase in tPA and uPA activity in the infected group, which was significantly reduced with celecoxib treatment (Fig. 11b). Furthermore, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is known to regulate inflammatory and fibrotic responses, was significantly activated in the infected group, as evidenced by increased levels of the PI3K and AKT proteins and their phosphorylation. Celecoxib treatment resulted in a marked reduction in both PI3K and AKT expression and their phosphorylation status, suggesting that inhibition of COX-2 affects the PI3K/AKT axis in the context of T. canis infection (Fig. 11c). Together, these results indicate that celecoxib exerts comprehensive anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects in lungs infected with T. canis, modulating COX-2 activity, plasminogen activator function, collagen deposition and PI3K/AKT signaling. This study underscores the therapeutic potential of COX-2 inhibitors in parasitic infections and associated lung pathologies.

Immunohistochemical location of COX-2 and plasminogen activators in T. canis-infected lung tissue

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed minimal or undetectable expression of COX-2, tPA, uPA, and COL6 in the lung tissues of the control group. At 7 dpi, significant upregulation of these proteins was observed in lung tissue infected with T. canis, particularly in areas of inflammatory infiltration and hemorrhage. In contrast, celecoxib treatment markedly reduced the expression of COX-2, tPA, uPA, and COL6, restoring levels comparable to those of the control group (Fig. 12). These findings suggest that the inhibition of COX-2 mitigates the upregulation of plasminogen activators associated with T. canis infection, potentially modulating inflammatory and fibrinolytic responses in the lung.

Discussion

Helminth migration through host tissues causes substantial damage and requires efficient repair. Research using helminth-rodent models has revealed crucial cellular and molecular pathways involved in tissue recovery after injury. Unlike other helminths, T. canis does not develop within the host but rather triggers a robust inflammatory response and structural damage to the lungs24. This study investigated the significant pathological changes induced by the migration of T. canis through host lung tissues. Histological analysis revealed extensive immune cell infiltration and hemorrhage, which is consistent with the clinical manifestations of pulmonary toxocariasis, including chronic lung dysfunction such as pneumonia and asthma. In particular, the observed immune response, characterized by peribronchiolitis and perivasculitis, is a hallmark of pulmonary toxocariasis. We also found a significant increase in larval-infected mice, with pronounced peribronchiolar and perivascular inflammation occurring in the early stages of infection. These pathological changes coincided with elevated immune cell infiltration and hemorrhage.

COX-2 plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory responses in various infectious diseases, including bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections25–27. Prostaglandins, especially PGE2, modulate lung function by influencing bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, and mucus secretion. Their immunomodulatory effects are well established in infections caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, Entamoeba histolytica, and Leishmania donovani28–30. This study presents the first evidence of activation of the COX-2/PGE2 axis in pulmonary toxocariasis, demonstrating a direct correlation between COX-2/PGE2 expression levels and larval burden. These results indicate that COX-2/PGE2 signaling contributes to exacerbated pulmonary inflammation by enhancing immune cell recruitment and vascular permeability, thus driving lung pathology.

The PA system is crucial for maintaining lung homeostasis and protection against fibrosis31. PA catalyze the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, facilitating the degradation of the ECM, immune cell infiltration, and tissue remodeling. However, PA activity has been extensively studied in bacterial32 and viral infections33. Furthermore, plasmin activates pro-MMPs, particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, which further drive ECM degradation and inflammation34,35. The role of PA in T. canis-induced lung injury remains poorly understood. Previous studies reported significant upregulation of MMP-9 in the lungs of BALB/c mice after larval migration of T. canis22,36. Our study showed that PA activity reached its maximum at 4 dpi, which was similar to the time when MMP-2 and MMP-9 activities appeared and was related to the number of larvae. In addition, tPA and uPA contribute to COL6 degradation, hemorrhage, and leukocyte migration to alveolar spaces during T. canis infection. These findings suggest that PA/MMP-driven degradation of COL6 is a key pathological mechanism in pulmonary toxocariasis and likely contributes to tissue damage and repair responses.

Collagen type VI, a crucial component of the ECM in the lung parenchyma, is integral to vascular remodeling and permeability37,38. The biologically active fragment of COL6, endotrophin, is involved in fibrosis, inflammation, and metabolic disorders39,40. In particular, endotrophin is associated with lung diseases such as COPD and COVID-1941. Our study revealed a progressive increase in COL6 expression from 4 to 56 dpi, with maximum degradation occurring between 4 and 14 dpi. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that COX-2, tPA, uPA and COL6 were expressed in the epithelial cells of the bronchioles and vessels, and the results of the correlation analysis also revealed a high degree of correlation between them. Selective COX-2 inhibitors, such as celecoxib, have shown efficacy in mitigating inflammation and tissue remodeling in various infectious diseases. Celecoxib attenuates PGE2 production, cytokine expression, and immune cell infiltration in infections such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Histoplasma capsulatum42. In mice infected with T. canis, celecoxib treatment significantly reduced lung hemorrhage, immune cell infiltration, COX-2, PGE2, PA and COL6. These findings are consistent with previous studies implicating COX-2 in parasitic and allergic lung inflammation; however, our data uniquely demonstrate a direct link between COX-2 activation and COL6 degradation through the PA proteinolytic system. Specifically, both tPA and uPA were strongly induced in response to infection, and their spatial locations corresponded to the COL6 sites. Therefore, the concurrent activation of COX-2 and PA suggests a tightly regulated COL6 remodeling process. Furthermore, it mitigates COL6 degradation and attenuates endotrophin-mediated inflammation.

COX-2/PGE2 signaling amplifies the inflammatory response via NF-κB activation, which is triggered by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β43. This pathway has been implicated in viral infections, where it drives cytokine production and immune activation. NADPH oxidase/MAPK/c-JUN/NF-κB signaling cascades are associated with the upregulation of COX-2 and PGE2 synthesis44. Similarly, bacterial pneumonia is associated with upregulation in response to TNF-α and IL-1β45. Our findings suggest that COX-2/PGE2 enhances PA activity, reinforcing its role in lung inflammation. Furthermore, celecoxib treatment significantly reduced the expression of the PI3K/AKT pathway in mice infected with T. canis. Mechanically, celecoxib decreased PA expression by regulating cytokines (INF-γ, TNF-α and IL-6) and the PI3K/AKT pathway, reducing COL6 degradation and attenuated endotrophin-mediated inflammation. These effects culminated in reduced lung damage. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the spatial disruption of this axis in celecoxib-treated lungs. We hypothesize that COX-2/PGE2-mediated COL6 degradation in pulmonary toxocariasis is regulated by the MAPK/c-JUN/NF-κB or PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, leading to increased PA/MMP activity and COL6 degradation. Importantly, our correlation analyses revealed a tightly coupled regulatory network in which COX-2 expression, PGE₂ production, PI3K/AKT activation, PA activity, and COL6 degradation progress in parallel. This integrated pathway likely contributes to the severity of lung injury observed in this infection model and may represent a broader mechanism of ECM damage in helminthic diseases.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes a mechanistic link between COX-2/PGE2-driven PA activation, COL6 degradation, immune cell infiltration, and lung pathology in T. canis-induced lung disease. By elucidating the molecular pathways underlying helminth-induced lung injury, we identified COX-2 as a promising therapeutic target. Future investigations should explore COX-2 inhibition as a potential intervention strategy for helminth-associated pulmonary pathology.
