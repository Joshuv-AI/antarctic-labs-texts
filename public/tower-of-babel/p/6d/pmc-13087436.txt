An Interpretable Machine Learning Model With Synthetic MRI‐Based Habitat Radiomics for Predicting Lymph Node Metastasis in Oral Cancer
Authors: Rui Wang, Jiliang Ren, Yong Zhang, Jie Shi, Ying Yuan, Xiaofeng Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo develop an interpretable radiomics model based on habitat for predicting lymph node metastasis in oral cancer using Synthetic MRI (SyMRI).MethodsA retrospective study of 101 oral cancer patients with pathologically confirmed lymph node status was performed, dividing them into a training cohort (N = 71) and a test cohort (N = 30). All patients underwent MRI, including the MAGiC (SyMRI) sequence. Intra‐tumoral volumes of interest (VOIs) were clustered into three spatial habitats using the K‐means algorithm. Radiomics models were developed for the entire tumor and clustered intra‐tumor regions, based on features from synthetic quantitative T1 and T2 maps. The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was used to interpret predictions. A radiomics nomogram was constructed by integrating independent variables, including the radiomics score and MRI‐reported status. Predictive performance was evaluated using AUC, DeLong's test, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe habitat with the highest T1 and a moderately low T2 values within the tumor showed the best predictive performance, compared to whole‐tumor radiomics (training: 0.87 vs. 0.82; test: 0.74 vs. 0.62). The radiomics nomogram, combining radiomics features and independent clinical variables, outperformed clinical diagnosis alone in both the training cohort (0.92 vs. 0.80; p = 0.0023) and test cohort (0.84 vs. 0.73; p = 0.0362).ConclusionThe habitat‐based radiomics signature, integrating SyMRI‐derived quantitative T1 and T2 maps, provides enhanced predictive accuracy for preoperative lymph node metastasis in oral cancer compared to clinical diagnosis.Keywords: habitat, lymph node metastasis, radiomics, SyMRI

Introduction

Oral cancers, accounting for approximately 3%–5% of all malignancies, are widely acknowledged as one of the most prevalent global malignancies, with an estimated 25,8440 new diagnoses and 14,1465 deaths in Asia in 2022 [1]. The presence of lymph node metastasis is significantly associated with a poor prognosis, leading to a 50% reduction in overall survival rates. Performing upfront elective neck dissection (END) can effectively avoid missed diagnoses, whereas unnecessary END may carry surgical risks and potential complications including damage to the accessory nerve, trapezius muscle dysfunction, and impaired shoulder function [2]. Additionally, the current studies on preoperative methods for assessing lymph node metastasis are inadequate in achieving a balance between precise and time‐efficient evaluations due to the complexity of methodologies and multiple sequences. Therefore, it is imperative to identify an accurate and feasible preoperative assessment method for determining the metastatic status, which would effectively guide treatment strategies and facilitate the evaluation of prognostic outcomes.

Magnetic Resonance Image Compilation (MAGiC), a type of Synthetic MRI (SyMRI), enables acquisition of both synthetic morphologic and quantitative images in a single scanning session with no contrast injection [3]. The combination of quantitative relaxation values could reflect specific tissue characteristics that are correlated with different physiological properties. Typically, quantitative T1 and T2 values are widely used for differential diagnosis and prediction of pathological features in various cancers [4, 5]. Previous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic potential of quantitative T1 and T2 maps in evaluating metastasis, showing superior performance compared to conventional sequences [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. However, limited research has explored the role of quantitative maps in predicting lymph node metastasis in primary oral cancer.

Radiomics integrates high‐throughput image‐based features with clinical data, pathology, and molecular biomarkers, providing deeper insights into tumor development and aligning closely with clinical reality [11, 12, 13]. However, conventional radiomics solely captures overall tumor characteristics from images and fails to capture the spatial heterogeneity of tumors. To address this limitation, a novel approach divides tumors into subregions known as habitats, containing voxels with similar attributes and consistent tumor biology [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of habitat analysis in predicting molecular expression, disease progression, treatment response, and survival in cancers such as high‐grade serous ovarian cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer using PET/CT and MRI [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Therefore, we hypothesize that analyzing radiomics features from primary intra‐tumor regions with quantitative technology will enhance lymph node metastasis prediction accuracy and reveal associations between primary tumor quantitative values and metastatic progression.

The objectives of this study were as follows: (1) Investigate the predictive potential of radiomics signatures from intra‐tumor habitats using T1 and T2 maps derived from SyMRI, compared to a traditional radiomics model based on the entire tumor; (2) Develop and validate a comprehensive radiomics nomogram model incorporating both radiomics and clinical signatures.

Material and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital. Written informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the nature of this retrospective study (SH9H‐2024‐T487‐1). All patients diagnosed with oral cancer were recruited between April 2023 and July 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients clinically diagnosed with primary oral cancer; (2) patients who underwent preoperative MRI using the MAGiC sequence. Exclusion criteria included: (1) poor image quality; (2) absence of clinical and pathological reports; (3) prior treatment before MRI scanning; (4) presence of concurrent malignant tumors; (5) failure in image reconstruction from the dedicated workstation. The flowchart of this study was presented in Figure 1.

The flowchart of this study.

Data Acquisition

The examinations were conducted on a 3.0‐T MR scanner (Premier, GE Healthcare, WI) with a 21‐channel head and neck coil. Synthetic MRI [MAGnetic resonance image Compilation (MAGiC)] images covering the oral cavity were acquired using multiple‐delay‐multiple‐echo (MDME) sequence with two echo times (14/92 msec) and four saturation delay times (170/670/1840/3840 msec). The scan parameters of the MAGiC sequence were: FOV, 240 × 192 mm2; acquisition matrix, 320*256; TR, 4648 ms; echo‐train length (ETL), 16; slice thickness/gap, 3.00/0.3 mm; NEX, 1.00; scan time, 3 min 42 s. Figure 2 illustrates the workflow of this study.

The workflow of radiomics analysis.

Tumor Segmentation

The synthetic MR images were reconstructed off‐line using the dedicated workstation including quantitative T1, T2 maps and T1WI (SyT1WI), T2WI (SyT2WI) derived from the MAGiC sequence. No additional preprocessing was necessary, as SyMRI provided consistently generated images.

For all patients, 3D delineation of the primary tumor contours (volumes of interest, VOIs) was manually performed slice by slice on the SyT2WI using 3D slicer, with reference to the SyT1WI by two experienced radiologists who reached a consensus. All VOIs were defined along the periphery of the lesions, excluding any vessels and adjacent structures. The delineated VOIs were then copied to the T1 and T2 maps.

Habitats Generation

To assess intra‐tumoral heterogeneity, an unsupervised clustering algorithm was applied to the T1, T2 maps. Firstly, a two‐dimensional feature vector, comprising intensity values from the two imaging modalities, was generated for each patient. To optimize clustering performance, the Calinski‐Harabasz scores were used to determine the optimal number of clusters (ranging from 2 to 6). We employed the K‐means algorithm from the scikit‐learn python package (https://scikit‐learn.org/stable/index.html) according to the results of Calinski–Harabasz scores, which facilitated the encoding of voxel‐specific habitats. All the individual voxels were grouped into different clusters based on their similarities using the K‐means algorithm, with squared Euclidean distances between voxel intensities serving as the similarity metric.

Feature Extraction and Selection

Feature extraction was performed using Pyradiomics in Python 3.12.3, yielding 214 features, including shape, first‐order, and texture features, including gray level co‐occurrence matrix‐based features (GLCM), gray level run length matrix‐based features (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix‐based features (GLSZM), Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix‐based features (NGTDM), which were extracted for each subregion, as well as for the entire tumor.

To address data imbalance between positive and negative samples in the training and test cohorts, the Synthetic Minority Over‐sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied. All features were then standardized using the Z‐score method to ensure uniform scaling.

For optimal feature selection, a two‐step approach was employed: (1) Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to identify highly correlated features within each subregion and the entire tumor. Features with an interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) > 0.90 were excluded. (2) The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was used to select features with significant correlations to the labels. The parameter λ was optimized via 10‐fold cross‐validation over 30,000 iterations, retaining features with non‐zero λ values for model construction.

Development and Explanation of Machine Learning Models

Radiomics signatures from sub‐regions (habitat‐n) and the entire tumor were extracted and selected, where ‘n’ denotes different clusters.

Five classifiers including support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) were employed for constructing models. The hyperparameter tuning for each classifier was performed in the training cohort using a Grid‐search algorithm and five‐fold cross‐validation, followed by validation on the test cohort.

The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, a post hoc interpretability method grounded in cooperative game theory, was conducted on the final selected radiomics signatures to elucidate the influence of these features on the model with the highest predictive performance. This analysis quantifies the importance of each feature and its impact on the model's predicted probability through SHAP values to understand the machine learning model in an explainable manner, thereby enhancing model transparency and interpretability [20]. We presented the SHAP values of each selected feature to reflect the varying degrees of influence that each feature has on the model's predictions, and to rank the importance of these features accordingly.

Construction and Validation of Radiomics Nomogram

To enhance the clinical practicability of radiomics, we developed a radiomics‐clinical model by constructing a radiomics nomogram that integrates radiomics signatures with independent clinical factors. The performance of the model was subsequently validated using calibration curves, the Hosmer‐Lemeshow test, and decision curve analysis (DCA). These assessments aimed to assess the concordance between predicted probabilities and actual lymph node metastasis outcomes, and determine clinical utility, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.3, R 4.4.0, and SPSS 16.0. The Mann–Whitney U test and chi‐square test were used to compare clinical characteristics between the training and test cohorts, as well as between lymph node metastasis‐positive and negative groups. DeLong's test was conducted to statistically compare the AUC values. To develop a predictive radiomics nomogram, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted without backward selection to identify independent predictors from variables with a p value < 0.05 in the univariable analysis.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 101 patients were enrolled for the development and validation of the radiomic signature. Patients were randomly assigned to the training (age, 54.32 ± 14.20; male = 52, female = 19; n = 71) and test (age, 53.73 ± 10.93; male = 19, female = 11; n = 30) cohorts, with no significant differences between two cohorts in clinical characteristics, including age (p = 0.344) and gender (p = 0.449). Patient characteristics for both cohorts are summarized in Table 1. No significant difference in lymph node (LN) metastasis prevalence was observed between the cohorts (p > 0.999), with metastasis rates of 35.21% and 33.33%, respectively. Although significant differences in age (p = 0.012) and T stage were noted between the LN metastasis‐positive and negative cohorts in the training cohort, no significant differences were found between the training and test cohorts, regardless of LN metastasis status.

Habitats Generation

The Calinski‐Harabasz score for combining T1–T2 with three clusters yielded the highest value (Figure 3a,b). Consequently, the intra‐tumoral regions were clustered into three sub‐regions using the K‐means algorithm, with cluster 2 displaying the highest T1 and a moderately low T2 values. The clustered results, along with the value differences for each sub‐region, are visually presented in Figure 3c and detailed in the Supporting Information.

Radiomics Signatures Selection

A total of 214 and 642 radiomic features were extracted from the entire tumor and its sub‐regions, respectively. Regarding the habitat signatures, a total of 87, 84, and 85 features were retained for clusters 1, 2, and 3, respectively after performing PCC analysis. Subsequently, a selection of optimal lambda values (0.0857, 0.0757, and 0.0970) resulted in retaining a total of 5, 3, and 5 significant features for each cluster, respectively. Furthermore, the entire tumoral signatures dataset underwent PCC analysis initially resulting in retention of all the initial set of 96 features, followed by selecting only 6 significant features using a lambda value of 0.1242.

Performance of the Machine Learning Models

In line with the predictive performance results for all signatures, the radiomics signatures of habitat‐2 using LightGBM demonstrated the highest performance compared to other sub‐regions and the whole tumor, achieving AUC values of 0.87 (training) and 0.74 (test), thereby highlighting the enhanced performance of habitat radiomics over conventional radiomics (Figure 4a,b, and Table 2). SVM and LR were selected as the classifiers to construct the Habitat‐1 and Habitat‐3 radiomics signatures respectively, while LightGBM was selected as the classifier to construct the Habitat‐2 and whole tumor radiomics signatures. The Lasso selection process and the ROC results for each model in both training and test cohorts were presented in Table S1 and Figure S1–S4. Additionally, the regions covered by habitat‐2 with the highest T1 values and a moderately low T2 values were found to contain the invasive anterior margin of the entire tumor (see Figure 3c–f).

Subsequently, a predictive model based on the rad‐score and independent factor was developed and presented as a radiomics nomogram (Figure 6a).

Compared to the performance of MRI‐reported status, the radiomics nomogram model exhibited a significant enhancement, as evidenced by a significantly higher AUC in both training (0.92 vs. 0.80; p = 0.0023) and test (0.84 vs. 0.73; p = 0.0362) (Figure 4c,d and Table 2). Specifically, the specificity was improved, indicating a reduced probability of false positives. The calibration curve of the radiomics nomogram model demonstrated excellent concordance between predicted and actual probabilities of metastasis status in training (Figure 6b) and test cohort (Figure 6c). Additionally, the Hosmer‐Lemeshow test yielded values of 0.742 (training) and 0.783 (test), indicating a high degree of model calibration.

The DCA was performed to assess the clinical utility of the radiomics nomogram model, radiomics signatures trained with the LightGBM classifier, and clinical independent predictive factors (Figure 6d). The results demonstrated that utilizing the radiomics nomogram model for predicting metastasis would yield greater benefits when the threshold probability of cancer exceeded 10%, as compared to treating all patients or none and relying solely on clinical reports.

Discussion

We have developed and validated a habitat‐based radiomics nomogram with SyMRI for preoperative prediction of lymph node metastasis in oral cancer. The radiomics signatures derived from the intratumor habitats characterized by high T1 values and low T2 values. By incorporating the radiomics signature with clinical MRI‐reported metastasis status into an easily applicable radiomics nomogram, we facilitate a more efficient personalized preoperative prediction of LN metastasis.

A previous study has focused on predicting LN metastasis in head and neck cancers, demonstrating that a combination of radiomics and deep learning (DL) features achieves optimal performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.928, 0.878, 0.796, and 0.834 in the training, test, external validation set 1, and external validation set 2, respectively [21]. One study specifically examined intratumor heterogeneity (ITH) radiomics for predicting occult lymph node metastasis in tongue squamous cell carcinoma, employing fusion models that achieved AUC values ranging from 0.81 to 0.85 across three test sets [22]. These results are comparable to our nomogram model, which demonstrated AUCs of 0.92 and 0.84 in the training and test cohorts, respectively. However, it is important to note that these studies utilized contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted imaging and other MRI sequences, which may limit applicability in patients with renal impairment and reduce examination efficiency. Our research avoids these limitations by conducting habitat analysis using SyMRI to delineate sub‐regions associated with metastasis, efficiently enhancing predictive accuracy without the need for contrast injection. SyMRI facilitates the acquisition of comprehensive quantitative data in a single scan with no contrast injection, thereby significantly improving examination efficiency. The obtained quantitative relaxation values reflect tissue characteristics, thus providing a microscopic perspective of their properties through imaging. The integration of quantitative values has demonstrated its potential for enhanced diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing metastatic from non‐metastatic lymph nodes in head and neck cancers [7, 8]. However, few studies have reported quantitative values for predicting lymph node metastasis by oral primary tumors. ITH drives tumor evolution, resulting in immune evasion and is associated with patient prognosis [23, 24]. Our findings demonstrate that radiomics signatures of the habitat with high T1 and low T2 values exhibit superior performance, surpassing other habitats as well as the entire tumor region, which predominantly contain the tumor front. Elevated T1 values correlate with increased cell proliferation, lymphatic vessel proliferation, and extracellular matrix abundance, while lower T2 values reflect reduced free water content due to increased cellularity and higher nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratios, indicative of malignancy [7, 25, 26, 27]. In our study, the three clustering subregions identified in our study are consistent with findings from previous research [28]. Cluster 2 represents an increased blood perfusion zone that has been shown to be strongly associated with early metastasis and high invasiveness [29, 30]. Previous studies indicate that higher T1 values show a higher accuracy and correlate with the increased vascularization and collagen deposition [31, 32], which in turn, promotes angiogenesis [33]. Cluster 3 corresponds to the tumor necrotic zone, characterized by higher T2 values due to a large number of cell deaths and the expansion of interstitial space. Cluster 1 represents a transitional zone between region 2 and 3. Therefore, the subregion characterized by high T1 values and low T2 values may serve as a critical indicator for predicting metastasis.

By analyzing the effect of radiomics signatures on the prediction, it helps us to understand the association between the quantitative changes of primary tumor and lymph node metastasis. The SHAP analysis revealed the Dependence Variance as the most significant predictors of lymph node metastasis status and higher Dependence Variance contributes positively to the prediction, which is highly interpretable. Higher Dependence Variance in habitat 2 indicates a more heterogeneous gray level dependency, suggesting more aggressive tumor characteristics. Higher heterogeneity may correspond to necrosis‐surviving‐cell junctions, abnormal proliferation of microvessels, and the tumor‐associated microbiota within the tumor. These conditions are often accompanied by metabolic activity and the release of pro‐transfer factors, which aligns with the theory that tumor heterogeneity plays a crucial role in metastasis and tumor development [34, 35]. Additionally, lower Normalized Run Length Non‐Uniformity and lower Imc2 positively contribute to the prediction of metastatic lymph nodes. A lower value of Run Length Non‐Uniformity Normalized signifies less variation in gray levels, often indicating homogeneity within the tumor tissue. However, it should be noted that in certain instances, lower Run Length Non‐Uniformity Normalized may correlate with enhanced infiltration capabilities in specific tumor regions, potentially increasing the likelihood of lymphatic spread, while Imc2 reflects the orderly structure of the tumor, with lower values typically correlating with higher invasiveness and more aggressive tumor behavior. Thus, these radiomics signatures have the potential to serve as potential predictor markers for lymph node metastasis in oral cancers.

There are also certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the sample size is relatively small, and our single‐center design lacks external independent validation, which limits generalizability. Future studies should aim to address this by including a larger dataset for validation. Secondly, the heterogeneity of tumor was analyzed from different habitats in this study. However, the underlying biological mechanisms linking habitat radiomic features to physiological changes remain unclear. These mechanisms could be further elucidated through the integration of multi‐omics studies. Thirdly, a more comprehensive comparison with conventional sequences and other quantitative imaging modalities would further highlight the advantages of SyMRI.

Conclusion

We have developed a habitat‐based radiomics nomogram that incorporates T1 and T2 maps derived from SyMRI, providing a feasible approach and microscopic perspective. This novel approach demonstrates greater predictive capability for accurate preoperative lymph node metastasis assessment as compared to clinical diagnostics.
