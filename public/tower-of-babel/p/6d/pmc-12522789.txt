Term birth and levothyroxine dosage are significant factors associated with permanent congenital hypothyroidism: experience from a medical center in Taiwan
Authors: Yu-En Kao, Wei-Hsin Ting, Yann-Jinn Lee, Chi-Yu Huang, Chao-Hsu Lin, Yi-Lei Wu, Lu-Ting Wang, Chin-Hui Tseng, Yi-Hsin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBefore the introduction of newborn screening, congenital hypothyroidism was the leading cause of intellectual disability in infants and children. Patients with permanent congenital hypothyroidism require lifelong levothyroxine supplementation to prevent intellectual disability and growth failure. With progressively lower thyrotropin (TSH) cutoffs in newborn screening programs, more transient congenital hypothyroidism cases-requiring only temporary treatment-have also been identified. To avoid unnecessary medication use and reduce the burden on healthcare systems, early differentiation is essential.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled congenital hypothyroidism patients born between 2004 and 2018 and followed at MacKay Children’s Hospital and classified them as permanent congenital hypothyroidism or transient congenital hypothyroidism based on levothyroxine dependence. Basic demographic data, including gender, gestational age, birth weight, newborn screening TSH levels, body height and weight, serum free T4, TSH levels, levothyroxine doses at every clinical visit, and age of TSH normalization were collected and compared between permanent and transient congenital hypothyroidism groups.ResultsA total of 152 infants were enrolled in this study, with 73 (48%) classified as permanent congenital hypothyroidism. Term births were more common in permanent than transient congenital hypothyroidism (80% vs. 48%, p < 0.01). TSH normalization took longer in permanent congenital hypothyroidism (75 vs. 45 days, p < 0.01). Serum TSH and levothyroxine doses remained higher in permanent congenital hypothyroidism at 6 months, and at 1, 2, and 3 years. The levothyroxine dose that provided the best discrimination between permanent and transient congenital hypothyroidism was 2.5 𝛍g/kg/d at age 2 years (sensitivity: 73%, specificity: 90%), and 1.8 𝛍g/kg/d at age 3 years (sensitivity: 82%, specificity: 91%).ConclusionsNearly half of the patients required lifelong levothyroxine supplements under the current newborn screening program. Permanent congenital hypothyroidism patients were more likely term-born, showed delayed TSH normalization, had higher TSH levels, and required higher levothyroxine doses during follow-up. The best cut-off level to discriminate permanent from transient congenital hypothyroidism was 2.5 and 1.8 µg/kg/day at the age of 2 and 3 years, respectively.

Background

Congenital hypothyroidism (CH) is a significant endocrine disease affecting children worldwide [1]. In the past, it was one of the most common causes of intellectual disability, and if left untreated, could also lead to growth retardation and short stature [2, 3]. Fortunately, the implementation of newborn screening had effectively prevented these adverse clinical outcomes. Before the introduction of screening programs between 1963 and 1978, approximately 1 in 6,100 to 1 in 6,900 births in developed European countries were diagnosed and treated for CH [4]. According to a study by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the prevalence of CH more than doubled following the introduction of newborn screening [4].

In the 1970 s, newborn screening of CH was first introduced in North America [5]. Subsequently, nationwide newborn screening for CH was implemented in Taiwan in1984, utilizing heel prick blood samples on filter paper to measure thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels. As the TSH threshold was gradually lowered to >7.5 µU/mL at the initial screening and >7.0 µU/mL at the repeat screening over time, the number of confirmed CH cases increased. One study demonstrated that for each 1µU/mL decrease in the TSH cutoff value, there was a 2.96% increase in CH incidence in China, rising from 4.01 per 10,000 in 2012 to 5.77 per 10,000 in 2019 [6]. Similarly, another study reported an increasing incidence of CH in the United States, from 1 per 4,094 births in 1987 to 1 per 2,372 births in 2002 [7].

The incidence of congenital hypothyroidism detected through newborn screening in Taiwan between 1988 and 2008 was approximately 1 in 2,000 [8], while the prevalence of permanent primary congenital hypothyroidism was reported to be approximately 1 in 5,800 during 1988–1990 [9]. Contrary to severe cases requiring lifelong levothyroxine supplementation, transient congenital hypothyroidism (TCH) that necessitate only temporary treatment have also been identified by current newborn screening program. Previous studies have reported that 11% to 78% CH patients identified by newborn screening program were later classified as permanent congenital hypothyroidism (PCH), requiring long term follow-up [5, 10–21]. Studies from Italy and Korea estimated the proportion of PCH to be 52.7% and 53% for those CH diagnosed by newborn screening [18, 20]. The consensus guidelines for congenital hypothyroidism by ENDO-European Reference Network recommend re-evaluating hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis at the of age 2 to 3 years in cases where PCH is not clearly diagnosed during early infancy, particularly in infants with gland in situ [1]. The timing is intended to ensure proper neurodevelopment and optimal growth.

Previous studies have identified various predictors for distinguishing the PCH group from the TCH group, including birth history, maternal history, gender, newborn screening (NBS) TSH, serum free T4, serum TSH levels, and levothyroxine dose at a specific age [5, 10–26]. Several studies have highlighted NBS TSH level as a strong predictor of PCH [5, 12, 14, 20, 22]. Some have also suggested that the initial TSH level may serve as an early indicator of PCH [13, 17], while others reported that follow-up serum TSH levels could aid in identifying PCH [11]. Additionally, nearly all studies reviewed emphasized the role of levothyroxine dose as an important predictor of PCH [5, 10–17, 19–22].

However, prior research has yielded inconsistent results, reflecting substantial variability across studies. The prevalence of permanent congenital hypothyroidism in Taiwan was last documented 30 years ago [9], and no study has yet identified specific factors distinguishing PCH from TCH in Taiwan. Therefore, our study aimed to determine the proportion of PCH cases in a tertiary medical center in northern Taiwan and to identify key predictors for PCH under current newborn screening program.

Methods

We retrospective collected data on patients with CH born between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2018, based on the International Classification of Disease (ICD)−9 codes 241, or ICD-10 codes E03.0 and E03.1. Those patients with the following criteria (1. coding errors; 2. missing critical data, such as newborn screening TSH levels or initial venous FT4 and TSH measurements; or 3. presence of major anomalies, such as Down syndrome, Williams syndrome, Sotos syndrome, and VACTERAL association) were excluded from our analysis.

Diagnosis of congenital hypothyroidism.

CH patients were initially identified via the national newborn screening program. In Taiwan, dried blood spot samples were generally collected on the third day of life, with repeat sampling if necessary, especially in preterm infants (Table 1). Abnormal results were defined as TSH > 7.5 µU/mL at the initial screening and > 7.0 µU/mL at the repeat screening, irrespective of gestational age. Newborns with abnormal neonatal screening results were referred to a designated referral medical center for confirmatory testing. At the referral center, serum free T4 and TSH levels were measured to establish the diagnosis.

CH was confirmed if the serum free T4 concentration was below the age-specific reference range, while TSH was clearly elevated. In cases with normal FT4 but elevated TSH, we performed serial follow-up tests. CH was diagnosed when TSH remained elevated with a downward trend in FT4. Free T4 levels were measured using a radioimmunoassay (IZOTOP, Budapest, Hungary; reference range: 0.8–1.77 ng/dL), and TSH levels were assessed using a radioimmunometric assay (IZOTOP, Budapest, Hungary; reference range: 0.30–4.00 µIU/mL).

Study design

Levothyroxine monotherapy was initiated immediately upon the diagnosis of congenital hypothyroidism. An initial serum thyroid function assessment was scheduled one to two weeks after treatment initiation. Once euthyroid status was achieved, the follow-up interval was extended to one to two months. Long-term clinical and biochemical monitoring was conducted at three-month intervals. Thyroid sonography was performed to assess whether the thyroid gland was normally located.

Around the age of two to three years, levothyroxine tapering and discontinuation were considered for patients whose dosage remained stable or decreased over time. Though trial-off strategies varied slightly across physicians, complete discontinuation was rarely immediate unless the dose was already minimal. During the withdrawal trial, serum free T4 and TSH, were performed every one to three months. A minimum follow-up period of one year after levothyroxine cessation was required. If FT4 dropped below the normal range or TSH exceeded 10 µIU/mL at any time, levothyroxine was promptly reintroduced and the patients were classified as PCH. If TSH remained between 4 and 10 µIU/mL with normal FT4, treatment was withheld and patients were observed. If thyroid function remained stable and growth was appropriate throughout the observation period, the patients were diagnosed with TCH. In brief, patients were classified into permanent congenital hypothyroidism (PCH) or transient congenital hypothyroidism (TCH) groups based on whether levothyroxine was successfully discontinued.

We collected data on patients’ gender, gestational age, birth weight, NBS TSH levels, initial serum TSH and free T4 levels. Additionally, body height, body weight, serum free T4 and TSH levels, and levothyroxine doses at each clinical visit were recorded. The time required for TSH normalization to < 4µIU/mL was also calculated and compared between groups. We also performed subgroup analyses according to term status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 26 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The prevalence of PCH was determined by calculating the proportion of patients who were unable to discontinue levothyroxine at approximately three years of age, divided by the total number of enrolled patients. Differences between groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square (χ2) test for categorical variables. Continuous data were expressed as the median (interquartile range), while categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To determine the optimal cutoff values for continuous biochemical predictors, receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve were performed. The optimal cutoff for each parameter was identified by maximizing the Youden index. A multiple logistic regression model was employed to access the association between PCH and significant predictors identified in the univariate analysis.

Results

A total of 449 patients initially met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 194 cases were miscoded, 85 cases lacked critical laboratory data, and 18 cases were associated with major congenital anomalies (Fig. 1). The high proportion of miscoded cases may be attributed to the spontaneous entry of ICD codes alongside thyroid function tests in newborns. Missing data were primarily due to early lost follow-up or transferal from other healthcare facilities at an older age.

Of the 152 children enrolled, 79 (52%) successfully discontinued levothyroxine and were classified as the TCH group. The remaining 73 (48%) either failed to discontinue levothyroxine or required increasing doses over time and were classified as the PCH group (Fig. 1). Among the 79 patients with TCH, 17 discontinued treatment before the age of 2, typically because of very low LT4 requirements or persistently high-normal FT4 levels, which allowed earlier tapering. Most of the remaining patients discontinued treatment between 2 and 3 years of age. Thyroid ultrasound was performed in 142 patients. Abnormal findings, including thyroid hypoplasia, agenesis, ectopia, or hemiagenesis, were more frequently reported in the PCH group (PCH vs. TCH, 45% vs. 11%, p < 0.01). Generally, there were no significant differences in height or weight between the two groups during follow-up (Table 2).

Gestational age was significantly higher in the PCH group compared to the TCH group (PCH vs. TCH, 38.9 vs. 36.9 weeks, p < 0.01), indicating a higher proportion of term infants in the PCH group (PCH vs. TCH, 80 vs. 48%, p < 0.01). Patients in the PCH group also exhibited higher NBS TSH and initial serum TSH levels (NBS TSH: PCH vs. TCH, 36.3 vs. 25.1 µIU/mL, p = 0.14; initial serum TSH: PCH vs. TCH, 62.8 vs. 5.9 µIU/mL, p = 0.13), however, these differences were not statistically significant. Serum free T4 levels at diagnosis did not differ significantly between the two groups (PCH vs. TCH, 0.86 vs. 0.78 ng/dL, p = 0.17). The age at which TSH normalized to < 4 mU/mL was significantly higher in the PCH group (PCH vs. TCH, 75 vs. 45 days old, p < 0.01). This pattern was consistent in analyses stratified by term status. The optimal age for TSH normalization to differentiate between PCH and TCH was 62 days, determined by ROC curve and Youden index (sensitivity: 58%, specificity: 80%).

At 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years of age, serum TSH levels remained significantly elevated in the PCH group, while no significant differences were observed in serum free T4 levels (Table 2). Levothyroxine dosages were also significantly higher in the PCH group compared to the TCH group across the follow-up period (Fig. 2; Table 2). The Youden index identified the best cut-off values for distinguishing between PCH and TCH as 2.5 µg/kg/day at 2 years of age (sensitivity: 73%, specificity: 90%) and 1.8 µg/kg/day at 3 years of age (sensitivity: 82%, specificity: 91%) (Fig. 3). Similar results were observed when analyses were stratified by term and preterm infants.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that nearly half of CH patients had permanent congenital hypothyroidism under current newborn screening program. The proportion of term babies was higher in the PCH group. Although NBS TSH and initial TSH levels were higher in the PCH group, the differences were not statistically significant. PCH patients spent more time to achieve TSH normalization while receiving levothyroxine supplementation. Throughout the follow-up period, both serum TSH levels and levothyroxine dosages were consistently higher in the PCH group. A levothyroxine dosage exceeding 2.5 µg/kg/day at 2 years of age and 1.8 µg/kg/day at 3 years of age were served as potential indicators of PCH.

Our study depicted that nearly half of CH patients identified by current newborn screening program were classified as PCH, consistent with findings from studies in France (46%), Turkey (45–46%), Iran (46%), and China (50%) [13, 19, 27–29].Recent studies have reported a global PCH proportion in CH ranging from 11% to 78% [5, 10–21]. This wide variation may be attributed to differences in inclusion and exclusion criteria, such as gestational age and the presence of thyroid dysgenesis. A study in Korea included only patients with eutopic thyroid glands and a high proportion of preterm births, which contributed to a lower PCH percentage of 11.3% [12]. In Germany, a study restricted to preterm infants born at < 32 weeks of gestation found a PCH percentage of 38% [30]. In contrast, a study in Japan excluded preterm infants, maternal thyroid disease, and patients who discontinued levothyroxine supplementation within the first year of life, leading to a higher PCH percentage of 75.8% [16].

Our study revealed that term infants were more prevalent in the PCH group, with significantly higher gestational age and birth weight, consistent with finding from a study conducted in Zurich [17]. It is well established that hypothyroidism is more common in preterm than in term babies [31]. The hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis in preterm neonates is immature, and free T4 concentrations decrease in proportion to the degree of prematurity [32]. Most preterm infants exhibit a transient postnatal elevation of serum TSH, which plays a role in stimulating the immature thyroid gland to achieve normal T4 production [33]. Some preterm infants may show a more pronounced and delayed TSH surge [34]. These TSH elevations are identified through repeated newborn screening specifically designed for preterm populations [33, 35]. Although congenital hypothyroidism can be diagnosed in preterm infants, these patients are more likely to successfully discontinue levothyroxine therapy later in life [31]. Our data revealed that term infants diagnosed with CH are more likely to require lifelong treatment, both in univariate and multivariate analyses.

While both NBS TSH and initial serum TSH levels were generally higher in the PCH group, these differences did not reach statistical significance in our analysis. Clinicians are actively seeking early predictors to differentiate TCH from PCH, aiming to prevent overtreatment and reduce unnecessary burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems. Although many studies have emphasized the importance of NBS TSH levels and initial serum TSH levels in this differentiation [5, 10–14, 17, 20, 22], considerable overlap between the two groups has been observed [16, 24]. Consequently, these parameters may not serve as reliable predictors for PCH, as demonstrated in our study.

Our study showed that PCH patients required an extended period to normalize TSH levels after levothyroxine therapy, and delayed normalization of TSH levels beyond two months was more frequently observed in patients with PCH. Nevertheless, the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of this time-based criterion remained suboptimal. Our findings are consistent with those of a Korean study, which also reported a significant longer time to TSH normalization in patients with PCH [20]. This delayed normalization may serve as a potential predictor in clinical practice.

Our data indicated that during every clinical visit, serum TSH levels and levothyroxine dosages remained higher in the PCH group. Besides, we also demonstrated that levothyroxine dosage at 2 and 3 years of age would be good predictors for PCH. Similar findings were reported in Iran and Korea, who also observed elevated serum TSH levels during follow-up [11, 20]. Several European cohorts have also proposed dose-based thresholds that align with our findings. A study from Macedonia identified a levothyroxine dose of < 2.8 µg/kg/day at 2 years as predictive of TCH [14]. In Germany, a dose < 2.0 µg/kg/day at 2 years identified TCH with 99% sensitivity [15]. Italian studies similarly reported that doses >2.25 µg/kg/day at 2 years were associated with PCH (60% sensitivity, 68% specificity) [18], and that doses < 2.47 µg/kg/day at 2 years predicted TCH (55% sensitivity, 85% specificity) [36]. A German study focusing on very preterm infants showed that at 2 years, the highest dose in TCH patients was 2.5 µg/kg/day, whereas the lowest in PCH patients was 2.8 µg/kg/day [30]. In addition, a recent review published in the European Thyroid Journal (“50 years of newborn screening for congenital hypothyroidism”) emphasized that, in clinical practice, re-evaluation at 24 months should be considered when the LT4 dose remains < 2.5 µg/kg/day [37]. Collectively, these European data support a practical threshold around 2.0–2.5 µg/kg/day at 2 years for distinguishing TCH from PCH, consistent with our cut-off of 2.5 µg/kg/day. A Japanese study further reported that a dose < 1.3 µg/kg/day at 3 years indicated TCH [25]. Notably, our study included a larger sample size and demonstrated similarly high sensitivity and specificity, supporting the reliability and clinical relevance of our findings. In our multivariable logistic regression analysis, even after adjusting for term status, the levothyroxine dose at 3 years remained the strongest independent predictor of PCH. In summary, patients requiring more than 1.8 µg/kg/day of levothyroxine at 3 years of age are more likely to have PCH and require lifelong hormone replacement.

The strength of this study was its relatively large sample size and longer follow-up duration. Additionally, it is the first study in Taiwan to investigate potential predictive factors for distinguishing PCH from TCH. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective nature of the study resulted in some missing data. Family history, including maternal thyroid status and exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, was incomplete. Second, genetic analysis was not routinely performed at the time of diagnosis, which would be potentially better predictor for PCH or TCH. Furthermore, variations in clinical management over time, including differences in trial-off strategies and levothyroxine dosage calibration, may have influenced the findings. Further research is needed to establish a clearer clinical flowchart for early differentiation between PCH and TCH.

In conclusion, nearly half of CH patients had PCH under the current newborn screening program. Infants in the PCH group were more likely to be born at term and had greater gestational age and birth weight. The time required for TSH normalization was longer in the PCH group. Additionally, at 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years of age, serum TSH levels and levothyroxine dose remained higher in the PCH group. The optimal cutoff dose for differentiating PCH from TCH was 2.5 µg/kg/day at 2 years of age and 1.8 µg/kg/day at 3 years of age.
