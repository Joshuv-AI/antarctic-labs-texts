A cross-sectional study of patients’ morbidity profiles and doctors’ drug prescribing patterns from outpatient departments of a community health centre from a district of Uttarakhand
Authors: Puneet Ohri, Megha Luthra, Mohd. Anshar, Sachin Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:A study of the pattern of disease occurrence provides inputs for diagnosis and treatment, and is a tool for administrators and policy makers. Periodic prescription analysis increases treatment effectiveness, provides feedback and decreases adverse effects.Materials and Methods:All patients attending the outpatient departments (OPDs) of the selected community health centre during the study period were included. A study of daily OPD records was done for morbidity pattern and its associated factors. Patient interview and prescription analysis of every second patient in each OPD, till a maximum cap of ten patients per OPD was reached, was done.Results:The study included 9631 patients [5316 (55.2%) females and 4315 (44.8%) males]. The highest age frequency was in the 20–29 year age group (2198, 22.8%). Maximum patients were diagnosed with infections (1405, 14.6%), followed by digestive disorders (1144, 11.9%). Six thousand five hundred thirty (67.8%) patients had single morbidity, 2267 (23.5%) had two comorbidities and 834 (8.7%) had more than two comorbidities.Conclusion:Certain infections and parasitic diseases were diagnosed; diseases of the digestive system (International Classification of Diseases 11th revision) were highest. Use of generic drugs, medicines from the National Essential Medicines List and legibility of handwriting were lower. Locally prevalent diseases were significantly associated with sex and solar calendar months.

Introduction

The effect of age, sex and environmental factors like calendar months on diseases has been recognized for long[1] and is the basis of ‘public health practice’.[2] The influence of seasons on health was documented by 460–377 BC in the writings of Hippocrates.[3] It is also known that drugs prescribed by generic names and the number of drugs from the National Essential Medicines List of India are low.[4] A study of the pattern of occurrence of diseases in a healthcare setting provides:

Inputs to physicians for diagnosis and treatment.A tool for administrators to monitor the progress of programs and to optimize the allocation of limited resources.A source of information for policy makers to reduce morbidity, mortality and overall burden of disease.

An assessment of morbidity patterns at a community health centre (CHC) provides real-world data of most common diseases, seasonal trends and co-morbidities. This allows better diagnosis, planning, and resource allocation. Periodic appraisal of prescribing patterns improves the effectiveness of treatment, provides valuable feedback to prescribers and decreases adverse effects. Prescription audit also helps to identify gaps in rational drug use. There are very few such studies from CHCs. Hence, this study was planned in OPDs (outpatient departments) of a CHC of Dehradun. District Dehradun has six CHCs spread over a population of about 1.69 million in seven tehsils and six community developmental blocks. As per CRMI (Compulsory Rotating Medical Internship) regulations,[5] interns posted in Community Medicine should also learn at CHC/District Hospital and were therefore involved in this work. Hospital records were the most appropriate source of information due to easy availability and resource friendliness. Space constraints made detailed patient interviews difficult.

The aim of this work was to study patients’ morbidity profiles and doctor’s prescriptions among attending OPDs of selected CHC to benefit patients, clinicians and planners and by strengthening evidence-based, safe and affordable care at the secondary care level. The findings offer a scope of direct feedback to practitioners for improving prescription quality, reducing adverse drug events and lowering treatment costs.

Materials and Methods

This study was a collaboration between the medical college and CHC. Institutional (IEC) Institutional Ethics Committee clearance was obtained for the study.

Study design

Cross-sectional analytical.

Study setting

This study was carried out in the OPDs of a CHC of Dehradun, where our interns are posted as per CRMI regulations.

Study population

All patients attending the OPDs of CHC Raipur during the study period constituted the study population. Among patients, those meeting following inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected:

1. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria: Written consent were included of the patients who attended the OPDs during the time of study and those who gave informed. Prescriptions of patients advised for admission were excluded. Prescriptions with ‘continue same treatment’ were excluded.

Study groups

Patients attending Medicine, Paediatrics, Gynaecology/Obstetrics and Surgery OPDs of CHC Raipur (four study groups).

Sample size

Total enumeration from records was done daily for the study of morbidity pattern and its associated factors. Patient interviews and prescription analysis of every second patient in each OPD, till a maximum cap of 10 patients per OPD has been reached, was done to study doctors’ drug prescribing patterns.

Study duration

One year (June 2024 to May 2025).

Data collection methods

(a) Interns posted to CHC from the Department of Community Medicine were data collectors for this study. They were imparted training in the Department of Community Medicine by investigators before starting data collection after informed written consent.(b) They studied OPD records daily after work and did data collection from there in the patient working proforma.(c) They did patient interviews and prescription analysis of every second patient in their respective OPD, till a maximum cap of 10 patients per OPD had been reached, and recorded in a separate proforma.(d) Both the working proformas were validated by two faculty members who were not a part of the study.

Data analysis

Data from proformas were entered into Microsoft Excel and then copied to IBM SPSS Statistics Version 30.0.0.0 (172). Percentages and proportions: Chi-square test of significance was used for deriving inferences. Latter was used because variables under study were categorical, and the objective was to determine whether a significant association exists between them. The test is nonparametric and compares observed frequencies with expected frequencies under the null hypothesis of no association. The confidence level was kept as 95%.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study analysed morbidity patterns and prescribing practices among 9,631 outpatients attending four OPDs of a CHC in Dehradun, Uttarakhand. The highest patient load was in the Medicine OPD (38.9%), and the most affected age group was 20-29 years. Major morbidities were infections (14.6%), digestive disorders (11.9%) and pregnancy/childbirth-related conditions among females. The study also demonstrated an average of 2.9 drugs per prescription, suboptimal generic and NLEM use, and occasional prescription legibility issues. Seasonal variation, OPD and sex-based differences in morbidity patterns were also evident.

A district hospital in Madhya Pradesh similarly reported the greatest patient load in the Medicine OPD with respiratory tract infections (16,890, 53%) most common, followed by abdominal pain in Surgery OPD (9,092, 44.8%), severe anaemia (461, 32.1%) in OBG and pneumonia and diarrhoea (41%) in Paediatrics.[6] The differences are explainable due to the use of ICD-11 classification in our study.

A tertiary care hospital in Tamil Nadu showed pain abdomen was the commonest symptom in 79 (7.82%), followed by knee pain in 62 (6.14%) and cough/cold in 54 (5.35%) patients. Among diagnoses, musculoskeletal problems predominated in 250 (24.77%) patients, followed by gastrointestinal diagnoses in 100 (9.91%) patients.[7] Variation in our study may be because of the different study settings of both studies.

A Sri Lankan study from primary and tertiary level OPDs showed the former had common cold (23.6%) and the latter had skin complaints (13.5%) most frequently. Dermatological symptoms including wounds and animal bites were 15.6% and 30.4%, respectively (χ2 = 22.404, P = 0.00001).[8] The discrepancy with our study may be due to different diagnostic criteria and settings.

In a general hospital of Bangladesh, the commonest symptom was indigestion (69%) and the highest diagnosis was for peptic ulcer disease (38%).[9] The difference may be due to our study in a hilly area. Morbidity profiles reported from other CHCs and secondary care hospitals corroborate our findings.[1011]

Prescription practices in our study showed rational drug use, comparable to other Indian studies. In a teaching hospital of north India, average drugs per patient were four resulting in drug interactions. Prescriptions with generic drugs were 271 (11%) and NLEM drugs 1120 (45.8%). Two hundred and sixty (40%) patients had knowledge of the correct dosage and route of administration.[12] Our study’s result is lower as compared to polypharmacy due to efforts to adhere to global standards.

In a teaching institute of central India, average drugs per prescription were 2.89, 65.69% drugs were by generic name and 75.37% from NLEM and comparable to other studies. Ninety-nine percent prescriptions were legible.[13] This is similar to our study.

In paediatric outpatients of Nagpur, 1339 (38.9%) drugs were from NLEM.[14] In another study, 100% patients knew the correct dosage schedule (strength, dosage form, duration, route); 4% of drugs were generic.[15]

In an Indian referral public hospital, the sex ratio was 1.69 male to 1 female visit. This was different from our study as the OBG OPD was excluded.[16]

In Chandigarh, morbidities peaked in the rainy season (23628, 36.1%). The seasonal pattern changed over two consecutive years. (PUO) Pyrexia of unknown Origin was highest in rainy (51.3%) and lowest in the summer season (20.6%) (P = 0.00). (ADD) Acute Diarrheal Diseases cases were maximum in the rainy season (41.3%) (P = 0.02).[17] Our study showed significant differences in diagnoses distribution over months.

From weighted National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey data in the USA, 10 of 23 diagnostic clusters showed statistically significant seasonal variability (P ≤ 0.001) between winter and summer. Rhinitis showed more spring to autumn; coronary artery disease showed more summer to spring variation.[18]

In a Nigerian university clinic, there were 3.8 drugs per prescription, 69.9% generic drugs, and 96.1% essential list drugs.[19]

Multiple recent studies support our findings on rational prescribing practices. Prescription audits improve rational drug use,[20] and WHO prescribing indicators show a persistent scope for improvement at the primary care level.[19] Other studies showed high adherence to (EML) Essential Medicines List and generic prescribing,[2021] whereas the Jan Aushadhi scheme significantly improved affordability and access to essential medicines.[22]

WHO prescription criteria analysis from primary care centres[23] and generic drug prescribing patterns from North India showed rational use of generics and essential medicines,[24] consistent with our results. Seasonal variability in OPD attendance and morbidity patterns has been documented in Chandigarh,[17] the United States,[18] Sri Lanka[10] and multicentre studies in India.[25]

Weather-related changes in patient load have also been shown recently.[26] Our findings align with these studies, demonstrating significant monthly variation in ICD-11-based morbidity patterns.

Implications of these comparative findings are:

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that infections, digestive disorders, and pregnancy/childbirth-related conditions constitute the major morbidity burden at the CHC level, with the highest patient load in the Medicine OPD and the most affected age group being 20-29 years. Significant seasonal and sex-related variation in morbidity patterns highlights the importance of continuous surveillance.

Prescribing indicators revealed an average of 2.9 drugs per prescription, rational prescribing trends and good adherence to NLEM and generic prescribing, although occasional prescription legibility issues were noted. The use of ICD-11 improved diagnostic precision and enabled meaningful comparison with national and international studies. Comparative analysis confirmed that morbidity and prescribing patterns at the CHC level are consistent with trends from other primary and secondary care settings.

Overall, the study underscores the relevance of data-driven decision-making at secondary care facilities for improving service delivery, rational drug use and patient outcomes.

Limitations

The study provides valuable evidence but is limited by a single-centre setting, reliance on OPD records, and a cross-sectional design.
