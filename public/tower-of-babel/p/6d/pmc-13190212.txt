Clinical impact of coinfection with SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and respiratory syncytial virus among adults: a multicenter retrospective cohort study [2021–2025]
Authors: Yuying Xing, Amit Bahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoinfections involving severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), influenza virus, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) may contribute to increased healthcare utilization and disease severity, particularly during periods of viral co-circulation. However, data on their combined impact in adult populations remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the association between monoinfection and coinfection with SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and/or RSV and the risk of hospital admission and severe clinical outcomes among adults presenting to the emergency department (ED).MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study conducted across eight hospitals in southeastern Michigan. We included all adults (≥18 years) with an ED encounter between January 1, 2021 and May 31, 2025, who received a principal diagnosis of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), influenza, or RSV. The primary outcome was hospital admission following the ED visit. The secondary outcome was a composite severe outcome, defined as intensive care unit (ICU) admission, mechanical ventilation, or in-hospital death. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations, with adjustment for age, sex, race, comorbidities, vaccination status, and respiratory virus season of encounter. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed, including a six-group infection classification and a prespecified subgroup of adults aged ≥65 years.ResultsAmong 68,697 adult ED encounters, 52,922 (77.0%) involved COVID-19 only, 13,996 (20.4%) influenza only, 1,356 (2.0%) RSV only, and 423 (0.6%) coinfections. In adjusted analyses, coinfection was associated with higher odds of admission compared to influenza only [adjusted odds ratio (aOR), 0.55; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.54–0.57] and COVID-19 only (aOR, 0.86; 95% CI: 0.84–0.89). Severe outcomes occurred in 3.5% of cases and were most frequent among RSV-only and coinfected patients, coinfection had a significantly higher hazard compared to influenza only [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 0.48; 95% CI: 0.25–0.90] and RSV only (aHR 0.45; 95% CI: 0.21–0.95). Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of findings.ConclusionsCoinfection with SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and/or RSV was associated with increased odds of hospital admission, particularly in older adults and when RSV was involved. Although severe outcomes were relatively uncommon, coinfected patients had a significantly higher hazard of severe outcomes compared with those infected with influenza or RSV alone. These findings support the need for ongoing viral surveillance, multiplex diagnostics, and preventive strategies during periods of respiratory virus co-circulation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Respiratory viral coinfection in adults was uncommon but associated with higher odds of hospital admission compared with coronavirus disease 2019-only and influenza-only infections.

• Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection, alone or in combination with another virus, conferred the highest admission risk, particularly among adults aged ≥65 years.

• Severe outcomes were infrequent overall; however, coinfection was associated with a higher hazard of severe outcomes compared with influenza-only and RSV-only infections.

What is known and what is new?

• Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, influenza, and RSV cause substantial morbidity in adults, but the clinical impact of viral coinfection remains incompletely defined.

• In a large, multicenter adult cohort spanning five respiratory seasons, viral coinfection—especially involving RSV—was associated with increased hospitalization risk, filling a key evidence gap in adult emergency care.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

Report here about implications and actions needed.

• RSV and viral coinfection should be recognized as markers of elevated hospitalization risk in adults presenting with respiratory illness.

• During periods of viral co-circulation, broader use of multiplex diagnostics, strengthened surveillance, and coordinated vaccination strategies may improve triage, preparedness, and prevention, particularly for older and high-risk patients.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza, and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) are leading causes of acute viral respiratory illness worldwide. Collectively, these pathogens place a substantial burden on healthcare systems and society at large. Each year in the United States, RSV and influenza account for hundreds of thousands of emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalizations, particularly among infants, older adults, and individuals with chronic medical conditions. Similarly, COVID-19 has led to recurring waves of severe illness, driving widespread morbidity, mortality, and prolonged strain on hospital capacity (1-4). The direct and indirect healthcare costs associated with these infections—including hospitalization, intensive care utilization, and post-acute sequelae—are considerable and continue to challenge public health preparedness and response efforts.

While monoinfection with a single virus [RSV, influenza, or severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)] remains the most common clinical presentation, coinfections involving two or more of these pathogens have also been reported. With the overlapping circulation of these viruses—particularly during peak respiratory seasons—the possibility of coinfection is a growing concern. Early evidence suggests that coinfections may impact disease severity, length of stay, and clinical outcomes, but data remain limited and at times conflicting. Some studies have suggested additive or synergistic effects of coinfection on respiratory compromise, while others have not demonstrated a clear difference in outcomes compared to monoinfection (5-7). Furthermore, most available research has focused on pediatric populations or single-center experiences, leaving significant gaps in our understanding of coinfection dynamics and their clinical implications in broader and more diverse patient populations.

Given these uncertainties, a clearer understanding of the clinical consequences of coinfection is urgently needed. In this study, we aim to compare the outcomes of ED patients with viral coinfections (RSV, influenza, and/or COVID-19) to those with monoinfections in a health system. By characterizing the epidemiology and impact of coinfections in a real-world inpatient setting across a broader population, we seek to better demonstrate the risk of more severe outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2682/rc).

Methods

Study design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted across EDs in eight hospitals within the Corewell Health East system in southeastern Michigan. These sites included both community-based hospitals and a large academic tertiary care center, representing a diverse healthcare setting. The study aimed to assess the clinical impact of coinfections involving COVID-19, influenza, and RSV among adult patients presenting for emergency care.

Selection of participants

We included all adult patients (aged ≥18 years) who presented to one of the eight participating EDs between January 1, 2021 and May 31, 2025, and had a principal discharge diagnosis of COVID-19, influenza, or RSV. Diagnosis was determined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes assigned at the conclusion of the ED encounter. Patients who had tested positive for one of the three viruses more than 28 days prior to the ED encounter were excluded because it was considered non-acute. The protocol was approved by the Corewell Health Institutional Review Board (IRB# 221-118), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research and minimal risk to participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All participating hospitals were informed of and agreed to the study.

Data source

Patient data were extracted from the Epic electronic health record (EHR) system (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, USA), used across all Corewell Health East hospitals. This EHR system captures real-time clinical, demographic, laboratory, and outcome data for all ED encounters. Data collected included patient demographics, comorbidities, immunization records, laboratory test results, treatments, and clinical outcomes such as hospital admission, intensive care unit (ICU) care, mechanical ventilation, and mortality. Comorbidities were coded using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index. COVID-19 and influenza vaccination status were obtained from the institutional EHR and verified through the Michigan Care Improvement Registry (MCIR), which consolidates statewide immunization data, including vaccinations received outside the Corewell system.

Definitions

Coinfection was defined as the presence of two or more of the three target diagnoses (COVID-19, influenza, RSV) identified from the principal and any secondary discharge diagnosis codes assigned during the same ED encounter. Diagnosis codes, supplemented by available laboratory data, were used rather than laboratory confirmation alone because comprehensive multiplex testing was not uniformly performed, and many patients had prior or external testing (e.g., outpatient, urgent care, or home testing) that informed the final discharge diagnoses but was not consistently captured in the hospital laboratory record. This approach also enabled exclusion of incidental positive test results unrelated to the primary reason for the ED visit, thereby better reflecting clinically relevant acute coinfections.

COVID-19 vaccination status was categorized as recent if at least one dose was received within 12 months prior to the ED visit, old if the last recorded dose was more than 12 months prior, and never if there was no documentation of prior vaccination. This classification was selected to account for waning immunity over time, which has been widely documented in the literature and may influence the risk of infection and disease severity (8-10). Influenza vaccination status was defined as yes or no based on whether the patient had received an influenza vaccine during the current influenza season prior to the ED encounter, aligning exposure with the annual July–June influenza season and reflecting the period during which vaccine-derived protection is clinically relevant. These definitions were used to reflect real-world immune protection at the time of presentation and were included as covariates in all regression models to adjust for their potential impact on clinical outcomes. RSV vaccination status was not included in this analysis due to its limited availability during the study period and inconsistent documentation within the EHR system.

Respiratory virus season was defined using the date of the ED visit, following a July–June structure to align with typical seasonal patterns of respiratory virus circulation and was included as a covariate in regression models to account for temporal variation in viral circulation and treatment patterns (9,11).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was hospital admission following the index ED encounter. This outcome was selected as a clinically relevant measure of acute illness severity requiring escalation of care. The secondary outcome was a composite measure of severe outcome, defined as the occurrence of any of the following during the index encounter or associated hospitalization: mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, or in-hospital death (12,13). Time to event analysis was conducted using Cox proportional hazards models, with time zero defined as the time of arrival in the ED. Patients were censored at discharge or death, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population across four infection groups: monoinfection with COVID-19, influenza, or RSV, and coinfection involving combinations of these viruses. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), as well as medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. To visualize the distribution and intersection of infection patterns, we constructed an UpSet plot, which depicts the frequency of unique combinations of COVID-19, influenza, and RSV infections. This allowed identification of the most prevalent monoinfection and coinfection profiles within the study cohort.

To evaluate associations between infection group and clinical outcomes, we used multivariable logistic regression for the outcome of hospital admission and Cox proportional hazards models for time-to-severe outcome. All models adjusted for the following covariates: age, sex, race, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, COVID-19 and influenza vaccination status, and respiratory virus season of presentation. To address potential confounding and baseline imbalance among infection groups, we applied multinomial propensity score weighting (PSW) using the same covariates (14). Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with absolute SMD <0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Covariate balance is shown in Figure S1. Importantly, all outcome models remained adjusted for the PSW covariates even after weighting to control for residual confounding.

A sensitivity analysis was performed using a more granular classification of infection status into six mutually exclusive groups: (I) COVID-19 only; (II) influenza only; (III) RSV only; (IV) COVID-19 + influenza; (V) COVID-19 + RSV; and (VI) influenza + RSV. This allowed assessment of whether specific viral coinfection combinations were differentially associated with hospital admission. Multinomial PSW was initially applied to adjust for baseline covariates across six infection groups. However, covariate balance could not be adequately achieved due to limited overlap and small subgroup sizes (Figure S2). As such, we conducted multivariable logistic regression adjusting for key confounders without weighting. This approach preserves interpretability while mitigating residual confounding to the extent possible. In the primary analysis, the coinfection group was used as the reference because it represented the main exposure of interest, allowing each monoinfection group to be directly compared against coinfection. For the sensitivity analysis, once coinfections were separated into three smaller subgroups, we selected influenza as the reference group due to its large sample size and clinical stability across seasons, ensuring more reliable and interpretable comparisons.

To further explore age-related risk, a subgroup analysis was conducted among adults aged ≥65 years, using both traditional multivariable logistic regression and PSW based on sex, race, comorbidities, vaccination status, and to assess hospital admission. Covariate balance for PSW is shown in Figure S3.

Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for logistic and survival models, respectively. All statistical tests were two-sided, with statistical significance defined as a P value <0.05. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 provided by the R Foundation for Statistical Computing (Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 68,697 adult ED encounters were included between January 1, 2021 and May 31, 2025. All patients had a principal diagnosis of COVID-19, influenza, or RSV. Among these, 52,922 (77.0%) were diagnosed with COVID-19 only, 13,996 (20.4%) with influenza only, 1,356 (2.0%) with RSV only. An additional 423 encounters were classified as coinfections involving two or more of these diseases based on discharge diagnoses. An UpSet plot (Figure 1) illustrated the distribution of mono- and coinfection combinations. The most common coinfection was COVID-19 + influenza, followed by COVID-19 + RSV. No triple infections were observed during the study period.

The mean age of the overall population was 53.0 years (SD 20.5 years), with RSV patients being the oldest (mean 61.5 years) and influenza patients the youngest (mean 47.0 years). Among coinfected patients, 37.1% were aged ≥65 years, compared to 32.9% in the COVID-19 group and 22.5% in the influenza group. Females comprised 58.6% of the total population, with a higher proportion among RSV-only (68.8%) and coinfected (62.9%) groups. Racial distribution also differed significantly across groups, with Black or African American patients representing 43.1% of influenza and 30.4% of RSV cases. Comorbidity burden, as measured by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, was higher among patients with RSV and coinfection, with 26.7% and 23.5% respectively having a score of ≥5. Vaccination coverage varied across infection groups: 30.8% of RSV patients had received a recent COVID-19 vaccine, compared to 18.4% in the coinfection group. For influenza vaccination, 34.3% of RSV patients had received a recent vaccine, versus 23.4% in the coinfection group. These differences were statistically significant (P<0.001). Among all encounters, 33.4% resulted in hospital admission. Admission rates were highest in the RSV group (49.8%) and the coinfection group (40.2%), compared to 36.2% for COVID-19 and 20.8% for influenza (Table 1). In multivariable logistic regression models weighted by propensity scores, compared to the coinfection group, the adjusted odds of hospital admission were significantly lower for patients with influenza (aOR 0.55; 95% CI: 0.54–0.57) and COVID-19 (aOR 0.86; 95% CI: 0.84–0.89). In contrast, patients with RSV infection had higher odds of admission relative to coinfection (aOR 2.17; 95% CI: 2.11–2.22) (Table 2). A sensitivity analysis examined six infection groups: three monoinfections and three coinfections (COVID-19 + influenza, COVID-19 + RSV, influenza + RSV) (Figure 2). Results were consistent with the primary analysis, showing higher odds of hospital admission for coinfections—particularly those involving RSV—compared to monoinfections. COVID-19 and influenza alone were associated with the lowest admission risk, reinforcing the robustness of the main findings.

The overall rate of severe outcome—defined as ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, or in-hospital death—was 3.5%. Severe outcome rates were highest among coinfected (4.0%) and RSV-only patients (3.9%). ICU admission occurred in 2.3% of all encounters, mechanical ventilation in 1.6%, and in-hospital mortality in 1.7% (Table 1). Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated the earliest divergence for patients with coinfection and RSV in time to severe outcome (Figure S4). In propensity score-weighted Cox proportional hazards models, influenza-only and RSV-only infections demonstrated significantly lower hazards of severe outcomes compared with coinfection (influenza: aHR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.90; RSV: aHR 0.45, 95% CI: 0.21–0.95) (Table 2). In contrast, coinfection did not differ significantly from COVID-19-only in the risk of severe outcomes (aHR 0.62; 95% CI: 0.32–1.19).

In a prespecified subgroup analysis of adults aged ≥65 years (n=21,397), coinfection was generally associated with higher odds of hospital admission compared to monoinfection with COVID-19 or influenza. This pattern was observed in both traditional multivariable logistic regression and PSW models, which produced directionally consistent and quantitatively similar estimates. However, the odds of admission were higher for RSV monoinfection compared to coinfection. In the PSW model, the association reached statistical significance. These findings highlight the elevated risk of hospitalization from RSV among older adults and suggest that viral coinfection confers greater admission risk than COVID-19 or influenza alone, but not necessarily more than RSV (Figure 3 and Table S1).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter retrospective cohort study of over 68,000 adult ED encounters, we found that coinfection with SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and/or RSV was associated with increased odds of hospital admission compared to monoinfection with COVID-19 or influenza. However, RSV monoinfection was associated with odds of admission that were comparable to or higher than coinfection in the main analysis. This pattern is consistent with the demographic characteristics of the RSV group in our cohort, who were the oldest on average and had the highest comorbidity burden, which likely contributes to their elevated admission risk. In a sensitivity analysis using six mutually exclusive infection groups, coinfections involving RSV were associated with the higher odds of admission, reinforcing the primary findings. A prespecified subgroup analysis among adults aged ≥65 years, a clinically vulnerable population (15-17), also demonstrated that coinfection was associated with significantly increased admission risk. These findings suggest that while coinfection generally increases the risk of hospitalization, this effect is most pronounced when RSV is involved and remains consistent in high-risk demographic groups.

While the hospitalization risk was clearly elevated in coinfected patients, differences in severe outcomes varied by infection group. Coinfection appeared to confer greater clinical severity than single virus influenza or RSV infections, although the risk of severe outcomes for coinfection did not differ significantly from COVID-19 alone. Specifically, only 423 encounters were classified as coinfections, and severe outcomes occurred in just 2,420 of all encounters (3.5%), including only 17 among coinfected patients. This low event rate prevented the performance of sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses for severe outcomes. These challenges are consistent with previous studies of respiratory virus coinfection, where sparse severe events often hinder robust estimation (5).

In addition to these clinical findings, demographic patterns showed that coinfection disproportionately affects vulnerable populations. Coinfected patients were more likely to be older, female, and have multiple comorbid conditions—known risk factors for poor outcomes from respiratory viral infections (18,19). They also had lower rates of recent vaccination against either SARS-CoV-2 or influenza, suggesting that gaps in immunization may increase the risk not only of infection but also of dual infection. From a public health standpoint, these findings emphasize the importance of timely and coordinated vaccination strategies, particularly among older adults and high-risk groups (10,20,21). It is possible that enhanced vaccine uptake for both viruses may reduce not only individual disease burden but also the likelihood of coinfection and subsequent system-level strain. Additionally, although not assessed in this study, bacterial coinfections, particularly secondary bacterial pneumonia following influenza, are known to exacerbate clinical severity and influence admission decisions, and may contribute to outcome patterns observed in viral respiratory infections.

This study addresses important gaps in the current literature. Prior research has typically examined viral infections in isolation or focused on pairwise coinfections, often in pediatric or single-center populations, and typically over shorter time frames. There remains limited understanding of how concurrent infections involving SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and RSV affect clinical outcomes in adults presenting to EDs. To our knowledge, few studies have examined these three respiratory viruses simultaneously in a large adult population over a multiyear period. By systematically analyzing mono- and coinfection patterns across five respiratory seasons, this study contributes new evidence on the relationship between coinfection and hospital admission, as well as on the demographic and vaccination-related factors that characterize these cases. The inclusion of comprehensive immunization data and use of rigorous confounder adjustment further strengthen the interpretability and potential applicability of our findings to clinical and public health settings.

Finally, the absence of any observed triple infections during the study period may reflect low background probability or diagnostic limitations. However, this observation aligns with other reports suggesting that while dual infections are clinically relevant, triple infections remain rare (22). Even so, the impact of dual infections alone on healthcare demand and triage decisions underscores the need for operational preparedness during periods of viral co-circulation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective observational design introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding despite statistical adjustment and PSW. Second, infection status was determined using discharge diagnoses supplemented by available laboratory data. Systematic multiplex testing was not uniformly performed due to resource constraints, and some testing occurred outside the health system and was not captured, which may have led to under- or overestimation of coinfection. This approach also allowed exclusion of incidental positive results unrelated to the primary ED diagnosis, but some misclassification of true viral etiology remains possible. Third, the number of coinfection cases was relatively small (n=423), and the number of severe outcomes among them was very low (n=17), limiting the ability to perform the sensitivity and subgroup analyses for secondary outcomes. Fourth, our vaccination data, though linked to the MCIR, may not capture all doses received outside the state or during incomplete records. RSV vaccination status was not included due to its recent introduction, initially restricted eligibility, and inconsistent documentation during much of the study period. Limited uptake and incomplete capture in the EHR and MCIR, particularly early in rollout, may be especially relevant when interpreting findings among adults aged ≥65 years. Lastly, we did not account for virus subtypes or coinfection sequencing (e.g., whether one virus preceded another), which could influence clinical severity.

Conclusions

In summary, respiratory viral coinfection—particularly involving RSV—was associated with higher odds of hospital admission compared to monoinfection with COVID-19 or influenza, especially among older adults and those with comorbidities. RSV monoinfection itself was linked to similarly elevated risk. For severe outcomes, coinfection showed higher hazard than influenza-only and RSV-only, but not different from COVID-19. These findings support the need for ongoing viral surveillance, multiplex diagnostics, and vaccination strategies targeting multiple pathogens to mitigate seasonal strain on healthcare systems. Further research is needed to clarify mechanisms underlying coinfection-related risk.
