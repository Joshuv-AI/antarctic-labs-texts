Simultaneous bioanalytical method development and validation of ibuprofen and phenyramidol HCl in human plasma using high–performance liquid chromatography with photodiode array detection and its application in a pharmacokinetic study
Authors: Nurgul Kızıltas, Tugrul Cagri Akman, Yasemin Karabayir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe co-administration of ibuprofen (IBU) and phenyramidol HCl (PHE) is frequently preferred for the management of musculoskeletal disorders to enhance therapeutic efficacy. This study aimed to develop and validate a rapid, sensitive and cost-effective high–performance liquid chromatography–photodiode array (HPLC–PDA) method for the simultaneous determination of IBU and PHE in human plasma and pharmaceutical preparations, and to demonstrate its applicability in pharmacokinetic studies. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a C18 column using a mobile phase consisting of phosphate buffer (pH 6.0 ± 0.05) and methanol (30:70, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL·min −1. The retention times of IBU and PHE were 3.7 and 2.7 min, respectively, with a total analysis time of 6 min. The method showed excellent linearity within the range of 0.05–40.0 μg·mL −1 for both drugs, with LLOQ values of 0.05 μg·mL−1 for IBU and 0.0025 μg·mL−1 for PHE. The plasma samples were collected at four time points (0.0, 1.0, 4.0 and 8.0 h) after oral administration of IBU and PHE. Cmax, Tmax and AUC0–8 were calculated, with Cmax of 16.14 μg·mL−1 for IBU and 3.71 μg·mL−1 for PHE, while Tmax was 1.0 h for both. Despite limitations in the number of sampling time points, the method proved highly applicable for determining pharmacokinetic profiles and for routine quality control analyses. Its short analysis time, high sensitivity and low sample volume requirement make it a powerful tool for clinical research and therapeutic monitoring. This is the first validated HPLC–PDA method applied to the simultaneous pharmacokinetic evaluation of IBU and PHE in human plasma.

Introduction

Acute low back pain (LBP) is a highly common medical condition that affects >90% of the adult population and occurs at least once in their lives. While it predominantly afflicts individuals of working age, it can impact people across all age groups, from young adults to the elderly. Although symptoms resolve on their own in the majority of cases, loss of workplace productivity can be significant. Many patients with acute LBP apply to physical therapy clinics and emergency departments. Treatment options include various nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioid analgesics, skeletal muscle relaxants or a combination of these medications for the management of LBP (1).

Ibuprofen (IBU), classified as an NSAID, exerts its anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects by inhibiting the biosynthesis of prostaglandins, which are lipid mediators involved in the propagation of inflammation, pain and pyresis within the body. In combination therapies for musculoskeletal ailments and associated discomfort, IBU is often supplemented with muscle relaxants like phenyramidol HCl (PHE), an aminopyridine derivative, to enhance therapeutic efficacy (2) (Figure 1).

Molecular structures of (A) IBU and (B) PHE.

In modern times, the main goal in developing separation techniques is to achieve reliable results by developing simple, fast and cost-effective methods. For this reason, many analytical methods developed for the quantitative analysis of drug active ingredients are presented to the literature. Comprehensive examination of the literature reveals numerous methods reported for IBU in various matrices. However, only a few studies have been identified for PHE before. High–performance thin layer chromatography (3), liquid chromatography (4–13), gas chromatography (14–17), high–performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with spectrofluorometric detection (18), capillary electrophoresis (19) and spectroscopic methods (20–25) have been reported for the determination of IBU. On the other hand, two HPLC methods (26, 27) and one spectroscopic method (28) have been reported for PHE in the literature. As can be seen, no analytical method has been developed in the literature for the simultaneous determination of IBU and PHE. No study has been reported to determine the pharmacokinetic parameters of these two drugs, which are frequently preferred in the clinic, when used together. Although LC–MS/MS and GC techniques exhibit remarkable sensitivity, they come with high costs. In contrast, spectroscopic methods are budget-friendly but struggle to accurately measure low concentrations. As a result of their affordability and exceptional sensitivity, HPLC methods have gained prominence in drug analysis within human plasma.

The primary aim of this study was to develop and validate an innovative HPLC–photodiode array (HPLC–PDA) method for quantifying levels of IBU and PHE during therapeutic drug monitoring. The proposed method prioritized achieving results with exceptional sensitivity and accuracy along with a highly efficient, rapid and effective extraction process of these drugs in human plasma. Validation of the method was conducted, assessing selectivity, linearity, precision, accuracy and sensitivity in accordance with the International Conference for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines. To showcase the reliability and applicability of this developed and validated method, the method was applied to both pharmaceutical preparations and real human plasma samples. By analyzing human plasma samples collected at five different time points, the pharmacokinetic parameters of the drugs were determined successfully.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

IBU (purity ≥98%, CAS No. 15687-27-1), certified to USP standard, was obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). PHE (purity >98%, CAS No. 326-43-2), was also sourced from Nosch Labs (Hyderabad, India). Disodium hydrogen phosphate (purity ≥99.0%), ortho-phosphoric acid (purity>85.0%), HPLC-grade methanol, methyl tertiary butyl ether (purity ≥99.8%) and dichloromethane (purity ≥99.8%) were also acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO). Caffeine (CAF) (purity 99.7%, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. 58-08-2) served as the internal standard (IS) for HPLC analysis. Hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30% (w/w) in H2O) solutions used in degradation studies were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO). Ultra-pure water was produced using an Elga Process Walter Medica Pro R 30 purification system, Veolia Waters (ELGA, High Wycombe, UK). Toledo Seven Compact S220 was used for pH adjustment. In the study, Whatman SPARTAN HPLC Syringe Filter (Regenerated Cellulose, 0.45 μm), Whatman filter paper (Grade 42), Eppendorf® vacuum concentrator plus centrifuge and Eppendorf® micropipettes were used while preparing standard and plasma samples. Artril® 400 mg tablets and Cabral® 400 mg tablets were purchased from pharmacies in Erzincan, Turkey. Human plasma was kindly supplied by Erzincan Binali Yildirim University-Mengücek Gazi Education and Research Hospital (Erzincan, Turkey) after obtaining donor consent, and was preserved at −80°C until needed.

Chromatographic conditions

In this research, an HPLC System (Shimadzu® LC-2050C 3D) was utilized for analyzing drugs in pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma. Data collection and processing were carried out using Labsolution® software. Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACE 5® C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size), which is suitable for conventional HPLC applications. The mobile phase was a mixture of phosphate buffer (pH: 6.0) and methanol (30:70, v:v). During analysis, the column and autosampler temperatures were maintained at 30°C and 25°C, respectively. The PDA detector settings for IBU, PHE and CAF were 220 nm, 240 nm, and 220 nm, respectively. For each run, 5 μL of the sample was injected, with the mobile phase flow rate set at 1 mL·min−1. The total analysis time was 6 min.

Preparation of calibration standards, quality controls and internal standards solutions

When preparing the phosphate buffer solution to use as the mobile phase, ~2,400 mg of disodium hydrogen phosphate was weighed and dissolved in 1,000 mL of ultra-pure water. The pH of the buffer solution was adjusted to 6.0 with ortho phosphoric acid. For the IS solution, the stock solution of CAF was prepared at a concentration of 100 μg·mL−1 with methanol in a volumetric flask. In total, 100 μL of CAF stock solution was added to each sample. For preparing the stock solutions of IBU and PHE at a concentration of 0.025 μg·mL−1, 25 mg of IBU and PHE were weighed and dissolved separately in methanol. The solutions were completed with methanol in a 100 mL volumetric flask. These stock solutions were used to prepare working standards and quality control (QC) solutions. The standard solutions of IBU and PHE were prepared in the concentration range of 0.05 to 40 μg·mL−1 by diluting the stock solutions with methanol. Each standard sample contained both IBU and PHE at an equal concentration (0.05–40 μg·mL-1) and CAF at a concentration of 10 μg·mL−1. QC samples of IBU and PHE were prepared at three different concentration levels: 0.125 μg·mL−1 (low), 12.5 μg·mL−1 (medium), and 35.0 μg·mL−1 (high), to fall within the linear range of calibration curve. All prepared solutions were kept at +4°C until they were analyzed.

Extraction procedure of IBU, PHE and CAF from human plasma

The human plasma, initially stored at −80°C, was allowed to thaw at ambient temperature. In this extraction procedure, 100 μL of IS (equivalent to 10 μg·mL−1 final concentration) were spiked to 250 μL of human plasma samples. To this mixture, varying volumes of IBU and PHE standard solutions were added to attain concentrations of 0.05, 0.25, 1.25, 7.5, 15.0, 25.0, 30.0 and 40.0 μg·mL−1. The resultant mixtures were subjected to vortex for 1 min. Subsequently, a liquid–liquid extraction process was applied by adding 1.0 mL of a solution of methyl tertiary butyl ether and dichloromethane (1:1, v:v). Post an additional 2 min of vortex, the samples were centrifugated at 16,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. The supernatants were then transferred to the new Eppendorf tubes by the micropipette and evaporated in a vacuum concentrator in V-Aqua mode at low pressure and 30°C. The dry residue dissolved with 100 μL of methanol. This same procedure was employed to prepared QC human plasma samples at concentrations of 0.125, 12.5 and 35.0 μg·mL−1. After the extraction process, both the standard and QC samples in human plasma were analyzed. Following filtration, a 5 μL aliquot was administered into the HPLC–PDA system for analysis. The quantification of IBU and PHE in the real human plasma samples was executed by referencing the established calibration curves.

A blank sample was prepared by applying this extraction procedure to drug-free human plasma. This blank sample was then evaluated to confirm that there was no interference from any endogenous substances with the peaks corresponding to IBU, PHE and IS. To create the calibration curves, the peak area ratio of IBU to IS (on the y-axis) was plotted against the IBU concentration (in μg·mL−1, on the x-axis). The same process was repeated to generate the calibration curves for PHE.

Method validation

The proposed analytical method was validated according to the ICH Guideline M10 and Q2(R2) to assess specificity/selectivity, carryover, linearity, precision and accuracy, sensitivity, robustness, matrix effect, recovery as well as stability of plasma samples (29, 30). All statistical values, including mean, percentage relative standard deviation (RSD%), relative error (RE), regression equation and correlation coefficient, were calculated using Microsoft Excel.

Specificity/selectivity and system suitability

In order to verify that endogenous plasma components and concomitant drugs did not affect the measurement of the analytes, the selectivity/specificity of the analytical method was determined by analyzing six individual human blank plasma samples. Each blank sample was tested for interferences under optimized chromatographic conditions after sample preparation and extraction procedures. All blank plasma samples used for selectivity parameter were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. The response of interfering components at analyte retention time should not be >20% of the lowest calibration standard (LLOQ) response and 5% of IS response.

Additionally, to further validate the robustness of the chromatographic method, additional system suitability parameters were evaluated under the optimized conditions. Resolution (Rs) between peaks, the RSD% of retention times, tailing factor, peak areas and number of theoretical plates were assessed to ensure the method’s effectiveness in separating the analytes. These parameters are critical for ensuring reliable quantification and identification of complex mixtures.

Matrix effect and carry-over

The matrix effect test determines whether the matrix suppresses or enhances analyte and IS responses. The matrix effect for analytes and IS was evaluated using six different plasma samples. The matrix effect is determined by calculating the ratio of the peak areas observed in the presence of a matrix to those observed in the absence of a matrix for the standard solutions of analytes and IS at equivalent concentrations. The %RSD at various concentration levels should be <15% for precision, and the accuracy should be within ±15% of the nominal concentration to achieve consistent results across different plasma sources.

The purpose of the carry-over test is to evaluate the degree of transfer of an analyte from one sample to another after each analysis. After analysis of a higher calibration concentration level (ULOQ, 40.0 μg·mL−1), the blank sample was injected into the HPLC–PDA system. The carry-over parameter of the analytical method was evaluated by comparing the peak area from blank samples with the peak area at LLOQ. Peak area of the blank sample should not be >20% of LLOQ and 5% of IS.

Linearity and sensitivity

The linearity of the analytical method was determined using the least squares method. Each working solutions prepared at eight different concentrations were analyzed six times. Six separate calibration curves were created by plotting the peak area ratio of IBU/IS and PHE/IS against the nominal concentration of the standards across eight different concentrations. The slope, intercept of the linear regression equation and the correlation coefficient was determined by least squares linear regression (1/x2 weighting). The acceptance criterion was that the deviation of the calculated concentration should be within ±15% of the nominal concentration, while the LLOQ should be within ±20%. On the other hand, when evaluating the sensitivity of the method, the peak area of the LLOQ should be greater than five times the response of the blank sample.

Accuracy and precision

The accuracy and precision of the analytical method was evaluated by analyzing QC samples at three different concentrations (0.125, 12.5 and 35.0 μg·mL−1) in the linear working range, both within the same day and across three different days. The lowest QC concentration was ≤3 times the LLOQ concentration, and the highest QC was 75% to 90% of the ULOQ. For accuracy, the RE% should not be >15% for QC samples and 20% for LLOQ. Precision is evaluated by the RSD%, and RSD% should be <15% for QC samples and 20% for LLOQ.

Robustness

To evaluate the robustness of the HPLC-PDA method, the effects of small changes in three factors, namely temperature (25°C and 35°C), flow rate (0.8 mL·min−1 and 1.2 mL·min−1) and pH of the mobile phase (5.9 and 6.1), on the peak areas of IBU, PHE and IS were investigated.

Extraction recovery

The purpose of the recovery test was to evaluate the efficiency of the liquid–liquid extraction process. The recovery was determined at three different concentrations of both IBU and PHE (0.125, 12.5 and 35.0 μg·mL−1) and one concentration for IS (10 μg·mL−1). The recovery of the analyte was calculated by comparing the mean peak areas of the analyte (extracted samples) added to the biological matrix and recovered with the peak areas of the blank plasma samples added at the same concentration after the extraction process. The recovery of IS was also determined by the same method.

Procedure for pharmaceutical preparations

There are no pharmaceutical preparations containing both IBU and PHE in Turkiye. For this reason, we applied the HPLC method to two separate commercial pharmaceutical preparations. For this purpose, Artril® 400 mg tablet preparations containing IBU and Cabral® 400 mg tablet preparations containing PHE were obtained from a local pharmacy. Stock and sample solutions of pharmaceutical preparations were prepared according to the following procedure: 10 tablets of the pharmaceutical preparation were taken and weighed individually. The average weight of 10 tablets was calculated and the weight corresponding to 1 tablet was determined. These tablets were powdered using a mortar and mixed homogeneously. Powder equal to the weight of a tablet (active ingredient and some excipients) was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask and methanol was added. To aid dissolution, the mixture was sonicated for at least 10 min before being filtered through Whatman filter paper 42 micron size. After filtering the solution into a new 100 mL volumetric flask, the solution was made up to 100 mL with methanol. An appropriate amount of solution was further diluted with methanol until the active ingredient concentration in the final solution fell within the working range. This procedure was applied separately to both Artril® 400 mg tablet and Cabral® 400 mg tablet preparations. The prepared samples were analyzed by the HPLC–PDA method.

Forced degradation studies

The mixture solution containing IBU at a concentration of 25.0 μg·mL−1, PHE at 25.0 μg·mL−1, and CAF at 10.0 μg·mL−1 was subjected to six repetitions of forced degradation testing under various conditions. Initially, 0.1 N HCl, NaOH and H2O2 solutions were studied for acidic, basic and oxidative degradation. However, since no degradation was observed at this concentration, these solutions were re-prepared for these degradation conditions and their concentrations were optimized to 1 N. The degradation experiments included hydrolysis (acidic, alkaline and neutral) and oxidative degradation. For acid hydrolysis, the mixture was heated at the room temperature for 5 h in 1 N HCl. In total, 1 N NaOH was used for neutralization. Alkaline hydrolysis was performed by incubating the mixture in 1 N NaOH at the room temperature for 5 h and 1 N HCl was used for neutralization. For neutral hydrolysis, the mixture was exposed to water and heated at 100°C for 5 h. Oxidative degradation was conducted by treating the mixture with 30% (w/w) H2O2 at the room temperature for 5 h. The stability of IBU and PHE under forced degradation conditions was evaluated by comparing the peak areas of standard solutions at the same concentration.

Human samples collection from patients with LBP

This research was permitted by Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No.: 2024-08/05, Ethics Committee Approval Date: 23.05.2024). Patients were informed about the study and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Six people who applied to Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University Faculty of Medicine Department of Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Polyclinic and took three doses of Artril® 400 mg tablet and three doses of Cabral® 400 mg tablets per day were included in the study. Blood samples were collected at four predefined time points: pre-dose (0 h) and 1.0, 4.0 and 8.0 h after oral co-administration of Artril® (400 mg) and Cabral® (400 mg), which were taken simultaneously in the morning under fed conditions. In addition, blood samples were taken from six healthy participants (18–75 years old) who did not use medication. The blood samples taken were placed in an anticoagulant tube (EDTA), 5 mL per person, and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to separate human plasma. These samples were kept at −80°C in polypropylene containers for IBU and PHE detection by HPLC–PDA.

The pharmacokinetic parameters of IBU and PHE were analyzed using Kinetica 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with non-compartmental models. Maximum plasma concentrations (Cmax) were obtained from individual concentration-time profiles, while the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the mixed log-linear method. Statistical analyses were conducted with the Mann–Whitney test, employing SPSS version 18 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) for non-parametric comparisons. All numerical data are presented as mean ± SD and rounded to two decimal places to ensure consistency throughout the manuscript.

Results

Method validation

Specificity/selectivity and system suitability

Specificity was assessed by comparing the chromatograms of six different blank human plasma samples with those of blank human plasma samples spiked with IBU, PHE and CAF. After analysis of six different blank samples, it was found that these samples did not contain simultaneous elution peaks at the retention times of IBU, PHE and CAF from endogenous biological matrices or structurally similar substances to the analytes (metabolites, isomers, impurities, degradation products formed during sample preparation). None of the six individual blank human plasma samples showed an interference peak area >20.0% of the mean analyte peak area of the LLOQ standards at peak retention time for IBU and PHE, and none of the six individual samples showed an interference peak area >5.0% of the mean IS peak area of the LLOQ standards at retention time for IS. These analyses showed that the method had high selectivity and specificity, as shown in Figure 2. The retention times for CAF, PHE and IBU were ~1.87, 2.71, and 3.75 min, respectively.

The system suitability test was conducted by analyzing a sample containing IBU at 25.0 μg·mL−1, PHE at 25.0 μg·mL−1, and CAF at 10.0 μg·mL−1, repeated six times. The results indicated that the method was accurate and reproducible (Table I). The Rs between IBU/PHE, IBU/CAF, as well as PHE/CAF, was calculated to be 4.6, 4.6 and 4.5, respectively, surpassing the minimum acceptance criteria of Rs > 1.5, indicating adequate separation. The mean retention times were 3.7, 2.7 and 1.88 for IBU, PHE and CAF, respectively, and since the RSD% of the retention times was <1, the results complied with the acceptance criterion. The mean tailing factors were ~1.2 (acceptance criteria 0.8–1.5), and the numbers of theoretical plates were >2,000, which is the acceptance criteria. The average of peak areas is 616,842 ± 4,824, 913,442 ± 1,680, and 274,476 ± 681 for IBU, PHE and CAF, respectively. As seen in Table I, tailing factor, theoretical plates, and Rs values were found to be consistent with the reference values. Based on these values, it was determined that the peaks of IBU, PHE and CAF did not interfere with any peaks, peak separation was achieved well and peak purity and Rs were very good.

Matrix effect and carry-over

The matrix effect was investigated to identify possible signal suppression or enhancement that could be produced by matrix components (30). From human plasma recoveries for IBU and PHE, matrix components did not appear to affect the peak area of both IBU and PHE (Figure 2A). In this study, the effects of the matrix on IBU and PHE were evaluated at concentrations of 0.125, 12.5 and 35.0 μg·mL−1. The findings showed consistent matrix effects, with values of 90.72 ± 1.54%, 88.49 ± 2.27%, and 91.8 ± 4.64% for IBU, and 87.15 ± 2.62%, 93.03 ± 3.19%, and 89.26 ± 3.52% for PHE. Additionally, the average matrix effect on CAF was 90.35 ± 5.14%, indicating no interference from co-eluting substances during the recoveries of IBU, PHE and CAF. The RSD% for IBU, PHE and CAF were found 1.17%, 2.49% and 1.34%, respectively.

In the carry-over test, the blank sample was injected after the analysis of ULOQ. The peak areas of the blank samples were compared with the peak area at LLOQ. Thus, the carry-over of the analyte between the samples during injection was evaluated. According to the results, it was determined that there was no noticeable carry-over between the samples since the values did not exceed 20% of LLOQ.

Linearity and sensitivity

The linearity of the method was assessed by generating calibration curves for IBU and PHE in blank human plasma. This was achieved by plotting the peak area ratio of IBU/IS and PHE/IS against the nominal concentration of the standards across eight different concentrations, ranging from 0.05 to 40 μg·mL−1 (Figure 2B). The method demonstrated consistent linearity within this concentration range for both human plasma and standard solutions of IBU and PHE. The calibration curve equations of IBU and PHE were y = 0.0907x + 0.0085 and y = 0.1343x + 0.0449, respectively. According to the regression analysis of these calibration curves, the correlation coefficients were >0.999 for both IBU and PHE (Table II and Figure 2C and D). The LLOQ values of IBU and PHE were determined 0.05 μg·mL−1 and 0.0025 μg·mL−1, respectively (Figure 3A).

Accuracy and precision

The intra- and inter-day precision and accuracy of the analytical method were evaluated by analyzing three QC samples contained IBU and PHE at concentrations of 0.125, 12.5 and 35.0 μg·mL−1, both within the same day and across three different days (Figure 3B). Precision was assessed using RSD%, while accuracy was determined by the RE. According to the intra-day and inter-day analysis results, the RSD% (0.32–6.39%) and RE% values (−4.23–4.32%) of the determined concentrations of the QC samples were found to be <18%. Thus, the developed method met the acceptance criteria for accuracy and precision parameters (Table III).

Extraction recovery from human plasma

The extraction efficiency of the solvent mixtures was assessed through quantitative analysis of the analytes recovered from the human plasma samples. In this study, various solvents were assessed for their effectiveness in extracting the analytes, including acetonitrile, methyl tertiary butyl ether, dichloromethane, methanol and hexane. The combination of methyl tertiary butyl ether and dichloromethane (1:1, v:v) demonstrated superior performance in terms of both recovery yield and purity of the extracted analyte. The recovery rates for each concentration level in the calibration curve were found to be 93.8% for IBU and 92.8% for PHE, demonstrating the high efficiency of the extraction procedure (Table IV). Consequently, it can be concluded that the combination of methyl tertiary butyl ether and dichloromethane (1:1, v:v) is the most suitable solvent for extracting the analytes from human plasma in this study. This optimal solvent mixture not only maximized the recovery rate but also minimized co-extraction of matrix components, thereby reducing potential interferences in subsequent analytical procedures. The improved recovery and reduced matrix effect are attributed to the complementary physicochemical properties of the two solvents, which together provide an balance polarity environment enabling effective analyte extraction.

Robustness

The following three factors were selected for robustness testing: column temperature, flow rate and phosphate buffer pH. It was investigated whether all the above-mentioned factors significantly affected the practical use of the developed method in the analysis of a mixture of 0.025 μg·mL−1 IBU, PHE and 1.0 μg·mL−1 CAF. Two experimental levels were tested for each factor: column temperature—25°C and 35°C, mobile phase flow rate—0.8 mL·min−1 and 1.2 mL·min−1 and phosphate buffer pH—5.9 and 6.1. According to the results of the robustness test, there was no significant change in peak areas of standard samples of IBU and PHE, and the RSD% values were found to be between 0.38% and 4.37% (Table SI).

Forced degradation study

Stability studies were conducted on standard samples of IBU and PHE (25.0 μg·mL−1) under acidic (1 N HCl), basic (1 N NaOH), neutral (water) and oxidative conditions (30% H2O2 (w/w)). The forced degradation behavior of IBU, PHE and CAF under acidic, basic, oxidative and hydrolytic stress conditions is summarized in Table SII, including peak area values and percentage changes relative to untreated samples. The peak shapes of both IBU and PHE remained unchanged across these conditions, though peak areas decreased by 2–9% (Table SII). For CAF, the peak shape was changed in acidic (Figure 4A) and neutral (Figure 4C) conditions, and the peak area increased significantly under oxidative condition (Figure 4D). Additionally, peak tailing was observed for CAF in the basic condition (Figure 4B). Consequently, it was determined that both IBU and PHE remained stable under acidic, basic, neutral and oxidative conditions.

Assay of tablet dosage forms

To separately determine the recovery of IBU and PHE in the presence of excipients present in the tablet formulation, samples at three different concentrations (0.75, 17.5 and 37.5 μg·mL−1) were tested by the standard addition method (2.5 μg·mL−1). Analytical recovery percentage values were calculated by comparing the concentrations obtained from the spiked samples with the spiked concentrations. According to the HPLC–PDA method, the average analytical recovery value in tablets was determined as 99.1% for IBU and 101.7% for PHE (Figure 5A).

Application of the method to real human plasma samples and pharmacokinetic studies

The HPLC–PDA method developed in this study was successfully applied to human plasma samples collected at predefined time points, including pre-dose (0 h) and 1, 4 and 8 h after oral co-administration of Artril® (400 mg) and Cabral® (400 mg) tablets (Figure 5B). A non-compartmental model with a mixed log-linear method was used for the pharmacokinetic analysis. The median maximum plasma concentrations of IBU and PHE were 16.14 (15.32–18.07) μg·mL−1 and 3.71 (3.04–4.19) μg·mL−1, respectively. The median AUC0–8 values of IBU and PHE were 76.4 (74.9–79.2) μg·h−1·mL−1 and 23.2 (22.3–24.9) μg·h−1·mL−1, respectively. The average Tmax values for both IBU and PHE were ~1 h (Table SIII; Figure 6).

Discussions

Muscle relaxants such as PHE and NSAIDs including IBU and diclofenac are frequently co-administered for the management of musculoskeletal disorders and associated pain. PHE functions by inducing relaxation of skeletal muscles, thereby mitigating muscle spasms and tension which are commonly associated with musculoskeletal conditions. Conversely, NSAIDs such as IBU and diclofenac exert their effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes, which subsequently decreases the synthesis of prostaglandins—key mediators of inflammation, pain and pyrexia. The synergistic potential of combining these pharmacological agents can enhance therapeutic efficacy, offering superior analgesia compared to monotherapy. This combinatory approach is particularly advantageous in the treatment of musculoskeletal injuries such as strains and sprains, where concurrent muscle relaxation and inflammation attenuation are critical for optimal pain relief and functional recovery (1, 2).

Although there are many analytical methods in the literature that analyze IBU and PHE separately, no analytical method that performs quantitative analysis simultaneously has been reported. In this study, a sensitive, robust and rapid HPLC–PDA method was developed and validated for the simultaneous quantification of IBU and PHE in plasma. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACE 5® column (5 μm, LC, 150 × 4.6 mm, C18) at 30°C. In total, 5 μL of each samples was run at a flow rate of 1 mL·min−1 with the mobile phase consisting of a mixture of methanol and phosphate buffer (pH: 6.0) in the system. The wavelength of the detector was set to 220 nm for IBU and CAF and 240 nm for PHE. This developed method allowed the separation and detection of IBU, PHE and CAF within 6 min.

Recent advances in column technology, including core–shell and sub-2 μm particle columns, have improved chromatographic efficiency in many separation applications. In the present study, the ACE 5® C18 column (5 μm) was deliberately selected to ensure method robustness, accessibility and applicability in routine analytical laboratories. Fully porous 5 μm C18 columns are still widely employed in pharmaceutical QC and clinical laboratories, particularly when conventional HPLC systems are used. The primary objective of this work was to develop a reliable, cost-effective and easily transferable HPLC–PDA method rather than to maximize chromatographic efficiency through advanced column technology. Nevertheless, the application of core–shell columns, such as ACE Ultracore C18, may be considered in future studies to further enhance separation performance or reduce analysis time.

This new liquid chromatographic method, developed for quantitation of IBU and PHE under optimized chromatographic conditions, shows significant advantages over previous reported studies in terms of cost, analysis time, sample volume, linear working range, high peak resolution, robustness and sensitivity. (Table SIV) Compared to reported spectroscopic and high-performance thin layer chromatography methods, the developed HPLC–PDA method offered simplicity, robustness and reliability in the determination of IBU and PHE due to its ability to handle low sample volumes, analyze low concentrations effectively and work over a wide concentration range (3, 20–25, 28). In the study, the use of a low-cost PDA detector as opposed to expensive mass detectors makes this method attractive in the routine QC analysis of pharmaceutical preparations containing IBU and PHE. Quantification of IBU and PHE at low concentrations in plasma with the PDA detector provides a financial advantage over previous studies for the pharmacokinetic studies (4, 5, 10, 14–17). In previous studies which the UV detector was used for the determination of IBU, linearity was achieved in a narrower working range compared to this new HPLC–PDA method (6, 8, 13, 18). Determination of IBU and PHE at short retention times in this HPLC method, 2.71 min for PHE and 3.75 min for IBU, allowed analysis of a larger number of samples in a limited timeframe. This advantage of the method is particularly notable compared to existing methodologies where longer retention times can lead to longer analysis times and possible sample degradation. The total analysis time of 6.0 min represents a substantial reduction from that of previously reported techniques, highlighting efficiency of the HPLC–PDA method (4–7, 10, 12–18, 26). Shorter retention times not only expedite the analytical process but also reduce the likelihood of analyte decomposition, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results. The robustness and reproducibility of the HPLC–PDA method demonstrated its suitability for routine QC analyzes and different matrices. Moreover, the improved data integrity and precision achieved with this method make it a valuable tool for both clinical and pharmaceutical applications. By minimizing sample handling and exposure to potential degradation factors, this method enabled consistent and accurate quantification of IBU and PHE. This reliability made it a very important alternative method for monitoring the effectiveness and safety of pharmaceutical formulations containing IBU or PHE.

Although CAF exhibited some degree of instability under certain forced degradation conditions, it was successfully employed as an internal standard for quantitative analysis in this study. Quantification of IBU and PHE was performed using the peak area ratios of IBU/CAF and PHE/CAF, and CAF demonstrated stable retention behavior and reproducible peak area responses under the applied sample preparation, storage and chromatographic conditions. Importantly, the degradation observed under stress testing conditions does not reflect the routine analytical workflow used for quantitative determination. Therefore, the controlled use of CAF as an internal standard under validated conditions did not compromise the accuracy or reliability of the quantitative analysis.

In the developed method, the LLOQ values were determined to be 0.05 μg·mL−1 for IBU and 0.0025 μg·mL−1 for PHE. This method had a lower LLOQ than methods reported in the literature for both IBU and PHE (5–8, 11, 27). As expected, the HPLC–PDA method presented high sensitivity, wide working range and analysis with less sample volume compared to previous spectroscopic methods (20–24, 26). Additionally, this method showed similar or even higher sensitivity and the ability to quantify analytes across a broader linear working range than the reported GC–MS methods (14–17). These findings revealed that the proposed method has a high ability to detect lower IBU and PHE concentrations in human plasma. Another important advantage of the HPLC–PDA method over GC–MS is the ability to analyze thermally unstable compounds or those that are not volatile enough for GC–MS analysis. Therefore, the proposed new HPLC–PDA method was able to separate and detect polar and non-volatile compounds such as PHE simultaneously with IBU with high sensitivity. Additionally, the low cost, simple use, rapid method optimization and high reproducibility of HPLC–PDA made it a reliable and effective option for the analysis of IBU and PHE in a wide range of sample matrices, including complex biological, environmental and pharmaceutical samples.

In the study, an extraction procedure was developed that allows high efficiency extraction of IBU (93.8%) and PHE (92.8%) from a human plasma sample with a volume as low as 250 μL (5–12, 14, 15, 18, 27). The applied liquid–liquid extraction process involves a simple process that does not require the preliminary preparations, commercial filters and hydrolysis processes applied in solid phase extraction (8, 9, 16). In this extraction procedure, we have streamlined the analytical process by significantly reducing the volume of biological material required. Additionally, the use of organic solvents in amounts as low as 1 mL increased the extraction efficiency. Thus, this procedure saved both time and chemical usage while minimizing potential contamination risks. The high efficiency and minimized sample amount of the extraction procedure highlighted the robustness and reliability of the HPLC–PDA method, making it particularly advantageous for studies requiring sensitive and accurate quantification of IBU and PHE in complex matrices.

Forced degradation experiments were conducted in this study to evaluate the stability behavior of IBU, PHE and CAF under acidic, basic, neutral and oxidative stress conditions. The results showed that IBU and PHE maintained their peak shapes under all applied stress conditions, with only limited reductions in peak areas of 2–9% (Table SII). These findings indicate that both active substances are stable under the defined stress conditions. In contrast, significant chromatographic changes were observed for CAF depending on the stress conditions; peak shape was distorted under acidic and neutral conditions, peak tailing increased under basic conditions, and a significant increase in peak area occurred under oxidative conditions. These differences indicate that CAF exhibits a more sensitive chromatographic behavior to some stress conditions compared to other compounds. Under the applied conditions, no additional peaks corresponding to new and well-separated degradation products were consistently observed for IBU, PHE, and CAF and the obtained data were evaluated based on chromatographic parameters such as peak shape, peak area and peak symmetry. These results support the suitability of the developed HPLC–PDA method in terms of stability assessment.

It can be said that the successful application of the developed method to commercial pharmaceutical preparations demonstrates the robustness and reliability of the method. The recovery rates in the acceptable range of 98–102% for IBU (Artril® 400 mg tablet) and PHE (Cabral® 400 mg tablet) clearly emphasize the accuracy and precision of the method in real samples. These results play a vital role in ensuring its usefulness in QC analyses of pharmaceutical products.

However, the validity of the method in determining analytes in biological samples also demonstrates its versatility and effectiveness in clinical settings. The ability to accurately measure IBU and PHE concentrations at various time points in human plasma samples is very important for pharmacokinetic studies. These measurements play a critical role in understanding the absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion profiles of drugs. Thus, information is provided about drug dosage regimens, efficacy and safety profiles.

The application of the method in pharmacokinetic studies provides valuable information on drug interaction dynamics when IBU and PHE are administered simultaneously. Accurate measurement of plasma levels of these drugs over time allows researchers to better understand pharmacological interactions and potential implications for therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, the developed method not only meets the required validation parameters but also demonstrates practical applicability in pharmaceutical QC and clinical pharmacokinetic studies. Future studies may investigate the application of the method to other pharmaceutical formulations and biological matrices, thus contributing to the advancement of pharmaceutical and clinical research.

In this study, due to ethics committee restrictions and factors limiting volunteer participation, blood samples could only be collected at four predefined time points (pre-dose, 1.0, 4.0 and 8.0 h). This limitation in sampling frequency may restrict a more detailed characterization of the early absorption phase and the late elimination phase of the drugs. In particular, inclusion of earlier time points (e.g. 0.5 h) and extended sampling at later time points (e.g. 12.0–24.0 h) in future studies could provide a more comprehensive pharmacokinetic evaluation. Future investigations incorporating extended and denser sampling schedules are therefore warranted to further refine the pharmacokinetic profiles of IBU and PHE. Despite these limitations in sampling times and sample numbers, the developed HPLC–PDA method demonstrated high sensitivity and accuracy using small plasma volumes, enabling the reliable calculation of key pharmacokinetic parameters and the determination of drug pharmacokinetic profiles. Moreover, the short analysis time, applicability with low-cost instrumentation and wide linear range of the method make it attractive for routine QC analyses as well as pharmacokinetic studies. Overall, these findings indicate that the proposed method can be safely and effectively applied in both clinical research and industrial laboratory settings.

Conclusions

In this study, an HPLC–PDA method was developed for the detection of IBU and PHE in human plasma and pharmaceutical preparations. Detailed and comprehensive validation based on ICH guidelines demonstrates that this method is simple, sensitive, accurate and robust for routine QC analysis, and consistent, precise and reproducible for pharmacokinetic studies. The retention times observed for IBU and PHE (<5 min) indicate that the method is fast and effective, allowing rapid analysis, a crucial factor for therapeutic monitoring. The extraction procedure provides highly efficient recovery of IBU and PHE. In summary, the developed liquid chromatographic method stands out as a superior alternative for the determination of IBU and PHE, offering remarkable improvements in analysis time, data integrity and overall analytical performance. The applicability of this method could lead to more efficient workflows in a variety of laboratory environments, ultimately benefiting both research and industrial applications.
