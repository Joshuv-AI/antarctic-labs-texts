Plasmatic CXCL13 as a biomarker of primary resistance to immunotherapy and impaired peripheral immunity in non-small cell lung cancer

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunotherapy (IO) has improved prognosis of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC); however, some patients experience primary IO resistance (PIR). CXCL13 could be a promising PIR biomarker due to its role in antitumor immune responses.Methods177 patients with stage IV NSCLC receiving IO from the observational, multicenter, real-world study BLI-O were included from 15 centers in Spain. CXCL13 and 39 other cytokines were measured in 158 baseline (BS1) and 98 post-first-cycle of IO (BS2) plasma samples. Additionally, peripheral T and B cell BS2 immunophenotypes were determined. CXCL13 levels were also measured in plasma samples from 38 stage IIIA NSCLC patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy from the NADIM II trial (NCT03838159). PIR was defined as disease progression within 3 months in non-surgical cases or incomplete pathological response in surgical cases.ResultsMetastatic PIR patients had higher CXCL13 plasma levels compared to non-PIR patients, especially in BS2 samples, and exhibited a greater increase of CXCL13 levels after the first cycle of IO. Patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels showed shorter PFS (p = 0.0002) and OS (p = 0.0007). Females showed a lower percentage of high CXCL13 cases compared to male cases. CXCL13 did not influence the growth of NSCLC cell lines in vitro. Moreover, the expression of the CXCL13/CXCR5 axis was restricted to the immune compartment of tumors. Plasmatic CXCL13 levels were not associated with PD-L1 TPS expression nor TLS density in tumor tissue. However, at systemic level, patients with high CXCL13 levels exhibited impaired B and T cell phenotypes, along with elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, after first IO cycle. Finally, high BS2 CXCL13 levels in resectable cases were also associated with PIR.ConclusionsElevated CXCL13 levels after the first cycle of IO are associated with PIR and an altered peripheral immune profile in NSCLC, supporting its potential as a prognostic biomarker in these patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40364-026-00929-9.Keywords: CXCL13, Immunotherapy, NSCLC, Primary resistance, Biomarker

Background

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents the major subtype of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung tumors, and remains the foremost cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1]. More than half of the cases are diagnosed at locally advanced or metastatic stages of the disease (stages III and IV, respectively) [2]. Immunotherapy (IO)-based treatments, especially anti-PD-1/PD-L1 axis inhibitory agents, have significantly improved the clinical outcomes of these patients [3–9]. However, some patients exhibit primary IO resistance (PIR), often resulting in poor clinical outcomes. In this context, there is a notable lack of reliable biomarkers to accurately identify responders to IO therapies.

Tumor-intrinsic factors, including PD-L1 expression by tumor cells, low tumor mutation burden (TMB) and defects in HLA class I antigen presentation, may contribute to PIR. Nevertheless, these elements have a limited prognostic value for IO response [4, 10]. Among extrinsic factors, the immune composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key determinant of the response to IO-based therapies [11, 12]. Within this framework, the chemokine CXCL13 has gained particular attention.

CXCL13 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13), also known as B lymphocyte chemoattractant (BLC), plays a crucial role in immune regulation by recruiting B cells to secondary lymphoid organs. In addition to its effects on B cells, CXCL13 also exhibits chemotactic activity toward certain subsets of T cells, such as follicular helper T (Tfh) cells [13, 14]. It is produced by a diverse range of cell types, including stromal cells, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and immune cells like Tfh cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs).

CXCR5 is the sole known receptor for CXCL13 and is expressed across various immune cell populations, most notably B cells. It is also present on Tfh, facilitating their interaction with B cells within germinal centers (GCs). The CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis is thus essential for the organization and function of GCs and plays a pivotal role in B cell maturation into antibody-secreting plasma cells [15].

In cancer, the CXCL13/CXCR5 axis has attracted increasing attention due to its dual and context-dependent roles, which may vary according to disease stage and the immune compartment involved [16]. Within the TME, CXCL13 contributes to protective immune responses by promoting the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), which are organized sites of local immune activation and coordination. The presence of TLS is frequently associated with improved survival, particularly in patients undergoing IO [17–22].

Conversely, elevated systemic levels of CXCL13 have been associated with metastatic progression, poor prognosis, and resistance to immunotherapeutic approaches. This potential detrimental effect of elevated plasma CXCL13 levels may be mediated through the promotion of tumorigenic properties in cancer cells [23–26], the dysregulation of peripheral immune responses [27], or a combination of both mechanisms.

Given the involvement of the CXCL13/CXCR5 axis in key tumor-immune processes, plasma levels of CXCL13 may be associated with primary resistance to immunotherapy and could potentially serve as a non-invasive biomarker of clinical outcomes. To address this, we evaluated the association between plasma CXCL13 levels and immunotherapy outcomes in NSCLC patients receiving IO.

Methods

Patients

This study included two independent cohorts of patients with NSCLC: one comprising prospectively-recruited real-world metastatic cases (BLI-O project) and the other, potentially resectable locally advanced cases from NADIM II trial (NCT03838159). The BLI-O cohort consisted of 177 patients with stage IV NSCLC who received IO-based treatment, including IO monotherapy or chemotherapy plus IO (ChIO) in the first-line setting, as well as IO monotherapy in the second-line setting. Given the exploratory design of this real-world prospective study, no formal sample size calculation was undertaken, and the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients consecutively included during the study period.

From the NADIM II clinical trial, 38 patients with stage IIIA/B (American Joint Committee on Cancer, 8th edition criteria) NSCLC treated with ChIO before surgery were included. Pathological response of the resectable tumors was evaluated by determination of the percentage of residual viable tumor in the primary tumor and lymph nodes tested, and pathological responses were classified as complete (CPR, 0% of viable tumor cells); major (MPR, < 10% of viable tumor); and incomplete (IPR, > 10% of viable tumor). Clinical outcomes of patients included in NADIM II have been published previously [5].

Definition of primary immunotherapy resistance (PIR)

In the metastatic cohort (BLI-O), primary immunotherapy resistance (PIR) was defined as radiological disease progression within 3 months of treatment initiation. This timepoint was selected to capture tumors that fail to derive any initial clinical benefit from immune checkpoint blockade.

In the locally advanced cohort (NADIM II), PIR was defined as incomplete pathological response (IPR; >10% residual viable tumor in the surgical specimen) following three cycles of neoadjuvant ChIO, indicating the presence of a substantial fraction of tumor cells with primary resistance.

To ensure adequate drug exposure for the evaluation of primary treatment resistance, all patients included in this analysis from both cohorts received at least two cycles of immunotherapy.

Samples and processing

Peripheral whole-blood samples were collected before IO-based treatment initiation (BS1) and after the first cycle of IO received (BS2) in both BLI-O and NADIM II cohorts in EDTA tubes and were maintained at room temperature until they were processed within 24 h. Plasma was separated from the cellular fraction by two consecutive centrifugations (400 g for 10 min and at 500 g for 10 min), and plasma samples were frozen at -80 °C until processing. Then, cellular fraction was diluted to 1:1 in RPMI 1640, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted by density gradient with Lymphoprep (Stemcell). PBMCs were cryopreserved in 1:1 RPMI/FBS + 10% DMSO freezing medium at -80 °C until use. In addition, tissue samples at surgery were obtained from NADIM II patients and preserved in Formalin-Fixed, Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) blocks.

Cytokine quantification

In metastatic BLI-O cohort, 40 immune-related cytokines (including CXCL13) were measured in 158 baseline and 98 post-first-cycle of IO plasma samples using a cytokine array (Human Inflammation Array GS3, RayBiotech). Signals were normalized by dividing each value by the median of target signals on its slide and multiplying by the mean of all slide medians to correct for between-slide variability. Quality control procedures included the use of internal positive control spots present on each array to monitor assay performance and support normalization across slides (no reference sample was used).

In addition, in locally advanced NADIM II cohort, CXCL13 concentration was assessed in 35 BS1 and 29 BS2 undiluted plasma samples by ELISA (PicoKine ELISA, Boster) using a standard curve for quantification. Absorbance was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell lines and proliferation assay

A549 and H1299 lung cancer cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). For the proliferation assay, 30 000 cells were seeded per well in the presence of recombinant human CXCL13 (PreproTech) (50 ng/mL); PBS was used as a negative control. Cells were plated at days − 4, −3, − 2, and − 1, and on day 0 the number of cells per well was manually counted. Both experimental and control conditions were performed in triplicate for each cell line. Subsequently, CXCR5 expression was assessed by flow cytometry (Miltenyi Biotec).

Single-cell RNAseq and spatial transcriptomics

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on 15 representative blood samples at baseline of NSCLC NADIM cohort using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ platform (10x Genomics). Data was processed with CellRanger software (10x Genomics) and visualized using Loupe Browser (V.8.1.2). All samples were normalized together using SCTransform, followed by principal component analysis (PCA), clustering, and visualization with UMAP. Cell type identification was then carried out using the Monaco Immune dataset as a reference, utilizing the SingleR package.

Spatial transcriptomics was conducted on 8 surgical tissue samples from NSCLC NADIM patients by Visium Spatial for FFPE Gene Expression Kit for Human Transcriptome (10x Genomics), and data was processed with SpaceRanger software (10x Genomics). Tumor and stroma areas were defined by a pathologist. B cell spots were defined by the expression of CD19 and MS4A1; CD4 T cells were identified by the expression of CD4, CD3D, CD3E, and CD3G; and tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) regions were defined by the expression of MS4A1, CD19, CR2, and CXCL13. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare CXCR5 expression between tumor and stroma, B cell and non-B cells, and TLS and non-TLS regions.

PD-L1 immunohistochemistry

PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) was determined by immunohistochemistry (22C3 pharmDx, code SK006; Dako) in 170 tumor samples from the BLI-O cohort and in 34 tumor samples from the NADIM II cohort. Samples were considered PD-L1–positive when ≥ 1% of tumor cells exhibited membranous PD-L1 expression.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on FFPE tissue sections from 16 patients treated with neoadjuvant ChIO. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, permeabilized, and subjected to antigen retrieval with 10 mM citric acid. After blocking with 1% BSA, slides were incubated with anti-CD20 (GA604, Agilent), anti-CD23 (GA78161-2, Agilent), and conjugated anti-CD3 antibodies (ab307335, Abcam). Secondary antibodies Goat anti-Mouse IgG2a AF594 (A-21135, Thermo Fisher) and Goat ANTI-Mouse IgG1 AF488 (A-21121, Thermo Fisher), and DAPI (D1306, Invitro) were applied before mounting. Whole-slide images were acquired using a Stellaris 8 confocal microscope at 10X zoom. TLS, defined as aggregates of > 20 T and B cells, were manually counted by two independent observers. Mature TLSs (mTLS) were defined by CD23 expression. TLS and mTLS density were calculated by dividing the total number of TLS or mTLS by tissue area.

Flow cytometry

Immunophenotype of circulating B and T cells was determined from 83 to 30 PBMCs samples, respectively. Approximately, 3 to 5 million cells per sample were thawed and stained with fluorochrome conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). Cells were incubated 30 min at 4 °C in PBS 1X with viability marker (LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Violet Dead Cell Stain Kit, ThermoFisher) and FcR Blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec) to exclude dead cells and increase the specificity of immunofluorescent staining. After centrifugation, cells were incubated with fluorochrome conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in PBS 1X and acquired in a MACSQuant10 flow cytometer with MACSQuantify 2.8 software (Miltenyi Biotec).

Immunophenotype analysis

Flow cytometry data were analyzed using the FlowJo LLC v10 software (RRID: SCR_008520). Traditional supervised gating strategy was performed setting the positivity threshold with fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls (gating strategy are shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and S2). The frequency and marker expression of circulating immune cell subtypes were assessed, with marker expression quantified as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each subpopulation.

Total B cells (CD19+CD20+) were classified as transitional B cells (CD24++CD38++CD27−CD10+), naïve B cells (CD24intCD38+CD10−CD27−), memory B cells (including CD24+CD38+CD27+, CD24+CD38−CD27+ and CD24−CD38− CD27+). In addition, the activated portion (CD25+) of total B cell population and each subpopulation was assessed.

Percentage T cells were also determined by three different flow cytometry panels. T helper cells (CD3+CD4+) were selected and then subdivide in three main populations based on CD127 and CD25 surface markers: exhausted/naïve T cells (CD127+CD25−), activated T cells (CD127+CD25+) and regulatory T cells (CD127−/lowCD25+). Frequency of cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) were also determined and classified in different populations based on CD62L and CCR7 surface markers. Both T helper and cytotoxic T cells were classified as naïve (CD45RA+CD45RO−) or memory (CD45RA−CD45RO+). Thus, the activated portion (CD25+) of total CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations and their subpopulations were calculated. Finally, the expression of exhaustion and inhibition markers as PD-1, PD-L1 and LAG-3 were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS software (V.25.0), GraphPad Prism (V.8.0.2), and R (V.4.4.2) were used for statistical analyses. Non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to analyze differences between PIR and non-PIR patients, as well as between CXCL13 high and CXCL13 low patients. Stratification of patients based on CXCL13 levels was established using the area under the curve (AUC) from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank tests, and hazard ratios (HR). Multivariant Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the independent prognostic value of CXCL13 levels. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High levels of CXCL13 after IO are associated with PIR in metastatic NSCLC

Of the 177 patients with metastatic NSCLC, 23.2% had disease progression within 3 months after immunotherapy (IO) treatment and therefore were classified as patients with primary IO resistance (PIR). Patients without disease progression in this period (76.8%) were defined as non-PIR patients (Supplementary Figure S3). All PIR patients received at least two cycles of immunotherapy, with a median of three cycles (range 2–4).

Comparing clinical characteristics of PIR and non-PIR patients (Table 1), there were no significant differences in age (p = 0.734), sex (p = 0.188), smoking history (p = 0.071), ECOG (p = 0.146), histology (p = 0.494) and treatment received (p = 0.269).

PIR, Primary IO Resistance

Sixty-five patients (66.3%) were then classified as high BS2 CXCL13 and 33 patients (33.7%) as low BS2 CXCL13 based on ROC curve analysis for PIR, with an AUC of 0.743 (95% CI 0.63–0.86; p = 0.0003) (Fig. 1C). Using a cut-off value of 8,469 arbitrary units, this threshold achieved a sensitivity of 77.9% and a specificity of 65.8% for distinguishing PIR patients. Comparison of clinical characteristics between BS2 CXCL13 high and low groups showed that patients in the high CXCL13 group were significantly older than those in the low CXCL13 group (p = 0.001), while no other clinical characteristics differed significantly (Supplementary Table S2).

In the CXCL13 high BS2 group, 17 of 33 cases (51.5%) had PIR and 16 of 33 (48.5%) had non-PIR, while in the CXCL13 low BS2 group, 9 of 65 cases (13.8%) had PIR and 56 of 65 (86.2%) had non-PIR (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 1D). In addition, patients with high CXCL13 levels after the first cycle of IO were associated with significantly lower PFS (median, 2.8 vs. 8.5 months, HR = 2.25 [95%CI 1.32–3.81]; p = 0.0002) and OS (median OS, 5.9 vs. 17.5 months, HR = 2.32 [95%CI 1.28–4.20]; p = 0.0007) compared to low BS2 CXCL13 patients (Fig. 1E).

The prognostic value of BS2 CXCL13 levels was well maintained stratifying the cohort by main clinical factors ECOG, IO indication, PD-L1 positivity and age (Supplementary Figures S4 and S5).

Stratifying patients by sex and analyzing distribution across the high and low BS2 CXCL13 groups defined previously, BS2 CXCL13 high patients were predominantly male, 90.9% of female were BS2 CXCL13 low (p = 0.039), and their survival rates were similar to those low BS2 CXCL13 male (PFS p = 0.471 and OS p = 0.158) (Fig. 1F and G). In male population, BS2 CXCL13 levels remained a good predictor of patient prognosis (PFS, HR = 2.3 [95%CI 1.34–4.07]; p = 0.0004 and OS, HR = 2.1 [95%CI 1.17–3.87]; p = 0.005) (Supplementary Figure S4).

Finally, multivariable Cox regression confirmed that post-cycle 1 CXCL13 levels were an independent prognostic factor for both PFS (HR = 2.85, 95% CI 1.63–4.98; p < 0.001) and OS (HR = 2.40, 95% CI 1.36–4.21; p = 0.002), after adjusting for PD-L1 TPS, line, treatment, ECOG, sex, and age (Fig. 1H). Higher CXCL13 levels were associated with a 185% increased risk of progression and a 140% increased risk of death. Other variables including PD-L1, ECOG, and sex also retained independent prognostic value within the model.

Elevated plasma levels of CXCL13 after the first cycle of IO are associated with an altered peripheral B cell immunophenotype

Since high BS2 CXCL13 levels were associated with PIR and poor prognosis in metastatic NSCLC patients, we sought to investigate the potential mechanisms underlying this association.

First, we evaluated the implications of plasmatic CXCL13 levels in tumor biology through the analysis of NSCLC cell lines. However, tumor cells did not express CXCR5 at baseline culture conditions and CXCL13 conditioning in cell media did not increase CXCR5 expression nor proliferation in NSCLC tumor cell lines (Supplementary Figure S6), supporting an exclusive immune-cell mediated mechanism controlling detrimental plasmatic CXCL13 effects. In line with this, no significant association was observed between circulating CXCL13 levels and tumor PD-L1 TPS (Supplementary Figure S7), suggesting that CXCL13-related effects are independent of this established tumor biomarker. Indeed, spatial transcriptomics data in post-treatment tumor tissues after neoadjuvant ChIO in 8 cases, showed that CXCR5 was mostly expressed in stroma areas compared to tumor areas (Supplementary Figure S8A and B), specifically on B cell and TLS regions within stroma areas (Supplementary Figure S8C). Moreover, single-cell analysis of PBMCs from 15 NSCLC patients revealed that CXCR5 expression was almost exclusively restricted to B cells, with minimal expression detected in T cells (Supplementary Figure S8D).

Thus, we hypothesized that circulating CXCL13 may exert its main effects either by modulating tumor associated TLS or by directly influencing B cell function at systemic level. Regarding the impact of plasma CXCL13 levels on intratumoral TLS and mTLS density, we did not observe a significant association (Supplementary Figure S9), supporting a role for plasmatic CXCL13 primarily as a systemic immune modulator.

These findings supported the analysis of circulating B cells to better understand the role of the CXCL13 plasmatic levels in NSCLC IO responses. The immunophenotype of B cells was assessed from 30 PBMC samples derived from BS2 extraction of patients with metastatic NSCLC (15 patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels and 15 with low CXCL13 levels according to ROC curve).

Patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels had a lower percentage of transitional B cells compared to patients with low BS2 CXCL13 levels (high CXCL13 0%, IQR 0.0-0.1 vs. low CXCL13 1.21%, IQR 0.31–2.4; p = 0.002), as well as lower frequency of activated transitional B cells (CD25+) (p = 0.010) (Fig. 2A).

Although there were no differences in the frequency of naïve and activated naïve B cells between groups (p = 0.361 and p = 0.454, respectively), MFI of CD24 was significantly reduced in both populations in the high BS2 CXCL13 group compared to the low BS2 CXCL13 group (757 vs. 1041, p = 0.002; and 794 vs. 1052, p = 0.002). In addition, MFI of CD38 was decreased in naïve B cells of patients with high CXCL13 levels after first cycle of IO (904 vs. 1155, p = 0.046), suggesting a more mature naïve B cell phenotype in these patients (Fig. 2B).

Regarding memory B cells, the frequency was lower in high BS2 CXCL13 patients compared to low BS2 CXCL13 patients (high CXCL13 43.9%, IQR 21.7–52.3 vs. low CXCL13 53.7%, IQR 47.8–64.5; p = 0.024), as well as the MFI of CD27 in memory B cell population and their activated fraction (CD25+) (p = 0.018 and p = 0.019, respectively) (Fig. 2C), supporting an impaired B cell memory formation.

Elevated levels of CXCL13 are associated to T cell dysregulation and increased inflammatory cytokine profile

Due to the close interaction between B and T cells, and the fact that a small subset of T cells may express CXCR5, we evaluated by flow cytometry whether high levels of CXCL13 could also be affecting the immunophenotype of circulating T cells.

Of the 83 PBMC BS2 samples analyzed, 36.1% corresponded to patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels and 63.9% to patients with low CXCL13 levels after the first cycle of IO. While there were no significant differences in total CD8+ T cells between high and low BS2 CXCL13 patients, the percentage of activated cytotoxic T cells (CD28+) was lower in high BS2 CXCL13 patients (high BS2 CXCL13 31.3%, IQR 21.6–57.3 vs. low CXCL13 50.8%, IQR 35.2–67.3; p = 0.011) (Fig. 3A and B). Consistent with this results, high BS2 CXCL13 patients showed a higher frequency of the fraction LAG3+ and PD-L1+ (p = 0.010 and p = 0.005, respectively), indicating a more inhibited immune phenotype in this patients (Fig. 3B).

Finally, patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels had a lower percentage of cytotoxic T cells with extravasation capacity (CD62L+) (p = 0.005), as well as a lower percentage of memory (CD45RA¯ CD45RO+) cytotoxic B cells CD62L+ (high BS2 CXCL13 16.8%, IQR 9.3–28.2 vs. low BS2 CXCL13 10.3%, IQR 5.9–24.1; p = 0.022) (Fig. 3C).

To obtain a more comprehensive view of the circulating environment in these patients, we analyzed 39 additional cytokines related to inflammatory processes in 98 plasma samples after the first cycle of IO. Regarding differential levels of cytokines between high and low BS2 CXCL13 patients (Fig. 3D), patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels exhibited significantly elevated concentrations of various cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-1α, and G-CSF. However, when comparing the two groups, no cytokine was found to be differentially downregulated in high BS2 CXCL13 patients, suggesting that these patients have a more inflamed circulating environment after the first cycle of IO.

High CXCL13 plasma levels after first cycle of IO are a PIR biomarker in locally advanced NSCLC

To validate the reliability of CXCL13 as a biomarker of PIR, we analyzed CXCL13 levels from 30 BS1 and 29 BS2 plasma samples corresponding to 38 locally advanced NSCLC patients treated with ChIO who were participants of NADIM II clinical trial. All patients received 3 cycles of neoadjuvant ChIO.

In this neoadjuvant surgical cohort, since 3-months PFS and OS was 100% (Fig. 4A), PIR was defined as patients with incomplete pathological response after neoadjuvant ChIO. Thus 16 patients (42.1%) achieved IPR and were classified as PIR, while 22 patients (57.9%) achieved CPR or MPR and were classified as non-PIR patients. Discussion

In this study we evaluated the implication of CXCL13 plasma levels in primary IO resistance (PIR) in patients with metastatic and potentially resectable locally advanced NSCLC treated with IO, as well as their influence in the circulating immune status and tumor TLS density after treatment.

PIR is a frequent event in NSCLC patients, since close to 30% of these patients exhibit early disease progression after treatment [28]. While the SITC consensus defines primary immunotherapy resistance (PIR) as progression within 6 months [29], we adopted a more stringent 3-month cutoff to specifically capture unequivocal primary resistance and exclude early acquired resistance. All PIR-classified patients received at least two treatment cycles, ensuring that early progression reflected true biological resistance. This approach is supported by the rapid nature of immune activation, which typically occurs within 4 weeks of checkpoint blockade initiation [30]. Following a comparable evaluative timeframe, primary resistance in the neoadjuvant cohort was assessed directly through pathological examination of the surgical specimen. Given that pathological response exists along a prognostic continuum [31], we utilized the clinically validated and commonly used Incomplete Pathological Response (IPR; >10% viable tumor) as a surrogate for primary resistance, indicating the presence of a substantial fraction of tumor cells with inherent resistance to therapy. We acknowledge that the definition of PIR in both cohorts remains a potential study limitation, as any discrete cutoff within a biological continuum is inherently subject to some degree of arbitrariness.

Classical tumor-intrinsic factors show limited ability to predict PIR, likely due to the spatial and temporal complexity of the tumor microenvironment. By contrast, peripheral blood offers a minimally invasive and more informative alternative, reflecting systemic immune responses and providing real-time insights into the immunological state [32].

In the present study, high plasma levels of CXCL13 were linked to PIR in metastatic and locally advanced patients treated with IO, particularly after the first cycle of immunotherapy (BS2). The association observed in two clinical cohorts with such different characteristics reinforces the clinical utility of CXCL13 as a PIR biomarker. Moreover, the finding that BS2 CXCL13 plasma levels, but not baseline levels, accurately predicted PIR highlights the importance of early longitudinal biomarker monitoring during treatment. In addition, the magnitude of CXCL13 increase after first dose of IO was greater in PIR patients, supporting that circulating CXCL13 may play a role during IO responses, specifically associated to mechanisms of immune evasion or dysregulated immune activation [33]. Importantly, the prognostic value of BS2 CXCL13 levels was independent of age, treatment regimen and ECOG status, which supports its potential utility in real-world clinical scenario. However, patients with high plasma CXCL13 levels were significantly older than those with low levels. Age-related increases in systemic inflammation (“inflammaging”) could contribute to higher CXCL13 levels, reflecting a more dysregulated immune environment in older patients [34]. Importantly, in our analysis, age was not associated with PIR, suggesting that the observed impact of CXCL13 on therapy resistance is independent of patient age, as confirmed multivariate analysis.

In addition, there was sex-based differences in CXLC13 plasmatic levels. Despite high BS2 CXCL13 levels remained a strong predictor of poor prognosis among male patients, most female patients had low BS2 CXCL13 plasma levels, and their survival outcomes were comparable to male patients with similarly low levels. Sex-based differences in immune system function and response to IO have been increasingly recognized, with male and female patients exhibiting distinct patterns in immune cell composition, activation thresholds, and cytokine production [35, 36]. Furthermore, sex hormones such as estrogen may influence B cell activation and other immune contexts [37]. These baseline immunological differences could modulate the systemic expression and impact of CXCL13.

Patients with lower post-treatment CXCL13 levels experienced a markedly reduced risk of disease progression and death, suggesting that early on-treatment CXCL13 dynamics may capture biological processes relevant to immune response that are not fully reflected by conventional clinical or tumor-based biomarkers. Indeed, there was no association between tissue PD-L1 levels and plasma CXCL13 levels after the first cycle of immunotherapy, suggesting that CXCL13 provides complementary information beyond established biomarkers in solid tumors. The independent prognostic value of CXCL13 highlights its potential as a minimally invasive biomarker for early risk stratification in patients receiving immunotherapy.

CXCL13 was initially identified as a B cell chemoattractant, but it has gained increasing relevance due to its association with the formation of TLS within the TME, that has been linked to favorable prognosis in NSCLC patients treated with IO [38].

In the present study, we did not observe an association between plasma CXCL13 levels at BS1 or BS2 and TLS or mTLS density after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy. This is consistent with the negative prognostic role of elevated plasma CXCL13 levels and suggests a distinct role for CXCL13 in the tumor—where its expression is associated with and restricted to the presence of TLS—and in the circulation, where we observed a clear association with a dysfunctional B and T cell phenotype and a pro-inflammatory cytokine profile. Beyond TLS abundance, the functional competence of mature TLS is likely critical for response to neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, and future studies should investigate how elevated systemic CXCL13 influences TLS function and the broader tumor-immune context [39].

Elevated CXCL13 levels have previously been associated with poor prognosis [23, 25, 26]. However, to our knowledge, this is the first time such an association has been linked to a systemically dysregulated immune phenotype, as most previous evidence has focused on the tumor compartment [40].

In our case, we did not observe relevant CXCR5 expression in the tumor compartment—neither in different cell lines nor in spatial transcriptomics data from tumors following neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy (ChIO). Additionally, circulating CXCL13 levels were independent of tumor PD-L1 TPS. Moreover, exogenous CXCL13 did not modulate CXCR5 expression or tumor cell proliferation, suggesting that the CXCL13/CXCR5 axis does not directly impact tumor cell-intrinsic behavior in NSCLC. Traditionally, CXCR5 expression has been primarily associated with B cells [15], a finding corroborated by our single-cell transcriptomic analyses as well as in spatial transcriptomics in which CXCL13 and CXCR5 is restricted to B and TLS areas.

B cell maturation from transitional precursors to long-lived memory is a complex, tightly regulated process that is highly sensitive to inflammatory cues and systemic immune perturbations [41].

Our data show that patients with high BS2 CXCL13 levels display a pro-inflammatory cytokine milieu with a transversal perturbation across the B-cell differentiation axis: a depletion of transitional B cells (total and activated), a shift toward a more “mature” phenotype within the naive compartment (reduced CD24 and CD38), and a contracted memory compartment with lower CD27 expression. The depletion of transitional B cells observed in high CXCL13 patients may reflect accelerated or dysregulated peripheral maturation, altered egress from the bone marrow, or preferential recruitment of these cells into inflamed tissues—phenomena previously described in chronic inflammatory and autoimmune conditions [41]. In fact, low transitional B cells are associated with defective B cell tolerance, increased autoreactive B cell survival, and enhanced autoantibody production, all of which are central to the pathogenesis and maintenance of chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases [41, 42]. Importantly, transitional B cells are enriched for immunoregulatory subsets that can temper excessive inflammation; loss of this compartment could therefore remove a critical brake on systemic immune activation and promote dysfunctional responses [43].

In the same line, naïve B cells from patients with high BS2 CXCL13 plasma levels displayed a more mature phenotype, characterized by decreased expression of CD24 and CD38 [44, 45]. Crucially, this apparent premature maturation does not culminate in effective memory formation: instead, we observe both a lower fraction of memory B cells and decreased CD27 expression, findings that are compatible with impaired memory generation described in chronic infection and inflammation [46, 47]. These results dovetail with our prior work showing that a low frequency of memory B cells is associated with poorer outcomes after perioperative chemo-immunotherapy [32], and collectively support a model in which sustained CXCL13-associated inflammation reprograms peripheral B-cell differentiation and potentially contributes to primary resistance to immunotherapy [48].

Although CXCR5 is highly expressed on B cells within immune populations, a smaller subset of T cells also expresses this receptor and is therefore responsive to CXCL13 [49]. According to our results, BS2 CXCL13 high patients have a lower percentage of activated cytotoxic T cells, which also display a more exhausted or inhibited T cell phenotype, along with impaired extravasation capacity in the memory subset. CXCR5⁺ cytotoxic T cells have been described as a distinct functional subset with antitumor activity and high potential to infiltrate certain tumors [50, 51]. However, cytotoxic T cells expressing LAG3 and PD-L1 have been associated with severe T cell exhaustion and represent a highly dysfunctional state [52]. In addition, overstimulation of T lymphocytes often leads to an exhausted phenotype that may also be affected by elevated levels of proinflammatory mediators such as IFN-γ, IL-1α and G-CSF in patients with elevated plasma levels of CXCL13 after the first cycle of IO. Indeed, the absence of downregulated cytokines in CXCL13 high patients underscores the proinflammatory state, which may result from the observed altered immune phenotypes [53]. Importantly, CXCL13 is a chemokine directly involved in B-cell recruitment and germinal center responses, which may capture aspects of immune dysregulation that are not fully reflected by traditional inflammation markers.

Given these associations, our study demonstrates strong correlative links between elevated CXCL13, B and T cell dysfunction, and a pro-inflammatory cytokine milieu, yet it remains uncertain whether CXCL13 actively contributes to this dysfunction or simply reflects underlying immune dysregulation associated with PIR. Further longitudinal mechanistic studies are needed to clarify this relationship and determine whether CXCL13 could be a potential therapeutic target.While our study provides valuable insights into the role of plasmatic CXCL13 as a biomarker of primary immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC, several limitations should be considered. First, the limited sample size within the NADIM II perioperative cohort. Second, in clinical practice, cut-offs would need to be validated in independent populations and potentially adjusted according to the specific assay and patient context. Third, subgroup analyses stratified by clinical characteristics often involved relatively small sample sizes, limiting statistical power and precluding a clear assessment of the prognostic impact within certain subcohorts. Fourth, different methodologies were used to assess plasma CXCL13 levels across cohorts, which may introduce variability. However, both methods are well-validated and widely used in clinical research, ensuring reliable quantification of CXCL13 levels, and positive results in both cohorts reinforce the utility of CXCL13 as biomarker.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated plasma levels of CXCL13 after the first cycle of IO are associated with primary IO resistance (PIR) in NSCLC patients, as well as increased systemic inflammation characterized by an immature and exhausted immune system, highlighting its promising role as an early biomarker for predicting PIR.
