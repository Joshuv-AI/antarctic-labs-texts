The Learning Curve of Junior Endoscopists in EUS‐Guided Gastroenterostomy With the Wireless Simplified Technique: A Prospective Study
Authors: Giuseppe Vanella, Francesco Frigo, Francesca Perelli, Rukaia Barà, Patrick Maisonneuve, Livia Archibugi, Matteo Tacelli, Gaetano Lauri, Daniele Bellafante, Niccolò Bina, Rubino Nunziata, Paolo Biamonte, Ruggero Ponz De Leon Pisani, Gabriele Capurso, Paolo Giorgio Arcidiacono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionLearning curve of EUS‐guided Gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE) has been explored through retrospective studies involving few elite senior endoscopists (SE), with procedural time as the main surrogate outcome. This study aims to evaluate the training of junior endoscopists (JE) approaching the technique and cover clinically relevant patient outcomes.MethodsFrom a prospective single‐center registry (PROTECT, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04813055) of EUS‐GE for malignant Gastric Outlet Obstruction using the Wireless Simplified [WEST] technique, the learning curve of 3 JE experienced in pancreatobiliary endoscopy was analyzed to identify changing points (“knots”) applying linear and spline regression and cumulative sum control chart (CUSUM) on several continuous and dichotomic variables. EUS‐GE performed by a SE and different indications were excluded.ResultsFrom a database of 165 EUS‐GEs, 100 were performed by one novice endoscopist with prior EUS‐GE exposure (JE1) and 35 by two naïve endoscopists (JE2/JE3). On the largest curve (JE1), procedural time and fluoroscopy time did not show any improvement. The use of fluoroscopy (median Air Kerma: 267 Gy [172–506]) showed a significant improvement after 57 interventions (spline regression) and 72 interventions (CUSUM), after which the curve stabilized. The need for supervision/backup of a SE showed a knot after 32 interventions. Technical success (99%), Clinical success (96%), Misdeployments (2%) and AEs (6%) were stable along the learning curve. Conversion from EUS‐GE to enteral stenting (11%) tended to decrease over time. Despite naïve endoscopists required significantly higher fluoroscopy time (234 vs. 187 s, p = 0.009) and procedural time (55 vs. 43 min, p = 0.002) than JE1, efficacy and safety outcomes were not different from the general cohort.ConclusionsFrom a prospective registry, ≈30 EUS‐GE seems required for independent practice, and ≈55–70 to minimize and stabilize the use of fluoroscopy. Endoscopists learning EUS‐GE in high‐volume institutions using a standardized technique do not compromise high clinical and safety standards.

Introduction

Gastric outlet obstruction (GOO), mostly related to pancreatobiliary or gastrointestinal malignancies, is a severe condition that can severely compromise the nutritional status and the quality of life of patients [1]. In this scenario, standard alternatives, such as enteral stents or surgical bypass have shown limited efficacy/safety ratios. In the last 10 years, EUS‐guided gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE), that is the possibility of creating gastro‐enteric bypass through a Lumen Apposing Metal Stent (LAMS) [2], has emerged as a leading therapeutic option, as long as data are showing comparable efficacy to surgery with faster recovery [3, 4, 5, 6] and reduced risk of reintervention compared with enteral stents [7, 8].

However, to date, EUS‐GE is perceived as one of the most challenging techniques in endoscopy, and little is known about its learning curve. This has been evaluated only through retrospective studies involving a limited number of highly experienced senior endoscopists (SE) [9, 10], pioneering the procedure using non‐standardized techniques. Moreover, procedural time was mostly used as the sole primary surrogate endpoint in these initiatives.

The aim of this study was to prospectively assess the training progression in EUS‐GE of junior endoscopists (JE) adopting the Wireless EUS‐guided gastroenterostomy simplified technique (WEST) [8, 11], focusing on technical performance as well as on clinical outcomes.

Methods

This was a single‐center prospective study. All consecutive patients referred for EUS‐GE at San Raffaele Institute, a tertiary referral academic hospital, between December 2020 and October 2024 were enrolled in a Prospective Registry Of Therapeutic EndoscopiC ulTrasound (PROTECT; NCT04813055). The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice and was approved by the local Ethics Committee (identifier, 178/INT/2020). Informed consent was obtained for the procedure and inclusion in the registry.

Baseline variables were registered upon inclusion in the registry. Intraprocedural technical variables were also recorded, including devices used, the endoscopists involved, intraprocedural troubleshooting and performance measures such as procedural time and the amount of fluoroscopy used.

After the procedure, patients received daily clinical evaluations during hospitalization and telephonic follow‐up, with revision of the electronic medical file, at least every 60 days after discharge.

Some of the procedures in this study have been previously reported [8, 12], while this cohort does not overlap with other initiatives including the Authors [4, 13, 14].

Endoscopists Involved in the Study

For the purpose of this study, Junior Endoscopists (JE) were defined as gastroenterologists experienced in pancreatobiliary endoscopy (PBE), performing > 300 EUS‐ERCP/year, and within the first 5 years post‐training status, having performed < 5 EUS‐GE at the beginning of the study.

In particular, one JE (JE1) underwent previous formal training in EUS‐GE and had performed < 5 supervised procedures before the beginning of the study.

Two additional endoscopists (JE2 and JE3) started their training in therapeutic EUS during the study interval and performed their first EUS‐GE within the study period.

All JEs had performed > 10 preparatory interventional EUS procedures (fluid collections, gallbladder or biliary drainage) before embarking on their first EUS‐GE. All procedures were performed under the supervision of an endoscopist with EUS‐GE experience of > 25 cases.

Procedure: EUS‐GE With the WEST Technique

All the procedures were performed according to the WEST technique (see Figure 1 and Video S1), as previously described [2, 8], under general anesthesia with orotracheal intubations, involving a single dose of prophylactic antibiotic or prolonged antibiotics in case of ascites.

EUS‐guided gastroenterostomy with the wireless simplified EUS‐GE technique (WEST). (a–b) A guidewire is placed across the stenosis under endoscopic and fluoroscopic guidance, followed by advancement of an oro‐jejunal tube (OJT), usually with a single pigtail placed in the first jejunal loop. (c–d) Under EUS and fluoroscopic guidance, the loop containing the OJT is found using a linear echoendoscope. (e) Saline is injected through the OJT to create an appropriate target for LAMS placement. (f) An electrocautery‐enhanced lumen apposing metal stent is advanced under EUS guidance. (g) Upon release of the proximal flange of the LAMS, blue‐dyed fluid (injected through the OJT) flows in the stomach; (h) “contrast bypass confirmation”: contrast injected through the OJT can be seen flowing through the LAMS in the stomach.

Briefly, a therapeutic gastroscope is typically used to identify the stenosis and advance a guidewire and an orojejunal single‐pigtail tube (OJT) across the stenosis in the first jejunal loop.

Thereafter, the small bowel was distended with a mixture of saline and indigo carmine to enhance visualization under EUS. Using a linear echoendoscope (EG38‐J10UT; Pentax Medical), a distended jejunal loop containing the OJT in close apposition to the gastric wall, with adequate stability and absence of intervening vessels was identified. An electrocautery‐enhanced LAMS (Hot Axios, Boston Scientific) is then advanced “freehand” applying pure cutting current through the gastric and intestinal walls, without the use of a guidewire (“wireless”). Once the jejunal lumen is accessed, the distal flange is deployed under EUS guidance. A gentle retraction of the system is then performed to secure wall apposition, followed by deployment of the proximal flange inside the operative channel of the scope, and then pushed into the stomach.

All procedures included in this study involved intraprocedural dilation of a 20 mm LAMS with a dilation balloon (CRE, Boston Scientific, usually up to 18 mm).

Performance Measures

Variables used for the learning curve analysis were divided into technical performance measures and clinical outcomes.

As for technical performance measures, procedural time and the use of fluoroscopy were used.

In particular, Procedural Time in minutes was defined as the time between the beginning and the end of EUS‐GE, including all the preliminary phases (e.g., identifying the stenosis, advancing the orojejunal tube) and the post‐release confirmation phases (e.g., injection of contrast through the OJT to confirm its aspiration in the stomach through the LAMS). A scope‐in/scope‐out definition was not used, as most procedures were also preceded by EUS with fine needle aspiration plus rapid on site evaluation or followed by biliary drainage [12].

The use of Fluoroscopy through a mobile under‐couch C‐arm fluoroscopic system (Ziehm Vision RFD, Reggio Emilia, Italy) was analyzed through two different exposure metrics. Fluoroscopy time was measured in seconds as reported by the C‐arm. Cumulative Radiation Dose was measured as Cumulative Air Kerma (Gy). The assumption to include these parameters was that the amount of fluoroscopy correlated with the confidence and dexterity in placing the oro‐jejunal tube (see Figure 1b), identifying an appropriate window for EUS‐GE (Figure 1c), and the need of additional confirmation maneuvers of correct LAMS placement (see Figure 1h).

The need for SE backup was registered for each procedure. This included any instance of intraprocedural consultation or the necessity to transfer control of the scope to a more experienced endosonographer, to the extent that the SE's involvement was ultimately reported in the procedural report.

As for clinical outcomes, technical success, clinical success and AEs were registered.

Technical success was defined as successful LAMS placement between the stomach and jejunum, independent of initial misdeployments.

Clinical success was defined as a Gastric Outlet Obstruction Scoring System (GOOSS) [15] ≥ 2 (i.e., ability to eat at least soft solids) after the intervention.

AEs were scored through the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) lexicon [16]. For the purpose of this study, misdeployments were classified as AEs even when they were intraprocedurally solved and did not require any escalation of medical care. In this case, the ASGE lexicon was declared Not Applicable (NA).

Statistical Analysis

Outcomes are reported as medians [Interquartile Range] or numbers (proportions).

Procedures performed by JE1, constituting the majority of EUS‐GE in this study, were used for quantitative analysis of technical performance measures through cumulative sum control chart (CUSUM) and adaptive regression.

CUSUM analysis is used to detect changes in operator performance and identify inflection points that may correspond to different learning phases (initial learning, competence, proficiency). For each case, the deviation from a predefined target value is calculated. These deviations are cumulatively summed over the sequence of cases, producing a CUSUM curve. A positive slope indicates performance below the target level, while a downward trend suggests improvement.

To model the evolution of operator performance over time, we also used Adaptive Regression [17]. This algorithm automatically constructs a piecewise linear model that captures nonlinear relationships by introducing breakpoints, or “knots”, along the sequence of cases. The resulting model consists of multiple linear segments, each fitted to a portion of the data, which together approximate the overall learning trajectory.

Technical performance measures and clinical outcomes were compared between JE1 and JE2‐3 to evaluate whether the very first initial phase of the learning curve resulted in worse performances and/or worse clinical outcomes for patients. Continuous variables were compared through the Mann‐Whitney test whilst categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐Squared or Fisher's test as appropriate

A two‐tailed p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analyses were performed using MedCalc software (Ostende, Belgium) and SAS software version 9.4 (Cary, NC).

The need for supervision/backup from a SE showed a statistically significant decrease after 32 procedures (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2d). Assistance primarily consisted of real‐time guidance or feedback on the appropriate operative window, device positioning and scope manipulation, but never involved scope takeover.

The rate of AEs did not show a significant decrease over time; however, it tended to a lower incidence after 32 interventions (4.4% vs. 9.4%, p = 0.3). The number of scheduled EUS‐GE converted to ES (N = 12, 10.7%) non‐significantly decreased after the N = 32 knot from 15.8% to 8.1% (p = 0.3).

Inter‐Operator Performance

The procedures performed by the two naïve endoscopists (JE2/3, 35 procedures, Table 2) were performed under the supervision of an SE in all but 2 cases (94.3%), but required scope takeover in only 11 (31.4%) cases. Discussion

According to the results of this prospective study, ∼30 EUS‐GE procedures are required to transition to independent practice, ∼55 to substantially reduce fluoroscopy use, and ∼70 to achieve consistently minimal fluoroscopy reliance.

These thresholds are slightly higher compared to similar initiatives. The study by Jovani et al. showed proficiency after approximately 25 cases and mastery after about 40 cases using CUSUM on procedural time [10]. A study by Tyberg et al. found that procedural efficiency was reached after about 7 cases using the same methodology [9]. Notably, procedural time did not significantly improve throughout the learning curve in our series.

Nevertheless, this study offers some unique characteristics.

First, previous studies were conducted during the early developmental stages of the technique, involving heterogenous procedural variations: non‐cautery‐enhanced LAMS, over‐the‐wire stent deployment, variable stent sizes, and heterogeneous strategies for small bowel identification and distention, including intraluminal balloons, direct needle puncture and even reverse enterogastrostomy, introducing variability in the most challenging part of the procedure. Indeed, these earlier studies report non‐negligible complication rates, up to a 30% rate of LAMS misdeployment, more frequent during the initial study phase. In contrast, the present study applied a fully standardized and mature protocol (the Wireless Simplified EUS‐GE Technique, or WEST [8, 11]), reducing technical variability and allowing for a more accurate assessment of learning progression. This might explain why naïve endoscopists obtained efficacy and safety results comparable to the rest of the cohort, and also why the general rate of misdeployment in this study is significantly lower (2.2%) than the one reported in the literature (approximately 10% [18]).

Furthermore, prior studies analyzed highly experienced endoscopists at the peak of their careers, who had presumably performed hundreds of propaedeutical EUS‐guided drainages, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true procedural volume required to master EUS‐GE in less experienced hands. Conversely, in our study, procedures performed by SEs were excluded, while the study focused on the progress of JEs with no to low‐volume personal exposure to EUS‐GE specifically. However, it is important to acknowledge that JEs had substantial prior expertise in PBE, namely solid basis of EUS, ERCP, adverse events management and previous exposure to at least 10 cases of interventional EUS. This background reinforces the notion that EUS‐GE should be undertaken by endoscopists with advanced therapeutic training given the technical complexity of the procedure.

Crucially, these JEs were trained within a high‐volume, experienced center, using a standardized technique and under structured supervision [19]. This supervised training, including verbal assistance and occasional instrument takeover, likely contributed to the preservation of excellent safety and efficacy outcomes despite longer procedural and fluoroscopy times in the early phases. Such a setting may also benefit from the so‐called “preceding surgeon factor” whereby knowledge transfer and accumulated institutional expertise flatten the learning curve for subsequent trainees in the same institution [20].

Moreover, in former initiatives, procedural time was the only evaluated outcome, which has been criticized as a surrogate marker for competency [21]. Indeed, competence should involve the evaluation of predefined technical or safety metrics. For EUS‐GE, this might be identified in the rate of correctly placed or misdeployed LAMS, respectively. However, misdeployment was a rare event in this cohort, and therefore not appropriate to capture learning curve inflection points. The relatively more frequent rate of AEs did not show any turning point during the learning curve. However, the study prospectively tracked whether the performing JE required the assistance of more experienced endosonographers, finding a significant knot after 32 procedures. Although this outcome inherently contains a subjective component, reliance on expert assistance was deemed to be a meaningful surrogate marker of procedural maturity in the context of consistently high safety and efficacy. Finally, the radiation dose was used as an objective surrogate marker of the endoscopists' confidence with the technique. Fluoroscopy is employed at multiple critical steps [19]: to advance the OJT, to confirm the echoendoscope position in relation to the OJT, to verify correct LAMS deployment, to guide LAMS dilation, and for troubleshooting when necessary [2]. Therefore, a reduction in radiation exposure may reflect increasing procedural fluency and confidence.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single high‐volume expert center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to settings with lower procedural volumes or less standardized training environments. Second, although the inclusion of JEs enhances the relevance of the learning curve analysis, their performance was closely supervised by SEs, potentially masking some difficulties that might emerge in less controlled settings. Lastly, the learning‐curve metrics were only derived from JE1, who contributed a sufficient number of procedures to allow reliable modeling; nevertheless, inclusion of JE2/JE3 remains meaningful, as it demonstrates that even before achieving independency, efficacy and safety are preserved under structured supervision.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this is the first prospective study reporting a structured assessment of the EUS‐GE learning curve of junior endoscopists within a standardized, high‐volume training program. The exclusive use of a mature, standardized technique (WEST) further enhances external validity and reproducibility. Rather than challenging existing learning‐curve models, these data refine them by separating early supervised independence (reduced need for senior oversight) from full technical mastery (stable minimal fluoroscopy use), and by incorporating objective markers beyond procedure duration, including adverse events.

The study suggests that when JEs are trained in expert centers using standardized protocols, their learning curve does not compromise clinical outcomes, reinforcing the feasibility and safety of scaling up EUS‐GE training programs in appropriately equipped settings. These findings prompt broader reflections on both operator and institutional prerequisites for EUS‐GE training. Endoscopists approaching this technique should possess a solid background in advanced endoscopy, particularly therapeutic EUS, with EUS‐GE representing the final step of a progressive training pathway including experience in fluid collection drainage, gallbladder and biliary drainage. Equally, centers aiming to train new operators must ensure sufficient procedural volume, potentially at least 50 EUS‐GE cases per year, to provide meaningful exposure before allowing independent practice. These considerations raise the question of whether such relatively infrequent procedures should be centralized in high‐volume referral centers. This finally reinforces the need for international endoscopy societies to lead the development of structured training curricula, designate accredited high‐volume training centers, and promote network‐based learning models to safely disseminate this promising technique and ensure its long‐term integration into clinical practice.

While structured training is increasingly available for core ERCP and EUS, formal curricula, simulation, and competence assessment tools are still lacking in most centers [22, 23], particularly for newer procedures such as EUS‐GE, which involve higher risks of severe adverse events [24] and lack validated ex vivo models. Especially since the EUS‐GE learning curve involves higher radiation doses than other interventional EUS techniques [25], this procedure is also more prone to gender‐related barriers [26, 27].

Looking ahead, innovative training modalities such as virtual proctoring and augmented reality platforms may help extend expert supervision beyond high‐volume centers [28, 29]. After an initial phase of hands‐on training in referral centers, remote guidance could support trainees in their local environments, potentially accelerating learning curves while reducing the logistical and financial burden of prolonged centralized training. Future studies are needed to validate the safety, feasibility, and effectiveness of such approaches in the context of complex procedures such as EUS‐GE.
