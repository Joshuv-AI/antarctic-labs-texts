The MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 Module Integrates Ethylene and Strigolactone Signals to Regulate Cold Tolerance via a CBF‐Dependent Pathway in Apple
Authors: Xiao‐Wei Zhang, Xiu‐Hong An, Rui‐Rui Xu, Yi Tian, Baoyou Liu, Yuepeng Han, Jian‐Ping An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough ethylene and strigolactone (SL) are key regulators of cold tolerance in plants, the molecular crosstalk between their signalling pathways is poorly understood. Here, we identified the transcription factor GOLDEN2‐LIKE1 (MdGLK1) as a central integrator of ethylene and SL signalling during the apple (Malus × domestica) cold stress response. MdGLK1 enhanced cold tolerance by recruiting BRASSINAZOLE‐RESISTANT1 (MdBZR1), a core component of brassinosteroid signalling, thereby promoting MdBZR1‐mediated transcriptional activation of the cold‐responsive genes C‐REPEAT BINDING FACTOR1 (MdCBF1) and MdCBF2. Ethylene signalling modulated this process through ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE3‐BINDING F‐BOX PROTEIN1 (MdEBF1), which targets MdGLK1 for proteasomal degradation, thereby attenuating MdGLK1‐enhanced cold tolerance. In addition, SUPPRESSOR OF MORE AXILLARY GROWTH2‐LIKE8 (MdSMXL8), a repressor of SL signalling, interfered with the formation of the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 complex, further suppressing MdGLK1‐mediated cold tolerance. Collectively, the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1‐MdCBF1/2 regulatory module operates as a signalling hub that integrates the ethylene and SL pathways during cold stress adaptation in apple. These findings elucidate the molecular mechanism by which MdGLK1 coordinates ethylene and SL signals to enhance cold tolerance, thereby offering potential targets for optimising the trade‐off between growth and stress tolerance in plants.

Introduction

Under global climate change, low‐temperature stress has become a major constraint on plant growth and development, significantly reducing crop yields and limiting geographic distribution (Kerbler and Wigge 2023; Qian et al. 2024). Cold stress compromises cell membrane integrity, promotes excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and suppresses photosynthetic efficiency, ultimately leading to death (Guan et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024). Plants have evolved complex adaptive mechanisms to survive such conditions (Dahro et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023; Ding et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the molecular regulatory networks that enable plants to maintain a dynamic balance between growth and cold tolerance under low‐temperature stress are poorly understood.

The C‐REPEAT BINDING FACTOR1 (CBF) transcription factors (TFs) are central regulators of the cold stress response (Jia et al. 2016; Barrero‐Gil and Salinas 2017; Shi et al. 2018). Upon exposure to cold stress, CBF genes are rapidly induced, and the activated CBF proteins bind specifically to the promoters of downstream cold‐responsive (COR) genes, activating their expression to enhance cold tolerance (Thomashow 2010; Song et al. 2021; Kidokoro et al. 2022). The transcriptional regulation of CBF genes is tightly controlled by many upstream TFs that interact with distinct cis‐regulatory elements within their promoters. Among these, INDUCER OF CBF EXPRESSION1 (ICE1) is the most extensively characterised activator (Chinnusamy et al. 2003; Tang et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2024). The ICE1‐CBF‐COR cascade constitutes a well‐established, evolutionarily conserved signalling module in the cold stress response (Kim et al. 2015; Ding and Yang 2022; Kidokoro et al. 2022). Several other transcriptional regulators besides ICE1 contribute to this network, including MYB15 (Agarwal et al. 2006), ICE2 (Fursova et al. 2009), SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CONSTANS1 (Seo et al. 2009), PSEUDO‐RESPONSE REGULATOR5/7/9 (Nakamichi et al. 2009), CALMODULIN‐BINDING TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATOR1/2/3/4/5 (Doherty et al. 2009; Kim et al. 2013; Kidokoro et al. 2017), PHYTOCHROME‐INTERACTING FACTOR3/4/7 (PIF3/4/7) (Kidokoro et al. 2009; Jiang et al. 2017; Xu and Deng 2020), CIRCADIAN CLOCK‐ASSOCIATED1 (Dong et al. 2011), LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (Dong et al. 2011), ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE3 (EIN3) (Shi et al. 2012), CESTA (Eremina et al. 2016), BRASSINAZOLE‐RESISTANT1 (BZR1) (Li, Ye, et al. 2017), BASIC TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR3/3‐LIKE (Ding et al. 2018), NIN‐LIKE PROTEIN7 (Ding et al. 2022) and REVEILLE4/8 (Kidokoro et al. 2023). These factors collectively regulate the CBF‐mediated COR transcriptional network in Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ). Upstream regulators of CBF in apple (Malus × domestica) include ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (MdHY5) (An et al. 2017), MdMYB23 (An et al. 2018), BASIC HELIX–LOOP‐HELIX33 (An et al. 2020), B‐BOX37 (An et al. 2021) and BRI1‐EMS‐SUPPRESSOR1‐INTERACTING MYC‐LIKE PROTEIN1 (MdBIM1) (An et al. 2023). Notably, besides the CBF‐dependent cold signalling pathway, plants utilise CBF‐independent pathways to respond to cold stress (Park et al. 2018). For example, HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENE9 (HOS9) and GIGANTEA enhance cold tolerance independently of CBF expression (Zhu et al. 2004; Cao et al. 2005), underscoring the complexity and diversity of COR regulatory networks in plants.

Ethylene is a key gaseous hormone that regulates diverse aspects of plant growth, development and the stress response (Schaller 2012; Dubois et al. 2018). However, its role in cold stress appears to be species‐dependent, as studies have reported varying and sometimes conflicting findings (Castroverde and Dina 2021; Huang et al. 2023). In winter rye ( Secale cereale ), grapevine ( Vitis vinifera L.), apple and tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum L.), cold stress induces ethylene biosynthesis (Yu et al. 2001; Sun, Zhao, et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2022; Lv et al. 2025). Exogenous application of the ethylene precursor 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid (ACC) enhances cold tolerance in tomato, Arabidopsis and trifoliate orange ( Poncirus trifoliata L.) (Catalá et al. 2014; Dong et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022). Furthermore, ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR52 (ERF52) positively regulates the cold stress response in rice ( Oryza sativa L.) (Xu et al. 2024). Taken together, these findings support a positive regulatory role for ethylene in cold tolerance. However, ethylene has been implicated as a negative regulator of cold tolerance. In Arabidopsis, gain‐of‐function ethylene mutants constitutive triple response1‐1 and ethylene response1‐1, as well as seedlings overexpressing EIN3, exhibit lower freezing tolerance (Shi et al. 2012). In soybean ( Glycine max ), ethylene suppresses CBF‐mediated cold stress responses (Robison et al. 2019). Similarly, tomato NAM, ATAF1/2 and CUC23 suppress cold resistance by promoting ethylene biosynthesis (Wang et al. 2024).

Strigolactones (SLs) are a novel class of carotenoid‐derived plant hormones that were first identified in 1966 from root exudates of cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) and named for their role in stimulating the germination of root parasitic plants (Cook et al. 1966; Yoneyama et al. 2010; Brewer et al. 2013). Substantial progress has been made in elucidating SL signal transduction pathways. SLs reportedly induce the formation of a complex between the receptor protein DWARF14 and the F‐box protein MORE AXILLARY GROWTH2 (MAX2), which facilitates ubiquitination and degradation of the transcriptional repressor SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2‐LIKE (SMXL), thereby activating SL signalling (Nakamura et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2013; Yao et al. 2016, 2018). SLs regulate a broad spectrum of developmental and stress‐related processes. They promote hyphal branching of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, suppress shoot branching, modulate root and leaf architecture and contribute to plant adaptation under abiotic stressors and nutrient limitations (Akiyama et al. 2005; Gomez‐Roldan et al. 2008; Umehara et al. 2008; Koltai 2011; Ha et al. 2014; Sun, Bi, et al. 2016). Emerging evidence highlights their role in the cold stress response. For example, applying the synthetic SL analogue GR245DS enhances cold tolerance in tomato (Chi et al. 2025). In Arabidopsis, MAX2 facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of WRKY41, relieving its repression of CBF gene expression, thereby improving cold tolerance (Wang et al. 2023).

Although the regulatory roles of ethylene and SL in the cold stress response have been documented, the molecular basis of their crosstalk remains unclear. In this study, we identified the TF GOLDEN2‐LIKE1 (MdGLK1) as a central regulator of cold tolerance in apple, acting through the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1‐MdCBF1/2 signalling module. MdGLK1 physically interacted with MdBZR1, a core transcriptional regulator of brassinosteroid (BR) signalling, and enhanced its ability to activate MdCBF1 and MdCBF2, thereby promoting cold tolerance. Ethylene and SL functioned as positive regulators of cold tolerance in apple. MdGLK1 mediated the integration of ethylene and SL signalling by recruiting the ethylene signalling element EIN3‐BINDING F‐BOX PROTEIN1 (MdEBF1) and the SL repressor MdSMXL8. Ethylene stabilised MdGLK1 by inhibiting MdEBF1‐mediated proteasomal degradation, thereby enhancing cold tolerance. Concurrently, SL signalling mitigated the repressive effect of MdSMXL8 on the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 complex, further strengthening the cold response. In conclusion, our findings reveal a molecular framework in which MdGLK1 coordinates ethylene and SL signals to regulate cold tolerance. These findings advance our understanding of cold response regulatory networks and offer a foundation for improving cold resilience in fruit crops through molecular breeding and biotechnological strategies.

Results

MdGLK1 Positively Modulates Plant Cold Tolerance

We focused on the regulatory mechanism of anthocyanin biosynthesis and the cold stress response in plants. GLK1 functions as a positive regulator of anthocyanin biosynthesis (Zhao et al. 2021; Li et al. 2023). Therefore, we investigated whether GLK1 is involved in the cold stress response. To this end, reverse transcription‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) was used to examine MdGLK1 expression in wild‐type plants under a chilling treatment. The transcription level of MdGLK1 was markedly induced by chilling, peaking at 1 h (Figure 1A). A 2000‐bp MdGLK1 promoter sequence was fused to the pCAMBIA1391 vector containing a GUS tag to generate the recombinant construct, which was genetically transformed into apple callus (Table S1). The ProMdGLK1‐GUS transgenic callus was treated at 4°C for 1 h, with untreated callus serving as the control. GUS staining and activity assays showed that chilling activated MdGLK1 promoter activity (Figure 1B).

To investigate the role of MdGLK1 in the response to cold stress, we generated three stable transgenic apple seedlings overexpressing MdGLK1. The freezing tolerance assay showed that overexpressing MdGLK1 conferred enhanced tolerance to freezing stress compared with the wild‐type control, as indicated by lower electrolyte leakage (Figure 1C–D). To determine whether MdGLK1 regulates cold tolerance via the CBF pathway, we analysed the expression of CBF genes and their downstream targets after the chilling treatment. The expression levels of CBFs and CBF target genes were significantly higher in MdGLK1‐overexpressing plants than in the wild‐type control (Figure 1E). We visually assessed the response of MdGLK1 transient transgenic apple leaves to cold stress using ROS staining. ROS accumulation was markedly reduced in leaves overexpressing MdGLK1 compared with the empty vector control, whereas suppressing MdGLK1 led to excessive ROS accumulation (Figure 1F). Similarly, overexpressing MdGLK1 upregulated, while its silencing downregulated, the expression of CBFs and their downstream targets (Figure 1G). A cold tolerance assay of apple callus showed that overexpressing MdGLK1 promoted better callus growth at low temperature (Figure S1), whereas callus with suppressed MdGLK1 expression was hypersensitive to chilling (Figure S1). Moreover, the role of MdGLK1 in cold tolerance was further validated by ectopic overexpression in Arabidopsis and poplar ( Populus trichocarpa ). MdGLK1 enhanced the survival rate of Arabidopsis seedlings and reduced electrolyte leakage in poplar seedlings under cold stress (Figure 1H–K), accompanied by the upregulation of CBF genes (Figure S2). Therefore, these results suggest that MdGLK1 positively regulates apple cold tolerance through a CBF‐dependent pathway.

MdBZR1 Physically Interacts With MdGLK1

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying MdGLK1‐mediated cold tolerance in apple, a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay was performed to screen for MdGLK1‐associated factors using precipitated MdGLK1‐GFP protein as bait. MdBZR1, a key component of BR signalling, was identified as a potential MdGLK1‐interacting partner (Figure S3). A yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay showed that yeast cells co‐transformed with MdGLK1 fused to pGAD and MdBZR1 fused to pGBD could grow on selective medium, whereas the absence of either construct impaired growth (Figure 2A), indicating that MdBZR1 directly interacts with MdGLK1 in yeast cells.

To confirm this interaction, an in vitro pull‐down assay was conducted using the purified recombinant proteins HIS‐tagged MdGLK1, GST‐tagged MdBZR1 and HIS tag alone. MdBZR1‐GST successfully pulled down MdGLK1‐HIS but not the HIS control (Figure 2B), indicating that MdBZR1 interacts with MdGLK1 in vitro. A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay further supported this interaction. Strong fluorescence signals were observed in the nuclei of onion epidermal cells co‐infiltrated with MdBZR1‐YFP C and MdGLK1‐YFP N (Figure 2C), demonstrating that MdBZR1 interacts with MdGLK1 in the nucleus. Consistent with the Y2H, pull‐down and BiFC results, a co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay confirmed that MdBZR1 interacts with MdGLK1 in vivo, as GUS‐tagged MdBZR1 was co‐immunoprecipitated by MdGLK1‐GFP (Figure 2D; Figure S4). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that MdBZR1 interacts with MdGLK1 in vitro and in vivo.

MdBZR1 Improves Plant Cold Tolerance by Directly Activating CBF Expression

We next evaluated the role of MdBZR1 in cold tolerance. Compared with the empty vector control, transient overexpression of MdBZR1 in apple leaves reduced chilling‐induced ROS accumulation, accompanied by upregulation of CBFs and their target genes; in contrast, silencing MdBZR1 led to excessive ROS accumulation and downregulation of CBFs and their targets (Figure 2E–F). Transgenic apple callus overexpressing MdBZR1 exhibited a chilling‐insensitive phenotype compared with wild‐type callus, whereas suppressing MdBZR1 caused chilling sensitivity (Figure S5). Approximately 10% of wild‐type Arabidopsis seedlings survived after freezing at −4°C for 1 h, while the survival rate of plants ectopically expressing MdBZR1 was ~40% (Figure 2G–H). MdBZR1 overexpression consistently increased the expression of CBF genes (Figure S6). To investigate the role of MdBZR1 in BR‐mediated cold tolerance, wild‐type and transgenic Arabidopsis plants were treated with 2,4‐epibrassinolide (EBR) and frozen. Notably, the survival rate of EBR‐treated MdBZR1 transgenic seedlings increased by 35%, whereas that of wild‐type seedlings increased by only 15% (Figure 2G–H). These results confirm that MdBZR1 acts as a positive regulator of BR‐mediated cold tolerance.

The markedly inducible expression of MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 by MdBZR1 suggests the possibility that MdBZR1 binds to their promoters. A previous study reported that the E‐box and BRRE motifs serve as BZR1 binding sites in the CBF1 and CBF2 promoters in Arabidopsis, respectively (Li, Ye, et al. 2017). We identified an E‐box and a BRRE motif in the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters, respectively (Figure 2I; Tables S2–S3). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)‐PCR analysis showed that the MdCBF1 promoter region containing the E‐box motif and the MdCBF2 promoter region containing the BRRE motif were enriched in the immunoprecipitated MdBZR1‐GUS protein (Figure 2J), indicating that MdBZR1 binds to the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters in vivo. Additionally, electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was used to assess the binding of MdBZR1 to the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters in vitro. When MdBZR1 was incubated with the MdCBF1 probe containing the E‐box motif and the MdCBF2 probe containing the BRRE motif, binding bands were detected (Figure 2K). Adding unbiotin‐labelled probes gradually weakened these bands, and mutation of the E‐box and BRRE motifs abolished MdBZR1 binding (Figure 2K). These results demonstrate that MdBZR1 binds to the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters in vivo and in vitro.

We also examined the effect of MdBZR1 on the transcriptional activity of the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters using a dual‐luciferase (LUC) assay. Co‐transfection of the MdBZR1 effector with the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 reporters, respectively, resulted in higher LUC activity; while mutation of the binding sites abolished the enhanced activity (Figure 2L), indicating that MdBZR1 activates MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 transcription.

MdGLK1 Promotes MdBZR1 Transcriptional Activity

To test the effect of MdGLK1 on MdBZR1‐mediated transcriptional regulation of CBF, we performed additional EMSA and dual‐LUC assays. Incubating MdGLK1 with CBF promoter probes did not produce binding bands, indicating that MdGLK1 does not directly bind to the MdCBF1 or MdCBF2 promoters (Figure 3A–B). However, adding MdGLK1 altered the binding ability of MdBZR1 to the CBF promoters. MdBZR1 binding to the MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoters gradually increased with increasing concentrations of MdGLK1 (Figure 3A–B). In the dual‐LUC assay, MdGLK1 alone did not affect CBF promoter activity; but co‐expression of MdGLK1 with MdBZR1 enhanced the transcriptional activation of CBF by MdBZR1 (Figure 3C–D). These results indicate that MdGLK1 enhances MdBZR1‐mediated transcriptional activation of MdCBF1 and MdCBF2.

To explore whether MdGLK1 regulates cold tolerance through MdBZR1, we silenced MdBZR1 in MdGLK1‐overexpressing apple leaves and callus (Figure S7). Silencing MdBZR1 reduced the cold tolerance conferred by MdGLK1, as indicated by the impaired ability of MdGLK1 to reduce the accumulation of ROS after the chilling treatment (Figure 3E). Similarly, inhibiting MdBZR1 increased the cold sensitivity of MdGLK1‐overexpressing callus (Figure 3F–G). These findings suggest that MdGLK1 regulates cold tolerance partially through MdBZR1.

MdEBF1 Physically Interacts With MdGLK1

Treatment with ACC, a direct precursor in ethylene biosynthesis, upregulated the expression of MdGLK1 and enhanced its protein accumulation (Figure 4A; Figure S8), suggesting that MdGLK1 may be involved in ethylene signalling. Ethylene influences cold tolerance in plants (Dong et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024; Lv et al. 2025). To examine the role of MdGLK1 in ethylene‐mediated cold tolerance, ACC was applied exogenously to wild‐type and MdGLK1 transgenic apple seedlings, followed by a cold tolerance assay. The ACC treatment reduced electrolyte leakage from 40% to 32% in wild‐type seedlings, and from 30% to 15% in MdGLK1‐overexpressing seedlings after freezing stress (Figure 4B–C), suggesting that MdGLK1 enhances ethylene‐mediated cold tolerance.

When screening for MdGLK1‐associated factors, the ethylene signalling regulator MdEBF1 was identified as a potential interacting partner of MdGLK1 (Figures S3, S9). We hypothesised that MdEBF1 may be involved in regulating MdGLK1‐mediated cold tolerance. To detect the interaction between MdEBF1 and MdGLK1 in vitro, we performed Y2H and pull‐down assays. Yeast cells co‐expressing MdEBF1‐pGBD and MdGLK1‐pGAD recombinant constructs grew on selective medium (Figure 4D), indicating that MdEBF1 interacts with MdGLK1 in yeast. The GST‐tagged MdEBF1 recombinant protein showed affinity for HIS‐tagged MdGLK1 (Figure 4E), confirming a physical interaction between MdEBF1 and MdGLK1. Additionally, recombinant MdEBF1 and MdGLK1 vectors were infiltrated into onion epidermal cells for a BiFC assay. Fluorescence signals were observed in the nuclei of cells co‐expressing MdEBF1‐YFP C and MdGLK1‐YFP N (Figure 4F), indicating that MdEBF1 and MdGLK1 interact in the nucleus. Moreover, the Co‐IP assay confirmed that MdEBF1 interacts with MdGLK1 in vivo (Figure 4G; Figure S10). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MdEBF1 interacts with MdGLK1 in vitro and in vivo.

MdEBF1 Reduces MdGLK1 ‐Enhanced Cold Tolerance

To investigate the role of MdEBF1 in cold tolerance, we examined the chilling tolerance of apple seedlings overexpressing MdEBF1. Under chilling stress, these seedlings exhibited increased electrolyte leakage and downregulated expression of CBFs and their target genes compared with wild‐type controls (Figure 5A–D), indicating that overexpressing MdEBF1 reduces chilling tolerance. A chilling tolerance assay using transiently transformed apple leaves also supported this finding. Compared with the empty vector control, leaves overexpressing MdEBF1 accumulated more ROS and showed reduced expression of CBFs and their target genes (Figure 5E–G). In contrast, apple leaves expressing a MdEBF1‐silencing construct accumulated less ROS than the wild‐type and exhibited induced expression of CBFs and their targets (Figure 5E–G). Consistent with these observations in apple seedlings and leaves, a chilling tolerance assay in apple callus further confirmed that MdEBF1 negatively regulates cold tolerance (Figure S11). To examine the role of MdEBF1 in ethylene‐mediated cold tolerance, ACC was applied exogenously to wild‐type and MdEBF1 transgenic apple seedlings, followed by a cold tolerance assay. ACC treatment reduced electrolyte leakage from 45% to 35% in wild‐type seedlings, whereas MdEBF1‐overexpressing seedlings showed only a slight change (Figure S12), suggesting that MdEBF1 antagonises ethylene‐improved cold tolerance.

To investigate the effect of MdEBF1 on MdGLK1‐mediated cold tolerance, we obtained transgenic apple leaves and callus co‐expressed by MdEBF1 and MdGLK1. Cold tolerance assays revealed that overexpressing MdEBF1 negatively affected the cold tolerance enhanced by MdGLK1 (Figure 5H–J; Figure S13).

MdEBF1 Facilitates the Proteasomal Degradation of MdGLK1

Given that the F‐box protein MdEBF1 interacts with MdGLK1 and negatively regulates MdGLK1‐mediated cold tolerance enhancement, we hypothesised that MdEBF1 may mediate MdGLK1 ubiquitination. Thus, we performed an in vivo ubiquitination assay. MdGLK1‐GFP protein was immunoprecipitated from MdGLK1 single transgenic and MdGLK1‐MdEBF1 co‐transgenic apple callus, and its ubiquitination level was assessed. A faint ubiquitinated MdGLK1 protein band was detected, and its intensity increased in the presence of MdEBF1 (Figure 6A), indicating that MdEBF1 promotes MdGLK1 ubiquitination in vivo. Next, we assessed the effect of MdEBF1 on MdGLK1 stability using an in vitro degradation assay. HIS‐tagged MdGLK1 protein was incubated with total proteins extracted from wild‐type or transgenic apple callus overexpressing or repressing MdEBF1. The half‐life of MdGLK1 was longer in wild‐type samples than in those overexpressing MdEBF1, but shorter than in MdEBF1‐repressing samples (Figure 6B; Figure S14). Applying the proteasome inhibitor MG132 abolished MdGLK1 degradation (Figure 6B). We further examined the effect of MdEBF1 on MdGLK1 protein abundance in vivo. Compared with MdGLK1 single transgenic callus, overexpressing MdEBF1 reduced MdGLK1 protein levels, while suppressing MdEBF1 promoted MdGLK1 accumulation (Figure 6C). These results indicate that MdEBF1 promotes MdGLK1 degradation via the proteasome pathway. To evaluate the role of MdEBF1 in ethylene‐regulated MdGLK1 ubiquitination and stability, we treated MdGLK1 transgenic callus with the ethylene biosynthesis inhibitor aminoethoxyvinylglycine (AVG). The AVG treatment increased MdGLK1 ubiquitination and decreased its protein abundance. However, suppressing MdEBF1 expression significantly impaired the AVG‐induced ubiquitination and degradation of MdGLK1 (Figure 6D–E). Taken together, these results suggest that MdEBF1 mediates the proteasomal degradation of MdGLK1 in an ethylene‐dependent manner.

Discussion

Apple is a globally important fruit crop whose growth, yield and quality are highly sensitive to cold stress. Frequent extreme climate events, particularly late‐spring frosts and winter freezing, have often inhibited growth, reduced yield and deteriorated fruit quality, causing significant economic losses. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the response of apple to low‐temperature stress will provide a theoretical foundation for breeding cold‐resistant cultivars and improving cold hardiness.

The MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 Complex Improves Cold Tolerance Through a CBF‐Dependent Pathway

GLK1 is a member of the GARP superfamily of MYB TFs and is a well‐established positive regulator of chloroplast development and photosynthesis (Fitter et al. 2002; Waters et al. 2008, 2009; Gang et al. 2019; Muino et al. 2024). It also regulates seedling photomorphogenesis (Alem et al. 2022; Veciana et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024), anthocyanin and carotenoid biosynthesis (Li et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025), flowering (Susila et al. 2023), leaf senescence (Rauf et al. 2013) and the biotic and abiotic stress responses (Savitch et al. 2007; Murmu et al. 2014; Li et al. 2022). However, its role in the cold stress response has not been reported. In this study, we identified MdGLK1 as a positive regulator of cold tolerance in apple, as its overexpression significantly enhanced cold tolerance; whereas silencing MdGLK1 increased sensitivity to cold stress (Figure 1).

GLK1 generally exerts its functions by directly binding to target gene promoters or recruiting interaction partners (Choi et al. 2024). CBF functions as a master regulatory switch in the plant cold stress response (Barrero‐Gil and Salinas 2017; Shi et al. 2018). The MdGLK1‐mediated upregulation of CBF suggests that MdGLK1 regulates apple cold tolerance in a CBF‐dependent manner (Figure 1). In Arabidopsis, BZR1, a core regulator of BR signalling, positively modulates cold tolerance by directly binding to the CBF promoter and activating its expression (Li, Ye, et al. 2017). The MdGLK1‐interacting protein MdBZR1 is conserved in apple and also enhances cold resistance by directly activating MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 expression (Figure 2). Although MdGLK1 does not directly activate MdCBF1 and MdCBF2, it significantly enhances MdBZR1‐mediated transcriptional activation of CBF. Moreover, the cold resistance conferred by MdGLK1 was markedly reduced when MdBZR1 expression was suppressed (Figure 3). In conclusion, MdGLK1 enhances apple cold tolerance through the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1‐MdCBF1/2 module. The characterisation of GLK1 in cold resistance expands its known pleiotropic regulatory roles (Zheng et al. 2024).

MdEBF1 Is Responsible for the Ethylene‐Regulated Protein Turnover of MdGLK1

Post‐translational modifications such as ubiquitination, phosphorylation and sumoylation play key roles in regulating the plant cold stress response (Barrero‐Gil and Salinas 2013). For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligase HOS1 mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of ICE1, thereby negatively regulating cold tolerance (Dong et al. 2006). The protein kinases MITOGEN‐ACTIVATED PROTEIN KINASE3/6 and BRASSINOSTEROID‐INSENSITIVE2 (BIN2) phosphorylate ICE1 to promote its degradation, whereas OPEN STOMATA1 stabilises ICE1 via phosphorylation (Ding et al. 2015; Li, Ding, et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2017; Ye et al. 2019). The SUMO ligase SAP and MIZ1 catalyse ICE1 sumoylation (Miura et al. 2007). In Arabidopsis, EBF1, a key component of the ethylene signalling pathway, promotes proteasomal degradation of PIF3, thereby relieving repression of CBF gene expression and enhancing cold tolerance (Jiang et al. 2017). Here, apple MdEBF1 exhibited a regulatory role opposite to that of Arabidopsis EBF1. Overexpressing MdEBF1 increased cold stress sensitivity in apple, whereas its silencing enhanced cold tolerance (Figure 5). Therefore, these findings reveal a species‐specific role of ethylene in the cold stress response and offer new insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying low‐temperature adaptation across plant species.

Studies on GLK1 have primarily focused on its transcriptional regulation, while its post‐translational modification remains relatively underexplored, with only sporadic reports of ubiquitination and phosphorylation. GLK1 is tightly regulated by the ubiquitin‐proteasome system in response to plastid signals (Tokumaru et al. 2017). The E3 ubiquitin ligase CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC1 mediates GLK1 ubiquitination and degradation to inhibit chloroplast development, whereas the protein kinase BIN2 stabilises GLK1 through phosphorylation (Lee et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021). Here, MdGLK1 participated in the ethylene‐mediated cold stress response and was identified as a ubiquitination substrate of the F‐box protein MdEBF1 (Figure 4). Further analysis revealed that ethylene modulated MdGLK1 ubiquitination and stability: treatment with the ethylene biosynthesis inhibitor AVG increased MdGLK1 ubiquitination and reduced its protein abundance, while suppressing MdEBF1 significantly attenuated the AVG‐induced destabilisation of MdGLK1 (Figure 6). These findings demonstrate that MdEBF1 regulates MdGLK1 turnover during ethylene‐mediated cold tolerance. The MdEBF1‐MdGLK1 module expands our understanding of the GLK1 ubiquitination network and offers a new direction for exploring GLK1 post‐translational modification.

MdGLK1 Serves as a Key Hub Integrating Ethylene and SL Signals

The synergistic regulation of hormones and cold signalling pathways is crucial for survival during the cold stress response (Peleg and Blumwald 2011; Shi et al. 2015; Waadt et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2024). Hormones co‐regulate cold resistance through a complex interaction network. In Arabidopsis, the jasmonic acid (JA) signalling repressor JASMONATE‐ZIM DOMAIN (JAZ) integrates the JA, ethylene and CBF cold stress signalling pathways by interacting with EIN3 (Zhu et al. 2011; Shi et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2013). Similarly, apple ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE4, a key regulator of abscisic acid (ABA) signalling, co‐regulates the ICE1‐CBF signalling pathway with MdJAZ1 and MdJAZ2 (An et al. 2022). MdHY5 integrates auxin and ABA signalling, while MdBIM1 mediates crosstalk between BR and JA signals (An et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025). In tomato, SlPIF4 regulates the biosynthesis of ABA, JA and gibberellin (GA) under low‐temperature stress, and in wheat ( Triticum aestivum L.), the methyltransferase TaSAMT1 is involved in BR‐ and salicylic acid (SA)‐mediated freezing tolerance (Wang et al. 2020; Chu et al. 2024). However, the mechanisms underlying the interactions among different hormone signalling cascades in cold resistance remain unclear. In this study, MdGLK1 was involved in the cold stress response regulated by ethylene and SL, providing new insight into the hormone interaction network.

GLK1 plays an important role in plant growth and development and is regulated by multiple hormone signals. Loss of GLK1 enhances SA‐induced stress responses (Lee et al. 2023). GLK1 regulates the ABA signalling pathway via the GLK1‐WRKY40‐ABI5 transcriptional cascade (Ahmad et al. 2019). It is also involved in JA‐mediated pathogen resistance (Murmu et al. 2014). In this study, we reveal the underlying molecular mechanism by which apple MdGLK1 positively regulates the ethylene‐ and SL‐modulated cold stress response. MdGLK1 enhances ethylene‐induced cold resistance through the MdEBF1‐MdGLK1 module. In the SL signalling pathway, the SL repressor MdSMXL8 suppresses MdGLK1‐mediated cold resistance by interfering with the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 interaction (Figures 7, 8). MdEBF1‐MdGLK1 and MdSMXL8‐MdGLK1 act as key regulatory modules through which MdGLK1 integrates ethylene and SL signals, offering new insight into the hormonal crosstalk of cold stress adaptation.

We propose a molecular model in which MdGLK1 regulates the cold stress response by integrating ethylene and SL signals in apple (Figure 8H). MdGLK1 enhances cold tolerance by promoting the transcriptional activation ability of MdBZR1 on MdCBF1 and MdCBF2. Ethylene and SL act as positive regulators in the apple cold stress response, with MdGLK1 functioning as a central hub linking their signals via interaction with MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8. Ethylene stabilises MdGLK1 by inhibiting MdEBF1‐mediated ubiquitination and degradation, while SL enhances cold tolerance by relieving MdSMXL8‐mediated suppression of the MdGLK1‐MdBZR1 complex. In conclusion, this study elucidated a key molecular mechanism by which MdGLK1 modulates the cold stress response and hormonal crosstalk, offering a theoretical basis and strategic direction for improving cold tolerance and stress resistance in apple breeding.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Apple tissue culture seedlings (Malus × domestica, GL‐3) and poplar seedlings were grown under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod at 24°C. Apple callus (Malus × domestica, Orin) was cultured in continuous darkness at 24°C. Arabidopsis seedlings ( Arabidopsis thaliana , Col‐0) were grown under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod at 22°C.

Plasmid Construction

The MdGLK1 coding sequence was fused with pXSN harbouring a GFP tag to generate an overexpression plasmid, while a specified MdGLK1 fragment was cloned into pXSN to create a suppressed expression plasmid. The coding sequences of MdBZR1, MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8 were individually inserted into pRI101‐GUS to generate overexpression constructs, whereas specified fragments of MdBZR1, MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8 were assembled into pRI101 to produce suppressed expression constructs. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

Generation of Transgenic Plant Materials

Stable genetic transformation of apple seedlings was performed using 20‐day‐old tissue culture seedlings. Wounds were made on young leaves using a sterile scalpel. After treatment, the leaves were incubated in the dark at 24°C for 1 day, then immersed in Agrobacterium at 24°C for 0.5 h. The infected leaves were kept in the dark for 3 days, then transferred to light conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 24°C for cultivation until buds emerged at the wound sites. Transgenic apple seedlings were identified by RT‐qPCR. Fifteen‐day‐old apple callus was used for stable transformation. The callus was incubated with Agrobacterium at 24°C for 0.5 h, then kept in the dark at 24°C for 1 day before being screened on selective medium containing designated antibiotics. Transgenic apple callus was identified by RT‐qPCR. Transient expression in detached apple leaves and stable transformation of poplar were conducted as previously described (An et al. 2021). Transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings were generated by the floral dip method (Clough and Bent 1998).

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the plant materials using the RNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), and cDNA was synthesised using the RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Ohtsu, Japan). RT‐qPCR analyses were performed on a Real‐Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S4.

Cold Tolerance Assay

Cold tolerance assays were conducted on apple seedlings and callus, detached apple leaves and Arabidopsis and poplar seedlings as previously described (An et al. 2023). Twenty‐day‐old apple tissue culture seedlings grown at 24°C were treated at 4°C for 6 days, followed by recovery at 24°C for 2 days to assess seedling growth. Newly cultured apple callus was grown at 24°C for 5 days, then exposed to 4°C for 10 days. The untreated callus served as the control. Freezing tolerance of callus was assessed by measuring fresh weight. To evaluate freezing resistance in wild‐type and transiently transformed apple leaves, detached leaves were treated at 4°C for 1 day and then subjected to nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining. Poplar seedlings grown at 24°C were treated at 4°C for 5 days and then returned to 24°C for 3 day to monitor their growth. Arabidopsis seedlings were exposed to a chilling treatment using a gradient cooling incubator that gradually lowered the temperature from 22°C to −4°C or raised it from −4°C to 22°C. For chilling assays, 8‐day‐old seedlings grown at 22°C were treated at −4°C for 1 h, then transferred back to 22°C for 2 days. Chilling resistance was evaluated based on the seedling survival rates.

Electrolyte Leakage

Plant leaves subjected to freezing treatment were punched into 1 cm diameter discs. The discs were immersed in deionised water, and initial conductivity (S0) was measured. The samples were shaken at 22°C for 30 min, and conductivity was recorded as S1. The samples were boiled for 15 min, shaken again at 22°C for 30 min, and the final conductivity (S2) was measured. Electrolyte leakage was calculated using the formula: (S1−S0)/(S2−S0).

NBT and DAB Staining

Post‐freezing treatment, detached apple leaves were soaked in NBT and DAB staining solutions at 24°C for 10 h. The stained leaves were transferred to a fixing solution comprised of anhydrous ethanol, lactic acid and glycerol, and boiled for 5 min. After cooling, anhydrous ethanol was added to visualise the staining patterns.

Identification of MdGLK1‐Interacting Proteins

A liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) system was used to identify potential interacting partners of MdGLK1. The MdGLK1‐GFP fusion protein was immunoprecipitated from 15‐day‐old apple callus overexpressing MdGLK1 using a GFP‐tag Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The immunoprecipitate was analysed using the LC–MS/MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Four candidate MdGLK1‐interacting proteins are shown in Figure S3.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid (Y2H) Assay

The Y2H assay was conducted using the Gold Yeast Two‐Hybrid System (Clontech, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The MdGLK1 coding sequence was cloned into pGAD424 as the prey, while the MdBZR1, MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8 coding sequences were individually cloned into pGBT9 as baits. Empty pGAD424 and pGBT9 vectors served as negative controls. The recombinant plasmid combinations were transformed into ‘Y2H GOLD’ yeast cells and grown on a medium lacking tryptophan and leucine (−T/−L). Transformants were selected on a medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, histidine and adenine (−T/−L/−H/−A). The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

Pull‐Down Assay

The pull‐down assay was performed following the manufacturer's instructions for the GST Pull‐Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The MdGLK1 coding sequence was cloned into pET32a, while the coding sequences of MdBZR1, MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8 were individually inserted into pGEX4T‐1. These recombinant vectors were separately transformed into E. coli BL21, and the fusion proteins MdGLK1‐HIS, MdBZR1‐GST, MdEBF1‐GST and MdSMXL8‐GST were expressed by isopropyl‐β‐d‐thiogalactoside (IPTG) induction. The recombinant proteins were incubated together in a centrifugal column containing glutathione agarose resin at 4°C for 2 h, with HIS used as the negative control. After washing the mixture three times, the eluent was collected by centrifugation and analysed using anti‐HIS and anti‐GST antibodies (Abmart, Shanghai, China) The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

BiFC Assay

The BiFC assay was performed as previously described (An et al. 2024). The MdGLK1 coding sequence was fused to pSPYNE‐35S/pUC‐SPYNE, while the coding sequences of MdBZR1, MdEBF1 and MdSMXL8 were individually cloned into pSPYCE‐35S/pUC‐SPYCE. pSPYNE‐35S/pUC‐SPYNE and pSPYCE‐35S/pUC‐SPYCE were used as negative controls. The recombinant vectors were individually introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404, and combinations of the strains were transiently expressed in onion epidermal cells. YFP signals were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

Co‐IP Assay

The Co‐IP assay was performed according to the instructions provided with the Co‐IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MdGLK1‐pXSN‐GFP, MdBZR1‐pRI101‐GUS, MdEBF1‐pRI101‐GUS and MdSMXL8‐pRI101‐GUS were co‐transformed into apple callus. MdGLK1‐GFP protein was immunoprecipitated from the resulting 15‐day‐old transgenic callus. The immunoprecipitated samples were detected using anti‐GFP and anti‐GUS antibodies (Abmart).

ChIP Assay

The ChIP assay was conducted as previously described (An et al. 2024). Fifteen‐day‐old wild‐type and MdBZR1‐overexpressing apple callus lines carrying the GUS tag were fixed in formaldehyde for 10 min, and the reaction was stopped with glycine. The chromatin was fragmented by sonication. DNA fragments bound to MdBZR1 were isolated using the ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an anti‐GUS antibody (Abmart). MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoter fragments enriched in the protein‐DNA complexes were quantified by RT‐qPCR using the primers listed in Table S4.

EMSA

The EMSA was performed as previously described (An et al. 2023). The HIS‐tagged MdGLK1 protein was produced by IPTG induction of E. coli BL21. The MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoter probes, containing E‐box and BRRE motifs respectively, were biotin‐labelled using the Biotin Labeling Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. MdGLK1‐HIS and the biotin‐labelled promoter probes were incubated at 24°C for 30 min, with HIS protein used as a negative control. Reaction mixtures were separated on an acrylamide‐TBE gel, and autoradiography was performed. An unlabeled promoter fragment served as a competitive probe. The sequences of the biotin‐labelled and mutated probes are listed in Table S4.

LUC Assay

The MdBZR1 coding sequence was inserted into pGreenII 62‐SK to generate the effector construct. The MdCBF1 and MdCBF2 promoter sequences were individually cloned into pGreenII 0800‐LUC and used as reporter constructs. pGreenII 62‐SK and pGreenII 0800‐LUC served as negative controls. The recombinant vectors were individually transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404, and the Agrobacterium strains were co‐infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Firefly and Renilla LUC activities were measured using the Dual LUC Reporter System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

In Vivo Ubiquitination Assay

The in vivo ubiquitination assay was performed as previously described (An et al. 2023). MdGLK1‐GFP was immunoprecipitated from 15‐day‐old apple callus expressing either MdGLK1 alone or both MdGLK1 and MdEBF1, using the Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an anti‐GFP antibody (Abmart). Polyubiquitination of MdGLK1 was detected with an anti‐Ubi antibody (Abmart), and MdGLK1‐GFP was detected with an anti‐GFP antibody (Abmart). For AVG treatment, apple callus co‐expressing MdGLK1 and MdEBF1 was treated with 2 μM AVG for 4 h.

In Vitro Protein Degradation Assay

The in vitro protein degradation assay was performed as previously described (An et al. 2024). The MdGLK1‐HIS fusion protein was produced by IPTG induction in E. coli BL21. MdGLK1‐HIS was incubated with total proteins extracted from wild‐type or MdEBF1 transgenic apple callus at 4°C for 6 h. Protein levels of MdGLK1 in samples collected at different time points were assessed using an anti‐HIS antibody (Abmart), with anti‐ACTIN (Abmart) as an internal control. For MG132 treatment, the total protein extract was pre‐incubated with 100 μM MG132 for 0.5 h.

Competitive Binding Assay

The competitive binding assay was conducted as previously described (An et al. 2023). MdSMXL8‐GUS protein was immunoprecipitated using the Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an anti‐GUS antibody from 15‐day‐old apple callus overexpressing MdSMXL8‐GUS. MdGLK1‐HIS and MdBZR1‐GST fusion proteins were produced by IPTG induction in E. coli ‘BL21’. MdSMXL8‐GUS, MdGLK1‐HIS and MdBZR1‐GST were incubated in a centrifugal column containing cobalt chelate agarose resin at 4°C for 2 h. After washing the mixed solution three times, the eluted proteins were collected by centrifugation and detected using anti‐GUS, anti‐HIS and anti‐GST antibodies, respectively. All antibodies were purchased from Abmart.

LUC Complementation Imaging Assay

The LUC complementation imaging assay was performed as previously described (An et al. 2024). The MdGLK1 and MdBZR1 coding sequences were cloned into pCAMBIA1300‐nLUC and pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC, respectively. These recombinant vectors were transformed into A. tumefaciens LBA4404, and combinations of strains were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. LUC activity was measured using the LUC Activity Determination Kit (Promega). To assess the effect of MdSMXL8 on LUC activity induced by MdGLK1 and MdBZR1, MdSMXL8‐pRI101‐GUS was co‐expressed with MdGLK1‐nLUC and MdBZR1‐cLUC in N. benthamiana leaves. GR245DS (2 μM) was sprayed on the leaves for the SL treatment. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S4.

Statistical Analysis

Differences were detected by one‐way ANOVA and Tukey's test or Student's t‐test in DPS software (version 9.50).
