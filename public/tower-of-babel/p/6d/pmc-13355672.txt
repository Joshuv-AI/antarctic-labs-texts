Protocol for ultrasound-guided injection into the murine portal vein to initiate liver metastasis
Authors: Nicole Henning, Fred Roberts, Virginia Spanoudaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a non-surgical protocol for modeling murine liver metastasis by disseminating injected cells or compounds into the murine portal vein. We describe steps for identifying the portal vein and other vital structures adjacent to and within the liver by ultrasound, determining an optimal route to avoid vital structures, and injecting into the portal vein. We also report on the findings for reproducibility, adverse effects, and histopathological findings.

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps for injecting into the portal vein by ultrasound guidance (USGI) within the liver of 6–15 weeks old male or female (17–21 g) mice of various immuno-competent and immuno-compromised strains. This protocol will describe the technique using an injection of sterile saline as a test injectate. In expected outcomes we report the feedback from researchers that have utilized the described injection protocol using cells with the goal of modeling liver metastasis.

Innovation

This protocol offers several advancements over previously reported methods.1 First, it uses anatomical landmarks that are simpler to identify and distinguish, namely the portal vein in the liver distinguished from the inferior vena cava and the abdominal aorta. Previous methods require advanced technical expertise to identify multiple organs and vessels, then tracing the vessels to distinguish them. Second, the current method opts for a needle path directly into the liver tissue avoiding other vital organs which are not intended for injection. Thus, avoiding cell seeding outside of the liver. Moreover, the liver tissue is easier to penetrate than the pancreas. Third, this protocol uses a commercially available attachment to allow for flexibility in visualizing the needle instead of 3D printing a needle fixation device. Additionally, the attachment aids in needle placement verification, as it enables visualization of a proximal portion of the vessel relative to the needle within it.

Expected outcomes

To investigate potential tissue damage in the liver caused by this method, two B6 female mice were injected with saline following the above steps and promptly euthanized. Livers were harvested, fixed in formalin, processed, paraffin-embedded and serially sectioned through the whole block at 20 micron intervals. All 5 micron H&E-stained liver sections were evaluated by a board-certified veterinary pathologist. The needle tract and associated tissue disruption was visualized and consisted mainly of minimal hepatocellular degeneration/necrosis directly adjacent to the needle tract and some minimal hemorrhage. The histopathologic evaluation confirmed that at the level of the portal vein, there was no evidence of substantial traumatic hemorrhage that would be of concern for the welfare of the mouse or the experimental endpoints. In conclusion, a needle tract lesion is anticipated, and the hepatocellular trauma was minimal and not considered limiting for this model. Summary of expected outcomes

Our first trial was an exploratory experiment in which 20 Balb/C female mice were injected with mouse colon adenocarcinoma cells (MC-26). 5 mice received 25 K cells, 10 mice received 50 K cells, and 5 mice received 100 K cells. Across all groups 80% of the mice developed liver metastasis while 20% did not develop tumors, and none of the mice developed tumors elsewhere in the peritoneum. Compared to a previously reported intrasplenic surgical injection,3 our intrahepatic ultrasound guided injection procedure led to liver metastasis nodules with more irregular tumor shape (see Figure 5A), and such morphological heterogeneity is histologically similarly to what is observed in the clinic.4 Figure 5 “Expected Outcomes Example” shows in-vivo (Bioluminescence (BLI) and Ultrasound) and ex-vivo (histology and necropsy) results from an injection of mouse colon adenocarcinoma cells (MC-26) at a concentration of 50 k in 100 μL PBS. The diffuse tumor BLI signal across the left side and abdominal area is confirmed to be numerous large tumors in the liver as visualized by ultrasound and corroborated at necropsy. The data in Figure 5 is courtesy of Dr. Liang Hao and Nour Saida Harzallah. A third trial (trial 3) study with a separate investigator reported 100% engraftment in 10 B6 female mice injected with 150 K colorectal tumor organoids from C57BL/6 mice with mutations in APC, KRAS, and p53 and with fluorescent reporter tdTomato (AKP-tdT CRCOs, dissociated to single cells).

A different trial (trial 4) with the same investigator was conducted on 11 B6 male mice with GFP background. The mice were injected with 150 K AKP-tdT CRCOs (with Diphtheria toxin receptor and expression of GFP) that were dissociated to single cells. Only 2 of the 11 mice were reported to have a successful engraftment, potentially due to the Diphtheria toxin receptor causing an immunogenic rejection in the liver. However, previous orthotopic transplantation in the colon by the investigator showed good engraftment.

A fifth trial (trial 5) with a third investigator sought to probe the metastatic potential of various mouse tumor cells. B6 female mice (n=5 per group) were injected with 100 K cells of the following cell lines:

AKP (colorectal tumor organoids dissociated to single cells).

APC (colorectal adenoma organoids dissociated to single cells).

PB2 (breast tumor cells).

4TO7 (breast tumor cells).

Livers from animals injected with the AKP and APC cell lines were evaluated by fluorescence microscopy ex-vivo at 28- and 35-days post injection. All AKP injected animals developed several metastatic colonies and all animals injected with APC cells were found to only have disseminated cells, but did not show metastatic colonization. Livers from animals injected with the PB2 and 4TO7 cell lines were evaluated by fluorescence microscopy ex-vivo at 28-days post injection For PB2 injected mice, the majority (80%) were found to have an abundant metastatic colony burden. Only 40% of the 4TO7 injected mice had disseminated cells detected in the liver and none of the mice developed metastatic colonization, possibly due to cell growth suppression caused by immunogenicity in the non-syngeneic host.

Future trials will include injections of several liver tumor cell lines, and further evaluations for cell concentration injections up to 400 k for AKP and APC cell lines in healthy young mice as well as in a mouse model of fatty liver disease.

All mice involved in the above trials were housed in static micro-isolator caging with eco-bedding or nestlet enrichment on standard lighting with ad libitum access to food and water. No analgesics have been given for this procedure. No mortality, no distress or poor body condition directly related to the procedure have been observed. Given the aforementioned outcomes, we believe that this ultrasound guided intrahepatic approach offers a refined injection alternative into the portal vein of mice that is in a less complicated location compared to what is reported elsewhere.2 The needle insertion through the liver assures no risk of perforating or causing tumor cell engraftment in the pancreas, stomach, kidney, spleen and bowel. Additionally, it is less complicated since it requires very basic organ and blood vessel identification. This approach is appropriate for cells when using the suggested gauge needle, leading to tumors that are distributed amongst the entirety of the liver instead of being isolated to the injection location.5 Cells and compounds of interest should be screened for shearing and damage when passed through the 33-gauge needle for this method.

Limitations

This technique has not been attempted yet in species other than mice, it has not yet been attempted in mice in a diseased condition, it has not been attempted in mice outside of the age and weight range specified, it has only been attempted with cancer cells and organoids as specified in expected outcomes section, and has not been attempted free-hand. This protocol has been implemented using components of the Vevo3100 Imaging System. Specifically, the MX550S transducer was mounted on the transducer micro-manipulator and the image guided injection system for syringe manipulations and has not been replicated in a different experimental setup. In our experience larger diameter needles (smaller numerical gauge size) have been less successful as their bevel is more difficult to fit fully within the lumen of the portal vein by visual verification (step 21), as was also concluded in Walker et al.5 During ultrasound a measurement of the portal vein diameter can be made to assist in selecting the appropriate needle bevel length and gauge in cases that may require a different size. We were not able to dilate the portal vein by increasing or decreasing body temperature to allow for easier access for injection. Further limitations of this technique can be determined once the method is applied on a sufficiently large number of mice as well as being attempted by other trained injectionists.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The transducer micro-manipulator is not available.

Potential solution

The Vevo Imaging Station 3D Motor can be used in place of the transducer micro-manipulator to adjust the transducer in the Y-axis. Adjustments to the transducer position would be made in the 3D imaging mode motor position panel of the Vevo3100 Imaging System. The 3D motor will need to be initialized prior to the transducer being lowered for imaging.

Problem 2

Difficulty finding a needle path to the portal vein that does not contain bowel in step 14.

Potential solution

The imaging platform can be rotated slightly clockwise or counter-clockwise to visualize a different path for the needle that does not contain bowel.

Problem 3

The length of the needle is only partially visualized in step 17.

Potential solution

At step 3 when placing the transducer and adjusting the angle of the transducer, take care to ensure these remain in parallel with the injection system as much as possible by eye. Also important for full needle visualization is the injection system holding the needle being in parallel with the transducer. Any slight rotation of the injection system will cause the needle to not appear in plane on the ultrasound image. The injection system rotation lock (Figure 3 label #5) can be released and re-tightened to allow the injection system to reset to the correct positioning. When the lock is loose and the injection system is free to rotate, a groove can be felt at the correct position.

Problem 4

In step 20 when advancing the needle, the tip of the needle is no longer visible.

Potential solution

This can occur due to the fine gauge of the needle easily deflecting in the tissue. Ensure you are using the shortest needle length at this gauge as longer needles are more easily deflected. Adjustment of transducer position with the knob on the transducer micro-manipulator should bring the needle bevel back in plane. The mouse position and the needle position should not be adjusted at this time as it could cause damage to abdominal organs.

Problem 5

In steps 20 and 21 the mouse position shifts slightly laterally when adjusting the transducer position using the transducer micro-manipulator.

Potential solution

This can occur if the supportive fingers of the right hand (placed in step 19) are removed and used to make the adjustments to the transducer micro-manipulator. Adjustments to the transducer micro-manipulator may need to be made with the left hand to allow for the right hand to remain in the supportive position.

Problem 6

In step 21, the needle does not puncture the portal vein.

Potential solution

It is possible the needle has dulled and should be replaced. The vessel walls can be quite elastic and may require quite a bit of force to puncture. Use care to slowly continue to advance the needle to create enough tension on the vessel wall to puncture. We recommend changing the needle any time it appears to have dulled and in compliance with local and institutional regulations.
