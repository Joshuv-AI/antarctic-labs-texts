Drug-Coated Balloon Angioplasty for de Novo Subclavian Artery Stenosis: Mid-Term Outcomes and Independent Risk Factors for Restenosis
Authors: Xiaosong Zhang, Yusheng Wang, Hao Tang, Da Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndovascular intervention is the first-line treatment for subclavian artery stenosis, but high-quality evidence for drug-coated balloon (DCB) angioplasty specifically in de novo lesions remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of DCB for de novo subclavian artery stenosis and to identify independent risk factors for restenosis.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled 53 consecutive patients with de novo subclavian artery stenosis (≥70% confirmed by quantitative coronary angiography, QCA) treated with a paclitaxel-coated balloon (3.0 μg/mm2; Orchid®, Acotec, Beijing, China) between January 2020 and September 2025. The primary endpoint was 24-month primary patency. Secondary endpoints included technical success, residual stenosis, clinically driven target lesion revascularization (CD-TLR), and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE). Bailout stenting was required in 35.8% (19/53) of patients due to flow-limiting dissection or significant elastic recoil. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were performed, with formal assessment of the proportional hazards assumption.ResultsMean age was 62.7±8.5 years (62.3% male). Technical success rate was 100%, with mean residual stenosis of 11.5±4.9%. Median follow-up was 24 months (IQR 12–36 months, range 6–74 months). Primary patency rates at 12, 24, and 36 months were consistently 94.3% (95% CI 87.6–100%), with all 3 restenosis cases detected at the 12-month CTA follow-up; all were asymptomatic and no CD-TLR was performed. Overall MACCE incidence was 3.8%. Severe lesion calcification (Grade 3) was the only independent risk factor for restenosis on multivariate Cox regression (HR=7.89, 95% CI 1.45–42.96, P=0.017); however, given the small number of events (n=3), these results should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionDCB angioplasty demonstrates favorable safety and mid-term patency for de novo subclavian artery stenosis in this single-center retrospective cohort. Severe calcification is associated with poorer patency outcomes, though this finding requires validation. These preliminary results suggest DCB as a potential endovascular option for non-severely calcified de novo subclavian lesions; however, the absence of a direct comparator group and the retrospective single-center design limit definitive efficacy conclusions, and prospective comparative studies are needed.

Introduction

Subclavian artery stenosis and occlusion are common manifestations of peripheral arterial disease, with atherosclerosis accounting for more than 90% of etiologies.1 Due to the anatomical proximity of the lesion to the origin of the vertebral artery, severe stenosis can lead to upper limb ischemia and vertebrobasilar insufficiency, clinically manifesting as intermittent claudication of the upper limb, dizziness, ataxia, and posterior circulation stroke, seriously affecting patients’ quality of life.2,3 Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of subclavian artery stenosis increases with age, with a left-to-right ratio of approximately 3:1, and the prevalence in people over 65 years old can reach 13%.1

In recent years, endovascular treatment has replaced open surgery as the first-line therapy for subclavian artery stenosis due to its minimal invasiveness, high success rate, and low perioperative risk.4 However, conventional endovascular strategies have inherent limitations: percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty (POBA) has a high restenosis rate of 15–30% during mid-term follow-up due to vascular elastic recoil and neointimal hyperplasia;5 while permanent stenting can effectively counteract elastic recoil, it carries long-term risks of stent fracture, in-stent restenosis (ISR), and requires prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), which poses increased bleeding risk particularly for elderly patients and those with coagulation disorders.6,7

Drug-coated balloon (DCB), as a “leave nothing behind” endovascular technology, delivers the anti-proliferative agent paclitaxel locally to the vessel wall during short-term inflation, effectively inhibiting smooth muscle cell proliferation and neointimal hyperplasia, while avoiding the risks associated with permanent implant retention.8 The subclavian artery represents a particularly compelling anatomical territory for the “leave nothing behind” approach. Unlike the coronary or infrainguinal arteries, the subclavian artery is subject to continuous mechanical stress from shoulder girdle and respiratory movements, which increases the risk of stent fracture and accelerated ISR following permanent stent implantation.9 Moreover, many subclavian artery stenoses are ostial lesions where precise stent positioning is technically challenging and stent protrusion into the aortic arch is associated with thromboembolic risks.4 Additionally, permanent stent implantation in the subclavian artery may complicate future coronary artery bypass grafting procedures that require harvesting the ipsilateral internal mammary artery, a clinical scenario where DCB offers a distinct advantage by preserving future surgical options. These biomechanical, anatomical, and surgical considerations collectively make DCB an especially attractive strategy for subclavian artery disease.

DCB has been widely established for femoropopliteal and coronary artery diseases, with 1-year primary patency rates of 90–95% confirmed in multiple randomized controlled trials.10,11 Current international guidelines, including the 2024 ESC/ESVS guidelines on peripheral arterial disease, endorse DCB as a class IIa/IIb recommendation for femoropopliteal lesions. However, no specific guideline recommendation currently exists for DCB use in subclavian artery disease, reflecting the scarcity of high-quality evidence in this anatomical territory.

Clinical studies on DCB treatment of subclavian artery disease are mostly limited to case reports and small-sample series, without systematic analysis of lesion-specific risk factors.12,13 Therefore, this study enrolled 53 consecutive patients with de novo subclavian artery stenosis treated with DCB angioplasty over a 5.75-year period, with systematic clinical endpoint definitions, detailed lesion characterization, and multivariate analysis of risk factors affecting postoperative patency, aiming to provide clinical evidence for the standardized application of DCB in subclavian artery disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This study was a single-center retrospective cohort study, which consecutively enrolled patients with de novo subclavian artery stenosis/occlusion who underwent DCB angioplasty at our institution from January 2020 to September 2025, with follow-up data censored at March 2026. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Lianyungang (approval number: LW-20250707001), and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for prospective informed consent due to the retrospective, non-interventional nature of the study. Written informed consent for the endovascular procedure was obtained from all patients before surgery. Publication of the case image in Figure 1 has obtained separate written informed consent from the involved patient. All clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s Electronic Medical Record (EMR), Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS), and angiographic QCA database, with complete traceability of the original data. This study was reported in strict accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Supplementary Figure 1). Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years; de novo atherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion of the subclavian artery confirmed by CTA or DSA; target lesion diameter stenosis ≥70% measured by offline QCA software (QAngio XA 7.3, Medis, Netherlands), consistent with SVS standards; at least one clinical indication (upper limb ischemic symptoms, vertebrobasilar insufficiency symptoms, preoperative preparation for coronary artery bypass grafting with internal mammary artery harvesting, or arteriovenous fistula dysfunction); and completion of DCB angioplasty (with or without bailout stenting) for the target lesion.

Exclusion criteria: non-atherosclerotic subclavian artery lesions (fibromuscular dysplasia, vasculitis, Takayasu arteritis, radiation-induced arteritis); ISR lesions; severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, or life expectancy <12 months; coagulation dysfunction or contraindications to antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy; acute thrombosis requiring emergency thrombolysis/thrombectomy; incomplete clinical data or loss to follow-up; and refusal of DCB treatment.

Lesion Characteristics Definition

Lesion characteristics were uniformly defined before data extraction. Ostial lesion was defined as the proximal edge of the lesion within 5 mm from the subclavian artery origin. Chronic total occlusion (CTO) was defined as complete occlusion with TIMI flow grade 0 for ≥90 days confirmed by history and imaging. Calcification grade was classified using the standard 4-grade angiographic classification: grade 0 (no calcification), grade 1 (mild: faint calcification visible only during cardiac cycle), grade 2 (moderate: clear unilateral calcification visible before contrast injection), and grade 3 (severe: dense bilateral/circumferential calcification visible before contrast injection). Lesion length was measured by QCA and defined as the total length of the segment with ≥50% diameter stenosis.

Procedural Details

All procedures were performed by 3 senior vascular surgeons with >10 years of endovascular experience, following our center’s standardized protocol. Prior to intervention, all patients received DAPT (aspirin 100 mg/day plus clopidogrel 75 mg/day for ≥3 days; or loading doses of aspirin 300 mg and clopidogrel 300 mg ≥6 hours before urgent procedures). For patients with confirmed clopidogrel resistance, ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily was substituted.

Vascular access was individualized according to the following criteria: femoral artery was preferred as the primary approach due to its larger caliber and superficial anatomical position facilitating safer puncture and compression hemostasis; brachial artery access was selected for patients with unfavorable femoral anatomy (severe tortuosity, calcification, or occlusion), or for subclavian artery ostial lesions where the femoral approach offered a suboptimal working angle; combined femoral-brachial bidirectional access was employed exclusively for complex CTO lesions where single-access wiring was insufficient.

Following initial aortography and road-map guidance, Command 18 or Command 14 guidewires (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA) were predominantly used to cross the stenotic/occlusive segment. A 7-French or 8-French 90 cm long sheath (Flexor Check-Flo, Cook, Bloomington, IN, USA) was deployed for complex cases. For CTO lesions failing antegrade wiring, a bidirectional approach was attempted. If both guidewires entered the subintimal space, a reverse-CART-like technique was applied to regain the true lumen.

Pre-dilation was performed in all patients using an Armada18 balloon (Abbott) at a balloon-to-vessel ratio of 0.8–1.0. For mild-to-moderate calcification lesions (grade 0–2), standard semi-compliant balloons were inflated at 8–12 atm for 60–120 seconds. For moderate-to-severe calcification lesions (grade 2–3), non-compliant balloons, cutting balloons, or scoring balloons were used at 12–16 atm to improve vessel wall compliance and facilitate adequate drug delivery. Subsequently, a paclitaxel-coated DCB (Orchid®; Acotec, Beijing, China; drug dose density 3.0 μg/mm2) with a balloon-to-vessel ratio of 1.0–1.1 was advanced to cover the entire lesion with 3–5 mm overlap at both ends, avoiding geographic miss. Prolonged inflation over 120 seconds was applied for sufficient drug transfer. DCB diameters ranged from 6 to 12 mm.

Bailout stenting was indicated when residual stenosis ≥30% persisted or flow-limiting dissection occurred post-DCB. In these cases, a balloon-expandable bare-metal stent (Omnilink, Abbott) was deployed. The rationale for selecting bailout rather than primary stenting was to maximize the proportion of patients benefiting from the “leave nothing behind” strategy, reserving permanent stent implantation only for cases with suboptimal immediate results. Routine vertebral artery protection was not implemented; selective protection was performed in only 2 patients with lesions involving the vertebral artery origin and high embolism risk.

Postoperative antiplatelet regimen: DCB-only patients received single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) long-term; patients with bailout stenting received DAPT for 3 months followed by long-term single antiplatelet therapy. All patients received standardized secondary prevention including blood pressure control, statin therapy, and smoking cessation counseling.

Follow-Up Protocol

All patients were followed at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months after discharge, with a minimum follow-up duration of 6 months. Follow-up assessments included clinical symptom evaluation, bilateral upper limb systolic blood pressure measurement, and duplex ultrasound (peak systolic velocity ratio, PSVR) at each time point. To standardize duplex ultrasound interpretation, PSVR >2.4 was used as the threshold for suspected restenosis ≥50%, consistent with SVS reporting standards. CTA or DSA with offline QCA measurement was performed at 12 and 24 months to objectively confirm patency status and to reconcile any discordance with ultrasound findings. In cases of discrepancy between duplex ultrasound and CTA findings, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) with offline QCA was performed and considered the gold standard for definitive diagnosis of restenosis. For patients with follow-up durations shorter than 24 months due to enrollment after September 2024, imaging-based patency assessment was performed at the most recent available time point and data were censored accordingly for Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Endpoints Definitions

All clinical endpoints were defined in accordance with SVS clinical reporting standards for peripheral arterial disease and international norms for DCB clinical research. The primary endpoint was 24-month primary patency, defined as absence of CD-TLR and target vessel diameter stenosis <50% confirmed by duplex ultrasound or QCA. Secondary endpoints included: technical success rate (successful guidewire crossing, completion of DCB angioplasty, residual stenosis <30% on final angiography, no perioperative major adverse events); immediate postoperative residual stenosis by QCA; improvement of interlimb systolic blood pressure difference; CD-TLR rate (repeat revascularization due to recurrent symptoms or imaging-confirmed stenosis ≥70%); and symptom relief rate (complete disappearance or significant improvement of preoperative ischemic symptoms).

The safety endpoint was MACCE incidence during the perioperative period (within 30 days) and follow-up. MACCE was defined as a composite of all-cause death, non-fatal ischemic stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, CD-TLR, and severe bleeding (BARC grade ≥3). All adverse events were independently adjudicated by two clinicians who did not participate in the procedures.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean±SD and compared using independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Sample size calculation was performed prior to study initiation. Based on a 24-month primary patency rate of 90% from previous literature and an expected 10% loss to follow-up, with two-sided α=0.05 and power=0.80, the calculated minimum sample size was 47 patients. We enrolled 53 consecutive patients over 5.75 years, and post hoc power analysis based on the observed 24-month primary patency rate of 94.3% showed statistical power (1-β) of 0.89. Importantly, this post hoc power calculation applies only to the primary endpoint of 24-month primary patency. The study was not specifically powered to identify independent predictors of restenosis through multivariable modeling, and the limited number of events (n=3 restenosis) substantially constrains the statistical power and reliability of the Cox regression analysis. Accordingly, the multivariable risk factor findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate primary patency, with 95% confidence intervals calculated by the Greenwood formula. Log rank tests were used to compare patency between subgroups (DCB-only vs DCB+bailout stenting; CTO vs non-CTO; severe vs non-severe calcification; ostial vs non-ostial). Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify potential risk factors for primary patency. Variables with P<0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate Cox regression (forward stepwise method) to identify independent risk factors, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI reported. Given the small number of restenosis events (n=3), the multivariate Cox regression model was intentionally kept parsimonious (no more than 2 predictors entered simultaneously) to minimize the risk of model overfitting and unstable estimates; all effect sizes should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. The proportional hazards assumption was formally assessed using Schoenfeld residuals for all covariates included in the final model, and no significant violations were identified (all P>0.05 for the time-dependent covariate test).

All QCA measurements were independently performed by two board-certified interventional radiologists with >5 years of peripheral vascular experience. The ICC for primary measurements (lesion length, diameter stenosis rate) was 0.92 (95% CI 0.87–0.95), indicating excellent inter-observer consistency. The average of two independent measurements was used for final analysis.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 53 patients with de novo subclavian artery stenosis/occlusion were enrolled over a 69-month period (January 2020 to September 2025), with a mean age of 62.7±8.5 years (range 43–79 years) and 62.3% (33/53) male. The STROBE patient flow diagram is provided in Supplementary Figure 1. Baseline cardiovascular risk factors were consistent with the characteristics of an atherosclerotic population: 67.9% (36/53) hypertension, 58.5% (31/53) dyslipidemia, 34.0% (18/53) diabetes mellitus, and 49.1% (26/53) current or former smoking. The main clinical manifestation was vertebrobasilar insufficiency symptoms (64.2%; 34/53), followed by upper limb ischemic symptoms including intermittent claudication (24.5%; 13/53) and numbness/weakness (17.0%; 9/53).

In terms of lesion characteristics, 81.1% (43/53) were located on the left side, 62.3% (33/53) were ostial lesions, and 17.0% (9/53) involved the vertebral artery origin. Mean lesion length was 2.8±0.9 cm; CTO lesions accounted for 26.4% (14/53). Calcification grades were: grade 0 (41.5%; 22/53), grade 1 (24.5%; 13/53), grade 2 (20.8%; 11/53), and grade 3 (severe; 13.2%; 7/53). Procedural and Immediate Outcomes

All 53 procedures achieved 100% technical success with no conversion to open surgery. Mean immediate residual stenosis by QCA was 11.5±4.9%. Mean interlimb systolic blood pressure difference decreased significantly from 35.9±12.9 mmHg preoperatively to 8.1±3.8 mmHg postoperatively (paired t-test, P<0.001).

Regarding vascular access, 54.7% (29/53) used femoral approach, 24.5% (13/53) used brachial approach, and 20.8% (11/53) used combined femoral-brachial approach (all for CTO lesions). Pre-dilation was performed in all patients: 75.5% (40/53) with standard semi-compliant balloons and 24.5% (13/53) with cutting/scoring balloons (all for moderate-to-severe calcification lesions). Mean DCB diameter was 8.1±1.1 mm and mean inflation time was 142±18 seconds. Bailout stenting was performed in 35.8% (19/53), with the main indications being flow-limiting dissection (11 cases) and severe elastic recoil with residual stenosis ≥30% (8 cases). No perioperative MACCE, vessel perforation, distal embolism, or stroke occurred within 30 days. Minor puncture site hematoma (BARC grade 1) occurred in 3 patients (5.7%), resolving with manual compression. Comparison Between DCB-Only and Bailout Stenting Groups

Patients were divided into DCB-only group (34 cases, 64.2%) and DCB+bailout stenting group (19 cases, 35.8%). No statistically significant differences were found between groups in age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors, lesion length, CTO ratio, ostial lesion ratio, or vertebral artery involvement (all P>0.05). The proportion of severe calcification (grade 3) was significantly higher in the bailout stenting group compared with the DCB-only group (26.3% vs 5.9%; P=0.042), which was clinically expected since severe calcification predisposes to elastic recoil and dissection requiring bailout stenting. The number of restenosis events was 2 in the DCB-only group and 1 in the bailout stenting group. The single restenosis event in the bailout stenting group occurred in a patient with severe circumferential calcification (grade 3), further supporting the association between severe calcification and poor outcomes regardless of treatment strategy. Follow-Up, Restenosis Characteristics, and Clinical Outcomes

All 53 patients completed follow-up, with a median duration of 24 months (IQR 12–36 months, range 6–74 months). The follow-up duration distribution was: 8 patients (15.1%) <12 months, 15 patients (28.3%) 12–24 months, and 30 patients (56.6%) ≥24 months. It should be noted that patients enrolled after September 2024 (ie., within the final 6–12 months of the enrollment period) had follow-up durations of less than 12 or 24 months by the data cutoff of March 2026; these patients contributed follow-up time until their last observed event-free visit and were censored at that point for the Kaplan-Meier analysis. The maximum follow-up of 74 months was observed in the earliest enrolled patients (enrolled January 2020, censored March 2026). Thus, the 74-month figure represents the maximum individual follow-up achieved, not the planned protocol duration.

During follow-up, all patients demonstrated significant improvement in preoperative ischemic symptoms (symptom relief rate 100%). A total of 3 patients (5.7%) developed target lesion restenosis ≥50%, all detected at the 12-month CTA re-examination. Regarding the clinical and lesion characteristics of these 3 restenosis cases: Case 1 had severe calcification (grade 3) and was in the DCB-only group; Case 2 had severe calcification (grade 3) and was in the DCB+bailout stenting group; Case 3 had a CTO lesion (non-severe calcification, grade 2) and was in the DCB-only group. Notably, none of the restenosis cases were isolated ostial lesions, consistent with the subsequent subgroup analysis showing no significant difference in patency between ostial and non-ostial lesions (log-rank P=0.872), and the overlap between severe calcification and CTO was absent in this cohort. All 3 restenosis cases were asymptomatic, and no CD-TLR was performed during the entire follow-up period. No additional restenosis occurred during subsequent follow-up.

The overall antiplatelet therapy compliance rate was 94.3% (50/53). Three patients adjusted their regimen (switching from DAPT to single antiplatelet therapy due to mild gastrointestinal discomfort), and no BARC grade ≥3 bleeding events were recorded. The overall MACCE incidence during follow-up was 3.8% (2/53): 1 patient had a non-fatal ischemic stroke at 12 months (confirmed to be in the contralateral hemisphere, unrelated to the target lesion); and 1 patient had a non-fatal myocardial infarction at 18 months (managed medically). No all-cause death, CD-TLR, severe bleeding, or new vertebrobasilar ischemic events occurred during the entire follow-up period.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that the primary patency rate of the overall cohort was consistently 94.3% (95% CI 87.6–100%) at 12, 24, and 36 months, with all 3 restenosis events occurring at the 12-month time point (Figure 2A). Given that 8 patients (15.1%) had follow-up durations shorter than 12 months and contributed limited person-time to the later intervals, the Kaplan-Meier estimates beyond 36 months should be interpreted with caution due to the small numbers at risk. Risk Factors for Restenosis

Univariate Cox regression showed that severe calcification (HR=8.23, 95% CI 1.52–44.58, P=0.014), CTO lesion (HR=5.12, 95% CI 0.87–30.12, P=0.071), and lesion length (HR=1.32, 95% CI 0.91–1.92, P=0.145) were potential factors. Variables with P<0.1 (severe calcification and CTO lesion) were entered into the multivariate model. Given the very small number of events (n=3), only 2 variables were entered simultaneously to prevent overfitting. The proportional hazards assumption was confirmed for all included covariates (Schoenfeld residuals test, all P>0.05). Severe calcification of the target lesion was the only independent risk factor for postoperative restenosis (HR=7.89, 95% CI 1.45–42.96, P=0.017). The wide confidence interval reflects the limited statistical power due to the small event number. Detailed Cox regression results are shown in Table 4. Discussion

This study presents one of the largest single-center cohorts of DCB angioplasty specifically for de novo subclavian artery stenosis/occlusion, reporting a 100% technical success rate, no major perioperative complications, and a 24-month primary patency rate of 94.3% in this single-arm cohort. Severe lesion calcification was identified as a potential independent risk factor for mid-term patency, although this finding is exploratory given the very limited number of restenosis events. To our knowledge, this represents the largest Chinese single-center study specifically focused on DCB for de novo subclavian artery lesions. While this study was conducted in China, comparable small-sample international studies have been reported from European and Middle Eastern centers,7,8,11 underscoring the global interest in this topic; however, no large-scale multicenter study has been published in any region to date.

In recent years, small-sample studies have initially explored DCB in subclavian artery disease. Albashaireh et al8 first reported 8 cases with a 12-month patency rate of 100%; Dinoto et al7 reported 6 cases involving the vertebral artery origin with no restenosis at 18 months; Van Nut et al11 included 41 patients treated with endovascular intervention, of whom only 12 received DCB. These studies are limited by extremely small sample sizes, incomplete lesion characterization, and absence of systematic prognostic analysis. Our results are consistent with these earlier observations while providing a substantially larger evidence base: the 100% technical success rate is comparable to conventional stenting (95–100% in published series);4,12 the 24-month primary patency of 94.3% compares favorably with reported POBA rates (70–85% reported)5 and is comparable to reported rates for modern bare metal stents (90–95% in published series).6,12 Importantly, only 35.8% of patients required bailout stenting, meaning nearly two-thirds avoided permanent stent implantation, which is the core clinical advantage of DCB.

A balanced discussion of the DCB-only and bailout stenting strategies is warranted. Despite 2 restenosis events in the DCB-only group versus 1 in the bailout stenting group, the patency rates were statistically equivalent (94.1% vs 94.7%; P=0.912). The bailout stenting group had a significantly higher proportion of severe calcification (26.3% vs 5.9%), representing a more anatomically complex population. The comparable patency outcomes between groups therefore suggest that bailout stenting did not simply “remedy” failed DCB, but rather that DCB-primed vessel walls may confer additional antiproliferative benefit even in the context of subsequent stent deployment. This “DCB pre-conditioning” effect has been proposed in the coronary ISR literature,14 and may explain why bailout stenting did not compromise long-term patency in our cohort despite treating a higher-risk population. Conversely, the absence of statistically significant difference may reflect limited statistical power given the sample size, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

The key finding of severe calcification as an independent predictor of restenosis (HR=7.89, P=0.017) is clinically highly relevant. Mechanistically, severe circumferential calcification leads to multiple interrelated adverse effects on DCB efficacy: it impairs vessel wall compliance, preventing adequate balloon expansion and uniform drug delivery to the subintimal layers; dense calcification creates a physical barrier that limits paclitaxel penetration into the vessel wall; incomplete pre-dilation in severely calcified lesions results in geographic miss or uneven drug distribution; and the residual elastic recoil following inadequate plaque modification increases the risk of early restenosis. These mechanisms are well-established in the coronary and femoropopliteal DCB literature.15,16 Based on these findings, we propose that for lesions with severe circumferential calcification (grade 3), dedicated plaque modification should be performed before DCB delivery. Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a particularly promising modality, using acoustic shockwaves to fracture both intimal and medial calcium, substantially improving vessel wall compliance without the vessel trauma associated with atherectomy.17 In the 7 patients with severe calcification in our cohort, IVL was not yet available at our center, and only cutting/scoring balloons were used for pre-dilation. Had IVL been available, the outcomes in this subgroup might have been meaningfully improved.

The 3 asymptomatic restenosis cases identified at 12-month CTA surveillance raise an important clinical question: what is the clinical significance of imaging-defined restenosis in the subclavian artery in the absence of symptoms? In coronary and infrainguinal DCB studies, asymptomatic restenosis is typically managed conservatively unless hemodynamic significance is confirmed.16,18 In subclavian artery disease, asymptomatic restenosis (defined as ≥50% stenosis without recurrent symptoms) may carry a more benign prognosis than symptomatic restenosis, given that the subclavian artery territory is well-collateralized via the vertebral, internal mammary, and thoracoacromial arterial networks. None of our 3 asymptomatic restenosis patients developed ischemic symptoms or required reintervention during continued surveillance. These findings suggest that asymptomatic subclavian artery restenosis may not routinely require reintervention; however, annual clinical and imaging surveillance is recommended to monitor for potential progression to hemodynamically significant or symptomatic stenosis, particularly in patients with underlying progressive atherosclerotic disease. The optimal surveillance protocol and the threshold for intervention in asymptomatic patients warrant further prospective study. The absence of CD-TLR in this cohort supports a conservative management approach for asymptomatic imaging-detected restenosis.

The economic implications of the DCB strategy deserve brief mention. By avoiding permanent stent implantation in 64.2% of patients, the DCB approach eliminates the need for prolonged DAPT (typically 3–6 months after bare metal stenting), reducing the associated costs of antiplatelet agents, monitoring for bleeding complications, and potential hospitalizations for DAPT-related adverse events. For high-volume centers treating elderly patients with polypharmacy and elevated bleeding risk, this pharmacoeconomic advantage may substantially offset the higher upfront cost of DCB devices compared with uncoated balloons.

This study has several important limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study with potential selection bias and information bias. All procedures were performed by experienced surgeons at a high-volume center, and results may not be fully reproducible at centers with limited endovascular intervention experience or lower procedural volumes, especially for complex lesions such as chronic total occlusions and severely calcified ostial lesions, which require advanced technical skills and specialized equipment.19 Second, the small sample size of key subgroups (severe calcification n=7, CTO lesions n=14) limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses; the wide confidence intervals (eg., severe calcification 44.8–100%) reflect this uncertainty and urge caution in interpretation. The multivariable Cox regression analysis identified severe calcification as an independent predictor of restenosis; however, given the extremely small number of events (n=3), this finding should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The study was not specifically powered to detect independent predictors of restenosis, and these results require validation in larger prospective cohorts with adequate statistical power. Third, the study is single-arm without a concurrent control group, precluding direct head-to-head comparison with POBA or primary stenting. Fourth, all DCBs were of the same product (Orchid®, Acotec, 3.0 μg/mm2 paclitaxel), and results may not be directly extrapolated to DCB platforms with different drug coating technologies or excipient formulations.10,20 Fifth, the 3 asymptomatic restenosis cases were detected by protocol-mandated CTA, which may introduce surveillance bias. Additionally, given the progressively decreasing number of patients at risk during later follow-up intervals, Kaplan-Meier estimates of primary patency beyond 36 months should be interpreted with particular caution, as the small number of patients contributing to these estimates limits their reliability and precision. Finally, the median follow-up of 24 months is insufficient to evaluate very late restenosis or stent-related complications; longer follow-up is needed.

Future research directions include: multicenter prospective RCTs comparing DCB with conventional stenting for subclavian artery stenosis; studies evaluating DCB combined with IVL for severely calcified lesions; long-term (≥5-year) follow-up cohorts to assess ultra-late outcomes; head-to-head comparisons of different DCB platforms; and prospective studies defining the optimal management of asymptomatic subclavian artery restenosis detected by imaging surveillance.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, DCB angioplasty demonstrated favorable safety and mid-term patency for de novo subclavian artery stenosis/occlusion, with a 100% technical success rate and a 24-month primary patency rate of 94.3%. Severe lesion calcification was identified as a potential independent risk factor for restenosis, though this finding is exploratory and hypothesis-generating given the very limited number of events. These preliminary findings suggest DCB as a potential endovascular option for non-severely calcified de novo subclavian artery lesions. However, the retrospective single-center design, absence of a direct control group, and limited event numbers preclude definitive conclusions regarding efficacy or superiority over conventional approaches; multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are needed to validate these findings.
