Muscle strength and power in young volleyball players: reference values and age group differences
Authors: Vladimir Grbić, Lazar Toskić, Milan Marković, Miloš Dakić, Saša Đurić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo date, there is a notable lack of comprehensive normative datasets that focus on youth volleyball players, which limits coaches’ and practitioners’ ability to objectively interpret individual test results, monitor longitudinal development, and distinguish normal maturation from training-induced adaptations. The study aims to establish reference values for muscle strength and power in young volleyball players across various age groups, and to determine age group differences. The sample consisted of 3,337 boys and girls aged 10 to 18 years. For assessment of muscle strength and power, the Hand Grip Strength test (HGS), along with countermovement jump without arm swing (CMJ), was implemented. To determine age group differences, one-way MANOVA and ANOVA with a Bonferroni post-hoc test were applied. The study results revealed the presence of significant age group differences in muscle strength and power in young volleyball players, in both males (F = 24.70, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.26) and females (F = 45.86, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.11). A significant increase according to age was observed in all measured parameters. Coaches are encouraged to implement tests for assessing the muscle strength and power, and use these reference values and percentile range in order to track the growth and development of children and youth involved in volleyball, monitor the training process, and identify potential talents in volleyball from the aspect of muscle strength and power.Keywords: Volleyball, Children and youth, Hand grip strength test, CMJ, Reference values

Introduction

The ability to generate relatively high muscle forces against large external resistances (i.e. muscle strength) and to generate these forces rapidly (i.e. muscle power) is crucial for various sports [1]. Muscle strength and power are a foundation of athletic performance, especially in sports that require high levels of functional abilities, such as changing direction, sprinting, jumping and throwing [2–7].

One of the sports characterized by explosive movements such as jumps, spikes, dives, sprints and agile whole-body movements in different directions is volleyball [8, 9]. Volleyball is a team sport played at all levels (e.g., recreational, youth, professional, Olympic) with complex technical, tactical and athletic demands. Serving, receiving, setting, attacking and blocking are typical game actions that are crucial for winning volleyball competitions [10, 11]. These skills require a high level of functional ability [3]. Several studies suggest that muscular strength and power play a central role in creating a competitive advantage at the elite level [5, 12, 13]. In particular, lower-limb strength and power have consistently been associated with improved jumping ability and volleyball-specific performance in both youth and adult volleyball players [13–16].

Previous studies have highlighted muscle power as a key factor that distinguishes athletic performance across various sport disciplines [17–19], but specifically in volleyball, scoring is strongly influenced by the effectiveness of aerial techniques such as spiking, blocking and serving [10]. In addition to aerial techniques, actions like setting, receiving and defensive manoeuvres rely heavily on a player’s ability to maintain a stable posture and execute movements with precision [20]. Inadequate or delayed adjustments can compromise performance, disrupt team strategy and increase the likelihood of injury [21].

On the other hand, strength refers to the maximum force a muscle can exert to overcome a resistance. While volleyball engages nearly all muscle groups with comparable importance, upper limb strength, particularly the arms, has been shown to be an important predictor of player performance [22]. Although handgrip strength does not directly replicate volleyball-specific skills, it is widely used as a robust proxy of overall neuromuscular capacity, shows strong associations with upper-body strength and sport performance, and allows reliable large-scale field testing in youth populations [23]. Research by Koley and Pal Kaur [24] showed a significant positive relationship between handgrip strength and spiking power. Another study investigated the influence of muscular endurance and strength on spiking ability among young volleyball players [25]. The results indicated that both back muscles and abdominal muscle strength, as well as inner thigh strength, significantly enhanced spiking performance, while other physical attributes were beneficial but not statistically significant. These findings highlight the importance of focusing on back and core endurance and inner leg strength in training programs for young volleyball players to optimize spiking effectiveness and vertical jump capacity.

Several studies have documented the importance of strength and power in differentiating between performance levels in adult volleyball players [9, 26, 27] and between starters and non-starters ([18, 28–29]). For example, a study by Sattler et al. [26] found that elite players have higher lower-body power as assessed by the vertical jump compared to sub-elite players. These differences emphasize the role of neuromuscular qualities in competitive success. According to Voelzke et al. [11], these differences in jumping and agility performance between volleyball players of different competitive levels represent a performance reserve in elite volleyball. Volleyball players who have a higher vertical jump score have a better performance [9, 26, 27]. One possible explanation is that greater vertical jumping ability may enhance the execution of volleyball-specific aerial actions such as attacking, blocking, and serving [9, 30]. However, these movements are not always performed at maximal jump height, as their effectiveness also depends on tactical context, game tempo, timing, coordination, and decision-making during play. Nevertheless, greater lower-limb power may provide players with a larger functional reserve, potentially improving the ability to perform explosive actions more effectively under different competitive situations.

While adult performance is relatively well researched, there is still a large gap in the literature regarding normative data for young volleyball players across different age categories. To our knowledge, no previous study has established large-scale, age- and sex-specific normative values for both muscle strength and muscle power in youth volleyball players across a continuous developmental age span. There are only a few studies that have investigated young volleyball players in general. The studies examined different variables such as muscle power or explosive strength [31–36], muscle strength [25, 32, 36–39] and muscle endurance [25]. The focus was on both the upper and lower extremities. Some authors attempted to establish useful relationships between these variables and accordingly tried to predict volleyball performance, such as spiking accuracy, spiking performance [25] and serve reception efficiency [37] in young volleyball players. The importance of this topic can be further illustrated by two studies that investigated an interesting aspect of the importance of these variables from the perspective of national team participation. A study by Tsoukos et al. [31] investigated whether anthropometrics, lower and upper body strength, speed and power predicted the selection of young volleyball players for a junior national team by expert coaches. The results showed that the spike jump and reach test and 3 kg medicine ball throw velocity can predict a large portion of the variance in expert coaches’ grading (63%) and successfully differentiate elite young male volleyball players for selection to a junior national team. The authors suggested that performance testing during a selection process might be reduced to only these two tests. Another study by Sheppard et al. [32] examined changes in performance indicators for strength and speed-strength development over a 2-year period in a group of volleyball players who successfully transitioned from age group (U21) to the senior national team during this period. The conclusion was that volleyball players need to increase their counter movement vertical jump and spike jump (with approach) in order to progress from the junior team to the senior national team.

It is important to note that the sample sizes in the aforementioned studies were generally small, typically ranging from 12 to 66 participants. However, two notable exceptions are the studies by Gantois et al. [32] with 239 participants and Majstorović et al. [38] with 371 participants. The first study aimed to investigate the relationship between explosive strength and biological maturation by sex, using skeletal age estimated through anthropometric measurements. The main finding was that skeletal age was positively associated with explosive strength of both upper and lower limbs. In addition, youth with advanced biological maturation demonstrated superior performance compared to their less mature peers, regardless of sex. In contrast, the study by Majstorović et al. [38] is, to our knowledge, the only one that attempted to develop an age-related model of multidimensional muscular contractile characteristics in young volleyball players. The authors reported significant age-group differences in strength-related characteristics in both males and females, suggesting that muscle contractile potential increases progressively throughout youth development. However, the study included a relatively modest and sex-imbalanced sample and focused exclusively on muscle strength assessed through isometric testing, without including muscle power assessments. Therefore, comprehensive age- and sex-specific reference values for both muscle strength and muscle power in young volleyball players are still lacking.

From a long-term athlete development perspective, monitoring muscle strength and power throughout childhood and adolescence is particularly important because these physical capacities undergo substantial changes during growth and biological maturation. Pubertal development is accompanied by neuromuscular and hormonal adaptations, including increases in muscle mass, neural activation capacity, and force production, which may substantially influence athletic performance and responsiveness to training [39, 40]. Therefore, age- and sex-specific reference values may help practitioners better distinguish normal developmental changes from training-induced adaptations in young volleyball players.

Establishing age- and sex-specific normative values for muscle strength and power in the youth volleyball population is critical for several reasons. First, such data can serve as a benchmarking tool for coaches, strength and conditioning experts and sports scientists. Second, it helps in the long-term development of athletes by identifying areas of strength or deficit relative to age-appropriate standards. Third, normative values provide a valuable framework for talent identification, as early detection of above-average physical potential may guide targeted training and further development.

To date, there is a notable lack of comprehensive normative datasets published in high-quality, peer-reviewed journals that focus on youth volleyball players. This lack of normative reference data limits the ability of coaches and practitioners to objectively interpret individual test results, monitor longitudinal development, and distinguish normal maturation from training-induced adaptations. Given the increasing professionalization of youth sports and the emphasis on evidence-based training, this represents a significant limitation in the field. Our study aims to address this gap by analyzing a large database of around 3,000 + young volleyball players to present reference values for muscle strength and power across various age groups. We hypothesized that muscle strength and power would increase progressively with age in both sexes, with sex-related divergence emerging around puberty due to maturation-related differences in muscle mass development, neuromuscular adaptations, and hormonal changes occurring during adolescence. Furthermore, we expected that the resulting reference values would provide practical benchmarks for monitoring development and supporting the evaluation of neuromuscular characteristics in youth volleyball players. However, volleyball performance and athlete selection are multifactorial processes influenced not only by muscle strength and power, but also by technical-tactical proficiency, perceptual-cognitive abilities, decision-making, and the ability to maintain performance under conditions of fatigue. Therefore, the presented reference values should be interpreted as one component within a broader athlete development and evaluation framework. In this way, practitioners and researchers may use these age- and sex-specific reference standards to support training monitoring, longitudinal development tracking, and individualized conditioning strategies in youth volleyball.

Methods

Participants

The sample consisted of 3,337 boys and girls aged 10 to 18 years (Table 1). All participants were actively involved in volleyball, with at least one year of training and competition experience, which was the inclusion criterion. The exclusion criteria were the presence of injury or illness. Participants were members of the International Volleyball Camp “Vladimir Vanja Grbić”. The participants’ parents signed consent forms for participation in the study. All testing was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the rules of the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Sport and Physical Education (IRB: 484-2).

Procedures

For assessment of muscle strength and power, the Hand Grip Strength test (HGS) along with countermovement jump without arm swing (CMJ) was implemented. Data were collected from 2018 to 2024 during the first day of the camp, which lasted seven days, in June, July, and August, at the sports facilities of the host cities. The same experienced staff conducted all measurements. Before testing, participants were familiarized with the testing procedures, basic anthropometric measures were collected (body height - BH and body weight - BW), and they performed a warm-up protocol (running, jumping, spiking, stretching, etc.) for about 15 min. The measurements were conducted on small groups, and the order of the tests was randomly chosen.

Measures

The maximal isometric handgrip strength was assessed using a standardized sliding device equipped with a fixed strain gauge designed to measure isometric finger flexor force. The device was connected to a force reader, providing measurements with a precision of ± 0.01 N. Data acquisition and detailed signal analysis were performed using a specially developed software-hardware system (Sports Medical Solutions, Belgrade, Serbia) [41]. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s guidelines prior to testing, and the signal was recorded at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz. The handgrip, characterized by the flexion of all fingers around the device, was chosen because it enables the production of higher maximal force compared to other grip types (e.g., precision, manipulative, or pinch grips) [38]. Participants were seated upright, holding the dynamometer 5 to 10 cm away from the body with the tested hand, while maintaining the arm fully extended alongside the trunk. They were instructed to maintain this position without supporting the hand or device on the thigh or any other part of the body [41]. Following a detailed explanation of the procedure, three consecutive measurements were taken on each upper limb, starting with the right hand (R) and then the left (L), with a 5-second rest interval between trials [38]. Participants were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer as hard and as fast as possible. Standardized verbal instructions and encouragement (high tone signals, ‘’go’’and ‘’hold it’’) were provided to ensure maximal performance. The highest recorded values of maximal force (Fmax) and maximal rate of force development (RFD) were used for subsequent analyses. RFD was calculated from the force–time curve as the slope of force increase from contraction onset to peak force (Δforce/Δtime, 50–80% of maximal force).

For the CMJ test, subjects started from an upright standing position with their feet placed between two sensors (approximately shoulder-width apart) using the Optojump photoelectric cell system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy), and their hands placed on their hips [42, 43]. After hearing the instruction to “jump”, they performed a countermovement to a self-selected depth to avoid changes in jump strategy (with monitoring and controling by measuring stuff), before performing a maximal vertical jump [44, 45]. In order not to jeopardize the result and avoid immeasurable work, the subjects were required to keep their legs extended and their hands on their hips throughout the entire flight phase. They also had to land in an approximately identical spot to where they jumped from and stabilize themselves in that position [43, 44]. Each CMJ assessment included 3 trials with a 10-second rest interval between them, except for the youngest participants and/or those with no prior experience in CMJ testing, for whom more trials were allowed. The results were measured in centimeters, and the best attempt was chosen for subsequent analysis [46].

Statistical analysis

To establish reference values for muscle strength and power parameters in young volleyball players, basic descriptive statistics (Mean, SD) along with percentile rank were used. To determine age group differences, one-way MANOVA (general differences) and ANOVA (differences in individual parameters) with a Bonferroni post-hoc test were applied. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software package (version 25, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and Microsoft Office Excel 2024 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). All variables are presented as mean and standard deviation. A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

Tables 5, 6 and 7 present the results of the ANOVA and MANOVA, that is, age group differences in muscle strength and power parameters. It has been shown that there are significant general differences in named tests in both males (F = 24.70, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.26) and females (F = 45.86, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.11), with medium to large effect sizes (Table 5). Also, there are significant between-group differences in all implemented parameters in males (from F = 67.41, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.52 to F = 136.39, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.66) and females (from F = 76.46, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.18, to F = 183.04, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.34), where large effect size was observed in all parameters (Table 6). Finally, based on the results of the post-hoc test (Table 7), significant age-related changes were observed across nearly all age categories for all implemented parameters in both males and females (from p = 0.047 to p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aimed to define the reference values and investigate age group differences in muscle strength and power among a sample of 3,337 young male and female volleyball players. Muscle strength and power were measured using the hand grip strength test and CMJ, that is, the vertical jump movement, which are among the most commonly used tests for assessing muscle strength/power. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to address this type of problem in such a large sample of participants, and the first study from the field of volleyball. The results of this study may have a significant impact on the development of training processes for young athletes and talent identification in volleyball. Additionally, the obtained results could reveal important information about the dynamics of the growth and development of children and youth from the aspect of muscle strength and power.

The study results revealed significant general age-group differences (in all measured parameters) and differences in individual muscle strength and power parameters among young volleyball players, in both males and females, with medium-to-large effect sizes (Tables 5 and 6). Significant differences were also obtained between nearly all age categories (Table 7). These results are in accordance with previous studies, which revealed significant age group differences in children and youth in both muscle strength measured by the method of isometric dynamometry – hand grip strength test [23, 47–51] and muscle power assessed through vertical jump tests [52–55]. In volleyball, where explosive actions such as jumping, hitting, and rapid directional changes are fundamental performance components, the progressive development of strength and power is expected throughout the developmental years. The present findings confirm that maximal force production, explosive force characteristics and muscle power improve continuously with age in young athletes actively engaged in volleyball training and competition, thus, the stated hypothesis is confirmed.

The obtained results can be explained by the combined influence of maturation and daily training regimen on the manifestation of muscle strength and power. Namely, it is known that muscle strength and power are dependent on the body size, body weight and muscle mass (size, volume) [56–59]. In addition to increases in muscle mass, age-related improvements in neural activation, motor unit recruitment, and intermuscular coordination likely contribute to the observed gains in strength and power across adolescence [60]. Previous studies have shown that there is a positive correlation between hand grip strength test values and various aspects of body dimension and body composition (body weight, muscle mass, muscle volume), both in adults [61–63] and children [64–68]. While the relationship between body size/body weight/muscle mass and muscle power assessed through different vertical jump tests is more complex [69], previous studies indicate a certain association, especially in the population of children and youth [70–74]. Since the maturation, that is, growth and development, is accompanied by an increase in muscle mass and body weight in general [75, 76], the increase in muscle strength and power is logical and expected. This increase is supported by their daily training regimens. Previous studies have shown that children who regularly practise volleyball exhibit greater muscle strength and power than their untrained peers [77], and that systematic and planned volleyball training leads to increased physical fitness in children and youth [78]. Accordingly, it is expected that continuous, systematic, and planned volleyball practice has a significant influence on the development and manifestation of muscle strength and power in children and adolescents.

As previously stated, a linear age increase was observed in nearly all measured parameters. This increase was, on average, 16.7% (year by year) regarding FmaxR, 18.4% in RFDR, 16.3% in FmaxL, 18.8% in RFDL and 9.4% in CMJ in males. In the female sample, the increase was smaller, 10.6% in FmaxR, 12.3% in RFDR, 10.7% in FmaxL, 13.5% in RFDL and 3.4% in CMJ. In both males and females, the increase was highest regarding the RFD parameter (18.6%, 12.9%, respectively). RFD refers to the explosive strength, that is, the ability to increase force or torque as quickly as possible during a rapid voluntary contraction realised from a low or resting level [79], and previous studies stated the relatively high RFD compared to the Fmax in children and youth, which may be affected by the lower muscle tendon stiffness [80]. On the other hand, the lowest increase was detected in the CMJ parameter (6.4%, on average). This result is expected, since the vertical jump is a complex movement [81], which implies movement in several joints, mutual synergy of different muscles, and finally, it depends to a certain extent on technique (experience), unlike the movement pattern characteristic for the hand grip strength test. Unlike isolated strength measures, CMJ performance is additionally constrained by stretch–shortening cycle efficiency, intermuscular coordination, and technical proficiency, which may attenuate relative age-related gains compared with isometric strength measures [82]. The increase in all measured parameters and both sexes was highest for the age group 10 vs. 11 (28.07%, on average), and 12 vs. 13 vs. 14 (17.8% on average), after which smaller differences were observed (7%, on average). In the most measured parameters, the increase after the age of 14 was smaller, until the age of 18, where the increase was high compared to the age of 17 (7.9%, on average). These results are in line with the growth dynamics of children and youth, and their increased periods of development [83, 84].

Although differences between sexes were not statistically examined, due to unequal sample size (539 males and 2798 females), there are noticeable differences in the manifestation of muscle strength and power between boys and girls involved in volleyball. Accordingly, the observed sex differences should be interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially. It can be noticed that boys and girls have similar values of measured parameters of muscle strength and power until the age of 12, when boys are separating, having higher values than girls in all of the measured parameters across all age categories. The percentage differences between sexes in all measured parameters at the age of 10 to 13 were 6.3% on average, and from the age of 14 to 18, 36.9%, on average, with an increase in the difference with age, and the maximum difference in the age of 18 (44.2%, on average). Boys and girls differ the most in the RFD parameters (25.2%, on average), and the least in the CMJ (19.1%).

The main aim of the study was to establish reference values of muscle strength and power in young volleyball players. Since the present study is conducted on the largest sample so far, established reference values can be generalized and they can serve coaches from the field of volleyball, but also for practitioners from other sports and areas of physical education. For example, athletes consistently performing above higher percentile thresholds (e.g., 75th or 90th percentile) for their age group may warrant closer monitoring or targeted development strategies within long-term athlete development frameworks. Namely, within the process of sports training and talent identification, coaches are encouraged to implement tests for assessing the muscle strength and power, and use these reference values and percentile range in order to track the growth and development of children and youth involved in volleyball, monitor the training process, and identify potential talents in volleyball from the aspect of muscle strength and power. Moreover, integrating these reference data and percentile range into talent identification and long-term athlete development (LTAD) frameworks supports a more individualized approach to youth training. Obtained age- and sex- based trends and normatives in the development of muscle strength and power can be used as a significant tool in an individual approach to the training process in volleyball.

Finally, the strengths and limitations of the study should be acknowledged. As previously mentioned, the main strength of the study is the sample of participants, with the largest sample size so far, enabling the generalization of the results. The main study limitation is the equipment for the assessment of muscle strength. The hand grip system used in the study has a specific sampling method for calculating the RFD, so the obtained norms are limited to this system alone. Additionally, normalization according to BW or BH was not performed. Another limitation is the absence of direct indicators of biological maturation (e.g., Tanner stage or maturity offset), which may partly explain inter-individual variability within the same chronological age groups. In addition, measurements were conducted during the summer, i.e. end of the competing season (fatigue impact), and during the volleyball camp (potential sampling bias), which may have influenced the results obtained and limited generalisation. Further, since the sample consisted of participants of heterogeneous ages, their training and competing experience were also heterogeneous. Finally, there is a sex imbalance in the participants’ sample, which disables the possibility of investigating a sex difference in measured properties. Future studies should take into consideration the previously mentioned factors.

Conclusion

The study results revealed the presence of significant age group differences in muscle strength and power in young volleyball players, in both males and females. A significant increase was observed in all measured parameters. In both males and females, the increase was highest regarding the RFD parameter. These changes are likely explained by the combined effects of biological maturation, increases in body size and muscle mass, neuromuscular development, and systematic volleyball training.

Since the present study is conducted on the largest sample so far, established reference values and percentile range can be generalized and they can serve coaches from the field of volleyball, but also for practitioners from other sports and areas of physical education. Coaches can use these reference values in order to track the growth and development of children and youth involved in volleyball, monitor the training process, and identify potential talents in volleyball from the aspect of muscle strength and power. Obtained age- and sex- based trends and normatives in the development of muscle strength and power can be used as a significant tool in an individual approach to the training process in volleyball.
