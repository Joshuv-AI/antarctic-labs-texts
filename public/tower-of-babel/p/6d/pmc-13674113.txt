Utilization patterns of low‐titer group O whole blood in a level 1 trauma center

INTRODUCTION

Whole blood (WB) transfusion has re‐emerged as a critical component of trauma resuscitation, particularly for patients with life‐threatening hemorrhage. 1 Use of low‐titer group O whole blood (LTOWB) has increased in both military and civilian settings due to its ease of storage, balanced composition of red blood cells, plasma, and platelets, which allows for rapid hemostatic volume resuscitation, and reduced amounts of saline additive solutions, and has been associated with early survival. 2 , 3 , 4 While trauma has been the primary indication for WB transfusion, its applications are expanding. Emerging evidence supports its use in additional acute care settings such as necrotizing soft tissue infections, major cardiac surgeries, gastrointestinal bleeding, and postpartum hemorrhage. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Multiple observational studies have examined clinical outcomes associated with WB use. At the same time, less attention has been paid to real‐world utilization patterns, operational constraints, and the final disposition of WB units once issued. In practice, WB availability is frequently limited by donor supply, storage requirements, and competing clinical priorities. As a result, retrospective data often reflect product availability rather than true clinical demand, limiting insight into how WB is deployed across complex systems. 9

Harborview Medical Center (HMC) is a Level 1 Trauma Center serving Washington, Alaska, Northern Idaho, and Western Montana. Since 2019, its Transfusion Service Laboratory (TSL) has provided LTOWB across various hospital settings, including trauma resuscitation, critical care, and surgery. They are also early in establishing an efficient pre‐hospital transfusion system through air and ground emergency medical services. 10 , 11 , 12 This study aims to explore the utilization patterns of WB units issued by HMC over a one‐year period during calendar year 2024.

METHODS

The study was conducted with approval from the University of Washington Human Subjects Protection Office. The study was exempt from informed consent because all data were obtained from existing patient care records, and no patients were contacted. Primary data were obtained from the Transfusion Service database (Sunquest, Tucson, AZ) and linked to individual patient medical records (EPIC, Verona, WI) using the medical record number associated with the final blood product issue. WB units were analyzed at the unit level, with final disposition defined as transfusion or wastage. Analyses were descriptive in nature; no comparative or inferential statistical testing was performed.

RESULTS

Overall whole blood utilization

During calendar year 2024, a total of 1915 WB units were issued by the Harborview Transfusion Service. Of these, 36 units (1.9%) were not transfused due to expiration or temperature excursions, leaving 1879 units (98.1%) transfused to 524 patients, including 237 (12.6%) which were administered prehospital. Among transfused units, 1817 units (96.7%) were administered to 477 patients treated at HMC, while 62 units (3.3%) were transfused in a pre‐hospital setting to 47 patients during transport to other facilities (Figure 1).

Patient demographics

Among the 477 Harborview patients who received WB during the study period, 358 (75.1%) male patients received a total of 1494 units (82.2%), while 119 (24.9%) female patients received 323 units (17.8%). The sex designation of the 47 patients who were transported to other facilities is not available, but all obstetric cases went to other hospitals.

Indications for whole blood transfusion

Among the 1817 WB units transfused to patients ultimately treated at HMC, trauma accounted for the majority of units issued. Motor vehicle collisions (MVCs) were the most common indication, representing 836 units (46.0%), followed by gunshot wounds with 467 units (25.7%). Additional traumatic indications included cuts, lacerations, stab wounds, or tears with 130 units (7.2%), falls with 129 units (7.1%), blunt assaults with 8 units (0.4%), and other traumatic mechanisms such as industrial injuries, tree‐related trauma, bicycle accidents, and dog bites with 22 units (1.2%). Collectively, traumatic mechanisms accounted for 1592 units (87.6%) transfused (Figure 2).

Pie chart showing the distribution of clinical indications among patients who received low‐titer group O whole blood, with trauma accounting for the majority of utilization.

WB was also transfused for selected non‐trauma indications, accounting for 225 units (12.4%). The highest use in this was found to be gastrointestinal bleeding with 68 units (3.7%), followed by abdominal aortic aneurysm repair and other vascular surgeries with 65 units (3.6%). The other indications included necrotizing soft tissue infections with 25 units (1.4%), burns and other surgical debridements with 7 units (0.4%), non‐trauma orthopedic procedures with 10 units (0.6%), and other surgical indications with 2 units (0.1%). Medical indications, including cardiac‐related conditions (in‐hospital and out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest and cardiac catheterization laboratory cases), with 28 units (1.5%), and other shock states (septic shock or toxic ingestions), with 20 units (1.1%).

Location and setting of transfusion

Pre‐hospital WB transfusion accounted for 237 units (12.6%) of all transfused WB units. This included 53 units (2.8%) administered during Airlift Northwest transport as part of the TOWAR (Type O Whole blood and assessment of Age during prehospital Resuscitation) trial and 184 units (9.8%) transfused by Medic One fire department ambulances. Among pre‐hospital transfusions, 49 Airlift Northwest units (20.7%) and 126 Medic One units (53.2%) were administered to patients ultimately treated at HMC, while 4 Airlift Northwest units (1.7%) and 58 Medic One units (24.5%) were transfused to the 47 patients transported to other hospitals (Figure 1).

Within HMC, WB was administered across multiple clinical settings. Of the 1642 in‐hospital transfused units, 900 units (54.8%) were given in the emergency department, 663 units (40.4%) in the operating room, 53 units (3.2%) in intensive care units, and 26 units (1.6%) were transfused in non‐emergency department interventional radiology or imaging locations.

Patient outcomes

Of the 477 patients admitted to HMC who received a total of 1817 WB units, 141 patients (29.6%) died during hospitalization and received 811 units (44.6%), while 336 patients (70.4%) survived to discharge and received 1006 units (55.4%). Outcomes for the 47 patients who received 62 WB units during transport to other facilities were not available.

DISCUSSION

Historically, WB transfusion was the primary modality for resuscitation in both military and civilian trauma care throughout much of the twentieth century. 2 The subsequent transition to component therapy enabled clinicians to tailor transfusions to patient needs across a broad range of clinical indications but introduced logistical complexity and potential inefficiencies during massive hemorrhage. In the setting of acute traumatic hemorrhage, these inefficiencies may delay balanced resuscitation or contribute to dilutional coagulopathy. Over the past two decades, renewed interest in low‐titer group O whole blood (LTOWB) has been driven by observational data from military and civilian settings suggesting improved early hemostasis, reduced overall blood product exposure, and associations with improved short‐term survival when administered early in resuscitation. 3 , 4 , 13 While these studies have been critical in re‐establishing WB as a viable resuscitation product, they have largely focused on patient outcomes rather than operational realities or patterns of use. 9 Our findings address this gap by describing WB deployment across a large, integrated level 1 trauma system under real‐world conditions.

HMC has been building a WB program since 2019. The program initially began at approximately 50 units a month, with the goals of developing accountable support for city fire department paramedic ambulances, building in‐hospital experience to support upcoming national randomized clinical trials, and hemorrhage resuscitation. 12 By 2024, the program had expanded to approximately 150 units per month and was supporting all five paramedic ambulance systems in the county, three national WB randomized clinical trials, and additional high‐blood use scenarios across multiple clinical specialties. Consistent with this growth, WB use remained predominantly trauma‐focused, particularly for motor vehicle collisions and penetrating injuries, reflecting prioritization for patients at the highest risk of death from massive hemorrhage. Importantly, this growth was driven primarily by the goal of supporting additional needs and clinical research; WB supply itself remained a persistent constraint. Trauma volume in the Pacific Northwest is also highly seasonal, with substantially higher demand during summer months, while suppliers favor steady, predictable orders, creating recurring tension between research and clinical demand and available supply.

Although trauma accounted for most of the WB utilization, meaningful use was observed in non‐trauma contexts, including gastrointestinal bleeding, vascular surgery, cardiac emergencies, necrotizing soft tissue infections, and other shock states (Figure 2). These findings are consistent with recent literature describing expanded WB use beyond trauma and suggest that trauma‐based WB programs are naturally evolving to support other high‐acuity clinical needs. 7 Importantly, this expansion occurred despite persistent supply limitations, suggesting an increase in clinical demand and WB utilization as supply improves.

A key contributor to the success and scalability of this program was the development of operational practices designed to support geographically distributed care while minimizing waste. Detailed methods for establishing our prehospital and community WB programs, including ambulance‐based deployment and economical distribution of components across a large geographic area, have been described previously. 10 , 12 Product conservation strategies implemented during this period of rapid expansion included redirecting short‐dated inventory to appropriate alternative clinical uses and validation of Credo coolers for seven‐day use in the relatively colder climate of Seattle, WA. These practices enabled safe deployment across air and ground EMS without compromising product integrity. In addition, TSL at HMC retained ownership of distributed WB units until the seal of the cooler was broken, permitting these products to remain in active inventory unless transfused or rendered unusable. This model allowed Airlift Northwest and King County Medic One to pay only for units that were used or damaged, supporting cost‐effective distribution of WB and contributing to utilization rates of approximately 98%, with projected expansion to more than 2500 units in 2025.

This study was undertaken to answer a practical operational question raised by peer trauma centers across the country: where does LTOWB go at a high‐volume Level 1 trauma center, and how has wastage been kept to a minimum? This descriptive analysis provides a snapshot of all WB units issued by Harborview Medical Center TSL during the calendar year 2024. Supplies of WB were intermittently constrained. On many days, we had insufficient inventory available to enroll patients in the largest national randomized clinical trial, and on some days, no WB remained after allocating inventory for pre‐hospital care. As a result, utilization patterns observed in this study reflect product availability and institutional prioritization rather than clinical demand. WB was reserved for trauma patients anticipated to have the highest transfusion requirements and worst outcomes, which likely contributed to the observed in‐hospital mortality. Despite these limitations, examination of the final issue data for all WB units issued over this one‐year period provides important insights into real‐world utilization across trauma, pre‐hospital care, clinical trials, and non‐trauma indications. These findings highlight the impact of supply, logistics, and system‐level infrastructure on WB availability and utilization and underscore the importance of incorporating both operational and logistical considerations into future WB research and program planning. Operational decisions specific to our setting should not be generalized without consideration of local context; centers seeking broader implementation guidance are encouraged to consult updated resources, including guidelines from the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma.

This study has several limitations. It is a single‐center, retrospective, descriptive analysis and is not designed to assess clinical efficacy, comparative effectiveness, or unmet clinical demand. Formal safety analysis of WB use was beyond the scope of this study. With fewer than 100 patients receiving WB for non‐trauma indications, our sample was insufficient to detect meaningful safety signals; notably, the two largest randomized trials of LTOWB in trauma reported no WB‐attributable safety concerns. 14 , 15 Utilization patterns reflect institutional priorities and may not generalize to systems with different donor availability or operational models. Nonetheless, these data inform future WB research by highlighting the importance of operational context and sustainable supply.

CONCLUSION

In this single‐center descriptive analysis, we demonstrate that WB is utilized across a broad range of clinical settings within a large, integrated trauma system, with most of the use occurring in patients with traumatic hemorrhage but meaningful application extending beyond trauma. Real‐world WB utilization was influenced by supply constraints, operational priorities, and system‐level logistics rather than clinical demand alone. Operational innovations, including extended storage cooler validation and inventory ownership models, enabled high utilization while supporting expansion across pre‐hospital and in‐hospital settings. As WB adoption continues to grow, incorporating utilization and operational data will be essential to inform future clinical trials, guide inventory planning, and ensure sustainable access within regional trauma systems.
