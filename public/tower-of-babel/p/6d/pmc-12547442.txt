Combined antegrade and retrograde approach for embolization of abdominal chyle leak
Authors: Kasthuri Nair, Mahmoud Abbas, Brandon Lei, Bryan Nicholas Swilley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChyle leaks are an uncommon but often morbid complication of surgical resections in the retroperitoneal space. They can lead to multiple/prolonged hospitalizations and significantly diminish patient quality of life. Here we present a case of recalcitrant chylous ascites following resection of a renal mass. Lymphatic embolization was attempted but unsuccessful on two occasions (antegrade followed by retrograde approach) at outside hospitals. We used a combined antegrade and retrograde approach to overcome his challenging anatomy and treat the chyle leak definitively. This case highlights the usefulness and reliability of this technique for achieving bidirectional control of the lymphatic system.Keywords: Chylous ascites, Lymphangiography, Lymphatic embolization

Introduction

The thoracic duct is a 2–5 mm diameter smooth muscle and connective tissue structure which commonly ascends from the superior aspect of the cisterna chyli and passes through the posterior mediastinum up to the lower cervical spine.1 It drains into the confluence of the left subclavian and internal jugular veins junction superiorly. However, multiple anatomic variations involving the origin, course, and termination of the thoracic duct exist.1 For instance, the cisterna chyli is absent in more than 50 % of the population.2 Other notable variations include a bifid lower trunk, replacement of the lower thoracic duct with a lymphatic vessel plexus, bilateral thoracic ducts, and termination into the azygous system.2,3 Congestive heart failure, cirrhosis/portal hypertension, malignancy, trauma, surgical changes, and radiation can lead to significant alterations in thoracic duct anatomy.

Common presentations of lymphatic injury include lymphatic ascites, lymphocele, lymphorrhea, lymphatic fistula, chylous ascites, chylorrhea, chyloretroperitoneum, and chylothorax.4

Conservative measures such diet modification and parenteral nutrition are a mainstay of management of chylous leaks.5 Lymphangiography, which offers both diagnostic and therapeutic value, is often warranted and effective; a systematic analysis of pooled data found lymphatic embolization following a postoperative lymphatic leak within the abdomen had a technical success rate of 97.4 % and clinical success rate of 70.3 %.6 However, establishing durable access into the lymphatic system can be both technically challenging and time-consuming.5,7 In certain instances, a retrograde approach may be desired but cannulation of the cervicothoracic duct can also be exceedingly difficult or outright unattainable.

Case description

A male in his 60s underwent resection for a left renal mass (stage 1A RCCA on pathologic examination). On post-operative day (POD) 1, he developed what was suspected to be chylous ascites but, in light of decreased drain output, he was discharged on POD 4.

A week later, he presented to another OSH with increasing abdominal distension. He was found to be hyponatremic (120 mmol/L) which improved gradually with IV repletion. Shortly thereafter, he underwent bilateral pelvic and retroperitoneal lymphangiogram which demonstrated a leak from a retroperitoneal lymph node that was treated using Onyx liquid embolic.

There was concern for continued leakage after this and increasing ascites was demonstrated on CT imaging. The patient returned to IR where retrograde thoracic duct cannulation was attempted but ultimately unsuccessful. The patient's ascites continued to worsen and he underwent two more paracentesis procedures, which yielded chylous fluid. Bowel rest and total parenteral nutrition were initiated and the patient was transferred to our institution for further treatment.

He initially underwent ultrasound-guided paracentesis that resulted in the successful removal of 2600 mL of abdominal fluid. Analysis of the aspirated peritoneal fluid revealed a triglyceride body fluid level at 104 mg/dL, protein body fluid level was 4 gm/dL, and creatinine body fluid level was 0.94 mg/dL. Other notable pre-procedure findings include a serum albumin level of 2.6 gm/dL and total protein of 5.1 gm/dL.

Preexisting radioopaque material within the lymphatics of the left hemiabdomen was seen at the onset of our procedure. The bilateral inguinal lymph nodes were accessed utilizing 22G 3.5-inch spinal needles. Lipiodol was slowly injected to opacify the retroperitoneal lymphatic ducts and eventually the cisterna chyli (Fig. 1a). The cisterna chyli was then punctured percutaneously with a 21 gauge Chiba needle and a 0.018″ Glidewire Advantage (Terumo Interventional Systems) was navigated antegrade into the thoracic duct. A 2.4F ProGreat microcatheter (Terumo Interventional Systems) was advanced over the wire (Fig. 1b).

(a) Bilateral groin lymph node access utilizing 22G spinal needles with opacification of the lymphatic system with lipiodol. Onyx is noted in the left paraspinal region along the left aspect of L3 vertebral body from prior embolization. (b) Access of the cisterna chyli utilizing 21G Chiba needle and 0.018″ Glidewire Advantage. (c) A combination of 6 F Rivera Balt catheter, Gooseneck 10 mm snare, and telescoping 3 Fr and 4 Fr Navi cross catheter were utilized and advanced across the venous lymphatic junction into the thoracic duct. (d,e) Embolization of the left cisterna chyli utilizing 1:1 Glue and Lipiodol, followed by deployment of 6 mm Nester coil. (f) After removal of all devices, final image shows stable position of embolization material in the left cisterna chyli.

It was noted that the site of lymphatic leakage was caudad to the percutaneous access at the level of the cisterna chyli. The leak could not be treated from this vantage point and more inferior access was not feasible due to miniscule size of the lymphatic ducts below the cisterna chyli.

The left basilic vein was accessed and a 6F vascular sheath was placed. An Amplatz 10 mm gooseneck snare (Medtronic) was positioned in the left subclavian vein. The 0.018″ Glidewire Advantage was then advanced from the thoracic duct through a tortuous venolymphatic junction into the subclavian vein where it was captured with the snare and pulled out through the transbasilic venous sheath to achieve floss access. 0.018″ compatible and 0.035” compatible Navicross catheters (Terumo Interventional Systems) were then backloaded coaxially onto the wire and advanced retrograde past the venolymphatic junction into the thoracic ducts (Fig. 1c).

Retrograde lymphangiography was performed using a Sniper balloon occlusion microcatheter (Varian). The offending lymphatic duct was then embolized with a 1:1 mixture of N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) and lipiodol. Following removal of the microcatheter, the injected material appeared to migrate out of the targeted duct and into the thoracic duct. The leaking duct was selected once more and treated with more 1:1 NBCA:lipiodol followed by a 6 mm Nester coil. Subsequent fluoroscopic imaging showed stable positioning of the glue and coil (Fig. 1 d-f).

Complication event & management

The patient remained hemodynamically stable and his diet was advanced on POD1. There was some concern for post-procedural intraabdominal bleeding but a three-phase CTA was negative for hematoma or active hemorrhage. He received one unit of packed RBCs and responded appropriately to that. Abdominal ultrasound on POD6 showed no significant free fluid within the abdomen.

By POD9, the patient's symptoms had improved significantly. He was tolerating PO intake and his parenteral nutrition was stopped. He was discharged in good condition and did not require further paracentesis procedures thereafter.

Discussion

Chylous ascites often manifests as worsening abdominal distension, but progresses to pain and peritonitis in 11 % of cases.8 This condition has high morbidity and mortality rates.9 Chylous ascites can be managed with non-invasive measures such as diet modification and pharmacological agents.10 If conservative management fails or is inadequate, other options for treatment include abdominal paracentesis, lymphangiography/lymphoscintigraphy with percutaneous embolization, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, peritoneovenous shunts, or suture ligation of the lymphatics.10 Glue embolization in particular was found to be a feasible and effective treatment option for chylous ascites following retroperitoneal surgery, as is the case of the patient presented here.11

The case of chylous ascites discussed in this report was initially resistant to multiple treatment modalities as described above. This is not an uncommon occurrence and studies have shown greater clinical success if the site of leakage is identified prior to treatment.12 In this patient, initial antegrade lymphangiography and embolization with onyx did not adequately treat the leak and a purely retrograde approach was stymied by the tortuosity of the veno lymphatic junction. We used a combination percutaneous antegrade and retrograde transvenous approach to successfully bypass the challenging anatomy and treat a leak in close proximity to the cisterna chyli.

Retrograde catheterization of the thoracic duct was first reported in literature by Mittleider et al., in 2008 when they used retrograde cannulation of the Thoracic Duct to successfully treat a patient with chylous ascites who failed conventional percutaneous direct cisterna chyli cannulation.13 The authors noted this novel approach is especially useful when “the leak emanates from the cisterna chyli itself or from proximal, smaller lymphatic channels,” the patient lacks a cisterna chyli, or has previous abdominal surgery or trauma that complicates the anterograde approach.13 Since the Mittleider et al. article, multiple case reports and series utilizing retrograde access have been published. The retrograde approach has largely been successful at identifying (78 %) and embolizing (89 %) leaks.14 Jun et al. also showed significant improved in technical and clinical success when retrograde thoracic duct cannulation was used to bail out conventional anterograde access.15

The one report with a low success rate (61.5 %) for transvenous retrograde thoracic ductography attributed it to patients with challenging thoracic duct anatomy at the cervical levels.16 The authors of that publication still recommended the retrograde approach prior to conservative therapy. They believed that transvenous retrograde thoracic ductography plus embolization could achieve shorter treatment periods than previously reported in literature.16

Conclusions

Patients with chylous ascites refractory to lifestyle management and anterograde lymphangiography with embolization can still benefit from minimally invasive interventions. Retrograde lymphangiography, though technically challenging, is a viable treatment option even in patients with challenging anatomies. A combination of approaches (anterograde and retrograde) can also be a feasible option.
