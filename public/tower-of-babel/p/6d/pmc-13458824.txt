Does adult lumbar scoliosis affect spine T-score accuracy in osteoporosis diagnosis?—a cross-sectional study of adults aged 50 years and older
Authors: Gabriel Liu, Seth Ian Sim, Bo Xiang Bryan Neo, Chan Yiong Huak, Wong Hee-Kit, Jun-Hao Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is the gold standard for assessing bone mineral density (BMD) and diagnosing osteoporosis (T-score ≤−2.5). Lumbar spine T-scores are often used when hip measurements are unavailable or unreliable. However, degenerative spinal changes can artifactually elevate lumbar spine BMD, leading to discordance between spine and hip T-scores and potential misclassification of osteoporosis. The impact of adult degenerative scoliosis on this discordance remains poorly defined. This study evaluates the association between lumbar scoliosis and spine-hip T-score discordance in adults ≥50 years.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study included consecutive adults ≥50 years who underwent DEXA scans over a 4-year period at a university hospital. Patients with secondary causes of osteoporosis were excluded. Participants were grouped by presence or absence of scoliosis. Spine and hip T-scores were recorded and classified as no, minor, or major discordance. No discordance indicated no grade difference between sites. Minor discordance referred to a one-grade difference (normal vs. osteopenia or osteopenia vs. osteoporosis). Major discordance referred to a two-grade difference (normal vs. osteoporosis).ResultsA total of 6,969 patients were included; 91.6% were female with a mean age of 60.6 years. Scoliosis was present in 637 patients (9.1%), with a mean Cobb angle of 17° (range, 10–66°). Spine T-scores classified more patients as normal (43.0% vs. 31.1%) and fewer as osteopenic (41.5% vs. 48.9%) or osteoporotic (15.5% vs. 19.1%) compared with hip T-scores. Patients with scoliosis were older (67 vs. 60 years, P<0.001) and more likely female (94% vs. 91%, P<0.001). Spine-hip discordance was more frequent in scoliosis patients, with minor discordance in 44.1% vs. 39.0% and major discordance in 5.0% vs. 2.8% of non-scoliosis patients.ConclusionsLumbar scoliosis is associated with increased spine-hip T-score discordance, limiting the reliability of lumbar spine measurements in osteoporosis assessment. These effects are more pronounced in older adults and females. Clinicians should interpret spine T-scores cautiously in the presence of scoliosis and prioritise hip measurements when assessing bone health or planning interventions where bone quality is critical.Keywords: Degenerative scoliosis, osteoporosis, discordance, bone mineral density (BMD)

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 6,969 adults aged ≥50 years, lumbar scoliosis significantly increased spine-hip T score discordance on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA).

• Patients with scoliosis had higher rates of both minor (44.1%) and major (5.0%) discordance compared with non-scoliotic patients (39.0% and 2.8%).

• The hip T-score was consistently more negative than the spine T-score in both groups, but the discrepancy was exaggerated in scoliosis.

• Male and age ≥65 years independently increased the risk of both minor and major discordance.

• Increasing Cobb angle was associated with progressively less negative spine T-scores relative to the hip.

What is known and what is new

• Degenerative spinal changes falsely elevate lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) on DEXA. This leads to spine-hip T-score discordance, typically with the spine appearing less osteoporotic than the hip. Prior studies described this phenomenon mainly in non-scoliotic degenerative spines.

• This is the largest population-based study to show that lumbar scoliosis itself independently worsens T-score discordance. The study identifies high-risk subgroups where spine T-scores are least reliable: males, adults aged more than 65 years old and patients with larger Cobb angles.

What is the implication and what should change now?

• In these patients, clinicians could consider hip or forearm T-scores and be aware of potential discordance when interpreting results. Reporting protocols might note scoliosis as a factor affecting spine BMD and suggest alternative sites for assessment. Incorporating spinal morphology into DEXA interpretation may help improve accuracy in patients with scoliosis.

Introduction

Osteoporosis affects more than 200 million women globally (1-3) with the incidence of hip fractures increasing 5 times in the past 40 years (3). The disease impacts patients’ quality of life and accounts for 1.2 million quality adjusted life years lost in the European Union in year 2010 (4). The dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) measures the bone mineral density (BMD) at the hip (proximal femur, femoral neck, or trochanter), spine or forearm (5-8), and is widely considered as the gold standard measurement tool for the diagnosis of osteoporosis. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines osteoporosis as having a BMD that is at least 2.5 standard deviations lower than the mean BMD of a young-adult referenced population (T-score ≤−2.5) (9). The International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) recommended that osteoporosis be diagnosed based on lowest T-score found at the spine or hip, or based on the forearm T-score if the hip or spine T-scores cannot be interpreted or measured (5).

While the gold standard of osteoporosis diagnosis relies on the hip T-score, the spine T-score is useful in diagnosing osteoporosis in patients with hip replacements, or for monitoring treatment progression (10). However, the use of the spine T-score is not without problems. Previous studies have demonstrated that degenerative spinal conditions falsely elevate the spine T-score in osteoporotic patients with spinal degeneration. This occurs due to formation of syndesmophytes, osteophytes, sclerotic endplates and other degenerative changes involving the spine which increases the density of the spine and elevates the spine T-score (10-15). This causes discordance between the spine and hip T-scores, with hip T-score having a more negative value (6,11,12,14-16). In patients with scoliosis, we postulate that this T-score discordance may be more pronounced. In the scoliotic spine, asymmetric biomechanical loading produces pathological stress concentrations on the concave side of the curve. This chronic mechanical overload accelerates cartilage degeneration at the facet joints, stimulates osteophyte and syndesmophyte proliferation, and drives localised subchondral sclerosis (6). The resulting heterogeneous increase in bone density is asymmetric—concentrated at the facet joints and posterior elements on the compressive side of the deformity (16). Standard lumbar DEXA imaging uses a two-dimensional anteroposterior projection, which cannot discriminate this localised posterior element sclerosis from true trabecular bone density in the vertebral body. Consequently, the artefactual contribution of facet hypertrophy and subchondral sclerosis is superimposed onto the true vertebral BMD signal, inflating the measured spine T-score (17).

Degenerative scoliosis has a reported prevalence of up to 9% in adults aged 50 and older (17,18). However, to the authors’ best knowledge, few studies have reported the use of the spine T-score in adult patients with degenerative scoliosis. The aim of this study is to analyse the impact of scoliosis on the discordance between spine and hip T-scores in the diagnosis of osteoporosis. We hypothesise that scoliosis will exaggerate the discordance between spine and hip T-scores. This would have significant impact on healthcare planning and monitoring of osteoporosis as the population ages. We present this article in accordance with the TREND reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0071/rc).

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cross-sectional study included all consecutive adults aged 50 years and older who underwent DEXA scans at National University Hospital over a 4-year period. Exclusion criteria were applied using electronic database filters and removed individuals with secondary causes of osteoporosis, including malignancy, prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy, severe pulmonary disease, and congenital bone disorders. Patients were classified as having scoliosis or no scoliosis based on assessment of the DEXA scout image. Scoliosis was defined according to Scoliosis Research Society criteria as a lateral curvature with a Cobb angle ≥10° on the coronal plane, and severity was measured using the Cobb method (19). The angle was defined as the angle subtended by lines drawn along the superior endplate of the upper end vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower end vertebra. Measurements were performed by a trained assessor using the institution’s imaging software.

Sample size

A total of 6,969 patients were available for analysis following the application of exclusion criteria. A power analysis was performed based on the observed difference in overall T-score discordance rates between scoliotic (49.1%) and non-scoliotic (41.8%) patients. With a two-tailed significance level of α=0.05, the final cohort of 6,969 patients achieved 94.6% statistical power to detect this difference.

Ethical approval

We conducted this study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study’s protocol was reviewed and approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (approval number: 2015/00576). The requirement for informed consent was waived given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymised data.

Outcome definitions

DEXA scans were performed using a GE HealthCare densitometer, calibrated daily using a manufacturer-supplied anthropometric spine phantom in accordance with ISCD guidelines (5). T-scores were calculated using the manufacturer’s normative reference database. Spine and hip T-scores were recorded for each patient and classified into ‘No Discordance’, ‘Minor Discordance’, or ‘Major Discordance’. ‘No Discordance’ is defined as no grade discrepancy between the two scores. ‘Minor Discordance’ is defined as a one-grade diagnostic discrepancy, occurring when a patient is categorised as normal based on one T-score but osteopenic based on the other, or similarly reclassified from osteopenic to osteoporotic. ‘Major Discordance’ is defined as a two-grade discrepancy, occurring when a patient is categorised as normal based on one T-score but osteoporotic based on the other. Osteoporosis is defined by the WHO as a T-score of −2.5 or less, osteopenia as a T-score between −1.0 and −2.5, and normal as a T-score greater than −1.0 (10,20-24).

Statistical analysis

The Chi-squared test and the independent t-test were used for univariate analyses to examine associations between risk factors and spine-hip T-score discordance. Risk factors including demographic data and the presence of scoliosis were simultaneously entered as independent variables into a multinomial logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of minor and major T-score discordance. The model was adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) as covariates, selected on the basis of their established biological plausibility as confounders. All reported P values are two-tailed, and a threshold of P<0.05 was taken as the level of statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 6,969 adults aged ≥50 years were included; the cohort was predominantly female (91.6%) with a mean age of 60.6 years; 637 patients (9.1%) had scoliosis, with a mean Cobb angle of 17° (range, 10–66°). Based on the hip T-score, 2,234 patients (32.1%) were classified as normal, 3,406 (48.9%) as osteopenic, and 1,329 (19.1%) as osteoporotic. Based on the spine T-score, 2,996 patients (43.0%) were classified as normal, 2,892 (41.5%) as osteopenic, and 1,081 (15.5%) as osteoporotic. The baseline demographics are summarised in Figure 1.

Patient demographics. Dis, discordance.

Patients with scoliosis were older (67 vs. 60 years, P<0.001) and had a higher proportion of females (94% vs. 91%, P<0.001). Spine-hip T-score discordance was more common in patients with scoliosis, with 44.1% showing minor and 5.0% major discordance, compared with 39.0% and 2.8% in those without scoliosis. A larger discrepancy in osteoporosis diagnosis was seen in patients with scoliosis, with 31.6% classified as osteoporotic using the hip T-score compared with 22.0% using the spine T-score. In contrast, the difference was smaller in patients without scoliosis (17.8% vs. 14.9%).

Using univariate analysis, significant discordance was found between hip and spine T-scores in the study population (Figure 1). There were significantly more patients with hip and spine T-score discordance in patients with scoliosis (P<0.001). In patients with scoliosis, 281 (44.1%) of patients had minor T-score discordance and 32 (5%) had major T-score discordance. In patients without scoliosis, 2,471 (39.0%) of patients had minor T-score discordance and 176 (2.8%) had major T-score discordance.

Increasing age as a factor affecting T-score discordance

Using bivariate analysis, an increase in T-score discordance rate was observed among patients with normal, osteopenic, and osteoporotic T-scores with an increase in the patients’ age.

There was a significant increase in T-score discordance in the non-scoliosis population (P<0.001). There were 1.1 times as many minor T-score discordance in patients aged ≥65 years old compared to <65 years old (43.0% vs. 37.4%). There were 2.8 times as many major T-score discordance in patients aged ≥65 years old compared to <65 years old (5.1% vs. 1.8%) (P<0.001). There was also a statistically significant increase in T-score discordance noted with increasing age ranges from <60, 60–69 to ≥70 years old. The most discordance was observed in patients aged 70 years and above (49.6%) (P<0.001) (Figure 2).

In patients with scoliosis, increase in T-score discordance rates were observed with increase with the patients’ age. T-score discordance rates increased from 44.2% (<60 years old), to 48.9% (60–69 years old), and to 52.4% (≥70 years old). However, age was not found to be significantly related to T-score discordance in this population (P=0.08) (Figure 2).

Gender as a factor affecting T-score discordance

Male vs. female

Male patients demonstrated higher rates of both minor and major T-score discordance compared to female patients in both the non-scoliosis and scoliosis groups.

In patients without scoliosis, minor T-score discordance was 1.4 times more prevalent in males than females (51.6% in males, 37.8% in females). An even greater prevalence was observed for major T-score discordance. Major T-score discordance was 7.1 times more prevalent in males without scoliosis than females without scoliosis (12.8% in males, 1.8% in females) (P<0.001) (Figure 3).

In patients with scoliosis, minor T-score discordance was 1.6 times more prevalent in males than females (66.7% in males, 42.6% in females). A slightly greater prevalence was observed for major T-score discordance. Major T-score discordance was 3.6 times more prevalent in males with scoliosis than females with scoliosis (15.4% in males, 4.3% in females) (P<0.001) (Figure 3).

Female scoliotic patients vs. female non-scoliotic patients

Female patients with and without scoliosis were compared. Minor T-score discordance was 1.1 times more prevalent in female patients with scoliosis than those without scoliosis (42.6% vs. 37.8%). Major T-score discordance was 2.4 times more prevalent in female patients with scoliosis than those without scoliosis (4.3% vs. 1.8%) (P<0.001) (Figure 3).

Male scoliotic patients vs. male non-scoliotic patients

Male patients with and without scoliosis were compared. Although statistically insignificant, minor T-score discordance was 1.3 times more prevalent in in those with scoliosis compared to those without (66.7% vs. 51.6%). Major T-score discordance was 1.2 times more prevalent in those with scoliosis compared to those without (15.4% vs. 12.8%) (P=0.08) (Figure 3).

Increasing Cobb angle as a factor affecting T-score discordance

The Cobb angle was measured in patients with scoliosis, and patients were grouped according to the magnitude of spinal curvature recorded. Although the current study demonstrates that the presence of scoliosis increases T-score discrepancy, subgroup analysis did not demonstrate a significant change in T-score discordance with increase in Cobb angle. There was no significant change in T-score discordance in patients with Cobb angle <30° and ≥30° (P=0.15), or in patients with Cobb angle 10° to 19°, 20° to 29°, 30° to 39° and ≥40° (P=0.26) (Figure 4). However, an increase in Cobb angle resulted in a less negative trend for the spine T-score compared to the hip T-score which had a more negative trend. This increased the discrepancy between the spine and hip T-scores.

T-score discordance with Cobb angle as a variable in patients with scoliosis.

On multivariate logistic regression analysis, four independent predictors of T-score discordance were identified. Age greater than 65 years was associated with a 1.3-fold increased likelihood of minor discordance [odds ratio (OR) 1.33, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.17–1.52, P<0.001] and a 1.8-fold increased likelihood of major discordance (OR 1.84, 95% CI: 1.19–2.83, P=0.006). Male was the strongest predictor, with male patients 2.6 times more likely to have minor discordance (OR 2.64, 95% CI: 2.08–3.35, P<0.001) and 14.5 times more likely to have major discordance (OR 14.53, 95% CI: 9.25–22.81, P<0.001) compared to female patients. The presence of scoliosis was independently associated with a 1.3-fold increased likelihood of minor discordance (OR 1.33, 95% CI: 1.08–1.63, P=0.007), though no significant association was found with major discordance (OR 1.10, 95% CI: 0.49–2.45, P=0.82). A BMI of 30 kg/m2 or below was associated with a 1.5-fold increased likelihood of minor discordance (OR 1.46, 95% CI: 1.14–1.87, P=0.002), with no significant association with major discordance (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.45–2.25, P=0.98). Race was not found to be a significant predictor of either minor or major T-score discordance (P=0.45 and P=0.06 respectively) (Table 1).

Discussion

Accurate T-scores are essential for monitoring osteoporosis treatment and predicting the fracture occurrence (10,13). Proper diagnosis is crucial due to the prevalence of osteoporosis and impact on surgical outcomes in patients with adult spinal deformities (6,17,25). This study found significant discordance between spine and hip T-scores, with the spine T-score being less accurate in diagnosis osteoporosis in both scoliosis and non-scoliosis patients.

A study of 3,015 non-scoliotic patients by El Maghraoui et al. demonstrated minor T-score discordance in 1,250 participants (42%), and major T-score discordance in 129 (4%) participants. The study found that patients with a history of osteoporotic fractures, menopause, and obesity were more likely to have major discordance between the spine and hip T-scores (11). Similar, another study involving 3,479 non-scoliotic patients by Mounach et al. found 1,449 patients (42%) with minor T-score discordance and 154 patients (4%) with major T-score discordance (12). Multivariate analysis performed in this study demonstrated that older age, menopause, and obesity correlated significantly with T-score discordance. When compared to earlier studies, the current study demonstrates similar prevalence of T-score discordance in patients without scoliosis (11,12,14,15). Of 6,969 patients involved, 2,471 (39%) were found to have minor T-score discordance, and 176 (3%) were found to have major T-score discordance (Table 2) (11,12,14,15).

Yagi et al. investigated 176 patients diagnosed with scoliosis. 39% of these patients were found to have minor T-score discordance, and 4% were found to have major T-score discordance (14). These findings are similar to those of the current study. The current study found that 281 (44.1%) and 32 (5.0%) of patients with scoliosis had minor and major T-score discordance respectively. Additionally, the current study found a greater prevalence of overall T-score discordance (minor and major) in patients with scoliosis, with 7.3% more patients compared to those without scoliosis. In this study, multivariate analysis demonstrated that patients with scoliosis were 1.3 times more likely to have minor T-score discordance compared to those without scoliosis. A similar trend was also observed for major T-score discordance. These findings suggest that scoliosis further exacerbates the discordance between spine and hip T-scores, rendering the spine T-score ineffective as a diagnostic tool for osteoporosis in patients with scoliosis.

The mechanism underlying the apparent elevation of lumbar spine T-scores in patients with scoliosis deserves further consideration. In a scoliotic spine, lateral curvature creates an asymmetric loading environment, with increased compressive forces on the concave side of the curve and tensile forces on the convex side (6). Over time, this imbalance contributes to accelerated degeneration of the facet joints on the concave side and promotes the formation of osteophytes and syndesmophytes through chronic mechanical stress at the periosteum and annulus fibrosus (16). Persistent compressive loading may also stimulate osteoblastic activity via mechanotransduction pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin signalling, resulting in localised subchondral sclerosis and cortical thickening of the facet joints and vertebral endplates (26). These structural changes have important implications for BMD assessment. Standard lumbar DEXA relies on a two-dimensional anteroposterior projection and cannot distinguish the mineral content of the vertebral body from that of adjacent structures within the imaging field. Consequently, dense degenerative changes such as sclerotic facet joints, thickened pedicles, and lateral osteophytes may be incorporated into the measured signal, leading to an overestimation of vertebral BMD and an artificially elevated lumbar spine T-score (17).

The severity of scoliosis showed no statistically significant relationship with T-score discordance in this study. However, a less negative trend in spine T-scores and a more negative trend in hip T-scores were observed with increase in Cobb angle. This suggest that spine T-scores may be less reliable in diagnosing osteoporosis in patients with more severe scoliosis. Pappou et al. also found a correlation between higher Cobb angles and greater T-score discordance in 454 scoliosis patients (6). Dense sclerotic changes in the scoliotic spine may lead to falsely elevated spine T-scores, making them less reliable for osteoporosis diagnosis in these patients (6,11,12,14,16).

This study found that the age of a patient was a risk factor impacting the accuracy of the spine T-score. As the age of the patient increased, there was a corresponding increase in spine and hip T-score discordance. These findings are consistent with a study by Mounach et al. which found that older age correlated significantly with T-score discordance in a multivariate analysis of patients without scoliosis (12). Similarly, Moayyeri et al. reported that patients above 65 years old were 1.2 times as likely to have minor T-score discordance (15). In the current study, multivariate analysis found that patients above 65 years old were 1.3 times as likely to have minor T-score discordance, and 1.8 times as likely to have major T-score discordance compared to those aged 65 years and younger. The authors postulate that older individuals are more likely to have sclerotic degenerative changes affecting the spine, regardless of the presence of scoliosis. These changes falsely elevate the spine T-score, resulting in higher T-score discordance in patients with and without scoliosis.

The current study also found that male patients had a greater prevalence of minor and major T-score discordance when compared to females in both the scoliosis and non-scoliosis groups. The authors hypothesise that the male subjects may have been involved in more physically rigorous work or daily activities, contributing to more degenerative changes in the spine and greater dense bone formation. These changes lead to a falsely elevated spine T-score and increased T-score discordance when compared to female patients.

Further analysis of female patients also found that females with scoliosis had higher prevalence of T-score discordance compared to those without scoliosis. Female patients with scoliosis had 1.1 times more minor T-score discordance and 2.4 times more major T-score discordance when compared to those without scoliosis. These findings again reflect the effect of scoliosis on the elevation of the spine T-score, emphasising the inability to reliably utilise the spine T-score in the diagnosis of osteoporosis for patients with scoliosis. Interestingly, this observation was not seen in the male patients of this study. This may be due to the significantly smaller proportion of males involved in the study; 5,786 of 6,332 (91%) patients without scoliosis, and 598 of 637 (94%) patients with scoliosis were females. The smaller sample size of male patients likely limited the power of comparison between males with and without scoliosis.

Of note in this study, patients with BMI ≤30 kg/m2 were found to be 1.5 times as likely to have spine and hip T-score discordance compared to patients with BMI >30 kg/m2. These findings contrast with existing studies, which report a correlation between obesity and an increase in T-score discordance (11,12,15). A possible explanation for the results of these earlier studies may be related to low oestrogen levels in obese patients. Oestrogen levels have been shown to affect both fat accumulation and bone density levels. The interaction of oestrogen with leptin, and the melanocortin system affects the regulation of adiposity. Decreased oestrogen levels in post-menopausal women affect these interactions and is associated with increased intra-abdominal fat accumulation (27). Xu et al. also described the process through which decreased interaction between oestrogen receptors and hypothalamic neurons pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and steroidogenic factor-1 (SF-1) cause increased food intake and visceral adiposity, and a decrease in energy expenditure (28). Low oestrogen levels have been shown to increase bone loss due to oestrogen withdrawal superimposed against age-related linear bone loss (29,30). Decreased serum oestrogen levels decrease the lifespan of osteoblasts and increase the lifespan of osteoclasts (30). This affects bone remodelling and results in a reduced BMD. A reduced BMD, coupled with differential in bone loss velocity between cortical and cancellous bone in the hip and spine, may result in greater T-score discordance (11).

Limitations

The authors identified several limitations of this study. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the study did not allow for a longitudinal observation of the relationship between scoliosis and the spine and hip T-scores. A further study doing so would be ideal. Also, this study did not distinguish between the different types of scoliosis, including degenerative and idiopathic scoliosis. This may provide additional information regarding the diagnosis and management in patients with differing types of scoliosis. Lastly, more information regarding age and sex related changes in spine and hip structure, and possible cause of secondary osteoporosis could have been recorded to provide further insight on this topic. Importantly, individuals with conditions known to substantially affect BMD, such as malignancy, chemotherapy or radiotherapy exposure, or congenital disorders, were excluded. As such, the inclusion of this subgroup is unlikely to have materially influenced the study findings.

Conclusions

This study analysed the prevalence of minor and major T-score discordance in patients with and without scoliosis. In patients without scoliosis, the prevalence of minor and major T-score discordance was 39% and 2.8% respectively. In patients with scoliosis, the prevalence of minor and major T-score discordance increased to 44% and 5% respectively. The total T-score discordance (minor and major) was 7.3% higher in patients with scoliosis when compared to patients without scoliosis.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first and largest population base cross-sectional study to demonstrate that the presence of lumbar scoliosis in adult patients may render the spine T-score less accurate in the diagnosis and monitoring of osteoporosis. Additionally, the current study identified the male, and ages of 65 years and above as risk factors that further reduce the accuracy of utilising the spine T-score in the diagnosis of osteoporosis when compared to hip T-scores. Our findings do not suggest that the hip is intrinsically more accurate, but rather that lumbar scoliosis may reduce the reliability of lumbar spine DXA measurements, particularly in older adults and males. Increasing scoliosis severity (Cobb angle) was associated with progressively greater discordance.

These findings are important and relevant to healthcare planning and the management of osteoporosis given the significant prevalence of osteoporosis and degenerative lumbar scoliosis in an ageing population. The findings of the study may suggest the omission of routine spine T-score as a tool for the diagnosis or monitoring of osteoporosis. This may reduce the burden of healthcare cost and minimise the risk of radiation on the patient. Finally, the findings of this study raise the need for new technology to better care for patients affected by both osteoporosis and scoliosis.
