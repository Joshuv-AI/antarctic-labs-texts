Patterns of acute watery diarrhea in Fiji: understanding the implications for water and sanitation services
Authors: Suliasi Mekerusa Batikawai, Nicholas J. Osborne, Phu Do, Toga Vosataki, Vimal Deo, Simon A. Reid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiarrheal disease, including acute watery diarrhea (AWD), remains a persistent global health challenge, particularly in settings with inadequate water and sanitation infrastructure. Understanding the local epidemiological features of AWD is a crucial first step to inform effective public health intervention. This study examines the AWD trends in Fiji and their association with water and sanitation service levels as defined by the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme.MethodsThis ecological study analysed longitudinal syndromic surveillance data on AWD cases reported in Fiji’s Central and Western Divisions from April 2016 to December 2021, alongside census derived water and sanitation data. Age-specific trends, seasonal patterns, and district-level variations were assessed using descriptive statistics and Pearson’s correlation analyses. The associations between AWD incidence, water and sanitation service levels were evaluated using negative binomial regression, adjusting for district-level population confounders. Additionally, population attributable risk (PAR) was calculated to estimate the proportion of AWD cases linked to unimproved water and sanitation services.ResultsChildren under 5 years old (U5) had the highest incidence of AWD throughout the study period, with both age groups showing a seasonal trend from February to April annually. AWD rates declined during the period when COVID-19 travel restrictions were in place. A strong correlation existed between U5 and O5 cases across all years (R = 0.59–0.88; 95% CI: 0.45–0.92; p < 0.001). Nearly 18% of AWD cases were attributable to unimproved water (PAR 17.46%) and sanitation (PAR 17.80%). Access to safely managed water service reduced AWD risk (reference category), while unimproved water (RR 3.42; 95% CI: 2.66–4.38) and sanitation (RR 7.56; 95% CI: 4.21–13.58) service levels posed the highest risks.ConclusionAdvancing access to safely managed water and sanitation is essential to mitigate AWD. Investments in water and sanitation infrastructure particularly in underserved and high-risk areas should be prioritised. The findings underscore the importance of context-specific Water and Sanitation interventions in Pacific Island settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24826-2.

Introduction

Diarrhea is one of the top ten causes of mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALY) globally [2]. With approximately 1.7 billion cases of childhood diarrhea worldwide, the condition is associated with substantial medical expenses and places a considerable economic burden on society [2, 3]. In addition, diarrheal disease is the second leading cause of death in children under five years old, responsible for approximately 2,195 child deaths daily, and accounting for one in nine child deaths worldwide [3, 4]. This surpasses the combined mortality rates associated with HIV/AIDS, malaria, and measles [4]. The under-5 age group is uniquely susceptible to AWD due to their developing immune systems and greater exposure risks [4, 5]. This burden is unevenly distributed, with low-income countries bearing a disproportionate share due to limited resources and healthcare infrastructure [2]. Extant research consistently highlights the significant impact of diarrheal diseases on regions with inadequate access to healthcare services and marginalized population [2].

In the Pacific region, diarrheal diseases continue to be a substantial cause of childhood deaths, primarily attributable to unsafe drinking water, poor sanitation, and hygiene practise [6]. While the case management of diarrhea has improved substantially, Pacific Island Countries (PICs) such as Kiribati, Nauru, and Papua New Guinea still report high mortality rates, with an estimated 1,000 people dying from diarrheal diseases each year [6]. PICs collectively exhibit the world’s lowest coverage of safely managed or basic drinking water and sanitation services [7]. As of 2020, only 47% of rural populations in PICs had access to basic drinking water sources. These deficits are compounded by the region’s: (a) geographic dispersion of rural and remote communities, (b) constrained service delivery systems with limited institutional capacity, and (c) fundamentally different water challenges faced by low-lying atolls compared to larger islands [8, 9]. Climate-related disruptions worsen these vulnerabilities, as extreme hydrological events including droughts, flooding and tropical cyclones disrupt water and sanitation infrastructure, thereby increasing human-pathogen contact and disease transmission risk [10, 11].

Fiji, while better resourced than many PICs, faces similar systemic barriers to diarrheal disease control. The vulnerability of existing infrastructure was demonstrated during post-2012 floods in Fiji’s Western Division, which led to widespread water contamination and AWD outbreaks [11]. According to 2017 census, 67% of households in Fiji have access to safely managed drinking water supply, while just 21% benefit from safely managed sanitation service. Although 76% have access to basic sanitation, these coverage levels still leave substantial portions of the population vulnerable to water-related diseases including AWD [12]. Transmission of diarrheal pathogens in Fiji follows well established fecal-oral pathways, as outlined in the F-diagram framework (fluids, fingers, flies, fields, floods, and food) [3, 13]. Recent research has provided specific evidence for these transmission mechanisms: Jenkins et al. (2019) demonstrated how unimproved pit latrines in permeable soils significantly increase typhoid risk [14], while Nelson et al. (2022) identified seasonality as a predictor for watery and bloody diarrhoea [15]. Together, leptospirosis, typhoid, dengue, and diarrhea (LTDD) have been considered the country’s “three plagues” [11]. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2019 indicated that diarrhea primarily linked to inadequate drinking water was responsible for 28 deaths and 1,659 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across all age groups in Fiji [13]. However, no studies have systematically examined the association between AWD incidence and water and sanitation service levels in Fiji and the Pacific region, a significant knowledge gap, particularly as the country strives to meet the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets for water and sanitation.

This study addresses this gap by characterising the incidence of AWD in Fiji and exploring its association with water and sanitation service levels, as classified by the WHO/UNICEF JMP. Utilizing data collected Fiji’s established syndromic surveillance system, the Early Warning and Response System (EWARS), and the national census, the analysis focuses on district-level patterns across the Central and Western Divisions. While causal inferences are beyond the scope of this analysis, the study provides critical insights into the contextual relationships between service access disparities and AWD burden. The findings aim to inform targeted and context-sensitive water, sanitation interventions to mitigate the disease transmission in Fiji and in similar Pacific Island setting.

Methods

Study location

The study was conducted in Fiji’s two most populous geographical divisions: the Central Division and the Western Division (Fig. 1) [1]. Fiji’s climate is classified as tropical and oceanic under the Köppen climate system, encompassing both Tropical rainforest (Af) and Semi-arid (BSh, BSk) zones [16]. The country experiences neither extreme cold nor heat but is regularly affected by tropical cyclones. The Central Division comprises five provinces and 19 districts, including Fiji’s capital, Suva. This division is characterized by high annual rainfall, particularly during the cyclone season [14]. In contrast, the Western Division, consisting of three provinces and 20 districts, is the largest division by land area and experiences lower annual rainfall with a distinct dry season. Nearly 70% of Fiji’s approximately 900,000 population reside on the main island of Viti Levu, which comprises of the Central (378,284) and Western Administrative Divisions (337,041) [1].

Data sources

All AWD notifications from the Central and Western Divisions were obtained from Fiji’s Early Warning Alert and Response System (EWARS) for the period April 2016 to December 2021 (5.7 years). EWARS is a syndromic surveillance system that collects weekly data from 80 sentinel health care facilities using standardized case definitions, with AWD defined as the occurrence of three or more loose or watery stools per day, lasting 14 days or less [17]. Cases were pre-categorized by age group: under 5 years (U5) and 5 years and older (O5), with their geographic locations recorded at the EWARS site level. A total of 23 sites in the Central Division and 24 in the Western Division contributed data to this study. The reliability of EWARS was validated during post cyclone surveillance in 2016, demonstrating 92% reporting completeness and 83% timeliness. Additional technical details on EWARS are available in the Ministry of Health and Medical Services (MoHMS) EWARS standard operating procedure [17]. Data were cleaned, de-duplicated and organised using Microsoft Excel prior to analysis.

Demographic water and sanitation infrastructure data were obtained from Fiji’s 2017 Population and Housing Census conducted by the Fiji Bureau of Statistics (FBoS). To enable district-level analysis, EWARS reporting sites were geographically matched to their corresponding administrative districts (n = 39) across Central and Western Divisions. This integration combined: (1) age-stratified population denominators (U5 and O5 groups), (2) reported AWD case counts, and (3) water and sanitation service levels. Water and sanitation services were classified according to the WHO/UNICEF JMP ladder framework (see Supplementary Table S1) [7]. For each district, the proportion of households with access to each JMP service level category was calculated and included in the analysis examining associations with AWD incidence.

Statistical analysis

Stratified descriptive analysis compared the AWD patterns between the U5 and O5 populations. Temporal patterns, including recurring seasonal peaks, were assessed through visual examination of constructed timelines. Annual Pearson correlation coefficients quantified the relationship between U5 and O5 incidence rates to provide insights into transmission dynamics. To visualise the geographical distribution, AWD cases were plotted and mapped by age group (U5 & O5) on ArcGIS Pro with a GeoJSON file of districts in Fiji. The population attributable risk percentage (PAR%) was calculated to estimate AWD cases preventable by improving water and sanitation services. The non exposed reference group had safely managed/basic services, while the exposed group had limited and unimproved services. PAR% was computed as [(total incidence – non exposed incidence)/total incidence] × 100 [18]. Associations between water and sanitation service levels and AWD incidence were examined using negative binomial regression, selected after overdispersion testing of Poisson models. The district was the unit of analysis, with AWD cases (all ages) as the outcome and the log of district population included as an offset. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v 4.2.2 [2022-10−31]).

Results

A total of 146,090 AWD notifications were recorded from April 2016 to December 2021, equating to an average of 71.76 notifications per day (Table 1). Data from EWARS sites maintained an average monthly completeness of approximately 92.08% and timeliness of about 83.58%. Annual notification counts varied considerably, with the highest number recorded in 2019 (27,499 cases) and the lowest in 2021 (20,494 cases). The annual incidence per 1,000 population ranged from 30.65 in 2016 to 23.16 in 2021. Temporal analysis revealed distinct patterns in AWD incidence across both divisions. A gradual decline in the AWD incidences across the combined Western and Central Division was observed from April 2016 to 2019 (Fig. 2). Volatility is evident in the early phase of the timeline, with a rapid increase in notifications over a six-month period followed by a slower decrease. The Western Division experienced a minor increase from the 1 st quarter of 2017 to the 3rd quarter of 2018, particularly among children (U5), a group that consistently showed higher age-adjusted rates throughout the study period. Several noteworthy shifts in AWD patterns were observed: before and after November 2018, during the COVID-19 pandemic, and with a subsequent rise starting in November 2021. The impact of COVID-19 restrictions on AWD patterns was mixed; the Central Division experienced significant AWD decreases during the second restrictions phase, while in the Western Division, where declines preceded these restrictions. In terms of seasonal peaks, an annual trend emerged with higher rates observed between February and April.

Correlation between under 5 and over 5 cases of acute watery diarrhea

There was a strong positive correlation between the incidence of AWD notifications in U5 and O5 across all years. The annual Pearson correlation coefficients ranged from 0.59 (95% CI: 0.32–0.77) in 2021 to 0.88 (95% CI: 0.78–0.94) in 2016 (all p < 0.001). These findings indicate a consistent and statistically significant association between AWD incidence in the two age groups at the district level (Fig. 3).

District-level variation in acute watery diarrhea

The incidence of AWD revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity across districts within the Central and Western Divisions, as presented in Fig. 4. While most districts displayed AWD incidence rates near or below the average, a distinct cluster of districts emerged as hotspots for AWD incidence. These hotspots namely Rakiraki, Rewa, Nasigatoka, and Bau exhibited significantly higher rates in both U5 and O5 age groups.

Population attributable risk

Analysis of Population Attributable Risk (PAR) revealed significant associations between inadequate water and sanitation service levels and increased AWD incidence across the two divisions during the study period. Households with lower service levels were consistently associated with higher AWD incidence rates. The PAR analysis demonstrated that 17.46% of AWD cases were attributable to unimproved water service levels, indicating the proportion of cases potentially preventable through improved water service. Similarly, 17.80% of AWD cases were associated with unimproved sanitation service levels, suggesting preventable disease burden through improved sanitation infrastructure (Full PAR estimates in Supplementary Table S4).

Model selection

Poisson regression showed overdispersion (likelihood ratio test: χ² = 520.7, p < 0.001); negative binomial regression was therefore used. All predictors had VIF < 2. Non-significant variables (p ≥ 0.05) without theoretical justification were sequentially removed. Model fit statistics are provided Supplementary Table S5.

Water and sanitation service ladders and AWD

Negative binomial regression analysis revealed a distinct risk gradient associated with water and sanitation service level and AWD incidence (Table 2). While the unadjusted model initially suggested water service levels below the ‘Safely Managed’ benchmark were associated with lower risk ratios, post-adjustment revealed the opposite pattern, with all lower service categories showing increased risk ratios compared to safely managed services: ‘Basic’ at 2.033 (95%CI: 1.54–2.66), ‘Limited’ at 2.159 (95% CI: 1.61–2.89), ‘Unimproved’ at 3.421 (95% CI: 2.66–4.38), and ‘Surface Water’ at 2.585 (95% CI: 1.94–3.45), all statistically significant (p < 0.001). For sanitation services, adjusted risk ratios demonstrated an even more pronounced risk gradient, with progressively higher risks associated with lower service levels compared to ‘Safely Managed’: ‘Basic’ at 3.318 (95% CI: 2.10–5.24), ‘Limited’ at 4.437 (95% CI: 2.46–8.02), and ‘Unimproved’ at 7.557 (95% CI: 4.21–13.58), each with p < 0.001. Discussion

The initial increase in AWD notifications from 2016 to 2018 corroborates the growing burden of AWD across both age groups (U5 and O5). The annual pattern showing surges in AWD cases in February to April is consistent with prior findings from EWARS surveillance [19]. Subsequently, the notable reduction in AWD notifications from 2019 to 2021 likely reflects the combined impact of enhanced public health interventions and improvements in water and sanitation infrastructure [20–22]. The influence of COVID 19 pandemic measures on AWD patterns was particularly evident during specific periods with distinct downward trend in 2020 (May-November) and 2021 (March-November) coinciding with travel restrictions. This pattern mirrors global observations of reduced healthcare utilization and emergency service demand during the pandemic [23]. However, the subsequent sharp increase in U5 AWD cases during the post restriction phase may be attributed primarily to resumed population mobility and gatherings [24], increased healthcare accessibility and potentially relaxed hygiene practices post lockdown. Beyond these pandemic-related effects, the overall temporal variations observed are compounded by Fiji’s dispersed island communities and climate-sensitive water and sanitation infrastructure [25]. Recurrent service disruptions driven by tropical cyclones, flooding, and sea level rise, all exacerbated by climate change, increase exposure pathways and influence health-seeking patterns [19, 26].

The strong correlation between AWD incidences in the U5 and O5 age groups, suggest linked epidemiological patterns. This association may stem from two primary factors. First, shared risk factors are prevalent across both age groups, including socio-economic conditions (e.g., financial status, household size and education level), environmental conditions (e.g. drinking water sources and sanitation facilities), and behavioural practices (e.g., bottle feeding and handwashing with soap) [27]. Second, household or community transmission dynamics operate independently of age, with common living spaces and close interpersonal contacts within families and communities facilitating pathogen transmission across different age groups [28, 29].

Our findings that AWD is more prevalent among younger children in Fiji align with epidemiological patterns showing diarrheal diseases disproportionately affect this age group, particularly within low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3, 30]. The uneven distribution of AWD cases across districts and age groups further highlights the increased vulnerability of young children to AWD [31, 32]. However, these patterns may be influenced by differential reporting behaviours. The under-five population is considered particularly vulnerable, potentially leading to more cautious health behaviours and consequently higher reporting rates for AWD in younger populations [33]. Conversely, perceived lower risk of AWD among populations aged five years and above may result in different health seeking and reporting patterns. Such differential reporting patterns have been aligned with previous research emphasizing the influence of risk perceptions on health behaviour and health outcome reporting [34]. Understanding and addressing this differential risk reporting is crucial for ensuring the accurate and thorough AWD surveillance across all age groups.

The Population Attributable Risk (PAR) estimates of 17.5% and 17.8% for unimproved water and sanitation services, respectively, indicate that a measurable fraction of AWD cases in Fiji can be directly attributed to inadequate infrastructure. These findings align with the recent WHO evidence establishing the causal relationship between unimproved water, sanitation, and diarrheal disease burden [13, 35]. While the remaining 82% of AWD cases reflects the multifactorial etiology of diarrheal diseases [36], behavioural factors [37], environmental exposures (i.e., climate factors and extreme weather events) [38], food safety [39], and socio-cultural practices that influence transmission dynamics [14]. The proportion attributable to infrastructure deficits provides clear evidence for policy prioritization. In resource-constrained Pacific settings, preventing even 20% of AWD cases through water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure improvements translates into significant health gains, reduced healthcare costs, and resilience benefits for communities highly exposed to climate extremes [10].

Disparities in AWD incidence across different water service levels reflects a complex interplay of demographic, environmental, and behavioural factors along with possible unaccounted confounding factors within the two divisions [27, 40]. Access to safely managed drinking water provided significant protection against AWD, congruent with recent evidence [21, 41–43]. The elevated AWD risk associated with ‘Limited’, ‘Unimproved’, and ‘Surface Water’ categories corroborates findings from Ethiopia and India documenting risks posed by unsafe water sources for diarrhea [44, 45]. Despite being an improvement, the ‘Basic’ service level still presents a substantial AWD risk, largely due to intermittent water supply issues commonly found in Fiji and other LMICs [43, 46]. Such intermittency compromises water quality and quantity, increasing diarrhea risks compared to continuous supplies, as demonstrated in Ethiopian and Egyptian studies [47, 48]. The evidence further amplifies the protective benefits of safely managed water service for AWD prevention.

Among sanitation categories, households with ‘Unimproved’ sanitation services, particularly those using open-pit latrines without slabs showed the highest AWD risk. This is consistent with findings from Cameroon, Congo, and Ethiopia, which link higher diarrhea rates to uncovered pit latrines [44, 49, 50]. Uncovered latrines attract flies, thereby increasing pathogen transmission and risk of diarrhea [21, 50]. Our findings align with a previous research of typhoid in Fiji, where the presence of unimproved pit latrines and damaged septic systems in permeable and highly erodible soil was a significant risk factor [51]. The association between higher AWD incidence and latrines classified as unimproved, limited, and basic service levels where sewage is not properly stored and treated corroborates research demonstrating how untreated wastewater from basic sanitation facilities contaminates the environment [43, 52–54]. This contamination risk is exacerbated during heavy rainfall and floods [51].

The study findings reveal a clear hierarchical structure in the relative risks across different water and sanitation service levels. Decision makers must weigh the feasibility of advancing safely managed service levels against the public health risk associated with any deviation, particularly given Fiji’s small island geography, diverse settings (i.e., urban, semi urban, informal) and climate pressures from intense cyclones, flooding, and coastal erosion that pose challenges to sustaining resilient WASH infrastructure [55]. While safely managed services remain the priority, practical pathways for incremental improvement are needed, including targeted investments in high risk or low coverage districts. Figure 4 and the district-level maps (see Supplementary 2 & 3) highlight geographic hotspots of AWD incidence, providing a decision-support tool for prioritizing resources and planning WASH interventions. Additionally, such maps also have potential utility for JMP reporting by illustrating sub national disparities that support equity focused programming [56]. Future research will benefit from incorporating individual level exposure assessment, environmental and sociocultural determinants, and spatial stratifications (e.g., urban/rural, densely/sparsely populated areas) to better understand the AWD’s multifactorial etiology.

Strength and limitation

This study benefits from Fiji’s robust nationwide AWD surveillance system, which provides systematic weekly syndromic data, combined with detailed water and sanitation service data from the 2017 national census that included visual inspection of dwellings. This integration enables systematic temporal and spatial analyses of AWD patterns across administrative levels while examining interactions with WASH services. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the AWD data lack granular age stratification beyond broad U5 and O5 categories, which may obscure age specific patterns. Second, surveillance data may be affected by reporting biases due to healthcare access, health seeking behaviour, and diagnostic capacity across districts. Third, while district-level aggregation helps mitigate some temporal variability, the ecological study design means associations reflect population level trends rather than individual risk factors. A key limitation is the reliance on 2017 Census data for covariates throughout the 2017–2021 period, which may not capture sub annual changes. However, our sensitivity analyses (including covariate perturbation, trimming of extremes, and mixed effects modelling) demonstrated that results were stable under plausible measurement error or temporal drift. Although updated annual data would be ideal, the 2017 Census represents the most complete and nationally representative source of population and WASH infrastructure characteristics for this period.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant associations between AWD incidence and Water and Sanitation service levels in Fiji, emphasizing the protective effect of safely managed water and sanitation. The findings underscore the need for targeted infrastructure improvements in high-risk districts and rural areas, particularly address intermittent water supplies and unimproved sanitation. While confirming the established water, sanitation – and diarrhea relationship, the analysis reveals context specific risks in Fiji’s most vulnerable regions underscoring the urgency of expanding safely managed services as essential for AWD reduction. The district-level analytical framework, integrating health surveillance data with JMP classifications, offers a replicable model for other Pacific Island settings. To extend these insights regionally, enhanced data-sharing systems and sustained investment in routine surveillance will be essential. Ultimately, continued investment in safely managed WASH services, alongside further research remains critical to closing remaining knowledge and service gaps.
