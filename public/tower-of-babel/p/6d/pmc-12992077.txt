Individual and combined ultrasonic–enzymatic treatments for modifying soybean residue (okara) fiber: impacts on structure, physicochemical, and functional properties
Authors: Jie Cui, Dandan Liu, Li Guo, Haiteng Tao, Wei Gao, Bo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined ultrasonic–enzymatic treatments on the structural, physicochemical, and functional properties of soybean residue (okara) dietary fiber (DF). Under the optimal combined ultrasonic-enzymatic treatment conditions, the soluble DF (SDF) yield and water-holding capacity (WHC) of the modified okara DF increased to 8.00 ± 1.04 g/100 g and 14.88 ± 0.29 g/g respectively, representing enhancements of 193.04% and 40.11% compared with unmodified okara DF. Structural analysis showed reduced particle size, a more uniform size distribution, and disrupted microstructures characterized by wrinkles, voids, and fragmentation. Physicochemical properties were also improved (p < 0.05), with WHC, oil-holding capacity (OHC), and swelling capacity (SC) increasing by 40.11%, 82.81%, and 40.19%, respectively. Moreover, the combined treatment exhibited stronger functional effects than individual methods, yielding 4.82- and 10.07-fold increases in cholesterol and bile salt adsorption capacities, and 1.35- and 7.78- fold enhancements in DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging activities. These enhancements are attributed to structural modifications, including reduced particle size, increased specific surface area, exposure of functional groups, and decreased crystallinity, which collectively improve the functional properties of okara DF. Overall, this study highlights the synergistic advantages of ultrasonic–enzymatic treatment and provides theoretical and technical support for the high-value utilization of okara DF.Keywords: Okara dietary fiber, Ultrasonic–enzymatic modification, Structural characteristics, Physicochemical properties, Functional properties

Introduction

China is one of the world’s largest producers of soybean-derived products. Soybean residue, commonly known as okara, is the primary by-product generated during soybean protein extraction from soybean meal. Owing to its high moisture content and susceptibility to microbial spoilage, large quantities of okara are often discarded or used as low-value feed, leading to resource underutilization and environmental burden [1]. However, from a nutritional standpoint, okara is rich in dietary fiber (DF), high-quality proteins, oligosaccharides, and bioactive components, among which DF accounts for more than 60% of the dry matter [2]. Therefore, enhancing the value-added utilization of okara is essential for promoting the sustainable and comprehensive use of by-products in soybean deep processing.

DF is widely recognized for its crucial health benefits, including promoting intestinal peristalsis, regulating blood lipids and glucose, and supporting beneficial gut microbiota [3]. However, native okara DF generally exhibits poor hydration capacity, limited solubility, compact microstructure, and insufficient functional performance due to the presence of highly cross-linked cell wall polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [4]. These intrinsic structural constraints restrict its further utilization in high-value food systems. Therefore, developing effective modification technologies to tailor the structure and functionality of okara DF is of great research and industrial interest.

Various modification techniques have been explored to improve the functionality of DF. Enzymatic treatment, particularly using carbohydrates such as cellulase, xylanase, and pectinase, has been widely adopted [5]. These enzymes selectively hydrolyze glycosidic bonds, breaking down the rigid cell wall structure, potentially increasing the proportion of soluble dietary fiber (SDF), and exposing more hydrophilic groups, thereby improving properties like water retention and oil adsorption [5]. For instance, Liu et al. [6] modified wheat bran DF using snail enzyme and found that the enzymatic degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose markedly enhanced its oil-holding capacity, glucose adsorption capacity, cholesterol adsorption capacity, and antioxidant activity. However, the efficiency of enzymatic hydrolysis alone may be limited by mass-transfer resistance and restricted substrate accessibility. Ultrasonic treatment has been widely recognized as an efficient physical tool capable of altering the structural organization of DF [7]. Ultrasound generates strong cavitation, shear forces, and micro-turbulence, which can disrupt the dense cell wall network, reduce particle size, increase surface area, and promote hydration [8]. Li et al. [9] demonstrated that ultrasound treatment altered the monosaccharide composition of corn bran DF and improved its water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), nitrite adsorption capacity, and antioxidant activity. Recently, combined ultrasonic–enzymatic modification has emerged as a green and efficient approach. The synergistic effects between ultrasound and enzymes enhance substrate accessibility, increase enzyme–substrate interaction efficiency, and thus improve both the rate and extent of modification. However, although both ultrasonic and enzymatic treatments have been applied to DF modification, systematic investigations on the synergistic modification of okara DF remain limited. In particular, comprehensive insights into its structural alterations and associated functional improvements are still lacking.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to comprehensively investigate the individual and combined effects of ultrasonic and enzymatic treatments on the structure, physicochemical, and functional properties of okara DF. The modification efficiency of different treatments was evaluated based on the SDF content and WHC. Structural alterations induced by each treatment were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and thermal analysis. Furthermore, key physicochemical properties, including WHC, OHC, and swelling capacity (SC), as well as functional properties such as antioxidant activity, glucose adsorption capacity, and cholesterol adsorption capacity, were evaluated. Moreover, the relationships between these structural changes and key physicochemical and functional properties were systematically analyzed and the underlying mechanism of the synergistic effects between ultrasound and enzymatic treatment was further explored. Overall, this study provides theoretical insights and technical support for the value-added utilization and high-efficiency modification of okara DF.

Materials and methods

Materials

Okara was obtained from Shandong Yuxin Bio-tech Co., LTD. (Shandong, China). α-Amylase (20,000 U/mL), alkaline proteinase (200 U/mg), amyloglucosidase from aspergillus nagger (100,000 U/mL), and cellulase (50 U/mg,) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Modification of okara DF

The extraction procedure was adopted and optimized based on the method reported by Lyu et al. [10]. Briefly, 10 g of dried okara DF was suspended in deionized water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30 (w/v) and magnetically stirred for 30 min. Thermostable α-amylase (250 μL) was added for hydrolysis at 95 °C for 35 min, Alcalase (1.5 mg) and glucoamylase (20 μL, pH adjusted to 4.6, 60 °C) were then added sequentially for 30 min of hydrolysis each. Enzymes were inactivated at 100 °C after each hydrolysis step. he treated mixture was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 20 min. The precipitate was washed with deionized water five times and freeze-dried to obtain insoluble dietary fiber (IDF). The supernatant was mixed with four volumes of anhydrous ethanol for 12 h of precipitation, then subjected to suction filtration and drying to SDF.

Ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined ultrasound-enzymatic modifications

Ultrasonic modification of okara IDF

The sieved okara IDF was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water and magnetically stirred for 30 min to prepare an DF suspension. Ultrasonic treatment was performed following the method of Zhao et al. [11] with minor modifications. The suspension was processed using an ultrasonic processor (SCIENTZ-IID, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Ningbo, China) equipped with a 6 mm probe sonicator. During treatment, the probe was immersed approximately 3 cm into the center of the suspension and operated at room temperature. The effects of ultrasonic power (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 W), this corresponds to a power density of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 W/mL, treatment duration (10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min), and duty cycle (work-to-rest intervals of 1 s:2s, 2 s:3s, 3 s:4s, 4 s:5s, and 5 s:6 s) on the water-holding capacity and SDF yield of okara DF were systematically investigated.

Enzymatic modification of okara IDF

Based on the aforementioned suspension preparation, cellulase was subsequently added. Cellulase was then added at concentrations of 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4%, and 0.5% (w/w). Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed under systematically varying conditions, including hydrolysis duration (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 h), pH values (4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, and 6.0), and temperature (45, 50, 55, 60, and 65 °C). The individual and combined effects of cellulase concentration, hydrolysis time, pH, and temperature on the WHC and SDF yield of okara DF were comprehensively evaluated.

Ultrasound-enzyme synergy for modifying okara IDF

For ultrasonic–enzymatic combined treatment, okara IDF (optimized ultrasonic pretreatment) was dispersed in deionized water as described above. Cellulase (0.2%, w/w) was added, and enzymatic hydrolysis was performed at 60 °C for 1 h with constant stirring in a water bath. Post-hydrolysis, the mixture was centrifuged to discard the supernatant. The collected precipitate was freeze-dried for 48 h and stored in a desiccator for subsequent analysis.

Basic composition analysis of insoluble dietary fiber (IDF)

The contents of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin in IDF were measured using commercial assay kits (BC4280-50 T/48S; BC4440-50 T/48S, BC4200-50 T/48S, Solarbio, Beijing).

Structure characteristics analysis

Particle size

The particle size of okara DF was determined using a laser particle size and shape analyzer (SYNC, Microtrac MRB, USA/Germany). The refractive indices of the sample and distilled water were set at 1.520 and 1.330, respectively, with both the focusing and measurement durations fixed at 30 s.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The FTIR analysis of DF was performed using the KBr pellet method, with minor modifications based on the procedure described by Ying et al. [12]. Specifically, mix the sample with KBr and compress it. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Frontier spectrometer (PerkinElmer Inc., USA), with air serving as the background reference. Spectral acquisition was performed at a resolution of 4 cm−1 across the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with 32 scans accumulated for each measurement.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD profiles were acquired using an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab SE, JEOL Ltd., Japan), following a procedure adapted from Wu et al. [13]. During measurement, the operating parameters were set to 40 kV and 40 mA. Diffraction data were collected over a 2θ range of 5-60°, with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 4°/min.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructures of okara DF before and after modification were photographed a SEM (Regulus 8220, Hitachi, Japan). Powder samples were mounted onto specimen holder, and subsequently sputter-coated with a thin gold layer. SEM observation was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV under high vacuum conditions, with micrographs captured at × 8.00 K magnification (scale bar: 5.0 μm).

Thermal stability

The thermal stability was evaluated using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (AmyA 6000, PerkinElmer, Shelton, USA). Approximately 5 mg of DF sample was weighed into a ceramic crucible and subjected to pyrolysis under a nitrogen atmosphere. The temperature program involved heating from 50 °C to 500 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min.

Results and discussion

Effects of different modification methods on okara DF

The effects of ultrasonic duty cycle, power intensity, and exposure duration on the SDF yield and WHC of okara DF were evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 1(a1), both SDF yield (1.97 ± 0.21 g/100 g) and WHC (11.63 ± 0.25 g/g) were maximized at an operated in pulse mode (3 s on and 4 s off). This intermittent mode effectively equilibrated cavitation-induced fiber dissolution against structural over-degradation from prolonged irradiation, thereby enhancing SDF liberation while maintaining hydration sites [20]. Fig. 1(a2) illustrates that an ultrasonic intensity of 300 W generated the most favorable SDF extraction (6.2 ± 0.53 g/100 g) and WHC (13.14 ± 0.46 g/g). Lower power levels (e.g., 100 W) produced insufficient cavitation for meaningful modification, whereas higher intensities (e.g., 500 W), despite markedly increasing SDF yield, provoked excessive structural fragmentation that impaired water retention [8]. According to Fig. 1(a3), a 20-min treatment duration achieved optimal SDF yield (2.23 ± 0.38 g/100 g) and WHC (9.82 ± 0.23 g/g). Shorter exposures (e.g., 10 min) led to inadequate structural disruption and limited SDF release, while extended durations (e.g., 30 min) caused molecular scission of SDF chains through mechanical shearing, consequently reducing hydration performance. In a corroborating study, Xie et al. [21] reported that ultrasonication combined with fermentation tripled SDF yield and significantly improved WHC, indicating the universal applicability of ultrasound in enhancing SDF extraction and hydration properties within multi-technique strategies. Similarly, Tang et al. [22] observed that ultrasonic treatment decomposed the okara matrix, generating a porous architecture with amplified collapse effects, which contributed to improved water/oil retention capacities and enhanced cholesterol adsorption capability.

The effects of cellulase concentration, hydrolysis duration, temperature, and pH on the SDF yield and WHC of okara DF were systematically evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1(b1), an enzyme concentration of 0.2% (w/w) resulted in optimal SDF yield (3.57 ± 0.81 g/100 g) and WHC (14.31 ± 0.50 g/g). This suggests that moderate enzyme levels sufficiently cleave glycosidic bonds to promote SDF release, whereas excessive concentrations (e.g., 0.5%) may induce structural degradation of DF through enzyme competition or over-hydrolysis, consequently diminishing WHC. According to Fig. 1(b2), hydrolysis at pH 5.0 yielded peak SDF extraction (2.76 ± 0.34 g/100 g) and WHC (11.33 ± 0.24 g/g). Deviations from this optimum (e.g., pH 5.5 or 6.0) reduced enzymatic efficacy, limiting SDF liberation, while extreme pH conditions could compromise hydrophilic groups in the fiber matrix, further impairing water retention. Fig. 1(b3) demonstrates that incubation at 60 °C achieved the most favorable SDF yield (2.00 ± 0.20 g/100 g) and WHC (10.85 ± 0.12 g/g). Lower temperatures (e.g., 45 °C) restricted enzymatic activity, while higher temperatures (e.g., 65 °C) promoted enzyme denaturation, both scenarios resulting in suboptimal performance. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b4), a hydrolysis period of 1.5 h maintained high SDF yield (2.83 ± 0.43 g/100 g) and WHC (11.23 ± 0.09 g/g). Shorter durations (e.g., 0.5 h) provided insufficient fiber hydrolysis, whereas prolonged treatment (e.g., 2.5 h) likely caused excessive breakdown of SDF molecules, reducing their hydration capability. Therefore, based on the above results, the optimal ultrasonic treatment conditions (designated as U) were a pulsed mode of 3 s on and 4 s off, ultrasonic power (300 W), and ultrasonic duration (20 min); the optimal enzymatic hydrolysis conditions (designated as M) were an enzyme concentration of 0.2%, pH 5.0, hydrolysis temperature of 60 °C, and hydrolysis time of 1.5 h. Under these conditions, the combined treatment of okara DF (designated as UM) resulted in SDF yield of 8.00 ± 1.04 g/100 g and a 40.11% increase in WHC. In the related study, Yan et al. [23] reported that cellulase-treated forsythia DF exhibited a more porous microstructure, reduced crystallinity, and enhanced functional properties including oil-binding capacity and cholesterol adsorption. Similarly, Hu et al. [24] demonstrated that shear homogenization-assisted cellulase extraction significantly improved SDF yield from pineapple pomace, confirming the efficacy of enzymatic treatment in enhancing soluble fiber extraction.

Okara DF composition analysis

As shown in Table 1, the IDF content decreased significantly (p ＜ 0.05) after ultrasonic treatment, enzymatic modification, and their combined application. This reduction is primarily attributed to the cavitation and thermal effects generated by ultrasound, which promote the conversion of IDF to SDF by degrading cellulosic and hemicellulose components into water-soluble saccharides [25]. Furthermore, the compositional analysis revealed that the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment resulted in substantial reductions of cellulose (26.16%), hemicellulose (19.00%), and lignin (48.23%) contents. It can thus be inferred that after ultrasonic‑coupled enzymatic treatment, IDF is degraded, generating partially soluble cellulose oligosaccharides. Changes in these components may lead to alterations in the microstructure and physicochemical properties of the sample. potentially affecting parameters such as water/oil holding capacities and adsorption capabilities [26].

Changes in structural characterization

Particle size analysis

Fig. 2(a) and Table 2 display the particle size distribution and mean particle diameter of okara DF subjected to different modification strategies. The control sample exhibited a relatively broad particle size distribution with a less distinct dominant fraction. In contrast, ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments resulted in sharper, more centralized peaks, indicating a narrower size distribution and improved particle uniformity. The narrowing of particle size distribution enhanced surface area and pore accessibility, thereby facilitating water penetration and adsorption interactions. Ultrasonic modification reduced particle size, likely due to cavitation-induced deformation and disruption of the cellular structure of the DF, leading to fragmentation [27]. Enzymatic treatment also decreased particle size and narrowed the distribution range, which may be attributed to the breakdown of DF into smaller molecular aggregates via enzymatic action [28]. The ultrasonic‑enzymatic treatment yielded the most pronounced effect, generating the highest and sharpest peak with the smallest D50 value. This indicates that the combined treatment significantly reduces the particle size of okara DF, which is likely to increase its specific surface area and thereby improve its functional properties, such as WHC, OHC, and adsorption capability. Similarly, Sang et al. [29] confirmed that the combined action of physical forces and enzymatic hydrolysis can effectively disrupt microscopic molecular structures and degrade cellulose, ultimately reducing particle size.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy serves as the sensitive technique for analyzing the structural characteristics of DF, revealing alterations in molecular groups and chemical bonds within modified okara DF. As demonstrated in Fig. 2(b), a broad absorption band observed around 3420 cm−1 was primarily attributed to O–H stretching vibrations in cellulose and hemicellulose of okara DF [13]. Under ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments, the absorption peaks of the samples shifted toward lower wavenumbers, with a gradual enhancement in peak intensity, indicating increased hydroxyl group exposure and strengthened inter- and intra-molecular interactions among polysaccharide chains. The narrowing of this peak following ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments suggests modifications in the associative structure of the fiber matrix [30]. The absorption at 2920 cm−1 arises from C–H stretching vibrations in methyl or methylene groups, representing typical structural features of polysaccharide cellulose [31]. The enhanced peak intensity indicates greater exposure of hydrophobic groups. The ratio of the peak areas at 3420 cm−1 to 2920 cm−1 was used as an indicator of hydrogen bonding intensity. Compared with the untreated sample, the ultrasonic–enzymatic treated DF exhibited a reduced hydrogen bonding index, suggesting the ultrasonic–enzymatic treatment reduced intermolecular hydrogen bond density, leading to a looser cellulose network and enhanced exposure of adsorption sites.

Peaks at 1745 cm−1 and 1631 cm−1 correspond to C=O stretching in esterified carboxyl groups of lignin and C=C stretching in aromatic rings, respectively. Additionally, the absorption in the 1055 cm−1 region is recognized as the carbohydrate fingerprint zone. FT-IR analysis confirmed that okara DF exhibits characteristic polysaccharide absorption peaks and functionally similar groups. The observed enhancement in peak intensity and increased peak area following various treatments may be attributed to two primary factors. First, the applied modification techniques facilitate structural reorganization of cellulose, including a reduction in crystallinity and the conversion of DF from an insoluble to a soluble state. Second, the inherent mechanical vibration, localized high temperature, and intense shear friction generated by ultrasound impart energy to the fiber molecules, thereby inducing alterations in the intensity of characteristic peaks within the spectrum. These changes subsequently modified the molecular weight and hydrophilicity of the DF [32]. While no new functional groups emerged after treatment, noticeable changes in absorption intensity and evidence of chemical bond cleavage indicated significant structural rearrangement within the DF matrix.

X-ray analysis (XRD)

XRD characterization disclosed the crystalline architecture of okara DF. All specimens exhibited a prominent reflection at approximately 21.5° and 16°, and a minor signal near 35°(Fig. 2c), signature of cellulose in its native type-I double-helix conformation—an arrangement in which crystalline lamellae interleave with amorphous domains [33]. Results revealed that after combined ultrasonic-enzymatic treatment, the diffraction peak intensity decreased and crystallinity was reduced (Table 3). This indicates that the modification process promoted the exposure of cellulose macromolecules from crystalline regions, increased components such as amorphous cellulose in the non-crystalline regions, and led to disruption of the original cellulose structure along with changes in crystallinity. Consequently, a more porous and disordered microstructure was formed (Fig. 3), which contributed to enhanced functional properties (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with previous studies in the field. The reduction in crystallinity contributed to the enhanced cholesterol adsorption capacity (Fig. 5f). Neither sonication, enzymatic hydrolysis, nor their sequential application altered peak position or breadth, indicating that the treatments left the cellulose allomorph unmodified and preserved the long-range order of crystallites [34]. Analogous observations were reported by Wu et al [13]., who documented unchanged diffraction angles despite pronounced particle-size reduction after steam-jet micronization of okara DF.

SEM analysis

SEM effectively revealed microstructural changes in okara DF following ultrasonic and enzymatic modifications. As shown in Fig. 3, the unmodified DF displayed large, compact aggregates with a relatively smooth and regular surface texture. In contrast, all modified samples exhibited disrupted surface integrity. Specifically, ultrasonically treated fiber (Fig. 3b) showed increased surface roughness and wrinkling. Enzymatic treatment (Fig. 3c) degraded the fiber by cleaving glycosidic bonds and disrupting intermolecular forces, resulting in fragmented morphologies and reduced polymerization degree [35]. The combined ultrasound-enzymatic approach (Fig. 3d) integrated the characteristics of both individual treatments, promoting partial conversion of IDF into lower molecular weight SDF. The degradation and dissolution of DF components, particularly within cellulose and hemicellulose, resulted in the breakdown of associated bonds. Consequently, the modified sample exhibited structural disruption and an increased specific surface area, which collectively enhanced its interaction with other substances. These structural alterations further influenced its functional properties and potential applications in food systems. This synergistic effect reduced particle size and increased surface area, likely because ultrasonication disrupted molecular architecture and exposed additional enzyme-accessible sites, thereby enhancing enzymatic efficiency [36].

Changes in physical and chemical properties

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

TGA is a well-established technique that is extensively utilized in the assessment of the thermal stability and behavior of materials. This technique was applied to assess the thermal properties and processing-induced effects on okara DF [37]. Fig. 4(a1) and (a2) depict the thermal stability profiles of the samples, indicating a general decrease in thermal resistance following various modification treatments. As shown in Fig. 4(a1), two main weight-loss stages were observed: the first stage around 125 °C, corresponding to moisture evaporation, and the second between 200 and 400 °C, representing rapid degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose. The untreated sample (CK) exhibited the highest initial decomposition temperature and maximal residual mass, indicating superior thermal stability. This likely stems from its tightly packed fibrous structure (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) and strong intermolecular interactions, requiring higher energy for decomposition. In contrast, ultrasonically and enzymatically treated fibers displayed steeper weight-loss curves and reduced residual mass compared to CK, suggesting structural disruption—such as glycosidic bond cleavage and crystalline domain degradation—leading to diminished thermal stability. The combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment resulted in the lowest onset temperature and minimal residual mass, reflecting the weakest thermal stability. This can be attributed to the synergistic disruption of ordered structures and significant weakening of intermolecular interactions, facilitating earlier and more complete decomposition. These results align with FTIR findings showing that all treatments disrupted hydrogen bonding between cellulose and hemicellulose and within crystalline regions, thereby decreasing crystallinity and compromising thermal resistance. Similarly, Xie et al. [35] reported that enzymatic depolymerization of crystalline regions in rice bran DF reduced crystallinity and thermal stability.

As illustrated in Fig. 4(a2), the untreated control (CK) exhibits a distinct thermal-event plateau between 350 °C and 400 °C, which corresponds to the dehydration, carbonization and chain scission of cellulose, accompanied by massive liberation and escape of volatiles. The progressive attenuation—or even disappearance—of this signature after assorted modification routes corroborates that cellulose has been pre-degraded into lower-molecular fragments. These observations align with the findings of Chen et al. [38], who reported that cellulose undergoes thermal depolymerization primarily between 300 and 400 °C, with its maximum rate of mass loss occurring near 355 °C.

Water holding capacity (WHC) analysis

A higher WHC enables improved moisture retention in food systems, thereby reducing dehydration-induced shrinkage. Additionally, DF contributes to enhanced satiety and aid in obesity prevention by adsorbing substantial amounts of water and increasing bulk. As shown in Fig. 4(a3) ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments markedly enhanced the WHC of IDF. Among them, the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment produced the highest WHC (14.88 ± 0.29 g/g), corresponding to increases of 40.11%, 23.29%, and 14.73% compared to the control, ultrasonic-only, and enzymatic-only groups, respectively. The improvement in WHC is likely attributable to structural modifications induced during processing. The improvement in WHC is likely attributable to structural modifications induced during processing, along with an increase in soluble components. The cavitation effect induced by ultrasound disrupted the original compact structure of DF, leading to the exposure of hydroxyl groups that were previously concealed within the molecular interior. This observation aligns with the findings obtained from FTIR analysis. These newly exposed hydroxyl groups can form hydrogen bonds with additional water molecules, thereby enhancing the WHC, which is consistent with previous reports [39]. Cellulase treatment decomposes complex macromolecular structures, resulting in a more porous and looser fiber matrix that facilitates increased water absorption and retention [40]. The superior WHC obtained with the combined treatment likely arises from a synergistic mechanism: ultrasonic pretreatment promotes matrix disruption and increases enzyme accessibility, thereby maximizing hydrolytic efficiency and exposing more hydrophilic domains, ultimately resulting in the greatest enhancement in WHC.

Oil holding capacity (OHC) analysis

OHC is an essential functional attribute in high-fat food applications, as a higher OHC enhances product stability and sensory quality while contributing to physiological benefits such as inhibiting cholesterol formation, reducing lipid absorption, and promoting lipid metabolism [41]. As shown in Fig. 4(a4), the OHC subjected to combined modification reached 14.57 ± 0.56 g/g, representing increases of 82.81%, 50.67%, and 54.18% compared to the control (CK), ultrasonic (U), and enzymatic (M) groups, respectively. This substantial improvement can be due to the reduced particle size and increased surface activity and structural looseness of the modified IDF [42]. The sufficient exposure of hydrophobic groups is further evidenced by the increased peak intensity and enhanced peak area of C-H bond absorption in the FTIR spectrum. Structural analyses (SEM) support that ultrasonic cavitation transformed the originally smooth and compact fiber into a looser architecture with higher specific surface area and more pores, improving enzymatic efficiency and facilitating oil droplet adsorption. Overall, a high OHC serves as a fundamental functional property of modified IDF, helping to prevent excessive intestinal fat absorption and supporting weight management, obesity prevention, and blood lipid reduction [43].

Swelling capacity (SC) analysis

Hydration capacity and SC are vital characteristic of DF. Higher SC enables the fiber to occupy more space in the stomach, thereby enhancing satiety. The resulting increase in bulk can further stimulate intestinal peristalsis, promote bowel movements, and aid in the prevention of constipation. As shown in Fig. 4(a5), the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment yielded the highest SC value (8.72 ± 0.48 mL/g), representing a 40.19% increase compared with the untreated okara DF. This improvement can be attributed to structural and surface modifications induced by the combined treatment, which enhanced the hydration and swelling ability. These observations align with the findings of Tang et al. [44], who reported that treatment-induced microstructural changes favorably influence the swelling behavior of DF. Therefore, the synergistic ultrasound-enzymatic modification effectively improves the swelling properties of DF, thereby exerting positive effects on its broader physicochemical and functional characteristics.

Chromaticity analysis

In the food industry, consumer acceptance is strongly influenced by product color [45]. The results of colorimetric analysis are presented in Table 4. The L* value represents lightness, while a* and b* values correspond to chromaticity indices. Compared to the control (CK), samples U (Individual ultrasonic treatment), M (Individual enzymatic treatment), and UM (Ultrasonic-enzymatic combined treatment) exhibited decreased L* (lightness) and a* (redness), alongside an increased b* (yellowness). This darkening trend in modified okara DF aligns with findings reported by Yue et al. [46]. Thus, ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments effectively alter the color of okara DF, resulting in a darker shade more suitable for incorporation into dark-colored food products. The UM sample showed a substantial total color difference (ΔE) relative to CK, characterized by significantly reduced L* and elevated b* (p < 0.05). This pronounced color chang indicates that the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment negatively affects fiber brightness, likely due to degradation of natural pigments and color-related reactions occurring during modification [47].

Functional properties

Glucose adsorption capacity (GAC) analysis

DF can modulate postprandial blood glucose by adsorbing glucose and reducing its digestion and absorption, and its GAC is closely linked to hypoglycemic efficacy [48]. As illustrated in Fig. 5(a), the sample treated with the combined ultrasound-enzymatic method exhibited the highest GAC value of 9.20 ± 0.01 mmol/g, demonstrating that the combined treatment effectively delays glucose diffusion, consistent with previous findings [44]. The GAC of DF primarily depends on its viscosity and the ability of its fibrous network to entrap glucose molecules, which is closely associated with its surface structure and compositional profile. The combined treatment induced structural fragmentation and surface wrinkling in the fiber, increasing the specific surface area and enabling more glucose molecules to be trapped within its porous matrix, thereby enhancing GAC.

Cholesterol adsorption capacity (CAC) analysis

Cholesterol is an essential structural component of the human body; however, excessive levels are associated with arteriosclerosis, gallstones, and cardiovascular diseases. DF can adsorb cholesterol through its surface functional groups, thereby reducing hepatic cholesterol content and plasma levels [49]. The CAC of DF before and after modification was evaluated under simulated intestinal (pH = 7.0) and gastric (pH = 2.0) conditions, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The results indicate that ultrasonically, enzymatically, and combinedly modified DF exhibited significantly stronger in vitro cholesterol adsorption. The CAC at pH 7.0 was markedly higher (p < 0.05) than that at pH 2.0, suggesting that modified DF adsorbs cholesterol more effectively in the intestinal environment [50]. The presence of H + ions in high concentrations is the main cause of this phenomenon. These ions carry a positive charge, which attracts the molecules of cellulose and cholesterol away from each other. This results in a weaker bond between the molecules. These findings are consistent with the report by Tang et al. [22]. At pH 2.0, the CAC of the UM sample (Ultrasonic-enzymatic combined treatment) reached 14.62 ± 0.94 mg/g, which was 5.82 times that of CK (2.51 ± 0.21 mg/g). At pH 7.0, the CAC of UM (10.35 ± 0.23 mg/g) was 8.28 times higher than that of CK (1.25 ± 0.72 mg/g). These results demonstrate that the UM sample significantly enhanced CAC under both simulated gastric and intestinal environments (p < 0.05).

The observed enhancement in cholesterol adsorption capacity can be attributed to the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose, which loosens the structural matrix and exposes additional hydrophilic groups. This observation aligns well with previous descriptions of increased structural porosity (SEM) and elevated OHC. The enhanced adsorption characteristics of DF can be attributed to the loosened surface structure and increased exposure of hydrophobic groups after the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment. These results are consistent with those reported by Si et al. [51], confirming that IDF exhibits stronger CAC at pH 7.0. Overall, the improved cholesterol-binding ability of the modified DF suggests its potential application as a functional ingredient in food processing aimed at lowering cholesterol levels.

Sodium cholate adsorption capacity (SCAC) analysis

The effects of different modification treatments on the SCAC of okara DF are shown in Fig. 5(c). The results demonstrate that both ultrasonic and enzymatic modifications significantly increased the adsorption quantity. The adsorption of sodium cholate by DF is closely related to its physical and chemical properties, with smaller fiber particle sizes generally contributing to higher adsorption capacity [52]. Among all treatments, the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment yielded the most pronounced enhancement (p < 0.05), with the UM sample achieving an SCAC of 18.27 ± 0.23 mg/g, significantly higher than that of CK, U, and M. This enhancement can be attributed to the synergistic effects of the two treatments. Ultrasonication disrupted the dense surface structure of the fiber, thereby improving the efficacy of the subsequent enzymatic treatment. The enzymatic action degraded macromolecules in the cell wall, loosening the fiber architecture and allowing smaller molecules to penetrate more easily. This process exposed previously encapsulated functional groups, thereby increasing the number of available binding sites for sodium cholate [42]. These interpretations are consistent with the structural observations from SEM. The results indicate that the ultrasound-enzymatically modified okara DF effectively adsorbs sodium cholate, which may help reduce bile acid accumulation in the liver and intestines and contribute to blood lipid-lowering functions.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity analysis

Fig. 5(d) illustrates the impact of ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined treatments on the DPPH radical-scavenging capacity of okara DF. All three modification approaches significantly enhanced antioxidant activity compared with the untreated control (p < 0.05). Among them, the combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatment achieved the highest inhibition value (37.89 ± 0.03%), representing a 2.35-fold increase relative to CK. This enhancement in antioxidant activity may be attributed to the cavitation effects and localized heating generated during ultrasound, which disrupt the compact fiber structure and expose previously inaccessible functional groups [53]. This structural alteration increases the susceptibility of IDF to subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis. As a result, enzymatic treatment facilitates the release of xylose and other short-chain polysaccharide fragments bearing hydroxyl groups capable of donating electrons or hydrogen atoms to quench DPPH radicals [54], thereby effectively terminating the free-radical chain reaction.

Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity analysis

Fig. 5(e) illustrates the impact of various modifications on the hydroxyl radical-scavenging capacity of okara DF. The results demonstrate that ultrasonic, enzymatic, and combined ultrasound-enzymatic treatments significantly enhanced the scavenging capacity compared with the control (p < 0.05). Among them, the combined treatment yielded the highest scavenging rate of 14.14 ± 0.12%, which was 8.78-, 6.20-, and 6.96-fold higher than those of the CK, U, and M groups, respectively. This notable improvement may be attributed to the increased uronic acid content in the modified fiber. Uronic acids, bearing carboxyl groups, are known to participate in electron or hydrogen donation reactions, thereby enhancing hydroxyl radical neutralization efficiency [50].

In vitro digestibility analysis

The low apparent digestibility observed for IDF is consistent with its insoluble nature and structural resistance to gastrointestinal enzymes (Fig. 4b). Following simulated digestion, IDF retained 88.84% and 81.81% of its original WHC and OHC, respectively. No significant loss of functional performance was observed after digestion. The high retention of hydration and oil-binding capacities after digestion demonstrates that the functional properties of IDF are stable under gastrointestinal conditions. This stability supports its potential application as a functional food ingredient, as the fiber can preserve its technological functionality during processing and physiological functionality during digestion.

Food matrix compatibility analysis

The dispersion stability and WHC of IDF exhibited highly consistent trends across different food model systems (Fig. 4c). The ultrasonic–enzymatic treatment resulted in the lowest sedimentation index and the highest WHC in all media, indicating a strong structure–function relationship. The enhanced WHC suggests the formation of a more porous and hydrophilic fiber network, which increases bound water content and effectively suppresses particle aggregation and sedimentation. In contrast, the reduced water availability and altered solvent structure in NaCl and sucrose solutions weakened hydration and dispersion to some extent; however, the ultrasonic–enzymatic treated fiber maintained superior performance, demonstrating improved compatibility with complex food matrices.

PCA analysis

PCA revealed that PC1 (86.94%) represents a dominant structural–functional regulation axis, in which crystallinity, hydrogen bonding index, and particle size were negatively associated with hydration and adsorption-related properties (Fig. 5f). Mechanistically, ultrasound treatment primarily disrupts intermolecular hydrogen bonds through cavitation-induced shear forces, reducing structural rigidity and enhancing surface accessibility. Cellulase subsequently hydrolyzes exposed amorphous regions, further decreasing crystallinity and releasing hydrophilic groups. The synergistic effect arises from ultrasound-enhanced enzyme accessibility and improved substrate diffusion, leading to coordinated structural reorganization. The consistency between PCA loadings and mechanistic transitions confirms that functional enhancement is not governed by isolated parameter changes, but by integrated structural modulation along a single dominant regulation axis.

Structure-function relationship and mechanisms analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 5g and h, the structural reorganization induced by ultrasound–cellulase treatment plays a decisive role in determining the functional properties of DF. Native fibers with high crystallinity and large particle size possess a dense and ordered cellulose arrangement, which restricts enzyme accessibility and limits the exposure of functional binding sites. In contrast, ultrasonic cavitation generates mechanical shear forces that weaken hydrogen bonding within crystalline domains, facilitating cellulase penetration and selective hydrolysis. The enhancement of adsorption and hydration properties observed in the modified DF can be attributed to coordinated changes in hydrogen bonding, crystalline structure, and particle size distribution. FTIR analysis revealed a noticeable red shift and intensity variation in the –OH stretching region, indicating the disruption and reorganization of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. This weakening of hydrogen bonding reduced the compactness of the cellulose network and promoted exposure of functional groups involved in adsorption interactions. XRD results further demonstrated a pronounced decrease in crystallinity index after ultrasonic–enzymatic treatment. Correlation analysis showed a significant negative relationship between crystallinity and cholesterol adsorption capacity (R2 = 0.99, p < 0.05; Fig. 5f), suggesting that the disruption of ordered crystalline domains plays a decisive role in enhancing functional performance. The reduction of crystalline regions increased the proportion of amorphous domains, which are more accessible to water molecules and hydrophobic substrates. Particle size distribution analysis revealed that after composite treatment, the particles exhibited a reduction in size and a narrower distribution, thereby increasing the specific surface area and shortening the diffusion path. This enhancement promotes mass transfer during hydration and adsorption processes. These structural changes collectively explain the improved water-holding and cholesterol adsorption capacities. Notably, the combined ultrasonic–enzymatic treatment resulted in more pronounced structural disruption and functional enhancement than either treatment alone, indicating a synergistic effect. Ultrasonic pretreatment likely promoted enzyme accessibility by loosening fiber aggregates and reducing crystallinity, thereby amplifying enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency. This synergy underscores the importance of integrating physical and biochemical modification strategies to tailor dietary fiber functionality.

From an application perspective, the structural modification achieved through ultrasound–cellulase treatment endows DF with enhanced lipid-related functionalities, highlighting its potential as a functional food ingredient. The simultaneous reduction in crystallinity and particle size leads to superior OHC and CAC, which are highly desirable properties for regulating lipid digestion and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. The increased amorphous regions and porous microstructure may facilitate the sequestration of dietary cholesterol and lipid micelles, thereby reducing cholesterol bioaccessibility during digestion. Overall, the ultrasound-cellulase method is a scalable, chemical-free route to modulate DF structure. The resulting low-crystallinity, small-particle DF can be incorporated into fat-rich or cholesterol-targeted foods to improve technological properties and lower cholesterol uptake. The data provide a structural basis for designing DF ingredients with enhanced hypocholesterolemic potential and broaden their use in functional foods and nutraceuticals.

Conclusion

In this study, okara DF was modified through ultrasonic treatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and their combined application, and the resulting changes in structural, physicochemical, and functional properties were systematically evaluated. The results demonstrated that the SDF yield and WHC of modified okara DF increased to 8.00 ± 1.04 g/100 g and 14.88 ± 0.29 g/g respectively, under optimal combined conditions (pulse mode of 3 s on/4s off, ultrasonic intensity of 300 W, ultrasonic duration of 20 min, enzyme concentration of 0.2% (w/w), pH 5.0, 60 °C, and hydrolysis period of 1.5 h). Combined ultrasound-enzymatic method effectively degraded okara DF, reducing cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content by 49.41%, 38.41%, and 12.06%, respectively. Structural alterations, including increased porosity, reduced particle size, increased specific surface area, exposure of functional groups, and decreased crystallinity, enhanced exposure of hydrophilic/hydrophobic functional groups (such as –COOH and –OH), contributed to notable improvements in the functional properties of okara DF. Specifically, WHC, OHC, and SC were significantly enhanced by 40.11%, 82.81%, and 40.19%, respectively. These changes further enhanced the functional performance of okara DF, markedly boosting its antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and hypolipidemic activities. Overall, the ultrasound–enzymatic approach provides an efficient strategy for okara DF modification and offers strong potential for the high-value utilization of okara in functional food applications.
