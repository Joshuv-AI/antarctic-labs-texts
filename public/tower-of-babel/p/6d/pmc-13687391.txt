Utility of slow conduction velocity area guided ablation in patients with persistent atrial fibrillation
Authors: Yousaku Okubo, Shunsuke Ishida, Sho Okamura, Naoki Ishibashi, Oe Hiroshi, Junji Maeda, Takumi Sakai, Naoto Oguri, Motoki Furutani, Shogo Miyamoto, Shunsuke Miyauchi, Takehito Tokuyama, Noboru Oda, Yukiko Nakano

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is a well-established treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF). However, in patients with persistent AF, PVI alone is often insufficient, and no adjunctive strategy with proven efficacy beyond PVI has been established. Recently, increasing attention has been directed toward local atrial conduction velocity (CV). Slow conduction velocity areas (SCVAs) have been regarded as potential arrhythmogenic substrates, and ablation strategies targeting SCVAs are under investigation. This study evaluated the efficacy of SCVA-guided ablation in patients with persistent AF.MethodsWe enrolled 175 patients with persistent AF who underwent initial radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA). SCVAs were defined as contiguous atrial regions with a local CV < 0.7 m/s and a surface area ≥ 2.0 cm². SCVA-guided ablation was performed in 84 patients, whereas standard anatomical PVI was performed in 91 patients. The primary outcome was recurrence of AF or occurrence of new-onset supraventricular tachycardia during a two-year follow-up period.ResultsDuring follow-up, clinical recurrence occurred in 63 patients (36.0%). Patients in the SCVA-guided ablation group demonstrated a significantly higher recurrence-free survival rate compared with those in the standard PVI group (72.6% vs. 56.1%; log-rank P = 0.01). Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained supraventricular arrhythmia and is associated with increased risks of heart failure, stroke, and mortality [1]. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone of catheter ablation for AF; however, outcomes in patients with persistent AF remain suboptimal compared with those in paroxysmal AF [2–4]. This limitation is thought to reflect the presence of advanced atrial remodeling and arrhythmogenic substrates extending beyond the pulmonary veins. Although multiple adjunctive ablation strategies have been proposed, no consensus has been established regarding the optimal substrate modification approach beyond PVI in persistent AF [5, 6]. Voltage-based mapping has been widely used to identify atrial substrate abnormalities, and several studies have demonstrated that ablation targeting low-voltage areas may improve arrhythmia-free survival [7–9]. Nevertheless, low-voltage areas are detected in only a subset of patients with persistent AF, limiting the generalizability of this strategy [8]. In addition, bipolar voltage amplitude is highly dependent on technical and physiological factors, including electrode configuration, wavefront direction, tissue contact, and cardiac rhythm during mapping, which may result in inconsistent substrate characterization [10, 11].

In recent years, atrial conduction velocity (CV) has emerged as an alternative functional marker of atrial remodeling [12]. Experimental and clinical data suggest that regions of slow atrial conduction are closely associated with fibrosis, conduction heterogeneity, and reentrant activity, all of which contribute to AF maintenance [13, 14]. CV–based mapping therefore offers a mechanistically distinct perspective on atrial substrate compared with voltage mapping alone. Previous observational investigations have reported that atrial regions exhibiting slow conduction are associated with arrhythmia recurrence after PVI; however, these studies did not clarify whether such regions should be considered therapeutic targets or merely markers of disease severity [15–19].

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of a CV–guided ablation strategy in patients with persistent AF. We compared long-term arrhythmia outcomes between a conventional PVI-based approach and an individualized strategy incorporating targeted ablation of slow conduction velocity areas (SCVAs) in addition to PVI.

Methods

Patient population

In this prospective non-randomized observational study, we screened 498 consecutive patients who underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) for AF at our institution between April 2022 and December 2023. Patients were excluded if they underwent repeat ablation for AF (n = 56), had paroxysmal AF (n = 231), or received PVI using a cryoballoon (n = 6) or laser balloon system (n = 10). In addition, 20 patients in whom sinus rhythm could not be maintained during SCVA mapping were excluded from the analysis. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 175 patients with persistent AF who underwent initial RFCA were included in the final analysis. Among the included patients, 84 underwent an SCVA-guided ablation strategy, whereas 91 underwent a conventional standard ablation strategy consisting of anatomical PVI alone (Fig. 1). The indications for RFCA and the definitions of paroxysmal and persistent AF were based on contemporary clinical practice guidelines. The choice of ablation strategy was determined at the discretion of the attending electrophysiologist. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hiroshima University Graduate School of Biomedical and Health Sciences. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to the procedure.

Conduction velocity mapping

After transseptal puncture, left atrial (LA) electroanatomical mapping was performed using a high-density mapping catheter (Advisor™ HD Grid Mapping Catheter, Abbott, St. Paul, MN) in conjunction with the EnSite™ X EP system (Abbott, St. Paul, MN) in patients assigned to the SCVA-guided ablation group. Because all patients had persistent AF, electrical cardioversion was performed in all cases prior to CV mapping to restore sinus rhythm. Conduction velocity mapping was then performed during high right atrial pacing at a cycle length of 600 ms. This pacing protocol was also selected to maintain consistency with our prior study using omnipolar CV mapping [18]. CV mapping was performed in the LA only and right atrial (RA) mapping was not performed. Mapping points were automatically acquired using the following predefined criteria: band-pass filter setting of 30–500 Hz, cycle length stability of 20 ms, catheter position stability of 1 mm, point density of 1 mm, internal projection of 7 mm, external projection of 7 mm, and interpolation of 7 mm.

Based on the previous study, a cutoff of 0.7 m/s demonstrated strong association with arrhythmogenic substrate and clinical outcomes. Therefore, in the present study, we adopted < 0.7 m/s as a representative threshold for defining SCVAs [18]. Regions exhibiting CV below this threshold were visualized as SCVAs using the Wave Speed software implemented in the EnSite™ X EP system. Clinically significant SCVA targeted for ablation were defined as contiguous regions with a local CV < 0.70 m/s and a surface area ≥ 2.0 cm². The area of each SCVA was manually measured on the corresponding CV map. CV in the present study was calculated using omnipolar technology. Specifically, CV was derived based on the ratio of the temporal derivative to the spatial derivative of the electrical field. The omnipolar algorithm identifies the direction in which the temporal and spatial derivatives are aligned, thereby determining the direction of wavefront propagation. The velocity amplitude is then calculated using the ratio of the peak-to-peak values of these signals. Bipolar electrograms alone cannot directly quantify CV. However, areas of slow conduction are often associated with fractionated and prolonged electrograms, reflecting local conduction delay and heterogeneous activation. To improve interpretability for readers, we have included representative intracardiac electrograms recorded from SCVAs using the HD Grid catheter in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Ablation procedure

Antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued for at least five half-lives prior to the ablation procedure. After transseptal access, systemic anticoagulation was maintained with an activated clotting time > 250 s throughout the procedure. Electrophysiological mapping and catheter ablation were performed by four experienced operators.

In the standard ablation group, circumferential PVI was performed around each pair of ipsilateral pulmonary veins using an 8-French, 4-mm flexible irrigated-tip ablation catheter with 2-2-2-mm interelectrode spacing (TactiCath™ SE Ablation Catheter, Abbott, St. Paul, MN). Radiofrequency energy was delivered with a power setting of 30–35 W, a maximum temperature of 42 °C, and a target lesion size index (LSI) of 5 (LSI of 4 at the posterior LA wall adjacent to the esophagus). Successful PVI was confirmed by demonstration of both entrance and exit block using a circular mapping catheter. After PVI, intravenous administration of 20 µg of isoproterenol and 20 mg of adenosine triphosphate was performed to assess for dormant pulmonary vein conduction. If atrial fibrillation was inducible after successful PVI, additional ablation targeting non–pulmonary vein triggers, linear lesions, or LA posterior wall isolation was performed according to the location of the identified triggers.

In the SCVA-guided ablation group, SCVAs were identified by conduction velocity mapping prior to ablation, and the pulmonary vein isolation lines were intentionally designed to incorporate SCVAs located within the LA antrum. In cases in which SCVAs were diffusely distributed along the posterior LA wall, posterior wall isolation was added in conjunction with PVI. In addition, SCVAs with an area ≥ 2.0 cm² located outside the pulmonary vein isolation region were individually ablated. The ablation catheter, radiofrequency energy settings, and target LSI values were identical to those used in the standard ablation group. As in the standard ablation group, dormant conduction was assessed by intravenous administration of isoproterenol and adenosine triphosphate. When non–pulmonary vein triggers originated from the superior vena cava (SVC), SVC isolation was performed from a site proximal to the identified focus in both groups.

Post procedural follow-up

All patients were followed at 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months after the index procedure to assess arrhythmia recurrence. At each scheduled visit, a clinical evaluation and a standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) were performed. In patients reporting symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia recurrence, including palpitations, fatigue, or dizziness, additional ECG recordings or 24-hour Holter monitoring were performed at the discretion of the referring physician. Antiarrhythmic drugs other than beta-blockers were discontinued after a 3-month blanking period. Arrhythmia recurrence was defined as the documentation of any atrial tachyarrhythmia lasting longer than 30 s occurring after the blanking period following the initial ablation procedure.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia documented by ECG-based examinations during the 12-month follow-up period after the initial procedure. Atrial tachyarrhythmia was defined as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia lasting more than 30 s and detected by 12-lead ECG or other appropriate rhythm monitoring modalities. Secondary outcomes included procedural characteristics, such as total procedure time, fluoroscopy time, and procedure-related complications.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the χ² test for categorical variables, unpaired two-tailed t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify factors associated with atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence. Variables with a P value < 0.10 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate models. In addition, logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of recurrence. Arrhythmia-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

SCVAs were most frequently identified within the PVs (45.2%) and at the PV antra (30.9%). Less frequently, they were observed in the LA anterior wall (8.3%), LA posterior wall (7.1%), and LA septal region (5.9%).

Outcomes

The primary outcome occurred in 63 patients (36.0%) during the follow-up period. Patients in the SCVA-guided ablation group demonstrated a significantly higher AF recurrence–free survival rate compared with those in the standard ablation group (72.6% vs. 56.1%; log-rank P = 0.01; Fig. 4). On multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis, SCVA-guided ablation was independently associated with a lower risk of AF recurrence (hazard ratio [HR] 0.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.21–0.67, P = 0.003), whereas left atrial volume index was independently associated with an increased risk of recurrence (HR 1.03 per mL/m², 95% CI 1.01–1.06, P = 0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective observational study of patients with persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), we demonstrated that a conduction velocity–guided ablation strategy incorporating targeted ablation of slow conduction velocity areas (SCVAs) in addition to pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) was associated with a significantly lower risk of arrhythmia recurrence compared with a standard anatomical PVI-based approach. Despite longer procedure and fluoroscopy times, SCVA-guided ablation did not result in an increased incidence of procedure-related complications, supporting the feasibility and safety of this strategy.

SCVAs as clinically relevant arrhythmogenic substrates

Persistent AF is characterized by progressive atrial remodeling, including fibrosis, conduction heterogeneity, and reentrant activity extending beyond the pulmonary veins [20, 21]. While voltage-based mapping has been widely used to identify such substrates, its clinical utility is limited by variability related to mapping conditions and by the absence of low-voltage areas in a substantial proportion of patients [6–9]. In contrast, atrial conduction velocity represents a functional property of myocardial tissue and reflects the combined effects of structural remodeling, intercellular coupling, and wavefront propagation [22].

In the present study, regions of slow conduction identified by conduction velocity mapping were incorporated into the ablation strategy, either by inclusion within the PVI line or by targeted ablation outside the pulmonary vein antra. Notably, the majority of SCVAs were located within or adjacent to the PVs. In the present study, SCVAs classified as being located in the pulmonary vein (PV) region included areas at or around the anatomical PV ostium extending into the adjacent antral region. When a slow conduction area spanned across the PV–left atrial junction, it was categorized as a PV-related SCVA. This definition implies that some SCVAs within the PV–antral region may have been incidentally modified during conventional anatomical PVI. However, in the SCVA-guided group, ablation lesions were intentionally extended to encompass all identified SCVAs, often resulting in wider lesion sets beyond standard PVI lines, particularly along the posterior aspect of the left PV antrum and the anterior region of the right PVs. This difference in lesion extent may have contributed to the observed differences in clinical outcomes. However, partial overlap between SCVA-guided ablation and conventional PVI is unavoidable and should be considered when interpreting the treatment effect.

Comparison with previous substrate-based ablation strategies

Several adjunctive ablation strategies beyond PVI, including linear ablation, complex fractionated atrial electrogram ablation, posterior wall isolation, and low-voltage area–guided ablation, have been evaluated in patients with persistent AF, with inconsistent results [3, 23]. Recent studies and meta-analyses have suggested potential benefit of low-voltage area–guided ablation; however, its applicability is limited by the absence of low-voltage regions in many patients and by technical variability inherent to voltage mapping [6, 24].

Conduction velocity–guided substrate modification differs fundamentally from voltage-based approaches by focusing on functional conduction abnormalities rather than signal amplitude alone [25]. By targeting regions of slowed conduction that may facilitate wavefront fragmentation and reentry, SCVA-guided ablation offers a mechanistically distinct and potentially more individualized strategy for substrate modification in persistent AF. SCVA should also be distinguished from complex fractionated atrial electrograms (CFAE). While CFAE is defined based on electrogram morphology during atrial fibrillation and is influenced by dynamic activation patterns, SCVA represents a quantitative and reproducible measure of conduction slowing obtained under controlled conditions during sinus rhythm or atrial pacing using omnipolar mapping. Because electrograms during atrial fibrillation were not systematically evaluated in the present study, the relationship between SCVA and CFAE remains unclear.

Procedural complexity and safety considerations

As expected, SCVA-guided ablation was associated with longer procedure and fluoroscopy times, reflecting the additional mapping and ablation required for substrate modification. Importantly, however, this increased procedural complexity did not translate into a higher rate of major complications. No severe adverse events directly attributable to SCVA-guided ablation were observed, and the overall complication profile was comparable between groups. These findings suggest that SCVA-guided ablation can be safely implemented in experienced centers.

Clinical implications

Our findings support the concept that conduction velocity–guided substrate modification may serve as a useful adjunct to PVI in selected patients with persistent AF. Rather than applying uniform additional lesion sets, SCVA-guided ablation allows for individualized tailoring of ablation lines based on patient-specific conduction properties. This approach may help bridge the gap between purely anatomical strategies and empiric extensive substrate ablation, potentially improving outcomes while limiting unnecessary lesions.

Although all ablations in the present study were performed using radiofrequency energy, the concept of slow conduction velocity area–guided ablation is based on substrate mapping rather than energy modality. Therefore, this strategy may remain applicable in the era of pulsed field ablation. However, given the differences in lesion characteristics between radiofrequency ablation and pulsed field ablation, further studies are needed to determine the optimal application of this approach with PFA.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center, non-randomized observational study, and the choice of ablation strategy was left to the operator’s discretion, which may introduce selection bias. Therefore, the observed association between SCVA-guided ablation and clinical outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should ideally adopt a prospective randomized design, in which patients undergo CV mapping and are randomly assigned to SCVA-guided ablation versus conventional ablation strategy. Second, arrhythmia recurrence was assessed using scheduled ECGs and symptom-driven monitoring, without the use of extended monitoring modalities such as long-term Holter monitoring, wearable devices (e.g., Apple Watch), or implantable loop recorders. Therefore, the incidence of atrial fibrillation recurrence may have been underestimated. Third, CV mapping was performed under paced rhythm conditions, which may not fully reflect conduction properties during AF. Fourth, CV mapping was not performed in the RA. Therefore, potential contributions of RA slow conduction to AF maintenance could not be evaluated in this study. Finally, the cutoff value used to define slow CV was based on prior studies and may not represent a universal threshold.

Conclusions

In patients with persistent AF undergoing initial catheter ablation, a conduction velocity–guided ablation strategy incorporating targeted ablation of SCVAs in addition to PVI was associated with improved arrhythmia-free survival compared with standard anatomical PVI alone. These findings suggest that SCVAs represent clinically relevant arrhythmogenic substrates and that conduction velocity–guided substrate modification may provide a rational and individualized approach for ablation beyond PVI in persistent AF. Prospective randomized studies are warranted to further validate these findings.
