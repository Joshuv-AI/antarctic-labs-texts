Adaptation of the state self-compassion scale short form into Turkish: a methodological study
Authors: Nisa Yavuzer Bayrak, Gülcan Bahçecioğlu Turan, Semra Köse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe present study was conducted to adapt the state self-compassion scale short form into Turkish.MethodsThis methodological study was conducted online between June and October 2025. Participants were recruited through a non-probability online convenience and snowball sampling strategy via social media platforms. Eligibility criteria were being 18 years of age or older, residing in Turkey, being able to read and understand Turkish, and providing electronic informed consent. After screening and consent procedures, the final sample consisted of 300 Turkish-speaking adult participants. “Personal Information Form” and the “State Self-Compassion Scale Short Form (SSCS-S)” were used for data collection.ResultsThe analyses revealed that the content validity index (I-CVI) values for each item ranged between 0.90 and 1.00, and the scale’s overall content validity index (S-CVI) was calculated to be 0.99. Item factor loadings supporting the scale’s construct validity were found to range between 0.480 and 0.670. The model fit indices found with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that the proposed model showed a good level of fit with the data set. The following results were found as a result of the analyses: χ²=13.08, sd = 7 (p < 0.05), χ²/sd = 1.86, RMSEA = 0.054, CFI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.032, TLI = 0.98, RMR = 0.038 and AIC = 41.08. These indicators show that the model meets the suitability criteria, supporting the construct validity of the scale. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.770 and McDonald’s omega coefficient was 0.756.ConclusionState Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form Turkish form, which aims to measure self-compassion levels, is a reliable and valid instrument in this Turkish-speaking adult sample.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: State self-compassion, Nursing research, Scale adaptation

Introductıon

The definition of self-compassion is the practice of being kind, caring, and understanding toward oneself, especially during difficult or unsuccessful times. In other words, it is developing a supportive and accepting attitude toward oneself in the face of life’s challenges [1]. When individuals need to accept themselves or calm their negative emotions, compassion takes on a softer and more supportive quality; when they need to protect themselves, meet their basic needs, or take action for change, it takes on a more determined and action-oriented form. In this sense, compassion can be considered not only a passive acceptance but also an active source of strength and motivation [2].

Self-compassion stands out as a fundamental building block in the process of protecting and maintaining mental health of individuals, providing a protective effect against psychological distress and developing emotional resilience [3, 4]. Within the context of nursing, self-compassion is important for the continuation of professional practice and the maintenance of quality care. Self-compassion requires individuals to be forgiving and kind to themselves in the face of adverse situations and to continue with a balanced approach to any negative emotions that may occur. These characteristics are critical for nursing because they have a direct impact on professional performance and patient care. Self-compassion is shown to be positively correlated with various aspects of nursing practice in various studies in the literature [5–7].

With these aspects, self-compassion is at the center of applied psychological interventions and also offers a comprehensive field of study for future scientific research. Therefore, as a nurse, conducting scale studies related to self-compassion to make it measurable is of great importance for both clinical practice and research [5, 7]. Self-compassion scales in the literature usually assess self-compassion as a personality trait that remains constant over time. In contrast, state self-compassion aims to measure the momentary self-compassion responses of individuals to a specific event or negative experience [2, 8]. Studies in the Turkish literature also tend to focus on measures of self-compassion from a personality perspective [9]. This situation shows that state self-compassion needs to be assessed in Turkish culture with a valid and reliable tool. In line with this need, the adaptation of the short version of State Self-Compassion Scale into Turkish provides the opportunity to examine situational changes in self-compassion levels among Turkish-speaking adults. In addition, the short structure of the scale provides a practical measurement tool for both experimental research and clinical applications by reducing the application time. The relevance of this study to nursing lies in the construct and intended application of the measurement tool. Nursing science addresses not only nurses but also adults, patients, caregivers, families, students, and communities whose coping responses, emotional regulation, resilience, and psychological well-being are central to nursing care. State self-compassion reflects individuals’ immediate responses to distressing experiences and is therefore relevant to nursing assessment, psychosocial care, health promotion, and intervention research. Accordingly, adapting the SSCS-S into Turkish may provide a brief and practical instrument for future nursing studies involving populations within the scope of nursing care.

Materials and methods

Design

The present study employed a methodological design. In this study, methodological design refers to a research approach used to adapt an existing measurement instrument to a different language and cultural context and to evaluate its psychometric properties. Accordingly, the Turkish version of the SSCS-S was examined in terms of linguistic and cultural equivalence, content validity, construct validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability.

Participants and sample

Data were collected online between June and October 2025. A non-probability online convenience and snowball sampling strategy was used. The online data collection form was prepared using Google Forms and distributed through social media platforms and online communication networks. The initial invitation included brief information about the purpose of the study, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the inclusion criteria. Participants who received the invitation were also encouraged to share the survey link with other individuals in their social networks who met the eligibility criteria.

The inclusion criteria were: being 18 years of age or older, residing in Turkey, being able to read and understand Turkish, and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. The online survey was designed with a forced-response format in Google Forms, requiring participants to answer all items before submission; therefore, no missing data were present in the final dataset. Individuals who did not meet these criteria or did not provide electronic informed consent were excluded. Screening was conducted at the beginning of the online form using eligibility and consent questions. Only participants who confirmed that they met the inclusion criteria and provided electronic informed consent were allowed to proceed to the questionnaire [8, 9].

In the initial recruitment phase, 880 individuals accessed or were reached through the online survey link. Of these, 220 individuals did not meet the eligibility criteria and were excluded. In addition, 360 individuals did not provide informed consent and therefore did not proceed to the questionnaire. As a result, data analysis was conducted with a final sample of 300 participants in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of online recruitment, eligibility screening, electronic consent, and final inclusion

Because the study used an online convenience and snowball sampling strategy, the sample was not randomly selected and should not be considered representative of the general Turkish adult population. This sampling approach may have increased the likelihood of selection bias, particularly by reaching individuals who were active on social media, had internet access, were willing to participate in online research, and were connected to the researchers’ or participants’ social networks. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as psychometric evidence obtained from the present Turkish-speaking adult sample rather than as nationally representative evidence.

It is emphasized in the literature that certain criteria should be considered for sample calculation in scale development studies or in adaptation of an existing scale to a different cultural context or language. In scale adaptation and psychometric validation studies, sample size should be determined by considering both the participant-to-item ratio and the absolute sample size required for factor analytic procedures. Although minimum recommendations vary across the literature, more conservative methodological guidelines commonly suggest using 15–20 participants per scale item, particularly when validity and reliability analyses include factor analysis. Since the SSCS-S consists of six items, this criterion would require a minimum sample size of approximately 90–120 participants. The present study included 300 participants, corresponding to 50 participants per item, which substantially exceeds this recommendation and provides an adequate sample size for the planned psychometric analyses.

Data collection tools

“Personal Information Form” and “State Self-Compassion Scale Short Form (SSCS-S)” were used for data collection.

Personal information form

Questions in the form aim to collect demographic and health-related information such as individuals’ age range, gender distribution, education level, marital status, employment status, and whether they have any chronic conditions.

State self-compassion scale short form (SSCS-S)

This is a 6-item measurement tool developed based on the 26-item Self-Compassion Scale created by Neff et al. While Self-Compassion Scale focuses on assessing individuals’ general (trait) levels of self-compassion, this short form aims to measure individuals’ momentary levels of self-compassion following a specific negative experience. Participants’ responses are evaluated by using a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from “not at all true for me” to “completely true for me.” Items 2, 4, and 6 on the scale are reversely-scored to calculate the total score, and then level of self-compassion is calculated with the average of items. Although there are no specific score ranges for self-compassion levels on the scale, scores within the range of 1.00–2.49 are considered low, scores within the range of 2.50–3.50 are considered moderate, and scores within the range of 3.51–5.00 are considered high levels of self-compassion [2, 10]. Before completing the SSCS-S, participants were asked to recall a recent negative or stressful experience and to keep this specific event in mind while responding to the scale items. The instruction provided in the online form was as follows: “Please think about a recent situation in which you experienced difficulty, failure, stress, or emotional discomfort. Keeping this specific event in mind, answer the following statements according to how you feel toward yourself right now in relation to this experience.” This recall-based instruction was used to ensure that the responses reflected momentary self-compassion toward a specific negative experience rather than general or trait-level self-compassion.

The process of adapting the scale into Turkish

The process of adapting the scale into Turkish was carried out in three main stages. In the first step, the six items in the original SSCS-S were translated into Turkish. At this stage, support was received from two English language experts. During the translation process, care was taken to prevent possible shifts in meaning that could result from language differences, taking into account both the semantic integrity and contextual accuracy of the items. The form translated into Turkish was submitted for review of validity to a total of 10 experts who were selected from different disciplines, including Turkish linguists, psychologists, and nurses, in an effort to ensure that the scale underwent a multidimensional evaluation process. As a result of the evaluations, all items were found to have a content validity index > 0.80, and it was decided to keep all the items. The grammatical accuracy, clarity of meaning, cultural appropriateness, and consistency of the items with the original scale were comprehensively evaluated by the experts. Based on the expert evaluations, no item required linguistic or wording revision, and all six translated items were retained without modification. The Turkish form was then back-translated into English by two independent English language experts. The back-translated version and the original scale were found to be semantically equivalent, and the scale was considered appropriate for the pilot application phase.

In the second stage, a pilot study was conducted with 43 participants to assess the clarity of the scale items, the appropriateness of the response options, and the feasibility of the measurement tool. In the pilot analysis, the corrected item-total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.350 to 0.515, and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.730. Since all corrected item-total correlations were above 0.30 and Cronbach’s alpha exceeded 0.70 [11, 12], no item was removed, and the main application phase was initiated with the original six-item structure of the scale.

In the third and final stage, the psychometric properties of the scale were evaluated using construct validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability analyses. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted on a sample of 300 participants, and model fit indices were assessed in accordance with the criteria reported in the literature [13, 14]. The results indicated that the proposed factor structure demonstrated acceptable fit, supporting the construct validity of the Turkish version. Internal consistency and test–retest analyses further indicated satisfactory reliability of the scale.

Retest intervals of 20 days were applied to assess stability over time. The 15–30 day retest interval recommended in the literature [11] was followed. Although the SSCS-S is designed to measure momentary/state self-compassion rather than trait self-compassion, the test–retest procedure was used to evaluate the reproducibility of the Turkish form under equivalent administration conditions. Therefore, test–retest results were interpreted not as evidence of stability in self-compassion itself, but as evidence that the Turkish version yields consistent scores when administered under standardized instructions at different time points. In both administrations, participants were instructed to recall a recent negative or stressful experience and to respond according to their current self-compassionate reactions to that specific experience.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected via Google Forms and transferred to Excel, then analyzed using SPSS 27 and LISREL software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Item-total correlations and internal consistency coefficients were calculated to assess item performance.Prior to analyses, data screening procedures were conducted to ensure data quality. The dataset was examined for duplicate responses and outliers, and no problematic cases were identified. The online survey was designed with a forced-response format; therefore, no missing data were observed in the dataset. Assumptions of normality were evaluated prior to confirmatory factor analysis by examining skewness and kurtosis values, which indicated that the data were within acceptable ranges for structural equation modeling. These preliminary checks confirmed that the dataset was appropriate for subsequent analyses. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) coefficient and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. A KMO value greater than 0.60 and a statistically significant Bartlett’s test indicate sampling adequacy [11]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate construct validity without performing exploratory factor analysis (EFA), as the study aimed to test a pre-established factor structure. This approach is consistent with cross-cultural adaptation guidelines (Beaton et al., 2000) and psychometric evaluation standards [8], and has been similarly applied in previous validation studies where CFA was performed without prior EFA when testing established measurement models [15–17] .Structural validity was tested using LISREL with maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including χ2/df, RMSEA, CFI, TLI, SRMR, and RMR [11, 13, 14, 18]. The items were treated as continuous variables, consistent with standard practice for Likert-type scales in CFA analyses when the number of response categories is ≥ 5. Convergent validity was assessed using Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE). Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. Temporal stability was examined using the test–retest method [11, 19].

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Fırat University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (21.08.2025 / 2025/11–32). Permission to adapt the SSCS-S scale into Turkish was obtained from the scale developer through official correspondence. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants read an online informed consent statement explaining the study purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw. Participants provided electronic informed consent by selecting the consent option on the first page of the survey. Only those who provided consent were allowed to proceed to the questionnaire.

Results on validity

Content validity

Item-based content validity index (I-CVI) values were between 0.90 and 1.00, while scale-based content validity index (S-CVI) value was found to be 0.99.

Construct validity

Sample adequacy for factor analysis was assessed using the KMO measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. The KMO value was 0.731, showing the data to be suitable for factor analysis. Bartlett’s Sphericity Test was statistically significant (χ2=542.328; p < 0.001). The use of a specific negative-experience recall prompt before administering the SSCS-S supported the state-based construct validity of the Turkish form by anchoring participants’ responses to their current self-compassionate reactions toward a particular adverse experience.

To test the validity of the single-factor and 6-item structure, CFA was conducted. Factor loadings were shown to range between 0.480 and 0.670 and confirmed the suitability of the single-factor structure (Table 2).

Discussion

Self-compassion is an important personal resource that increases resilience and reduces stress and burnout among both the general adult population and healthcare professionals in recent years [2, 4]. Although the present sample consisted of general adults recruited online, the findings provide preliminary psychometric evidence for the Turkish SSCS-S and an initial basis for its use in adult populations relevant to nursing research and practice. The scale may be useful in studies examining stress, coping, emotional regulation, resilience, and psychosocial well-being among patients, caregivers, nursing students, nurses, and community-dwelling adults. However, the sampling strategy should be considered when interpreting these findings. Participants were recruited through online convenience and snowball sampling rather than probability sampling, which may have led to an overrepresentation of younger, highly educated adults with internet access, social media use, and similar social networks. Therefore, future studies should further test the Turkish SSCS-S in more diverse and probability-based samples, including nurses, nursing students, clinical populations, older adults, individuals with lower educational levels, rural populations, and adults with limited access to digital platforms.

In the literature, an item-level content validity index (I-CVI) value greater than 0.80 is considered sufficient for content validity of scale items [20]. In the present study, the I-CVI values ranged between 0.90 and 1.00, indicating that all items had adequate item-level content validity. In addition, the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was 0.99, demonstrating a high level of expert agreement regarding the overall content validity of the scale.

Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were used to evaluate sample size adequacy and the factorability of the data. Bartlett test value must be statistically significant and the KMO value must be at least 0.60 for factor analysis [21]. While the Bartlett test gave statistically significant results (p < 0.05), the KMO value was 0.731. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was applied to validate the single-factor structure of the existing 6-item scale. According to criteria accepted in the literature [11, 22], factor loadings of items must be > 0.30 for scale validity. CFA results showed factor loadings were between 0.480 and 0.670, exceeding the specified threshold value. These findings reveal that the single-factor structure of the scale has been statistically confirmed. The convergent validity of the Turkish SSCS-S was evaluated using composite reliability and average variance extracted. Although the Composite Reliability (CR = 0.765) exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating acceptable internal consistency, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE = 0.355) remained below the recommended level of 0.50. This pattern is commonly observed in brief unidimensional scales, where a limited number of items may reduce the proportion of variance captured by the latent construct. In such cases, composite reliability is often considered a more stable indicator of convergent validity than AVE. Therefore, convergent validity was primarily interpreted based on composite reliability and standardized factor loadings, consistent with established methodological recommendations [23, 24].

The present findings are generally consistent with the original validation study of the SSCS-S. In the original study, Neff et al. developed the six-item SSCS-S as a brief measure of global state self-compassion and reported support for a single-factor structure. The SSCS-S was also found to be reliable and strongly associated with theoretically related constructs, such as positive and negative affect. Similarly, the present Turkish version demonstrated acceptable item factor loadings, adequate model fit, and acceptable internal consistency, supporting the applicability of the single-factor structure in a Turkish-speaking adult sample. The factor loadings obtained in the present study ranged from 0.480 to 0.670, and all items exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.30, indicating that each item contributed meaningfully to the latent construct. In addition, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and McDonald’s omega value were both above the acceptable threshold, suggesting that the Turkish SSCS-S has adequate reliability for assessing global state self-compassion.

The following fit index values were found with confirmatory factor analysis: χ²=13.08, sd = 7 (p < 0.05), χ²/sd = 1.86, RMSEA = 0.054, CFI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.032, TLI = 0.98, RMR = 0.038 and AIC = 41.08. It has been stated in the literature that for model fit to be considered acceptable, the CMIN/DF value should not be > 5, RMSEA and SRMR should be < 0.08, and TLI should be > 0.90 [12, 20, 21]. According to our results, fit indices were at a good level and single-factor structure consisting of six items was validated in the present Turkish-speaking adult sample.

Reliability analyses were conducted after construct validity was confirmed. First, Cronbach’s alpha value was evaluated for internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which is an indicator of homogeneity within a scale [25], indicates whether the items measure similar characteristics. The literature indicates that a reliability coefficient of ≥ 0.70 should be targeted [26]. Total Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was 0.770, while the Omega reliability value was 0.756. It was noted that the Cronbach’s alpha value in the short version of the original scale was at an adequate level [2].

The relationship between scale items and other items and the total score is assessed with item-total correlation coefficients, and a value of > 0.30 is considered acceptable [11, 22]. When the item-total correlation coefficients of the scale were examined, it was found that all item-total correlation coefficients ranged between 0.438 and 0.625. Correlation coefficients of ≥ 0.80 indicate a high level of relationship, values between 0.60 and 0.80 indicate a strong level of relationship, values between 0.40 and 0.59 indicate a moderate level of relationship, values between 0.20 and 0.39 indicate a low level of relationship, and values of < 0.20 indicate a very weak level of relationship [27]. tThe values found in the study indicate a moderately strong relationship. The correlation value was r = 0.877, and test and retest results did not show significant difference. The results show that the scale has a high level of reliability.

The Turkish SSCS-S may have several practical implications for nursing and health-related research. Because the scale is brief and easy to administer, it may be useful in studies where participant burden must be minimized, such as research with patients, caregivers, nursing students, nurses, and community-dwelling adults. The scale may also be used to assess momentary self-compassion in relation to stress, burnout, resilience, coping, emotional regulation, psychological well-being, and adaptation to illness. In nursing education, the Turkish SSCS-S may help evaluate interventions designed to strengthen emotional awareness, mindfulness, compassion, and coping skills among students. In clinical and community settings, it may provide a practical tool for examining how individuals respond to difficult experiences, illness-related distress, caregiving burden, or occupational stress. However, given the characteristics of the present sample, further validation studies are needed before the scale can be confidently recommended for routine clinical use or for specific professional groups such as nurses and nursing students.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the data were collected online using non-probability convenience and snowball sampling via social media platforms. Therefore, individuals without internet access, those who do not actively use social media, and those outside the researchers’ and participants’ networks may have been underrepresented. Second, because participation was voluntary, self-selection bias may have occurred, as individuals with greater interest in psychological well-being, self-compassion, or online research may have been more likely to participate. Third, the sample was relatively young, highly educated, and mostly unemployed, which limits its representativeness of the broader Turkish adult population. For these reasons, the findings should not be generalized to all Turkish adults and should instead be interpreted as preliminary psychometric evidence for the Turkish-speaking adult sample included in this study. Future research should examine the Turkish SSCS-S in larger, more heterogeneous, and preferably probability-based samples, including older adults, individuals with lower educational levels, clinical populations, nurses, nursing students, and community-based groups. In addition, although convergent validity was supported through composite reliability and standardized factor loadings, the relatively low average variance extracted may reflect the brief and unidimensional structure of the scale as well as the limited number of items, which should be considered when interpreting the strength of the convergent validity evidence. Furthermore, discriminant validity could not be assessed due to the unidimensional nature of the scale, which limits the evaluation of the construct from a multi-dimensional perspective. Moreover, exploratory factor analysis procedures were not comprehensively reported in terms of extraction method, rotation method, explained variance, eigenvalues, scree plot, and communalities, which may limit the transparency of the factor structure evaluation.

Conclusion

According to these findings, the Turkish version of the State Self-Compassion Scale Short Form demonstrated acceptable preliminary psychometric properties in the present Turkish-speaking adult sample. However, because the study was based on online convenience and snowball sampling, further research with more representative and diverse samples is needed before broader generalizations can be made.
