Clinical lipidomics in bacterial sepsis: a systematic review of serum and plasma evaluations
Authors: Nurul Saadah Ahmad, Rosni Ibrahim, Toh Leong Tan, Syarul Nataqain Baharum, Zamberi Sekawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipid metabolism plays a central role in host-pathogen interactions and immune regulation during bacterial sepsis, with its dysregulation contributing to organ failure and mortality. Blood-based lipidomics has emerged as a promising approach for identifying diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in sepsis. However, discrepancies in lipid profiles between serum and plasma remain a major challenge for clinical translation. This systematic review synthesizes lipidomic evidence from serum and plasma in bacterial sepsis patients to identify reproducible lipid biomarkers, evaluate methodological heterogeneity and assess associations with clinical outcomes.MethodsA systematic search of five databases (Scopus, Pubmed, Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science and Google Scholar) identified nine eligible studies published between 2016 and 2025 that applied untargeted or targeted lipidomics on human serum and plasma samples from adult bacterial sepsis patients (PROSPERO: CRD420251086177).ResultsAcross both matrices, reproducible candidate lipid biomarkers included triacylglycerols (TAG), phosphatidylcholines (PC), lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC), sphingomyelins (SM) and ceramides. Synthesized findings revealed characteristically distinct directional trajectories: consistent elevation of TAG, bidirectional shifts of PC, depletion of LPC, bidirectional shifts of SM and elevation of SM. Preliminary trend within the literature indicates that serum-based studies more frequently captured prognostic alterations, whereas plasma-based studies often focused on diagnostic discrimination. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in sample handling, lipid extraction protocols and analytical platforms.ConclusionsOverall, both matrices offer valuable clinical insights, though these apparent differences in diagnostic or prognostic utility may reflect primary study designs rather than intrinsic matrix superiorities. Methodological heterogeneity limits reproducibility, highlighting the need for standardized workflows to enable translation of sepsis lipid biomarkers.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02488-4.

Introduction

Bacterial sepsis involves complex sequence of inflammatory events following bacterial infection. These events can lead to organ failure and, ultimately, death (Singer et al., 2016). This syndrome involves physiological, pathological and biochemical disturbances in the host body system. Sepsis is a major healthcare problem and affects millions of people around the world. In 2022, The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that physicians in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) frequently encounter serious complications due to infections in these particular regions (WHO, 2023). In 2021, sepsis was estimated to account for approximately 166 million cases and 21.4 million sepsis-related deaths worldwide, representing 31.5% of all global deaths (Gray et al., 2025). According to WHO (2023), the most frequent cause of infection is sepsis patients is bacterial sepsis. Although patients with sepsis may present with signs of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), it can be difficult for clinicians to distinguish between sepsis is due to bacterial infection or other causes (Kapasi et al., 2016; Shappell et al., 2022).

The classical diagnostic method is based on clinical signs of an inflammatory response and microbial testing. Today, sepsis diagnosis follows a clinical operationalization whereby organ dysfunction is represented by an increase in the Sequential [Sepsis-related] Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of 2 points or more, accessing respiration, coagulation, cardiovascular, central nervous system, liver function tests and renal profile, which can be exhaustive for patients that present acutely to the Emergency Department. A new measure of qSOFA (for quick SOFA) which includes altered mental status, systolic blood pressure of 100mmHg or less, and respiratory rate of 22/min or greater, provides simple bedside criteria for consideration of a possible infection in patients previously not recognized as infected (Singer et al., 2016). A culture-dependent diagnosis of bacterial infection is still considered as gold standard assessment despite it is time-consuming (> 48 h). Early detection remains critical in improving outcomes and blood has emerged as an ideal biological material for identifying diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers due to its accessibility and rapid turnaround for clinical testing. Despite advances in critical care, available biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, procalcitonin and lactate are limited by variable sensitivity and specificity and often fail to provide early mechanistic insight into evolving organ failure. Consequently, clinicians still rely heavily on severity scors system (e.g., SOFA) that reflect established organ dysfunction rather than imminent deterioration (Rudd et al., 2020; Singer et al., 2016). Consequently, there is increasing interest in exploring composition of blood, particularly lipids to uncover novel indicators of sepsis (Bai et al., 2025; Birner et al., 2024; Mecatti et al., 2020). A panel of biomarkers rather than a single biomarker has been suggested to provide a more rapid and accurate way to rule in or out bacterial sepsis syndrome.

Recent evidence demonstrates lipid metabolism plays a major role in the pathophysiology of sepsis. Inflammation, immunity and cell signaling causing extensive modification of lipid classes (Hofmaenner et al., 2022), which can contribute to adverse outcomes such as organ dysfunction and increased mortality risk (Amunugama et al., 2021; Muniz-Santos et al., 2023; Wasyluk & Zwolak, 2021). In the context of bacterial infection, systemic lipid alterations profound metabolic shifts that function as both mechanistic drivers of infection progression and active modulators of the inflammatory response (Cheng et al., 2026; Escoll & Buchrieser, 2018; Kominsky et al., 2010; Troha & Ayres, 2020). Specifically, increased levels of triacylglycerols (TAG) act as primary mechanistic drivers of systemic metabolic failure (Júnior et al., 2012). This accumulation is precipitated by pro-inflammatory cytokine which stimulate hepatic TAG synthesis while concurrently suppressing fatty acid oxidation and TAG clearance. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction caused lipolysis in peripheral tissue worsens the progression of infection (Birner et al., 2024; Wasyluk & Zwolak, 2021). Clinical studies have consistently reported that plasma concentration of fatty acids (FA) and TAG in septic patients are up to four times higher than those observed in healthy cohorts, reflecting a critical disruption of systemic lipid homestasis (Golucci et al. 2018; Wendel et al. 2007a).

Phosphatidylcholine (PC) and lysophosphatodylcholine (LPC) function as active modulators that regulate the host inflammatory response (Wang et al., 2019). The hydrolysis of PC by phospholipase A2 (PLA2) yields fatty acids and LPC. PC levels typically exhibit bidirectional changes during sepsis (De Araujo et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2025) where their fluctuation reflects a complex balance between mitochondrial dysfunction and urgent inflammatory demand for lipid mobilization (Ridgway, 2013). Conversely, LPC is utilized to amplify inflammation by signalling for increased chemokine production and the recruitment of leukocyte (Amunugama et al., 2021; Ferrario et al., 2016). Consequently, decreased LPC concentrations are frequently reported in septic patients (Bruegel et al., 2012; Drobnik et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2020; Mecatti et al., 2018), including in patients with confirmed bacteraemia (Cho et al., 2012; Neugebauer et al., 2016).

Sphingomyelins (SM) and ceramide functions as mechanistic driver of infection progression (Amunugama et al., 2021). SM hydrolyse by Acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) and neutral SMases to produce ceramide. Ceramide also produced de novo in endoplasmic reticulum (Grassmé & Becker, 2013; Liu et al., 2016). Similarly to PC, SM also reported bidirectional flux as part of a “sphingolipid rheostat”, where their enzymatic alterations through the sphingomyelinase pathway provides essential substrates for immune cell activation and ceramide accumulation (Taniguchi & Okazaki, 2020). Elevated ceramides mediate systemic stress responses, including oxidative stress and inflammation, while directly contributing to endothelial dysfunction and apoptosis (Amunugama et al., 2021). In clinical settings, increased ceramide levels in isolated immune cells are associated with the incidence of multiple organ dysfunction (Delogu et al., 1999) and the progressively rising ceramide-to-SM ratio serves as a critical prognostic indicator for sepsis nonsurvivors (Drobnik et al., 2003). These distinct lipid profiles provide high discriminatory value for distinguishing sepsis from non-infectious systemic inflammation, as well as bacteraemia from non-bacteraemia. Beyond their diagnostic utility, these lipid alterations are also linked to clinical severity and higher mortality. Investigating these specific lipid alterations not only strengthens their role as clinically meaningful biomarkers but also illuminates the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of sepsis, potentially guiding the development of more targeted and effective therapeutic strategies to restore lipid homeostasis.

Lipidomics has been suggested to be one of potential omics to better understanding of this syndrome. Advances in mass spectrometry and chromatography have enabled both untargeted and targeted lipidomic approaches, allowing researchers to profile a wide range of lipid species, from membrane-associated phospholipids to free fatty acids and lipid mediators (Han & Gross, 2022). Untargeted lipidomics provides broad coverage of the lipidome, ideal for discovering new biomarkers, while targeted approaches offer precise quantification of specific lipid classes relevant to disease mechanisms (Hornemann, 2021; Lerner et al., 2023; Sethi & Brietzke, 2017; Wei et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2014). Lipidomic profiling enables the measurement of various lipid levels in the bloodstream, helping to identify specific metabolic alterations associated with bacterial sepsis (Amunugama et al., 2021; Mecatti et al., 2018). However, lipidomic outcomes can vary substantially depending on the biological matrix analyzed, as well as pre-analytical and analytical platform settings.

These insights highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate biological matrix, as the choice between serum and plasma directly influence the lipidomic signatures for diagnostic and prognostic purposes. Serum is often regarded as the gold standard for analysis because it is derived from coagulated blood without any additives, thereby reducing the potential for chemical interference (Ishikawa et al., 2013). In contrast, plasma requires the addition of anticoagulants to prevent clotting prior to separation. The type of anticoagulant used can significantly alter the resulting metabolite composition (Gonzalez-Covarrubias et al., 2013). Several studies have examined how anticoagulant of blood collection tubes affect metabolic profiles in plasma sample (Sotelo-Orozco et al., 2021). However, substantial evidence has focused on EDTA and heparin plasma tubes(Bernini et al. 2011; Cruickshank-Quinn et al. 2018; Denery et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2018; López-Bascón et al. 2016; Nishiumi et al. 2018; Paglia et al. 2018; Pereira et al. 2010; Suarez-Diez et al. 2017; Teahan et al. 2006; Wedge et al. 2011; Yu et al. 2011) with fewer studies investigating other plasma tubes such as citrate plasma(Bernini et al., 2011) and potassium oxalate (Yin et al., 2013). Considering the variety of plasma collection tubes available (EDTA, heparin, citrate and potassium oxalate tubes) and the diversity of blood samples stored in biobanks, there is a need for systematic evaluations of how these pre-analytical factor impact lipidomic outcomes. Additionally, differences in sample handling—including processing time and temperature can introduce variability, which highlights the need for standardized protocols to ensure reproducibility in lipidomic research (Cruickshank-Quinn et al., 2018; Thachil et al., 2024). Despite this progress, existing studies often report inconsistent findings regarding lipid species composition and concentration, partly due to variability in sample types (serum vs. plasma), extraction methods and analytical platforms. These inconsistencies could hinder the reproducibility and clinical translation of lipid-based biomarkers for sepsis.

Hence, this systematic review aims to critically evaluate and synthesize evidence from nine high-quality lipidomics studies investigating serum or plasma samples from bacterial sepsis patients. By evaluating lipid compositions and diversity across sample types, this review seeks to identify reproducible lipid biomarkers, examine the influence of method heterogeneity and assess associations of clinical outcomes.

Methodology

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: PROSPERO 2025 CRD420251086177). The relevant studies were obtained from five online databases namely Scopus, PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science and Google Scholar with no restriction on publication period. The final search was conducted on 30th October 2025. Search terms used for each database can be found in Supplementary File 1.

Eligibility criteria

Only full-length original research articles published in English were included. The literature search and articles screening were conducted according to the population, exposure, comparison, outcome and study design (PECOS) framework, as outlined in Table 1.

Only sample types of human serum and plasma were included in this review. Studies were excluded if (i) the determination of bacterial infection were not clearly stated; (ii) the inclusion and exclusion criteria were not clear; and (iii) clinical study is lacking of lipidomic profiling. Papers with metabolomics analysis without specific lipidomic focus were excluded. Additionally, experimental studies involving in-vivo, in-vitro, or ex-vivo methods were also not considered.

Study selection process

Articles retrieved from the databases were independently reviewed by two authors. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion to reach a consensus. The screening process was conducted in three stages. First, articles that clearly did not meet the selection criteria were excluded based on their titles. Second, abstracts were reviewed to eliminate studies irrelevant to lipidomics and bacterial sepsis. For reporting we referred to the PRISMA guidelines. Finally, full-text articles were thoroughly assessed and those not fulfilling the inclusion criteria were excluded.

A standardized data collection form was utilized to extract the following information from eligible studies: (i) study metadata, including authors, publication year, and country; (ii) study population characteristics: study type, sample size, population, grouping, sepsis definition, bacterial confirmation, age/gender matched and biological sample type; (iii) pre-analytical details: blood collection tube, time sample collected, storage, extraction method, internal standards, column type, analytical platform and lipidomic approach; (iv) lipid changes and clinical relevance: biomarker purpose, lipid species, lipid class, effect directions, associated clinical outcomes, level of lipids and relevant metabolic pathway.

A total of 783 records were initially identified. After the screening process, nine articles were selected for inclusion in the final review. The detailed selection and exclusion process is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment/risk of bias

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which is widely applicable to observational studies such as cohort and cross-sectional designs (Wells et al., 2006). This tool was selected to ensure appropriate evaluation based on study design, enabling a comprehensive assessment of study validity, comparability and outcome reliability. Each observational study was scored out of a maximum of 9 points. Studies scoring ≥ 7 was considered high quality, 5–6 moderate quality and < 5 low quality.

Two reviewers independently performed the quality assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus. The results of the quality assessment are summarized in Table 2. No studies were excluded solely based on quality scores; however, the risk of bias was taken into consideration during data synthesis and interpretation of findings.

Results

Studies selected

A total of 783 articles were found in five online databases, including 237 articles from Scopus, 81 articles from PubMed, 78 articles from Ovid MEDLINE, 118 articles from Web of Science and 269 articles from Google Scholar. Subsequently, 233 articles were removed due to duplication. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, 532 papers were excluded. The remaining 23 full-length original articles were retrieved and thoroughly reviewed. Of these, 17 were excluded based on the eligibility criteria. The remaining six articles were combined with three additional articles were manually retrieved from the references list of initially included studies. In total, nine articles published between 2016 and 2025 met the search criteria and were included in this review. Figure 1 showed the flowchart of article selection process.

Study characteristics

A total of nine studies published between 2016 and 2025 met all the criteria and were included in the final analysis. The general characteristics of each study are summarized in Table 3. These studies represent diverse geographical regions including France, Germany (n = 2), China (n = 2), Spain, Norway, the USA and Italy. Most studies adopted a prospective cross-sectional design with only one retrospective study identified. Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from small pilot studies (19 participants) to larger clinical studies (224 participants). Across studies, populations included various forms of bacterial sepsis or mixed infection cohorts, such as community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), acute pancreatitis (AP) with bacterial infection, ICU sepsis, ED sepsis and catheter-related bloodstream infections. Healthy volunteers were used as control groups in most studies.

All studies included involve comparative group structures, consisting of a sepsis or infection group and either other than bacterial infections or healthy volunteers. Several studies further differentiated groups based on sepsis severity classifications. For example, Birner et al. (2024) included sepsis, septic shock, and control patients, whereas Mosevoll et al. (2023) categorized patients according to Sepsis-2 or Sepsis-3 definitions with evidence of multiorgan failure. Studies involving specific clinical conditions (e.g., CAP, ARDS, AP) similarly incorporated severity-based subgroups, enabling more granular assessment of lipidomic changes associated with progression or organ dysfunction. 0.

Definitions and confirmation of sepsis also varied. While the majority employed Sepsis-3 criteria (SOFA or MOF-based evidence), earlier works used Sepsis-2 definitions and SIRS. All studies required laboratory confirmation of bacterial infection, most commonly via blood culture with some incorporating CSF, urine or stool cultures, depending on clinical presentation. For sample type, serum (n = 4) and plasma (n = 5) were utilized.

Regarding the screening of confounding factors, baseline demographic matching was minimal. Only Chen et al. (2021) achieved full age- and gender-matching, while Liu et al. (2024) accounted for age matching only. The remaining studies (77.8%) did not perform matching for these primary demographic variables. Baseline comorbidities were screened in five studies, whereas the confounding effects of medication history were explicitly addressed in only two studies (Neb et al., 2024; Batra et al., 2022). Notably, none of the included studies recorded for dietary history of participants.

Pre-analytical, analytical and statistical methodology characteristics

The pre-analytical workflows, analytical platforms and statistical methodology of the nine included studies exhibited heterogeneity (Table 4). Regarding pre-analytical procedures, all studies collected serum sample were using gel-separator tubes (n = 4). Citrate tubes (n = 2) and EDTA tubes (n = 3) were used to collect plasma samples. Sampling was performed at time of admission or between 24 and 72 h of being admitted to the hospital or intensive care unit (ICU). Although nearly all studies stored samples at − 80 °C to preserve lipid stability, one study used − 70 °C. Other than that, extraction methods varied widely. Classical biphasic extractions such as Bligh and Dyer, Folch, and MTBE were frequently applied, while others used modified protocols such as hexane/ethyl acetate, butanol–methanol or methanol–dichloromethane. Notably, internal standard was incorporated in seven out of the nine studies to ensure quantitative and technical reliability, however, only five studies explicitly specified the names of the internal standards used, while two studies did not report any internal standard (Iftimie et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024).

As for analytical platforms, high-performance and ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS, UHPLC-Orbitrap) were the most common systems particularly for untargeted analyses (n = 4). Targeted studies (n = 5) tended to employ QTRAP or triple-quadrupole MS and Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LCMS/MS). Chromatographic separation columns C18 was most reported (n = 4) and only one study used C8, while others were not mentioned.

Mitigation strategies for batch effects, such as the use of pooled quality control (QC) samples, constant sum normalization or standardized sampling protocols were documented in only four studies (Batra et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021; Iftimie et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023). To control for type 1 errors (false positives) in high-dimensional lipidomics data, multiple-testing correction using False Discovery Rate (FDR) or the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was implemented in five studies (55.6%). Furthermore, the confidence levels for lipid annotation ranged from targeted multi-reaction monitoring (MRM) modes to untargeted high-resolution MS/MS workflows (e.g., LipidSearch). Finally, methodological validation approach varied with four studies relied on analytically and clinically validated commercial kits and pipelines (Ferrario et al., 2016; Mosevoll et al., 2023; Neb et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025), whereas the remaining studies utilized internal statistical validation techniques such as cross-validation, CV-ANOVA and permutation testing.

Serum-based studies identified broader lipid changes across triacylglycerols (TAG), glycerophosphocholines (GPC) and sphingolipids (SL). Liu et al. (2024) and Mosevoll et al. (2023) found marked alterations in TAG, LPC and several sphingolipid species with many lipid levels correlating with disease severity, organ failure or bacteremia. Within these cohorts, serum LPC reductions and TAG elevations were repeatedly associated with clinically meaningful outcomes such as progression to organ dysfunction according to Sepsis-3 criteria and disease severity (Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023). Additionally, Chen et al. (2021) further identified a panel of phosphatidylcholine species including PC 18:2_20:4, PC 36:4 and PC 38:6 that were significantly decreased in non-survivors of septic shock Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP) patients with several PCs yielding high AUC values (> 0.85) in ROC analyses. Additionally, Iftimie et al. (2024) observed alterations in HDL characteristics and LDL-TG/LDL-C ratios between bacterial infection versus healthy control. Notably, based on significant results within this specific subset of investigations (n = 4), two serum-based studies showed prognostic biomarker potential (Chen et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024), one demonstrated diagnostic utility (Iftimie et al., 2024) and one explored both prognostic and diagnostic utility simultaneously (Mosevoll et al., 2023).

In plasma-based study, Batra et al. (2022) reported altered plasma TAG and diacylglycerol (DAG) levels in bacterial sepsis–induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) compared to COVID-19 ARDS cohorts and healthy controls. A total of 33 lipid species (31 positive and 2 negative associated) demonstrated significant associations with platelet counts within the bacterial sepsis ARDS group. However, no significant associations were found between these lipid levels and mortality, acute kidney injury (AKI) or PaO2/FiO2 ratios.

Furthermore, Ferrario et al. (2016) noted extensive modulation within the GPC class, which varied distinctively according to the timeline of blood sampling. At Day 1 (D1) blood sampling, the non-survivor of 28-day mortality revealed a decreased of 1 LPC and 2 PC species, alongside increase of 2 PC species. Concurrently, decreased in 3 PC species were found in 90-days mortality. At Day 7 (D7) blood sampling, plasma profiles demonstrated a more pronounced downward trajectory in the non-survivor cohorts; a decreased in 3 LPC and 5 PC species was observed in non-survivor at 28-days mortality, while substantial decreased of 3 LPC and 11 PC species at 90-days mortality.

Specific LPC and PC species demonstrated distinct correlation trend with patients outcomes. At D1, diacyl-phosphatidylcholine (PC aa) C38:1 and C4 were strongly positive correlated with 28-day mortality, whereas PC aa C40:6 and PC ae C38:0 was negatively correlated. At D7, only PC aa C42:4 was positively correlated with 28-day mortality. In contrast, negative correlations were observed for PC aa C40:1, acyl-ether-phosphatidylcholine (PC ae) C40:1, as well as LPC a C18:0, LPC a C29:1 and LPC a C24:0. Other than that, physiological parameters including daily urinary output, mean arterial pressure (MAP) and plasma level of LPC C24:0 were negatively associated with both 28-day and 90-day mortality. Conversely, positive association were identified in cardiovascular component of SOFA score in relation to 28-day mortality, and for patients who underwent renal replacement therapy in relation to 90-day mortality (Ferrario et al., 2016).

Neb et al. (2024) reported extensive shifts across multiple lipid mediators, including increased ceramide and eicosanoid species alongside decreased levels of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), with several other lipid species falling outside normal physiological ranges. In contrast to the extensive observed in complex lipid classes, Nguyen et al. (2025) reported no significant differences in several hydroxylated FFA species when comparing sepsis patients directly against healthy control groups. Similarly, Birner et al. (2024) reported no significant changes in free cholesterol, cholesterol esters or TAG across different infection groups. Notably, within this specific cohort of plasma-based studies (n = 5), four demonstrated diagnostic biomarker potential (Batra et al., 2022; Birner et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025), while and one focused strictly on prognostic utility (Ferrario et al., 2016).

Triacylglycerols (TAG) were the most consistently reported biomarkers within the GL class. Increased levels of TAG in both serum and plasma were consistently associated with disease severity and the onset of multi-organ Failure (MOF)(Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023). Furthermore, TAG demonstrated diagnostic potential in differentiating between bacterial and viral aetiologies (Batra et al., 2022; Mosevoll et al., 2023).

Within the glycerophosphocholine (GPC) class, specific phosphotidylcholine (PC) species demonstrated bidirectional changes and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) showed a consistent decrease across multiple studies. Specific long-chain PCs, including PC 16_18:1; PC aa C38:1 and PC C42:4 were found to be significantly elevated in non survivors (Chen et al., 2021; Ferrario et al., 2016). Conversely, a wider range of PC species showed a decrease in severe cases. This included C18:2_20:4, PC 36:4, PC 38:6 and several diacyl (aa) and acyl-ether (ae) species such as PC aa C40:6, PC ae C38:0, PC aa C40:1 and PC ae C40:1 (Chen et al., 2021; Ferrario et al., 2016). These PC species were characterized as discriminatory diagnostic markers for mortality risk. Additionally, downregulation of LPC was observed across studies (Ferrario et al., 2016; Mosevoll et al., 2023), where lowered LPC levels were identified as consistent markers for bacterial infection, disease severity (MOF) and increased mortality risk.

Sphingolipids (SL) primarily appear in two forms: sphingomyelin (SM) and ceramides. SM demonstrated bidirectional changes, while Mosevoll et al. (2023) reported an increase SM was associated with MOF and bacterial aetiology, studies by Liu et al. (2024) and Neb et al. (2024) observed a significant decrease, linking SM depletion to potential immune dysfunction. Despite these directional differences, SM remains a significant biomarker for both diagnostic and prognostic clinical utility. In contrast, ceramides were consistently reported to increase in both serum and plasma. Ceramides were identified as relevant mediators of pro-inflammatory processes and bacterial aetiology; acting as potential drivers of disease progression and serving as markers for clinical severity.

Overall, Table 6 highlights several lipid classes particularly TAG, PC, LPC, SM and ceramides that were consistently reported across multiple studies. These recurring lipid alterations represent the most robust and reproducible biomarker candidates identified within the current bacterial sepsis lipidomics literature, offering significant potential for future clinical validation in both serum and plasma samples.

Discussions

This systematic review synthesised nine lipidomics studies and demonstrates consistent disruptions across major lipid classes in bacterial sepsis, although the direction and magnitude of these changes varied according to sample type, pre-analytical approach and clinically significant. Serum-based studies more frequently identified prognostically relevant alterations, including marked reductions in LPC and specific PC species, along with consistent elevations in TAG, all of which showed strong associations with disease severity, organ dysfunction and mortality (Table 6). In contrast, plasma-based studies captured broader immunometabolic disturbances including elevations in ceramides, eicosanoids and other inflammatory mediators that were more robust for diagnostic discrimination, although some plasma lipid species showed inconsistent associations across studies. The consistent cross-study biomarkers identified were TAG, LPC, selected PC species, SM and ceramides (Table 6), collectively highlighting a reproducible pattern of dysregulated triacylglycerol metabolism (TAG), glycerophospholipid metabolism (LPC and selected PC species) and sphingolipid metabolism (SM and ceramide) in bacterial sepsis.

Influence of sample type (serum vs. plasma) on lipid profiles

Differences between serum and plasma matrices appeared to influence both lipid class coverage and clinical interpretability (Table 5). Serum studies consistently showed broader alterations across TAG, PC, LPC, SM and ceramides (Batra et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023), a pattern that reflects coagulation-associated lipid release(Burla et al., 2018; Vignoli et al., 2022) and enhanced detection of phospholipase-dependent metabolites in the serum fraction (Kaluarachchi et al., 2018). Plasma studies revealed similar patterns (Batra et al., 2022; Ferrario et al., 2016; Neb et al., 2024), with Nguyen et al. (2025) found no significant differences in hydroxylated FFAs and Birner et al. (2024) found no significant change in APoA-IV, FC, CE and TG. These discrepancies are likely influenced by anticoagulant choice (EDTA tube vs. citrate tube), which is known to differentially affect lipid recovery, sphingolipid stability and enzymatic activity(Ulmer et al., 2020).

The observed higher lipid yield and comparable diversity of lipid classes in serum compared to plasma samples can be attributed to the biological and technical differences in sample preparation between the two matrices. Serum is generated from natural clotting process. During the process, activation of coagulation factors and stimulation of platelets occur leading to release of bioactive lipid mediators, substantially influencing measured lipid abundances. In particular, coagulation has been shown to markedly affect levels of lysophospholipids, sphingo-1-phosphates, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, resolvins and other oxylipins which may elevate or deplete relatively to their in vivo circulating concentrations (Burla et al., 2018; Colas et al., 2014; Dorow et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018; Ono et al., 2013). On another note, enhanced detection of phospholipase-dependent metabolites in the serum fraction is explained by phospholipase A2 activity. During clotting process, hydrolyzes of PC to LPC and free fatty acids, amplifying these signals in serum lipidomics. Serum shows higher lysoPC(16:0), lysoPE(18:0), and lysoPA(20:0) versus plasma, reflecting phospholipase-dependent breakdown absent in anticoagulated samples. This leads to consistent elevations in serum for 37 HDL-related lipoprotein subclasses and VLDL/LDL lipids as reported by Kaluarachchi et al. (2018).

In contrast, plasma is prepared in the presence of anticoagulants such as EDTA, citrate or heparin, which inhibit clot formation and reduce platelet activation. This suppression of coagulation-related pathways may limit the liberation of certain lipid species into the fluid fraction, thereby narrowing the lipidome detectable in plasma(Denery et al., 2011; Ishikawa et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2010; Yu et al., 2011). This may offer advantages in studies aiming to minimize ex vivo alterations and focus on lipid less influenced by coagulation. As a result, plasma may more closely reflect in vivo metabolism and reduce pre-analytical variability (Burla et al., 2018), making it particularly advantageous for diagnostic biomarker discovery in targeted assays and biomarker validation purposes. However, this emphasis on analytical stability introduces a critical translational trade-off, as the suppression of clotting-induced pathways may simultaneously narrow the biological richness of the detectable lipidome. Consequently, plasma matrices might fail to capture low-abundance or transient lipid signals that carry substantial prognosis or mechanistic value (Lehmann, 2021; Vignoli et al., 2022). Nevertheless, previous studies demonstrated that residual platelets in plasma can confound the measured plasma levels of specific lipid species. High variation in residual platelet content is often due to differences in plasma density and viscosity, which are influenced by the erythrocyte sedimentation rate. This condition is varying between individuals and is elevated in many inflammatory conditions (Erikssen et al., 2000; Yin et al., 2017).

These biological and technical differences have important implications when interpreting lipidomic changes in the context of sepsis. As sepsis is characterized by profound immune activation, coagulation dysregulation and metabolic reprogramming, all of these processes can influence circulating lipid profiles (Amunugama et al., 2021; Delano & Ward, 2016; Jarczak et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). The clotting process inherent in serum preparation may accentuate or reveal certain lipid alterations related to platelet activation, endothelial dysfunction or inflammatory mediator release pathophysiological processes that are central to sepsis progression. Previous studies have reported elevated concentrations of lipids such as thromboxane B₂, 12-hydroxy-eicosatetraenoic acid (12-HETE) and 12-hydroxy-eicosapentaenoic acid (12-HEPE) in serum compared to plasma (Ishikawa et al., 2013). These dynamics are consistent with the broader lipid shifts documented in the serum-based sepsis studies, where markers of inflammation, platelet activation during clotting, endothelial dysfunction and metabolic stress were more readily captured.

This divergence highlights the practical trade-off for matrix selection in clinical lipidomics. Choosing plasma prioritizes analytical robustness and true in-vivo physiological snapshot, but risks sacrificing the broader biological spectrum that serum offers via clotting-induces amplification (Vignoli et al., 2022). In sepsis research, where pathophysiological cascades involve intense platelet and endothelial activation, the ‘artificial’ lipid release during serum clotting may paradoxically serve a valuable diagnostic proxy, uncovering hidden mechanistic insights that are otherwise masked in plasma (Amunugama et al., 2021; Greco et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2025). Overall, the difference in utility of sample type is reflected in the patterns observed across the included studies, where serum studies more consistently identified prognostically relevant patterns in TAG, LPC and SL classes, while plasma studies demonstrated stronger discriminatory performance for acute diagnostic lipid markers. However, this divergence must be interpreted with caution, as this may reflects on how these independent studies may originally structured. Consequently, this restricts our capacity to universally generalize the clinical utility based on current literature alone.

Influence of pre-analytical, analytical and statistical methodology on lipidomic data quality

The observed variation in total lipid yield across studies reflects not only biological differences but also methodological inconsistencies. This review highlights the significance role of pre-analytical variables particularly time of sample collection, storage and extraction method in determining the comprehensiveness and reproducibility of lipidomic profiling (Table 4). Immediate processing of samples, ideally within four hours of collection, followed by ultra-low temperature storage at − 80 °C, consistently preserved lipid integrity and minimized degradation, particularly for sensitive species like polyunsaturated fatty acids and oxylipins. By contrast, storage at higher temperatures such as −20 °C, can compromise lipid stability over time (Prentice et al., 2013; Reis et al., 2021; Ulmer et al., 2018). These findings reinforce the necessity for strict adherence to optimal handling protocols to ensure data comparability and robust lipidome coverage across laboratories.

Extraction methodology further shaped lipid detectability, with classical biphasic chloroform–methanol systems such as Bligh and Dyer or Folch remaining widely used due to their broad molecular coverage (Bligh & Dyer, 1959; Folch et al., 1957). However more recent alternative, the methyl-tert-butyl ether (MTBE) method developed byMatyash et al. (2008) offers cleaner phase separation and is particularly advantageous for high-throughput serum lipidomics. Similarly, the butanol–methanol (BuMe/BUME) method has been optimized for plasma and shown to improve recovery of polar and medium-polarity lipids (Löfgren et al., 2012, 2016), suggesting that extraction strategies should be tailored to the sample matrix. Recent benchmarking studies indicate that no single protocol is universally superior: extraction efficiency is lipid-class and matrix dependent and modified Matyash/MTBE or BuMe variants can outperform classical protocols for particular sample types or target analytes (Liebisch et al., 2022; Ulmer et al., 2018). This matrix-dependent optimization is particularly important for targeted lipidomics, where recovery of specific lipid classes is critical for downstream quantitative analyses and biomarker discovery.

Although numerous studies have examined the performance of various lipid extraction protocols for blood plasma and serum, however, comprehensive evaluations of both monophasic and biphasic liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) methods particularly those capable of capturing the full diversity of lipid subclasses and species remains limited (Alshehry et al., 2015; Anwar et al., 2015; Bligh & Dyer, 1959; Furse et al., 2020; Matyash et al., 2008). Accurate evaluation of extraction efficiency is essential for optimizing lipidomic workflows, as the quality of recovered lipids directly influences downstream analyses and the reliability of findings. The efficiency of LLE protocols is shaped by factors such as molecular coverage, total lipid yield and reproducibility, all of which underpin the robustness of the extraction process. Supplementary File 3 showed comparison of monophasic and biphasic LLE in lipidomics. While untargeted mass spectrometry (MS) approaches have been widely applied to assess extraction performance, their limited sensitivity often constrains the detection of low-abundance lipid species and may reduce specificity in discriminating distinct lipid profiles across different tissue types (Bögl et al., 2022; Furse et al., 2020; Löfgren et al., 2016; Matyash et al., 2008; Omar & Zhang, 2023).

Analytical platform selection further shaped the detection landscape with Liquid Chromatography- Mass Spectrometry (LCMS) and Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC–MS) remain the dominant frontline techniques for clinical lipidomics. However, instrument choice necessitates careful balancing of sensitivity, analytical throughput and structural resolution to align with specific study objectives and resource constraints. High-resolution accurate-mass (HRAM) analyzers (Orbitrap, Q-TOF, FT-ICR) excel in untargeted discovery by providing mass accuracy and MS/MS information that improve lipid identification, whereas triple-quadrupole (QQQ) systems deliver superior sensitivity and quantitative precision for targeted assays (Anh et al., 2024; Cajka & Fiehn, 2016; Salihovic et al., 2023). Recent advances including Trapped Ion Mobility Spectrometry (TIMS) combined with Parallel Accumulation-Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) and Field Asymmetric Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) front ends have further expanded orthogonal separation space and annotation confidence while increasing throughput (Hohenwallner et al., 2024; Lerner et al., 2023; Meier et al., 2021).

Crucially, when stratified by methodological similarities, sub-groups of studies within this review offer compelling evidence of partial mutual verification and cross-validation thereby mitigating concerns regarding overall cohort heterogeneity. A prime example is demonstrated by technical alignment between Chen et al. (2021) and Liu et al. (2024). Both investigators employed identical pre-analytical and analytical workflows, utilizing serum collected in gel-separator plain tubes stored at −80 °C, analyzed via untargeted UHPLC coupled with Q-Exactive Orbitrap high-resolution accurate-mass (HRAM) mass spectrometry. Under these tightly matched technical conditions, both studies yielded highly concordant biological findings, specifically demonstrating a reproducible systemic elevation of TAG alongside a coordinated downregulation of polysaturated phosphatidylcholines (e.g., PC 36:4 and PC 38:6) as hallmarks of severe sepsis and mortality risk. Similarly, within plasma based-workflows, Ferrario et al. (2016) and Batra et al. (2022) exhibited technical concordance by utilizing EDTA plasma stored at −80 °C and analyzed on high-sensitivity Sciex triple-quadrupole/QTRAP platforms. Despite variations in specific clinical configurations, both platforms cross-validated pronounced shifts in TAG, PC and LPC during critical sepsis endpoints. Highlighting these highly reproducible lipid signatures under identical pre-analytical conditions, matrix and instrumental environments significantly bolsters structural confidence in these candidates, confirming that they represent genuine biological drivers of bacterial sepsis rather than artifacts of analytical variance.

Another crucial consideration involves the rigorous accounting of confounding factors within the statistical methodologies of the reviewed literature (Table 3). Currently, there is a of stringent confounding control across the evaluated studies. Circulating lipid profiles are highly dynamic networks profoundly susceptible to baseline demographic variations (age and gender) and chronic metabolic comorbidities. Both factors can fundamentally alter systemic lipid transport and inflammatory responses, potentially masking acute and disease-specific pathophysiology (Onoiu et al., 2025; Rout et al., 2025). This biological noise is compounded by the exogenous confounders, particularly medication and dietary histories. Critically ill intensive care unit (ICU) patients routinely receive complex pharmacological regimens, including lipid-altering agents such as statins or lipid-emulsion-formulated sedative. For example, propofol has been reported to artificially distort circulating triglyceride levels and lead to therapeutic artifacts being misidentified as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers (Devaud et al., 2012). Furthermore, the total absence of dietary monitoring or fasting status control across the studies poses a profound challenge, as acute nutritional intake and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) cause immediate, sweeping fluctuations in blood lipid abundances (Hamdan and Puckett 2023; Wendel et al. 2007b). Without standardizing comprehensive confounder tracking and employing multivariable regression adjustments, the clinical utility oof these candidate biomarkers remain restricted.

A critical appraisal of the analytical and statistical frameworks across the reviewed literature further reveals profound gaps in technical standardization and validation that threaten the reproducibility, internal validity and clinical translation of potential sepsis lipid biomarkers (Table 4). Methodologically, internal standard was completely omitted in two untargeted workflows, severely restrict the capacity for quantitative meta-synthesis (Alseekh et al., 2021; Gertsman & Barshop, 2018). Divergent lipid annotation confidence ranging from structurally resolved targeted modes to automated software which introduces high rates of false-positive identifications due to uncurated isobaric interferences (Köfeler et al., 2021). This analytical variance is heavily compounded by the fact that less than half of the studies documented batch-effect mitigation strategies, such as pooled quality control (QC) sample injections or constant sum normalization (Kirwan et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2024). Furthermore, downstream statistical rigor remains inadequate. Nearly 45% of the studies neglected multiple-testing corrections False Discovery Rate (FDR) or the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure despite high risk of Type 1 especially in high-dimensional datasets (Kanduri et al., 2025; Rastahi et al., 2024). Crucially, biomarker validation remains minimal with most studied solely on internal statistic validation techniques (e.g., cross-validation, CV-ANOVA) which increase the risk of data overfitting in independent cohorts (Castaldi et al., 2011; Ramspek et al., 2020). To overcome these methodological and reporting discrepancies, the adoption of standardized consensus guidelines, such as Lipidomics Minimal Reporting Checklist is highly encouraged for future investigations (Kopczynski et al., 2024).

Importantly, community ring trials and inter-laboratory benchmarking (e.g., using NIST SRM-1950) demonstrate that harmonized workflows, standardized reference materials and transparent reporting of MS settings are essential to achieve concordant results across platforms and laboratories (Bowden et al., 2017; Torta et al., 2024). Taken together, these synthesize data emphasize that reproducible clinical lipidomics depends less on a single “best” instrument or extraction method but more on a harmonized end-to-end workflow, from sample collection and extraction to chromatography, MS acquisition and rigorous quality control to ensure data quality, comparability and successful biomarker validation. Overall, these findings highlight that meticulous sample handling, standardized extraction procedures and analytical platforms are critical to maximizing lipid yield and ensuring reproducible lipidomic profiling.

Clinical significance: diagnostic, prognostic and severity-related lipids

Diagnostic utility of lipid alterations in bacterial sepsis

In our study, plasma lipid mediators and selected glycerophospholipids consistently demonstrated diagnostic discrimination in bacterial sepsis cohorts (Table 5). Neb et al. (2024) reporting marked increases in ceramides and eicosanoids alongside decreases in S1P and LPA, reflecting acute inflammatory (Hahnefeld et al. 2020; G. Sun et al. 2023) and endothelial activation (Amunugama et al. 2021). Our findings corroborate prior targeted lipidomic studies demonstrating specific LC and LPC species can discriminate bacterial infections from viral or non-infectious conditions (Pavel et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2019). This is also consistent with broader metabolomics evidence showing that circulating lipid panels can enhance pathogen-level classification beyond conventional biomarkers. Integrated lipid-metabolomic models can identify key lipids such as phosphatidylcholines, to distinguish bacterial from viral infection based on lipid network enrichment analysis confirming fatty acid transfer processes as major modulators of inflammatory host-response. These biomarker panels capture infection-related metabolomic dynamics that are often missed by standard diagnostic tests, consequently aiding precision diagnosis (Belhaj et al. 2021; Saballs et al. 2024).

Conversely, the diagnostic performance was lipid-class dependent with hydroxylated FFAs showed no significant differences (Nguyen et al., 2025), whereas TAG/DAG alterations in bacterial sepsis–associated ARDS differentiated clinical phenotypes and correlated strongly with platelet count but not with mortality or organ dysfunction (Batra et al., 2022). Some classes (e.g., hydroxylated FFAs) showed inconsistent diagnostic signal across plasma studies, highlighting that diagnostic utility is lipid-class and context dependent (Nguyen et al., 2025). Multiple studies have shown that ceramides provide robust signals (AUC > 0.85), but hydroxylated FFAs exhibit high inter-study %CV (> 30%) due to enzymatic instability and matrix effects in plasma. Broader panels integrating TGs and PCs outperform single-class markers like hydroxylated FFAs for pathogen classification (Mecatti et al., 2018; Rischke et al., 2025; Sulaiman et al., 2024). Taken together, ceramide elevation and defined PC/LPC signatures are repeatedly observed as promising diagnostic markers in bacterial sepsis lipidomics, especially in plasma sample.

Prognostic value of lipids in predicting mortality and organ failure

Several lipid species demonstrated strong prognostic value across studies, with marked decreases in PUFA-rich PC species—such as PC 36:4, PC 38:6 and PC 18:2_20:4—predicting mortality (Chen et al., 2021), while elevations PC and sphingomyelin were consistently observed in non-survivors (Ferrario et al., 2016). Serum TAG alterations also correlated with 30- and 90-day mortality (Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023), highlighting neutral lipid dysregulation as an additional prognostic axis. Lipid patterns were equally informative for organ dysfunction, with lower LPC and increased TAG profiles associated with progression to Sepsis-3 organ failure (Mosevoll et al., 2023) and rising ceramide levels linked to microvascular injury and multi-organ impairment (Neb et al., 2024) (Table 5). Independent population analyses also report that specific triglyceride ranges and triglyceride-derived indices correlate with short-term mortality (Huang & Sun, 2024), while ceramide predicts microvascular injury and multi-organ failure in mechanistic and translational work (Chung et al., 2017; Delogu et al., 1999). These convergent findings indicate that lipid panels by combining TAG, LPC, PC and ceramide measures prognostic information beyond single conventional markers.

Lipidomic indicators of disease severity

Across studies, lipid profiles demonstrated clear severity-related patterns, with progressive decreases in LPC and PUFA-containing PC species accompanying transitions from sepsis to septic shock, while ceramides and other sphingolipids increased in parallel with inflammatory intensity; these trends were consistently observed across Sepsis-2, Sepsis-3 and organ dysfunction subgroups. Phenotype-specific analyses further revealed distinct metabolic signatures, with differential TAG/DAG and phophatidylcholine (GPC) shifts in CAP, ARDS and mixed bacterial sepsis cohorts (Batra et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021), alongside severity-linked alterations in HDL and LDL composition reported byIftimie et al., (2024) (Table 5). Severity of illness maps onto reproducible lipidomic patterns: progressive loss of protective PUFA-PCs and many LPCs parallels escalation from sepsis to septic shock, whereas ceramides and certain neutral lipids (TAG/DAG) rise with worsening systemic inflammation and metabolic failure (Amunugama et al., 2021; Muniz-Santos et al., 2023). Serum studies in particular have shown that increased TAG and reduced LPC associate with organ dysfunction onset (Sepsis-3 criteria) and with bacteraemia status (Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023). The directionality of some lipids may be biphasic—elevated at early adaptive phases and depleted in late, exhaustion phases—explaining apparent inconsistencies across cross-sectional cohorts.

Collectively, TAG increases index systemic metabolic reprogramming (lipolysis, hepatic VLDL synthesis and impaired β-oxidation), decreased LPC, bidirectional PC shifts reflect membrane remodelling and immune signalling, and ceramide elevation signals endothelial stress and apoptotic pathways; together these lipid axes capture complementary pathophysiological dimensions that link to diagnosis, severity and outcome (Guo et al., 2024; Muniz-Santos et al., 2023). The recurring observation across the nine included studies that TAGs, LPC/PC subclasses and ceramides associate with clinical parameters (severity, bacteraemia, shock, mortality) supports the development of multi-lipid panels and composite scores for prognostic stratification, but also highlights the pressing need for harmonised pre-analytical and analytical protocols to allow reliable multicentre validation (Bowden et al., 2017).

Consistent lipid biomarkers across studies

Across studies, several lipid classes demonstrated reproducible dysregulation irrespective of population heterogeneity or analytical platform (Table 6). TAG elevations were among the most consistent findings, a biologically plausible pattern given the well-established suppression of lipoprotein lipase activity and shifts in hepatic VLDL synthesis during systemic inflammation (Jinno et al., 2011). Specific phosphatidylcholine (PC) species exhibited similarly consistent bidirectional patterns: saturated and monosaturated PCs (such as PC 16:0_18:1) were frequently elevated, whereas polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing PCs (such as PC 18:2_20:4, PC 36:4 and PC 38:6) were consistently reduced in severe sepsis and among non-survivors. This divergence aligns with known mechanisms of oxidative degradation and impaired membrane remodelling in critical illness (Lin et al., 2025; Nagar et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, ceramide elevations observed in both serum and plasma, further highlight activation of sphingomyelinase pathways that contribute to endothelial injury and apoptosis (Feng et al., 2019; Krones et al., 2021). LPC showed decreased trend in two included studies reflects the rapid consumption during inflammatory signalling (Gonçalves et al., 2012; Sevastou et al., 2013). Taken together, TAG, PC, LPC, SM and ceramides emerged as the most reproducible biomarker candidates for bacterial sepsis. Among these, ceramides and polyunsaturated PC depletion showing strong prognostic value through associations with mortality, organ failure and SOFA scores, whereas TAG and LPC demonstrated both diagnostic and prognostic relevance. These convergent lipidomic patterns align with recent multi-omics work indicating that lipid signatures may outperform conventional inflammatory biomarkers in predicting septic shock and mortality (Sulaiman et al., 2024 b; Sun et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). The highlighted consistent lipid biomarkers will be discussed in details hereafter.

Triacylglycerols (TAG)

TAG increases were consistently reported in three independent studies across different populations (Batra et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024; Mosevoll et al., 2023) all of which were rated medium to high quality based on NOS criteria (Table 2). Triacylglycerol (TAG) elevation appears as one of the most consistent lipid signatures in bacterial sepsis, reflecting a shared pattern of cytokine-driven metabolic dysregulation. Inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 enhance hepatic TAG synthesis, stimulate VLDL secretion and promote peripheral lipolysis, resulting in elevated circulating TAGs. Several investigations report this trend of triglycerides were significantly higher in septic patients than in healthy controls in a recent metabolic review (Birner et al., 2024; Karampela et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024), and a large MIMIC cohort showed that elevated TAG levels were strongly associated with 30-day mortality, with the highest risk observed in the 74–103 mg/dL range (Huang & Sun, 2024). Earlier clinical data also support the prognostic relevance of TAGs, with increased admission and day-1 TAG concentrations predicting mortality (Lee et al., 2015). Furthermore, contemporary lipidomics studies demonstrate similar patterns, with untargeted plasma profiling revealing systemic lipidome shifts consistent with TAG accumulation in critical illness (Nguyen et al., 2025), and outcome-based analyses linking higher TAGs to poor clinical outcomes and unresolved inflammation (Cetinkaya et al., 2014; Huang & Sun, 2024). Collectively, these findings reinforce TAG elevation as a reproducible marker of metabolic stress in sepsis with potential diagnostic and prognostic utility.

Glycerophospholipid (PC and LPC)

Across reviewed studies, a subset of glycerophospholipids particularly selected phosphatidylcholine (PC) and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) demonstrate reproducible biomarkers in bacterial sepsis, exhibiting distinct context-specific and species-dependent alterations (Table 6). For PC subclasses, a prominent bidirectional behavior was identified as a prognostic indicator for mortality risk in non-survivors. Specifically, certain saturated and monounsaturated species, including PC 16_18:1 PC aa C38:1 and PC aa C42:4, were consistently up-regulated in non-survivors across the cohorts of Chen et al. (2021) and Ferrario et al. (2016). Biologically, this elevation reflects hepatic acute-phase remodelling, as cytokine stimulation enhances hepatic lipoprotein synthesis to support immune cell membrane turnover and repair during early host responses (Amunugama et al., 2021). Earlier work in febrile children also reported increased levels of PC(16:0/18:2), PC(18:0/18:1), PC(18:0/18:2), PC(16:0/16:0) and PC(16:0/18:1) in bacterial versus viral infections, demonstrating diagnostic utility of PC elevations (Wang et al., 2019). Conversely, polyunsaturated PC species, such as PC 18:2 20:4, PC 36:4, PC 38:6, PC aa C40:6 PC ae C38:0, PC aa C40:1 and PC ae C40:1, were down-regulated in non-survivors within the same studies (Chen et al., 2021; Ferrario et al., 2016). Based on a study done by (Qin et al., 2025), significantly decreased plasma phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) levels in patients with sepsis-associated encephalopathy were correlated with inflammatory activation and neuroinflammation. This further supported by Sun et al. (2025) who reported that plasma lipidomic analysis also revealed reduced PC and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) levels in sepsis patients compared to controls, with these reductions correlating with increased severity and immune dysregulation. This marked depletion of specific PC species under intensified systematic inflammation points to oxidative stress-mediated lipid peroxidation and pathogen-driven phospholipid degradation, potentially mediated by bacterial phospholipase D targets PC membrane structures and trigger cellular damage (Wu et al., 2025).

In contrast to the bidirectional nature of PCs, LPC species demonstrate down-regulation across the critical cohorts evaluated by Mosevoll et al. (2023) and Ferrario et al. (2016), serving as valuable diagnostic and prognostic markers for bacterial aetiology, multi-organ failure (MOF) and high mortality risk. This aligned with recent study in bacterial community-acquired pneumonia sepsis showed that although many LPC species declined with severity, a subset remained preserved or mildly elevated (Chouchane et al., 2024). Bacterial infection activates phospholipase A₂ (PLA₂), which hydrolyses membrane phosphatidylcholines and generates LPC species. Regulation of PLA₂ activation can consequently elevate circulating LPC prior to the onset of severe systematic inflammation that induces lipid depletion. This further depletes membrane PC stores and impaired cellular integrity. This process was explained by Sevastou et al. (2013), they reported that rapid LPC consumption by autotaxin to LPA sustains signalling but exacerbates systematic lipid loss in cachexia-linked inflammation. This accelerated consumption, compounded by impaired hepatic lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) synthesis during severe sepsis, drives the profound systemic lipid loss observed in non-survivors, marking LPC depletion as a reliable signature of critical clinical endpoints.

Sphingolipids (sphingimyelins and ceramides)

Sphingolipid (SM) dysregulation emerged as one of the most consistent findings across serum and plasma analyses (Table 6). Plasma SM levels exhibited distinct bidirectional trajectories across studies, heavily dependent on the clinical context and infection stage. Mosevoll et al. (2023) reported a significant increase in circulating SM, positioning it as a key diagnostic and prognostic marker linked with multi-organ failure (MOF) and bacterial etiology. Conversely, investigations by Liu et al. (2024) and Neb et al. (2024) observed a significant decrease in SM levels, associating this depletion with disease severity and potential immune dysfunction.

Plasma SM levels showed bidirectional changes decreased in Liu et al. (2024) and Neb et al. (2024) but increased in Ferrario et al. (2016) and Mosevoll et al. (2023). Previous study reported that plasma sphingomyelin levels show bidirectional changes in bacterial sepsis, with some SM species elevated and others decreased depending on disease severity, reflecting complex ceramide metabolism shifts and inflammatory regulation (Toro et al. 2023). This further supported by community-acquired pneumonia-attributable sepsis study which showed 58% of plasma lipids including several SM species decreased, but others increased, correlating variably with inflammation and clinical severity scores (Chouchane et al. 2024). In contrast, plasma sphingomyelins elevated in bacterial sepsis caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, persisting after adjustment for clinical severity and also reported on disease progression in some sepsis cohorts (Y. Sun et al. 2025; Xia et al. 2024). These studies collectively suggest plasma SM levels in bacterial sepsis are dynamically regulated, showing both increases and decreases linked to infection stage, severity, inflammation, and host immune-metabolic responses.

The intricate bidirectional flux and metabolic diversion of sphongomyelins are fundamentally governed by the “sphingolipid rheostat”, a critical enzymatic rheostatic balance that regulates cell fate by shifting the cellular equilibrium between pro-survival structural sphingomyelins and bioactive, pro-inflammatory ceramides. During acute bacterial sepsis, this rheostat is heavily skewed toward ceramide generation, driving consistent increase of ceramide level is studies reported by Neb et al. (2024) and Mosevoll et al. (2023). The increasing trend supporting their role in endothelial injury (Claus & Graeler, 2021), cell death pathways (Chung & Claus, 2021) and immune dysfunction in sepsis (Chouchane et al., 2024; Chung & Claus, 2021). The rheostatic shift is propelled by the hyperactive sphingomyelinase (ASM) in sepsis patients, resulting in breakdown of sphingomyelin and elevation of ceramide in endothelial cells (Li et al., 2019). The study postulate that ASM/ceramide system is involved in pathogenesis and cellular protection in bacterial sepsis caused by Staphylococcus aureus. Furthermore, a study on COVID-19 and bacterial sepsis showed that increased ASM activity leads to rapid ceramide generation, which is linked to cell activation, damage, apoptosis, and inflammation. Increased serum ASM activity distinguished intensive care patients from healthy controls, reflecting sphingolipid reprogramming in sepsis (Abusukhun et al., 2021). Nonetheless, the consistent elevation of ceramides across studies strengthens their potential as prognostic biomarkers, especially for organ failure and shock progression.

Limitations & future recommendations

In this systematic review, most included studies were assessed as high quality based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) scoring system (Table 2), with seven studies scoring ≥ 7 stars. Nevertheless, several intrinsic clinical and methodological limitations must be acknowledged.

A primary limitation of the current literature is the predominance of small sample sizes across several investigations, with some cohorts enrolling a highly restricted number of septic patients (e.g., n = 9). In our screening protocol, sample size was deliberately omitted as a strict exclusion criterion. This was a necessary methodological decision to comprehensively map the pioneering and emerging landscape of high-resolution clinical lipidomics in sepsis, which could otherwise be severely restricted by arbitrary size thresholds. Nevertheless, this approach poses significant challenge to the interpretation of the synthesized data. Such constrained sample sizes inherently compromise statistical stability, rendering p-values highly volatile and elevating the risk of false-positive discoveries (Type 1 errors) driven by individual patient outliers. Notably, to compensate for these small numbers and mitigate the influence of confounding clinical noise, Neb et al. (2024) enhanced the mathematical rigor of their findings by elevating the threshold for significant lipid regulation to a ± 2-fold change relative to the 95% Normal Reference Limit (NRL 95%). Furthermore, because sepsis is a highly heterogenous syndrome characterized by diverse pathogen aetiologies and host immune responses, these micro-cohorts severely restrict external validity. The distinct lipidomic alterations identified in a limited group of patients cannot account for extensive clinical confounding factors (e.g.: age, gender, baseline metabolism comorbidities and primary infection sites). Ultimately, these small sample sizes and heterogenous populations critically limit generalisation ability of the findings to the broader septic population.

Another critical challenge during our appraisal involves residual clinical confounding and baseline patient heterogeneity across the included studies. Although the majority of the investigations demonstrated well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, complete control over non-sepsis induce lipid variations remains an inherent limitation of human clinical cohorts. Specifically, none of the studies could actively account for short-term dietary lipid intake prior admission or the concurrent use of lipid lowering medications, both of which alter the baseline of circulating lipidome. Rather than being a artifact of study design, the data gap is primarily attributable to the acute and emergent nature if sepsis presentations, where gathering meticulous pre-admission lifestyle and medication histories is often secondary to immediate life-saving interventions. Furthermore, the management of metabolic comorbidities was inconsistent across the literature, while some investigators strictly excluded baseline diseases known to heavily disrupt lipid metabolism such as diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, kidney failure and hypothyroidism, while others permitted these populations into their cohorts. This discrepancy introduces a major confounding layer, as these chronic conditions may mask or artificially skew the acute lipidomic shifts driven purely by bacterial sepsis, thereby complicating cross-study synthesis.

Notably, differences in diagnostic criteria (Sepsis-1, Sepsis-2, and Sepsis-3) were acknowledged as a potential source of heterogeneity, since biomarker profiles may vary according to the criteria applied. During data synthesis, studies were categorized according to the sepsis definition they employed and this information was explicitly reported in the evidence tables. Where possible, findings were interpreted in light of the diagnostic framework used with attention given to overlaps or differences across criteria. However, due to limited study numbers in some categories, a pooled comparison stratified by diagnostic criteria was not feasible. Instead, the variability was highlighted as an important limitation and a consideration for future research.

Other than that, the wide variation in blood sampling timelines across the synthesized studies represents a notable limitation. While some studies performed lipid profiling immediately upon hospital or ICU admission, others collected within a broader window ranging from 24 to 72 h post admission. As sepsis is a dynamic syndrome characterized by rapid metabolic reprogramming, lipid concentrations can fluctuate significantly within this 24 to 72 h interval. These dynamics are often heavily influenced by early therapeutic interventions such as fluid resuscitation, antibiotic administration or nutritional support. This temporal heterogeneity introduces pre-analytical variability and may confound between-group comparisons. Future systematic efforts should benefit from more standardized, longitudinal sampling protocols to accurately capture the kinetic trajectory of the lipidome during sepsis progression.

In addition, the pool of high-quality, directly comparative studies remains small constrained by heterogeneity in pre-analytical methods, analytical platforms and reporting standards. This methodological diversity complicates the synthesis of findings. Furthermore, restricting inclusion to English-language publications may introduces language bias, potentially excluding valuable findings published in other languages.

Future research is suggested to prioritise direct head-to-head comparisons of serum and plasma collected from the same patients to determine which matrix offers the most biologically reliable and clinically informative lipidomic signatures. Such studies are essential for resolving matrix-dependent discrepancies observed in TAG, GPC and SL would clarify whether serum’s broader lipid diversity or plasma’s stability better supports different clinical applications. In parallel, rigorous validation of the top biomarker candidates especially TAG, GPC and SL subclasses across large, diverse and multi-centre cohorts are urgently needed to establish their diagnostic and prognostic utility.

To advance the field, future lipidomic studies in sepsis must adopt harmonized protocols across all stage of the workflow, from sample collection to data analysis. The pre-registration of protocols using “Lipidomics Minimal Reporting Checklist” proposed by Dominik et al. (2024) and inclusion of quality-control materials (e.g., NIST SRM 1950) are also essential to strengthen methodological rigor and enhance translational potential (Bowden et al., 2017; Torta et al., 2024). Condensing key experimental information into a common terminology through such checklists will significantly enhance the field’s consistency, comparability and repeatability, ultimately increase transparency and accelerate the translation of lipidomic biomarkers into clinical sepsis management. Furthermore, collaborative multicentre studies using standard operating procedures for pre-analytical handling, extraction methods and analytical platforms would significantly improve reproducibility and cross-study comparability (Burla et al., 2018). Conclusions

Across both serum and plasma-based studies, consistent dysregulation of TAG, PC, LPC, SM and ceramides emerged as potential lipid biomarker candidates in bacterial sepsis. Both serum and plasma represent viable analytical matrices for clinical sepsis lipidomics. However, neither can be regarded as universally superior as the optimal choice depends on the study objectives and sampling constraints. Within the limited scope of the reviewed literature, a preliminary trend suggests that serum-based lipidomics may be frequently suited for prognostic assessment, capturing broader systemic alterations across lipid classes that were associated with disease severity, bacteraemia status and mortality risk. Conversely, plasma-based studies were appeared to pivot primarily toward diagnostic applications, focusing on the early discrimination of acute infection states. While the absolute lipid yields in plasma is often more conservative, they may offer a more physiologically stable presentation of the acute circulating lipidome.

Nevertheless, these matrix-specific observations must be interpreted with caution. The substantial methodological heterogeneity, divergent analytical platforms and lack of confounding control identified across the current primary studies remain major limitations that prelude definitive generalizations, Ultimately, these finding underscore an immediate need for the widespread adoption of harmonized end-to-end workflows and standardized reporting frameworks (such as the Lipidomics Minimal Reporting Checklist) to enhance cross-laboratory reproducibility, minimize technical artifacts and successfully accelerate the clinical translation of lipid-based diagnostic and prognostic tools in sepsis management.
