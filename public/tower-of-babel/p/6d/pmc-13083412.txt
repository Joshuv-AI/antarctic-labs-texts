Myconanotechnology: evaluation of Ag-Cu bimetallic nanoparticles synthesized by Ganoderma aff. australe against pathogens and cancer cells
Authors: Edwis R García-Villavicencio, O Wilder Montiel-Lucio, Eduardo Lira-Díaz, Raúl A Morales-Luckie, Armando Sunny, Cristina Burrola-Aguilar, María Elena Estrada-Zúñiga, César Díaz-Talamantes, Carmen Zepeda-Gómez, Juan J Acevedo Fernández, María G González-Pedroza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports the biogenic synthesis of silver–copper (Ag/Cu) bimetallic nanoparticles using an aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe collected in the State of Mexico. Taxonomic identification of the fungus was confirmed through basidiospore morphology. Mycochemical screening revealed the presence of alkaloids, flavonoids, reducing sugars, quinones, and coumarins in the extract, suggesting their involvement as reducing and stabilizing agents in nanoparticle formation. Structural characterization by UV–Vis, FTIR, SEM–EDS, and TEM confirmed the successful synthesis of quasi‑spherical Ag/Cu nanoparticles with an average diameter of 17.15 nm and a composition predominantly of copper (87.84%) with silver (12.16%). Antibacterial assays demonstrated promising inhibitory activity against both Gram‑positive (Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram‑negative (Escherichia coli) strains, with formulation M2‑3‑3 exhibiting the most consistent performance. Cytotoxicity assays (MTT) revealed selective anticancer activity of the nanoparticles, particularly against glioblastoma cell lines (A‑172 and U‑87 MG), while showing reduced cytotoxicity toward non‑cancerous fibroblasts (HDFn and Detroit 551). These findings highlight G. aff. australe‑mediated Ag/Cu nanoparticles as a sustainable and effective platform with dual antibacterial and anticancer potential. The study underscores the relevance of myconanotechnology for developing eco‑friendly nanomaterials with biomedical applications.Keywords: Nanomaterials, Myconanotechnology, Organic compounds, Ganoderma aff. australe, Antibacterial activity

Introduction

Bacterial resistance has become an increasing global health concern, driving the search for new therapeutic approaches (Patini et al. 2020; Varela et al. 2021). In this context, the evaluation of the antibacterial potential of nanostructured systems has emerged as a crucial area of research. The synthesis of Ag/Cu nanoparticles from aqueous extracts of natural resources such as fungi has garnered interest due to their potential antibacterial properties (Valodkar et al. 2011; Jogaiah et al. 2019; López-Ubaldo et al. 2020). These nanoparticles have demonstrated efficacy against a variety of bacteria, suggesting new therapeutic strategies to combat bacterial resistance. Additionally, recent findings indicate that these nanoparticles may possess selective cytotoxic activity against various cancer cell lines, broadening their potential therapeutic applications. This study will be conducted in the State of Mexico, where environmental conditions favor the growth of Ganoderma aff. australe, and aims not only to explore its antibacterial and anticancer potential but also to integrate traditional knowledge with modern scientific research.

Ganoderma genus has long been valued as a natural resource due to its rich composition of bioactive compounds, such as triterpenes, polysaccharides, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids, which are associated with various therapeutic properties (Peng and Qiu 2018; Cateni et al. 2022). The emergence of myconanotechnology, a field that uses fungi to synthesize nanoparticles in an environmentally friendly way, has brought renewed focus to these organic compounds. This innovative approach harnesses the natural metabolites produced by fungi, such as Ganoderma, to biologically mediate the synthesis of nanoparticles, offering a green alternative to traditional physicochemical methods. The resulting Ag/Cu nanoparticles derived from aqueous extracts of fungi have sparked interest due to their dual therapeutic potential: antibacterial activity against resistant pathogens (Paszkiewicz et al. 2016; Nowak et al. 2016; Sharma et al. 2021) and selective cytotoxicity against various cancer cell lines, including Caco-2, HT-29, MCF7, A-172, and U-87 MG, with particular efficacy observed in glioblastoma cell lines. Notably, these nanoparticles demonstrate selectivity toward cancerous cells while exhibiting lower toxicity in non-cancerous cell lines like HDFn and Detroit 551, suggesting their potential for developing targeted therapeutic approaches. While species like Ganoderma lucidum and Ganoderma applanatum have been well-studied for this purpose (Moradali et al. 2006; Luangharn et al. 2017; Ahmad et al. 2021), research on Ganoderma aff. australe, native to the State of Mexico where environmental conditions favor its growth, remains limited. This underscores the need for further exploration of this underexplored natural resource. The objective of this study is to taxonomically characterize Ganoderma aff. australe, identify biochemical compounds with antibacterial and anticancer activity, and evaluate the effectiveness of Ag/Cu nanoparticles derived from its aqueous extract, establishing the foundation for the responsible utilization of this species in the development of alternative antibacterial systems and potential cancer treatments.

Materials and methods

Collect and taxonomic identification

The fruiting bodies were collected in Hermenegildo Galeana Park in the State of Mexico (19.005223, −99.610514), through targeted sampling with information obtained from residents. They were then taken to the Mycology Laboratory of the Center for Research in Biotic Resources of the Autonomous University of the State of Mexico. The collection of basidiocarps is supported by collection permit No. SPARN/DGVS/00666/22. For the identification and counting of spores, 50 spores from the collected basidiocarps were selected and observed under an optical microscope. The spores were measured for size using a microscopy Motic BA310E with the software Motic Images plus 3.0, and their shape was described. The presence of a double membrane characteristic of the genus Ganoderma was confirmed, and the ornamentation on the spore membrane was observed and described. The morphological characteristics were compared with descriptions from De Oliveira Campos et al. (2024) and Muñoz et al. (2022) for species confirmation. Identification was confirmed through microscopic examination of basidiospore morphology, including measurement of the double membrane (exospore and endospore), spore ornamentation, and comparison of basidiocarp macroscopic features with published descriptions.

Molecular characterization

Molecular identification was performed through DNA extraction from fresh tissue of the basidiocarp context sample using standardized thermolysis protocols. Two regions of ribosomal DNA were amplified: the ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) region using primers ITS5 (5’-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3’) and ITS4 (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’), and the LSU (Large Subunit) region using primers LROR (5’-ACCCGCTGAACTTAAGC-3’) and LR5 (5’-TCCTGAGGGAAACTTCG-3’). Following DNA extraction, genes were amplified using PCR technique, and amplicons were visualized by horizontal electrophoresis in 1.6% agarose gel. Once successful amplifications free of contamination were verified, samples were sent for sequencing. Sequences were read bidirectionally (forward and reverse), and consensus sequences were generated. The obtained sequences were edited, assembled, and compared against the GenBank database of NCBI through BLAST analysis to determine the taxonomic affinity of the specimens. For species-level determination, specialized taxonomic keys from Ryvarden and Melo (2014) for neotropical polypores, morphological descriptions from Moncalvo (2000) for the genus Ganoderma, and molecular identification keys based on ITS sequences from Zhou et al. (2015) were utilized (Moncalvo 2000; Ryvarden and Melo 2014; Zhou et al. 2015).

Process for obtaining the aqueous extract

The fresh fruiting bodies were initially rinsed three times with distilled water to remove debris, followed by surface sterilization using absolute ethyl alcohol (99.8%) for 2 min to prevent microbial contamination. The sterilized mushrooms were air-dried at room temperature (25 °C) for six to seven days until constant weight was achieved. The dried material was then ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle and sieved through a mesh size of 0.5 mm. For extract preparation, two concentrations were prepared: 0.5 g and 1.0 g of powdered material were added to 50 ml of distilled water (JVC Laboratories) in separate flasks. The mixtures were heated to boiling point (93 °C) for five minutes under constant stirring. After cooling to room temperature, the extracts were filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper and stored at 4 °C until use within 24 h.

Qualitative analysis of mycochemicals

Qualitative mycochemical screening of the Ganoderma aff. australe extract was performed using classical phytochemical test protocols. The analysis included detection of alkaloids, flavonoids, cyanogenic glycosides, reducing sugars, saponins, tannins, quinones, coumarins, cardiac glycosides, and sesquiterpene lactones. Each metabolite class was identified using specific qualitative reactions validated in phytochemical methodology. Alkaloids were tested using Dragendorff and Sonneschein reagents, following standard precipitation-based identification methods documented for preliminary screening of plant secondary metabolites. Flavonoids were assessed through the Shinoda reaction and NaOH-induced color change, both widely accepted for detecting flavonoid cores. Cyanogenic glycosides were evaluated using HCl acidification followed by chloroform extraction, while reducing sugars were identified via Fehling’s and Benedict’s colorimetric assays. Saponins were detected using the foam stability test and the Liebermann–Burchard reaction, whereas tannins were revealed through gelatin precipitation and ferric chloride color complexation. Quinones were tested using ammonium hydroxide and sulfuric acid, and coumarins were screened via the Erlich reagent and ammonium hydroxide under UV exposure. These qualitative methods follow established phytochemical screening procedures described by Maheshwaran et al. (2024) and the comprehensive overview of classical qualitative assays provided by Shaikh and Patil (2020) (Shaikh and Patil 2020; Maheshwaran et al. 2024).

Biosynthesis of Silver/Copper nanoparticles with extract of Ganoderma aff. australe

Following the extraction, different volumes of the aqueous extract at concentrations of 0.5 g/50 mL and 0.5 g/100 mL were placed into sterile test tubes. Stock solutions of 1 × 10⁻³ M AgNO₃ (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 × 10⁻³ M CuSO₄ (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared fresh in distilled water immediately before use. A series of 20 experiments were conducted, varying the volumetric ratios of extract, AgNO₃, and CuSO₄. Based on preliminary UV-Vis screening, three formulations were selected for detailed characterization: M2-3-3 (2 mL extract + 3 mL AgNO₃ + 3 mL CuSO₄), M3-2-2 (3 mL extract + 2 mL AgNO₃ + 2 mL CuSO₄), and M5-3-3 (5 mL extract + 3 mL AgNO₃ + 3 mL CuSO₄). The reactions were stored for 6 h, during which visual color changes from pale yellow to light amber were monitored as a qualitative indicator of nanoparticle formation.

Characterization

Characterization of optical properties of Ag/Cu NPs

The optical properties of the nanoparticles were examined using a VELAB Ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (VE-5100UV, USA). A 3 ml aliquot from each sample was placed in glass cells. Distilled water was used as a blank for comparison, and the samples were analyzed over a wavelength range of 650 nm to 300 nm.

Characterization of the main functional groups present in the extract of G. aff. australe and Ag/Cu NPs

An infrared spectrophotometry unit (TENSOR 27, GER) was used to determine the vibrational modes of the bonds in the organic compounds within the extract. The Ag/Cu NPs sample and a sample of the aqueous extract were placed in separate test tubes and allowed to evaporate at room temperature. After evaporation, the resulting solids were collected and placed in the equipment’s support to measure and obtain the transmittance spectra in the range of 4000 cm⁻¹ to 500 cm⁻¹.

Morphological and structural characterization of Ag/Cu NPs

The structural properties of the formed nanostructures, including morphology, particle size, and distribution, were characterized using a JEOL-2100 200 kV Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM). A drop of the Ag/Cu NPs sample was placed on a rack to dry, then introduced to the sample holder and assembled in the equipment. Additionally, a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) was employed for chemical mapping of the main elements present, along with an EDS analysis to identify and determine them.

Chemical mapping of Ag/Cu NPs

The chemical mapping of the nanoparticles was conducted using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) with a JEOL SEM-EDS unit (JSM-IT100, JAP), operating at a magnification of 1500x and 12 mm from the sample. Fifty drops of the Ag/Cu NPs sample were evaporated at 50 °C on a 1 cm diameter cylindrical aluminum support. The support was then placed in the equipment under vacuum at 12 mm, and the analysis was performed. Various regions of the sample were examined, with the optimal region being analyzed by EDS.

Antibacterial activity

Kirby-Bauer

Antibacterial activity was evaluated using the Kirby-Bauer agar disk diffusion method. Petri dishes containing Müller Hinton agar were inoculated with bacterial suspensions of Escherichia coli (ATCC-25922) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC-25923), with turbidity matching the 0.5 McFarland standard, equivalent to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Filter paper disks impregnated with aliquots of each sample were placed on the agar. The dishes were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters.

Broth microdilution method

The antibacterial activity assays followed the Performance Standards for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Weinstein 2019). Ag/Cu NPs were tested against human pathogenic strains, including Escherichia coli (ATCC-25922) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC-25923), to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum effective concentration (MEC). The bacterial suspension was prepared to match the turbidity of a 0.5 McFarland standard, equivalent to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. In each well of the microplate, 100 µl of Mueller–Hinton broth was added, followed by 100 µl of Ag/Cu NPs. Serial dilutions were performed starting from the first row. A negative control (broth and microorganisms) and a sterile control (broth only) were included in the assay. Each well was then inoculated with 10 µl of bacterial suspension, resulting in a final concentration of approximately 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. The assays were performed in triplicate for each concentration and strain, and the inoculated microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Cell culture conditions and MTT assay

For this work, we employ cancerous and normal cell lines. Cancerous cell lines include the Caco-2 (colon cancer; ATCC, HTB-37), HT-29 (colon cancer, ATCC, HTN-38), MCF7 (breast cancer, ATCC, HTB-22), A-172 (glioblastoma, ATCC, CRL-1620) and U-87 MG (glioblastoma, ATCC, HTB-14) cell lines. Fibroblast cell lines HDFn and Detroit 551 (ATCC, PCS-201-010 and CCL-110, respectively) were used to control non-cancerous cells. All cell lines were growth with DMEM/F12 medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium F-12; Gibco, cat. 12500), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest, cat. S1650), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco, cat. 15240062), 1% glutamine (Gibco, cat. 25030081), 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco, cat. 11140050), and 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco, cat. 11360070). All the cells were maintained under standard culture conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

For the MTT assay, 4,500 cells per well of each cell line were seeded in a 96-well plate and allowed 24 h to adhere to the bottom. The concentrations of each nanoparticle solution were determined based on the amount of aqueous extract used to synthesize the nanoparticles. Additionally, the aqueous extract was used as control. See Table 1 for the tested evaluations. All solutions were incubated with the cells for 72 h. After incubation, the cells were rinsed with PBS; fresh medium was added and then incubated for an additional 4 h with 10 µL of MTT (5 mg/ml in PBS). Subsequently, 100 µl/well of DMSO was added, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm. Three independent experiments were carried out in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data on the inhibition zones of S. aureus and E. coli were analyzed using R. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare the diameters of the zones across different samples, followed by a Tukey multiple comparisons test to identify significant differences between groups. Boxplots were generated to visualize data distribution, and the assumptions of ANOVA (normality and homogeneity of variances) were verified using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. QQ and residual plots were used to ensure the data met statistical requirements. Additionally, for MTT assay a one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey multiple comparisons test was performed.

To evaluate the effect of Ag/Cu NPs biosynthesized from G. aff. australe and its extracts on bacterial growth, statistical analyses were performed in R. Normality and homoscedasticity assumptions were checked using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. An ANOVA was then conducted to identify significant differences between treatment groups. Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons between treatments (nanoparticles, extracts, and control), with a significance level of 5%. Cohen’s d statistic was calculated to quantify the effect size of the differences observed between treatments. Finally, the results were visualized using box-and-scatter plots, showing both observed values and model predictions.

Results and discussion

Taxonomic identification of Ganoderma aff. australe

The fruiting bodies collected in Hermenegildo Galeana Park (19.005223, −99.610514) were subjected to a thorough morphological analysis based on updated literature on species of the genus Ganoderma. Using microscopic preparation techniques with potassium hydroxide and Congo red, a precise identification of the morphological characteristics of the fruiting bodies was achieved, both at the macroscopic and microscopic levels. The results revealed a notable concordance with the literary description of Ganoderma aff. australe, highlighting its perennial, pileate, semi-stipitate basidiocarps with a wood-like consistency. The upper surface, flat and furrowed, presents a distinctive crust that evolves from an initial dull brown color to darker tones with age, while the pores show a transition from creamy white to ochraceous or pale gray tones as illustrated in Fig. 1A and B.

Basidiospore measurements confirmed their ellipsoid morphology with truncated apex, double membrane, and characteristic ornamentation. Average basidiocarp dimensions were length 19.6 cm, width 14.35 cm, and edge height 3.1 cm, consistent with G. aff. australe descriptions in the literature.

Ganoderma aff. australe, a fungus belonging to the G. applanatum complex, can be distinguished from G. applanatum through microscopic examination, particularly by its larger basidiospores. The results obtained in this study show a remarkable concordance with the morphological characteristics described in the literature for Ganoderma australe. The detailed measurements of the basidiocarps, including length, width and rim height, are consistent with the observations reported in the paper by De Oliveira Campos et al. (2024), where Ganoderma fruiting bodies with a similar structure and a woody consistency are described (De Oliveira Campos et al. 2024). Furthermore, the microscopic characteristics of the spores, such as the ellipsoidal morphology with truncated apex and the double membrane, match the detailed descriptions in the study by Muñoz et al. (2022), which also highlights the importance of these characteristics for the accurate identification of species of Ganoderma (Muñoz-Castiblanco et al. 2022). Spore measurements, including exospore and endospore length and width, as well as interspace, reinforce the accurate identification of Ganoderma aff. australe, aligning with the data presented in both references. These findings consolidate the morphological identification of this species and underline the consistency of the methods used in this study with current scientific standards.

Molecular confirmation of species identity

DNA extraction from basidiocarp sample was successfully performed using standardized protocols. Both the ITS region (using ITS5/ITS4 primers) and the LSU region (using LROR/LR5 primers) were successfully amplified. Bidirectional sequencing with both primers enabled the generation of high-quality consensus sequences for phylogenetic analysis. BLAST analysis of the consensus sequences against the GenBank database revealed that the samples correspond to the genus Ganoderma, with similarity levels suggesting affinity to G. aff. australe. However, given that base concordance is not exactly 100%, additional taxonomic work is required for detailed comparison with sequences from type material of the phylogenetically closest species. This situation reflects the known taxonomic complexity within the genus Ganoderma, where the existence of multiple sequences in GenBank attributed to the same species but with significant genetic variations requires critical evaluation. Definitive species-level identification necessitates the integration of molecular data with macro- and microscopic morphological characteristics, comparing with type specimens or reference sequences validated by the scientific community. The molecular characterization, combined with the detailed morphological analysis described above (basidiocarp dimensions, basidiospore measurements showing exospore dimensions of 9.9 × 6.4 μm and endospore dimensions of 7.9 × 4.8 μm), provides robust taxonomic confirmation of our specimens as Ganoderma aff. australe, supporting the validity of using this species for subsequent nanoparticle biosynthesis experiments.

The qualitative mycochemical analysis of the aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe revealed the presence of several bioactive compounds represented in Table 2. Alkaloids, flavonoids, reducing sugars, quinones, and coumarins were detected, indicating their potential contribution to the extract’s bioactivity. Conversely, cyanogenic glycosides, saponins, and tannins were absent. These findings suggest that the aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe possesses a diverse array of mycochemicals, which may be responsible for its observed pharmacological properties.

Biosynthesis of Ag/Cu NPs

In the biosynthesis of Ag/Cu NPs, a series of 20 experiments were conducted, varying the volumes of the extract and the metal salts. Among these, samples M2-3-3 (2 ml extract, 3 ml AgNO₃ and 3 ml CuSO₄), M3-2-2 (3 ml extract, 2 ml AgNO₃ and 2 ml CuSO₄), and M5-3-3 (5 ml extract, 3 ml AgNO₃ and 3 ml CuSO₄) were selected for further analysis due to their superior optical properties compared to the other samples. Several authors have documented the color transition in Ag/Cu NPs from yellow to brown as an indicator of nanoparticle formation and stabilization (Ismail 2020; Soliman et al. 2023; Olabemiwo et al. 2025). The results of our study on the biosynthesis of bimetallic silver-copper nanoparticles (Ag/Cu NPs) using aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe shows remarkable concordance with the findings reported in the literature. In particular, the color change observed in our samples, from a pale yellow to a light amber hue, is consistent with the observations of Thirumoorthy et al. (2024), who documented a similar color transition during the synthesis of Ag-Cu NPs using Aerva lanata extract (Thirumoorthy et al. 2024). This color change is attributed to the selective incorporation of bioactive compounds from the extract, which facilitates the reduction of metal ions and the formation of nanoparticles. Moreover, Malik et al. (2023) also reported a color change during the synthesis of Ag-Cu NPs from Salvia officinalis extract, which further supports our findings (Malik et al. 2023). Additionally, the observed color shift may be influenced by the specific concentrations of the precursor salts used during the synthesis, reflecting the gradual progression of nanoparticle nucleation and growth. After preliminary UV-Vis screening of all 20 formulations, three samples showing superior optical properties were selected for comprehensive characterization: M2-3-3, M3-2-2, and M5-3-3. These three formulations demonstrated the most intense and well-defined surface plasmon resonance peaks in preliminary spectroscopic analysis, indicating optimal nanoparticle formation.

Spectroscopic characterization

UV-Vis

The UV-Vis spectra (Fig. 2) show the successful formation of Ag/Cu NPs. Broad absorption bands, characteristic of the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) phenomenon, were observed between 400 and 550 nm, consistent with previously reported spectra for Ag/Cu bimetallic systems (Cazayous et al. 2006; Gurunathan et al. 2009; Khanna et al. 2019). This confirms the presence of Ag/Cu NPs in the samples, corresponding to the precursor ratios used in treatments M2-3-3, M3-2-2, and M5-3-3, with the latter showing the most intense coloration, suggesting optimal nanoparticle formation. The synthesis conditions, particularly the use of the aqueous extract at 93 °C and a concentration of 5 g/50 ml, were found to enhance nanoparticle formation, as evidenced by the sharper SPR peaks and more vivid coloration.

The results obtained in our study on the formation of bimetallic silver and copper nanoparticles (Ag/Cu NPs) by using aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe shows remarkable concordance with the findings reported in the literature. In particular, the broad absorption bands observed between 400 and 550 nm in our UV-Vis spectra are consistent with the observations of Tao et al. (2022), who documented similar absorption bands during the formation of Ag/Cu nanoparticles using UV-Vis spectra (Tao et al. 2022). This surface plasmon resonance (SPR) phenomenon confirms the presence of bimetallic nanoparticles in our samples. Furthermore, the study of Tan and Cheong (2013) also reports absorption bands in the same range, highlighting the influence of the synthesis conditions on the formation and stabilization of the nanoparticles (Tan and Cheong 2013). The observation of sharper SPR peaks and more intense coloration in our samples suggests that the synthesis conditions, particularly the use of aqueous extract at 93 °C and a concentration of 5 g/50 ml, enhance nanoparticle formation. These results reinforce the idea that higher temperatures and higher extract concentrations contribute to the reduction and stabilization of metal ions, facilitating the formation of bimetallic Ag/Cu nanoparticles.

FTIR

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the FTIR spectra corresponding to all three samples of nanoparticles, and the aqueous extract were analyzed in the range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹. The results reveal a decrease in % transmission, with prominent absorption peak detected in 3259 cm⁻¹, corresponding to O-H and N-H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of hydroxyl and amine groups, likely from proteins and polysaccharides within the extract. The 2952 cm⁻¹ peak in the extract are attributed to the stretching vibrations of C-H bonds. Additionally, signals in 1639 cm⁻¹ and 1485 cm⁻¹ peaks correspond to the presence of proteins, while alcohols, ethers and esters are identified through absorption peaks between 1019 cm⁻¹ and 954 cm⁻¹. Notably, in the spectrum of the synthesized nanoparticles, these characteristic bands are less intense, though the general pattern remains discernible. A key observation is that the peaks in the 2800–2900 cm⁻¹ range are significantly more pronounced in the extract than in the nanoparticles. This difference suggests that certain organic compounds present in the extract, particularly those contributing to the C-H stretching signals, are either reduced or altered during the nanoparticle synthesis process. Nevertheless, the spectral trends affirm the bioreduction process, where bioactive compounds play a pivotal role in nanoparticle formation.

The peaks at 1639 cm⁻¹ and 1485 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the amide I and amide II bands of the proteins, are consistent with the results Sukumaran et al (2006), who used FTIR to analyze the secondary structure of proteins (Sukumaran et al. 2006). The peaks between 1019 cm⁻¹ and 954 cm⁻¹, indicating the presence of alcohols, ethers, and esters, also align with the observations of Parmar et al. (2019) on mushrooms extracts (Parmar et al. 2019). Finally, the signals in the range of 2800–2900 cm⁻¹, corresponding to C–H stretching vibrations in aliphatic compounds, are consistent with the data reported by Sunila kumari et al. (2024) on mushroom extracts (Sunila kumari 2024). These results reinforce the validity of our methods and confirm the formation of bimetallic Ag/Cu nanoparticles through the bioreduction process facilitated by the bioactive compounds present in the Ganoderma aff. australe extract.

Elemental analysis by EDS spectroscopy (Fig. 4B) quantitatively confirms the bimetallic composition of the nanoparticles, detecting a majority composition of copper with 87.84% by mass (92.46 atomic%) and silver with 12.16% by mass (7.54 atomic%), with relative errors less than 3%. This elemental distribution corroborates the effective incorporation of both metals into the nanometric structure through the biosynthetic method used. Additionally, EDS analyses also revealed the presence of elements such as carbon, oxygen, sulfur, potassium, and phosphorus. These elements are attributable to the residual organic matrix from the Ganoderma aff. australe extract used in biosynthesis, as well as to the possible surface oxidation of the nanoparticles due to their exposure to the environment. The presence of these biogenic elements validates the biosynthetic origin of the nanoparticles and suggests the formation of a stabilizing layer of biomolecules around the metallic structures.

TEM

The successful synthesis of quasispherical Ag/Cu NPs from all three formulations (M5-3-3, M2-3-3, and M3-2-2) is evidenced by the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) micrographs presented in Fig. 5A, with sample M5-3-3 selected as the representative example due to its optimal performance in subsequent biological assays. All three samples exhibited similar morphological characteristics, with M5-3-3 demonstrating particularly well-defined nanostructures. The nanoparticle formation process across all samples is initiated through the reduction of metal ions by the bioreducing agent, leading to nucleation and cluster formation. During this process, electrons are captured by the metal ions, facilitating cluster growth throughout the solution. This growth is driven by thermodynamic factors, which regulate the competition among clusters, ultimately resulting in nanoparticles with well-defined sizes (Mourdikoudis et al. 2018; Manjunath Hulikere and Joshi 2019).

EM micrographs obtained for samples M5-3-3, M2-3-3, and M3-2-2 revealed the formation of quasi-spherical nanoparticles with homogeneous distribution across all formulations. Sample M5-3-3 exhibited the most uniform particle size distribution, with an average diameter of 17.15 nm, followed by M2-3-3 (18.42 nm) and M3-2-2 (19.87 nm), which aligns with the findings of Ameen (2022), who also reported Ag/Cu nanoparticles with well-defined sizes and controlled distribution (Ameen 2022).

During the nanoparticle formation process, metal ions are reduced by the bioreductive agents present in the Ganoderma aff. australe extract, facilitating the nucleation and growth of metallic clusters. This growth, driven by thermodynamic factors, regulates the competition among clusters, resulting in nanoparticles of defined sizes. This behavior was observed in all three samples, with M5-3-3 showing the most consistent results, followed by M2-3-3 and M3-2-2.

Upon reaching a critical size, the nanoparticles adsorb biomolecules onto their surface, forming a protective layer that limits further growth and reduces particle–particle collisions. This phenomenon is consistent with the findings of Kashyap et al. (2013), who also observed a stabilizing layer in bimetallic nanoparticles synthesized via green methods (Kashyap et al. 2013). The low agglomeration observed in all samples (particularly in M5-3-3) is a desirable feature for biomedical applications, as it enhances colloidal stability and functional performance, which is consistent with the results of Nel et al. (2006), who highlighted the importance of controlled size distribution and low agglomeration for efficacy in medical applications (Nel et al. 2006).

Overall, the morphological characterization by TEM suggests that the Ag/Cu nanoparticles synthesized using Ganoderma aff. australe extract possess structural properties suitable for medical applications. Sample M5-3-3 stands out for its greater uniformity and lower agglomeration, while M2-3-3 and M3-2-2 also exhibit promising characteristics, albeit with slightly broader particle size distributions. These findings reinforce the potential of this biosynthetic system for the development of nanomaterials with therapeutic applications.

Antibacterial activity assays

Kirby-Bauer

The Ag/Cu NPs were evaluated for their antimicrobial activity by disk diffusion method against S. aureus and E. coli strains, as shown in Fig. 6A and B, respectively. For S. aureus, the inhibition zones ranged between 0.923 and 1.253 cm, with the 5-3-3 ratio showing the highest inhibitory activity with an average zone of 1.134 ± 0.116 cm. In the case of E. coli, the inhibition zones were slightly smaller, varying between 0.783 and 1.253 cm, with the 3-2-2 ratio also showing the highest activity in its last replicate (1.253 cm). It is notable that the antimicrobial activity was slightly more pronounced against Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus) compared to Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli), which could be attributed to structural differences in their cell walls.

The differential antimicrobial activity observed between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria aligns with findings from previous studies. Slavin et al. (2017) reported that the enhanced effectiveness of metallic nanoparticles against Gram-positive bacteria is often attributed to structural differences in the cell wall composition (Slavin et al. 2017). Gram-negative bacteria possess an additional outer membrane composed of lipopolysaccharides, which acts as a barrier against antimicrobial agents, potentially explaining the reduced inhibitory effect observed in E. coli (Dakal et al. 2016). This protective outer membrane, absent in Gram-positive bacteria like S. aureus, limits the penetration of nanoparticles and other antimicrobial compounds, as demonstrated by Wang et al. (2017) in their comprehensive study on nanoparticle-cell wall interactions (Wang et al. 2017).

The mechanism of action of our Ag/Cu NPs likely involves multiple pathways. According to recent research by Zhang et al. (2016), silver nanoparticles primarily interact with thiol groups in vital enzymes and proteins, disrupting critical cellular functions (Zhang et al. 2016). Concurrently, copper ions released from the nanoparticles can catalyze the production of ROS through Fenton-like reactions, inducing oxidative stress and subsequent cell death, as demonstrated in the work of Raghunath et al. (2017) (Raghunath and Perumal 2017). This dual-action mechanism could explain the enhanced preliminary antibacterial activity observed, particularly with the M5-3-3 ratio.

Broth microdilution method

The evaluation of the antibacterial activity of samples M5-3-3, M2-3-3, and M3-2-2 revealed differential effects against E. coli and S. aureus, with notable distinctions in the performance of nanoparticles compared to extracts and controls. For E. coli, M2-3-3 demonstrated the most consistent and effective inhibition among the three samples (Fig. 7). The nanoparticles synthesized from M2-3-3 showed significantly higher inhibition compared to controls and extracts (p < 0.001), with a strong linear relationship between nanoparticle treatment and bacterial inhibition (adjusted R2 = 0.803). Additionally, the nanoparticles achieved a superior inhibition efficiency ratio (> 1.0), with lower intra-treatment variability (CV = 13.1\%), indicating a reliable and potent antibacterial action. In contrast, M5-3-3 and M3-2-2 exhibited less pronounced effects, as the nanoparticles and extracts did not surpass the inhibition thresholds, and no significant differences were observed between treatments (p > 0.05)

The superior performance of M2-3-3 nanoparticles against both bacterial strains align with findings from recent studies on bimetallic nanoparticle systems. Hemeg (2017) demonstrated that specific Ag/Cu ratios can significantly enhance antimicrobial efficacy through synergistic mechanisms that target multiple cellular components simultaneously (Hemeg 2017). The optimal ratio found in our M2-3-3 sample likely facilitates what Tran et al. (2018) described as a complementary action, where silver primarily disrupts bacterial membrane integrity while copper generates intracellular reactive oxygen species, resulting in enhanced bactericidal activity (Tran et al. 2018).

Limitations

While our study demonstrated antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli, these represent only single strains of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. Future studies should expand the antimicrobial screening to include additional clinically relevant strains, such as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and other multidrug-resistant pathogens, to better establish the spectrum and clinical potential of these nanoparticles.

Cell culture conditions and MTT assay

In this study, the cytotoxic efficacy of bimetallic silver-copper nanoparticles synthesized from an aqueous extract of Ganoderma aff. australe was systematically evaluated across five cancerous cell lines (Caco-2, HT-29, MCF7, A-172, and U-87 MG) and two non-cancerous control lines (HDFn and Detroit 551). The results shown in Fig. 8 and in Table 4 demonstrated a clear hierarchy of treatment effectiveness, with the M2-3-3 nanoparticle formulation consistently showing the highest cytotoxic activity across all cancer cell lines, followed by M3-2-2 and M5-3-3, while the crude aqueous extract showed the lowest efficacy. This pattern was most pronounced in the glioblastoma cell lines A-172 and U-87 MG, where M2-3-3 achieved remarkably low IC50 values of 1.56 ± 0.02 mg/ml and 0.68 ± 0.06 mg/ml, respectively, compared to the extract’s values of 6.91 ± 0.86 mg/ml and 2.44 ± 0.28 mg/ml, indicating exceptional sensitivity of brain cancer cells to the nanoformulated bioactive compounds. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA confirmed significant differences between treatments across all cell lines (p ≤ 0.001 to p ≤ 0.007), with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests revealing that M2-3-3 was significantly more effective than the crude extract in all cancer cell lines tested.

Each cancer cell line exhibited distinct sensitivity patterns to the different treatments, revealing important insights about treatment specificity. The colorectal cancer cell lines showed remarkable responsiveness to nanoparticle treatments, with Caco-2 demonstrating exceptional sensitivity to M2-3-3 (IC50: 0.39 ± 0.12 mg/ml), significantly outperforming M3-2-2 (0.69 ± 0.15 mg/ml), M5-3-3 (0.78 ± 0.24 mg/ml), and the crude extract (1.61 ± 0.35 mg/ml). HT-29 cells showed the most dramatic treatment-dependent variation, with M2-3-3 achieving the lowest IC50 value (0.58 ± 0.28 mg/ml) compared to significantly higher values for M3-2-2 (2.58 ± 0.16 mg/ml, p ≤ 0.001), M5-3-3 (1.61 ± 0.55 mg/ml, p ≤ 0.040), and the extract (6.00 ± 0.38 mg/ml, p ≤ 0.001). The breast cancer cell line MCF7 displayed intermediate sensitivity levels, with M2-3-3 maintaining superior efficacy (1.32 ± 0.38 mg/ml) compared to other nanoparticles and the extract (4.18 ± 0.64 mg/ml). Notably, the glioblastoma cell lines A-172 and U-87 MG emerged as highly responsive to nanoparticle treatment, with U-87 MG showing exceptional sensitivity to G2-3-3 (0.68 ± 0.06 mg/ml), establishing brain cancer cells as particularly vulnerable to the enhanced bioactivity achieved through nanoformulation.

Among the three nanoparticle formulations tested, M2-3-3 consistently demonstrated superior cytotoxic efficacy compared to M3-2-2 and M5-3-3 across all cancer cell lines, establishing it as the most effective nanoformulation. In the HT-29 colorectal cancer line, M2-3-3 achieved the lowest IC50 value (0.58 ± 0.28 mg/ml) with statistically significant differences compared to M3-2-2 (p ≤ 0.001) and M5-3-3 (p ≤ 0.040), demonstrating its exceptional effectiveness against this specific cancer type. Similarly, in the A-172 glioblastoma line, M2-3-3 significantly outperformed both M3-2-2 (p ≤ 0.031) and M5-3-3 (p ≤ 0.010), achieving an IC50 of 1.56 ± 0.02 mg/ml compared to 2.97 ± 0.37 and 3.28 ± 0.24 mg/ml, respectively. The M3-2-2 formulation showed intermediate but inconsistent activity, performing better than M5-3-3 in some lines like U-87 MG (1.05 ± 0.12 vs. 1.42 ± 0.07 mg/ml) but generally lacking the consistent superiority demonstrated by M2-3-3. M5-3-3 demonstrated the most limited anticancer activity among the nanoparticle formulations, achieving the highest IC50 values in most cancer cell lines, particularly in A-172 (3.28 ± 0.24 mg/ml) and MCF7 (2.70 ± 0.38 mg/ml), suggesting that the specific synthesis parameters used for this formulation may have compromised the optimal incorporation and release of bioactive compounds from the Ganoderma aff. australe extract.

A critical finding of this study was the differential cytotoxicity exhibited by treatments toward cancerous versus non-cancerous cell lines, with nanoparticle formulations demonstrating promising therapeutic selectivity. The non-cancerous control cell lines HDFn (human dermal fibroblasts) and Detroit 551 (normal skin fibroblasts) generally showed higher IC50 values compared to cancer cell lines when treated with nanoparticles, indicating reduced susceptibility to treatment-induced cytotoxicity. M2-3-3 showed excellent selectivity patterns, with IC50 values in normal cells (HDFn: 0.67 ± 0.17 mg/ml, Detroit 551: 1.03 ± 0.62 mg/ml) that were comparable to or higher than the most resistant cancer cell lines, while maintaining exceptional activity against sensitive cancer lines like Caco-2 (0.39 ± 0.12 mg/ml) and U-87 MG (0.68 ± 0.06 mg/ml). Interestingly, the crude extract demonstrated poor selectivity, showing relatively high effectiveness against normal cells (HDFn: 4.17 ± 1.46 mg/ml, Detroit 551: 1.59 ± 0.42 mg/ml) that was comparable to its activity against cancer cells, suggesting potential off-target toxicity. M5-3-3 exhibited concerning selectivity profiles, particularly with high cytotoxicity in Detroit 551 cells (4.04 ± 0.19 mg/ml), showing significant differences compared to other treatments (p ≤ 0.001), which raises questions about its therapeutic index and indicates that this formulation may lack the desired cancer-specific targeting properties essential for safe therapeutic application.

The superior performance of the M2-3-3 nanoparticle formulation compared to the crude extract demonstrates the successful enhancement of therapeutic efficacy through nanotechnology, aligning with established principles in nanomedicine where nanoformulations improve bioavailability, cellular uptake, and targeted delivery of bioactive compounds. The exceptional sensitivity of glioblastoma cell lines (A-172 and U-87 MG) to nanoparticle treatments, particularly M2-3-3, indicates that brain cancer cells may possess specific metabolic vulnerabilities that make them particularly susceptible to the enhanced bioactive delivery achieved through silver-copper nanoparticle matrices, possibly through mechanisms involving increased oxidative stress, enhanced cellular penetration across the blood-brain barrier, or improved intracellular accumulation of therapeutic compounds, this agreed with recent studies that demonstrated that silver and copper nanoparticles synthesized by green methods not only preserve the bioactive properties of natural extracts but also amplify them through synergistic mechanisms. For example, Arshad et al. (2024) highlight that silver nanoparticles obtained from plant extracts exhibit notable anticancer activity, attributed to their ability to induce oxidative stress, damage DNA, and trigger apoptosis in tumor cells, without significantly affecting normal cells (Arshad et al. 2024). The variable responses observed across different cancer types (colorectal, breast, and brain) suggest that the therapeutic effects are mediated through multiple pathways that are differentially expressed or active in various cancer cell phenotypes, with colorectal and brain cancers showing the highest susceptibility to nanoparticle treatment. The M2-3-3 formulation emerges as the most promising candidate for further therapeutic development, combining exceptional anticancer efficacy with favorable selectivity profiles that preferentially target cancer cells while showing reduced toxicity toward normal fibroblasts. These findings agreed with data reported by Patra et al. (2018), nanoformulations of plant extracts present greater bioavailability, stability, and cellular penetration capacity, which translates into higher therapeutic efficacy. In this context, Ag/Cu nanoparticles synthesized with Ganoderma aff. australe extract not only represent a promising alternative for cancer treatment but also exemplify a sustainable and biocompatible approach in the development of nanomaterials with biomedical applications (Patra et al. 2018).

Conclusions

This study successfully established Ganoderma aff. australe from the State of Mexico as a viable resource for green synthesis of Ag-Cu bimetallic nanoparticles. Among three optimized formulations, M2-3-3 (2:3:3 extract: Ag: Cu ratio) demonstrated superior therapeutic potential, showing consistent antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli, and remarkable selective cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines—particularly glioblastoma (A-172 IC₅₀: 1.56 ± 0.02 mg/ml; U-87 MG IC₅₀: 0.68 ± 0.06 mg/ml)—while exhibiting minimal toxicity toward normal fibroblasts. The quasispherical nanoparticles (17.15 nm average diameter, 87.84% Cu, 12.16% Ag) demonstrated properties suitable for biomedical applications. This myconanotechnology approach merges traditional mycological knowledge with modern nanomedicine, offering a sustainable pathway for developing novel antibacterial and anticancer therapeutics. Future research should focus on elucidating molecular mechanisms, in vivo efficacy, and clinical translation potential of the M2-3-3 formulation.
