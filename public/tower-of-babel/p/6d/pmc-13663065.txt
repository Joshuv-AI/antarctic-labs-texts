Exosomes in cancer drug resistance: dual roles in therapy failure and emerging precision therapeutics
Authors: Bantayehu Addis Tegegne, Habtamu Belew, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Mekuriaw Belayneh, Desalegn Abebaw, Misganaw Fikrie, Mohammed Jemal, Temesgen Baylie, Fasil Tadesse, Samuel Agegnew Wondm, Tirsit Ketsela Zeleke, Wubetu Yihunie Belay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes play a key role in cancer, functioning both as drivers of drug resistance and as tools for therapy. Tumor-derived exosomes facilitate intercellular communication through selective transfer of bioactive cargo, including proteins (e.g., P-gp, PD-L1), nucleic acids (e.g., miR-21, lncRNA H19), lipids, and metabolites. These cargos remodel the tumor microenvironment, activate oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, and promote drug resistance, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming. This work presents a conceptually integrated translational perspective, introducing the ‘exosome paradox’ to unify the dual roles of exosomes in therapy resistance and therapeutic application. The review incorporates cancer-specific insights, highlighting how resistance mechanisms vary across pancreatic, breast, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancers, underscoring the need for context-dependent strategies. Mechanistically, we integrate emerging advances in exosome heterogeneity, selective cargo loading (including SUMOylation-dependent pathways), and the metabolic-epigenetic interface, particularly the lactate-lactylation axis. We also explore the growing role of artificial intelligence in biomarker discovery, cargo profiling, and rational design of engineered exosomes. Therapeutically, two complementary strategies are emphasized: inhibition of pathogenic exosome biogenesis, secretion, and uptake, and development of engineered exosomes as delivery vehicles for chemotherapeutics, RNA-based therapies, and CRISPR/Cas9 systems. Despite promising preclinical advances, clinical translation remains limited by challenges in targeting specificity, pharmacokinetics, scalable manufacturing, and regulatory standardization. This review provides a unified framework combining suppression of pathogenic exosome signaling with strategic engineering of therapeutic exosomes, offering new directions for overcoming drug resistance and advancing precision oncology.Keywords: Artificial intelligence in oncology, Cancer drug resistance, Exosome-based therapeutics, Exosome paradox, Precision oncology, Tumor-derived exosomes

Introduction

Despite transformative advances in early detection and precision oncology, drug resistance remains a leading cause of therapeutic failure in metastatic cancers, accounting for millions of deaths worldwide [1]. While intrinsic genetic alterations are well-established drivers of resistance, increasing evidence highlights the tumor microenvironment (TME) as a critical extrinsic contributor through complex intercellular communication networks [2].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), membrane-bound nanoparticles released by virtually all cell types, are key mediators of this communication. Based on their biogenesis and size, EVs are broadly classified into ectosomes, exosomes, apoptotic bodies, and oncosomes [3].

Among these, exosomes (30–150 nm) originate from the endosomal pathway via multivesicular bodies and function as conserved vehicles for intercellular transfer of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [4]. Their ability to reflect the molecular composition of parental cells enables them to modulate recipient cell behavior across physiological and pathological contexts, including cancer [5–7].

Within oncology, tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) play central roles in tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling and cancer progression [8]. These vesicles carry bioactive cargo that promotes angiogenesis, immune evasion, invasion, and metastasis. Although intrinsic resistance mechanisms have been extensively studied, the contribution of TDEs to therapy resistance has only recently gained recognition [9, 10]. In malignancy, this communication system becomes co-opted: TDEs transfer resistance-associated molecules, including drug efflux transporters, oncogenic microRNAs, and immunosuppressive factors, enabling tumor survival under therapeutic stress [11–13].

Single-vesicle analyses reveal marked cargo heterogeneity, with distinct exosome subpopulations selectively targeting cancer stem cells or reprogramming immune cells. Collectively, these processes establish an integrated resistance network that undermines conventional therapies. Paradoxically, the same properties that enable TDEs to drive resistance—stability, immune evasion, and tissue-targeting capacity—also make them attractive therapeutic platforms [14]. This duality is conceptually analogous to the well-established ‘virus paradox’ in biomedical research. Naturally occurring viruses cause disease through mechanisms including host cell invasion, immune evasion, and genomic integration—yet these same properties have been harnessed to create oncolytic viruses and viral vectors for gene therapy [15, 16]. Engineered viruses such as adenoviruses, lentiviruses, and adeno-associated viruses are now routinely used as therapeutic delivery vehicles for cancer immunotherapy, gene replacement, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing [17]. Similarly, just as virology has leveraged viral pathogenesis for therapeutic benefit, exosome biology is now exploiting the inherent properties of these nanovesicles—their stability, targeting capacity, and ability to transfer functional cargo—to develop next-generation precision therapeutics. This parallel underscores the broader principle that evolutionarily optimized biological systems, whether viruses or exosomes, can be repurposed from agents of disease to tools of healing. Accordingly, current research seeks to exploit this duality by combining inhibition of pathogenic exosome signaling with development of engineered exosome-based therapies. While preclinical studies using inhibitors of key regulators such as Rab27a and nSMase2 demonstrate reduced secretion of resistance-associated exosomes, clinical translation remains limited by challenges including specificity, pharmacokinetics, and lack of clinically viable inhibitors [18–20]. Methodology (narrative review design)

This article is a narrative review synthesizing current evidence on the dual role of exosomes in cancer drug resistance. A structured but non-systematic literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify peer-reviewed studies relevant to exosome biology and cancer drug resistance. Search terms captured two central themes: [1] exosomes as mediators of therapy resistance (exosomal drug resistance, chemotherapy resistance, tumor microenvironment, immunosuppressive factors, oncogenic microRNAs, drug efflux pumps) and [2] exosomes as therapeutic tools (engineered exosomes, therapeutic delivery, CRISPR/Cas9, RNA therapeutics, drug delivery systems). Keywords and Medical Subject Headings were combined using Boolean operators. Publications from 2000 to 2026 were considered, with emphasis on studies published between 2010 and 2026. Priority was given to original experimental studies, clinical and translational research, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Case reports, editorials, non-peer-reviewed sources, and non-English publications were excluded.

Exosome biogenesis, heterogeneity, and the precision of cargo loading

Exosomes are actively generated nanovesicles with distinct biological roles. Their functional impact depends on molecular cargo shaped by complex biogenesis mechanisms and intrinsic heterogeneity [21, 22].

Exosome biogenesis

Exosomes originate in the endosomal system through inward budding of the limiting membrane of early endosomes, forming intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies [23, 24]. ILV biogenesis occurs primarily through two complementary pathways: the ESCRT-dependent mechanism [25] and ESCRT-independent pathways. Mature multivesicular bodies traffic to and fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing ILVs into the extracellular space as exosomes [26, 27] as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The ESCRT-independent pathway involves ceramide synthesis via nSMase2, tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81), and ARF6/PLD2-mediated budding. The ESCRT-dependent pathway

The Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport machinery is a highly conserved multi-protein complex critical for membrane remodeling and cargo sorting [28]. Comprising four core complexes (ESCRT-0, -I, -II, and -III) alongside accessory proteins, it orchestrates ILV formation in a precise sequence [29]. ESCRT-0 recognizes and binds ubiquitinated protein cargo on the endosomal membrane, then recruits ESCRT-I and ESCRT-II, which act as scaffolds to promote ESCRT-III polymerization. ESCRT-III drives membrane deformation and scission, pinching off the cargo-loaded vesicle into the MVB lumen. VPS4 ATPase then hydrolyzes ATP to disassemble and recycle ESCRT-III components [28, 30–32]. The syndecan-syntenin-ALIX pathway operates through direct interactions between syntenin and ALIX, bridging transmembrane cargo with vesicle formation machinery [33].

The ESCRT-independent pathway

Exosome biogenesis can also occur without ESCRT complexes, driven by lipid-based mechanisms and tetraspanin proteins [34, 35]. Tetraspanins (e.g., CD63, CD81, CD9) form specialized membrane microdomains that facilitate selective cargo recruitment and vesicle budding [36, 37]. ARF6 and its effector PLD2 regulate this pathway by generating phosphatidic acid, which alters membrane curvature and promotes ILV formation [38, 39].

Exosome heterogeneity

The concurrent operation of multiple biogenesis pathways within a single cell is a primary source of exosome heterogeneity, resulting in vesicles with vast differences in size, molecular composition, surface markers, and biological function [40]. Fig. 3 provides a comprehensive framework for understanding this heterogeneity across six dimensions: size, tetraspanin surface markers, molecular cargo, cellular origin, functional tropism, and therapeutic implications. Recognizing that exosomes are not a uniform population but rather a collection of functionally distinct subpopulations is essential for interpreting experimental results and developing selective therapeutic strategies [27, 41]. For example, CD63 + exosomes are preferentially enriched in PD-L1 and oncogenic miRNAs and drive immune evasion [42, 43], whereas CD81 + exosomes are linked to chemoresistance signaling [44], and CD9 + exosomes promote metastatic niche formation [45]. Similarly, exosomes derived from different parental cells—tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, immune cells, or mesenchymal stem cells—carry distinct cargo profiles [46] that dictate their functional effects on recipient cells. Understanding this heterogeneity enables the field to move beyond bulk exosome analysis toward subpopulation-specific diagnostics and therapeutics [47].

Understanding heterogeneity enables precision exosome-based strategies

Tumor cells exploit these mechanisms to package and secrete factors that promote survival and progression, including microRNAs like miR-21 that confer drug resistance and immune checkpoint proteins like PD-L1 [48]. RNA-binding proteins and post-translational modifications regulate this selective packaging, ensuring precise oncogenic signal transmission [49–51]. These biogenesis and cargo loading mechanisms directly enable TDEs to promote therapy resistance [52].

Protein cargo

Exosomes carry functional ATP-binding cassette transporters, including P-gp and BCRP. Upon transfer to recipient cells, these transporters actively efflux chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin and paclitaxel, reducing intracellular drug accumulation and therapeutic efficacy [11, 53, 54]. Proteomic and single-vesicle analyses demonstrate that exosome cargo is organized into functionally distinct subpopulations. Tetraspanin-defined subsets such as CD63 + and CD81 + exosomes exhibit context-specific roles in resistance [55–57]. Cargo composition varies with tumor type, treatment exposure, and microenvironmental conditions [58, 59].

Exosomal immune checkpoint proteins further modulate anti-tumor immunity. PD-L1-bearing exosomes engage PD-1 on T cells within the tumor microenvironment and draining lymph nodes, suppressing T-cell activation and contributing to resistance against immune checkpoint blockade. Similarly, exosomal TGF-β fosters an immunosuppressive environment [60–63]. These findings highlight the importance of selectively targeting pathogenic exosome subpopulations rather than indiscriminately inhibiting all exosomes.

Nucleic acid cargo

Exosomal miR-21-5p drives resistance by downregulating the tumor suppressor PTEN, leading to constitutive activation of the pro-survival PI3K/AKT pathway, rendering cells resistant to apoptosis induced by various chemotherapies and targeted agents [64, 65]. Long non-coding RNAs like lncRNA H19 promote cancer stemness and EMT by activating pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin [66–68].

SUMOylation has emerged as a critical regulatory mechanism governing selective packaging of resistance-associated molecules. The RNA-binding protein hnRNPA2B1 requires SUMOylation to recognize EXOmotifs within microRNAs and facilitate their selective loading into exosomes. Disruption of this process impairs miRNA sorting and alters horizontal transfer of oncogenic signals [69, 70].

Recent studies show that extracellular vesicle-associated circBIRC6 from cancer-associated fibroblasts promotes platinum resistance in pancreatic cancer by enhancing SUMOylation of the DNA repair protein XRCC4, facilitating non-homologous end joining repair [71–73].

Lipid/metabolite cargo: altering the pharmacological microenvironment

Ceramide, a sphingolipid pivotal for ESCRT-independent exosome biogenesis, mediates chemoresistance by facilitating spontaneous inward budding of endosomal membranes, enabling direct sequestration and loading of chemotherapeutic agents into exosomes as a vesicular drug efflux mechanism [74, 75].

Exosomes facilitate lactate export via monocarboxylate transporters MCT1/MCT4, acidifying the TME and promoting therapy resistance. This acidification inactivates pH-sensitive drugs, suppresses immune cell function, and supports metabolic coupling between stromal and cancer cells under therapy-induced stress [76–79].

Lactate shuttling and the exosome-lactate-lactylation axis

Beyond acidifying the TME, exosomes function as central orchestrators of metabolic symbiosis through lactate shuttling—the coordinated transfer of lactate between glycolytic and oxidative cells within the TME [80, 81]. TDEs deliver lactate, glycolytic enzymes, and regulatory lncRNAs that reprogram recipient cell metabolism toward aerobic glycolysis [82]. Recent studies reveal that exosome-mediated lactate transfer influences gene expression through protein lactylation, a post-translational modification first described in 2019 [83]. Lactylation occurs when lactate is converted to lactyl-CoA and transferred to lysine residues on histone and non-histone proteins, altering chromatin structure and transcriptional programs [84–87].

Lactate-driven lactylation of DNA repair proteins such as XRCC1 and MRE11 enhances homologous recombination repair capacity, conferring resistance to DNA-damaging chemotherapies including platinum-based agents and PARP inhibitors [88]. Histone lactylation at promoters of resistance-associated genes directly upregulates their expression, linking metabolic reprogramming to durable epigenetic memory of therapy resistance [89]. Strategies targeting this axis include MCT1/4 inhibition (e.g., AZD3965, in Phase I trials, NCT01791595), LDHA inhibitors (FX11, GSK2837808A), and small molecules disrupting lactylation writer enzymes [90, 91]. When combined with inhibitors of exosome biogenesis (GW4869) or secretion (Rab27a silencing), these approaches may synergistically disrupt the metabolic-epigenetic communication network underlying therapy resistance [92, 93]. Cancer-specific roles of exosomes in drug resistance

TDEs mediate drug resistance in a context-dependent manner, influenced by cancer-specific genetic profiles, TME, and therapy type [108]. Distinct exosomal cargos and activated pathways vary by malignancy, necessitating tailored interventions [10, 109].

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma exhibits profound chemoresistance, where exosomes significantly contribute by shuttling oncogenic RNAs from cancer-associated fibroblasts [110]. CAF-derived exosomes transfer circBIRC6, promoting platinum resistance by enhancing SUMOylation of XRCC4, thereby boosting DNA repair via non-homologous end joining [111, 112]. Exosomal miR-21 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, supporting tumor cell survival and resistance to gemcitabine [113, 114].

Breast cancer

In breast cancer, exosomes transfer ABC transporters like P-gp, driving multidrug resistance. Exosomal miR-155 and miR-222 promote resistance by downregulating tumor suppressors and fostering EMT. Exosomal PD-L1 further aids immune evasion, diminishing effectiveness of checkpoint inhibitors [115].

Lung cancer

Non-small cell lung cancer uses exosomes to spread resistance to targeted therapies like EGFR inhibitors through transfer of mutant EGFR proteins and resistance-linked miRNAs. Exosomal PD-L1 also plays a key role in evading immunotherapy [115, 116].

Colorectal cancer

In colorectal cancer, exosomes mediate resistance through transfer of lncRNAs and miRNAs that regulate Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [116–118]. Exosomal cargo also contributes to metabolic reprogramming, enabling tumor cells to adapt to therapeutic stress [115, 119, 120].

Ovarian cancer

Exosomes in ovarian cancer are strongly associated with platinum resistance, transferring DNA repair proteins and regulatory RNAs that enhance homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining pathways. Exosomal TGF-β contributes to an immunosuppressive microenvironment [121, 122].

These findings highlight that exosome-mediated drug resistance is shaped by tumor-specific biology, emphasizing the need for precision strategies targeting the most relevant exosomal pathways in each cancer type [123].

Therapeutic application and targeting of exosomes in oncology

Chapter 1 Current research focuses on two main strategies: inhibiting release and uptake of pathogenic exosomes, and engineering therapeutic exosomes [124–126].

Inhibition of pro-tumorigenic exosome activity

Blocking biogenesis and secretion

Chapter 2 Exosome biogenesis and release can be modulated using small-molecule inhibitors targeting key regulators such as Rab27a (MVB docking) and nSMase2 (ceramide-driven ILV formation) [127]. Preclinical compounds such as GW4869 have been widely employed, decreasing secretion of exosomes carrying chemoresistance-associated factors [127–129]. However, translation to clinical use remains challenging due to limited specificity, suboptimal pharmacokinetics, and lack of selective clinically viable inhibitors [124, 130].

Neutralizing uptake

Blocking exosome uptake by recipient cells can be achieved by targeting exosome-cell surface interactions. Heparin acts as a competitive inhibitor, binding to exosomes and preventing adhesion to cell surface HSPGs. Antibodies against surface proteins like tetherin can also interfere with exosome-cell interactions [127, 131, 132].

Engineered exosomes as drug delivery systems

Chapter 3 Leveraging exosomes’ natural tropism and low immunogenicity offers a novel pharmacological delivery platform.

CRISPR/Cas9 delivery

Chapter 4 Exosomes can be engineered to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 components for targeted modification of cancer-associated genes, including oncogenic KRAS and EGFR mutants [133]. For example, McAndrews et al., demonstrated that exosome-mediated delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 targeting the mutant KRAS^G12D allele in pancreatic cancer models reduced tumor growth by approximately 50% in xenograft mouse models [19, 134]. Challenges remain in loading efficiency, off-target effects, and tissue-specific delivery [135].

Targeted cytotoxic delivery

Exosomes loaded with chemotherapeutic drugs and surface-functionalized with ligands for tumor-specific receptors enhance tumor-specific delivery, maximize local drug concentration, and minimize systemic toxicity [136, 137]. For instance, Kim et al. showed that exosome-encapsulated paclitaxel delivered via macrophage-derived exosomes overcame P-gp-mediated multidrug resistance in lung cancer cells, achieving an IC50 nearly 50-fold lower than free paclitaxel [138, 139]. Similarly, Hadla et al. demonstrated that doxorubicin-loaded mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes reduced cardiotoxicity while maintaining antitumor efficacy in breast cancer models [136, 140].

RNA interference delivery

Exosomes are ideal vectors for delivering RNAi molecules like siRNA or miRNA to silence resistance genes such as MDR1 (encoding P-gp) or oncogenes, helping to re-sensitize tumors to treatment [141–143]. For example, Bellavia et al. designed IL-3 receptor-targeted exosomes delivering BCR-ABL siRNA, which restored imatinib sensitivity in chronic myelogenous leukemia cells and reduced tumor growth in xenograft models [144, 145]. In glioblastoma, Monfared et al. showed that exosomes loaded with an anti-miR-21 sponge construct neutralized oncogenic miR-21, leading to reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis [146].

Exosomes compared to established delivery platforms (LNPs and AAVs)

While exosomes offer unique advantages as biologic delivery vehicles, it is essential to critically compare them with established gold standards in nucleic acid and gene therapy delivery: lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) [147, 148]. This comparison provides necessary context for evaluating the true innovation and remaining challenges of exosome-based platforms [149].

LNPs are synthetic lipid-based carriers that have become the leading non-viral platform for RNA delivery, exemplified by their successful use in Pfizer/BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, as well as the FDA-approved siRNA therapeutic Onpattro (patisiran) for hereditary transthyretin-mediated amyloidosis [147, 150]. LNPs offer several advantages: (i) well-established, scalable Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing processes; (ii) predictable pharmacokinetic profiles; (iii) extensive clinical validation across multiple indications; and (iv) a clear regulatory pathway [150]. However, LNPs have significant limitations: they exhibit inherent liver tropism due to apolipoprotein E binding, making extrahepatic targeting challenging; they can trigger immune responses, particularly against PEGylated components; and they have limited capacity for active targeting to specific cell types [151, 152].

AAVs are the most clinically advanced viral vectors for gene therapy, with several FDA-approved products including Luxturna (RPE65 mutation-associated blindness), Zolgensma (spinal muscular atrophy), and Hemgenix (hemophilia B)[148, 153]. AAVs offer: (i) established manufacturing platforms with approved products; (ii) defined serotypes with inherent tissue tropism; (iii) long-term transgene expression in non-dividing cells; and (iv) well-characterized safety profiles. However, AAVs face critical limitations: small cargo capacity (~ 4.7 kb, restricting delivery of larger CRISPR/Cas9 systems); high immunogenicity (pre-existing neutralizing antibodies in 30–70% of humans); risk of insertional mutagenesis (though low); high manufacturing costs; and limited ability to re-dose due to immune responses[154, 155, 189, 190].

Exosomes offer distinct advantages over both LNPs and AAVs. Compared to LNPs, exosomes exhibit lower immunogenicity, superior targeting capability through surface engineering (e.g., displaying antibodies, peptides, or aptamers), and enhanced ability to cross biological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier via receptor-mediated transcytosis [156, 157]. Compared to AAVs, exosomes lack the risk of insertional mutagenesis, have higher cargo capacity (exosomes can carry up to ~ 5–10 kb of nucleic acid, sufficient for larger CRISPR/Cas9 systems), and are less likely to trigger pre-existing immunity, enabling potential re-dosing [149].

However, exosomes face substantial challenges that LNPs and AAVs have largely overcome. Unlike these established platforms, exosomes lack: (i) standardized, scalable GMP-compliant manufacturing processes; (ii) robust analytical characterization methods for quality control; (iii) predictable in vivo biodistribution and pharmacokinetic profiles; (iv) established regulatory pathways for clinical approval; and (v) demonstrated efficacy in large animal models or human trials [158, 159]. In summary, exosomes are not yet ready to replace LNPs or AAVs as first-line delivery platforms. Instead, they should be viewed as complementary technologies that may offer unique advantages for specific applications where LNPs and AAVs fall short: (i) delivery across the blood-brain barrier for central nervous system malignancies; (ii) repeated dosing without immune neutralization; (iii) delivery of larger genetic payloads (e.g., full CRISPR/Cas9 systems); and (iv) active targeting to extrahepatic tissues. Achieving these advantages in clinical settings will require overcoming the substantial manufacturing, analytical, and regulatory challenges that currently distinguish exosomes from their more mature competitors.

Combination therapy strategies

Inhibitors of exosome biogenesis and release (e.g., GW4869, Nexinhib20) demonstrate decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis in tumor models [163, 164]. Combining exosome inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade reduces exosomal PD-L1 release, enhancing T-cell activation and improving responses to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies. A notable example comes from Poggio et al., who demonstrated that inhibiting exosome secretion using GW4869 in combination with anti-PD-L1 antibodies reduced tumor growth and induced systemic antitumor immunity in mouse models of breast cancer and melanoma [165–167].

More recently, Irep et al., showed that exosome inhibition using Nexinhib20 improved response to first-line chemotherapy in small cell lung cancer models by reducing exosomal transfer of chemoresistance-associated miRNAs. While emerging therapeutic strategies target exosomes directly, advances in artificial intelligence are further accelerating progress in this field [164, 168–170]. Artificial intelligence in exosome research for precision therapeutics

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly advancing exosome research by enabling high-resolution analysis of complex biological data and accelerating translation of exosome-based strategies into precision oncology. Given the intrinsic heterogeneity of EVs and their cargo, conventional analytical approaches often fail to capture the full spectrum of biologically relevant signals. AI-driven methodologies, including machine learning and deep learning, provide powerful tools to overcome these limitations [164].

AI plays a central role in exosome profiling and biomarker discovery by integrating multi-omics datasets such as proteomics, transcriptomics, and lipidomics, enabling identification of exosome-derived biomarkers associated with cancer progression and drug resistance. Machine learning algorithms can classify exosome subpopulations based on cargo composition, size, and surface markers, improving early diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and prediction of therapeutic response [163, 166].

A major advantage of AI is its ability to address exosome heterogeneity and cargo complexity. The processes governing exosome biogenesis and selective cargo loading—often regulated by post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination and SUMOylation—are highly complex. AI-driven pattern recognition can uncover hidden relationships between molecular features and cargo enrichment, enabling identification of functionally distinct exosome subpopulations, including those that drive chemoresistance [165, 170].

Beyond analysis, AI is increasingly used to guide engineering of therapeutic exosomes. Predictive models can optimize cargo loading strategies, surface modifications, and targeting ligand selection to enhance delivery efficiency and tissue specificity. In CRISPR/Cas9-based therapies, AI can support guide RNA design and reduce off-target effects [166]. AI enables predictive modeling that forecasts treatment responses and guides therapeutic decision-making, particularly valuable for designing personalized exosome-based therapies.

Despite its promise, integration of AI into exosome research faces challenges including limited availability of high-quality standardized datasets, variability in exosome isolation methods, lack of regulatory frameworks for AI-driven clinical tools, and issues related to model interpretability and reproducibility [169].

Current challenges in translational pharmacology

Manufacturing and standardization

Scaling up production of clinical-grade engineered exosomes remains a major bottleneck due to challenges in achieving consistent cargo loading, reproducible manufacturing, and regulatory compliance. Both pre-loading strategies (engineering donor cells) and post-loading approaches (e.g., electroporation) are widely used, yet maintaining efficiency and uniformity at industrial scale remains difficult [181]. Bioreactor-based systems and purification methods such as tangential flow filtration improve yield and batch-to-batch consistency, but standardization across platforms and alignment with GMP requirements remain unresolved [159, 181].

Pharmacokinetics and safety

The in vivo biodistribution, off-target effects, and long-term safety of exosome-inhibitory strategies and engineered exosome therapeutics require comprehensive evaluation [182]. Inhibitors targeting exosome biogenesis, secretion, or uptake may disrupt multiple cellular pathways, raising concerns about unintended systemic effects [183]. Preclinical studies of therapeutic exosomes, particularly those derived from stem cells, have generally shown favorable safety profiles, but robust pharmacokinetic data, long-term biodistribution studies, and cross-species validation remain limited [181, 184].

To date, most evidence supporting these strategies remains confined to preclinical or early translational studies [185]. A key safety concern is the potential impact of globally inhibiting exosome biogenesis regulators such as Rab27a and nSMase2 [186, 187].

Rab27a regulates lytic granule exocytosis in cytotoxic lymphocytes, including CD8 + T cells and NK cells. Germline mutations in RAB27A cause Griscelli syndrome, characterized by immunodeficiency and life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndromes. Preclinical studies demonstrate that Rab27a-deficient NK cells and CD8 + T cells display severely reduced degranulation and cytolytic ability [185, 188, 189].

Similarly, nSMase2 is abundantly expressed in the CNS, where it regulates sphingolipid metabolism and EV biogenesis. Dysregulation of nSMase2 has been implicated in Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, ALS, and prion diseases. Chronic use of nSMase2 inhibitors raises concerns about potential neurotoxicity. These dual concerns represent major translational hurdles requiring rigorous preclinical safety evaluation [186, 187, 190, 191].

Diagnostic integration

Exosomes represent promising liquid biopsy platforms for real-time monitoring of tumor evolution and therapy resistance [192]. Changes in circulating exosomal cargo, such as PD-L1 or oncogenic miRNAs, can reflect immune evasion and emerging resistance [193, 194].

However, the literature on exosomal PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker remains conflicting. While some studies report strong correlations with clinical response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, others show no significant relationship. Discrepancies stem from technical variability: ELISAs cannot distinguish exosomal PD-L1 from soluble or non-exosomal vesicle PD-L1, while microfluidic-based immunoaffinity capture offers improved specificity but is less standardized. Until these variables are systematically addressed through multi-center standardization efforts, exosomal PD-L1 should be considered investigational [62, 195–198].

Analytical characterization of engineered exosomes

A critical but often underappreciated challenge in translating engineered exosomes to the clinic is the lack of robust, standardized analytical methods for characterizing these complex biologic nanoparticles. Unlike small molecules or even LNPs, exosomes possess heterogeneous size, surface chemistry, cargo composition, and batch-to-batch variability, making quality control exceptionally difficult [199–202].

Size and concentration analysis

The most commonly used methods for exosome sizing and concentration measurement are nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). NTA provides particle size distribution and concentration by tracking Brownian motion of individual vesicles, but it cannot distinguish exosomes from non-exosomal vesicles (e.g., ectosomes, apoptotic bodies) or lipoproteins of similar size [203, 204]. DLS is faster and requires less sample but averages over all particles in solution, masking heterogeneity and reporting intensity-weighted distributions that overrepresent larger particles [205, 206].

Tunable resistive pulse sensing (TRPS) offers single-vesicle resolution and simultaneous size and concentration measurement, but it has lower throughput and requires calibration with standards. Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) provides the highest resolution structural information, enabling visualization of exosome morphology, membrane integrity, and even cargo density, but it is labor-intensive, low-throughput, and unsuitable for routine quality control [207].

Surface marker characterization

Characterizing exosome surface markers (e.g., tetraspanins CD63, CD81, CD9; targeting ligands; functional proteins) is essential for confirming identity and assessing engineered modifications [201, 208]. However, conventional flow cytometry cannot detect individual exosomes because they fall below the size detection limit (~ 200–300 nm for most instruments). Nano-flow cytometry (nFCM) and high-resolution flow cytometry have emerged as solutions, enabling single-exosome analysis, but these instruments are not widely available and require specialized expertise [201, 209].

Western blotting and ELISA remain standard for bulk exosome characterization but provide population averages, cannot distinguish subpopulations, and require relatively large sample volumes [199, 200]. Emerging technologies such as single-particle interferometric reflectance imaging sensing (SP-IRIS) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) enable label-free, real-time detection of exosome surface markers, but these methods are still in development for routine use [210, 211].

Cargo characterization

Characterizing exosomal cargo—proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites—requires orthogonal approaches. Proteomic analysis by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) provides comprehensive protein identification but is destructive, low-throughput, and cannot distinguish between lumenal and surface proteins [212]. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantitative PCR (qPCR) are widely used for nucleic acid cargo analysis, but exosome isolation methods significantly influence RNA profiles, and contamination with non-exosomal RNA remains a concern [213–215].

Lipidomic and metabolomic analyses pose significant challenges due to the lipidome’s complexity and the low abundance of many metabolites. These fields struggle with metabolite identification, standardization of sample processing, and biological variability, complicating accurate quantification and reproducibility. Low-abundance lipids often evade detection without advanced sensitivity improvements [216–219].

Batch-to-batch consistency and reference materials

Standardized protocols for exosome attributes—such as identity, purity, and potency—remain undefined across cell types and production methods. This lack of agreement delays therapeutic commercialization [159]. A major barrier to clinical translation is the lack of batch-to-batch consistency in exosome production. Even when using the same cell source and isolation method, exosome yield, size distribution, surface marker expression, and cargo loading can vary substantially between batches. This variability complicates dose determination, pharmacokinetic modeling, and efficacy assessment [220–222]. The field urgently needs standardized reference materials—well-characterized exosome preparations that can be used as benchmarks for comparing results across laboratories and manufacturing runs [223, 224]. The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) has proposed Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) guidelines, but compliance remains voluntary, and quantitative benchmarks are lacking [225, 226].

Toward standardized analytical frameworks

Addressing these analytical challenges requires orthogonal, high-throughput characterization platforms and certified reference materials to benchmark across labs. As noted above, CQAs for engineered exosomes remain undefined and MISEV compliance voluntary. Without broader adoption of standardized reporting guidelines, clinical translation of exosome therapeutics will continue to be limited by uncertainties in identity, purity, potency, and stability [227, 228].

Exosomes: Bridging cancer biology and clinical innovation

Exosomes represent a critical interface between fundamental cancer biology and clinical application. Their translation into reliable diagnostic and therapeutic tools depends on overcoming scalable manufacturing, improved pharmacokinetic characterization, and standardized diagnostic framework. The promise of exosomes for non-invasive, real-time monitoring depends on solving diagnostic integration challenges. Once standardized, their presence in accessible biofluids will allow robust tracking of tumor progression, metastatic potential, and early emergence of therapeutic failure, enabling adaptive therapy [163, 229, 230].

The therapeutic application of engineered exosomes hinges on overcoming manufacturing and safety challenges [231, 232]. Achieving scalable, good manufacturing practice‑compliant production with consistent cargo loading moves engineered exosomes from preclinical proof‑of‑concept to clinical‑grade products [159]. Once these barriers are addressed, engineered exosomes can deliver chemotherapeutics, siRNAs, and CRISPR/Cas9 components across restrictive barriers like the blood–brain barrier. The immunomodulatory applications of exosomes are similarly gated by translational readiness. Manufacturing consistency is vital for producing exosome‑based vaccines or engineered immune cell exosomes. Diagnostic integration of exosomal PD‑L1 measurements can guide combination strategies, and exosome‑inhibition agents can be rationally combined with checkpoint blockers [159, 231, 233].

Standardized production enables reliable therapeutics and diagnostics; validated diagnostics guide intelligent therapeutic combinations. By systematically addressing these translational pillars, the field can transform exosomes into next-generation precision oncology tools [232].

Conclusion and future perspectives: navigating the exosome paradox

Exosomes embody a central paradox in oncology: the same properties that drive drug resistance—stability, immune evasion, and targeted intercellular communication—also make them powerful therapeutic platforms. This review has advanced this framework by integrating emerging mechanisms including SUMOylation-dependent cargo sorting, the lactate-lactylation metabolic-epigenetic axis, and functionally distinct exosome subpopulations. Therapeutically, two complementary strategies are converging: inhibiting pathogenic exosome biogenesis, release, and uptake; and engineering exosomes as delivery vehicles for chemotherapeutics, RNA therapies, and CRISPR/Cas9 systems. However, clinical translation faces five specific barriers that must be prioritized: [1] undefined critical quality attributes (CQAs) for engineered exosomes; [2] lack of standardized reference materials and batch-to-batch consistency; [3] voluntary compliance only with MISEV guidelines; [4] limited specificity and potential off-target effects of current exosome inhibitors (e.g., Rab27a, nSMase2); and [5] absence of scalable, GMP-compliant manufacturing platforms.

Future progress requires three actionable steps: first, establishment of consensus CQAs through multi-center collaboration; second, integration of artificial intelligence for cargo profiling and therapeutic optimization; and third, development of certified reference materials to benchmark across laboratories and manufacturing runs. By simultaneously suppressing pathogenic exosome signaling and harnessing their delivery capabilities, exosome-based strategies can overcome current therapeutic limitations and enable next-generation precision oncology.
