Centrifuge model study on consolidation characteristics of sand-based fill soil subjected to pulse-induced water bag + vacuum preloading
Authors: Qiunan Chen, Xiaodi Xu, Guanhao Wang, Wenbing Zhou, Yan Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For a sandy fill foundation reinforcement project in Dongguan City, a combined water bag + vacuum preloading method was employed. Centrifugal model tests investigated the behavior of sandy fill foundations under pulsed loading during consolidation, including pore water pressure, earth pressure, settlement, and changes in moisture content and shear strength before and after consolidation. Results were compared with those from water bag + vacuum preloading consolidation tests without pulsed loading. Results indicate that increasing the number of drainage plates significantly enhances settlement reduction and drainage volume while markedly improving soil mechanical properties. Pulsed air pressure effectively fractures silt-clogged soil around drainage plates, thereby consolidating the reinforced soil mass.

Introduction

Since the 21 st century, coastal regions have typically employed large dredgers to extract silt and sand from marine areas and dredge fill them into low-lying areas, forming reclaimed land1. Dredged fill exhibits three high properties and two low properties (high moisture content, high void ratio, high compressibility, low shear strength, low permeability), resulting in low bearing capacity2. To enhance the bearing capacity of reclaimed land, common reinforcement methods for fill soil include preloading, vacuum preloading, and combined techniques3–8. Given the coastal location of fill soil areas, where earth and stone resources are scarce but water resources are abundant, water bags are frequently employed as the source of preloading pressure9.

Regarding the analysis of the effectiveness of combined dynamic loading and vacuum preloading for reinforcing soft soil foundations, numerous methods are currently employed, such as numerical simulation10–13、field testing4,14,15, theoretical analysis16–20, model testing21,22 and centrifuge model testing. While these research methods effectively investigate the reinforcement of soft soil foundations through load-bearing and vacuum preloading, studies employing centrifugal model testing currently exhibit certain limitations.

Centrifugal modelling experiments utilise the principle of similarity between centrifugal and gravitational fields, accounting for the effects of geomaterials’ non-linearity and self-weight stresses on geostructures. This ensures that the stress conditions at each point within the model correspond precisely to those in the prototype, thereby satisfying the requirement for physical similarity23. Centrifuge model testing is extensively employed in the geotechnical field for investigating diverse rock and soil formations, including but not limited to slopes, tunnels, foundations, and excavation pits. NG centrifuge model testing involves subjecting a model scaled down by a factor of N to a centrifugal force field equivalent to N times the gravitational acceleration g. Under identical material conditions, the model exhibits behaviour proportional to 1/N of the prototype, with consolidation time reduced to 1/N² of the prototype. Centrifugal model testing reduces model scale by increasing centrifugal force, enabling rapid acquisition of stress, deformation, and other data under realistic conditions. This approach shortens testing cycles and enhances experimental precision. Capable of controlling multiple test parameters, centrifugal model testing serves as a vital tool for evaluating soil properties and structural safety. Consequently, its application in studying vacuum preloading for soft ground foundation reinforcement constitutes a reliable methodology.

Currently, some scholars employ centrifuge model tests to investigate vacuum preloading for consolidating soft soil foundations. For instance, Wang Cun24 conducted centrifuge model tests on vacuum preloading consolidation for engineering revetment foundations, obtaining data on settlement and pore pressure during the consolidation process. Liu Chunyuan25 employed centrifugal model tests to investigate combined vacuum preloading and loading reinforcement of lacustrine soft soil foundations by widening drainage plates, obtaining settlement data and comparing it with field engineering results; Chen Keping26 employed sand wells to simulate drainage plates and conducted centrifuge model tests using positive pressure instead of negative pressure, obtaining data on soil settlement and pore water pressure; Hu Liwen27 conducted centrifuge model tests on underwater vacuum preloading consolidation of soft soil foundations, utilising a self-made underwater vacuum generator to simulate underwater vacuum preloading, achieving a satisfactory simulation of the process; Liu Songyu28 and colleagues combined pneumatic splitting with vacuum preloading to invent the splitting vacuum method. This technique enhances the effective treatment depth of vacuum preloading, improves the permeability of deep soil layers, reduces preloading duration, and controls post-construction settlement. Existing research primarily focuses on conventional loading and vacuum preloading consolidation techniques, with limited studies addressing loading and vacuum preloading consolidation under pulsed loading conditions. Few investigations have combined in-situ unconsolidated-undrained triaxial shear tests to evaluate consolidation effectiveness.

This study employs centrifuge modelling experiments to investigate the consolidation effects of single to multiple drainage boards under pulsed loading. The experimental process yielded pore pressure variation curves, earth pressure variation curves, settlement curves, and drainage volumes. Soil moisture content and cross-plate shear strength were measured before and after testing. Following completion, c and φ values were determined via triaxial tests on undisturbed soil. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on the consolidation settlement characteristics of reclaimed land foundations.

Project overview and test materials

Project overview

This study examines a land reclamation foundation project in Dongguan City, Guangdong Province. On-site implementation involved combined reinforcement of the reclaimed land foundation using water bag loading and vacuum preloading, with field photographs shown in Fig. 1. Given the site’s proximity to the coast and abundant water resources, soil resources were scarce. Water bag loading effectively addressed this issue. However, single water bag loading typically achieves a 2 m load height, providing 20 kPa loading pressure. This alone could not attain the desired soil strength. Conversely, employing vacuum preloading alone to achieve optimal vacuum pressure would incur high economic costs. Therefore, simultaneous water bag loading and vacuum preloading were adopted for reinforcement. To investigate improved reinforcement methods, centrifugal model tests examined the combined effect of water bag loading and vacuum preloading under pulsed air pressure. These results were contrasted with the combined reinforcement achieved under non-pulsed conditions.

The instruments required for the test can be categorized into test platforms, measurement devices, and auxiliary equipment.

The test was conducted on the WZU-150 supergravity centrifuge at the Zhejiang Provincial Key Laboratory of Weak Soil Foundation and Coastal Reclamation Engineering Technology, Wenzhou University. This centrifuge has a maximum capacity of 150 g·t, a maximum acceleration of 200 g (1 g = 9.8 m/s²), and an effective rotational radius of 3.0 m. The model was designed for an acceleration of 50 g, with dimensions of 500 mm × 500 mm × 800 mm (length × width × height). The Wenzhou University centrifuge is shown in Fig. 4.

The soil consolidation settlement test primarily employs displacement sensors, soil pressure sensors, and pore water pressure sensors for monitoring. The displacement sensor used is the CYY-LVDT displacement gauge with a range of 30 mm; The soil pressure sensor is an HC-16 miniature soil stress sensor with a measurement range of 300 kPa; the pore water pressure sensor is a PDCR-81 pore water pressure sensor with a measurement range of 300 kPa. The various sensors are shown in Fig. 5. Both the soil pressure sensor and the pore water pressure sensor measure horizontal directional pressure.

Test method

Centrifugal model design

This experimental study investigates the combined reinforcement effects of vacuum preloading with water bag loading under pulsed and non-pulsed conditions, employing single sand wells, single-scale drainage boards, dual-scale drainage boards, and multiple scale drainage boards. Corresponding to Tests 1#, 2#, 3#, and 4#: Area 2 featured a single scaled-down drainage board, measuring 250 mm × 250 mm; Area 3 investigated two drainage boards spaced 52 mm apart, scaled from the 2600 mm spacing used in actual engineering projects26; Area 4 employed multiple drainage boards for joint drainage, comprising eight boards in total. Each zone is separated using fish pond liner, which is heat-sealed for jointing. This industrial-grade, high-tenacity woven base fabric features a double-sided waterproof coating to ensure soil water isolation. The moisture content of the test soil is configured based on the liquid limit. Soil moisture content is measured after conditioning but prior to loading into the model box. Following completion of the vacuum preloading centrifuge test, moisture content is measured in each zone. Pore water pressure sensors and soil pressure sensors were installed in each zone, alongside displacement sensors. The model zoning and sensor layout are shown in Fig. 6. From the top view, displacement measurement points align with soil pressure and pore water pressure points, positioned 26 mm from the drainage side of the vacuum preloading drainage boards. Zone 4 features a distinct measurement point located at the model’s central position. In the model, the pore water pressure sensor and soil pressure sensor are installed 20 cm above the base in the vertical direction, corresponding to the mid-height of the drainage board embedded in the hydraulic fill.

The reclamation foundation undergoes drainage consolidation through the installation of plastic drainage boards, with dimensions of 10,000 mm × 100 mm × 4 mm. Due to scaling limitations, these boards prove difficult to replicate within the centrifuge model. To address the thickness dimension effect of the drainage boards, the sand well equivalent substitution method26 and the approach of increasing the drainage board width25 were employed.

1) Based on Hou Yujing’s29 research employing the equivalent sand well substitution method, plastic drainage boards are converted into equivalent sand wells using the following specific conversion formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\mathrm{p}}=\alpha\:\frac{2\left(b+\delta\:\right)}{\pi\:}$$\end{document}

In the formula: Dp represents the equivalent prototype diameter; b denotes the width of the plastic drainage board, b = 100 mm; δ indicates the thickness of the drainage board, δ = 4 mm; α is the conversion coefficient, with a value of 0.8829.

The equivalent prototype diameter Dp calculated from the above formula is 58.26 mm; considering the scale ratio, this converts to a model sand well diameter of 1.17 mm. The equivalent pore size of the plastic drainage board filter membrane does not exceed 75 μm. A medium-speed quantitative filter paper with a pore size of 30–50 μm, rolled into a cylindrical shape, is selected as a substitute for the sand well. This ensures that the permeability coefficient of the sand well bag does not affect soil drainage while preventing soil particles from clogging the filter paper.

2) Based on Liu Chunyuan’s26 research on widening drainage boards, considering that 2 mm wide and 0.08 mm thick drainage boards are impractical for both manufacturing and construction, this experiment increased the width and thickness of the drainage boards. The drainage boards used in the project were cut along the internal plastic skeleton to achieve a width of 8 mm. To prevent fill soil from entering the drainage board interior, the bottom of the drainage board was sealed with hot melt adhesive.

The “equivalent sand drain substitution method” idealises the prefabricated vertical drain as a cylindrical sand drain through theoretical derivation, yielding a relatively rigorous theoretical framework; however, it is difficult to apply pulsed air pressure in this model, so it is more suitable for investigating macroscopic responses such as the overall performance of the drainage system, including consolidation rate and settlement evolution. In contrast, the “widened drainage board method” has a comparatively weaker theoretical basis, but the contact interface between the drainage board and the surrounding soil more closely reflects engineering practice and facilitates the application of pulsed air pressure.

Centrifugal model experiment

The centrifuge model experiments were divided into two groups: Group 1 involved vacuum preloading combined with dynamic loading preloading on a sandy fill foundation without pulsed pressure, while Group 2 involved vacuum preloading combined with dynamic loading preloading on a sandy fill foundation with pulsed pressure. Each group was subdivided into Zones 1, 2, 3, and 4. The primary difference between the two groups was the application of pulsed pressure during the test process. Prior to installing drainage boards and applying loads, soil samples underwent saturation treatment followed by preconsolidation to achieve initial stress equilibrium. After preconsolidation, sand wells were installed, drainage boards were embedded, a top sand cushion was laid, and a top-load was applied. Displacement gauges were then installed, initiating the consolidation process. Specific procedures are detailed below.

1) First, saturate the soil samples. Separately sun-dry, sieve, and immerse the foundation soil samples in water to achieve saturation. To ensure 100% saturation for testing, soak the samples for two weeks, thoroughly mixing them each night using a mixer. Soil density and moisture content were measured using both the ring knife method and oven drying method. Saturation was calculated by combining these measurements with the soil specific gravity obtained from the previous hydrometer test.

2) Preconsolidation. After saturation, remove the supernatant from the top of the soil sample using a syringe. Repeat mixing, then take samples from four distinct regions within the sample to determine the moisture content of the saturated soil. Place the saturated soil sample into a centrifuge mold box for preconsolidation lasting 2 years (equivalent to 7.008 h inside the centrifuge).

3) Combined consolidation using water bag + vacuum preloading without pulsation. During vacuum preloading construction, vacuum equipment applies negative pressure to the in-situ foundation. In testing, positive pressure is typically substituted for negative pressure to meet loading requirements26. Sand is placed atop the model box foundation to provide a uniformly distributed positive load. In field applications, negative pressure is supplied at 60 kPa, while water bag loading pressure is 20 kPa, resulting in a total applied pressure of 80 kPa. After soil samples complete preconsolidation, the centrifuge is stopped. Superficial clear liquid is drained from each zone, followed by installation of sand drains and drainage boards, laying of a sand cushion layer, erection of displacement sensor frames, and mounting of displacement sensors. Upon completion of all debugging, the centrifuge is restarted to load to 50 g within 5 min, then continues operating for 0.96 h—equivalent to 100 days of field vacuum preloading. Upon reaching the designed operating time, decelerate the centrifuge until it stops.

4) Combined Consolidation of Water Bag + Vacuum Preloading under Pulsed Action. Compared to the combined consolidation of water bag + vacuum preloading without pulsed action, the primary difference is stopping the centrifuge rotation after 0.32 h of operation. Apply pulsed air pressure to the drainage board using an air compressor, to fracture the consolidated soil columns around the drainage plates. The centrifuge is then restarted. After another 0.32 h of operation, the centrifuge is stopped and pulsed air pressure is applied again. The centrifuge is restarted and continues operating for 0.32 h before decelerating to a stop. Figure 7 shows photographs from centrifugal model tests. (a) depicts the first group of tests without pulsed air pressure, while (b) shows the second group of tests with pulsed air pressure applied.

According to laboratory test results, the time required for complete clogging of the drainage board is approximately 10–20 d, which corresponds to 0.096–0.192 h under the centrifuge model test conditions. In view of the high experimental cost associated with frequent centrifuge stoppages and their potential adverse effects on soil rebound and sensor readings, the number of stoppages was reduced and the interval between successive stoppages was appropriately increased, and this interval was uniformly taken as 0.32 h while ensuring test accuracy.

The pulsed air pressure fluctuates cyclically between 650 kPa and 850 kPa. After system start-up, the pressure increases to 850 kPa, at which point the air compressor stops; the pressure then decays to 650 kPa, triggering the compressor to restart, and this cycle repeats. The entire process lasts for 1 h. The pressure conversion cycle is governed by the soil permeability coefficient: in the initial stage, due to poor permeability and the absence of effective gas pressure pathways, the pressure must overcome the resistance of the silt-clogged layer, resulting in a relatively long cycle of approximately 140 s. In the middle and late stages of operation, the soil surrounding the drainage boards becomes stabilised, the pulsed air pressure can no longer clear the silt-clogged layer, and the cycle consequently shortens and stabilises at around 64 s.

Cross-plate shear test and UU triaxial test

To evaluate the reinforcement effectiveness, both test groups conducted cross-plate shear tests in Zones 1, 2, 3, and 4 before and after the centrifugal vacuum preloading model tests. 4# zones. This determined the shear strength of the cross-plate before and after reinforcement. Samples were collected from each zone to measure soil moisture content. Test results and analysis

After 57.6 min of centrifuge consolidation, the model foundation achieved the consolidation state equivalent to 100 days of vacuum preloading. During the test, various sensors recorded the pore pressure curve, soil pressure curve, and settlement curve, while simultaneously monitoring drainage volume changes. Cross-plate shear tests and moisture content tests were conducted before and after the experiment to determine changes in moisture content and cross-plate shear strength Su. After the experiment, in-situ soil samples were collected and subjected to UU triaxial tests to obtain cu and φu values. Finally, the results from both sets of tests were compared.

Analysis of pore water pressure variation results

The pore pressure variation curves for each region are shown in Fig. 8. As depicted in Fig. 8, the period from 0 to 300 s represents the centrifuge acceleration phase, during which pore pressure increases sharply. Pore pressure in all regions reaches its maximum value around 300 s, corresponding to the centrifuge achieving a 50 g gravitational environment. In the combined consolidation model test of water bag + vacuum preloading without pulse action, the later consolidation phase from 300 to 3800 s represents the vacuum preloading consolidation process maintaining the original stress state. The dissipation rates across all regions were slightly faster between 300 and 800 s, then essentially flattened into a straight line after 800 s, with dissipation rates remaining largely constant. In the pulsed water bag + vacuum preloading combined consolidation model test, data processing removed measurements from the two subsequent periods between centrifuge stops and restart to 50 g. Regions #2, #3, and #4 showed results similar to the non-pulsed test between 300 and 1531 s, with sudden increases at 1582 s and 2755 s. This occurred when the centrifuge stopped and pulsed air pressure was applied, causing an increase in pore water pressure. In contrast, Zone 1, which utilized sand drains for dewatering and did not receive pulsed air pressure, showed no abrupt changes in pore water pressure data at 1582 s and 2755 s. Furthermore, the figure indicates that after applying pulsed air pressure, the decline in pore water pressure accelerated within a short timeframe.

Analysis of soil pressure variation results

The results of soil pressure changes in each area for the two sets of tests are shown in Fig. 9. As seen in Fig. 9, soil pressure exhibits a linear upward trend as the G-value increases. When the G-value reaches 50 g, soil pressure begins to decrease gradually. Analysis indicates that this phenomenon occurs because the soil is saturated. pore water within the soil drained through the drainage plates into the top sand cushion layer, causing natural settlement of the soil mass. However, the soil pressure sensors were fixed to the frame and did not settle with the soil mass. This led to a gradual reduction in the soil mass above the sensors, resulting in a gradual decrease in soil pressure at the sensor locations. The sudden increases observed at 1582 s and 2755 s also stem from soil rebound occurring as the centrifuge stopped, causing an increase in the volume of soil above the sensor.

These were then cut into 38 mm × 76 mm cylindrical specimens using a soil cutter, saturated in saturators, and subjected to UU triaxial tests. The cu and φu values for Zones 1 and 2 are relatively close, as both zones simulate drainage by a single drainage board, resulting in similar soil moisture content. The cu and φu values for Zones 3 and 4 show a marked increase compared to Zones 1 and 2, attributable to a significant reduction in internal soil moisture content.

Comparing the cross-plate shear strength Su with the UU triaxial shear strength cu reveals that Su consistently exceeds cu. Analysis suggests this disparity arises from the substantially higher shear rate in cross-plate testing (typically 6°–12°/min) compared to laboratory triaxial tests (e.g., strain rate 0.1%/min). The cross-plate shear rate substantially exceeds that of the UU test. Excessively high shear rates prevent pore water pressure from dissipating sufficiently, resulting in elevated measured cross-plate shear strength values (Su).

Conclusion

This project employed Wenzhou University’s WZU-150 ultra-gravity centrifuge to simulate vacuum preloading for reinforcing reclaimed land foundations. It validated the effectiveness of combined water bag loading and vacuum preloading under pulsed air pressure for strengthening reclaimed land foundations, yielding the following conclusions:

(1) Tests show that drainage boards fully clog after about 14 days; pore water pressure, earth pressure and settlement responses confirm that pulsed air pressure breaks the silt clogging around the boards and accelerates consolidation, so in similar field conditions it should be applied at intervals no longer than 14 days to control clogging and maintain drainage performance.

(2) Pre- and post-test moisture content measurements, corroborated by drainage volume data during testing, confirmed that pulsed air pressure accelerates soil drainage. Drainage volume increased markedly with additional drainage plates, and the number of drainage plates significantly reduced post-test moisture content.

(3) Comparative analysis of cross-plate shear tests and UU triaxial test results post-experiment clearly demonstrated that soil mechanical properties improved substantially with increased drainage plate numbers following consolidation.
