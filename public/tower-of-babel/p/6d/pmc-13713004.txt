Extension of penA diversity within the genus Neisseria: characterization of a penA/ftsI homolog in Neisseria bacilliformis associated with elevated ceftriaxone MICs
Authors: Sheeba Santhini Manoharan-Basil, Tessa de Block, Izumo Kanesaka, Saïd Abdellati, Thibaut Vanbaelen, Chris Kenyon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReduced susceptibility to extended-spectrum cephalosporins in Neisseria gonorrhoeae is primarily mediated by structural alterations in penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2), encoded by penA, frequently arising through interspecies recombination with commensal Neisseria. While mosaic penA diversity has been extensively characterized in coccoid Neisseria species, rod-shaped commensals such as Neisseria bacilliformis remain poorly explored. Here, we investigated the β-lactam target repertoire of N. bacilliformis using whole-genome sequencing, comparative genomics, mutation profiling, and structural modeling. Fifteen N. bacilliformis genomes were analyzed, including eight isolates with elevated ceftriaxone minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) (range 0.094–1 µg/mL; median 0.5 µg/mL). Interrogation of the PubMLST locus NEIS1753 did not identify matches under default parameters. However, de novo annotation revealed a syntenic high-molecular-mass transpeptidase annotated as ftsI, occupying the conserved rsmH-ftsL-ftsI-murE chromosomal context observed in N. gonorrhoeae. BLASTP analysis demonstrated ~59%–61% amino-acid identity to gonococcal PBP2. Structural modeling using AlphaFold and template-based approaches confirmed preservation of the canonical class B penicillin-binding protein fold, including conserved catalytic motifs. Across 24 positions previously associated with altered cephalosporin susceptibility in gonococci, multiple canonical substitutions were conserved in N. bacilliformis, alongside lineage-specific variants not reported in N. gonorrhoeae. Interestingly, polymorphism at positions A328 and A341 was observed in isolates with higher MICs (1 µg/mL). Definitive genotype-phenotype associations could not be established. Recombination analysis did not identify discrete parental donors but suggested a complex evolutionary history. These findings demonstrate that N. bacilliformis encodes a structurally conserved PBP2 homolog annotated as FtsI and expand the landscape of β-lactam target diversity within the genus Neisseria.

INTRODUCTION

The genus Neisseria comprises a diverse group of gram-negative, oxidase- and catalase-positive bacteria that colonize epithelial surfaces of humans and animals (1, 2). Among the human-restricted species, two principal morphologies are observed: coccoid (Neisseria cinerea, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria polysaccharea, Neisseria lactamica, Neisseria meningitidis, Neisseria mucosa, Neisseria oralis, and Neisseria subflava) and rod-shaped organisms (Neisseria elongata and Neisseria bacilliformis) (3–9). Most members of the genus are commensals of the oro- and nasopharyngeal mucosa. However, N. meningitidis and N. gonorrhoeae are pathogens capable of causing a range of infections, including urethritis, cervicitis, meningitis, septicemia, and disseminated gonococcal disease (10–12).

The genome of N. gonorrhoeae encodes five penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), comprising two essential high-molecular-mass (HMM) transpeptidases and three low-molecular-mass (LMM) class C enzymes (13). The HMM PBPs include PBP1 (ponA), homolog of Escherichia coli PBP1a (mrcA), a class A bifunctional enzyme responsible for both trans glycosylation and transpeptidation during cell growth and elongation, and PBP2 (penA), homolog of E. coli PBP3 (ftsI), a class B septal transpeptidase essential for peptidoglycan cross-linking during cell division (14, 15). In contrast, the three LMM PBPs—PBP3 (dacB), PBP4 (pbpG), and DacC—primarily exhibit carboxypeptidase and/or endopeptidase activity and are not essential for viability but contribute to cell wall remodeling and morphology (16, 17).

PBP2, encoded by penA, is the principal determinant of extended-spectrum cephalosporin susceptibility and constitutes the primary β-lactam target in N. gonorrhoeae (18, 19). Ceftriaxone remains the first-line therapy for N. gonorrhoeae; however, its clinical efficacy is increasingly threatened by the global emergence and dissemination of strains with reduced susceptibility (20). Decreased susceptibility is predominantly mediated by structural alterations in PBP2, most notably through the evolution of mosaic penA alleles generated via interspecies recombination with commensal Neisseria species (21–24). Within genomic surveillance frameworks, penA is curated in the PubMLST Neisseria database as locus NEIS1753, and recent population-level analyses have comprehensively mapped mosaic penA diversity and its commensal origins across multiple Neisseria species within the genus (25). Nevertheless, rod-shaped commensals, including N. elongata and N. bacilliformis, remain underrepresented in this analysis.

Previous studies have substantially advanced our understanding of how commensal Neisseria contributes to the evolutionary reservoir of penA diversity. Building on these analyses, exploration of penA homologs in additional Neisseria species may offer complementary insights, particularly where functionally equivalent loci are annotated under alternative gene names across the genus. N. bacilliformis is an oropharyngeal commensal species that has not been previously included in systematic analyses of penA diversity (25). As clinical breakpoints for ceftriaxone susceptibility have not been established for N. bacilliformis, EUCAST interpretive criteria defined for N. gonorrhoeae were applied when evaluating minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). Recent commensal surveillance in Belgium reported comparatively high ceftriaxone MICs in N. bacilliformis (median 0.5 mg/L; IQR 0.38–0.75) (26). In addition, a clinical N. bacilliformis isolate (isolate id: AR-0943) also showed high ceftriaxone resistance (MIC 16 mg/L) (26, 27). Despite these observations, the contribution of N. bacilliformis to the diversity and evolution of penicillin-binding proteins within the genus remains unexplored. Whether this species harbors a penA equivalent locus, and how such a gene relates structurally and evolutionarily to canonical gonococcal PBP2, has not previously been investigated.

In this study, we use long-read whole-genome sequencing, comparative genomics, and structural modeling to characterize a penA/ftsI homolog in N. bacilliformis. We show that this species encodes a high-molecular-mass transpeptidase annotated as ftsI by Prokka annotation tool (28). The gene occupies a chromosomal context syntenic to gonococcal penA and exhibits strong structural homology to PBP2. Furthermore, recombination detection analysis did not identify statistically supported parental donors, although sequence similarity patterns suggest a complex evolutionary history that cannot be fully resolved with currently available genomes. Notably, the N. bacilliformis transpeptidase shares several amino-acid substitutions with gonococcal PBP2 at positions previously associated with decreased cephalosporin susceptibility. Together, these findings extend current frameworks describing penicillin-binding protein diversity within the genus Neisseria.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study isolates and whole-genome sequencing

Eight N. bacilliformis isolates previously identified during commensal Neisseria surveillance in Belgium with high ceftriaxone (CRO) MIC (Table S1) were selected for whole-genome sequencing (26).

Genomic DNA was extracted from a single colony using the MasterPure Complete DNA and RNA Purification Kit (Epicenter, Madison, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the Oxford Nanopore Technologies Native Barcoding Kit (SQK-NBD114.24) and sequenced on a MinION R10.4 flow cell. The R10 chemistry sequence accuracy is comparable to Illumina platforms and enables stable phylogenetic reconstruction, making it suitable for detecting single-nucleotide polymorphism (29). Base calling was performed with Dorado (v0.9.1; https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado) using the super-accuracy (sup) model. De novo genome assemblies were generated using Autocycler (v0.3.0) (30) and polished using Dorado polish with --bacteria parameter. Assembly quality was assessed using QUAST (v5.3.0) (31). Final assemblies were uploaded to the PubMLST Neisseria database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/neisseria-spp) for locus interrogation and allele assignment. The IDs of the assemblies in PubMLST are as follows: 169223, 169224, 169225, 169226, 169227, 169228, 169229, and 169230.

Gene annotation and locus interrogation

De novo annotation was performed using Prokka (v1.14.6) with default parameters (28). To identify homologs of the canonical gonococcal penA locus, the PubMLST Neisseria database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/neisseria-spp) was used, where penA is curated as locus NEIS1753. Assembled N. bacilliformis genomes were uploaded to PubMLST and initially queried using the default parameters, which resulted in “no-hits” to NEIS1753. A secondary search strategy was then applied using less stringent similarity thresholds (Min %identity: 30, Min % alignment: 10, and BLASTn wordsize: 7) to recover divergent homologs. Candidate loci identified through this approach were further examined through manual inspection and comparative annotation.

Structural modeling and comparative analysis

The full-length FtsI amino-acid sequence from N. bacilliformis was subjected to structural prediction using AlphaFold with default parameters (32). Model confidence was assessed using predicted TM-score (pTM), per-residue predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) values, and predicted aligned error (PAE) matrices. Independent template-based modeling was performed using SWISS-MODEL (33). Template selection was based on sequence identity, coverage, and model quality metrics, including Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE) and QMEANDisCo scores.

To assess chromosomal context, the genomic region spanning rsmH-ftsL-[penA/ftsI]-murE was extracted from each assembly and compared with N. gonorrhoeae FA1090. Gene order and orientation were inspected using CLC Genomics Workbench v25.

Dataset used, allele identification, and representative sequence selection for recombination analysis

In the current study, eight N. bacilliformis were sequenced, from which the ftsI-annotated penA homolog was extracted and combined with seven publicly available N. bacilliformis genomes retrieved from NCBI, resulting in a total of fifteen WGS for N. bacilliformis. For N. gonorrhoeae, all penA alleles (n = 899) were downloaded from NG-STAR database (https://ngstar.canada.ca/alleles/loci_selection?lang=en). N. meningitidis sequences (n = 46,446) corresponding to locus NEIS1753 were exported from the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/neisseria-spp). In addition, penA sequences from commensal Neisseria species (n = 115) were obtained from a previously published commensal Neisseria surveillance study in Belgium (34).

To reduce redundancy and computational burden for recombination detection, penA nucleotide sequences from N. meningitidis data set were clustered using cd-hit-est v4.8.1 at 99% nucleotide identity with a word size of 10 (-c 0.99 -n 10) (35). For each cluster, the centroid sequence selected by cd-hit-est was retained as the representative sequence, while all other sequences sharing ≥99% identity were designated as cluster members. Representative identifiers were extracted from the cd-hit-est output and used to retrieve the corresponding aligned sequences from the multiple sequence alignment generated using MAFFT (v7.453) (36). This resulted in a reduced, non-redundant aligned data set of 611 N. meningitidis sequences suitable for recombination analysis. Cluster information was parsed from the cd-hit-est.clstr file and summarized in a mapping table linking each sequence to its cluster and representative status.

The final data set (n = 1,640) used for recombination detection composed of 15 N. bacilliformis sequences, 899 N. gonorrhoeae alleles, 115 commensal Neisseria sequences, and 611 non-redundant N. meningitidis sequences. The recombination analysis was carried out as described in reference 37. In brief, the Recombination Detection Program (RDP5; v.5.7.5) was used to carry out the analysis (38, 39). Recombinant events supported by at least two of the nine algorithms available in RDP software: RDP, GENECONV, Bootscan, Maxchi, Chimera, SiSscan, PhyloPro, LARD, and 3Seq were used with default settings (38, 40). According to the definition in RDP5, the minor parent is the parental sequence that contributes the smaller fraction of the recombinant sequence, while the major parent is the parental sequence that contributes the larger fraction (38)

Detection of amino-acid substitutions

Amino-acid substitutions within the penA/ftsI alignment were identified through comparison of translated sequences against the reference N. gonorrhoeae FA1090 PBP2 sequence. Alignment positions were mapped to FA1090 amino-acid coordinates, CARD ARO:3004832 reference numbering, and corresponding N. bacilliformis positions to ensure consistent cross-species positional comparison. The data set for this analysis is composed of 15 N. bacilliformis and 899 N. gonorrhoeae penA sequences.

Substitutions previously associated with reduced cephalosporin susceptibility were compiled from three independent sources: (i) Zhang et al (41), (ii) van Hal et al (42), and (iii) the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) (43). Each reported position was manually cross-referenced against the multiple sequence alignment to confirm positional equivalence across species. Presence or absence of the corresponding amino-acid substitution in N. bacilliformis and comparator Neisseria data sets was manually recorded. Positions were categorized according to mosaic or non-mosaic association based on established gonococcal penA classifications. For each site, amino-acid substitution was annotated using FA1090 numbering (e.g., V279A, E285D, K288R, Q291R, A311V, I312M, V316P, A328P, A341P, T483S, T485I, A459T, A501P/T/V, F504LV, A510V, N512Y, A516G, H541N, G542S, G545S, P551L/S, P552V, K555Q, and I556V). Both alignment coordinates and species-specific amino-acid coordinates were retained to enable transparent cross-referencing between data sets. This approach ensured harmonized positional mapping across studies and allowed systematic interrogation of amino-acid substitutions reported in the literature within the context of the N. bacilliformis locus. In the present study, amino-acid substitutions previously reported in association with altered cephalosporin susceptibility and documented in the literature or curated resistance databases were classified as canonical substitutions, and amino-acid substitutions identified that were not previously reported in these sources were designated as non-canonical substitutions.

RESULTS

Genome sequencing and identification of a penA/ftsI homolog in N. bacilliformis

Long-read whole-genome sequencing was performed for eight N. bacilliformis isolates. De novo annotation using Prokka identified a high-molecular-mass class B transpeptidase annotated as ftsI in all isolates. No locus was annotated as penA. Interrogation of the PubMLST Neisseria database, where gonococcal penA is curated as locus NEIS1753, did not yield matches under default allele-matching parameters when N. bacilliformis assemblies were uploaded and queried. However, relaxed similarity thresholds recovered a divergent homolog corresponding to the Prokka-annotated ftsI locus.

Structural conservation of the FtsI transpeptidase domain

AlphaFold modeling predicted a well-defined global fold for N. bacilliformis FtsI (pTM = 0.82), indicating high confidence in overall domain architecture. The core transpeptidase domain exhibited uniformly high per-residue confidence (pLDDT > 90), whereas reduced confidence was confined to the distal C-terminal region, consistent with flexible or membrane-associated segments typical of FtsI homologs (Fig. S1). The PAE matrix demonstrated strong local structural reliability across the catalytic domain.

Template-based modeling using SWISS-MODEL independently corroborated these findings. Among 1,333 candidate templates, the top-ranked structure corresponded to a peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase FtsI homolog (92.19% sequence identity; GMQE = 0.88), indicating high predicted structural accuracy. Crystal structures of N. gonorrhoeae PBP2 (PDB: 5KSH, 3EQV) were also identified among the highest-ranking templates, sharing approximately 60%–61% sequence identity across the aligned region.

Conservation of chromosomal context and synteny

Comparative analysis of chromosomal synteny demonstrated that the N. bacilliformis ftsI locus is positioned within a conserved genomic region spanning the gene order rsmH-ftsL-[ftsI/penA]-murE. This mirrors the gene order observed in N. gonorrhoeae FA1090 (rsmH-ftsL-penA-murE), supporting positional orthology between ftsI in N. bacilliformis and gonococcal penA. No large-scale rearrangements or inversions were observed within this locus across the 15 genomes analyzed.

A total of 24 amino-acid substitution sites previously reported in association with altered cephalosporin susceptibility were interrogated across the 15 N. bacilliformis genomes. Among mosaic-associated positions, several canonical substitutions were uniformly present in all isolates (15/15), including E285D, A311V, I312M, V316P, A459T, and P552V (Table 1). Additional canonical substitutions classified as non-mosaic in gonococci (F504V and A516G) were also conserved across all isolates. In contrast, a subset of substitutions is not currently reported in gonococcal penA alleles, including K288E, Q291E, T485R, N512D, and K555N (Table 1). These substitutions were conserved across all 15 N. bacilliformis isolates.

At positions A328 and A341, intra-species polymorphism was observed. The canonical substitutions A328P and A341P were detected in the same 3/15 isolates, whereas 12/15 isolates encoded threonine at each respective position (Table 1). The A328P variant was observed in 21/899 gonococcal alleles (Table S2). Ceftriaxone MIC data were available only for the eight newly sequenced N. bacilliformis isolates; therefore, Fig. 2 was restricted to these isolates to allow direct visualization of amino acid substitution profiles alongside isolate-specific MIC values. Of the three N. bacilliformis isolates harboring the A328P/A341P substitutions, two originated from the Belgian Neisseria commensal surveillance study and had ceftriaxone MICs of 1 mg/L (Fig. 2 and Tables S1 and S2). Several positions retained the FA1090 reference residue (WT) across all isolates, including V279, T483, A501, A510, G542, G545, P551, and I556.

Recombination analysis

Recombination detection using RDP5 across 1,640 aligned penA/ftsI nucleotide sequences identified 544 putative recombination events. Five of these events (342 and 541–544) involved N. bacilliformis sequences (Table S3). However, none were supported by statistically significant P-values across the nine detection algorithms. For example, event 542 had no significant P-value, and N. meningitidis (71530|NZ16MI0023|New_Zealand|2016) was the recombinant and had corresponding minor parent N. bacilliformis (169223_854_1) and major parent N. gonorrhoeae (penA_344.001; Table S3). Several breakpoint positions were marked as undetermined or potentially confounded by overlapping events. However, in several cases, inferred parental assignments pointed to unknown parents, suggesting that broader genomic sampling of rod-shaped and related commensal Neisseria species may be required to fully resolve the evolutionary origins.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identify a high-molecular-mass class B transpeptidase in Neisseria bacilliformis annotated as ftsI that is syntenic with gonococcal penA and structurally homologous to PBP2. Although no allele matches were detected under default PubMLST interrogation of locus NEIS1753, relaxed similarity searches, comparative genomic, and structural analyses establish this locus as the PBP2 ortholog in N. bacilliformis, embedded within the conserved rsmH-ftsL-ftsI-murE genomic context. This annotation is consistent with the established homology between gonococcal PBP2, encoded by penA, and Escherichia coli PBP3, encoded by ftsI, and therefore reflects annotation nomenclature rather than the absence of a penA-equivalent locus (14, 15). Thus, the absence of a default NEIS1753 match should be interpreted as an annotation and database-match limitation rather than the absence of a penA-equivalent β-lactam target. The high conservation observed across the N. bacilliformis ftsI/penA homolog further supports its classification as an essential class B transpeptidase ortholog. Together, these findings extend current frameworks describing β-lactam target evolution across the genus Neisseria and underscore the importance of annotation-aware comparative genomics.

Alterations in PBP2 are the principal determinant of reduced susceptibility to extended-spectrum cephalosporins in N. gonorrhoeae, particularly through mosaic penA alleles generated by interspecies recombination (15, 25, 44–46). While large-scale genomic analyses have delineated the commensal origins of mosaic penA diversity in coccoid Neisseria (25), rod-shaped taxa such as N. bacilliformis have remained comparatively unexplored. Our analysis demonstrates that N. bacilliformis harbors multiple amino acid substitutions at positions previously implicated in altered cephalosporin susceptibility, including canonical variants described in gonococcal penA alleles as well as lineage-specific substitutions not observed in N. gonorrhoeae. These findings expand the evolutionary landscape of class B transpeptidase diversity within the genus Neisseria.

Structural modeling using AlphaFold and template-based approaches confirmed preservation of the canonical class B PBP architecture, including conserved catalytic motifs and active-site configuration. The high-confidence transpeptidase core supports the interpretation that substitutions identified in N. bacilliformis occur within a structurally conserved scaffold analogous to gonococcal PBP2. This conservation aligns with the essential role of FtsI/PBP2 in septal peptidoglycan cross-linking during cell division (14, 15).

Recent evolutionary analyses of Neisseriaceae further contextualize these findings. Rod-shaped members, including species closely related to N. bacilliformis, undergo longitudinal rather than transverse division while retaining core divisome components such as FtsZ and FtsI (47). Morphological innovation within the family therefore appears to arise through spatial and regulatory reorganization of the divisome rather than replacement of essential peptidoglycan synthesis enzymes. The structural conservation of FtsI observed here supports the notion that β-lactam targets remain evolutionarily conserved even in morphologically divergent lineages.

Across 24 positions previously associated with altered cephalosporin susceptibility in gonococci, N. bacilliformis exhibited both canonical and non-canonical substitutions. Several canonical substitutions frequently observed in mosaic gonococcal penA alleles, including A311V, I312M, V316P, A459T, F504V, A516G, and P552V, were uniformly conserved across isolates. However, these substitutions were not organized into clustered mosaic segments characteristic of highly resistant gonococcal alleles (25, 46, 48), suggesting that their structural context differs in N. bacilliformis. Consistent with functional studies in gonococci demonstrating that elevated ceftriaxone MICs arise from cumulative structural perturbations and epistatic interactions rather than isolated substitutions (14, 19), the presence of individual canonical variants in N. bacilliformis does not imply equivalent phenotypic consequences.

Emerging reports of elevated ceftriaxone MICs in N. bacilliformis underscore the relevance of characterizing β-lactam targets in this species (26). At positions A328 and A341, intra-species polymorphism was observed. The canonical A328P and A341P substitutions were present in 3/15 isolates, while the remaining isolates encoded threonine at both positions. The A328P variant has been reported in 21 of 899 gonococcal penA alleles, indicating that although uncommon, it is not unique to N. bacilliformis. Notably, two N. bacilliformis isolates harboring the A328P/A341P configuration exhibited the highest ceftriaxone MICs in our data set (1 mg/L). These residues localize within the transpeptidase domain, raising the possibility that local conformational effects may influence substrate or β-lactam interactions. However, given the limited sample size and shared substitution background, a direct genotype-phenotype relationship cannot be inferred. Targeted mutagenesis and β-lactam binding assays will be necessary to determine functional impact.

Several substitutions identified in N. bacilliformis, including K288E, Q291E, T485R, N512D, and K555N, have not been reported in gonococcal penA alleles and likely represent lineage-specific diversification of the class B transpeptidase scaffold. Their evolutionary significance remains unclear but may reflect adaptive processes distinct from those driving mosaic penA evolution in N. gonorrhoeae.

Although numerous recombination events were detected across the broader Neisseria data set, only a small subset (n = 5 events) involved N. bacilliformis, and none were statistically significant across the nine methods. Nevertheless, mosaic penA alleles in N. gonorrhoeae are known to arise via interspecies recombination with commensal Neisseria species (25, 44). The absence of discrete breakpoints in N. bacilliformis suggests limited sampling of rod-shaped taxa, and thus, expanded sampling of rod-shaped commensals may be required to resolve the evolutionary exchange.

Notably, isolation of N. bacilliformis in the study by Abdellati et al. was facilitated by the use of oral rinse sampling, consistent with previous observations that this approach increases recovery of commensal Neisseria compared to conventional swab-based methods (26). Incorporation of oral rinse sampling into future surveillance frameworks may therefore improve detection of morphologically and ecologically distinct commensal species that could contribute to the broader β-lactam resistance reservoir.

A further limitation of the study is that the amino-acid substitution analysis in the present study is relative to N. gonorrhoeae, because the canonical substitutions interrogated here were derived from gonococcal ceftriaxone-resistance literature. Although N. meningitidis NEIS1753 sequences and commensal Neisseria sequences were included in the recombination data set, broader genus-wide comparison of substitution frequencies across all Neisseria species was beyond the scope of this study. Such analyses will be important in future work to determine whether substitutions classified here as non-canonical relative to N. gonorrhoeae occur in other commensal or pathogenic Neisseria lineages.

Our findings broaden the evolutionary landscape of β-lactam target diversity within the genus Neisseria and underscore the importance of annotation-aware comparative genomics for identifying homologous resistance-associated loci across species. However, the limited number of currently available N. bacilliformis genomes constrains population-level evolutionary inference. Expanded genomic surveillance of rod-shaped commensal species will therefore be essential to define their contribution to the broader reservoir of β-lactam target diversity with potential relevance to antimicrobial resistance in pathogenic Neisseria.
