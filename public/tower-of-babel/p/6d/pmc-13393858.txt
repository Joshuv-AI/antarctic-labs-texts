Analysis of trait differences and identification of superior early growth combinations in interspecific hybrid progenies of Gleditsia sinensis
Authors: Qingying Zeng, Xiurong Wang, Meixuan Xie, Fuhua Liu, Xingfu Zhang, Yang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterspecific hybridization is a key strategy for germplasm innovation in Gleditsia, yet systematic evaluations of hybrid progeny at the seedling stage remain limited. Effective interspecific breeding relies not only on parental compatibility but also on the genetic plasticity and environmental adaptability of the resulting progeny. A systematic analysis of progeny trait variation is therefore essential for developing informed breeding strategies, enhancing selection efficiency, and accelerating the hybridization process. In the present study, interspecific hybrids of Gleditsia were used as the experimental material. Specifically, Gleditsia sinensis served as the female parent and was crossed with four male parents: Gleditsia japonica var. delavayi, Gleditsia japonica, G. sinensis (intraspecific control), and Gleditsia fera. A comprehensive evaluation of progeny seedlings was conducted by comparing growth traits, root system characteristics, photosynthetic capacity, and chlorophyll fluorescence kinetics, with the aim of identifying superior traits and genetic potential among different hybrid combinations. Significant trait variation was observed among the hybrid combinations. Specifically, G. sinensis × G. fera exhibited superior stomatal conductance, high-light adaptation, average root diameter, and ground diameter, indicating strong hybrid compatibility. G. sinensis × G. delavayi showed the highest photochemical efficiency and electron transport rate, reflecting efficient light utilization. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica performed poorly across most measured traits. Additionally, thorn number exhibited high heritability (h²=79.6%), confirming its utility as a reliable early selection indicator. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for early selection and prediction of environmental adaptability in Gleditsia interspecific hybrid seedlings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09094-5.Keywords: Interspecific hybridization, Photosynthetic characteristics, Heterosis, Genetic variation, Seedling traits

Introduction

The genus Gleditsia comprises multiple tree species distributed across Asia, tropical Africa, and the Americas, with approximately 16 documented species worldwide [1]. In China, this genus includes six species and two varieties (http://www.iplant.cn/info/Gleditsia?t=foc). Among these, G. sinensis is the most well-known and is widely utilized in afforestation, landscape greening, and traditional medicine due to its considerable economic value [2, 3]. G. delavayi and G. japonica are distinct from other Gleditsia species due to their curled pods, while G. fera is recognized for its distinctive leaf morphology and vigorous growth [4]. In G. sinensis, male trees bear only functional staminate flowers, while female trees exhibit morphologically hermaphroditic flowers with abnormal anther development, rendering them unable to produce viable pollen [5]. This partial degeneration of sexual organs promotes outcrossing, thereby enhancing offspring adaptability and genetic diversity [6]. However, current cultivation practices predominantly rely on monocultures of G. sinensis, which may constrain the full expression of its genetic potential and limit stable yield improvement. Consequently, fully exploiting and utilizing the rich germplasm resources within the genus Gleditsia is of significant importance for breeding new cultivars with multifunctionality and high adaptability.

Currently, Gleditsia breeding still relies predominantly on conventional methods. Through systematic phenotypic observation and directional selection, superior varieties with important economic traits—such as being thorn-rich and high-yielding—have been successfully developed [7]. However, conventional breeding is constrained by the limited genetic variation within species, leading to challenges such as long breeding cycles and difficulties in breaking through key trait improvements [8]. Interspecific hybridization serves as a crucial approach for creating novel germplasm and exploiting heterosis. It enables the integration of desirable traits from different species, offering potential for breeding new varieties with stronger adaptability and superior comprehensive traits [9]. To date, research on Gleditsia interspecific hybridization has largely focused on preliminary descriptions of fruit characteristics, while comprehensive comparative studies on hybrid progeny at the seedling stage remain scarce [10]. This gap hinders the early evaluation of hybrid combinations and indirectly prolongs the breeding cycle. By harnessing the diverse genetic backgrounds of Gleditsia species, we aimed to develop hybrids with strong growth performance and broad physiological adaptability.

The phenotypic of hybrid progeny is a complex outcome resulting from the interactions between parental genomes, epigenetic regulation, and environmental factors [11]. The seedling stage represents a critical phase in the plant life cycle, characterized by high sensitivity to environmental changes and considerable phenotypic plasticity [12]. Early-stage traits such as root system architecture, photosynthetic efficiency, and photosystem stability not only directly influence establishment success and early competitive ability but also serve as important indicators for predicting long-term growth performance and environmental adaptation potential [13, 14]. Comprehensive evaluation of early growth in hybrid offspring enables effective screening of superior parental combinations. For instance, growth trait assessment at the seedling stage in Robinia pseudoacacia progeny can identify superior provenances and families, while studies on Pinus armandii progeny from mixed pollination have shown that tree height and ground diameter exhibit high heritability, providing valuable criteria for parental selection [15, 16]. This strategy of inferring parental genetic potential based on progeny performance offers a more scientific and efficient technical pathway for forest tree hybridization breeding.

Our preliminary studies have confirmed the reproductive compatibility among interspecific combinations within the Gleditsia genus and have observed significant morphological and size variations in hybrid pods, thereby establishing a feasibility basis for targeted hybridization breeding [10]. However, the success of interspecific hybridization lies not only in obtaining fertile offspring, but in the stable expression of target traits and favorable adaptive plasticity in the progeny [17]. Therefore, in this study, G. sinensis was used as the maternal parent and artificially crossed with G. sinensis, G. delavayi, G. japonica, and G. fera, respectively. Using progeny seedlings, we systematically compared seedling-stage growth traits, root system architecture, and chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics across different hybrid combinations. This study aims to elucidate the advantageous traits and genetic quality of each hybrid progeny, identify hybrid combinations with early growth superiority, and thereby provide a theoretical basis for early selection and environmental adaptability prediction in Gleditsia interspecific hybrid progeny.

Materials and methods

Overview of the experimental site and materials

The hybridization experiment was conducted in a G. sinensis plantation in Maochang Town, Zhijin County, Guizhou Province (26°32’19.554” N, 106°2’14.455” E). Six robust, pest and disease free grafted female G. sinensis trees of similar size and age were selected as maternal parents. The scions of all trees were derived from the same donor plant; the grafted trees were approximately 12 years old, with a mean trunk diameter of 15.7 cm and a height of 6–8 m. Male parent pollen from four different Gleditsia species—G. delavayi, G. japonica, G. sinensis, and G. fera—was collected from selected locations in Guizhou Province, including Huaxi District (Guiyang), Wudang District (Guiyang), Zhijin County (Bijie), and Anlong County (Qianxinan Prefecture). For paternal pollen collection, open inflorescences were harvested from each paternal species during their respective peak blooming periods. Anthers that had not yet dehisced were manually excised using fine-tipped forceps and placed in a desiccator to dry for 12 h to promote pollen release. Following dehiscence, the anthers were transferred to small drying vials containing color-changing silica gel. After pollen viability was assessed, the vials were stored in darkness at -80℃ until use. The detailed information on the paternal sources is provided in Supplementary Table 1, and detailed flowering phenology data for the hybrid parental species are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. All plant materials were collected with permission from local forest farms or forestry authorities. The species were formally identified by Professor Yang Zhao from Guizhou University. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Herbarium of the College of Forestry, Guizhou University (voucher numbers: GZU.G2023016-GZU.G2023019). All experimental research on plants complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines. Field studies were conducted in accordance with local legislation in Guizhou Province, China.

Hand pollination

For maternal pollination, prior to anthesis, well-developed inflorescences with petals not yet fully open (i.e., unfertilized) were selected from all directions of the maternal trees as pollination targets. Based on previously determined pollen grain size, 500-mesh pollen isolation bags were used to cover the selected inflorescences to prevent external pollen contamination. Prior to artificial pollination, flowers were observed daily until the stigmas became enlarged and secreted visible mucilage, indicating a moist and receptive state. Pollination was then performed on clear, windless days. Before pollination, pollen viability of the paternal parents and stigma receptivity of the maternal flowers were assessed. Subsequently, a pollination stick was used to gently apply pollen from each paternal species onto the stigmas of the female flowers. Each inflorescence was pollinated twice on consecutive days to maximize fertilization success. Immediately after pollination, the isolation mesh bags were sealed, and the pollination date and number of pollinated flowers were recorded on labeled tags. The isolation bags were removed 10 days after pollination. Approximately 250–350 flowers were pollinated per tree for each cross combination. The collection of paternal pollen and the hand pollination experiments were conducted during the flowering period from late April to early May 2024. The hybrid seeds were subsequently harvested when the pods reached full maturity in October of the same year. The experimental trees were maintained under standard cultivation practices with balanced fruit loads and protected by insect-proof nets to prevent pest and disease damage.

Cultivation of hybrid progeny seedlings

From January to March of the following year, the harvested hybrid seeds were germinated and grown into seedlings. Seeds from each hybrid combination were scarified using sandpaper and then immersed in a constant-temperature water bath at 35 °C to promote full imbibition. For germination testing, 200 fully imbibed seeds per combination were selected and placed in germination boxes (50 seeds per box) with four replicates. The germination experiment was conducted in an artificial climate chamber set at 25 °C, 80% relative humidity, and a 12-hour photoperiod. A commercial nutrient soil (Zhonghe Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd.) was sieved through a 2-mm mesh and used as the growth substrate. Successfully germinated seeds were transplanted into seedling pots. All seedlings were then grown under uniform, routine management in the experimental greenhouse of the Forestry College, Guizhou University (106.66207° E, 26.45167° N) under controlled and uniform conditions. The greenhouse temperature was maintained at 25 ± 3 °C during the day and 18 ± 2 °C at night, with relative humidity ranging from 60% to 75%. Seedlings were irrigated every two days with tap water to maintain the substrate near field capacity, and a half‑strength Hoagland nutrient solution was applied weekly. All seedlings were randomly repositioned every two weeks to minimize positional effects within the greenhouse. After 60 days of cultivation, growth and physiological indices of the hybrid progeny seedlings were measured.

After 60 days of uniform cultivation, 30 seedlings were randomly selected from each pollination combination. Seedling height (accurate to 0.1 cm) and ground diameter (accurate to 0.01 mm) were measured using a ruler and a vernier caliper, respectively. The number of leaf pairs and thorns was also recorded.

Leaf trait measurements

Ten seedlings were randomly selected from each pollination combination, and nine mature leaves were collected from the middle-upper portion of each seedling. Leaf images were obtained using a leaf scanner, and leaf length, width, and area were measured using ImageJ software. Leaf margin morphology was observed under a stereomicroscope. Leaves were treated with a NaOH-Na2CO3 mixed solution (2:1) to soften the mesophyll tissue. After ultrasonic cleaning to remove the mesophyll, the retained vein networks were stained, mounted on glass slides, and photographed. Total vein length, vein density, and the number of vein junctions were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Photosynthetic characteristics and chlorophyll fluorescence imaging

Photosynthetic parameters, including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), stomatal conductance (Gs), water use efficiency (Wue), and intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), were measured on clear days between 9:00 and 11:00 AM using a Li-6800 portable photosynthesis system (Li‑Cor Inc., USA). The relative chlorophyll content (SPAD) was simultaneously determined with a chlorophyll meter. For each hybrid combination, five seedlings were randomly selected, and three mature functional leaves from the same nodal position per seedling were measured.

Chlorophyll fluorescence imaging was performed during the same time period using a FluorCam chlorophyll fluorescence imaging system (Beijing EcoTech Technology Co., Ltd., China). Prior to measurement, five randomly selected seedlings per combination were dark‑adapted for 20 min and then placed in the imaging chamber. The quenching analysis protocol was run with an Fo duration of 5 s and a saturation pulse of 800 ms to obtain fluorescence quenching parameters, including minimal fluorescence yield (Fo), maximal fluorescence yield (Fm), maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm), photochemical quenching coefficient (qp), non‑photochemical quenching coefficient (qn), and the steady‑state fluorescence decline ratio (Rfd). Subsequently, a light‑curve protocol with 11 photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) steps ranging from 0 to 2040 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ was executed to obtain the electron transport rate (ETR) at different PAR levels. Rapid light-response curves were subsequently fitted based on the measured ETR data.

Determination of root system indices

Five seedlings were randomly selected from each hybrid combination. After collecting the complete root systems, they were gently washed and scanned using a multifunctional root analysis system (Epson Expression 12000XL, Japan). Root images were then analyzed with WinRHIZO software to obtain root characteristic parameters, including total root length, total root surface area, average root diameter, and number of root tips.

Data processing and analysis

Mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation were calculated using Microsoft Excel. All the measured trait values were subjected to one-way analysis of variance and multiple comparisons using IBM SPSS Statistics 23. Prior to ANOVA, the normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test. The detailed test results are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, confirming that the assumptions were generally satisfied. When ANOVA indicated significant differences among hybrid combinations (p < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test. The growth characteristics of hybrid progeny seedlings

As shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2, there were significant differences in seedling growth traits and the degree of variation among the different hybrid combinations. Plant height exhibited moderate variation among combinations, with coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 19.81% to 28%. Seedlings from G. sinensis × G. sinensis and G. sinensis × G. delavayi showed superior plant height, with the former having the widest range (8.1–26.6 cm). In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica seedlings exhibited the poorest stability in height, with the highest coefficient of variation of 28%. Regarding branch number, G. sinensis × G. delavayi seedlings produced the highest number of branches, significantly exceeding other combinations, with a CV of 18.72% and a relatively wide variation range. The seedlings of G. sinensis × G. fera exhibited a relatively robust stem base and the largest ground diameter, with a coefficient of variation of 10.88%. For thorn number, the seedlings of G. sinensis × G. sinensis and G. sinensis × G. fera exhibited significantly higher counts than the other combinations, with relatively low coefficients of variation ranging from 15% to 16.41%. In contrast, seedlings with G. delavayi and G. japonica as paternal parents had fewer thorns and higher coefficient of variation (40.05%–46.58%). Based on the results of heritability analysis (Table 2), thorn number and branch number exhibited relatively high heritability, with thorn number showing a particularly high heritability of 79.6%. In contrast, seedling height and ground diameter displayed lower heritability. The heritability values of the traits, in descending order, were: thorn number > branch number > plant height > ground diameter.

Chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics of hybrid progeny seedlings

Chlorophyll fluorescence, as a probe in photosynthesis research, can intuitively reflect the intrinsic characteristics of plants. By comparing the chlorophyll fluorescence of whole hybrid seedlings (Figs. 5 and 6), obvious spatial heterogeneity was observed in the chlorophyll fluorescence among seedlings from different hybrid combinations. Seedlings of G. sinensis × G. delavayi showed significantly higher Fv/Fm than those with G. japonica or G. fera as paternal parents, but their qp and Rfd were significantly lower, suggesting a possible greater focus on efficient light energy capture. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. sinensis seedlings exhibited higher qp and Rfd along with lower Fm, while seedlings with G. japonica as the paternal parent displayed the highest Fm. These differences reflect divergence among hybrid progeny in the operational efficiency of the photosystem and their strategies for energy allocation.

Rapid light curves of hybrid progeny seedlings

Rapid light curves differed significantly among seedlings of different hybrid combinations (Fig. 7). As the photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) increased, the curves gradually reached a plateau, indicating that electron transport became the primary limiting factor for photosynthesis, and photosynthetic capacity leveled off. Among the combinations, G. sinensis × G. delavayi seedlings showed the highest electron transport rate (ETR), reflecting a strong capacity for light‑energy conversion. Analysis of the rapid light curve parameters (Table 5) further revealed that G. sinensis × G. delavayi and G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings exhibited relatively high maximum relative electron transport rates (rETRmax), suggesting that both could maintain robust electron transport under high‑light conditions and possessed considerable photosynthetic potential. The half‑saturation light intensity (Ik) was highest in G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings, indicating a stronger adaptation to high‑light environments. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica seedlings reached light saturation at relatively low PAR, reflecting a more limited ability to utilize intense light.

Over-standard heterosis of traits in interspecific hybrid progeny seedlings

Taking the G. sinensis × G. sinensis combination as the reference, seedlings from different interspecific crosses exhibited differentiated over-standard heterosis across multiple traits (Fig. 8). Specifically, the G. sinensis × G. japonica combination showed significant heterosis in leaf traits such as leaf area and leaf length, but performed weakly in most other traits. The G. sinensis × G. delavayi combination exhibits an advantage in the branch number of seedlings but shows a disadvantage in thorn number. In contrast, the G. sinensis × G. fera combination demonstrated strong heterosis in ground diameter, average root diameter, stomatal conductance, and half‑saturation light intensity. However, for root‑system traits such as total root length, number of branches, and number of intersections, all interspecific hybrid seedlings performed inferior to the intraspecific G. sinensis × G. sinensis seedlings, which demonstrated a clear advantage. This suggests that such fundamental growth traits may possess higher genetic stability and expression advantages under intraspecific hybridization.

Discussion

Leaf morphological traits are important manifestations of plant adaptation and evolution, exhibiting rich diversity in shape, structure, and arrangement. These characteristics reflect adaptive strategies and also provide key references for taxonomy [18, 19]. In this study, seedlings with G. fera as the paternal parent displayed distinct serrated leaf margins, dark green coloration, and evergreen habit, clearly distinguishing them from other combinations. This indicates that the trait was consistently expressed across all F₁ progeny, likely under strong paternal control at this early stage. The key leaf morphological traits of woody plants are typically regulated by major genetic factors and can be used as reliable early selection markers [20]. Structural traits such as leaf area, leaf length, and vein density directly influence water and nutrient transport as well as photosynthetic efficiency in plants [21, 22]. In the leaves of higher plants, veins organized in specific patterns (venation) form a vascular network responsible for transporting nutrients, signaling molecules, and water [23]. Research has proposed that the leaf venation network is crucial for leaf hydraulic conductance, thus influencing photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance [24–26]. In this study, G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings exhibited the highest number of vein junctions and the greatest total vein length, indicating a well‑developed and complex venation system conducive to enhanced resource transport capacity [27]. Additionally, this hybrid combination exhibited higher stomatal conductance, intercellular CO₂ concentration, and relative chlorophyll content in the leaves. Although high stomatal conductance increases water loss through transpiration, its well-developed venation system may compensate for this loss by enabling efficient water transport. This suggests that the hybrid may possess enhanced gas exchange capacity and photosynthetic potential, allowing for greater CO₂ uptake and thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency [27, 28]. The leaves of G. sinensis × G. sinensis seedlings exhibited the highest vein density, which may confer greater mechanical strength and support [29, 30]. Additionally, the increased vein density shortens the average distance between mesophyll cells and vascular bundles, contributing to effective water management and reduced transpirational losses, which may partly explain the higher water use efficiency observed in these leaves [22, 31]. Moreover, the leaves of G. sinensis × G. sinensis seedlings demonstrated a high net photosynthetic rate alongside a low intercellular CO₂ concentration, reflecting efficient CO₂ utilization that favors biomass accumulation and rapid growth. This coordinated leaf-level physiology aligns well with the superior growth traits observed in this combination, suggesting that high photosynthetic water-use efficiency is a key driver of early growth vigor in Gleditsia hybrids [32].

Seedling growth traits are important indicators for evaluating seedling quality. Analyzing their early-stage performance and genetic variability helps optimize breeding strategies and accelerate the selection of superior cultivars [33, 34]. In this study, analysis of variance revealed significant differences among hybrid combinations in plant height, ground diameter, branch number, and thorn number, indicating substantial genetic variation among the crosses. Among them, seedlings with G. delavayi or G. sinensis as the paternal parent performed notably well in plant height and branch number, with wide variation ranges. In contrast, seedlings with G. japonica as the paternal parent showed the poorest in plant height and ground diameter, along with a high coefficient of variation in plant height. This may be attributed to the greater genetic distance between the parents, leading to unfavorable phenotypic segregation in the hybrid progeny [35]. Furthermore, the performance of seedling growth traits followed a trend consistent with their germination capacity. Specifically, the germination energy and germination index of seeds with G. delavayi and G. sinensis as the male parent were significantly higher than those of the other hybrid combinations. This may partly explain their superior performance in seedling height and branch number. Previous studies on endosperm quality also indicated that G. sinensis × G. delavayi hybrid seeds possess favorable endosperm traits and higher hundred-seed weight, which likely provided a material basis for their higher germination rate and germination potential [10]. Heritability reflects the contribution of genetic factors to trait variation and serves as an important reference for assessing the genetic potential of traits and guiding breeding selection [36, 37]. In our study, owing to the constraints of the mating design, heritability was approximated using variance component estimates, and these values may be inflated by factors not fully controlled in the current experimental design. Nevertheless, the approximate heritability estimates still provide some indication of the degree to which different traits are under genetic control and their relative efficiency for early selection, thereby offering preliminary guidance for refining future breeding strategies. This study found that thorn and branch number exhibited high paternal variance component estimates (approximate heritability), indicating that their variation is substantially influenced by paternal genetic effects. Notably, the phenotypic variation in thorn number is largely driven by genetic differences, therefore exhibiting high selection efficiency, whereas branch number can be improved through family selection. The G. sinensis × G. sinensis seedlings exhibited the highest thorn number with a low coefficient of variation, while the interspecific hybrid seedlings with G. delavayi and G. japonica as the male parent showed fewer thorns and greater variability. This may be due to the introduction of non-additive genetic effects, such as dominance or epistasis, in interspecific hybridization, leading to unstable trait expression. In contrast, the heritability of height and ground diameter is low, and they are significantly influenced by non-additive genetic effects (e.g., dominance) and environmental interactions [38]. Future studies adopting half-sib or full-sib family mating designs with multiple maternal parents would enable a more rigorous partitioning of additive genetic variance, dominance variance, and maternal genetic effects, thereby yielding more accurate heritability estimates and genetic analyses. Regarding over-standard heterosis across the traits, G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings exhibited clear positive heterosis in average root diameter, stomatal conductance, and half‑saturation light intensity. These advantages align with their larger ground diameter and stronger photosynthetic capacity, likely resulting from the synergistic interaction between the deep‑rooting characteristic of G. fera and the high‑light‑use efficiency of G. sinensis in the hybrid combination. This coordinated above‑ and below‑ground performance highlights the importance of simultaneously evaluating root and shoot traits in early hybrid screening. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica seedlings performed poorly in most traits, with particularly inferior root system architecture, which likely contributed to their significantly lower plant height and ground diameter compared to other combinations.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters serve as important indicators for assessing the photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (PSII) and the photosynthetic acclimation capacity of plants [39]. They reflect intrinsic processes such as light energy absorption, transfer, allocation, and dissipation, enabling the detection of subtle differences in the activity of photosynthetic apparatus [40]. Among these parameters, maximum fluorescence yield (Fm) is the fluorescence yield when all PSII reaction centers are closed, reflecting the electron transport activity through PSII, while the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) indicates the efficiency of primary photochemical energy conversion within the PSII reaction centers [41]. In this study, G. sinensis × G. delavayi seedlings exhibited higher Fv/Fm and Fm, indicating superior performance in light capture and conversion. Although the G. sinensis × G. sinensis combination showed lower Fm, it displayed the highest fluorescence decline ratio (Rfd), suggesting that despite lower light-harvesting efficiency, this combination possesses high electron transport chain activity and can rapidly regulate and utilize captured light energy to maintain high photosynthetic efficiency. These fluorescence characteristics are consistent with the growth patterns described above, where efficient light processing translates into better biomass accumulation. Rapid light curves (RLCs) provide further insights into the saturation characteristics of electron transport and overall photosynthetic performance. The rETRmax represents potential photosynthetic capacity, while the Ik reflects the plant’s adaptability to high light [42, 43]. In this study, G. sinensis × G. delavayi seedlings had the highest rETRmax, which may be supported by their higher Fv/Fm, indicating strong electron transport and photochemical performance. The G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings exhibited high values for both rETRmax and Ik, suggesting that they can maintain effective photosynthesis even under high-light conditions. This aligns with their higher stomatal conductance and other photosynthetic parameters, reflecting good high-light adaptability. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica seedlings had the lowest Ik, indicating that they reached light saturation at relatively low irradiance. Their relatively high Fm may help maintain certain photosynthetic efficiency under low light, but could limit photosynthetic performance under high light [44].

Roots are vital organs for plants to absorb nutrients and water. Parameters such as root branch number, intersection count, total root length, and average diameter serve as important indicators for evaluating root structure and function, significantly influencing plant growth and resource acquisition capacity [45]. Therefore, a rational root architecture is an essential prerequisite for ensuring high plant yield and efficient nutrient acquisition. Genotype plays a major role in determining root morphology, which in turn affects water and nutrient use efficiency of the plant [46]. Numerous studies have also demonstrated significant genotypic variation in root system traits [47]. In this study, seedlings with G. sinensis or G. fera as the male parent performed excellently in most root parameters, exhibiting more robust root systems and stronger resource acquisition capacity. In particular, the hybrid seedlings with G. sinensis as the male parent ranked first in root branch number, intersection count, and total root length. A more extensive and highly branched root system is known to enhance soil exploration and water acquisition capacity, which in turn can support higher stomatal conductance while minimizing leaf-level water loss, thereby contributing to improved intrinsic water use efficiency [48]. Moreover, this combination had finer roots, and fine roots (typically < 1 mm in diameter) play a dominant role in water and nutrient absorption, directly influencing radial water transport [49]. Consistently, this combination also exhibited the highest WUE values among all hybrid progenies (Table 4), suggesting that superior root water acquisition likely contributed to the higher WUE observed in this combination. This functional coupling between root architecture and leaf-level WUE may represent a key adaptive advantage for this hybrid combination under water-limited conditions. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. fera seedlings exhibited the largest average root diameter. Thicker roots generally provide better mechanical support, which may help the plant cope with environmental stress. Furthermore, plants with thicker roots often possess larger-diameter vessels (such as xylem vessels), facilitating more efficient transport of water and nutrients from the roots to the aboveground parts and enhancing overall transport efficiency [50, 51]. Overall, the integration of chlorophyll fluorescence with leaf gas exchange and root traits reveals how different hybrid combinations achieve photosynthetic success through distinct physiological strategies, further reinforcing the value of a multi‑trait selection approach in hybrid breeding programs. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. For root traits with high variability, substantial replication is typically required to ensure adequate statistical power; the relatively small sample size in this study may constrain the ability to detect significant differences. Furthermore, all seedlings were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions, and while this minimizes environmental noise and aids genetic interpretation, it also means that the physiological and growth data should be extrapolated to field performance with caution. Therefore, future investigations should employ larger sample sizes, repeated evaluations across multiple growing seasons, and field validation trials to confirm the performance of these promising hybrid combinations under natural conditions.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the phenotypic and physiological traits of interspecific hybrid progeny at the seedling stage, with Gleditsia sinensis as the maternal parent crossed with different paternal species. The results demonstrate that interspecific hybridization within the genus Gleditsia generates substantial trait variation at the early growth stage, and different hybrid combinations exhibit divergent adaptive strategies and distinct patterns of early heterosis. Compared with the intraspecific control, hybridization with G. fera as the paternal parent significantly enhanced Gs, IK, root diameter, and ground diameter, indicating strong hybrid compatibility with G. sinensis and excellent cultivation potential under high-light environments. Hybridization with G. delavayi showed outstanding performance in photochemical efficiency, maximum electron transport rate, and branch number, reflecting efficient light utilization and vigorous early growth vigor, making it a promising candidate for productivity-oriented breeding. In contrast, G. sinensis × G. japonica performed poorly in root architecture and photosynthesis, suggesting limited combining ability. Notably, the intraspecific control maintained advantages in root traits and thorn number, the latter showing high approximate heritability and serving as a reliable early selection indicator. Therefore, these findings provide a strategic basis for early selection of Gleditsia hybrids, enabling flexible paternal parent selection according to breeding goals to shorten the breeding cycle and develop novel germplasm with enhanced adaptability and productivity.
