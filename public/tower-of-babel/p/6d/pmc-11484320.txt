Prognostic value of albumin-based indices for mortality after heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ji Jiang, Ping Miao, Gang Xin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlbumin-based indices have been used to predict mortality after heart failure (HF). We hereby examined the utility of albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, blood urea nitrogen (BUN)-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin ratio, and albumin-bilirubin score (ABS) in predicting mortality after HF.MethodsWe searched Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases up to 2nd October 2024 for studies examining association between albumin-based indices and mortality after HF. A random-effect meta-analysis was conducted using multivariate adjusted data to calculate pooled hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subgroup analysis was conducted for short-term (< 90 days) and long-term mortality (≥ 1 year) where possible.ResultsThere were seven studies on ABS, three studies each on BUN-albumin and fibrinogen-albumin ratio, and two studies each for albumin-globulin and lactate-albumin ratio. Meta-analysis showed that ABS was predictive of both short-term (HR: 2.01 95% CI: 1.01, 4.00 I2 = 62% p = 0.05) and long-term (HR: 1.36 95% CI: 1.05, 1.76 I2 = 49% p = 0.02) mortality after HF. Meta-analysis also showed that high fibrinogen-albumin ratio (HR: 1.12 95% CI: 1.09, 1.14 I2 = 0% p < 0.00001), BUN-albumin ratio (HR: 1.84 95% CI: 1.28, 2.64 I2 = 64% p = 0.001), and lactate-albumin ratio (HR: 1.84 95% CI: 1.68, 2.01 I2 = 0% p < 0.00001) were predictors of mortality after HF.ConclusionVery low quality of evidence indicates that albumin-based indices can predict mortality after HF. ABS was found to independently predict short and long-term mortality after HF. Other ratios like the albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, and fibrinogen-albumin ratio were also found to predict mortality after HF albeit with limited data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04244-9.Keywords: Malnutrition, Prognosis, Heart failure, Cardiovascular, Survival, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a highly prevalent cardiac disorder characterized by symptoms and/or signs due to a structural and/or functional cardiac abnormality [1]. Its prevalence in Europe is reported to be 1–2% with an incidence of 5 cases per 1000 person-years in adults. It is also a common cause of cardiac mortality with data suggesting a 13% increase in the number of death from HF in the United States of America (USA) between the years 2009–2014 [2]. A range of conditions like metabolic disorders, systemic diseases, and hereditary defects can cause HF [3]. Owing to the steady increase in lifestyle-related diseases, the prevalence of HF is estimated to increase in the near future. Moreover, the prognosis of HF remains poor with 1/3rd of patients not surviving after one year of diagnosis [4]. Based on the European Society of Cardiology Heart Failure Long-Term Registry, long-term mortality after acute and chronic HF is 23.6% and 6.4% respectively [5]. Given the substantial mortality burden of HF, prognostic indicators must be identified that can guide treatment and help in the risk stratification of patients [6].

There has been intense research on the development of prognostic markers for HF [7–9]. On one hand, there are uncommonly used plasma biomarkers like N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, angiopoietin-2, C-reactive protein, fibroblast growth factor-23 and osteoprotegerin which can predict outcomes but are not routinely available in every healthcare setup [9]. Further, a single biomarker approach generally quantifies only one pathophysiologic pathway, which may not be suitable for risk stratification and adverse outcome prediction in a heterogenous disorder like HF which has several phenotypes. On the other hand, there are several scores like Emergency Heart Failure Mortality Risk Grade, Ottawa Heart Failure Risk Scale, Multiple Estimation of Risk based on the Emergency Department Spanish Score, Get With the Guidelines Heart Failure, etc. which require multiple values, are time-consuming, and not easily adaptable to every clinical practice [8]. Hence, there is a need for simple, rapidly calculable, and routinely available indices to predict mortality after HF.

Systemic inflammation is a well-recognized in the pathophysiology of HF [10]. A large pool of evidence shows that adverse outcomes of HF are perpetuated by a prothrombotic state linked to chronic inflammation [11]. Albumin is a widely used marker of inflammation and is a known prognostic factor for cardiac diseases including HF [12]. Hypoalbuminemia can independently predict long-term mortality in patients with coronary artery diseases and its predictive ability is increased in combination with other markers [13]. To overcome the drawbacks of a single biological marker, several albumin-based markers like albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, blood urea nitrogen (BUN)-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin ratio and albumin-bilirubin score (ABS) have been recently reported in the literature to predict mortality after HF. Kurniawan et al. [14] in 2024 reviewed the evidence on one such marker i.e. the C-reactive protein-albumin ratio, and found it to predict HF survival. However, no review has comprehensively examined the utility of other albumin-based markers. Our study collated data from the literature to provide high-quality evidence on the prognostic ability of the albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin ratio, and ABS for HF.

Materials and methods

Search methodology

This review was registered on PROSPERO under the number CRD42024519072. The protocol can be accessed at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=519072. PRISMA guidelines were followed [15]. We began with a detailed and systematic search of the publication repositories of Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search was concluded by two reviewers. All peer-reviewed articles irrespective of the date of publication were searched provided they were online till 2nd October 2024. We also conduct manual searches on https://clinicaltrials.gov and Google Scholar to ensure comprehensive coverage of gray literature. Literature was explored using the search query ((((((bilirubin) OR (fibrinogen)) OR (blood urea nitrogen)) OR (lactate)) OR (globulin)) AND (albumin)) AND (heart failure). The query was common for all databases.

The search outcomes of all repositories were collated and then deduplicated in reference manager software (EndNote, Clarivate, USA). Primary eligibility was assessed by reading the titles and abstracts of the unique articles. Two investigators were involved in the study screening. Irrelevant articles were eliminated and potentially eligible studies were acquired for further review. The investigators read the full texts and selected eligible articles for this review. We also hand-searched the references of all studies to check any missed articles.

Eligibility criteria

The current review included studies based on the following PECOS criteria:

Population: Any type of HF; Exposure: High values of albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin, or ABS; Comparison: Low values of albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin, or ABS; Outcome: Mortality reported as a multivariate-adjusted ratio with 95% confidence intervals (CI); Study type: Cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control studies.

Studies not on any of the predetermined ratios, not exclusively on HF, reporting only univariate data, reporting only Kaplan-Meier curves and not numerical data, published in non-English language or only as abstracts were excluded. If two articles reported duplicate sample sizes with the same ratio, we included the one with most participants. If two articles reported only a partial overlap of data, we included both studies.

Quality of studies

The Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to examine the risk of bias [16]. The tool examines three study parameters. The maximum possible total points are 4 points for selection, 2 points for comparability, and 3 points for outcomes. Studies with ≥ 7 points were considered as “good”, 2 to 6 points as “fair”, and ≤ 1 point as “poor” quality [14]. Risk of bias assessment was performed independently by two authors. Any disagreement was discussed with the senior author for a solution.

Data management

Relevant data extracted from studies included: name of author, study type, location, included HF patients, sample size, demographic details, New York Heart Association class, diabetes, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ischemic etiology of HF, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), medications (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, beta blocker), cut-off of the ratio, adjusted factors, and follow-up. All data was sourced by two authors in a pre-formatted table and cross-checked for any mistakes.

Outcome data as effect size with 95% CI was extracted from each study. Based on availability, separate data was extracted for the ratios used as continuous or categorical variables. Meta-analysis was planned only if two studies were reporting a homogenous form of data. A narrative synthesis was planned when a meta-analysis was not possible. Detailed individual study results were also reported when there were ≤ 3 studies for a ratio.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). All study-specific ratios were amalgamated using inverse variance-weighted averages of logarithmic hazard ratios (HRs). We used the random-effects model. If the study divided the sample into tertiles or quartiles, we combined the different groups into a single group using the review manager software.

Heterogeneity among studies was quantified by obtaining the I values and the Chi-square test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and an I2 value of 50% or more indicated the presence of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine how each study contributed to the pooled estimate. We sequentially excluded individual studies and recalculating the pooled HR for the remaining studies. The reanalysis of data was done in the software itself. Publication bias was assessed by the funnel plot and Egger’s test. A subgroup analysis based on the timing of mortality was conducted where possible. Short-term mortality was defined as mortality at < 90 days while long-term mortality was mortality after ≥ 1 year. We examined the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tool based on the GRADEpro GDT software.

Results

Search results

The reviewers obtained 2146 articles from all databases. Of these, 1194 were overlapping and hence removed. The remaining 952 were screened and 35 were selected for further analysis. Studies not reporting on the predetermined ratios, not exclusively reporting on HF, not reporting on mortality, and duplicate data were excluded. Seventeen studies [17–33] finally met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). No disagreements were recorded between reviewers for inclusion of the studies. No additional studies were identified on gray literature and reference search.

Study flowchart

Baseline information

Study details extracted by the authors are presented in Table 1. Studies were segregated based on the ratio studied. There were seven studies on ABS [17, 18, 26–30], three studies each on BUN-albumin ratio [19, 20, 33] and fibrinogen-albumin [21, 22, 25], and two studies each for albumin-globulin [23, 24] and lactate-albumin ratio [31, 32]. No study reported the use of more than one ratio. Studies were predominantly on Asian populations mostly from China and Japan. Five studies [19, 21, 28, 31, 32] used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care derived from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Boston, USA [34, 35]. The two studies [31, 32] on lactate-albumin ratio had a partial overlap of data of 4 years. All studies were retrospective, except for one study each on albumin-bilirubin [26] and BUN-albumin ratios [33], which were prospective. The majority of studies included both acute and chronic HF patients. Three studies each included only chronic HF [20, 22, 30] or acute HF [25–27]. Two studies on ABS included HF patients undergoing cardiac resynchronization therapy. The pooled sample size for studies on ABS, BUN-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin, albumin-globulin, and lactate-albumin ratio was 8954, 4313, 3156, 1403, and 8253 respectively. All studies recorded the ratios either at admission or before beginning therapy. The study population was predominantly above the age of 50 years across studies. A male predominance was noted across the majority of studies. Data on comorbidities, ischemic etiology, and LVEF was not consistently reported by the included studies. ABS

There were seven studies [17, 18, 26–30] reporting on ABS using a cut-off ranging from − 2 to -2.6. Saito et al. reported outcome data using ABS only as a continuous variable while all other studies divided their cohorts based on a specific cut-off. Two studies [17, 27] reported only in-hospital mortality while all others reported long-term mortality. Luo et al. [28] reported both 90-day mortality and one-year mortality. We therefore grouped in-hospital and 90-day mortality data as short-term mortality. Meta-analysis showed that ABS was a significant predictor of mortality in patients with HF (HR: 1.54 95% CI: 1.17, 2.04 I2 = 61% p = 0.002) (Fig. 2). The ratio was found to predict both short-term (HR: 2.01 95% CI: 1.01, 4.00 I2 = 62% p = 0.05) and long-term (HR: 1.36 95% CI: 1.05, 1.76 I2 = 49% p = 0.02) mortality after HF. We did not notice any change in the significance of the results on sensitivity analysis (data not shown). The funnel plot did not demonstrate any major asymmetry and Egger’s test was non-significant (p = 0.62) (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis of the association between ABS and mortality after HF with subgroup analysis based on timing of mortality

Forest plot for assessing publication bias of studies on ABS

Three studies [17, 18, 26] reported HR of mortality with a per unit increase in ABS. Combining them, we noted that per unit increase in ABS led to significant increased risk of mortality in HF (HR: 1.18 95% CI: 1.05, 1.32 I2 = 49% p = 0.006) (Fig. 4).

Meta-analysis of the association between per unit increase in ABS and mortality after HF

Fibrinogen-albumin ratio

Three studies [21, 22, 25] reported on the fibrinogen-albumin ratio. Yang et al. [22] noted that the fibrinogen-albumin ratio independently predicted mortality for all chronic HF patients. Similar results were obtained on subgroup analysis of HR with reduced ejection fraction, preserved ejection fraction, and median ejection fraction. Xu et al. [21] in their cohort noted that the fibrinogen-albumin ratio was an independent predictor for both 90-day and 1-year mortality after HF. Sawatani et al. [25] found that high fibrinogen-albumin ratio was not a predictor for in-hospital and long-term mortality in acute HF. Meta-analysis of these studies showed that high fibrinogen-albumin ratio predicted mortality after HF (HR: 1.12 95% CI: 1.09, 1.14 I2 = 0% p < 0.00001) (Fig. 5).

Meta-analysis of the association between fibrinogen-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio and mortality after HF

BUN-albumin ratio

There were three studies [19, 20, 33] on the BUN-albumin ratio using a cut-off of 0.83–1.6. Lin et al. in a cohort of 1545 critically ill HF patients noted the BUN-albumin ratio to independently predict both in-hospital and 90-day mortality. Zhao et al. [33] prospectively assessed the relationship between the BUN-albumin ratio and long-term mortality in all HF patients. Results showed that a high BUN-albumin ratio led to increased mortality. Zhang et al. [20] in their retrospective study found the BUN-albumin ratio as a predictor of both 28 and 90-day mortality. Lin et al. [19] in their retrospective cohort study also found that the BUN-albumin ratio independently predicted in-hospital and 90-day mortality in HF. The meta-analysis found that BUN-albumin ratio was a predictor of mortality in HF (HR: 1.84 95% CI: 1.28, 2.64 I2 = 64% p = 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Lactate-albumin ratio

There were two studies [31, 32] on lactate-albumin ratio, both conducted on USA populations and using a cut-off of 0.31 and 0.67. One study [32] reported one-year mortality while the other [31] reported in-hospital mortality. Both studies found a significant association between lactate-albumin ratio and mortality in HF patients. Pooled analysis concurred with the individual results (HR: 1.84 95% CI: 1.68, 2.01 I2 = 0% p < 0.00001) (Fig. 5).

Albumin-globulin ratio

Two studies [23, 24] reported data on albumin-globulin ratio. Both were retrospective studies based from China and Poland. Li et al. [24] divided their cohort into two groups based on the cut-off of 1.48 and found that a high albumin-globulin ratio was associated with better overall survival (HR: 0.61 95%CI: 0.38, 0.98 p = 0.04). Niedziela et al. [23] found that per 0.1 decrease in the albumin-globulin ratio led to increased mortality at 90 days (HR: 1.18 95%CI: 1.07, 1.30 p = 0.01) and 1 year of follow-up (HR: 1.07 95%CI: 1.01, 1.13 p = 0.01). Since the data was not homogenous, a meta-analysis was not conducted.

GRADE assessment

The certainty of evidence was examined using GRADE. Results are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The certainty of evidence was found to be “very low” for all outcomes.

Discussion

A number of biomarkers have been reported to predict mortality of HF in literature. A meta-analysis by Yu et al. have shown that higher levels of triglyceride-glucose index is associated with statistically significant increase in the risk of mortality after HF [36]. Another meta-analysis shows that circulating levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor can also predict poor prognosis in HF [37]. Sharma et al. [2] have noted that body mass index is also an independent predictor of mortality in HF patients. Nevertheless, there is limited literature on the utility of albumin-based indices for predicting mortality after HF. This study is the first meta-analysis examining the utility of five different albumin-based indices in predicting mortality after HF. A detailed literature search was conducted on several databases which led us to 16 studies examining the prognostic ability of albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, fibrinogen-albumin ratio, or ABS in HF. The majority of the evidence was for ABS while limited data was available for the remaining ratios. The meta-analysis found that higher ABS was an independent predictor of both short and long-term mortality increasing the risk of death by 100% and 36% respectively. While higher cut-offs of ABS were associated with mortality in one meta-analysis, the pooled analysis also found that a per unit increase in ABS led to worst survival after HF. The results were robust on sensitivity analysis adding to the credibility of the results. Additionally, the meta-analyses of limited studies also found that higher fibrinogen-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, and lactate-albumin ratio significantly increased the risk of mortality in HF by 12%, 84%, and 84% respectively. A narrative review of the evidence for the remaining ratios showed that combining fibrinogen, BUN, lactate, and globulin with albumin can aid in the prognostication of HF patients.

The results of our review are in agreement with several studies in the literature that have demonstrated the prognostic ability of all five indices for different diseases. The ABS was initially developed in Japanese hepatocellular carcinoma patients to estimate the level of liver dysfunction [38]. However, over the last decade, the score has found its utility in several non-hepatic diseases. Szor et al. [39] have found that gastric cancer patients with high ABS have worse overall survival and disease-free survival as compared to low ABS. Similarly, the score has been found to predict disease-free survival and cancer-specific survival in stage III colon cancer [40]. The score has also been used for other cardiovascular diseases with Jiang et al. [41] reporting ABS to independently predict in-hospital major adverse cardiac events and long-term mortality in idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy. Duman et al. [42] have recently shown that patients with high ABS undergoing cardiac valvular surgery have an increased risk of acute kidney injury and in-hospital mortality. Similarly, Chen et al. [43] also noted ABS to be a predictor of acute kidney injury and mortality in a cohort of patients undergoing elective percutaneous coronary intervention.

The fibrinogen-albumin ratio has also found its role in predicting adverse outcomes in acute coronary syndrome patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention. A meta-analysis of 10 studies has shown that patients with higher ratios were at an increased risk of mortality, major adverse cardiac events, no-reflow, and revascularization needs [44]. The BUN-albumin ratio is an independent predictor of mortality and prolonged intensive care unit and hospital stay in patients undergoing cardiac surgeries [45]. Wang et al. [46] have shown that a high lactate-albumin ratio is associated with 14-day, 28-day, and 90-day all-cause mortality in acute myocardial infarction patients. The ratio also predicts mortality in critically ill patients with atrial fibrillation [47]. Lastly, the albumin-globulin ratio has been found to predict functional outcomes and mortality in patients with acute ischemic stroke [48]. Liu et al. [49] have shown that a low albumin-globulin ratio is a predictor of mortality in a mixed cohort of critically ill patients. Azab et al. [50] have demonstrated that the ratio has a linear association with mortality in acute myocardial infarction patients.

Literature shows that serum albumin per se is an independent predictor of outcomes of HF. A meta-analysis of 48 studies has shown that hypoalbuminemia was seen in 32% of cases of HF and is a strong independent predictor of short and long-term mortality [12]. Given such evidence for serum albumin alone, why would there be a need for combining albumin with other markers? Firstly, serum albumin varies significantly with nutritional status, inflammation, hepatorenal conditions, and volume status in HF patients [12]. There have been studies that show that hypoalbuminemia may not be consistent across all HF patients and may not predict outcomes. Data from two large prospective HF trials (DOSE-AHF and ROSE-AHF) have found that serum albumin levels were mostly normal in acute HF patients and were not linked with mortality, rehospitalization, and emergency visits [51]. Therefore, combining albumin with other relevant markers has the potential to overcome individual pathophysiological variations and improve the prognostic ability. Bilirubin [52], fibrinogen [53], BUN [54], lactate [55], and globulin [56] have all been found to predict mortality in HF in their individual capacities. Hence, it was postulated and verified by this review that a combination of these variables with albumin can aid in the prognostication of HF.

The exact pathophysiological roles of all these markers in HF are unclear but several potential mechanisms exist. Albumin is a known marker of inflammation, cachexia, and malnutrition in HF patients. Albumin also has anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties which may prevent inflammation and oxidative stress-mediated cardiovascular events [12, 57]. Secondly, diuretic resistance and pulmonary edema are commonly seen in HF with severe hypoalbuminemia which may lead to increased mortality [12]. The role of cardiohepatic interactions in the prognosis of HF has also been recognized. Severe congestive HF can compromise blood supply due to diminished cardiac output leading to acute hepatocellular necrosis. Elevated central venous pressure also causes passive hepatic congestion resulting in incremental elevation of liver enzymes including indirect and direct circulating bilirubin [28]. Liver dysfunction reduces the energy supply of the heart thereby increasing mortality rates [17]. Fibrinogen is also produced by the liver and has a role in the pathophysiology of coagulation and inflammation. Levels of fibrinogen increase in acute and chronic inflammation, procoagulant states, and upregulation of adhesion molecule expression [22]. Higher fibrinogen levels are seen in patients with higher grades of HF [53]. The reduced cardiac output in HF also compromises blood supply to the kidneys and peripheral tissues. Decrease in blood flow of the kidneys despite normal renal tubular function causes arginine-vasopressin activation and overactivity of the renin- angiotensin-aldosterone system which increases the uptake of BUN [20]. Poor tissue perfusion is seen with higher grades of HF leading to increased anaerobic metabolism and lactate synthesis. Thus, lactate level is a marker of hypoperfusion and hypoxia and increased levels denote hemodynamic instability [32]. Like albumin, globulin is also a serum protein. High globulin levels are associated with chronic inflammation and indicate cumulative exposure to different proinflammatory cytokines. Chronic inflammation is associated with incidence, development, and survival in HF [58].

Our results have important clinical implications for a primary-care physician who can make a rapid assessment of the prognosis of HF patients based on these simple ratios. Importantly, there can be several advantages to using albumin-based markers for HF over other biomarkers. Firstly, all the above ratios are easily available worldwide and do not require any high-cost equipment. They all can be obtained rapidly thereby providing quick prognostication of HF. Secondly, the use of two variables overcomes individual limitations and patient variations thereby improving prognostic ability. However, disadvantages include a lack of a clearly defined cut-off for all markers. As seen in our review, all studies used different cut-offs for different markers based on their study population. To clinically use these ratios, optimal cut-offs must be derived at each institute. Further, it is unclear how these variables change during HF and if there are variations amongst HF phenotypes. There is a need for further studies to answer these queries.

Limitations of the current review include the small number of studies available for ratios other than ABS. The predominance of retrospective data is a major drawback given its association with selection bias and errors in data recording. The limited number of studies prevented us from performing a detailed subgroup and meta-regression analysis to explore the source of high heterogeneity in the meta-analysis. Also, variations in HF phenotype, severity of disease, treatments, and follow-up could have skewed the current results. Lastly, the predominance of Asian data limits the generalizability of evidence.

Conclusions

Albumin-based indices have a potential role in predicting mortality after HF. ABS was found to be an independent predictor of short and long-term mortality after HF. Other ratios like the albumin-globin ratio, lactate-albumin ratio, BUN-albumin ratio, and fibrinogen-albumin ratio were also found to predict mortality after HF albeit with limited data. However, the certainty of evidence is very low and further studies are needed to increase the power of the current review. Future studies should provide comparative evidence on the discriminative power of these ratios to facilitate accurate prognostication.
