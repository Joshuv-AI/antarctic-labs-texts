Resource Recovery Strategy of Rapid Screening Kits for Diseases through Ozonation
Authors: Hengjun Shang, Yue Wang, Yao Chen, Xiang Chen, Jianjiang Zhou, Lele Bai, Fujian Lv, Zhenfeng Bian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid disease screening kits are widely used due to their efficiency and convenience in disease screening. However, the resulting medical waste poses a significant environmental burden. Recycling this waste in a resourceful manner not only protects the environment from pollution but also maximizes savings in resources and energy consumption. In this study, we developed a scalable, simple, and efficient method for the resourceful recovery of assay kits. The method achieves simultaneous 100% pathogen inactivation in the kits, complete leaching of gold nanoparticles (Au NPs), and nondestructive recovery of plastics and tampons. Additionally, our mechanistic study on Au leaching has demonstrated that water plays a crucial role as the initiator for the Au dissolution reaction, and the hydroxyl radical (•OH) serves as an important reactive oxygen species. This research introduces a novel approach for dissolving the precious metal gold and provides a viable technology for the resource utilization of waste disease screening kits.Keywords: rapid screening kit, gold recovery, ozonation, resource utilization, reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Rapid test kits are extensively utilized for their ability to quickly and easily detect various infectious diseases (such as coronavirus disease 2019, human immunodeficiency virus, and hepatitis B) as well as the presence of drugs and conditions like heart disease.1−3 During the new coronavirus epidemic, there was a significant surge in the usage of antigen detection kits.4 However, discarded kits inevitably contain bacteria and viruses, making it essential to eliminate their infectious potential before disposal. Furthermore, discarded kits that employ the colloidal gold (Au) method possess valuable recyclable resources, such as Au nanoparticles (NPs), plastic, and nitrocellulose. Therefore, the integration of efficient sterilization and precious metal recovery from waste kits holds immense practical significance for both environmental protection and resource recycling.

Currently, waste kits containing colloidal Au are primarily disposed through incineration and landfill methods. However, incineration leads to substantial air pollution, generating large amounts of carbon dioxide (CO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), dioxins, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs).5−8 On the other hand, landfills occupy significant amounts of land with nonbiodegradable plastics, resulting in long-term damage to the environment.9 Moreover, these disposal methods fail to recover valuable resources like AuNPs. The key aspect of waste kit resource recovery lies in the dissolution and retrieval of AuNPs. Traditional techniques such as aqua regia and cyanidation employ corrosive or toxic substances for Au leaching, causing secondary pollution and high costs.10−12 Thus, the recovery of resources from kits remains a challenge, lacking an economical and environmentally friendly method.13

Ozone (O3), a relatively mild advanced oxidant with an oxidation potential of 2.07 eV,14,15 shows promise as an oxidant for nonferrous, rare, and precious metal extraction from minerals via cyanidation.16−18 Additionally, O3 exhibits potent bactericidal and viral inactivation properties.19,20 Tizaoui et al. conducted a study on SARS-CoV-2 inactivation with O3, demonstrating its effectiveness in eliminating viruses and bacteria in aerosols and on solid surfaces.21,22 Therefore, O3 proves to be a highly promising bifunctional oxidant for both waste kit sterilization and precious metal recovery. Furthermore, previous research by our group discovered that acetonitrile (MeCN), a solvent employed in green photocatalytic technology, facilitates the dissolution of precious metals. MeCN serves as a favorable ligand, providing cyano or amine ligands for Au.23,24

In this study, we achieved the efficient sterilization and resource recovery of waste kits through a one-step process involving the injection of O3 into the MeCN solvent. This catalyst-free and energy-neutral process not only offers a simple and effective means to mitigate the risk of virus transmission but also enables the environmentally friendly and cost-effective recovery of precious metals, plastics, and other valuable resources from the kits. This method presents a viable strategy for recycling precious metal resources and other kit materials.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of AuNPs and AuNPs/SiO2

The AuNPs were synthesized using the sodium citrate reduction method. In this process, 1 mL of 10 mmol/L chloroauric acid was added to 100 mL of deionized water, and the solution was heated in a water bath. Subsequently, 5 mL of a 1 mol/L sodium citrate solution was added. The solution underwent a color change from dark blue to red. Once this transformation occurred, the heating ceased and natural cooling was allowed, resulting in the formation of a 10 nm colloidal Au solution. To obtain a AuNPs/SiO2 (1 wt %) powder, 100 mL of the colloidal Au solution was mixed with 1 g of SiO2 powder, thoroughly dispersed using ultrasound, and subsequently dried using a rotary evaporation instrument.

Characterization

The dissolution was calculated by measuring the concentration of precious metal in the solution by an inductively coupled plasma emission spectrometer (ICP, a Jarrel-Asm Atom Scan 2000). The standard concentration curve was made using a standard concentration metal ion solution feed, and the fitting curve yielded an R2 ≥ 0.99999. RF power, 1150 W; nebulizer flow rate, 0.7 SLM; auxiliary gas flow rate, 1.0 SLM; cooling gas flow rate, 12 SLM; analytical pump speed, 50 r/min; and rinse pump speed, 100 rpm. The solution containing the dissolved AuNPs was subjected to steam drying in a constant temperature water bath set at 45 °C. This process resulted in the formation of a golden yellow solid, which was further characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/MAX-2000, Cu Kα source, operated at 40 kV and 20 mA, scanned at a rate of 5°/min), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PerkinElmer PHI 5000C, Al Kα), and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR, Bruker AV 400 M, 400.0 MHz). For Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet IS 10) analysis, a small amount of the product sample was mixed with the KBr powder and thoroughly ground. The resulting mixture was dried, and then the FTIR test was conducted in the wavelength scanning range of 400–4000 cm–1. To examine the morphology and element distribution of the AuNPs within the kit, scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI S4800, operated at 50 kV) was employed. Additionally, electron spin resonance spectroscopy (ESR, Bruker EMXplus-6/1) was used to detect the presence of free radicals. 5,5-Dietyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was used as the free radical trapping agent (the concentration of DMPO was controlled to be 2–5 mmol/L). Furthermore, intermediates present in the MeCN solvent were identified through gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS, QP2010 SE) analysis. The column was Rxi-5MS, 0.25 mm in diameter, and the spectra were acquired in SCAN mode.

Dissolution of AuNPs

At a temperature of 25 °C, 50 mg of AuNPs/SiO2 (1%) powder was taken and placed in a 50 mL reaction flask. Then, 20 mL of pure MeCN or other solvents was placed in the flask. Subsequently, O3 gas was introduced into the solvents using an O3 generator (Chuang-yue O3 generator, CYA-S5G), which utilizes air as the oxygen source. In our laboratory, the relative humidity of air is maintained at 60%. The flow rate of O3 gas was set to 0.1 L/min (resulting in an O3 concentration of 1 ppm) using a flow controller. Throughout the process, the solution was stirred at 500 r/min. The concentration of the dissolved components in the solution was measured by using inductively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy (ICP), and the dissolution rate was calculated accordingly.

The sample was dissolved in aqua regia within a well-ventilated cabinet and prepared in an equivalent volume. The waste kits underwent dismantling, and the nitrocellulose strips containing attached Au nanoparticles were immersed in MeCN. At a temperature of 25 °C, 20 intact cellulose strips were placed into a 200 mL reaction flask, and 100 mL of pure MeCN was added. To prevent solvent volatilization, the flask was connected to a reflux condensation device. O3 gas was introduced into the MeCN solvent through a silicone tube, with the O3 gas being generated by an O3 generator (Chuang-yue O3 generator, CYA-S5G). Sterilization Test in MeCN Solvent25

Escherichiacoli (E.coli) were aseptically transferred to a sterile growth medium on a clean bench. Subsequently, it was incubated in a biochemical incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. The activated culture was then inoculated into a nutrient solution and incubated with continuous shaking at a constant temperature to facilitate exponential growth. The concentration of the bacterial solution, once growth was completed, ranged from 5 × 107 to 8 × 107 CFU mL–1. To assess the number of viable bacteria, 5 mL of the bacterial solution was mixed with 15 mL of MeCN and stirred. The solution was then subjected to a series of dilutions, 100 μL at a time, using a salt solution to achieve a dilution factor of 105 (resulting in an expected zero-moment colony count of 50–80 CFU/plate). Subsequently, 100 μL of the suspension was evenly spread on LB agar plates at intervals of 0, 0.5, 5, and 10 min. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the bacterial colonies were counted to determine the number of viable bacteria.

Results and Discussion

Recovery of AuNPs from Antigen Kits

To investigate the feasibility of dissolving AuNPs present in fast screening kits using the ozone–acetonitrile (O3–MeCN) system, we selected a new coronavirus antigen kit (colloidal Au method) as our study object. This kit consists of a plastic housing and test strips (Figure 1a), with purple colloidal Au (AuNPs) on the test strips acting as tracer markers for labeling the antigen. Through SEM analysis of the portion of the test strip containing Au nanoparticles, we observed that the fibers on the test strip were arranged in a staggered and adhered manner (Figure 1b). Furthermore, by conducting elemental mapping at a local magnification, we observed that the Au elements were uniformly distributed on the fiber surface, constituting 10% of the total content (in relation to the proportions of Au, C, and O). The C and O elements were associated with the Au colloid-labeled antigens (Figures 1c–1e).26

(a) Schematic diagram of the internal disassembly of the antigen kit. (b and c) SEM images coupled with (d) EDS mapping of the interior. (e) Atomic number ratios of the Au, O, and C elements in the fiber.

The test strips from the antigen kit, which contained precious metals, were immersed in MeCN while the O3 gas was introduced. The reaction was conducted for 30 min, resulting in a gradual disappearance of the purple color exhibited by the AuNPs on the test strips (Figures 2a and 2c). Simultaneously, the color of the reaction solvent changed from colorless to light yellow (Figures 2b and 2d). Postreaction, SEM analysis revealed the absence of previously adhered AuNPs on the fibers, which transformed the fiber surface into a smooth and flat texture, thereby facilitating the recycling of waste nitrocellulose (Figures 2e and 2f). Further analysis through mapping and EDS (Figures 2g and 2h) confirmed that the Au content on the surface of the reacted strips was reduced to 0%. Additionally, the amount of elemental Au in the ICP test of the reaction solvent aligned with the amount of colloidal Au measured using the aqua regia method on the kit, indicating the complete dissolution of AuNPs from the fiber substrate within 30 min (Figure 2i). Furthermore, to assess the reusability of the MeCN solvent, the solvent was collected after each reaction through distillation, and subsequent tests were conducted by immersing 20 fiber substrate strips in 100 mL of MeCN to measure the dissolution rate each time (Figure 2j). The results demonstrated that MeCN could be recycled for at least 10 cycles. Thus, this process enables the complete recovery of precious metals from waste kits along with the recycling of resources such as plastics and nitrocellulose, all achieved under mild conditions.

Dissolution Studies of AuNPs

Considering that the negatively charged Au colloids in the fast screening kits bind with the proteins in the antigen, we used the AuNPs/SiO2 sample as a simulated dissolution object to investigate the mechanism of dissolving AuNPs in the O3–MeCN system. Initially, to validate the effectiveness and superiority of the O3–MeCN system in dissolving AuNPs, we employed various solvents with O3 for Au dissolution on AuNPs/SiO2 (Figure S1). The ICP results indicated that the O3–MeCN system exhibited the highest dissolution rate, followed by those of ethylenediamine (C2H8N2) and ethanolamine (C2H7NO). This can be attributed to the presence of nitrile (−CN) or amine groups (−NH2) containing nitrogen, which act as favorable ligands for Au (Figure S2).27 Within the O3–MeCN system, complete dissolution of AuNPs was achieved within 40 min (Figure S3). Furthermore, we investigated the influence of the O3 concentration and AuNP particle size on the reaction rate within the O3–MeCN system. As depicted in Figures 3a and 3b, we dissolved AuNPs using different O3 concentrations and calculated the leaching kinetics. The results demonstrated a positive correlation between the dissolution rate and the O3 concentration. Higher O3 concentrations increased the likelihood of collision and reaction between O3 molecules, MeCN molecules, and AuNPs. Similarly, we dissolved AuNPs of varying sizes and evaluated their leaching kinetics (Figure 3d). The dissolution rate of AuNPs was found to be higher for smaller particle sizes, indicating size-dependent dissolution kinetics.

Exploration of the Mechanism

We employed various characterization techniques to explore the dissolution products of the AuNPs in this system. XPS analysis revealed that the Au element in the product predominantly existed as Au+ (84.2 and 87.8 eV) (Figure 4a),28 indicating complete dissolution and oxidation of the AuNPs. The peaks at 288.9 eV in the C 1s spectrum and 399.6 eV in the N 1s spectrum (Figure S4) corresponded to the C≡N– in the product. The FTIR spectrum of the solid product exhibited a peak at 2250 cm–1, confirming the presence of −CN (Figure 4b).29 The results of the XRD analysis of the products were consistent with the previous analysis, and the resulting peaks precisely matched the standard card of AuCN (Figure 4c).30 These analyses collectively indicate that the main oxidation product of Au is AuCN. In addition to the primary product, we detected other byproducts. The FTIR spectrum of the solid product displayed a medium-intensity absorption peak at 1662 cm–1, which was attributed to the carbonyl (−C=O) stretching vibration. Moreover, we demonstrated the presence of −C=O in the product by XPS. The C 1s spectrum in Figure S4a revealed peaks at 288.9 and 286.5 eV, corresponding to the C in the −C=O bond and the C in the C–H bond, respectively. The O 1s spectrum displayed a peak at 535.5 eV, corresponding to the −C=O group in the product. Furthermore, the stretching vibration peak at 3450 cm–1 in the FTIR spectrum indicated the absorption peak of the N–H bond. These results suggested the presence of an amide byproduct in the product, which was further confirmed by GC–MS analysis, demonstrating the production of a small amount of acetamide during the reaction (Figure S5).31

(a) XPS spectra, (b) FTIR spectrum, and (c) XRD pattern of the Au product from the solution after dissolution. The evolution process of free radical species (d) •OH, (e) •CH2CN, and (f) •CN (DMPO acts as a trapping agent) during the dissolution reaction of Au in MeCN.

To investigate the mechanism of AuNP dissolution in the O3–MeCN system, we utilized 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) to capture generated radicals and detected the signals of radical adducts through electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (ESR). The signal peaks with a peak intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1 were assigned to the DMPO–•OH adduct (AN = AHβ = 12.4 G) (Figure 4d).32 The spectra in Figures 4e and 4f represent the signal peaks of the DMPO–•CH2CN (AN = 16 G, AHβ = 22.4 G) and DMPO–•CN adducts (AN = 14.5 G, AHβ = 22.4 G), respectively.33 It was observed that •OH attacked the CH3CN hydrocarbon bond to form •CH2CN, which then reacted with another CH3CN to produce •CN and propanenitrile (CH3CH2CN).33 The presence of the byproduct CH3CH2CN was also confirmed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis (Figure S6). As for the source of •OH in the free radical chain reaction, its origin is unclear. However, we speculate that it may arise from the trace amount of water present in the O3 gas.34,35 To verify the role of water, we conducted Au dissolution experiments using dried gas passed into MeCN. The results demonstrated that dried and dewatered O3 gas with pure CH3CN did not dissolve the Au particles (Figure S7), indicating that trace amounts of water trigger the Au dissolution reaction and serve as the source of •OH.

Therefore, we propose a plausible mechanism for the dissolution of AuNPs (Figure 5). In the O3–CH3CN system, the trace amount of water present in the air acts as a free radical initiator. This water, in the form of small droplets, readily combines with O3, generating •OH. The •OH radicals then attack the C–H bond of CH3CN, leading to the formation of •CH2CN.36 In the presence of the •CH2CN radicals, diffusion of Au atoms has the lowest ΔGdislocation, −0.13 eV on the Au (211) surface, followed by the •OH radicals with a ΔGdislocation value of +0.15 eV, slightly higher than that of •CH2CN.27 The •CN produced acts as a coordinating group with the Au atom. The coordination effect of the ligand significantly reduces the difficulty of electron loss from the Au atom, facilitating its dissolution as AuCN in cooperation with the oxidizing agent O3, which withdraws electrons from the Au atom.

Schematic diagram of the mechanism of the dissolution of AuNPs by O3 in MeCN.

Furthermore, we investigated the germicidal disinfection performance of the O3–MeCN system. A 5 mL suspension of E. coli was placed in 15 mL of MeCN and stirred, and the number of viable bacteria was determined after each sample through the dilution coating plate method.25,37Figure S8 demonstrates that E. coli cannot survive in a MeCN solution alone, indicating that the low biotoxicity of MeCN itself can effectively kill E. coli. Moreover, when the bactericidal property of O3 is introduced into MeCN, the strong bactericidal property of O3 further enhances the germ-killing ability.

Finally, a comprehensive quantitative assessment was conducted to evaluate the environmental and economic aspects of the O3–MeCN recycling process for fast screening kits in a rational manner. As an illustrative example, each ton of waste from the colloidal Au method kits was estimated to comprise 227 100 individual kits, containing approximately 0.61 g of Au, 969.72 kg of plystyrene plastic (PS), and 24.75 kg of nitrocellulose. The remaining 5.53 kg consisted of viscous gum and undissolved absorbent cotton dissolved in MeCN (Table S1). The O3–MeCN system enables the gentle recovery of Au particles, plastics, and other resources from the fast screening kits for reuse (Figure 6). Considering the current market price, the value of each ton of waste kits amounts to $1205.24, while the cost of recovery (including MeCN, equipment, and electricity costs) is $299.79 per ton. Consequently, the profit gain is $905.45 per ton, indicating the significant potential for commercial application (Table S2). From an environmental standpoint, the Biwer–Heinzle method (BHM) was employed for environmental impact assessment, demonstrating the minimal risk associated with this process (Table 1).38,39 Thus, this approach holds considerable promise for the large-scale recycling of medical waste, particularly waste containing precious metals.

Conclusion

In summary, our study encompassed several key aspects. First, we thoroughly investigated the internal structure and composition of disease fast screening test kits. Subsequently, we examined the feasibility of dissolving Au using the O3–MeCN system, employing AuNPs/SiO2 as a simulated sample. We conducted a comprehensive investigation into the mechanism of Au dissolution facilitated by O3 in MeCN. The findings revealed that O3 possesses strong oxidizing properties and prompts the formation of •OH by reacting with water, which, in turn, reacts with the MeCN solvent to generate the effective ligand −CN for Au. Furthermore, we conducted leaching studies on the precious metal Au using actual antigen kits. Additionally, we demonstrated the rapid killing of E. coli in a MeCN solution without the need for an O3 gas. Finally, we developed a large-scale, straightforward, and efficient strategy for the recovery of resources from antigen detection kits. By utilizing the O3–MeCN system, we achieved disinfection and sterilization of the kits while effectively recovering the precious metal Au. This process enables the efficient recycling of waste disease rapid screening kits, contributing to the alleviation of resource constraints and the reduction of resource waste.
