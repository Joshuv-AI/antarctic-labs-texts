High-throughput phenotyping of nutritional quality components in sweet potato roots by near-infrared spectroscopy and chemometrics methods
Authors: Chaochen Tang, Bingzhi Jiang, Irsa Ejaz, Asif Ameen, Rong Zhang, Xueying Mo, Zhangying Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) was used to quality evaluation of sweet potato.•Eight optimized NIRS models were developed for rapid content determination.•NIRS applicable for large-scale screening of sweet potato germplasm.

Introduction

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas [L.] Lam) is a major root crop that plays an indispensable role in ensuring food security and improving diversified diets due to its high yield potential and health-promoting values (Alam, 2021, Wang et al., 2016). In addition, sweet potatoes are affordable, abundant, and a good source of carbohydrates and antioxidants, making them an essential product in the food industry supply chain (Sanchez, Hashim, Shamsudin, & Mohd Nor, 2020). Despite strenuous efforts from breeders and scientists to obtain higher yields from sweet potato roots in recent decades, improvements in the nutritional quality components of sweet potatoes have remained limited and insufficient. As the demand for high-quality agricultural products increases with the “return to nature trend”, particular attention should be paid towards cultivating dedicated sweet potato varieties with excellent quality to sustainably meet healthy lifestyle and industrial application requirements.

Starch (amylose and amylopectin), soluble sugar, crude protein, total flavonoid content, and total phenolic content are proximate components and predominant phenolic compounds of sweet potato roots, which directly affect the breeding and practical application of this crop (Alam, 2021, Wang et al., 2016). These quality indicators ordinarily exist in sweet potatoes of all flesh-colored types, but they vary widely among different genotypes (Hossain, Rahim, Moutosi, & Das, 2022). Furthermore, these quality traits are usually heritable since sweet potato is an asexual propagation crop, and conventional crossbreeding can deliver promising variety with improved nutritional value (Laurie et al., 2020, Lu et al., 2006). Quality improvement using the conventional method depends on the availability of genetic resources and practical screening tools to reveal the extent of variability in the quality traits of interest (Laurie et al., 2020). Despite having extensive germplasm of sweet potato that could provide a broad genetic basis for breeding and scientific research, there is scarce information on their quality traits due to the lack of practical screening tools. Therefore, a quantitative evaluation of sweet potato's target nutritional quality components is needed to screen elite germplasm for more efficient and precise breeding purpose.

Wet chemistry and high-performance liquid chromatography serve as the universal methods to quantify quality indicators in sweet potato roots. However, there are several limitations to their use, including the hazardous nature of chemicals, the time-consuming process of sample preparation and analysis, the requirement for experienced analysts, and the unsuitability of these methods for detecting scaled-up to hundreds of samples (Sanchez et al., 2020). In particular, in quality breeding programs, it is challenging to rapidly select elite breeding material from a large pool of sweet potato germplasm without the support of high-throughput technology (Lebot et al., 2011, Lu et al., 2006, Magwaza et al., 2016). Moreover, the development of genomic tools and the reduction of sequencing costs have enabled crop breeders to pursue large-scale precision breeding (Wang et al., 2021). Large-scale phenotypic and genotypic analyses through association studies would significantly accelerate crop quality improvement (Li et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2020). However, acquiring large-scale phenotypic data has emerged as a bottleneck that hinders crop genetic improvement. Hence, establishing a high-throughput approach for precise quality evaluation is a key in guiding quality breeding, recommending proper quality grading, and promoting the sweet potato-based food industry.

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is an alternative method with fast detection speed, high efficiency, simplicity, and chemical-free analysis (Dossa et al., 2018, Xu et al., 2013). NIRS coupled with chemometrics has been regarded as a powerful tool for high-throughput phenotyping of plant samples according to the target quality components (Li et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2021). Partial least squares regression (PLSR), as the most prevalent analytical method, has been extensively verified in quantitative analysis and can obtain precise and reliable results (Li, He, Wang, Liu, & Xie, 2018). Thus far, NIRS coupled with PLSR has been applied for predicting proximate components of sweet potato roots, such as starch, sugars, protein, and fiber (Diaz et al., 2014, He et al., 2022, Lebot et al., 2011, Lu et al., 2006, Magwaza et al., 2016), but has not yet been used for functional components (i.e., content of total flavonoid and total phenolic) phenotyping. Besides, the calibration equations generated in these early studies primarily utilized a sample set with less variability in quality components, meaning that NIRS models established for one population may not be useful for another, even within the same specie (Magwaza et al., 2016). Furthermore, optimization of sample subset partitioning and spectral variable selection using appropriate chemometrics to enhance the accuracy and robustness of prediction models has yet to be discussed, especially for sweet potato. On the one hand, the predictive capability of the NIRS model largely relies on the internal relationship between spectral features and analytical properties of the sample subset (Li, Wang, Du, Diallo, & Xie, 2017). On the other hand, although it is widely acknowledged that PLSR cannot fully resolve the overfitting problem in NIRS data, variable selection methods have become a powerful platform to address this issue (Li et al., 2018, Yun et al., 2019). For instance, competitive adaptive reweighted sampling (CARS), random frog (RF), and Monte Carlo-uninformative variable elimination (MC-UVE) are considered effective algorithms for obtaining optimal spectral variables (Li et al., 2012, Li et al., 2017, Yun et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the impact of these chemometric algorithms on the performance of NIRS predictive models for application in quantitative analysis of quality components in sweet potatoes remained unclear.

Keeping in view the above scenario, we conducted a series of analyses on the chemical components and NIRS results of representative sweet potato root samples. The main objectives of the present study were (1) to comparatively analyze the three dual-optimized strategies for improving the predictive capability of PLSR multivariate calibrations; and (2) to develop optimized NIRS-based prediction models for nutritional profiling of sweet potato roots involving total starch, amylose, amylopectin, the ratio of amylopectin to amylose, soluble sugar, crude protein, total flavonoid content, and total phenolic content. This approach would achieve high-throughput phenotyping for chemical components of sweet potato roots, thus overcoming the bottleneck of large-scale germplasm screening and providing a theoretical basis and technical support for quality improvement in the future.

Materials and methods

Representative sample selection and preparation

To ensure an ideal sample population for NIRS modeling, we carefully selected 125 samples of sweet potato roots based on their remarkable variations in both genotype and phenotype. These representative samples were sourced from a core collection of sweet potatoes (Chen, Tang, Zhang, Yao, Jiang, & Wang, 2023). They were collected during an observational trial conducted at the Baiyun Experimental Station (23°23′N, 113°26′E) of the Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences in 2020 (Table S1). The flesh color of sweet potatoes within the samples varied from white, cream, yellow, orange, pink, and red to purple.

The fresh, medium-sized storage roots (100–250 g) of each selected sample were manually harvested 130 days after planting and promptly transported to the analytical laboratory for preparation. The sampled roots were washed under running tap water, dried with absorbent paper, cut into shreds, and separated into two sub-samples using a point-centred quarter method. Based on our preliminary laboratory data and previous reports (Jeng et al., 2015, Xu et al., 2020), we found that hot air drying generally results in the degradation of bioactive components and a relatively high temperature (such as 75 °C) also leads to a reduction in soluble sugar content. Although freeze drying has been deemed the most effective method for obtaining superior-quality products, it is time-consuming and expensive for large samples. Therefore, all samples were duplicated for this study using two drying methods. One set was placed in a 45 °C oven and dried until a constant weight was achieved. The dried samples were ground into powder, passed through a 40–80 mesh screen, and finally stored at 4 °C until they were used to assess proximate components quantitatively. The other set of samples was freeze-dried using a bench-top freeze dryer (Christ Beta 2–8 LD plus, Osterode, Germany) at a temperature of −50 °C for 72 h or until thoroughly dried. These samples were ground in a liquid nitrogen grinder (A10 basic, IKA, Staufen, Germany) and stored at −20 °C to determine the functional components.

Laboratory chemical analysis

Proximate components

Approximately 0.1 g of each sample was weighed for determining each proximate component. The total starch content was measured using the Megazyme assay kit (K-TSTA-100A, Wicklow, Ireland) based on the spectrophotometric AOAC method 996.11. The amylose content was assessed with the Megazyme assay kit (K-AMYL, Wicklow, Ireland), and the amylopectin content was calculated by subtracting the amylose value from the total starch content. The ratio of amylopectin to amylose was calculated based on amylopectin and amylose values. According to the operational processes described by Hansen and Møller (1975), soluble sugar was measured at 620 nm by anthrone sulfuric acid colorimetry. Crude protein was determined by the Kjeldahl method, using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25.

Functional components

The total flavonoid content was quantified at 510 nm through the aluminum nitrate colorimetric method according to the protocol described by Xu et al. (2020). Briefly, 0.1 g of dry ground sample was extracted with 10 mL of ethanol (70 %), which was then subjected to ultrasound treatment for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm/min for 10 min. Next, 5 mL of the extracted sample was mixed with 0.3 mL of sodium nitrite (5 %) and left to stand for 5 min, after which 0.3 mL of aluminum nitrate (10 %) was added. The resulting mixture remained undisturbed for 6 min before adding 4 mL of sodium hydroxide (1 mol/L) and 0.4 mL of 70 % ethanol. The reaction mixture was shaken and allowed to stand for 10 min before measuring with an ultraviolet and visible spectrophotometer (DU800, Beckman Coulter). The total flavonoid content was calculated using a rutin standard curve (R2 = 0.999) and expressed as grams rutin equivalent per 100 g of dry matter (g RE/100 g, % dry basis).

To analyze the total phenolic content, the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method was adopted, described by Singleton, Orthofer, and Lamuela-Raventós (1999), and the absorbance was measured at 760 nm. Briefly, 0.1 g of dry ground sample was extracted with 10 mL of ethanol (60 %), which was then subjected to ultrasound treatment for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm/min for 10 min. Next, 0.2 mL of the extracted sample was mixed with 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (0.2 mol/L). The mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min before adding 0.8 mL of sodium carbonate (7.5 %). The reaction mixture was shaken and kept in darkness at room temperature (25 °C) for 1 h before measuring with a spectrophotometer (DU800, Beckman Coulter). The total phenolic content was calculated using a gallic acid standard curve (R2 = 0.997) and expressed as grams gallic acid equivalent per 100 g of dry matter (g GAE/100 g, % dry basis).

Spectral data acquisition and pretreatments

During the analysis of each sample lot, the corresponding NIRS data were collected immediately. Approximately 6 g of each sample was placed into a mini-sample cup. The spectra measurements were taken in reflectance mode using an FT-9700 NIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and a reflectance accessory equipped with an InGaAs detector. The final spectrum was obtained as the mean of 64 scans with a resolution of 8 cm−1, which ranged from 10,000 cm−1 to 4,000 cm−1 at room temperature (25 °C). The reflectance data were recorded as log1/R, where R is the reflectance of the sweet potato sample. The obtained continuous reflectance values were subsequently averaged for further analysis.

The Unscrambler v10.4 software (CAMO, Corvallis, USA) was used to collect spectrometer data and perform principal component analysis (PCA) for characterizing the structure and variability of the spectral population while identifying outliers. Afterward, the ChemDataSolution software (version 3.5, Dalian ChemData Solution Technology Co. Ltd., Dalian, China) was employed for the chemometric processing of raw spectral data and NIRS calibration. The raw spectra were adjusted using the standard normal variate (SNV) method to rectify spectral differences caused by scattering and mitigate the influence of interference (Jia et al., 2022, Payne and Wolfrum, 2015). The Savitzky–Golay algorithm for smoothing and second derivative processing were further utilized to minimize baseline offset and reduce random noise (Li et al., 2017).

NIRS modeling and performance evaluation

Robust multivariate calibration is widely recognized to depend significantly on the quality of both representative calibration sets and external validation sets (Li et al., 2018). In this study, we used the Kennard–Stone (KS) algorithm to divide the spectral data into calibration and validation sets at a ratio of 4:1 (Ejaz et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2021; Payne et al., 2015). To improve prediction accuracy and eliminate irrelevant redundant information, CARS, RF, and MC-UVE algorithms were employed to select an optimal wavelength from the full spectrum (Jia et al., 2022, Li et al., 2018, Yun et al., 2019). Using the PLSR method, a regression analysis between processed spectral absorbance and laboratory-measured data was conducted to create a prediction equation. To evaluate the performance of the generated calibration equations, we used the leave-one-out cross-validation method for internal cross-validation, while independent samples in the validation set used for external validation (He et al., 2022). Moreover, the root mean square error of calibration/cross-validation/prediction (RMSEC/RMSECV/RMSEP) and coefficient of determination of calibration/cross-validation/external validation (R2C/R2CV/R2V) were obtained during NIRS calibration to select the best equations (Wang et al., 2021). The potential of NIRS models for application in the breeding program was evaluated by calculating the ratio of prediction to deviation (RPD) and range error ratio (RER) (Li et al., 2017, Yang et al., 2016).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in biological triplicates, and the obtained data represented the mean values as a percentage of dry weight. The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated based on the original determinations and defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean value. Data standardization and Shannon-Weiner index calculation were performed using Excel 2019 software. To assess any significant differences between calibration and validation sets and between NIRS predicted and reference values for each quality component at p < 0.05, we conducted an independent t-test using SPSS 26.0 analytical software (IBM, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Diverse quality components of representative samples

Owing to genotype diversity, significant variations were observed in 125 representative samples of sweet potato roots in terms of nutritional quality components, resulting in a better normal distribution (Fig. 1 and Table S2). Three clusters of sweet potato samples grouped by quality components were obtained (Fig. S1), including cluster Ⅰ with high crude protein, total flavonoid content, and total phenolic content, cluster Ⅱ had high total starch and amylopectin content, and cluster Ⅲ with high soluble sugar content (Table S3). Specifically, the total starch ranged from 28.21 % to 64.77 %, amylose ranged from 10.15 % to 16.99 %, amylopectin ranged from 15.28 % to 50.06 %, and soluble sugar ranged from 5.99 % to 36.76 % (Fig. 1a). The ratio of amylopectin to amylose varied from 1.13 to 4.14, while crude protein varied from 1.99 % to 7.01 % (Fig. 1b). Moreover, significant variation in the total flavonoid content (0.17 % to 5.41 %) and total phenolic content (0.07 % to 2.16 %) was observed within the representative samples, which illustrated the highest CV of 93.33 % and 89.74 %, respectively. In contrast, soluble sugar, crude protein, the ratio of amylopectin to amylose, amylopectin, total starch, and amylose exhibited relatively low variability, with CV values of 37.35 %, 27.20 %, 18.89 %, 17.22 %, 13.13 %, and 8.59 %, respectively. After standardizing the data across samples, the Shannon-Weiner index on quality components ranked from highest to lowest was 1.97 (the ratio of amylopectin to amylose), 1.92 (crude protein), 1.87 (total starch), 1.84 (amylopectin), 1.79 (amylose), 1.45 (soluble sugar), 0.99 (total phenolic content), and 0.67 (total flavonoid content). Higher CV values for functional components indicate lower stability, while a greater diversity index for proximate components represents richer diversity. Optimization of sample subset partitioning by KS

No significant differences were observed between the calibration and validation sets for each quality component (p > 0.05). A descriptive statistical analysis compared the calibration and validation sets regarding the minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, and CV values (Table 1). Total flavonoid and phenolic content had the highest CV values for the calibration (94.00 % and 92.14 %, respectively) and validation sets (94.22 % and 89.90 %, respectively). Soluble sugar had CV values of 39.23 % and 28.21 % in the calibration and validation sets, followed by crude protein (27.72 % and 25.09 %), the ratio of amylopectin to amylose (20.13 % and 13.61 %), amylopectin (18.41 % and 11.54 %), starch (13.96 % and 9.07 %), and amylose (8.85 % and 7.79 %), respectively. These findings showed that the calibration and validation sets were comparable in terms of their quality characteristics. In addition, frequency distribution was conducted to compare the calibration and validation sets of the nutritional quality components, which indicated that all these values displayed a similar normal distribution (Fig. S2). The PCA scores of NIRS data from both calibration and validation sets were well mixed and distributed quite evenly (Fig. 2b and d). Optimization of spectral variable selection by CARS, RF, and MC-UVE

In the present study, we employed the CARS, RF, and MC-UVE algorithms for spectral variable selection of nutritional quality components of sweet potato samples. The different spectral variable subsets were selected using the three algorithms before NIRS calibration, reflecting their significantly different theoretical underpinnings (Fig. 3). Specifically, the populations of spectral subsets chosen by CARS and RF algorithms were smaller compared to those selected by the MC-UVE algorithm, indicating relatively higher efficiency over MC-UVE in selecting informative variables and judiciously eliminating uninformative variables. Across the eight quality indicators, CARS and RF selected only 22–55 and 50–120 optimal spectral variables, respectively, from 751 variables in the entire spectra. In contrast, the MC-UVE algorithm identified between 328 and 503 spectral variables. In short, the selection of these variables effectively reduces the high collinearity of NIRS and overcomes the limitations of PLSR, providing helpful information to facilitate further quality component prediction. NIRS modeling of quality components

Eight PLSR models were established using a full-range spectrum as control to ensure a fair comparison. Meanwhile, 24 optimized models were developed for various quality components of sweet potato samples, including total starch, amylose, amylopectin, the ratio of amylopectin to amylose, soluble sugar, crude protein, total flavonoid content, and total phenolic content. To better visualize the overall parameters and facilitate greater comprehension, a spider diagram was created to compare the performance of the various employed PLSR models (Fig. 4). Furthermore, external validation sets were applied as independent validation assays to assess the prediction performance of the obtained calibration models. The results indicated that the RMSEP (0.06–1.83 %) values of all 32 models were lower than the RMSECV values, indicating the models were robust and could be extended over an independent sample set. Optimized calibrations showed lower RMSEP values (0.06–1.56 %) and higher RPD (5.78–11.35) and RER (23.47–52.67) values than CK (0.09–1.83 %, 4.82–9.81, and 20.12–31.60), which further demonstrated the utility of spectral variable optimization for predicting multiple chemical properties. The CARS-optimized PLSR models performed better than RF-optimized and MC-UVE-optimized models when predicting the ratio of amylopectin to amylose, soluble sugar, and total phenolic content (Fig. 4). On the other hand, RF optimization significantly improved the performance of PLSR calibrations in accurately predicting total starch, amylose, amylopectin, crude protein, and total flavonoid content. Overall, CARS and RF algorithms can effectively enhance the predictive accuracy of PLSR models for quality components prediction, as they exhibit superior efficiency in selecting informative variables and eliminating uninformative variables.

Discussion

Modern plant breeding and genetic studies increasingly involve many germplasm resources, highlighting the significance of rapid and precise identification of their phenotypes (Oblath et al., 2016, Zhong et al., 2021). Regrettably, traditional strategies for screening germplasm have primarily focused on agronomic traits and root yield, neglecting the quality properties of sweet potatoes. As a result, fewer studies have been conducted to quantify the essential quality traits of roots in large germplasm sets (Laurie et al., 2020). To bridge this critical gap, high-throughput phenotyping of nutritional quality components in sweet potato roots has become increasingly necessary, particularly in quality breeding programs. Among the promising alternative techniques, NIRS has gained significant popularity due to its ability to determine target quality components rapidly and accurately (Li et al., 2017, Tomar et al., 2021). This technique can be a game-changer in this field, offering a high-throughput approach for quality evaluation of sweet potatoes under various breeding schemes, enabling fast screening of vast genetic resources, including parental clones and hybrid progenies (Laurie et al., 2020, Lu et al., 2006). Therefore, establishing a NIRS-based quantitative analysis approach can help to overcome the current limitations of making incremental improvements in the nutritional quality of sweet potato roots.

It is widely accepted that the precision and reliability of NIRS models are predominantly impacted by the number of samples (at least 100), sample representativeness, and genetic variability for traits (Liu et al., 2021, Lu et al., 2006, Xu et al., 2013). Correspondingly, to achieve sufficient variability and diversity in chemical components for NIRS modeling, 125 representative samples were collected from a core collection of sweet potatoes. As anticipated, the results of this study revealed considerable genetic diversity (1.56) and variation coefficient (38.18 %) of quality components among the evaluated samples, which likely associated with self-incompatibility and the long domestication history of sweet potato species (Baccichet, Chiozzotto, Spinardi, Gardana, Bassi, & Cirilli, 2022). Adequate genetic variation, particularly for functional components, enables breeders to take advantage of additive and non-additive gene effects, transgressive segregation, and heterosis (Pfeiffer and McClafferty, 2007, Wang et al., 2022). Therefore, considerable variability observed in the quality components of the samples in this study is desirable not only to build a comprehensive and universal NIRS model but also to ensure the applicability of the model in future predictions across different sample types and component content range (Ejaz et al., 2021).

To improve the accuracy and robustness of NIRS calibrations, it is necessary to optimize the partitioning of sample subsets and the selection of spectral variables (Li et al., 2018, Xu et al., 2013). However, studies on optimizing sample subset partitioning have been largely unnoticed in sweet potato modeling. Random sampling is often used due to its simplicity, but it does not ensure the representativeness of the selected sample subset (Li et al., 2017). In this work, we utilized the KS algorithm to optimize the calibration and validation sets, rendering them more suitable for subsequent multivariate calibrations. In addition, wavelength selection is critical in multivariate calibration, improving prediction performance, ensuring calibration reliability, and providing a more straightforward interpretation (Yun et al., 2019). Previous NIRS calibration models of sweet potatoes were often established based on full-spectrum wavelengths containing numerous irrelevant spectral variables (Diaz et al., 2014, Lebot et al., 2011, Lu et al., 2006, Magwaza et al., 2016). What needs to be improved is that appropriate chemometrics should be employed for model optimization, as NIRS often characterized by high degree overlapping bands and nonspecific peaks (Cao et al., 2022). Among various algorithms, CARS, RF, and MC-UVE displayed relatively higher efficiency in selecting wavelengths with higher regression coefficients in PLSR models and removing those with lower weight (Jia et al., 2022, Li et al., 2018, Li et al., 2017). Indeed, this study employed CARS and RF to obtain optimal variables, resulting in eight robust and accurate NIRS models with significantly lower RMSEC/RMSECV/RMSEP and higher R2C/R2CV/R2V values. The RPD and RER values obtained in this study, exceeding 3 and 15 respectively, indicate the excellent predictive power of the models for the studied quality components, allowing for better practical applications (Li et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2016). Furthermore, higher values of R2V and RPD were observed in this study for NIRS models compared with the results of starch, amylose, and sugars as reported by Lu et al., 2006, Lebot et al., 2011, Diaz et al., 2014, and He et al. (2022). This can be attributed to the utilization of a relatively larger calibration set with ample variability in this study, as well as the optimal wavelength selection achieved through CARS and RF algorithms (He et al., 2022, Shi et al., 2022). For modeling of functional components, this study provides calibration models of total flavonoid content and total phenolic content in sweet potato for the first time, yielding super-high fitness index on the models. In short, this study provides a systematic NIRS assay for high-throughput analysis of sweet potato root quality. The precision achieved is sufficient to allow breeders to screen ideal accessions with high-quality traits and enable food enterprises to determine the various uses of sweet potatoes.

In addition, conventional selection criteria for elite sweet potatoes have predominantly relied on external characteristics such as shape, size, color, and injuries of roots (Sanchez et al., 2020). However, little attention has been given to quality parameters either in germplasm screening or food processing. The present study and recent research have demonstrated the tremendous potential of NIRS technology to support breeding projects in rapidly identifying interesting genotypes based on their quality traits (Hernández-Hernández, Fernández-Cabanás, Rodríguez-Gutiérrez, Fernández-Prior, & Morales-Sillero, 2022). Future breeding programs could rely on the NIRS models developed in this study to screen out and select ideal parent material and release superior varieties those meet the demands of sweet potato market (Dossa et al., 2018). These quantitative models also show great potential for applications in the food industry, including the development of novel value-added and functional food products made from sweet potatoes (Alam, 2021). More importantly, there is a growing need to focus on genome-wide association studies that utilize large-scale quality data (phenotype) obtained through NIRS and genotype data achieved via sequencing technology (Hu et al., 2021, Li et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2020). This approach could provide valuable insights into identifying genetic loci/genes related to the quality traits of interest in sweet potatoes. In summary, spectroscopic techniques offer several advantages, including rapid quantification of multiple quality traits, food quality control, thorough screening of germplasm resources, and the identification of key genes that regulate quality traits in sweet potatoes.

Conclusions

A high-throughput assay to determine the quality components is of great value for screening large-scale germplasm resources. This study utilized 125 representative samples of sweet potato roots with broad diversity (1.56) and variation (38.18 %) for quality characterization and NIRS calibration. Through a dual-optimized strategy of sample subset partitioning and spectral variable selection, eight reliable and optimal PLSR models were successfully constructed. These models exhibited high R2C (0.95–0.99), R2CV (0.93–0.98), R2V (0.89–0.96), RPD (6.33–11.35), and RER (26.29–52.67) values, while relatively low RMSEC (0.05–1.17 %), RMSECV (0.06–1.66 %), and RMSECP (0.06–1.29 %), indicating their excellent prediction capacity. In crux, this study presents a high-throughput assay for the rapid detection of quality components, diversified selection of germplasm resources, and application of interesting genotypes to achieve more efficient and precise sweet potato breeding.
