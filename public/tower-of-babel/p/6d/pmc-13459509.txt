Rewriting the future of anal cancer care: From disparities to data-driven decisions
Authors: D. Arnold, K.-L.G. Spindler
Published: 2026

Anal cancer is a rare, complex disease and an increasingly important global health concern. Current global estimates show more than 54,000 new cases annually, while epidemiological analyses document increasing incidence in many high-income countries [1,2]. The burden is not distributed equally. People living with HIV have a markedly elevated risk of anal cancer, and socioeconomic disparities affect timely diagnosis, access to specialist care and outcomes [3,4]. Despite excellent curative potential for many patients, treatment is associated with major physical, psychological and social consequences, and progress has historically been slowed by the rarity of the disease, stigma, limited commercial incentives and fragmented research infrastructure [5, 6, 7, 8].

The field is now beginning to change. Recent prospective and randomized studies challenge the perception that progress in anal cancer must rely mainly on historical standards, retrospective series or extrapolation from other tumour types. The PLATO programme has provided a contemporary framework for risk-adapted radiotherapy dose personalisation in localized disease, with ACT4 supporting de-escalation in carefully selected early-stage patients, while early ACT5 data underline the complexity of escalation strategies in higher-risk disease [9, 10, 11]. In advanced disease, POD1UM-303/InterAACT-2 has introduced randomized phase III evidence supporting chemoimmunotherapy with retifanlimab plus carboplatin and paclitaxel as a new therapeutic direction [12, 13, 14]. These developments are important; however, this special issue emphasizes that further progress will require internationally coordinated advances across prevention, further clinical trial design, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, biology, survivorship and patient-centred care.

This Special Issue of ESMO Gastrointestinal Oncology gathers experts to address these gaps and opportunities across the care continuum. Collectively, the contributions move beyond summarising evidence and define a research agenda for a disease in transition. The issue underlines that progress in anal cancer will depend not only on new treatments, but also on how well the community harmonises data, embeds translational research, listens to patients, and translates innovation into equitable care.

Placing Patients at the Centre

A central message throughout this issue is the need to place patients at the heart of progress. In Patients' Perspective in Anal Cancer, Manfrida et al. emphasise that the possibility of long-term survival after chemoradiotherapy is inseparable from the burden of treatment and survivorship [15]. Their contribution highlights physical, psychological, sexual and social consequences that are often insufficiently captured in traditional oncological endpoints. Progress must therefore be measured not only by tumour control, but also by dignity, function and quality of life. Meaningful patient participation, from trial design to dissemination, should become a defining feature of future anal cancer research.

Understanding the Evidence Base

Anal cancer research reflects both remarkable success and prolonged inertia. As Glynne-Jones and Mawdsley describe in The Historical Perspective on Anal Cancer Therapy, concurrent chemoradiotherapy transformed the disease in the 1980s and remains the curative backbone today [16]. Their historical analysis also shows how few subsequent trials have changed the fundamental treatment paradigm. Verschoor et al. extend this perspective in An Overview of Clinical Trials in Anal Cancer 1999-2024, documenting the limited number of randomized studies and the need for innovative designs, translational endpoints and global trial collaboration [17].

Complementing these insights, Incidence and Burden of Anal Cancer - Time to Fight the Growing Disparities by Pedersen et al. places the disease in a global and public health context [18]. Their review highlights increasing incidence, persistent gaps in HPV vaccination and screening strategies, and major disparities in prevention and care. Together, these articles remind us that anal cancer is both a clinical and a societal challenge. International collaborations such as International Anal Neoplasia Society, IANS and International Multidisciplinary Anal Cancer Consortium, IMACC are therefore not optional additions, but necessary infrastructures for a rare cancer in which no single country can generate all the evidence required.

Addressing Clinical Complexity

Standard chemoradiotherapy remains highly effective, but carries substantial acute and late toxicity [7,8]. In Optimising Chemoradiotherapy in Anal Cancer, Franco et al. show how imaging, adaptive planning, target definition and personalised dosing can refine treatment intensity while preserving function [19]. Gilbert et al., in Treatment of Anal Cancer in Older Patients and Patients with Frailty, highlight another dimension of complexity: the need to tailor therapy in ageing populations, balancing cure, tolerance, frailty, comorbidity and patient preference [20].

Evin et al., in Therapy for Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Anus in HIV-Positive Patients, address a group central to the epidemiology and equity agenda of anal cancer [21]. Their review emphasises the importance of inclusive research, contemporary HIV management, stigma-free care and careful interpretation of historical outcome differences. Johnsson et al., in Management of Oligometastatic Anal Cancer - A Literature Review and Proposed Treatment Algorithm, provide the first structured overview of an increasingly relevant clinical scenario and propose a pragmatic multimodal approach for selected patients [22]. Similarly, Surgery in Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Anus by Renehan and Nilsson re-affirms that salvage surgery remains vital for persistent or recurrent disease, while calling for international databases and standardised outcomes to advance surgical science [23].

Bridging the Biological Gap

Translational progress in anal cancer has lagged behind clinical development, leaving a gap between biological discovery and patient benefit. In Biological Features of Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Anus - A Deep Dive Review, Samuel et al. synthesise the molecular and immunological landscape, including HPV status, tumour microenvironment, immune response and host genetics [24]. Their review illustrates both the promise of active biological research and the need for greater coordination. Standardisation of biospecimen handling, validation of biomarkers and integration of molecular data with clinical outcomes are essential if biology is to become a driver of treatment adaptation rather than a descriptive add-on.

Circulating HPV DNA is a particularly promising example of this translational agenda. Recent studies support its potential as a prognostic and surveillance biomarker after chemoradiotherapy, but clinical utility still requires prospective testing [25,26]. The Nordic NOAC9 trial, by the Nordic Anal Cancer Group, is directly addressing this gap by evaluating pHPV/ctDNA-guided imaging follow-up after curative chemoradiotherapy [27,28]. In this way, the biological themes raised in the Special Issue are already moving into clinical trial structures that may define how molecular monitoring can be used to detect recurrence earlier, guide imaging and ultimately improve the chance of salvage.

Innovation in Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy remains the backbone of curative treatment but is rapidly evolving. In Innovative Radiotherapy Approaches, Chiloiro et al. describe the shift toward precision-guided techniques such as image-guided radiotherapy, proton therapy and adaptive dosing [29]. These approaches are not technical refinements alone; they represent an opportunity to reduce toxicity, improve functional outcomes and integrate patient-reported and biological endpoints into future trials. The challenge will be to ensure that innovation in radiotherapy is evaluated rigorously and implemented equitably, rather than widening gaps between specialised centres and routine clinical practice.

Evolving Systemic Therapy

Systemic therapy for squamous cell carcinoma of the anus is entering a period of renewal. In Neoadjuvant and Adjuvant Chemotherapy in Anal Cancer - Is There a Role in the Future?, Vendrely et al. revisit historical evidence and outline why systemic intensification remains a rational question for selected high-risk patients [30]. This discussion is particularly timely because ongoing collaborative trials are now testing whether systemic treatment can be moved earlier in the disease course [31,32, 33].

Ntellas et al., in Advances in Anal Cancer Treatment: Current Standards, the Role of Immunotherapy and Emerging Strategies, place immunotherapy and emerging combinations in the context of current standards [34]. Their review captures a field that is no longer confined to single-arm studies in heavily pre-treated disease but is beginning to ask broader questions about first-line treatment, combinations with radiotherapy, predictive biomarkers and sequencing. The publication of POD1UM-303/InterAACT-2 strengthens the relevance of these questions, but the Special Issue appropriately keeps the focus wider: how to build a systemic treatment strategy that is evidence-based, biomarker-informed and accessible across healthcare systems.

A Call for Coordinated Action

This Special Issue is more than a collection of reviews; it is a coordinated call to action. Together, these overviews provide a roadmap for future research. Across all contributions, one message is clear: collaboration, inclusion and transparency are indispensable to progress.

Initiatives such as International Multidisciplinary Anal Cancer Consortium (IMACC) already work to embody a philosophy which unites clinicians, researchers and patients to transform anal cancer from an under-researched malignancy into a model for equitable, data-driven and patient-focused oncology. The challenge now is to ensure that the momentum generated by new trials, new technologies and new collaborations is translated into international evidence that improves survival, reduces toxicity and reflects what matters most to patients.
