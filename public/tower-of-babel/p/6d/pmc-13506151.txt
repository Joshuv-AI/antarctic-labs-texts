Covert Embolic Brain Infarcts, Remote Cortical Thinning, and Cognitive Impairment in Atrial Fibrillation: An Analysis of the Swiss-AF Cohort
Authors: Tim Sinnecker, Stefanie Aeschbacher, Anne Springer, Michael Coslovsky, Juerg Beer, Giorgio Moschovitis, Urs Fischer, Nicolas Rodondi, David Conen, Philipp Krisai, Michael Kühne, Stefan Osswald, Leo H. Bonati, on behalf of the Swiss-AF study investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with atrial fibrillation (AF) exhibit a notable prevalence of both (covert) brain infarcts and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs). They face an elevated risk of cognitive decline and dementia, yet the pathogenesis remains largely unknown. Here, we aimed to investigate a potential relationship between lesion location of embolic brain infarcts or WMHs and (i) remote cortical volume using a tract-based analysis, as well as (ii) global cognitive function using voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional analysis from a prospective, multicenter cohort study of unselected patients with AF (Swiss-AF) enrolling 1,716 patients with eligible MRI data (mean age 73 ± 8 years, 27% women, 90% anticoagulated). We assessed the association of brain lesions including embolic brain infarcts and WMHs in specific white matter tracts with remote cortical volume by using LASSO regression. In addition, voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping was deployed to assess the effect of WMH location in relationship to embolic brain infarcts on cognitive function.ResultsWe observed WMH in 1,699 of 1,716 (99%) and embolic brain infarcts in 395 of 1,716 (23%) patients. The presence of embolic infarcts within most fiber tracts (mean, IQR: 55, 8–66 cm3) accounted for more variability in remote cortical volumes than WMH regional volume (mean, IQR: 366, 63–657 cm3) despite their lower volume. In addition, while the presence of WMH was initially associated with cognitive function across most brain regions (61.8% of voxels with significant association), when adjusting models for the presence of embolic infarcts, only a minor fraction of WMH remained significantly associated with cognitive function (23.6% of voxels).ConclusionsAmong patients with AF, (covert) embolic brain infarcts and WMHs are associated with remote cortical atrophy and with lower cognitive performance. The disruption of brain networks through embolic infarcts seems to contribute to cognitive impairment in patients with AF.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF), a prevalent condition in Western countries, significantly contributes to the incidence of stroke. As the population ages, the prevalence of AF continues to rise [1]. Recent evidence indicates that patients with AF are also at higher risk of cognitive dysfunction and dementia [2]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying cognitive decline in AF remain poorly understood.

Brain infarcts, often occurring without a history of stroke (referred to as covert brain infarcts) [3], and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are frequently observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans in a significant proportion of patients with AF [4]. Moreover, both types of brain lesions have been associated with a decline of cognitive functions [4–6] and mounting evidence suggests that their coexistence plays a pivotal role in cognitive impairment. However, these findings may not be generalizable to all AF patients, as this distinct patient group bears a substantial burden of covert brain infarcts, and the mechanisms of white matter damage may differ between AF and non-AF patients [7]. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether WMHs, brain infarcts, or a combination of both serve as the primary drivers of cognitive impairment in AF, which could significantly impact prevention strategies.

Incorporating lesion location information in the study of this question could enhance reliability and specificity. By doing so, the results can be cross-checked for anatomical plausibility. Additionally, this approach mitigates the impact of other factors that affect larger brain areas, such as the presence of neurodegenerative diseases.

Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping (VLSM) is an approach that investigates the relationship between clinical symptoms and the presence of a lesion at a specific location (voxel) within the brain using MRI. In vascular research [6, 8–11], VLSM has been successfully employed to reveal associations between certain brain lesions and cognitive functions. For instance, frontal WMHs have been linked to slower processing speed and reduced executive functions [8, 10, 11]. Moreover, temporal WMHs have shown an association with memory impairment, and deep WMHs have been studied in relation to motor speed performance [9]. However, VLSM has limitations: it requires a substantial number of lesions at a specific brain location to draw meaningful conclusions. To overcome this, a tract-based approach considers all lesions within each fiber tract of interest during analysis. This technique proves particularly useful when assessing the effect of focal brain lesions on remote cortical thinning due to secondary neurodegeneration [12–14].

To gain deeper insights into the mechanistic factors contributing to the elevated risk of cognitive decline and dementia in patients with AF, our study aimed to investigate the contribution of focal embolic brain infarcts and WMHs in a large cohort of unselected patients with AF on two key aspects: remote cortical volume and global cognitive function. For this purpose, we conducted a fiber tract-based analysis to evaluate associations with remote cortical volume and VLSM to assess associations with cognitive function. Materials and Methods

Study Population

The Swiss-AF study is a prospective cohort study including 2,415 patients with documented AF. The detailed study design has been described previously [3, 15]. Per the original recruitment criteria, patients were ≥65 years of age; an additional 10–15% of patients between 45 and 65 years were recruited to assess the effects of AF on individuals who were part of the active workforce. The final analyzed cohort had an age range of 38.4–95.2 years. While the upper bound fully aligns with the ≥65 years eligibility criterion, a small number of patients were enrolled slightly below the 45-year threshold under protocol extensions to capture notable cases of early-onset AF.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz, Switzerland, and all local Ethics Committees at the study sites (project No. 2021-00701 from the lead Ethics Committee). This full list of participating sites and Ethics Committees can be found at https://www.swissaf.ch and in Reference [15]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Neurocognitive Testing

For these analyses, the following neurocognitive scores were of interest: the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score, the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) score, and the study-specific CoCo score. The MoCA evaluates visuospatial and executive functions, confrontation naming, memory, attention, language, and abstraction. Patients can obtain a maximum of 30 points – a score below 26 usually indicates cognitive impairment [16]. For these analyses, we used the MoCA without correction for years of education. The DSST is a subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale and was employed to assess aspects of cognition such as information processing speed, visuomotor coordination, and attention [17]. The CoCo is a study-specific weighted factor score based on all neurocognitive test scores assessed in the Swiss-AF study [15, 18].

Brain MRI and Lesion Segmentation

MRI of the brain was acquired on 1.5 or 3.0 Tesla scanners. The complete standardized protocol was described previously and included a 3D T1-weighted (T1w) magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (spatial resolution 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3) and a 2D axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR; spatial resolution 1.0 × 1.0 × 3.0 mm3) sequence, which are of relevance for the present analyses. Out of the 2,415 patients enrolled in Swiss-AF, complete baseline brain MRI was available in 1,748 patients. The most frequent reason for missing MRI was the presence of an implanted cardiac device (n = 461).

Segmentation and classification of brain lesions was done on FLAIR images by trained and experienced raters at MIAC AG (Medical Image Analysis Center AG, Basel, Switzerland) according to in-house standard operating procedures approved and validated for international clinical trials [3]. Hereby, blinded expert readers first manually marked the different lesion types. Lesion volumes were then segmented semi-automatically using Amira software (Mercury Computer Systems Inc., Chelmsford, MA) on T2-weighted FLAIR images. All ratings were confirmed by board-certified neuroradiologists. In this study, we focused on embolic infarcts only as they are more relevant in patients with AF. Embolic infarcts were defined as large non-cortical infarcts (hyperintense lesions >20 mm in diameter on FLAIR on axial sections) and/or cortical infarcts (hyperintense lesions of any size on FLAIR involving the cortex) as done previously [3]. This classification is based on accumulating evidence that larger subcortical infarcts are associated with cardioembolic mechanisms rather than small vessel disease (SVD) [19, 20]. The >20 mm threshold was chosen based on the neuroimaging standards for research into SVD (STRIVE-2 criteria) [21], which defines recent small subcortical infarcts with a maximal axial lesion diameter of 20 mm. We are aware that lacunes of presumed vascular origin were defined in the same work with a maximal axial lesion diameter of 15 mm but decided to keep the more conservative 20 mm threshold for consistency and on the background that not all of these infarcts represent lacunes. Infarcts were considered covert in patients without a stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). WMHs were defined in accordance with STRIVE-2 criteria as T2-weighted FLAIR hyperintense lesions in either the periventricular or deep white matter, excluding perivascular spaces defined by tubular morphology [22]. The distinction between embolic infarcts and WMHs was further improved by considering the morphology of lesions as expert raters specifically looked for patterns that deviated from the overall WMH picture. Also, the degree of assumed tissue damage was considered by the expert raters, including T1-weighted hypointensity and the presence of lacunes or CSF-isointense areas within lesions. Lesion masks were dilated slice by slice by one voxel because diffusion tensor imaging studies have shown that WM integrity is compromised in the voxels immediately surrounding the lesions [23].

Anatomical Segmentation, Image Registration, and Spatial Lesion Distribution

Segmentation of subcortical and cortical areas was done using FreeSurfer v6.0 (Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Boston, MA, USA) on T1w MRI images and reviewed for quality. For analysis of lesions within specific white matter tracts, FLAIR lesion masks were registered onto MNI152 standard space using FLIRT (FMRIB’s linear registration tool; using default settings, nearest-neighbor interpolation for lesion masks) [24] to calculate lesion volumes in specific white matter tracts as defined by the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) white-matter tractography atlas [25, 26]. This atlas includes 20 probabilistically identified white matter tracts derived from deterministic diffusion tensor imaging-based tractography and was used previously to assess tract-dependent effects of vascular lesions [8, 10, 14]. Associations of white matter tracts with remote cortical areas were identified by assessing the overlap with the Desikan-Killiany atlas used by FreeSurfer. The small fiber tract #19 (temporal part of left superior longitudinal fasciculus) only showed marginal overlap with the Desikan-Killiany atlas and was thus excluded.

For VLSM, FLAIR images and lesion masks were first linearly registered onto T1w image coordinates using FLIRT as described above. At this step, the patients’ skulls were removed from the T1w images using FreeSurfer (binarization of “aseg.auto” output from “recon-all” pipeline). In each slice, a rim of two voxels was added to the extracted brains since a less restrictive brain extraction yielded better results in the later registration. Then, WMH and embolic infarcts were filled on T1w images to minimize their effect on nonlinear image registration. This was done using the lesion filling algorithm in FSL v5.0 (FMRIB’s Software Library; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki) [27] and masking of the lesion portions within the gray matter.

Second, FLAIR-to-T1w images and lesion masks were nonlinearly registered onto an age-specific standard brain template from the BRAINS imagebank (https://www.brainsimagebank.ac.uk/; for brains early to mid-seventies; spatial resolution 1.0 × 1.3 × 1.0 mm3) using FNIRT (FMRIB’s nonlinear registration tool; settings as specified in the provided configuration file, taking as reference the age-specific brain template, nearest-neighbor interpolation for lesion masks). The quality of the registration was assessed in all patients by a trained and experienced rater. Patients with misregistrations were excluded from subsequent analyses. Finally, the co-registered WMH masks of all included patients were overlaid on the age-specific brain template. In this way, a spatial lesion distribution map was obtained.

Statistics: White Matter Tract-Based Analysis

All statistical analyses were implemented in R v3.6.2 (https://r-project.org/). For each white matter fiber tract, we created a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model after scaling and centering of data with cortical volume remote to the given fiber tract as the dependent variable. Age, sex, study center, whole brain volume, whole brain total FLAIR lesion volume, volume of WMH in the given fiber tract, and volume of embolic lesions in the given fiber tract were included as candidate predictors in the starting model prior to LASSO regularization. Covariate selection was justified as follows: age was included as a covariate in all statistical models to isolate the independent effects of lesion location and WMH burden on cognitive performance and cortical volume while controlling for the well-established association between age and cognitive decline. Sex is also known to influence cortical thickness and lesion burden. Study center accounts for potential differences in MRI acquisition protocols across sites. Whole brain volume corrects for global brain atrophy to isolate regional effects. Whole brain total FLAIR lesion volume is included as a nuisance covariate for global lesion burden, following established practice in regional lesion-symptom mapping that controls for total lesion load, so the regional predictor can address whether lesion location adds explanatory power beyond global disease severity [28]. Regional lesion volumes (WMH and embolic lesions in the given fiber tract) are the primary predictors of interest; LASSO regularization is well-suited to this question because the goal is variable selection letting the data adjudicate which signal (global or regional) carries predictive weight rather than estimating independent partial coefficients for potentially collinear predictors. LASSO is a regression analysis method that uses L1 regularization. By shrinking some coefficients to zero, LASSO effectively performs feature selection, simplifying the model and making it more interpretable. There are different ways to interpret variable importance in LASSO regression. Larger coefficients, if data were standardized, usually indicate stronger associations between predictors and the dependent variable. Also, coefficients with a value above 0 indicate that the given covariate has significantly contributed to the model. Another way to consider importance in LASSO is by examining the order in which variables are penalized to zero. Variables that remain in the model longer (i.e., are less aggressively penalized) could be considered more important. The analysis of the magnitude of coefficients was in alignment with the order of penalization in all LASSO models. The optimal regularization parameter was chosen by cross-validation. LASSO regression was implemented using 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal regularization parameter (lambda.min). All predictors were standardized prior to model fitting. Model performance was assessed using R2 metrics.

Statistics: VLSM

To identify lesion locations significantly influencing the patient’s cognition, a VLSM approach was applied [6]. Two groups of patients were defined for each voxel: one group including all the patients with a lesion in this voxel and the second group including all the patients without a lesion in the same voxel. In separate models, the clinical scores were then compared between these two groups using the Brunner-Munzel test [29]. This is a nonparametric alternative to the two-sample t test, frequently used for VLSM analyses. The Brunner-Munzel test provides better power than the t test when the assumptions of the t test are violated. It is also applicable in situations where there are ties or where the data are based on discrete numerical clinical scores [30]. For every patient, cognitive scores were norm-corrected for age, gender, and years of education (“model 1”), and – in separate models – additionally for history of prior TIA or ischemic stroke (“model 1 + history”), or for the presence of infarcts – covert and overt – on MRI (“model 1 + infarcts”). A minimal lesion occurrence threshold of 10 lesions per voxel was set since studies have shown that this test produces inflated z scores when used at any voxel where there are fewer than 10 subjects in each group [31]. A Benjamini and Hochberg procedure (false discovery rate correction) was applied to correct results for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes (ESs), describing the “probability of a better outcome” in the group of patients without a lesion compared to those with a lesion in a specific voxel (with benchmark values of 0.56 = “small,” 0.64 = “medium,” and 0.71 = “large” ESs), were reported. Only ESs of voxels characterized by significant associations (q < 0.05) were color-coded in the resulting maps.

Statistics: Additional Multivariable Analyses

To examine hemispheric differences and cortical-involvement classification in the association between embolic infarcts and cognitive performance, we conducted multivariable linear regression analyses. Covariates in all models included age, sex, education, history of stroke/TIA, cardiovascular risk factors, and WMH volume. Separate models were created for total versus left/right hemispheric LNCCI (large non-cortical and cortical infarcts) burden and for cortical involvement classifications (lesion-based and voxel-based fractional-split statistics derived from FreeSurfer cortical segmentations). Results of the cortical involvement classification analyses are presented in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/10.1159/000553651). To further evaluate the robustness of the LNCCI-cognition relationship across different age strata, we performed age-interaction analyses (with age centered on the cohort mean) and stratified linear regression models (<65, 65–75, and >75 years). The full stratified results are provided in online supplementary Table 2. To test whether our findings were driven by post-stroke cognitive impairment, we additionally stratified the cohort by history of prior stroke/TIA and re-ran the multivariable MoCA model in each subgroup (with history of stroke/TIA excluded as a covariate). The full stratified results are provided in online supplementary Table 3.

The white matter fiber tract-based analysis involved additional registration and segmentation steps to calculate cortical volumes. It was successfully performed in 1,221 patients. The heatmaps depicting the total lesion volume in each white matter fiber tract reveal that the volume of WMH exceeds that of embolic lesions within each tract (as summarized in Table 2).

The LASSO models – with candidate predictors comprising age, sex, study center, whole brain volume, whole brain total FLAIR lesion volume, volume of WMH in a given fiber tract, and volume of embolic lesions in a given fiber tract – explained a median of 24.2% (range 16.2–32.7%) of the variance in remote cortical volume at the cross-validation-selected regularization parameter (lambda.min). Notably, patient sex and age emerged as the most influential factors. Additionally, the imaging center was ranked as significant by the LASSO models across fiber tracts, further emphasizing its impact (also detailed in Table 2). Lastly, the whole brain total FLAIR lesion volume, along with the volume of WMH in a given fiber tract, and the volume of embolic lesions in a given fiber tract were all classified as relevant contributors.

In most fiber tracts with substantial lesion volumes, the volume of embolic lesions ranked higher in importance than the volume of WMH or the whole brain total FLAIR lesion volume (as indicated in Table 2). For instance, consider the left and right superior longitudinal fasciculus (tracts #15 and #16), which exhibited significant presence and overlap of both WMH and embolic lesions. However, the embolic lesions were consistently ranked as more relevant. Notably, WMH in the right superior longitudinal fasciculus (tract #16) did not substantially contribute to the model (Table 2; Fig. 3). In fiber tracts where WMH and embolic lesions had limited presence and overlap (e.g., tracts #17–20, Fig. 3), their importance ranking was comparatively low.

Voxels with significant associations were evenly spread across the periventricular white matter (Fig. 4, 5). Embolic Infarcts and Association between WMH and Cognition

When including the covariate “history of prior TIA or stroke,” the number of voxels with WMH associated with cognitive scores decreased (Fig. 4, 5, middle row; Table 3, “model 1 + history”). Furthermore, when incorporating the binary variable “presence of infarcts” that is not causally related to WMH volume as an additional covariate, even fewer voxels demonstrated statistical significance (Fig. 4, 5, bottom row; Table 3, “model 1 + infarcts”).

Left versus Right Hemispheric Infarcts and Cognitive Performance

In additional analyses examining hemispheric differences, we found that both left and right hemispheric embolic infarcts showed significant independent associations with worse cognitive performance. The left hemisphere demonstrated a stronger ES for both count (β = −0.0362, p = 0.0007) and volume (β = −0.1529, p < 0.001) measures compared to the right hemisphere (count: β = −0.0249, p = 0.0040; volume: β = −0.0492, p = 0.0186). This finding is consistent with the known lateralization of language and executive function networks predominantly in the left hemisphere, suggesting that left hemispheric infarcts may have a more pronounced impact on the cognitive domains assessed in our study. These results align with previous work documenting right hemispheric predominance of brain infarcts in AF patients [32], while demonstrating that left-sided lesions have stronger cognitive effects.

Discussion

In our study involving patients with AF, we investigated covert and clinically overt embolic brain infarcts as well as individual WMH and analyzed their association with remote cortical volume using a tract-based analysis. Additionally, we explored their impact on global cognitive impairment through VLSM.

We discovered that, although embolic brain infarcts have a smaller volume, they account for more variability in remote cortical volumes compared to the presence of WMH. While examining cognition, we noted an association between WMH and cognitive impairment across most brain areas. However, when considering the presence of embolic brain infarcts, the ESs diminished, and only a small subset of WMH remained associated with cognitive function. Together, these findings highlight embolic infarcts as a relevant determinant of cognitive function in our AF cohort.

The reasons for this phenomenon remain speculative. In addition to the direct effects of damaged brain tissue and networks resulting from brain infarcts, it is possible that infarcts amplify the influence of WMH on cognition. In simpler terms, when WMH occurs in less vulnerable brain regions, the brain can compensate effectively without crossing the clinical threshold, especially in the absence of brain infarcts. Embolic brain infarcts, whether clinically overt or not, likely reduce this threshold by constraining compensatory mechanisms. As a result, WMH that would otherwise remain asymptomatic can still negatively impact cognitive performance when they coexist with infarcts.

This hypothesis is supported by previous studies that have observed an association of focal [33] and global WMH [34] or white matter integrity loss [35] with reduced cortical thickness following damage to associated fiber tracts and anterograde degeneration [13], cortical network breakdown, and cognitive impairment in patients with SVD [33].

In accordance, focal thinning of anatomically connected cortical areas was also observed following lacunar infarcts [36], subcortical acute infarcts [12], and stroke in general [37]. Secondary neurodegeneration with trans-synaptic effects was also suspected as the cause [12].

In the context of the coexistence of WMH and ischemic stroke, an association of WMH with cognitive function was observed in patients with ischemic stroke [38–40] while the strongest correlations with MoCA were observed when combining both stroke and SVD-associated damage [41]. Longitudinal studies revealed similar results: in patients with first-ever ischemic events, WMH in the corpus callosum, corona radiata, and thalamic radiation better explained long-term outcomes than the acute ischemic lesion itself [42]. In addition, a higher WMH burden was found to be predictive of worse cognitive outcomes in patients with minor stroke or TIA [43]. Similar results have been observed 1 year after mild to moderate stroke [44], in younger patients with cryptogenic stroke [45], and with regard to periventricular WMH in particular [46].

Our study builds upon prior research by emphasizing the significance of (covert) embolic brain infarcts within the specific patient population affected by AF. This is relevant for two key reasons:

First, findings from studies involving patients with SVD may not be directly applicable to AF patients as the underlying mechanisms causing white matter damage could diverge in AF patients despite the shared vascular risk factors between these two groups [7]. Second, existing clinical models and risk stratification scores used to estimate stroke risk may not adequately predict the occurrence of covert brain infarcts in patients with AF. The population-based Rotterdam Scan Study, for example, did not find an association between covert brain infarcts and established vascular risk factors such as diabetes mellitus and smoking [47]. Another study observed a high prevalence of covert brain infarcts (11.7–60%) in asymptomatic AF patients with low-to-moderate embolic risk according to CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc [48, 49]. In our own AF cohort, we reported that important components of the CHA2DS2-VASc Score – namely, history of heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, and female sex – were not significantly associated with covert brain infarcts [49].

One idea to address this challenge would be to incorporate brain MRI screening into the clinical workup for patients with AF, thereby identifying those who are at heightened risk of cognitive decline. However, at present there are no consensus guidelines or proven therapeutic pathways for managing covert infarcts once they are detected, so the clinical utility of such screening is uncertain. Prospective, long-term studies are needed to determine whether identification of covert lesions leads to improved cognitive outcomes through intensified cardiovascular risk factor control.

Our study, while informative, is not without limitations. First, due to differences in prevalence and lesion overlap between brain infarcts and WMHs, the use of VLSM was not suitable for directly studying the effects of brain infarcts. Instead, we opted for a tract-based approach, which allowed us to eventually explore these effects. Another limitation lies in our inability to directly compare the impact of covert brain infarcts versus overt brain infarcts. Patients with a history of clinical stroke episodes may also exhibit covert brain infarcts on MRI scans, making it challenging to retrospectively distinguish them from overt brain infarcts. Additionally, distinguishing WMH from embolic infarcts based solely on their morphology can be unreliable. WMH may not always be clearly differentiated from embolic infarcts. Furthermore, we cannot rule out the possibility that covert brain infarcts and WMH existed prior to the onset of AF. Lastly, the effect of embolic infarcts on white matter tracts and their potential impact on remote cortical volume may be overestimated due to the presence of cortical microinfarcts in associated vascular territories. Additionally, the use of atlas-based tractography introduces limitations compared to individual anatomical reconstructions as standardized atlases may not fully capture individual variations in white matter tract anatomy. The transformation of 2D FLAIR lesion masks to MNI152 space using nearest neighbor interpolation preserves lesion boundaries but represents an inherent trade-off between anatomical precision and analytical consistency.

Despite these limitations, our study’s strength lies in its systematic assessment of both covert and overt brain infarcts in an unselected but well-characterized AF population. In summary, our research findings indicate that in patients with AF, covert and/or overt embolic brain infarcts are associated with remote cortical atrophy. Additionally, these infarcts appear to impact cognition beyond WMH, potentially due to disruptions in brain networks. Consequently, therapeutic and risk stratification strategies should address both preventing embolic events and managing SVD in this specific patient population.
