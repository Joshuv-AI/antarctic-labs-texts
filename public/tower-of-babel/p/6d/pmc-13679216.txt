4-phenylbutyric acid-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and reduce intraocular pressure in glaucoma model
Authors: Xuejing Yan, Wu Qin, Xiaoming Gan, Yingyan Mao, Shen Wu, Shipeng Wen, Jingxue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop a mesoporous silica nanoparticle-based delivery system for 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA) and evaluate its potential to alleviate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and improve mutant myocilin (MYOC)-associated glaucoma.Materials and methodsWe synthesized hollow/snowflake-like mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with 4-PBA (mSiO₂-PBA) for drug loading and release. Cellular uptake and cytotoxicity were evaluated in human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and human trabecular meshwork cells (HTMCs). The effects of mSiO₂-PBA on mutant MYOC secretion and ER stress-associated proteins were assessed by Western blot analysis. In Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, ocular distribution of mSiO₂-PBA was examined by fluorescence imaging and high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated by intraocular pressure (IOP) measurements, immunofluorescence analysis of MYOC accumulation and ER stress-associated markers in trabecular meshwork tissue, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).ResultsmSiO₂-PBA exhibited efficient cellular uptake and favorable biocompatibility in vitro. Treatment with mSiO₂-PBA enhanced mutant MYOC secretion and reduced ER stress-associated KDEL-positive proteins in HTMCs. Following topical administration, mSiO₂-PBA achieved ocular penetration and exposure in trabecular meshwork tissue. In Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, mSiO₂-PBA significantly reduced IOP, decreased mutant MYOC accumulation, and improved ER ultrastructural abnormalities in trabecular meshwork cells.ConclusionsmSiO₂-PBA represents a promising nanoparticle-based delivery platform for 4-PBA in MYOC-associated glaucoma. By promoting mutant MYOC secretion and alleviating ER stress, mSiO₂-PBA demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo, supporting its potential as a nanotherapeutic strategy for MYOC-associated glaucoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00907-z.Keywords: Glaucoma, Trabecular meshwork, MYOC, mSiO₂-PBA

Introduction

Glaucoma, the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, represents a critical global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization, the global prevalence of glaucoma reached 76 million in 2020, with 22 million cases reported in China [1]. It is further estimated that this number will rise to 111.8 million worldwide by 2040 [2]. Pathological elevation of intraocular pressure (IOP) is a major driver of glaucoma pathogenesis, primarily attributed to impaired aqueous humor outflow. Approximately 85% of aqueous humor drainage occurs through the trabecular meshwork (TM), highlighting the pivotal role of trabecular meshwork cell (TMC) function in maintaining normal IOP [3].

Recent advances in molecular genetics, from our team and other researchers, have clarified the key mechanisms underlying TMC dysfunction, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [4, 5], ferroptosis [6, 7] and mitochondrial dysfunction [8, 9], thereby providing new directions for glaucoma therapeutics. Among the associated genes, the myocilin (MYOC) gene, encoding TM glucocorticoid-induced response protein, was the first and remains the most frequently identified causative gene for glaucoma [10]. Notably, the S341P MYOC mutation impairs cellular resilience, rendering TM cells more sensitive to pathological stimuli such as cyclic mechanical stretch [8]. Both S341P and N450Y MYOC mutations induce MYOC misfolding, leading to intracellular aggregation within the ER and subsequent ER stress. Intervention with the chemical chaperone 4-PBA has been shown to successfully correct MYOC misfolding, restore extracellular secretion, and preserve TMC function [11]. As a drug approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of urea cycle disorders [12–14], 4-PBA can mitigate protein misfolding and suppress ER stress. Previous studies have confirmed that systemic or topical administration of 4-PBA reduces pathological IOP elevation in animal models, positioning 4-PBA as a promising dual-action therapeutic for glaucoma that concurrently alleviates TMC damage and lowers IOP [15, 16].

However, the clinical translation of 4-PBA is severely hindered by its rapid metabolism, which requires frequent administration (up to 6–7 times daily). This is particularly problematic for glaucoma, a chronic disease that demands lifelong pharmacological intervention. Therefore, developing a delivery system with controlled release characteristics to improve ocular exposure of 4-PBA represents an important strategy for enhancing its therapeutic potential.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (mSiO₂) offer a promising approach to overcoming these challenges [17, 18]. Their stable chemical structure, tunable size, and favorable biocompatibility make them attractive drug-delivery vehicles. The hollow interior provides high drug-loading capacity, while the mesoporous shell enables controlled drug release [19]. In addition, surface hydroxyl groups can form hydrogen bonds with mucin, potentially enhancing corneal adhesion. Therefore, mSiO₂-based systems may improve the ocular bioavailability of 4-PBA compared with conventional formulations. To further optimize ocular delivery, we developed a novel snowflake-like hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticle platform using upconversion luminescent nanoparticles as sacrificial templates. The unique hollow-mesoporous architecture provides a large drug reservoir while maintaining a particle size suitable for ocular administration. We hypothesized that this nanoplatform could improve ocular exposure of 4-PBA and facilitate its therapeutic application in MYOC-associated glaucoma. Based on these considerations, the present study investigated the effects of 4-PBA-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (mSiO₂-PBA) on mutant MYOC-associated trabecular meshwork dysfunction, ER stress, and intraocular pressure in both in vitro models and a Tg-MYOCN450Y mouse model of glaucoma.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of NaYF4:Yb, Er nanocrystals

YCl₃·6 H₂O, YbCl₃·6 H₂O, and ErCl₃·6 H₂O were dissolved in oleic acid/1-octadecene and maintained at 160 °C for 30 min. Upon cooling, the methanol solution of NH₄F and NaOH were added. Methanol was removed at 70 °C, followed by heating at 300 °C under N₂ for 1 h. The product was precipitated with ethanol and purified by centrifugation to yield NaYF₄:Yb, Er(UCNP), store in hexane solution at -5 °C.

Synthesis of mesoporous/hollow mSiO2 coatings

UCNP were initially coated with DSPE-PEG2k-OSilane in CH₂Cl₂. After rotary-evaporated and freeze-dried, the sample was redispersed in water to form UCNP@DSPE. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were emulsified in a hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium chloride/p-xylene aqueous system, followed by the addition of tetraethyl orthosilicate and L-arginine, and stirred at 45 °C for 24 h to obtain UCNP@DSPE@mSiO₂. The template was removed by refluxing in ethanol/HCl solution at 90 °C for 6 h, and the resulting mesoporous/hollow mSiO₂ was purified by dialysis.

Fluorescent labeling

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) reacted with 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane in anhydrous ethanol under dark conditions for 12 h, followed by reaction with mSiO₂ for another 12 h. The obtained fluorescently labeled nanoparticles (mSiO₂-FITC) were obtained by centrifugation, washing, and drying.

Cell culture

Primary human trabecular meshwork cells (HTMCs; ScienCell, Cat# 6590/5987) were maintained in complete TMCM Medium (ScienCell) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum, antibiotics, and growth factors. Only early-passage cells (P3-P6) were utilized in experimental procedures.

An immortalized human corneal epithelial cell (HCEC) line was kindly provided by our colleague Yaqiong Li. HCECs were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco) with the addition of 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1×penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco).

Cell permeability detection

The mSiO₂-FITC (12.5 mg/mL) was added to the culture medium of HTMCs or HCECs for 48 h. Fluorescent images were captured at various time points by ZEISS microscopy.

Evaluation of mSiO₂ biosafety

The biosafety of mSiO₂ was evaluated by investigating the viability of cells after coculture with mSiO₂ for 24 h using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. HTMCs or HCECs were seeded in triplicate into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. After 24 h, the cell culture medium was replaced with medium containing various concentrations of mSiO₂ followed by incubation for another 24 h. Next, CCK-8 solution (10 µL) was added to each well and samples were incubated for 2 h. Finally, cell viability was detected by measuring absorbance at 450 nm wavelength using a microplate reader.

Tissue distribution of mSiO₂

N450Y-mutant myocilin transgenic mice (Tg-MYOCN450Y) on a C57BL/6J background were generated as previously reported [4]. Briefly, human MYOC cDNA carrying the Asn450Tyr (N450Y) mutation was introduced into C57BL/6J mice using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homologous recombination strategy. Transgenic founders were identified by PCR genotyping. Primers for genotyping the MYOC-N450Y knock-in allele were as follows: forward, 5’-GGGTTCGGCTTCTGGCGTGT-3’; reverse 5’- TGAGTCGAGCTTTGGTGGCCT-3’. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University (AEEI-2023-310) and adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

The mSiO₂-FITC (20 µL per eye, 12.5 mg/mL) were administered as eye drops twice daily (10:00, 17:00). The distribution of mSiO₂-FITC was monitored at various time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h) post-administration using fluorescence microscopy (ZEISS). Eye samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in OCT, and sectioned (thickness: 5 μm) for microscopy imaging.

Lentivirus infection

The complete lentivirus vector map and detailed lentiviruses construction procedures have been fully described in our previously published study [20]. For lentivirus transfection, 1.0 × 105 HTMCs were seeded into six-well plates and cultured for approximately 12 h. Lentivirus-expressing wild-type (WT) or N450Y MYOC-infected HTMCs were added at a multiplicity of infection of 5. Polybrene (5 µg/mL) was used to enhance lentivirus infection efficiency. Culture medium was exchanged for fresh medium within 24 h. Only early-passage cells (P2-P3) were used for subsequent experiments.

Western blotting

Harvested cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, P0013B). Protein content was measured with a Thermo Scientific bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (23227), and 40 µg aliquots were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis before electroblotting onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk (2 h), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) followed by washing with tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 and incubation with a secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an Bio-Rad imaging system. The antibodies used in this study were MYOC (Santa Cruz, sc-515500), KDEL (abcam, ab176333), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Santa Cruz, sc-25778), which served as a loading control.

Slit lamp examination

Ocular anterior segment structures (cornea, iris, and lens) were assessed using a Haag-Streit BX-900 slit-lamp biomicroscope. For documentation purposes, a Canon digital SLR camera was mounted to the slit-lamp, maintaining identical exposure parameters (aperture, International Organization for Standardization [ISO], shutter speed) across all imaging sessions.

IOP measurement

Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for 5–6 min and a Tonolab rebound tonometer was used to detect IOP. Five sequential IOP readings were obtained per eye using automated tonometry, with the device’s internal algorithm computing the mean value. This measurement cycle was performed thrice for each eye, and all resultant pressure values were documented for analysis. All the measurements were performed between 15:00 and 17:00.

Hematoxylin-and-eosin staining

The enucleated eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Subsequently, sections were cut (thickness: 5 μm), dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Immunostaining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to standard deparaffinization and rehydration. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the samples in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After washing thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), nonspecific binding sites were blocked with a solution containing 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma–Aldrich) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Solarbio) for 60 min at room temperature. Incubation with primary antibodies was carried out using a rabbit monoclonal antibody against KDEL (diluted 1:100; Abcam, ab176333) or a mouse monoclonal antibody against MYOC (diluted 1:50, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-515500) at 4°C overnight. Following washes with PBS, sections were treated with a Alexa Fluor™ 546 labeled Donkey anti-Rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) Secondary Antibody (diluted in 1:800; Thermo Fisher, A10040) or Fluor™ 546 labeled Donkey anti-Mouse IgG Secondary Antibody (diluted in 1:800; Thermo Fisher, A10036) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were visualized using 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma–Aldrich), and fluorescence images were captured with a Leica confocal imaging system.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following a 2 h primary fixation at room temperature with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, enucleated eyes were dissected. One quadrant of the anterior segment was reserved at 4 °C, rinsed thrice with PBS, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. A graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, 100%) was used for dehydration prior to embedding in epoxy resin for ultrastructural examination.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± SD. The sample size (n), type of replicate (biological or technical), and statistical test used for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Longitudinal intraocular pressure measurements were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterizations of mSiO₂ and mSiO₂-FITC

The process for the preparation of monodisperse mesoporous-shelled/hollow-core “snowflake-like” mSiO₂ microspheres is illustrated in Fig. 1A. The uniform particle size and well-defined structure of the microspheres mainly depend on the size and regularity of the UCNP core templates. By tuning the core template size, the diameter of the hollow cavity can be controlled. In this study, NaYF₄:Yb, Er nanoparticles were selected as core templates and synthesized via a solvothermal method. TEM images in Fig. 1B show that the NaYF₄:Yb, Er nanoparticles exhibit a regular morphology, uniform particle size, and hexagonal cross-sections. Statistical analysis of the TEM images in Fig. 1C indicated that the nanoparticles have an average particle diameter of approximately 43.8 nm. TEM images of the mesoporous-shelled/hollow-core “snowflake-like” mSiO₂ microspheres are shown in Fig. 1D. The dark comb-like regions correspond to the silica mesoporous shell, while the light central area represents the hollow cavity. SEM images, shown in Fig. 1E, further confirm the porous morphology on the particle surface. The as-prepared microspheres display uniform size distribution, well-defined snowflake-like morphology, clear pore structures, and excellent monodispersity. The hollow cavity provides abundant internal space, thereby enhancing the potential drug-loading capacity. The N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution curves of the mesoporous-shelled/hollow-core mSiO₂ nanoparticles, shown in Fig. 1F and G, respectively, exhibit typical type IV behavior, confirming their mesoporous nature. The obtained porous particles possess a specific surface area of 267.92 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.486 cm³/g, and an average pore diameter of 15.23 nm. These characteristics enable mSiO2 nanoparticles to be suitable for drug adsorption and diffusion.

As one of the important organs of the human body, the eye has a strong self-protective structure. The ability of drugs to penetrate the cornea and accumulate around the target site is particularly important in the treatment of eyes. The mSiO₂ microspheres have nanoscale size and stable architecture, allowing them to penetrate the cornea and accumulate in deep ocular tissues. To validate the penetration process, mSiO₂ nanospheres were surface-functionalized with amino groups in (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane. Subsequently, the isothiocyanate group (–N = C=S) in FITC reacted with the amino groups on the surface of mSiO₂ nanospheres, enabling covalent grafting of FITC to mSiO₂ [21, 22]. The grafting reaction is illustrated in Fig. 1H and I. A characteristic C = S stretching band at 1,125 cm⁻¹ and an –NH stretching vibration at 3,351 cm⁻¹ verified the presence of FITC moieties on the modified mSiO₂ microspheres.

These results indicate that the synthesized monodisperse mesoporous-shelled/hollow-core “snowflake-like” mSiO₂ microspheres possess uniform size, well-defined morphology, high specific surface area, and a hollow cavity favorable for drug loading. Moreover, FITC labeling enables fluorescence imaging to evaluate ocular penetration.

Biosafety and cellular uptake of mSiO₂-PBA

Hollow mesoporous mSiO₂ microspheres were utilized to encapsulate 4-PBA via electrostatic adsorption. A standard calibration curve for 4-PBA at 260 nm was established (Fig. 2A), yielding an encapsulation efficiency of 79.6% and a drug loading capacity of 37.6%, confirming efficient entrapment of 4-PBA within the mesoporous-hollow structure.

Following successful encapsulation, the in vitro drug release profile of mSiO₂-PBA nanocomposites was evaluated. The nanocomposites were dispersed in pH 7.4 PBS at 37 °C under gentle agitation, and released 4-PBA concentrations were quantified by absorbance at 260 nm at serial time points. As shown in Fig. 2B, mSiO₂-PBA exhibited a sustained release profile, with ~ 82% of 4-PBA released over 24 h, in contrast to the instantaneous release of free 4-PBA. These results demonstrate that the mesoporous-hollow architecture enables controlled drug release and eliminates premature burst release.

To assess suitability for topical ocular application, the biocompatibility of the mSiO₂-PBA was evaluated in relevant ocular cell lines. Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) were exposed to increasing concentrations of mSiO₂-PBA. As shown in Fig. 2C, mSiO₂ did not affect HCEC viability at 0.1 and 0.3 mM, but significantly inhibited viability at 0.5, 1, and 2.5mM. Cytotoxicity was also assessed in human trabecular meshwork cells (HTMCs), the target cells for 4-PBA therapy. Figure 2D shows that mSiO₂ exerted minimal cytotoxicity in HTMCs at low concentrations, with a marked reduction in cell viability observed at higher concentrations. Per ISO 10,993 international standards, mSiO₂ materials were classified as Grade 1 cytotoxicity, meeting qualification requirements and confirming favorable biocompatibility. Based on these results, 0.1 mM and 0.3 mM were selected for subsequent in vitro assays.

Next, cellular uptake of mSiO₂ was evaluated in HCECs and HTMCs. To visualize biodistribution, mSiO₂ was conjugated with FITC to generate fluorescently labeled mSiO₂-FITC. In HCECs, the strongest FITC fluorescence signal was detected at 1.5 h, gradually diminishing thereafter and completely clearing by 48 h (Fig. 2E). In HTMCs, fluorescence was detectable as early as 0.5 h, peaked at 24 h, and was undetectable by 48 h (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these findings confirm the biosafety of mSiO₂-based nanocarriers and their ability to be internalized by ocular cells, supporting their potential for topical ocular drug delivery.

mSiO₂-PBA partially promotes mutant MYOC secretion and alleviates ER stress

Previous studies reported that 4-PBA could promote mutant MYOC secretion and alleviate ER stress [11, 16]. We next investigated whether mSiO₂-PBA could ameliorate the cellular phenotype in MYOC-overexpressing HTMCs. As shown in Fig. 3A, both wild-type (WT) MYOC and mutant MYOC were significantly overexpressed in HTMCs compared with the control group. As shown in Fig. 3B-D, Western blot analysis using a KDEL antibody revealed two predominant KDEL-reactive bands migrating at molecular weights consistent with GRP94 (~ 100 kDa) and GRP78 (~ 78 kDa), respectively. In MYOCN450Y -overexpressing HTMCs, treatment with 0.3 mM 4-PBA or mSiO₂-PBA significantly reduced intensity of both KDEL-positive bands compared with the vehicle control. In MYOCWT cells, no consistent changes in these ER-associated proteins were observed following 4-PBA or mSiO₂-PBA treatment. Furthermore, we evaluated MYOC secretion in the culture supernatant. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, in MYOCN450Y cells, treatment with 4-PBA or mSiO₂-PBA significantly increased the secretion of mutant MYOC in MYOCN450Y cells. In contrast, no significant change in MYOC secretion was observed in MYOCWT cells following treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that mSiO₂-PBA promotes the secretion of mutant MYOC and reduces ER stress-associated KDEL-positive proteins in MYOCN450Y-overexpressing HTMCs, supporting an alleviation of mutant MYOC-induced ER stress.

In vivo distribution of mSiO₂-loaded drugs

Building on the favorable in vitro biocompatibility and cellular uptake profiles, we next evaluated the ocular distribution of mSiO₂-PBA in the Tg-MYOCN450Y (MYOCN450Y) transgenic mouse model of glaucoma, which recapitulates impaired aqueous humor outflow and elevated IOP. First, we examined the ocular distribution of FITC-labeled mSiO₂ (mSiO₂-FITC) following topical administration. As shown in Fig. 4A, fluorescence signals were observed to move from the ocular surface into the anterior segment over time. While fluorescence intensity on the corneal surface gradually decreased, signals became detectable in the trabecular meshwork (TM) and Schlemm’s canal regions, suggesting delivery of the fluorescently labeled nanoparticles to these tissues. To further characterize ocular tissue exposure, the distribution of 4-PBA following topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). At 2 h post-administration, 4-PBA was detected in the aqueous humor (40.30 ng/mL), TM (22.54 ng/g), and retina (19.11 ng/g). At 6 h, measurable levels remained detectable in all three tissues, with concentrations of 7.08 ng/mL in aqueous humor, 9.78 ng/g in TM, and 6.58 ng/g in retina (Fig. 4B-C). These findings demonstrate that topically administered mSiO₂-PBA can penetrate ocular tissues and achieve exposure within the TM following topical administration. Taken together, the fluorescence imaging and HPLC-MS/MS analyses support the feasibility of delivering mSiO₂-PBA to anterior segment tissues, including the TM, after topical administration.

IOP reduction and alleviation of ER stress following topical mSiO₂-PBA treatment in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of mSiO₂-PBA, we utilized the Tg-MYOCN450Y transgenic mouse model of glaucoma, which recapitulates elevated IOP and TM dysfunction associated with mutant MYOC-induced ER stress.

First, slit-lamp examination revealed no observable anterior segment abnormalities following topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA. Ocular morphology appeared comparable among WT mice, untreated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, and mSiO₂-PBA-treated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice (Fig. 5A), supporting the ocular tolerability of the formulation. Next, we assessed the IOP-lowering effect of mSiO₂-PBA in 9-month-old Tg-MYOCN450Y mice. As shown in Fig. 5B, untreated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice exhibited significantly elevated IOP relative to WT controls throughout the study period. Topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA significantly reduced IOP in Tg-MYOCN450Y mice beginning at day 9, and the reduction was maintained throughout the 28-day treatment period.

Given that mutant MYOC accumulation is known to induce ER stress in TM cells [11, 15, 23], we next investigated whether mSiO₂-PBA affected this pathological process. Immunofluorescence staining of iridocorneal angle sections was performed using antibodies against MYOC and KDEL-positive ER chaperones. Compared with WT mice, untreated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice exhibited MYOC accumulation and increased KDEL immunoreactivity within the TM (Fig. 5C). Following topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA, both MYOC accumulation and KDEL immunoreactivity were visibly reduced in the TM of Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, suggesting attenuation of mutant MYOC-associated ER stress.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further supported these observations at the ultrastructural level. TM cells from WT mice displayed normal ER morphology without apparent dilation. In contrast, TM cells from untreated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice exhibited pronounced ER enlargement and dilation, consistent with ER stress. Following mSiO₂-PBA treatment, ER dilation was reduced and ER morphology appeared improved relative to untreated Tg-MYOCN450Y mice (Fig. 5D).

Collectively, these findings indicate that topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA lowers IOP, reduces mutant MYOC accumulation, and alleviates ER stress-associated pathological changes in the trabecular meshwork of Tg-MYOCN450Y mice.

Discussion

This study developed a novel mesoporous silica nanoparticle-based delivery system (mSiO₂-PBA) for the treatment of MYOC‑associated glaucoma. The nanoplatform demonstrated favorable biocompatibility and cellular uptake, promoted mutant MYOC secretion, and reduced ER stress-associated KDEL-positive proteins in vitro. In the Tg-MYOCN450Y mouse model, topical administration of mSiO₂-PBA reduced IOP, decreased mutant MYOC accumulation, and alleviated ER stress-associated pathological changes in TM tissue, supporting its potential as a nanotherapeutic strategy for MYOC-associated glaucoma.

Effective glaucoma management requires sustained IOP control; however, the bioavailability of conventional topical ophthalmic formulations is often limited by multiple ocular barriers and rapid precorneal clearance. As a result, only a small fraction of administered drug reaches intraocular tissues, frequently necessitating repeated administration. These challenges have motivated the development of nanoparticle-based delivery systems capable of improving ocular drug delivery. In the present study, a hollow/snowflake-like mesoporous silica nanoplatform was developed to facilitate ocular delivery of 4-PBA. The large internal cavity provides substantial drug-loading capacity, whereas the mesoporous shell enables controlled drug release. In addition, surface hydroxyl groups may promote interactions with the ocular surface, potentially enhancing residence time following topical administration. Consistent with these design features, fluorescence imaging and HPLC-MS/MS analyses demonstrated that topically administered mSiO₂-PBA was capable of reaching anterior segment tissues, including the TM. Furthermore, mSiO₂-PBA promoted mutant MYOC secretion and reduced ER stress-associated protein expression in MYOCN450Y-overexpressing HTMCs. Similar therapeutic effects were observed in Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, in which mSiO₂-PBA reduced IOP and alleviated ER stress-associated pathological alterations in TM tissue. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of using mesoporous silica nanoparticles as a delivery platform for 4-PBA in MYOC-associated glaucoma.

Currently, the therapeutic strategies in MYOC-mutant glaucoma include but are not limited to knockout of the mutant MYOC gene by CRISPR Cas9 technology [24], inhibition of GRP94 [25] and pharmacological restoration of mutant MYOC secretion using 4-PBA [11, 16]. In this study, mSiO₂-PBA was designed to address the underlying ER stress associated with mutant MYOC accumulation while simultaneously improving ocular delivery of 4-PBA. Our findings demonstrate that mSiO₂-PBA promotes mutant MYOC secretion, reduces ER stress-associated changes, and lowers IOP in a MYOC-associated glaucoma model, supporting its therapeutic potential.

Compared with existing strategies, mSiO₂-PBA offers several potential advantages. Although CRISPR-Cas9-based gene therapy represents a promising strategy, concerns regarding off-target effects, delivery efficiency, and long-term safety remain. In contrast, mSiO₂-PBA provides a pharmacological approach that does not require permanent genome modification. Similarly, while GRP94 inhibition may reduce mutant MYOC aggregation, interference with molecular chaperone networks may have unintended biological consequences. By incorporating 4-PBA into a mesoporous silica nanoplatform, mSiO₂-PBA provides a means of delivering a clinically relevant chemical chaperone to ocular tissues. Previous studies have demonstrated that free 4-PBA can restore mutant MYOC secretion and alleviate ER stress [15, 16]; however, direct comparisons between free 4-PBA and mSiO₂-PBA were not included in the current study. Therefore, whether the nanocarrier provides additional therapeutic benefits beyond free 4-PBA remains to be determined in future studies. Nevertheless, the favorable biocompatibility, drug-loading capacity, and ocular delivery characteristics of mesoporous silica nanoparticles make them attractive candidates for further development as ocular drug-delivery systems.

Recent advances in nanomedicine have highlighted a variety of emerging nanocarrier platforms for drug delivery in confined biological environments, including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and nanoflower-like architectures. These systems have demonstrated significant potential due to their unique structural and functional properties. For instance, MOFs provide highly ordered porous networks and tunable metal-ligand coordination environments, enabling exceptionally high drug loading and potential stimuli-responsive release behavior [26, 27]. COFs, composed entirely of covalent linkages, offer excellent chemical stability and programmable pore architectures that can be tailored for selective host-guest interactions and controlled release applications. In addition, nanoflower-like structures with hierarchical morphologies provide large surface areas and abundant interaction sites, which may enhance cellular uptake and local drug accumulation [28, 29]. Compared with these emerging systems, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (mSiO₂) used in this study offer several practical advantages, including well-established biocompatibility, robust physicochemical stability, and straightforward silane-based surface functionalization. The hollow-mesoporous architecture further provides both high drug-loading capacity and sustained release behavior while maintaining excellent colloidal stability. However, unlike some MOF-or COF-based systems, the present silica platform does not inherently possess stimuli-responsiveness or externally controllable navigation capability, which may limit its performance in highly complex in vivo environments. Therefore, further integration with smart or hybrid delivery systems may further enhance therapeutic precision and efficacy.

Importantly, beyond material optimization, system-level engineering may further improve targeted ocular delivery. Recently, magnetic microrobotic systems have emerged as an attractive strategy for actively navigating complex biological environments under external magnetic field guidance. By embedding mSiO₂-PBA nanoparticles within such microrobots, it may be possible to overcome the limitations of passive diffusion and achieve highly localized drug delivery to specific ocular tissues. This approach has demonstrated promising potential for improving spatial precision and reducing off-target exposure in challenging anatomical environments [30]. In this context, mSiO₂-PBA could serve as a modular drug reservoir compatible with microrobotic platforms, representing a future direction for precision ophthalmic therapy. In addition, further physicochemical characterization will be essential to support future translation of such advanced delivery systems. Systematic UV–Vis spectrophotometry can be employed for accurate quantification of drug loading and release kinetics, while zeta potential measurements can provide critical insights into colloidal stability and surface charge behavior under physiological conditions. These parameters will be important for optimizing in vivo performance and ensuring reproducibility in future studies.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the pharmacokinetic profile of mSiO₂-PBA was incompletely characterized, as ocular distribution was assessed only at 2 and 6 h post-administration. These data confirm early ocular exposure but do not define long-term retention. Future studies including later time points (e.g., 12, 24, and 48 h) are needed to fully establish ocular pharmacokinetics. Second, although FITC labeling enabled visualization of ocular distribution, fluorescence imaging may be influenced by potential signal dissociation or redistribution. Thus, these results should be interpreted alongside HPLC-MS/MS data. Additional validation using free FITC controls and co-localization with trabecular meshwork specific markers would further strengthen localization accuracy. Third, while mSiO₂-PBA effectively reduced IOP and alleviated ER stress in the trabecular meshwork of Tg-MYOCN450Y mice, retinal safety and visual function were not comprehensively evaluated. Although only trace retinal distribution was observed and no gross ocular abnormalities were detected, detailed assessments including OCT, ERG, histology, and retinal cell–based assays are required to confirm long-term retinal safety. ARPE-19 and EOC13.31 cells have been widely used for retinal biocompatibility assessment [31]. Future studies combining these models with in vivo structural and functional analyses are needed to evaluate the long-term retinal safety of mSiO₂-PBA. Finally, the mechanistic investigation of ER stress alleviation remains limited. Although KDEL-positive ER chaperones, MYOC accumulation, and ER ultrastructural changes consistently support attenuation of ER stress, classical unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling pathways, including PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/CHOP and IRE1α/XBP1s, were not systematically assessed. Further studies are needed to fully delineate the involvement of these canonical pathways.

Conclusions: mSiO₂-PBA represents a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for MYOC-associated glaucoma by providing controlled drug release characteristics, facilitating ocular exposure of 4-PBA, promoting mutant MYOC secretion, and attenuating ER stress in vitro and in vivo.
