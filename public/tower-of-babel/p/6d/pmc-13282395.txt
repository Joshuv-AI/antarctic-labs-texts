The role of temporomandibular joint mobilization in the management of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dilara Güler, Ayça Araci, Hüseyin Günizi, Murat Kar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common peripheral vestibular disorder and is characterized by brief episodes of vertigo triggered by changes in head position. Although canalith repositioning maneuvers effectively treat the underlying mechanical pathology, cranio-cervical musculoskeletal factors may influence dizziness perception and postural control. Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction has been associated with vestibular symptoms, including dizziness, imbalance, and tinnit us. This study evaluated the effectiveness of TMJ and soft tissue mobilization as an adjunct to standard treatment in individuals with BPPV. In this parallel-group randomized controlled trial, 42 adults diagnosed with BPPV were randomly allocated using simple randomization to an intervention group or a control group. One participant was excluded due to non-adherence, and 41 participants completed the study (intervention n = 20; control n = 21; mean age 53.9 ± 9.8 years). The intervention group received TMJ and soft tissue mobilization once weekly for four weeks in addition to canalith repositioning maneuvers, while the control group received standard treatment alone. The primary outcome was dizziness-related disability assessed using the Dizziness Disability Inventory. Additional outcomes included assessments of temporomandibular function and postural balance. Independent t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, paired t-tests, and Wilcoxon tests were applied. Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in dizziness-related outcomes (p < 0.001). However, no significant between-group differences were observed for dizziness measures. The intervention group showed significantly greater improvement in temporomandibular function (p < 0.001). In addition, balance performance during tandem stance with eyes open improved significantly more in the intervention group compared with the control group (mean difference 3.90 s; 95% CI 1.70–6.10; p = 0.001). Introduction

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common peripheral vestibular disorder and represents a major cause of dizziness-related consultations in clinical practice. It is characterized by recurrent, brief episodes of vertigo triggered by changes in head position relative to gravity, such as lying down, turning in bed, looking upward, or bending forward1 Epidemiological studies indicate that BPPV accounts for approximately 25% of all vertigo cases and nearly 60% of peripheral vestibular disorders2 The lifetime prevalence of BPPV in the general population has been estimated to range between 2.4% and 10%, with a higher incidence observed among older adults and females3.

The pathophysiology of BPPV is primarily explained by the displacement of otoconia calcium carbonate crystals originating from the utricle into one or more of the semicircular canals. These displaced particles disturb the normal flow of endolymph during head movements and induce abnormal stimulation of vestibular receptors, resulting in vertigo and positional nystagmus2,3. Canalith repositioning maneuvers, particularly the Epley maneuver, are widely accepted as the gold-standard treatment for posterior canal BPPV, with high short-term success rates reported across clinical studies4. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients continue to experience residual symptoms, including dizziness, imbalance, and cervical discomfort, even after successful repositioning procedures5. These persistent symptoms are thought to be associated with altered cervical proprioception, muscular tension, and impaired sensorimotor integration among vestibular, visual, and somatosensory systems.

Recent research has increasingly emphasized the interaction between cranio-cervical structures, the stomatognathic system, and postural control mechanisms. From a myofascial chain perspective, postural misalignment and cranio-cervical dysfunction may influence the biomechanical function and positioning of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), potentially altering head and neck alignment and the proprioceptive feedback mechanisms involved in balance regulation6. The TMJ is a diarthrodial synovial joint formed by the articulation between the mandibular condyle and the mandibular fossa of the temporal bone and is capable of both hinge and translational movements that are essential for mastication and speech7.

Increasing evidence indicates that the temporomandibular system has complex neuroanatomical and functional connections with vestibular and autonomic regulatory pathways. The trigeminal nerve, which innervates the temporomandibular joint and the masticatory muscles, has direct projections to the vestibular nuclei and other brainstem structures involved in spatial orientation and postural regulation8. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that trigeminal afferents can modulate vestibular neuronal activity through trigemino-vestibular reflex pathways, suggesting a functional interaction between the stomatognathic and vestibular systems9. In addition, trigeminal sensory inputs interact with autonomic regulatory centers within the brainstem, including the nucleus tractus solitarius, which integrates vestibular, visceral, and autonomic responses associated with dizziness and spatial disorientation6.

Consistent with these neurophysiological mechanisms, several clinical studies have reported associations between temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and otologic symptoms such as dizziness, tinnitus, ear fullness, and imbalance10–13.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis further demonstrated a high co-occurrence between temporomandibular disorders and tinnitus, with approximately half of tinnitus patients presenting signs of TMD, suggesting a significant clinical interaction between the auditory and temporomandibular systems13. These findings support the hypothesis that dysfunction within the temporomandibular system may influence vestibular perception and postural stability through both biomechanical and neurophysiological pathways.

Despite growing recognition of these interactions, the clinical implications of temporomandibular interventions in individuals with vestibular disorders remain insufficiently explored. In particular, evidence regarding the potential therapeutic role of temporomandibular manual therapy as an adjunctive intervention in patients with BPPV is limited. Most current treatment approaches primarily target the mechanical repositioning of otoliths and rarely address possible cranio-cervical or stomatognathic contributions to persistent vestibular symptoms.

Therefore, the present randomized controlled study aimed to investigate the effects of temporomandibular joint mobilization and soft tissue techniques on dizziness, anxiety, tinnitus, balance performance, and temporomandibular joint function in individuals diagnosed with benign paroxysmal positional vertigo. The primary outcome of the study was dizziness-related disability, while balance performance and temporomandibular joint parameters were considered secondary outcomes.

Based on the proposed trigeminal–vestibular interactions and the potential influence of the stomatognathic system on postural regulation, we hypothesized that temporomandibular joint mobilization combined with standard BPPV management would provide additional benefits in dizziness-related disability, balance performance, and temporomandibular joint function compared with standard treatment alone.

Results

A total of 42 patients diagnosed with benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) were randomized to the study group (n = 21) or the control group (n = 21). One participant in the study group was excluded from the final analysis due to non-adherence to the intervention protocol (missing two consecutive sessions). Consequently, 41 participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis (study group: n = 20; control group: n = 21) (Fig. 1).

No serious adverse events or treatment-related complications were observed during the intervention period. Participants were monitored throughout the study for potential adverse effects, including increased dizziness, headache, temporomandibular discomfort, or musculoskeletal pain. No participant required modification or discontinuation of the intervention due to adverse effects. Importantly, no adverse events related to TMJ mobilization or manual therapy procedures were reported.

Discussion

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common peripheral vestibular disorder and is characterized by brief episodes of vertigo triggered by changes in head position. The condition is primarily attributed to the displacement of otoconia from the utricle into the semicircular canals, resulting in abnormal endolymphatic stimulation during positional changes2,3. Canalith repositioning maneuvers are therefore considered the first-line treatment for BPPV and have consistently demonstrated high effectiveness in resolving vertigo symptoms. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience residual dizziness, imbalance, or postural instability even after successful repositioning maneuvers, suggesting that factors beyond the mechanical pathology of the vestibular system may contribute to persistent symptoms and functional recovery14.

The present study investigated whether temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and soft-tissue mobilization, applied in addition to conventional BPPV management, could provide therapeutic benefits in individuals diagnosed with BPPV.

The primary finding of this study is that although both groups demonstrated substantial and clinically meaningful improvements in dizziness-related symptoms, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for the primary outcome. This indicates that the observed improvements were largely attributable to conventional BPPV management rather than the additional temporomandibular intervention.

In contrast, the intervention group demonstrated greater improvements in temporomandibular function and selected balance parameters; however, these effects were not consistently observed across all outcomes and should therefore be interpreted as domain-specific rather than indicative of a generalized treatment effect.

The significant reduction in dizziness-related symptoms observed in both groups is consistent with the well-established effectiveness of canalith repositioning maneuvers in treating BPPV. These maneuvers aim to restore normal vestibular function by relocating displaced otoconia from the semicircular canals back into the utricle, thereby eliminating abnormal vestibular stimulation. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated rapid symptom resolution following repositioning maneuvers and have reported high treatment success rates2,5. In agreement with this evidence, both groups in the present study demonstrated substantial improvements in dizziness-related disability and symptom severity following treatment.

Despite these improvements, previous research has shown that residual dizziness or balance disturbances may persist even after successful repositioning maneuvers. Residual symptoms have been reported in approximately 20–30% of patients and may be associated with incomplete vestibular compensation, psychological factors such as anxiety, or disturbances in sensorimotor integration processes14. These findings support the growing perspective that dizziness perception and postural control may involve complex interactions between vestibular, somatosensory, and musculoskeletal systems rather than representing purely peripheral vestibular dysfunction.

One notable finding of the present study was greater improvement in balance performance in the intervention group, particularly during the tandem stance test with eyes open. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution regarding its clinical significance. Although a statistically significant between-group difference was identified for this specific parameter, no consistent between-group differences were observed across other balance measures, including tandem stance with eyes closed and semi-tandem conditions.

These results suggest that the observed improvements in balance performance may reflect task-specific or parameter-specific adaptations, rather than a generalized enhancement of postural control. It is therefore possible that the intervention influenced certain aspects of sensorimotor control, particularly under less demanding or visually supported conditions, without producing a robust and global improvement in balance function15–17.

From a neurophysiological perspective, temporomandibular and cranio-cervical interventions may modulate proprioceptive input and sensorimotor integration processes, which could partially explain the observed improvements in selected balance parameters. However, given the lack of consistent findings across outcomes, these mechanisms should be interpreted as hypothetical and require further confirmation. Taken together, while the findings indicate a potential contribution of temporomandibular interventions to balance performance, they should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of clinically meaningful improvement18,19.

Manual therapy interventions targeting the temporomandibular and cranio-cervical regions may influence these sensorimotor pathways by modulating proprioceptive input and neuromuscular coordination. Recent evidence suggests that interventions addressing cervical dysfunction, such as manual therapy and sensorimotor training, may reduce dizziness symptoms and improve balance performance by enhancing cervical proprioception and vestibular integration20–23. Similarly, studies examining cranial osteopathic techniques have demonstrated positive effects on vertigo symptoms and functional outcomes in individuals with vestibular disorders24. Although the precise mechanisms underlying these improvements remain incompletely understood, these findings suggest that musculoskeletal interventions targeting cranio-cervical structures may contribute to vestibular rehabilitation by influencing sensorimotor integration.

Another important finding of the present study was the significant improvement in temporomandibular joint function observed in the intervention group. Participants receiving TMJ-targeted therapy demonstrated marked improvements in Fonseca Anamnestic Index scores, mouth opening, and mandibular mobility parameters compared with the control group. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that manual therapy and mobilization techniques can effectively improve temporomandibular mobility and reduce symptoms associated with temporomandibular dysfunction25.

Anxiety and tinnitus outcomes, included among the study objectives, did not show significant between-group differences despite improvements in both groups. This finding may be explained by the strong therapeutic effects of conventional BPPV management and vestibular rehabilitation, which are known to reduce symptom burden and improve overall well-being, including psychological distress associated with dizziness26.

The absence of significant between-group differences in these domains suggests that temporomandibular interventions may have limited additional impact on anxiety and tinnitus outcomes, particularly in the context of effective vestibular treatment.

Anxiety is a common comorbidity in patients with vestibular disorders and is closely linked to dizziness perception and functional disability. The significant within-group reductions observed in both groups suggest that improvements in vestibular symptoms may have contributed to decreased anxiety levels, regardless of the addition of temporomandibular interventions.

Similarly, tinnitus-related outcomes did not show significant changes between groups, which may reflect the multifactorial nature of tinnitus and its complex neurophysiological mechanisms. Although temporomandibular dysfunction has been associated with tinnitus in previous studies, the short intervention duration and the absence of targeted tinnitus-specific treatment strategies in the present study may have limited the potential for measurable changes in tinnitus severity.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while temporomandibular interventions may influence certain somatosensory and functional domains, their additional contribution to anxiety and tinnitus outcomes remains unclear and may be secondary to the overall improvement in vestibular symptoms. Further studies specifically designed to target these domains are warranted.

The absence of statistically significant between-group differences in most dizziness-related scales should be interpreted in light of the highly effective nature of repositioning maneuvers for treating BPPV. Because both groups received identical vestibular treatment, substantial symptom resolution was expected. Under such circumstances, detecting additional improvements beyond those produced by repositioning maneuvers alone may be challenging, particularly in studies with relatively small sample sizes.

Some outcomes demonstrated relatively small effect sizes and wide confidence intervals, suggesting variability in individual responses and limited statistical precision. These findings further support the possibility that the study was underpowered to detect subtle between-group differences.

Therefore, although the intervention appears to provide benefits in specific domains, particularly temporomandibular function and selected balance parameters, the magnitude and consistency of these effects remain uncertain and should be interpreted with caution. Larger randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these findings.

In conclusion, both groups demonstrated significant improvements in dizziness-related symptoms following conventional BPPV management. However, no significant between-group differences were observed for most dizziness-related outcomes, suggesting that the addition of temporomandibular interventions may not provide substantial additional benefit over standard care for the primary outcome.

Limitations Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, the relatively modest sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle between-group differences in certain outcomes. Although the sample size was determined using a priori power analysis, the possibility of a type II error, particularly for secondary outcomes, cannot be entirely excluded.

Second, due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and the treating physiotherapist was not feasible, which may have introduced a risk of performance bias. However, to mitigate this risk, outcome assessor and data analyst blinding were rigorously implemented and maintained throughout the study, thereby minimizing potential bias in detection and analysis.

Third, balance performance was assessed using clinical postural tests rather than objective instrumental methods. While tests such as the Romberg and tandem stance provide clinically meaningful and functionally relevant information, more sensitive tools, such as computerized dynamic posturography, could offer a more detailed evaluation of sensorimotor integration processes.

Fourth, temporomandibular dysfunction was assessed using the Fonseca Anamnestic Index, which provides a global evaluation of symptom severity. However, more detailed characterization of pain, including intensity, duration, and chronicity, was not separately analyzed, which may limit the interpretation of pain-related outcomes.

Fifth, the follow-up period was restricted to the short-term intervention phase. Therefore, the long-term sustainability and persistence of the observed improvements remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Sixth, the trial was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The study was initially conducted within the context of a master’s thesis, and prospective trial registration was not mandated at the time of study initiation. Nevertheless, all study procedures, including randomization and allocation concealment, were predefined and implemented prospectively. The retrospective registration should therefore be interpreted as a reporting limitation rather than a methodological flaw, although it remains an important consideration when evaluating the study’s transparency.

Seventh, the absence of a sham manual therapy intervention in the control group should be acknowledged. Participants in the intervention group received additional therapist contact, which may have introduced non-specific treatment effects, such as placebo responses or increased therapeutic interaction. Future randomized controlled trials incorporating sham-controlled designs are needed to better isolate the specific effects of temporomandibular interventions.

In addition, adherence to the home-based exercise program was monitored through self-report during follow-up calls, which may be subject to reporting bias and overestimation of compliance. Therefore, the potential influence of variations in exercise adherence on the observed outcomes cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, the study was conducted in a single clinical center with a limited number of eligible participants, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. In addition, adherence to the home-based exercise program was monitored through self-report, which may be subject to reporting bias and overestimation of compliance.

The addition of temporomandibular joint and soft tissue mobilization was associated with greater improvements in specific outcomes, particularly temporomandibular function and selected balance parameters. However, given the lack of consistent findings across all balance measures and the potential influence of exercise adherence and non-specific treatment effects, these improvements should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, the findings suggest that temporomandibular interventions may provide complementary and context-specific benefits rather than producing generalized improvements in vestibular function or postural control.

Further well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, objective adherence monitoring, and sham-controlled designs are needed to confirm the clinical relevance and underlying mechanisms of these effects.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions Despite these limitations, the findings offer clinically relevant insights into the potential contribution of the temporomandibular system to vestibular rehabilitation. The results point to a possible interaction between cranio-cervical musculoskeletal structures and postural control mechanisms in individuals with BPPV. From a clinical standpoint, temporomandibular interventions may provide complementary benefits in selected cases, particularly in patients who continue to experience postural instability despite standard BPPV management. However, these approaches should not be considered a primary strategy for improving overall balance, but rather as part of a broader, multimodal rehabilitation approach.

Further research with larger samples, extended follow-up periods, and the use of objective posturographic measures is needed to better understand the clinical relevance of these findings. It remains unclear whether the observed changes reflect meaningful improvements in balance or more task-specific adaptations. Incorporating standardized minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for balance outcomes would also enhance the clinical interpretability of future studies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as a parallel-group randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio, incorporating assessor and data analyst blinding, and designed within a superiority framework. The aim was to evaluate the effectiveness of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) mobilization as an adjunct to standard management in patients with benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). The study was carried out at the Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) outpatient clinic of Alanya Alaaddin Keykubat University Training and Research Hospital (Antalya, Türkiye) between June and September 2022.

An experienced otorhinolaryngologist evaluated patients presenting with dizziness, and BPPV was diagnosed based on established clinical criteria. Outcome measures were collected at baseline (Pre-T) and at the end of the 4-week intervention period (Post-T).

Following baseline assessment, participants were randomly allocated to either the study group (SG) or the control group (CG). All participants received standard BPPV management, including diagnostic evaluation and canalith repositioning maneuvers, under the supervision of an otorhinolaryngologist. In addition, a home-based vestibular rehabilitation program was prescribed to all participants, to be performed twice daily for four weeks, with exercise instructions delivered via WhatsApp. Participants in the SG also received TMJ mobilization and soft-tissue manual therapy, administered by a licensed physiotherapist and osteopath with over 20 years of clinical experience.

A total of 42 participants were enrolled and randomized. One participant from the study group discontinued due to non-adherence to the intervention protocol, leaving 41 participants for the final analysis (SG: n = 20; CG: n = 21).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Alanya Alaaddin Keykubat University, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Blinding was maintained throughout the study; both the outcome assessor and the data analyst remained unaware of group allocation. The assessor did not have access to allocation information at any stage, and participants were instructed not to disclose their assigned intervention during assessments. No instances of unblinding were reported.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was conducted based on effect sizes reported in previous studies evaluating manual therapy interventions for temporomandibular disorders. Randomized controlled trials in this area have generally reported large treatment effects on symptom severity and functional outcomes25,28,29, with one study indicating an effect size of d = 1.33 28. To provide a more conservative estimate, an effect size of Cohen’s d = 1.0 was assumed for the present study.

Based on this assumption, a minimum total sample of 34 participants (17 per group) was required to achieve 80% statistical power at a 5% significance level. To account for potential attrition, the sample size was increased by approximately 15%. Accordingly, 42 participants were recruited and randomized into 2 groups (21 per group).

During the study, one participant in the study group discontinued due to non-adherence to the intervention protocol. The final analysis, therefore, included 41 participants (SG: n = 20; CG: n = 21), which remained above the minimum required sample size to ensure adequate statistical power.

Randomization and Allocation Concealment: Following eligibility screening and written informed consent, baseline assessments were completed before group assignment. Participants were then randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the study or control group. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher who was not involved in recruitment, assessment, or intervention procedures. To ensure allocation concealment, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes were prepared in advance. These envelopes were identical and securely sealed, and were opened in sequence only after baseline assessments had been completed and the participant had been formally enrolled. The researcher responsible for enrollment did not have access to the allocation sequence at any point. No blocking or stratification methods were used, and randomization was performed using a simple randomization approach.

Blinding: Due to the nature of the intervention, it was not possible to blind participants or the treating physiotherapist. All interventions were delivered by a single licensed physiotherapist who was aware of group allocation. Outcome assessments, however, were performed by an independent evaluator who was not involved in the intervention and remained blinded to group assignment. The assessor had no access to the randomization sequence or allocation information at any stage. Participants were instructed not to disclose their assigned group during assessments, and evaluation sessions were conducted in a standardized manner without discussion of treatment. Blinding of the outcome assessor was preserved throughout the study, with no instances of unblinding reported. In addition, data analysis was carried out using anonymized group labels (e.g., Group A and Group B), ensuring that the statistician remained blinded to allocation throughout the analysis.

Diagnosis and standard BPPV management

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), classified according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10 code: H81.1), was diagnosed by an experienced otorhinolaryngologist using the Dix–Hallpike maneuver. This positional test was performed to provoke characteristic vertigo and positional nystagmus, enabling identification of the affected ear and confirmation of posterior semicircular canal involvement. The Dix–Hallpike maneuver is considered the gold standard clinical test for diagnosing posterior canal BPPV and for guiding treatment selection9. Only patients with posterior canal BPPV were included in the present study.

Patients with a positive Dix–Hallpike test underwent canalith repositioning treatment using the Epley maneuver. The maneuver was performed on the affected side to reposition displaced otoliths within the posterior semicircular canal. Following the initial maneuver, patients were scheduled for follow-up evaluation three days later. If the Dix–Hallpike test remained positive, the Epley maneuver was repeated. Patients were subsequently re-evaluated 1 week later, and the repositioning maneuver was repeated as needed. This procedure was continued at weekly intervals until the Dix–Hallpike test result became negative30. The number of repositioning maneuvers required until symptom resolution and a negative Dix–Hallpike result was documented for each participant.

Pharmacological treatment was not routinely administered, and canalith repositioning maneuvers constituted the primary treatment approach. However, in patients presenting with severe symptoms such as persistent vertigo, nausea, or vomiting, symptomatic pharmacological therapy was prescribed by the same otorhinolaryngologist when clinically indicated. In such cases, betahistine dihydrochloride (24 mg twice daily) and other vestibular suppressants (e.g., antihistamines or antiemetics) were administered as supportive therapy.

The prescription of pharmacological therapy was determined solely by the otorhinolaryngologist, based on clinical need and independent of group allocation, ensuring that pharmacological management was comparable across the study groups.

Interventions: To ensure appropriate clinical management and preserve ecological validity, both groups received standard treatment for benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). The study was designed to investigate the additional effect of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) mobilization when combined with conventional BPPV care. All interventions were delivered using a standardized protocol to maintain consistency across participants.

Study Group (SG): In addition to standard BPPV management and home-based exercises, participants in the SG received TMJ mobilization and soft-tissue manual therapy. These interventions were administered by a licensed physiotherapist experienced in musculoskeletal and vestibular rehabilitation. Treatment sessions lasted approximately 30 min and were conducted once weekly over four consecutive weeks.

The manual therapy approach included post-isometric relaxation (PIR) techniques targeting the masseter, temporalis, and pterygoid muscles, followed by gentle TMJ mobilization. During PIR, participants were comfortably positioned in the supine position, and submaximal isometric contractions (approximately 20–30% of maximal effort) were performed against light resistance for 7–10 s. This was followed by relaxation and passive stretching of the involved muscles, typically repeated three to five times for each muscle group. Low-grade TMJ mobilization techniques were then applied within the physiological range to improve mandibular mobility and reduce joint stiffness, without provoking pain.

A detailed description of the manual therapy protocol, including target structures, sequence of techniques, treatment intensity, and duration, is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Control Group (CG): Participants in the CG received standard BPPV management and the same home-based exercise program, but did not receive any manual therapy.

Home exercise program

Home Exercise Program: All participants were asked to perform a standardized vestibular rehabilitation program twice daily for a period of four weeks. The exercises focused on adaptation training to stabilize retinal images during horizontal and vertical head movements while maintaining visual fixation on a stationary target. Before the intervention, all exercises were demonstrated in person, and participants were provided with instructional videos via mobile messaging (WhatsApp) to support correct execution at home.

Adherence to the program was monitored through weekly follow-up phone calls, during which participants reported the frequency and regularity of their exercise practice. Participants were also encouraged to maintain consistent engagement with the program throughout the study period. However, adherence was based on self-report and was not objectively verified.

A detailed description of the vestibular rehabilitation protocol is provided in Supplementary Appendix 2 to allow replication of the intervention.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome of the study was defined as the change in Dizziness Disability Inventory (DDI) scores. Secondary outcomes included dizziness-related symptom measures (VAS, VSS, and VDI). Temporomandibular joint function parameters (FAI, mouth opening, mandibular deviation, and protrusion) and balance assessments (Romberg test) were considered exploratory outcomes.

Measures of dizziness, balance, tinnitus, and quality of life in BPPV

Dizziness Disability Inventory (DDI): The Dizziness Disability Inventory (DDI) is designed to assess the sensory and functional impairments associated with vestibular disorders and to identify factors that exacerbate dizziness and balance dysfunction. The scale comprises 25 items, grouped into three domains: physical, sensory, and functional. Each item is scored as follows: “Yes” = 4 points, “Sometimes” = 2 points, “No” = 0 points. A score of 28 or higher indicates a physical disability, while scores of 36 or more in the functional or sensory domains indicate respective impairments31.

Dizziness Rating – Visual Analog Scale (VAS): The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is used to measure the subjective intensity of dizziness. Patients are asked to rate their dizziness on a 0–10 scale, where: 0 = No dizziness, 1–4 = Mild dizziness, 4–8 = Moderate dizziness, 10 = Unbearable dizziness32.

Vertigo Symptom Scale – Short Form (VSS): The Vertigo Symptom Scale – Short Form (VSS) is a 15-item questionnaire designed to evaluate dizziness, imbalance, autonomic disturbances, and anxiety symptoms experienced over the past month. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), yielding a total score of 0–60. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. A total score of 12 or more is considered indicative of clinically significant vertigo symptoms33. The scale consists of two subdomains: (1) Vertigo-related symptoms: 8 items (maximum score: 32), (2) Autonomic-related symptoms: 7 items (maximum score: 28).

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI): The Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) is a widely used self-report measure designed to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms and their impact on quality of life. It consists of 21 items, each rated on a 4-point scale based on how much the symptom has bothered the individual in the past week. Total scores are interpreted as follows:8–15 points: Mild anxiety, 16–25 points: Moderate anxiety, 26–63 points: Severe anxiety34.

Vertigo-Dizziness-Imbalance Questionnaire (VDI): The Vertigo-Dizziness-Imbalance Questionnaire (VDI) was used to evaluate the impact of vertigo and dizziness on patients’ quality of life, particularly in the context of daily living activities. The questionnaire consists of 36 items divided into two subscales: Symptom Subscale (14 items; maximum score: 70) and Quality of Life Subscale (22 items; maximum score: 110). The maximum total score is 180, with higher scores indicating fewer symptoms and a better quality of life35.

Tinnitus Disability Scale (TDS): The Tinnitus Disability Scale (TDS) is used to evaluate the severity of tinnitus and its impact on daily living activities. It assesses various domains, including mental, social, occupational, and physical functioning, as well as emotional responses such as nervousness, perceived control over tinnitus, and the degree to which tinnitus interferes with daily life. The scale consists of 25 items, each offering three response options:” Yes” = 4 points, “Sometimes” = 2 points, “No” = 0 points. The minimum total score is 0, and the maximum is 100. Higher scores reflect greater disability. Tinnitus severity is categorized as follows9,36: 0–16: Very mild (Grade 1) – Heard only in quiet environments, 18–36: Mild (Grade 2) – Easily masked by environmental noise, 38–56: Moderate (Grade 3) – Audible over background noise but does not interfere with daily activities, 58–78: Severe (Grade 4) – Almost always audible; interferes with daily activities, 78–100: Very severe (Grade 5) – Constantly audible; disrupts sleep and significantly affects quality of life.

Assessment of Balance in BPPV Using Postural Tests Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is frequently associated with dizziness and impaired balance. Therefore, postural assessments such as the Romberg test, tandem stance, and semi-tandem stance are used as objective tools to evaluate vestibular function and postural control mechanisms. These tests collectively assess the integration of vestibular, somatosensory, and motor systems responsible for maintaining balance. Specifically, the Romberg test is primarily utilized to detect vestibular-related balance impairments. The tandem and semi-tandem stance tests are designed to assess both static and dynamic postural control. Inability to maintain balance during these tests in patients with BPPV is considered an indicator of increased vestibular dysfunction severity37.

Romberg Posture Test The Romberg test assesses the integration of sensory, visual, and vestibular inputs to evaluate postural control mechanisms. During the procedure, participants stand upright with their feet shoulder-width apart, and arms relaxed at their sides38.

To ensure safety, all participants were asked to wear hard-soled, flat, and thin-soled shoes. Patients were then instructed to close their eyes and maintain balance without external support. Appropriate safety precautions were implemented throughout the procedure to minimize the risk of falls. In individuals with BPPV, a postural deviation or imbalance toward the affected vestibular system is commonly observed.

Tandem Posture Test The Tandem Posture Test was used to evaluate dynamic balance and the vestibular system’s contribution to postural control. During the test, the participant stood upright with one foot placed heel-to-toe in front of the other, and both arms extended forward and parallel to the floor. Participants were instructed to close their eyes and maintain this position for 30 s. The duration for which the balance was sustained was recorded in seconds. In patients with BPPV, this duration is typically shorter than that observed in healthy individuals, due to increased postural instability37.

Semi-Tandem Posture Test A less challenging variant of the tandem test, designed to detect milder vestibular dysfunction symptoms. In this test, the participant assumed an upright stance with one foot placed slightly ahead and adjacent to the other, and arms extended forward and parallel to the ground. Participants were asked to close their eyes and maintain this position for 30 s, during which the time spent balancing was recorded. This test is particularly useful in elderly populations and individuals with vestibular impairment, as it facilitates the early detection of postural instability37.

Temporomandibular joint evaluation

Fonseca’s Anamnestic Index (FAI): The Fonseca’s Anamnestic Index (FAI) is used to screen for temporomandibular disorders (TMD) by evaluating symptoms such as pain in the TMJ and back region, pain during mastication, movement limitations, joint sounds, and stress-related responses. The FAI consists of 10 questions, each offering three response options: “Yes” = 10 points, “Sometimes” = 5 points, “No” = 0 points. Total scores are interpreted as follows39: “0–15”: No signs or symptoms of TMD, “20–45”: Mild TMD, “50–65”: Moderate TMD, “70–100”: Severe TMD.

Measurement of Maximum Mouth Opening Maximum mouth opening was assessed using a standard ruler by measuring the horizontal distance between the upper and lower central incisors. Patients were instructed to protrude the lower jaw as far forward as possible while maintaining posterior tooth contact40,41.

Maximum mouth opening, protrusion, and mandibular deviation were measured using a millimeter ruler and recorded in centimeters (cm)40,41.

Assessment of Mandibular Protrusion and Lateral Deviation To assess right- and left-lateral movements, patients were first asked to close their mouths with the posterior teeth in contact, and the alignment of the upper and lower incisors was examined. If a deviation was observed, its direction and magnitude were recorded. In cases where a slip occurred, and the direction of slip matched the evaluated side, the value was subtracted from the total movement. If the deviation was toward the opposite side, it was added to the measured value40,41.

Evaluation of TMJ Movement Quality: The qualitative assessment of TMJ movement involved palpation of the meatus acusticus externus while the patient opened and closed their mouth. This examination focused on detecting: Joint movement quality, Deflection (left/right), Gliding patterns, and Joint sounds such as clicking or crepitation. A stethoscope was used to assess acoustic signs during mandibular movement42.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Given that one participant withdrew after randomization and no follow-up outcome data were available, statistical analyses were performed using a per-protocol approach, including participants who completed the study.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms.

Between-group comparisons for primary and secondary outcomes were performed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. Within-group changes were analyzed using paired samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. The choice of statistical test for change scores (Δ values) was based on the distribution characteristics of the respective variables.

Categorical variables, including adverse events, were analyzed using the chi-square test. No formal statistical comparison of harms was performed because no adverse events occurred.

Baseline demographic variables, including age and gender, were compared between groups to assess group homogeneity and potential confounding effects.

The primary outcome of the study was defined as the change in Dizziness Disability Inventory (DDI) scores. Secondary outcomes included dizziness-related symptom measures (VAS, VSS, and VDI). Temporomandibular joint function parameters (FAI, mouth opening, mandibular deviation, and protrusion) and balance assessments (Romberg, tandem, and semi-tandem tests) were considered exploratory outcomes. In addition to within-group analyses, between-group comparisons of change scores (Δ values) were performed to evaluate treatment-related differences between the study and control groups.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the level of significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of between-group differences, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for key outcome measures.
