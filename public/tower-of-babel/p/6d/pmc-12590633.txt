Genome-wide identification of the MADS-box gene family and their role in the formation of faba bean (Vicia faba L.) growth habits
Authors: Xianli Zhou, Changcai Teng, Zhihao Sun, Wanwei Hou, Hongyan Zhang, Tao Yang, Dong Zheng, Xiaoxing Peng, Shubao Lou, Rong Liu, Yujiao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe MADS-box gene family is important in florescence development and flower organ formation. Members can directly or indirectly influence leguminous crops growth habits. However, this gene family in faba bean (Vicia faba L.) and its role in the development of various growth habits have not been systematically reported.ResultsWe identify 82 VfMADS genes in faba bean. Phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, conserved motifs, and expression patterns in varieties with different growth habits are investigated. Of them, 41 VfMADS proteins are classified as Type II and are attributed to 11 subfamilies. We report 12 pairs of tandem duplication events in VfMADS genes that involve 26 genes. Cis-acting elements in promoter regions suggest that these genes are involved mainly with hormone and stress responses. A new type of semi-determinate growth habit is reported for faba bean and transcriptome sequencing reveals 56 MADS-box genes to be differentially expressed in accessions with 3 types of growth habits. Significant differences are reported for expression levels of 10 VfMADS genes in different growth habits and tissues, indicating that VfMADS genes are closely related to floral transition, especially the floral termination at the shoot apex.ConclusionsWe systematically identify MADS-box genes in faba bean and investigate their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal distribution, conserved motifs, cis-acting elements and expression patterns in faba bean with different growth habits. By doing so, we improve understanding of the regulatory network of growth habits and provide a theoretical basis for future studies on the functional characteristics of VfMADS genes in faba bean.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07563-x.Keywords: Vicia faba, MADS-box gene family, Genome-wide identification, Growth habits, Gene expression

Background

Faba bean (Vicia faba L.), the third most important cool-season food legume crop globally, has high yield potential [1]. Its wide adaptability, value as a restorative crop in rotations and high nutritional density have led to it being cultivated worldwide on all continents except Antarctica [2, 3]. The growth habits of faba bean determine plant architecture, which influences traits related to mechanical harvesting such as flowering, pod distribution, lodging resistance, and maturity. Growth habit, therefore, is an important morphological and agronomic trait in breeding [4–6].

Growth habit can be determinate, indeterminate, or semi-determinate, with the latter resembling the indeterminate type, with terminal inflorescence of the determinate type [7, 8]. The semi-determinate type germplasm resource is further characterized by concentrated pod setting, effective suppression of apical dominance, and high yield potential, making it highly valuable for variety improvement. Because semi-determinacy is a counterpart of the “Green Revolution” trait in cereals that significantly increased grain yields [9–11], it is important to understand the molecular basis and regulatory mechanism of growth habit.

The fundamental difference between indeterminate, semi-determinate, and determinate growth habits lies in the transition of shoot apical meristem (SAM) from vegetative growth to reproductive growth [12–14]. In soybean (Glycine max), stem growth habit was regulated by two critical loci, designated Determinacy 1 (Dt1) and Determinacy 2 (Dt2). Dt1 is an ortholog of Arabidopsis TERMINAL FLOWER1 (TFL1) and specifies an indeterminate stem growth habit, whereas Dt2 specifies a semi-determinate growth habit [6]. MADS-box proteins, including Dt2, SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CONSTANS1 (SOC1) and members downregulated by E1, facilitate the transition of SAM from vegetative to reproductive growth by suppressing the expression of Dt1, thereby influencing the formation of stem growth habits [15, 16]. MADS-box genes play important roles in inflorescence development and flower organ formation [17, 18]. However, their role and expression patterns in faba bean during formation of various growth habits are unknown.

The MADS-box transcription factor (TF) family is characterized by having a conserved domain of approximately 58–60 amino acids in the N-terminal region. It is divided into Types I and Ⅱ [19, 20]. Type I MADS-box genes can be further classified into three groups (Mα, Mβ, Mγ), mainly consisting of M domains, encoded by a DNA sequence approximately 180 bp in length [21, 22]. Type II is mainly composed of M, I, K, and C domains, which together form the MIKC structure, divided into Mδ and MIKC types [23]. Whole-genome sequencing for the common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), grass pea (Lathyrus sativus), and pigeonpea (Cajanus cajan) has revealed 79, 46, and 102 MADS-box members, respectively [24–26].

MADS-box genes are involved in multiple developmental processes in plants, especially the initiation of flowering, architecture of inflorescence, determination of flower organ identity, seed development, and stress responses [27, 28]. Pollen-specific MIKC*-type MADS TFs AGAMOUS-LIKE 30 (AGL30), AGL65, AGL66, AGL94, and AGL104 activate JINGUBANG (JGB) transcription, thereby influencing the degree of pollen dormancy in Arabidopsis [29]. Overexpression of soybean GmMADS66 also induced early flowering in A. thaliana under both long- and short-day conditions [30].

Most known MADS-box genes are of Type Ⅱ, particularly floral homologs from the “ABCDE model” [31]. Many TFs controlling SAM and flower development constitute an “ABCDE model”, with most of them being MADS-box genes that regulate floral development [32]. SOC1 and SVP are involved in the dynamic regulation of gibberellin biosynthesis and catabolism during the transition from the apical to floral meristem in A. thaliana [33]. SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP) and AGL24 can redundantly dimerize with AP1 to recruit the LUGSEU co-repressor complex, thereby repressing class E (SEPALLATA3 (SEP3)), B (PISTILLATA (PI) and APETALA3 (AP3)), and C (AG) members to prevent precocious floral meristem differentiation [34].

Systematic identification and characterization of TF families in faba bean at the genomic level have not been performed. We identify 82 VfMADS genes in the V. faba genome, and analyze their gene structures, physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal distribution, conservative motifs, and cis-acting elements. We also report the expression patterns and expression levels of VfMADS genes in various growth habits and tissues. These results provide a theoretical foundation for clarifying VfMADS genes functions and facilitate ideotype breeding in faba bean.

Results

Identification of MADS-box genes in faba bean

A total of 82 MADS-box genes were identified from the genome of faba bean (Hedin/2) [2] by searching profiles of MADS-box (PF00319) and K-box (PF01486) domain profiles using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM); we designate these VfMADS1–VfMADS82 based on chromosomal location. Gene ID, chromosomal location, physical and chemical properties, and predicted subcellular localization are detailed in Table S1. The longest protein was VfMADS30 (849 amino acids), and the shortest, VfMADS39 (107 amino acids). The molecular weight (MW) of the VfMADS proteins ranged 19.59–96.11 kDa (average 26.96 kDa). For MADS proteins, 75.61% had an isoelectric point (pI) ≥ 7, indicating that the majority encoded by the VfMADS genes were rich in basic amino acids. The instability index ranged 31.08 (VfMADS79) to 84.16 (VfMADS39), with 82.93% of them classified as “unstable”. Except for VfMADS79, hydrophobicity analysis revealed all VfMADS proteins to be hydrophilic. Of 82 VfMADS, subcellular localization prediction analysis (WoLF PSORT) revealed 57 to be located in the nucleus, 9 in the cytoplasm, 7 in mitochondria, 4 in the chloroplast, 3 in the plasma membrane, and 1 each in the golgi and chloroplast_mitochondria.

Classification and phylogenetic analysis of VfMADS proteins

To clarify evolutionary relationships among MADS-box genes in faba bean and A. thaliana, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MAGE7 software based on the 82 VfMADS protein sequences and 102 identified AtMADS (Fig. 1). With reference to the classification of MADS-box genes in A. thaliana, all genes from faba bean were divided into Type Ⅰ (41) and Type Ⅱ (41). The Type Ⅰ subfamily can be further classified into Mα, Mβ and Mγ (23, 2 and 16 genes, respectively). The Type Ⅱ subfamily can be subdivided into two subclades (Mδ and MIKC), with 4 and 37 members, respectively. MICK VfMADS proteins were distributed in 11 subfamilies, with 1 to 9 genes in each. The conserved domain of VfMADS protein was aligned using MAGE7 and GeneDoc software. In MICK and Mδ subfamilies, the MADS domain of most genes was more conserved; the Mα subfamily was less conserved, indicating that it evolved faster and was more prone to mutation (Fig. 2).

Gene structure and conserved domains analysis of VfMADS genes

A phylogenetic tree consisting of 82 VfMADS genes divided all genes into two subgroups, with the main clades largely consistent with subgroup division (Fig. 3A), each gene followed by their exon-intron structural map, conserved motifs, and conserved domains based on the faba bean genome sequence (Fig. 3). The structure of 82 VfMADS genes varied (Fig. 3B). Structures of VfMADS genes belonging to the same subgroup were similar, and members of Type I differed from those of Type II, suggesting that genes within the same branch probably had similar functions. Numbers of exons ranged from 1 to 11, among which 3 genes (VfMADS11, 30, 40) had the most (11) exons, and 31 genes had only one exon and no intron. Excepting VfMADS30, all Type I genes had 1–3 exons; all genes without introns were Type I. Most Type II genes (35 of 41) had 7–11 exons and more complex gene structures.

To better understand protein structures of VfMADS genes, we analyzed conserved motifs and domains through MEME website and Batch CD-Search. Ten conserved motifs (1–10) were identified (Fig. 3C); the sequence logo of each is depicted in Figure S1. Except for VfMADS51, 36, and 21, all MADS-box proteins had motif 1 at the N-terminal. Motif 1 represents the typical MADS-box domain (Fig. 3C, D). In total, 34 Type II VfMADS proteins had the K-box domain, encoded by motif 2. Motifs 2 and 7 are unique structural domains in Type II VfMADS proteins, possibly explaining why they have more biological functions than Type I MADS-box genes. Motifs 4, 6 and 10 occurred only in the Mα subfamily; motifs 8 and 9 were specific to the Mβ and Mγ subfamilies.

VfMADS gene family chromosome location and gene duplication events

The location of VfMADS genes on chromosomes was mapped using the faba bean reference genome. The 82 VfMADS genes are unevenly distributed on six chromosomes and one contig (Fig. 4A); genes within a subfamily are randomly distributed across chromosomes. Among them, chr2 had the most VfMADS genes (25 genes, 30.49%), followed by chromosome 1 (17 genes), 4 (15 genes), 3 (12 genes), 6 (7 genes), and 5 (5 genes).

Gene duplication events play important roles in biological evolution, the formation of new functions, and adaptive evolution. Therefore, duplication events in the VfMADS genes were analyzed using MCScanX software. Twelve tandem duplication events involving 26 VfMADSs were identified on chromosomes 1–4. Among them, four genes had two tandem duplicate events (VfMADS25 and VfMADS24/VfMADS26, VfMADS38 and VfMADS37/VfMADS39, VfMADS59 and VfMADS58/VfMADS60, VfMADS60 and VfMADS59/VfMADS61). All genes that underwent tandem duplication belonged to the same subfamily. For example, VfMADS24, 25, and 26 belong to the Mγ subfamily, and VfMADS56 and VfMADS57 belong to the PISTILLATA (PI) subfamily. Among the 26 tandem duplicated genes, 16 are Type I and 8 are Type II, with 6 genes in SOC subfamily. To explore how VfMADS genes expanded, a circos map was constructed. Fourteen homologous members and nine pairs of segmental duplication events were identified (Fig. 4B). Among these genes, VfMADS16 and VfMADS62 were involved in multiple duplication events. The proliferation of repeat elements largely explains why the faba bean genome is larger than that of the related common vetch (Vicia sativa).

To explore evolutionary relationships between faba bean and other species, collinear maps of faba bean with three representative species (A. thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa) and grape (Vitis vinifera)) and three legume crops (G. max, pea (Pisum sativum) and barrelclover (Medicago truncatula)) were constructed (Fig. 5). There were 27, 7, 36, 86, 52, and 41 pairs of collinear MADS-box genes between faba bean and these species, respectively, involving 19, 6, 26, 34, 40, and 35 VfMADS genes. Except for rice, the collinear gene pairs of the other five plants were randomly distributed on the six faba bean chromosomes. Genes on rice chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 7 were collinear with VfMADS genes on chromosomes 3, 4, 6 (Fig. 5E). MADS-box genes of faba bean have a high homology with those of pea and barrelclover.

Analysis of cis-acting elements in promoter of VfMADS genes

To explore the transcriptional regulatory mechanism of the VfMADS genes, the cis-acting elements of each VfMADS gene were predicted using the PlantCare service within the 2000 bp sequence upstream of the promoter region. We extracted 18 major functional cis-acting elements, comprising 765 components, and classified into four categories: light responsive elements, hormone responsive elements, environmental stress-related elements and development-related elements (Fig. 6). Hormone responsive elements were the most abundant cis-acting elements in the promoter region, with 392 components (51.24%), including responsive elements for salicylic acid (SA) (TCA element, 48 components), Methyl jasmonate (MeJA) (TGACG-motif and CGTCA-motif, 150 components), gibberellin (GA) (P-box, TATC-box and GARE-motif, 45 components), auxin (TGA-element and AuxRR-core, 37 components) and abscisic acid (ABA) (ABRE, 112 components). The second largest category was environmental stress-related elements; development-related elements were third (75/765, 9.80%), including differentiation of the palisade mesophyll cells, regulation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, seed-specific regulation, zein metabolism regulation, meristem expression, circadian control, and cell cycle regulation. Remaining light responsive elements (category four) were mostly Type I VfMADS genes. Analysis of cis-acting elements reveals that VfMADS genes can respond to growth and development, hormones, and stresses.

VfMADS protein-protein interaction network

To understand faba bean MADS-box gene regulatory mechanisms, orthologous genes from A. thaliana were used to predict the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of the VfMADS genes (Fig. 7). 7 genes among the 12 interacting VfMADSs were interacted with VfMADS7 and VfMADS68. Both VfMADS7 and VfMADS68 belonged to AP1 subfamily, and VfMADS7 was the orthologs AP1 of Arabidopsis. In soybean, Dt2, an ortholog of AP1/SQUA from A. thaliana, suppresses Dt1 expression, leading to a semi-determinate phenotype [16, 35].

Analysis of VfMADS gene expression patterns by growth habits based on RNA-seq data

The MADS-box gene family plays an important role in the induction of floral transition, especially in SAM transition from vegetative to reproductive growth, thereby influencing the formation of growth habits. To explore the potential roles of VfMADS genes in faba bean of different growth habits, we analyzed the expression pattern of VfMADS genes in shoot tips using transcriptome data at various developmental stages. Sampling periods for the transcriptome were determined by the SAMs of different growth habits ceased to differentiate.

Of the 82 VfMADS genes, 56 exhibited distinct expression patterns at various stages for different growth habits; 26 Type I MADS-box genes were not detected (Fig. 8). Type II MADS-box genes may be more involved in formation of faba bean growth habits. 23 Type Ⅱ genes (e.g.,VfMADS3, 14, 16, 40, 57, 58, 67, 72) showed high expression in the shoot tips during formation of inflorescence meristems and terminal flowers in the three types of growth habits. Six genes from VfMADS6 to VfMADS70 were highly expressed only at the 9-leaf stage in Qingcan17 (indeterminate growth habit), while VfMADS11, 42, and 76 showed relatively highly expression at the 7-leaf stage. VfMADS71 was highly expressed only at the 4-leaf stage in RF22 (semi-determinate growth habit), and VfMADS74 was highly expressed at the 5-leaf stage. Most VfMADS genes had relatively low expression at the 3- and 4-leaf stages in Qingcan16 (determinate growth habit), while VfMADS4 was highly expressed only at the 3-leaf stage.

Sample names are formatted “variety_sampling stage.” The color bar represents normalized values (Z-score) of fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM); colors represent relative expression levels: high (red), low (blue)

The majority of Type II VfMADS genes exhibited different expression patterns at various stages in different growth habits; a small number of Type I VfMADS genes were highly expressed in the shoot tip at specific stages. This suggests that Type I and Type II MADS-box genes interact to regulate growth habits formation.

Expression pattern of VfMADS genes in different tissues

Using available transcriptome data for faba bean, we analyzed the expression pattern of VfMADS genes in five tissues. While 18 genes belonging to the Mα subfamily were not expressed in any of these tissues, the expression of other genes was tissue-specific. Most members had relatively low expression in leaf, root, and pod tissues (Fig. 9). Expression levels of genes in the SOC subfamily (e.g., VfMADS46, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, and 61) in leaf tissues were significantly up-regulated. In 25-day-old pod (pod_25d) tissues, MIKC subfamily members VfMADS3, 14, 16, 81, 65, and 68 all exhibited high expression levels. In roots, VfMADS17, 28, 42, 65, 78, and 79 were highly expressed, of which only VfMADS28 belonged to the Type I subfamily.

Expression patterns of VfMADS genes in flower, leaf, pod, root, and seed tissues. The color bar represents normalized values (Z-score) of FPKM; different colors represent relative expression levels: high (red), low (blue)

During early seed development (seed_15d), the expression of many VfMADS genes was significantly up-regulated. With continued seed development, expression levels of VfMADS genes gradually declined. In seed_35d, relative expression levels of all VfMADS members were down-regulated. More Type II VfMADS genes (e.g., VfMADS6, 7, 13, 41, 62, 64, 80, especially in AP1 and AP3 subgroups) were expressed in clusters in flower tissues, with 8 VfMADS genes (VfMADS11, 15, 20, 21, 29, 55, 66, 77) being highly expressed.

Expression levels of 10 type Ⅱ VfMADS genes at different developmental stages

To analyze expression levels of VfMADS genes in shoot tip tissues for different growth habits, ten genes were chosen from the ten Type II subfamilies for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). These genes were highly homologous with known A. thaliana genes and could fully represent the diversity of the VfMADS family. Three type of growth habit varieties were chosen: Qingcan17 (indeterminate), Qingcan16 (determinate), and RF22 (semi-determinate). Because the determinate inflorescence phenotype is caused by development and differentiation of the SAM, we selected shoot tip tissues.

The majority of VfMADS genes were more highly expressed in RF22 than in Qingcan17 and Qingcan16 (e.g., VfMADS5, VfMADS33, VfMADS41). VfMADS33 (PI subgroup) was extremely highly expressed. VfMADS46, VfMADS74, and VfMADS78 in RF22 were also more highly expressed than those in Qingcan17 and Qingcan16. This suggests that these genes might be closely related to the semi-determinate growth habit of faba bean. Although determinate and semi-determinate growth habit varieties both generate terminal inflorescence, these MADS-box family genes may interact with each other and participate in regulation of growth and development, maintaining meristem activity, and thereby influencing the formation of individual architecture.

Expression levels of VfMADS2, VfMADS5, VfMADS6, VfMADS33, VfMADS41, VfMADS66, VfMADS69, and VfMADS78 were significantly higher when the determinate SAM ceased differentiating (from leaf stages 4 or 5) compared with the 3-leaf stage. When the semi-determinate SAM stopped differentiating (from leaf stages 8 or 9), VfMADS5, VfMADS33, VfMADS41, VfMADS46, and VfMADS66 were also most highly expressed in shoot tips. High expression levels of these genes may prompt the SAM to gradually cease differentiating, resulting in the plant entering a reproductive growth stage.

During the later developmental stage of Qingcan17, expression levels of VfMADS2, VfMADS33, VfMADS66, and VfMADS69 increased. There were no significant differences in expression levels of VfMADS6, VfMADS46, or VfMADS78 at different developmental stages (Fig. 10).

Discussion

The growth habits of faba bean significantly influence plant architecture. A semi-determinate growth habit is characterized by a compact plant structure and high yield potential, and plays an important role in improving plant architecture and production efficiency. The formation of different growth habits is attributed to the transition of the SAM from vegetative to reproductive growth. The MADS-box gene family plays a major role in flower initiation, inflorescence structure, and the determination of floral organ identity [36, 37]. The MADS-box TF family has been widely characterized in plants [38–40].

We identified 82 MADS-box genes based on the Hedin/2 reference genome of faba bean. Numbers of MADS-box genes in faba bean are similar to those in rice (75) and common bean (79), but lower than in soybean (106) and A. thaliana (107) [24, 41–43], possibly because of genome or gene duplication [44]. There were significant differences in amino acid length (107–849) among the 82 VfMADS proteins. The instability index ranged 31.08 (VfMADS79) to 84.16 (VfMADS39), of which 82.93% were classified as “unstable”. The pI of VfMADS proteins ranged 4.98–9.97 (mostly > 7), indicating that this gene family tends to be enriched in basic amino acids. The predicted subcellular localization revealed the majority of VfMADSs (57) to be localized in the nucleus, indicating that the VfMADS gene family plays a dominant role mainly within the nucleus. Based on the classification of A. thaliana MADS-box genes, the 82 VfMADS genes were divided into Type I (41) and Type II (41), and subdivided into Type 1 (Mα (23), Mβ (2), Mγ (16)) and Type II (Mδ (4), and MIKC (37)). In rice and A. thaliana, the Mδ subfamily was classified into Type II, while these genes were treated as Type I subgroup in wheat (Triticum aestivum) and linseed (Linum usitatissimum) [41, 43, 45, 46]. The number of type I and II MADS-box genes in faba bean is the same, unlike A. thaliana and rice in which type I MADS-box genes predominate [41, 43].

Most Type I genes contained only 1 or 2 exons, and gene structure was simpler than for most Type II genes (with 7–11 exons). This is similar to the MADS-box gene structures of wheat [43] and litchi (Litchi chinensis) [44], indicating that the MADS family is relatively conserved. Variations in gene number and intron patterns indicate lineage-specific evolution and potential functional diversification [21]. Almost all MADS-box proteins had motif 1 at the N-terminal, which represents the typical MADS-box domain; 37 Type II VfMADS proteins had the K-box domain, encoded by motif 2, consistent with findings in other studies [24]. Strangely, VfMADS30 (Type I subfamily) contained two typical MADS-box domains, and its gene structure was relatively complex; we speculate that this was caused by gene assembly error.

Duplicated genes are generated through evolutionary mechanisms such as genome-wide duplication, homologous recombination, or transposon events. These mechanisms lead to additional gene copies in the genome and represent a major cause of genome expansion [47, 48]. We report 12 tandem duplication events involving 26 VfMADS genes and 9 pairs of segmental duplication events involving 14 homologous members, indicating that many tandem duplication events occurred in the faba bean genome. This proliferation of repeat elements may explain why the faba bean genome is more than seven times larger than that of the related common vetch [2, 49]. The study of synteny provides further evidence of evolutionary relationships and gene conservation among legumes [50]. Synteny analysis revealed 7–86 pairs of collinear MADS-box genes between faba bean and six other species, indicating that the VfMADS genes of faba bean were highly conserved. Promoter cis-acting elements are specific binding sites for proteins involved in initiating and regulating transcription [51]. In the promoter region of the VfMADS genes, we found many cis-acting elements that respond to plant hormones and stress, with the MeJA responsiveness and ABA responsiveness being more widely and broadly distributed, suggesting that the VfMADS genes can be involved in plant growth, development, and hormonal signaling pathways.

Gene function can be predicted by expression pattern. We report 56 of 82 VfMADS genes to exhibit distinct expression patterns at various stages in different varieties and growth habits. Some Type Ⅱ MADS-box genes identified in faba bean were differentially expressed within the three growth habit types. Most members showed relatively low expression in leaf, root, and pod tissues, but more Type II VfMADS genes (e.g., VfMADS6, 7, 13, 41, 62, 64, 80, especially in AP1 and AP3 subgroups) were expressed in clusters in flower tissues.

The functions and molecular mechanisms of members of the MADS-box gene family in soybean (e.g., SVP, SOC1, FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC), FRUITFULL (FUL), AP1, and SEP) have been described, providing a reference for predicting VfMADS gene functions in faba bean. PPI analysis indicated that 7 genes among the 12 interacting VfMADS members were interacted with VfMADS7 and VfMADS68 (AP1 subfamily), and VfMADS7 was the orthologs AP1 of Arabidopsis. We report VfMADS7 and VfMADS68 to be highly expression in the shoot tips during the formation of inflorescence meristems and terminal flowers in the three types of growth habits. In soybean, Dt2, an ortholog of AP1/SQUA from Arabidopsis, suppresses Dt1 expression, leading to a semi-determinate phenotype [18, 35]. VfMADS7 and VfMADS68 may also be involved in the formation of growth habits in faba bean. VfMADS2 (AP1 subfamily) and VfMADS16 (AG homologues) showed highly expression at the shoot apex. Members of the AG and AP1 genes in soybean [42], A. thaliana [43], are primarily expressed in flowers, fruits, and floral buds. Interactions between the AG protein and three SEP proteins (SEP1–3) and AGL6 are related to the termination of floral organ identity in A. thaliana [37, 52]. We also report expression of VfMADS5 (SEP subfamily) to be significantly up-regulated in late-stage SAM development of both Qingcan16 (determinate growth habit) and RF22 (semi-determinate growth habit). VfMADS41, the homologous gene of AP3, is actively expressed in determinate and semi-determinate varieties, while AP3 affects structural changes in floral organs in A. thaliana [53]. These results indicate that the VfMADS genes are widely involved in the formation of various growth habits. However, limitations in the faba bean transformation system only allow us to predict the functions of key genes (no functional verification was performed). Future research could examine the functional mechanisms of these genes.

Conclusion

We identified 82 VfMADS genes in faba bean, of which 41 VfMADS proteins classified as Type II were attributed to 11 subfamilies. Twelve pairs of tandem duplication events in VfMADS genes involved 26 genes. Cis-acting elements in promoter regions suggest that these genes mainly respond to hormone and stress responses. Of these 82 VfMADS genes, transcriptome sequencing revealed 56 of them to be differentially expressed in accessions with different growth habits. Expression levels of ten VfMADS genes in three growth habits and tissues differed significantly, indicating that VfMADS genes are closely related to floral transition, especially the floral termination at the shoot apex. These results provide a theoretical basis for future studies on the functional characteristics of VfMADS genes in faba bean and improve our understanding of the regulatory network of growth habits.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of MADS-box genes in faba bean

The whole genome sequence of faba bean was downloaded from published genomic data (https://projects.au.dk/fabagenome/genomics-data). The HMM file corresponding to the MADS structure domain (PF00319) was obtained from the PFAM database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/search/phmmer), and VfMADS genes were predicted. SMART (http://smart.embl.de/smart/batch.pl) and CD-Search database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) were used to filter and correct the predicted VfMADS genes. From this, 82 VfMADS genes were obtained (VfMADS1–VfMADS82, named according to their chromosomal location). The physical and chemical properties of VfMADS proteins (coding length, number of amino acids, MW, pI, GRAVY, and subcellular localization) were calculated using online websites ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) and WoLF PSORT (https://psort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic analysis, gene structure, conserved motifs, and cis-acting elements

The TAIR website was used to download (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) the A. thaliana MADS protein sequence; a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA7 based on the principle of maximum likelihood principle (parameter: bootstrap 1000) [54, 55]. Multiple sequence comparison analysis of the faba bean and A. thaliana MADS-box families was performed using MEGA7 software based on ClustalW default parameter. A structural map of VfMADS genes was constructed from faba bean genomic data using TBtools software [56]. Conserved motifs of the VfMADS protein family were predicted using the online software MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the maximum conserved motif search value set to 10 [57]. Cis-acting elements in the VfMADS genes promoter sequence (upstream 2000 bp) were predicted at the PlantCare website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [58].

Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis of VfMADS genes

VfMADS genes were localized to the faba bean chromosome using Circos [59] of TBtools based on faba bean genomic data. Tandem and segmental duplication events were analyzed using the MCScanX [60] with default parameters; the Dual Synteny Plotter was used to analyze tandem duplication events and homology of the MADS-box genes in faba bean and six other species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, V. vinifera, G. max, P. sativum and M. truncatula) [56].

Plant materials and transcriptome analysis of the VfMADS genes

All faba bean varieties were obtained from Qinghai Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences (QAAFS). The faba bean cultivars “Qingcan 16” (determinate growth habit) and “Qingcan 17” (indeterminate growth habit) were bred by Yujiao Liu of QAAFS, and awarded a Certificate of Plant Variety Rights of China (Qingcan16, CNA20130685.3; Qingcan17, CNA20151082.8). “RF22” was a superior V. faba germplasm resource (semi-determinate growth habit) identified by Yujiao Liu (FBIS1PN-2008) (Fig. 11). These three varieties (of uniform size and pest- and disease free) were cultivated in an experimental field of the QAAFS (101.75°E, 36.73°N), Xining, Qinghai Province, China. Tissue samples collected from the 3–9-leaf stages were rapidly placed into liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃. Sampling periods were determined when SAMs of different growth habits ceased to differentiate.

Growth habits: (A) indeterminate (Qingcan17), (B) semi-determinate (RF22), and (C) determinate (Qingcan16)

To explore gene expression pathways underlying faba bean growth habits, transcriptome sequencing was performed using shoot tips of Qingcan17, Qingcan 16, and RF22 from the 3–5-leaf stages, and those of RF22 and Qingcan 17 from the 7–9-leaf stages, on an Illumina sequencing platform. FPKM was normalized by Z-score. Expression profiles of VfMADS genes were extracted from transcriptome data and visualized by heatmap using the NovoMagic Cloud platform. Raw data are available in SRA (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) (PRJNA1262842).

Raw data of faba bean RNA-seq datasets were used in this study: flower, leaf, pod_15d, pod_25d, pod_35d, root, seed_15d, seed_25d, and seed_35d (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) (PRJNA995224). RNA-seq data were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [61], compressed and sorted using SAMtools [62], and quantified by Htseq [63] and R4.3.2 [64]. Finally, R4.3.2 was used to draw a heatmap of normalized FPKM values (Z-score).

RNA extraction, reverse transcription and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. First-strand cDNA was generated from total RNA using FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (Tiangen). Based on phylogenetic relationships, we selected one VfMADS gene from each of 11 Type Ⅱ subfamilies for qRT-PCR. Transcriptome sequencing revealed expression profiles of these genes differed among varieties. Two primers for the VfMADS16 of the SEEDSTICK (STK) subfamily failed to amplify. Upstream and downstream primers were designed by NCBI Primer-blast (Table S4). RT-qPCR was performed using FastReal qPCR Premix kits (Tiangen). Real-time PCR was performed using a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio Rad). Each sample was repeated three times, using ELF1A as a reference gene. Samples of three varieties from 3–9-leaf stages were used to detect the specificity and stability of ELF1A. Melting curves indicated that ELF1A had single signal peaks, showing good specificity (Fig. 12). A standard deviation (0.89) and coefficient of variation (6.11%) calculated using the BestKeeper algorithm indicated good expression stability of ELF1A [65]. Technical replicates were averaged, relative expression was normalized to the actin gene (ELF1A) expression (as the internal control), and was calculated using the 2−(ΔΔCt) method [66].

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2010 and SPSS 26.0 were used for data entry and statistical analyses. Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. SPSS 26.0 was used to perform the analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests, and multiple comparison tests of ANOVA results were performed using the Duncan method at p < 0.05 (*) and p < 0.01 (**).
