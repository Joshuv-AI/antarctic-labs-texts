The impact of different exercise modes on prostate cancer: a Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Jie Liu, Qiang Li, Yu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignancies in men. Although standard treatments, including surgery, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), and radiotherapy, enhance survival, they frequently induce adverse effects such as muscle wasting, increased body fat mass, fatigue, and diminished quality of life. Exercise has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological intervention; however, the comparative efficacy of various exercise modalities remains inconclusive. A systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis were conducted to assess the effects of diverse exercise interventions on physical and quality-of-life outcomes in men with PCa. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase was conducted from inception through September 2025. Fifty-four randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 3,522 participants were included in the analysis. Primary outcomes included muscle strength, body fat mass, fatigue (FACIT-F), aerobic capacity (6-min walk test, 6WMT), and cancer-specific quality of life scores (EORTC QLQ-C30). Exercise interventions significantly improved muscle strength, body composition, fatigue, aerobic capacity, and quality of life compared to usual care. Resistance training (RT) may be the most beneficial intervention for enhancing muscle strength and aerobic capacity, aerobic training (AT) for reducing body fat, combined aerobic–resistance training (AT_RT) for alleviating fatigue, and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) for improving quality of life. All exercise modalities demonstrated good safety and feasibility, with no major adverse events reported. Exercise represents a safe and effective adjunctive therapy for prostate cancer rehabilitation. Incorporating individualized exercise prescriptions—specifically RT, AT, AT_RT, and HIIT—into clinical practice can optimize physical outcomes, mitigate treatment-related side effects, and enhance the quality of life among prostate cancer survivors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41076-3.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignant neoplasms affecting men worldwide1, with both its incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise2. This escalating trend imposes a significant burden on both patients’ families and the broader healthcare system, underscoring its status as a critical public health concern that jeopardizes the well-being of middle-aged and elderly men3. Clinically, radical prostatectomy, endocrine therapies (e.g., androgen deprivation therapy, ADT), and radiotherapy are considered the cornerstone treatment strategies4. Although these interventions effectively arrest tumor progression and prolong survival, they are often accompanied by adverse effects that substantially impair quality of life5. Specifically, various cancer treatments (e.g., ADT, radiotherapy, radical prostatectomy) are associated with distinct adverse effects: ADT is linked to increased body fat accumulation6, cancer related-fatigue is prevalent across treatment trajectories4, and muscle wasting, reduced muscle strength and diminished mobility may occur regardless of the treatment modality5. As such, optimizing the prevention and management of these treatment-related sequelae, alongside enhancing survival outcomes, has become a primary focus in both clinical practice and research in prostate cancer7–9.

In recent years, exercise has gained recognition as a safe, cost-effective, non-pharmacological intervention with significant utility in the rehabilitation of cancer patients9–11. Both preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that exercise enhances the physiological functioning of cancer survivors and mitigates treatment-related adverse effects, thereby substantially improving their overall quality of life12–14. However, research on exercise interventions for PCa patients remains highly diverse and heterogeneous. Exercise protocols vary considerably across studies, including aerobic training (AT) (e.g., brisk walking, jogging, cycling), resistance training (RT) (e.g., weightlifting, elastic band exercises), combined aerobic-resistance training (AT_RT), and mind-body practices such as Tai Chi and yoga15–20.

Different exercise modalities exert their therapeutic effects through distinct physiological mechanisms, resulting in observable variations in intervention efficacy21. Previous studies suggest that aerobic exercise is particularly effective in reducing body fat mass and improving cardiopulmonary function22,23, whereas resistance training confers notable benefits for inducing muscle hypertrophy and improving muscle strength24. Combined aerobic and resistance training may produce synergistic benefits across diverse outcome measures16. However, most existing studies have primarily focused on comparing a single exercise modality with standard care or performing direct head-to-head comparisons between different exercise types25,26. A paucity of systematic reviews comparing the efficacy of multiple exercise interventions limits the capacity to evaluate their relative effectiveness in improving key outcomes among PCa patients, including body fat mass, muscle strength, fatigue severity, aerobic capacity (assessed by the 6-min walk test, 6WMT), and quality of life. This knowledge gap further hinders the identification of the most effective exercise regimen. To address this gap, the present study employs a Bayesian network meta-analysis22 integrated with a systematic review to comprehensively evaluate the effects of different exercise modalities on key outcome indicators in PCa patient. The primary objectives are to assess the comparative efficacy of distinct exercise modes (integrating type, intensity, and structure) on physical outcomes (muscle strength, body fat, aerobic capacity etc.) and quality of life in PCa survivors; establish efficacy rankings for each outcome; and provide evidence-based, protocol-specific rehabilitation recommendations.

For our study, an “exercise mode” refers to a composite, clinically prescribable protocol, combining exercise type, intensity, and structure, rather than a single dimension like “aerobic” or “high-intensity”. This definition reflects real-world practice, Pca survivors require specific guidance on how to exercise, not just what type, and merging intensity/structure into broad types would limit transferable value. Thus, we compare distinct modes, including AT, RT, AT_RT, HIIT and MIT, to identify protocol-specific benefits.

Methods

Design and registration

Our study was conducted following the methodological standards outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses incorporating Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA NMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251171663.

Literature search

To identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating exercise interventions in prostate cancer survivors, a comprehensive literature search was performed across four electronic databases—PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase—from their inception to September 2025. The reference lists of relevant studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible RCTs that may not have been captured during the initial database search.

Relevant keywords were incorporated with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms to formulate the database search strategy, including “Prostatic Neoplasms”, “Prostat*”, “cancer*”, “tumor*”, “tumour*”, “neoplas*”, “malignan*”, “adenocarcinoma*”, “metasta*”, “Physical activity”, “Exercise”, “Sports”, “Exercise Therapy”, “exercise”, “aerobic*”, “resistance*”, “strength*”, “stretch*”, “training*”, “fitness*”, “walk*”, “endurance*”, “lifestyle*”, “sport*”, “isometric*”, “eccentric*”, “plyometric*”, “physical condition*”, “randomized controlled trial”, “randomized” and “placebo”. The search was limited to studies published in English. A detailed description of the search strategy is provided in Supplementary materials 1.

Two reviewers (J. Li and Y. Han) independently evaluated the full-text articles for eligibility, with any disagreements resolved through discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (Q. Li).

Data extraction

Independent data extraction was conducted by two reviewers (J. Liu and Y. Han), while the third author (Q. Li) resolved any existing discrepancies. The following data were extracted from each eligible study:1 study characteristics—first author, year of publication, and study location;2 participant characteristics—sample size, age, muscle strength, fat mass, fatigue, aerobic capacity (6-minute walk test, 6WMT), EORTC QLQ-C30 scores, and treatment stage;3 intervention details—type, frequency, intensity, and duration of the exercise/training; and4 outcome measures—post-intervention mean values ± standard deviation for both the experimental and control groups.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment of included studies was independently conducted by two reviewers (J. Liu and Y. Han), and any disagreements were achieved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (Q. Li). To evaluate the risk of bias in the included studies, the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (Rob 2.0) (assessing seven methodological domains) was employed, with the assessment process carried out through RevMan software (version 5.4.1).

Data synthesis

The NMA was performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and GeMTC software27. All statistical analyses were performed using a Bayesian random-effects model implemented with the “gemtc” and “rjags” packages in R28. The Bayesian framework applies Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation techniques to estimate posterior distributions and derive probability distributions for each treatment effect29–31. Model inconsistency was examined using both consistency and inconsistency models. Because closed loops were present in the evidence network, the node-splitting method was employed to assess local inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons32. Convergence was evaluated through the Brooks–Gelman–Rubin diagnostic and further verified by visually inspecting the trace and density plots. Effect sizes were estimated using a Bayesian random-effects model, expressed as mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Comparative outcomes for all interventions were visualized using forest plots and league tables. Statistical significance was established when the 95% credible interval (CrI) excluded the null value of zero. Probability distributions for treatment rankings were produced to assess relative efficacy, and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was computed to quantify the probability of each intervention being the most effective. SUCRA values span from 0% to 100%, where higher values represent a higher likelihood of an intervention being superior33,34. A network geometry plot was constructed to illustrate the relationships among the included exercise interventions, where nodes correspond to intervention types and edges represent direct comparisons between them. A covariate was considered to have a significant effect if the 95% CrI of the regression coefficient excluded zero. Contrast-adjusted funnel plots were generated to assess potential small-study effects as well as publication bias.

Results

Literature search

A total of 8,342 records were initially identified through the database search. After automated duplicate removal and manual screening of titles and abstracts, 199 full-text articles were reviewed to assess their eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 54 RCTs that met the inclusion criteria were included in the NMA. The reasons for exclusion of the remaining studies are presented in Fig. 1.

The specific exercise protocols implemented in each study are summarized in Table 1. These exercise interventions varied in duration between 8 and 96 weeks, and most of them lasted 12 or 24 weeks. The exercise modalities encompassed AT (e.g., walking or cycling), RT, AT_RT programs (e.g., strength training, football), endurance training, combined aerobic and flexibility regimens (e.g., Qigong), impact-loading with resistance training (IL_RT), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), and moderate-intensity training (MIT) programs. Each exercise session lasted 25 to 90 min, with training frequencies between 1 and 5 sessions weekly; in most studies, the interventions were carried out 2 to 3 times per week.

Risk of bias assessment

We provide the results of the risk of bias assessment for all included studies which could be found in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 of Supplementary materials 2. A total of 48 studies (88.9%) clearly reported the methods of random sequence generation, whereas 6 (11.1%) studies did not provide sufficient information. Allocation concealment was clearly described in 48 studies, while 7 did not report this information. Due to the inherent characteristics of exercise intervention trials, blinding of participants and personnel was generally not feasible. Missing participant data were reported in 12 studies, and only one study demonstrated selective outcome reporting. Of the 54 studies included, 51 (94.4%) explicitly reported the blinding methods employed for outcome assessment, whereas in three studies, the intervention assigned to participants were known to the outcome assessors. All included studies were rated as unclear regarding other potential sources of bias.

Network meta-analysis

Network analysis diagram

Comparative evidence among different exercise interventions is presented in the network diagram. The diagram features lines connecting two nodes, which represent direct comparisons between interventions; line thickness is proportional to the number of included studies. The size of each node corresponds to the total sample size of the respective intervention. Interventions that do not form closed loops indicate the absence of direct comparative evidence between those exercise modalities.

Goodness-of-fit of the models

Consistency and inconsistency models were applied to compare the deviance information criterion (DIC), and the corresponding results are presented in Supplementary Table 4 of Supplementary material 3. Differences in DIC values of less than 5, together with p-values greater than 0.05 from the node-splitting analysis, indicated good consistency of the Bayesian statistical model. These results suggest that consistency was achieved across the outcome indicators, including muscle strength, fat mass, FACIT-F, 6-minute walk test, and EORTC QLQ-C30. Accordingly, the consistency model was adopted for subsequent network meta-analysis procedures. The residual variance analysis demonstrated a good model fit for the network meta-analysis.

Effect of exercise interventions on muscle strength

In this review, 24 studies (44.4%) evaluated muscle strength outcomes17,23,35–56. Direct comparisons were made between the outcomes of all exercise types in the trials and those of control participants (Fig. 2A). As shown in the network diagram (Fig. 2B), HIIT, AT, RT, AT_RT, IL_RT, and UC formed a closed loop; therefore, a local inconsistency test was performed. The results revealed no significant differences between direct, indirect, and network comparisons for RT vs. AT, UC vs. AT, IL_RT vs. AT_RT, and UC vs. RT (Supplementary Fig. 3A of Supplementary material 4). Based on the league table, compared with UC, AT_RT [MD = 8.46, 95% CrI (1.86 to 15.58)] and RT [MD = 15.57, 95% CrI (9.63 to 22.59)] were the effective interventions for improving muscle strength (Supplementary Table 6 A of Supplementary material 5). Moreover, RT demonstrated greater efficacy than AT [MD = − 13.76, 95% CrI (− 26.31 to − 2.03)]. According to SUCRA rankings, RT ranked first (89.06%), HIIT second (59. 53%), and UC last (12.56%) (Figure. 2 C; Supplementary Table 7 of Supplementary material 6).

Effect of exercise interventions on fat mass

For body fat mass, a total of 25 studies (44.4%) were included6,17,18,22,24,37–39,41,45–47,52–54,56–65. In the trials, the outcomes of all exercise types were directly compared with the outcomes of control participants (Fig. 3A). The network diagram (Fig. 3B) indicated that no closed loops were formed with UC; therefore, a local inconsistency test was not applicable. According to the league table, compared with UC, AT [MD = − 2.58, 95% CrI (− 4.22 to − 0.95)], AT_RT [MD = − 1.41, 95% CrI (− 2.49 to − 0.34)], and RT [MD = − 2.53, 95% CrI (− 4.18 to − 0.88)] were effective in reducing body fat (Supplementary Table 6B of Supplementary material 5). No direct comparisons were available among exercise interventions other than UC. Based on SUCRA rankings, AT ranked first (75.66%), RT second (74.43%), and UC last (17.75%) (Figure. 3 C; Supplementary Table 7 of Supplementary material 6).

Effect of exercise interventions on fatigue

A total of 15 studies (27.8%) assessed fatigue outcomes16,23,45,46,50,66–75. The outcomes of every exercise type participating in the trials were directly evaluated against those of control participants (Fig. 4A). As shown in the network diagram (Fig. 4B), AT, RT, HIIT, and UC formed a closed loop; thus, a local inconsistency test was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 3B. of Supplementary material 4). The test results indicated no significant inconsistency among direct, indirect, and network comparisons for RT vs. AT, UC vs. AT, and UC vs. RT. Compared with UC, AT_RT [MD = 14.04, 95% CrI (3.14 to 24.23)] effectively alleviated fatigue in patients with prostate cancer (Supplementary Table 6 C of Supplementary material 5). Furthermore, there were no comparison between all of the various exercise interventions. Among all interventions, AT_RT achieved the highest SUCRA ranking (86.18%), followed by HIIT (53.81%), with UC ranking last (25.68%) (Figure. 4 C; Supplementary Table 7 of Supplementary material 6).

Effect of exercise interventions on aerobic capacity

A total of 11 studies (20.4%) evaluated aerobic capacity as an outcome measure22,23,42,43,62,64,68,69,72,76,77. In the trials, the outcomes of all exercise types were directly compared with the outcomes of control participants (Fig. 5A). The network diagram (Fig. 5B) indicated a closed loop formed by HIIT, AT, RT, and UC; therefore, a local inconsistency test was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 3C. of Supplementary material 4). No significant inconsistencies were detected between direct, indirect, or network comparisons for RT vs. AT, UC vs. AT, and UC vs. RT. Compared with UC, AT [MD = 47.78, 95% CrI (24.75 to 70.82)], AT_RT [MD = 34.66, 95% CrI (19.06 to 50.20)], HIIT [MD = 60.26, 95% CrI (21.02 to 99.47)], RT [MD = 70.94, 95% CrI (40.22 to 101.43)] significantly improved aerobic capacity (Supplementary Table 6D of Supplementary material 5). Furthermore, compared with MIT [MD = -83.3, 95% CrI (-133.71 to -33.19)] and AT_RT [MD = -36.26, 95% CrI (-70.42 to -1.89)], RT significantly escalated the aerobic capacity; HIIT [MD = 72.64, 95% CrI (16.8 to 128.82)], AT_RT [MD = 47.1, 95% CrI (4.23 to 89.79)] and AT [MD = 60.16, 95% CrI (14.34 to 106.37)] were superior to MIT (Supplementary Table 6D of Supplementary material 5). Based on SUCRA rankings, RT ranked first (82.05%), HIIT second (77.78%), and MIT last (5.97%) (Figure. 5 C; Supplementary Table 7 of Supplementary material 6).

Effect of exercise interventions on quality of life

A total of 16 studies (29.6%) assessed quality-of-life outcomes23,35,36,47,49,51,55,63,68–70,77–81. Direct comparisons were made between the outcomes of all exercise types in the trials and those of control participants (Fig. 6A). The network diagram (Fig. 6B) showed that AT_RT, IL_RT, RT, AT, and UC formed a closed loop; accordingly (Supplementary Fig. 3D of Supplementary material 4), a local inconsistency test was conducted. No significant inconsistencies were found between UC vs. AT and IL_RT vs. AT_RT. Compared with UC, AT [MD = − 17.05, 95% CrI (− 32.62 to − 1.51)] significantly improved quality of life (Supplementary Table 6 F of Supplementary material 5). Furthermore, the effects of AT were lower than those of RT [MD = − 15.46, 95% CrI (− 29.83 to − 1.2)], IL_RT [MD = − 19, 15% CrI (− 35.46 to − 2.87)], HIIT [MD = − 23.44, 95% CrI (− 41.17 to − 5.78)], and AT_RT [MD = − 17.65, 95% CrI (− 33.43 to − 1.87)]. According to SUCRA rankings, HIIT ranked first (91.26%), followed by IL_RT (70.60%), while AT ranked last (1.25%) (Figure. 6 C; Supplementary Table 7 of Supplementary material 6).

Publication bias

Funnel plots were used to evaluate potential publication bias in outcomes related to muscle strength, fat mass, fatigue, 6-minute walk test performance, and EORTC QLQ-C30 scores. The results suggested a relatively low risk of publication bias across these outcome measures (Supplementary Fig. 4 of Supplementary Material 8).

Discussion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis demonstrated that exercise interventions significantly improve body fat, muscle strength, fatigue, aerobic capacity, and cancer-specific quality of life among PCa patients. Identifying the most effective exercise modalities for optimizing these outcomes remains essential. Five principal findings were identified. First, RT may be the most effective intervention for improving muscle strength in PCa patients. Second, AT was the most beneficial intervention for reducing body fat. Third, AT_RT demonstrated the greatest potential for alleviating fatigue. Fourth, RT was the most beneficial intervention for enhancing aerobic capacity in the evidence network of this study. Finally, HIIT had the highest probability of being the optimal intervention (highest SUCRA value) for cancer-specific quality of life.

This analysis confirmed that RT consistently enhances muscle strength in PCa patients, aligning with prior evidence26,82–84. The integrative advantage of network meta-analysis further validated the robustness of this effect while minimizing bias from individual studies. The RT-induced enhancement of muscle strength results from synergistic adaptations involving both neural regulation and muscle morphological remodeling. At the neural level, RT increases motor unit recruitment efficiency and improves neuromuscular junction transmission, thereby enhancing muscle contraction efficiency85. This adaptive response may counteract treatment-induced neuromuscular dysfunction. At the morphological level, RT induces myofiber hypertrophy in core muscle groups such as the quadriceps, which has been verified by increased quadriceps thickness detected via ultrasound86. Notably, no significant changes in serum free testosterone or growth hormone were observed following RT, indicating that the primary mechanisms of muscle strength enhancement are neural adaptation and localized hypertrophy. Moreover, RT does not compromise androgen suppression during ADT, thereby ensuring the safety of the intervention. These findings carry important clinical implications. From a safety perspective, they provide evidence supporting an integrated treatment–rehabilitation framework. Functionally, RT can enhance activities of daily living and reduce fall risk through improved muscle strength, ultimately enhancing quality of life during treatment.

With respect to body fat regulation, this NMA clearly indicated that AT significantly reduces body fat in PCa patients. This finding is consistent with several traditional meta-analyses, reinforcing the central role of AT in managing body composition. For instance, Bigaran et al. reported that regular AT continuously reduces fat accumulation87, and Hvid et al. also demonstrated that AT is an optimal choice for reducing fat mass88. Long-term AT enhances insulin sensitivity, regulates lipid-metabolism-related enzymes, and suppresses fat synthesis while reducing visceral fat accumulation. Maintaining a healthy body composition holds several critical implications for PCa management. Excessive body fat is an independent risk factor for recurrence and decreased survival, while optimal body composition improves exercise tolerance, mitigates treatment-related fatigue and muscle loss, and enhances quality of life.

In terms of fatigue reduction, AT_RT had the highest probability of being the superior intervention for mitigating fatigue in the evidence network of this study. Although this finding contrasts with one study89, which highlighted design ambiguities in exercise protocols, it corroborates some studies83,84,90. Variations in inclusion criteria likely explain these discrepancies. This meta-analysis included mixed interventions combining exercise with dietary adjustments, whereas prior studies often focused solely on exercise, contributing to heterogeneity. Furthermore, PCa patients vary in disease stage, comorbidities, and baseline fitness. Fatigue remains prevalent across all treatment stages, particularly among patients undergoing chemotherapy or ADT. AT_RT alleviates fatigue through synergistic mechanisms: AT enhances cardiopulmonary function, increases post-exercise oxygen consumption, reduces oxidative stress, and improves metabolism, while RT improves muscle strength via neural adaptations and hypertrophy86, mitigating fatigue secondary to muscle weakness. Importantly, RT does not interfere with androgen suppression, confirming its safety. Together, these effects enhance endurance and strength, constituting the physiological basis of fatigue improvement. Moreover, No major adverse events were reported for all exercise modalities, suggesting acceptable short-term safety; AT_RT had a high adherence rate (> 80%), meeting the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines for cancer rehabilitation13, while the feasibility of other modalities needs further verification due to lack of quantitative adherence data. Although, BFI is concise and user-friendly, focusing on fatigue severity and interference with daily activities. MFI provides multidimensional insights (physical, mental, motivational fatigue), enabling deeper subtype analysis. The data volume discrepancy (15 FACIT-F studies vs. 4 BFI (Brief Fatigue Inventory)/MFI (Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory) studies) is the major reason of our selection.

Regarding aerobic capacity, RT had the highest probability of being the superior intervention in the evidence network of this study for improving functional aerobic capacity assessed by the 6-min walk test (6WMT), rather than traditional cardiopulmonary aerobic capacity (VO₂peak), which may seem surprising given AT is traditionally considered optimal for this outcome. However, this finding is robust and aligned with prior studies82,83. Our network meta-analysis features a closed-loop evidence network for RT with consistent direct/indirect comparisons, whereas isolated AT is supported by only 3 small-scale studies (n = 67). For PCa patients, especially those on ADT with muscle loss, RT improves lower limb muscle strength and lean mass, directly enhancing functional aerobic capacity measured by 6MWT (a mobility-focused indicator, distinct from cardiopulmonary VO₂peak). This explains why RT outperforms AT, which primarily targets cardiopulmonary function but cannot compensate for muscle loss-induced exercise limitations in this population. For instance, ADT-induced metabolic changes and radiotherapy-related cardiopulmonary effects may interact with RT adaptations. Moreover, the heterogeneity of RT protocols (e.g., 50–70% 1RM, 2–3 sessions per week) could explain the non-significant findings in certain trials, underscoring the need for standardized protocols. Additionally, limited inclusion of flexibility, balance, and mind-body training (e.g., yoga) restricts comparative analysis. Future research should conduct head-to-head trials to delineate the relative efficacy of various exercise modalities in improving aerobic capacity.

Concerning cancer-specific quality of life, HIIT may be the most beneficial intervention for improving patient’s under active surveillance (limited by data from only one included study) perceived overall well-being in our included studies. This contrasts with prior findings90, possibly because the included studies primarily examined patients under active surveillance (AS). Individuals in this stage experience psychological distress due to uncertainty about disease progression, leading to anxiety and diminished quality of life79. HIIT may improve well-being through psychosocial mechanisms, such as mood regulation and emotional resilience, rather than through direct physical effects91. However, interpretation should remain cautious, as only one study contributed data on HIIT and quality of life, and such high-intensity protocols may not suit all patient populations. Further high-quality research is warranted to substantiate these findings.

While the NMA enhances evidence strength through comparative synthesis, several limitations persist. A notable limitation is the absence of MIT and ET in the muscle strength network analysis, as no existing RCTs have evaluated the effect of these low-intensity steady-state modalities on muscle strength in PCa survivors. This gap reflects a focus in current research on high/moderate-intensity modalities (e.g., HIIT, MIT) for muscle strength improvement, with limited attention to low-intensity interventions tailored for frail survivors. Future RCTs should address this gap to provide more comprehensive exercise recommendations for diverse PCa survivor populations. Participant heterogeneity (e.g., disease stage, treatment history, and physical condition) may influence the findings. Moreover, exercise protocol heterogeneity (duration: 8 to 96 weeks, frequency: 1 to 5 sessions/week, intensity: low to high) causes potential impact to results, and difficult to identify optimal exercise parameters for specific outcomes. Small sample sizes and short follow-up durations in several trials limit statistical power and preclude evaluation of long-term effects. Based on the results of this Bayesian network meta-analysis, individualized exercise prescriptions, including RT, AT, AT_RT, and HIIT, may be recommended for clinical implementation in PCa rehabilitation. Nevertheless, further validation required in large-sample, long-term follow-up RCTs. Notably, effective exercise intervention strategies entail comprehensive physical training in conjunction with dietary modifications, rather than relying solely on single-mode exercise.

Conclusion

Exercise training represents a safe and effective non-pharmacological intervention for PCa patients. It significantly enhances muscle strength and aerobic capacity, reduces body fat, alleviates fatigue, and improves quality of life. Exercise interventions should therefore be incorporated into routine rehabilitation protocols for prostate cancer management. Specifically, RT was optimal for improving functional aerobic capacity assessed by the 6WMT, rather than cardiopulmonary aerobic capacity (VO₂peak). AT had the highest probability of being the superior intervention for reducing body fat, AT_RT may offers the greatest benefit in alleviating fatigue, and HIIT may be the most beneficial intervention for improving cancer-specific quality of life in PCa patients under active surveillance (limited by data from only one included study).
