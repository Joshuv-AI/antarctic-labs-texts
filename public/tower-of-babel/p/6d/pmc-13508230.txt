Kinetic and isotherm evaluation of xanthene triphenylmethane dye mixture adsorption on cobalt doped coconut husk encapsulated biochar
Authors: Ajay Khajuria, Vatsala Anand, Sandeep Kumar, Ravinder Singh, Chirag Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater is an essential resource, but its quality is increasingly threatened by industrial activities. Textile industries are major contributors to water pollution due to the discharge of untreated synthetic dye effluents into water bodies. In this study, cobalt-doped coconut husk biochar (Co@CHBc) was synthesized by pyrolysis method at 600 °C. The manufactured material was characterized by FESEM, Mapping, EDX, FTIR, BET and XRD. The maximum removal of mixture of cationic dyes such as Rhodamine-B (Rh-B) and Malachite Green (MG) is observed at pH 8, contact time of 60 min, 20 mg/L of mixture of dye concentration, 600 mg of Co@CHBc dosage and at room temperature. The Kinetics data is best fitted with the Pseudo Second Order (PSO) having regression coefficients (R2) of 0.996. The isotherm data is perfectly match with Langmuir Isotherm having R2 of 0.9989. The adsorption capacity of the Co@CHBc was determined to be 31.74 mg/g. Thermodynamics study studies shows that the adsorption is exothermic for the mixture of cationic dyes. The reusability and regeneration studies were performed to check the capability of the Co@CHBc. A comparison studies of Co@CHBc is performed with different dyes such as Erichrome Black T (EBT), Methylene Blue (MB), Congo Red (CR), Methyl Orange (MO) and Crystal Violet (CV). Based on the models a purposed mechanism is designed between mixture of cationic dyes and Co@CHBc. The results indicate that cobalt-doped biochar is a low-cost and sustainable material with strong potential for wastewater treatment applications.Keywords: Biochar, Adsorption, Synthetic dyes, Industrial effluents, Wastewater treatment, Coconut husk

Introduction

Water is an indispensable element for supporting life on our planet, and its significance cannot be emphasized enough. It occupies around 71% of the Earth’s surface and is a crucial element to all living beings [1, 2]. However, in modern times, human activities are causing water pollution, including industrial practices that introduce hazardous substances like heavy metals and organic chemicals into the water [3–6]. The contamination of water caused by textile dyeing especially Azo-dyes has emerged as a prominent global environmental problem. The production of various types of dyes exceeding 7 × 105 metric tonnes annually makes the textile dyeing industry the most damaging sector for the environment globally [7, 8]. The process of dyeing and finishing textiles requires a considerable volume of water, and the resulting wastewater contains a range of detrimental substances such as acids, alkalis, heavy metals, and organic compounds. Additionally, textile dyes are highly concentrated and non-biodegradable, implying that they remain in the ecosystem for extended periods [9, 10].

Textile dyes are categorized as either natural or synthetic. Natural dyes are obtained from various sources such as plants, insects, or minerals, while synthetic dyes are created by using chemicals obtained from petroleum, coal tar, or other sources [2, 11, 12]. Synthetic dyes are more prevalent in the textile industry due to their widespread availability, colourfastness, and ease of use [13, 14]. Numerous techniques have been suggested to tackle the issue of water pollution resulting from textile dyes. The optimal method for wastewater treatment relies on the kind of wastewater, the pollutants present, and the level of treatment required. Some of the most frequently employed approaches for wastewater treatment include chemical, physical, and biological treatment, as well as membrane filtration, membrane bioreactor, photocatalysis, adsorption, chemical oxidation, UV/H2O2, microfiltration, reverse osmosis, Fenton, flocculation, coagulation, and ozonation [15, 16].

Adsorption is a technique commonly employed to remove textile dyes from wastewater because it is inexpensive, simple, and effective. The adsorbents utilized in the removal of textile dyes can be either natural or synthetic materials [17, 18]. While synthetic materials like polymers, zeolites, activated carbon, nanocomposite, metal-organic framework, and graphene oxide are commonly used due to their high adsorption capacity and selectivity, they are also expensive.On the other hand, natural waste materials from agriculture like lemon, peanut shell, coconut husk (CH), chitin, wood, pistachio shells, coffee husk, lemon leaf, peat, corn cob, tobacco stem, sugar cane, moringa, walnut shell, rice husk, banana peel, wheat husk, strawberry seeds, almond shell, Luffa acutangula peels and potato leaves have been utilized as adsorbents due to their high surface area, low cost, and availability [19–24].

Coconut husk is composed of several components, including lignin (20 to 30%), cellulose (41 to 45%), and hemicellulose (25–30%). The remaining portion comprises other components such as pectin, ash, and proteins. Coconut husk (CH) is an excellent adsorbent due to its high surface area and porosity [25]. It contains a high amount of lignin, which has a complex three-dimensional structure and contains many functional groups such as carboxylic and phenolic groups, making it an ideal material for the adsorption of various pollutants [19]. The presence of cellulose and hemicellulose provides a large number of sites for adsorption of contaminants. The high fiber content of coconut husk also contributes to its ability to adsorb pollutants, as fibers can physically trap and remove contaminants from the water. Finally, the coconut husk is abundant, renewable, and low-cost, making it an attractive alternative to synthetic adsorbents [26].

When coconut husk (CH) is subjected to pyrolysis without the presence of oxygen, it undergoes a transformation into CH biochar. CH biochar possesses a number of characteristics that enable it to act as an efficient adsorbent for eliminating pollutants such as textile dyes from wastewater. The Doping of coconut husk with cobalt acetate tetrahydrate in the given ratio of 1:10 (dopant: substrate mass ratio) can lead to several changes in the adsorption properties of pollutants. The Cobalt doped Coconut husk biochar (Co@CHBc) enhances its adsorption capacity, improved selectivity, faster adsorption kinetics, and greater stability in water, making it more effective at removing pollutants from wastewater.

Several investigations have examined the use of Co@CHBc to eliminate the textile dyes from wastewater in this article. The results indicate that Co@CHBc is a competent adsorbent for the removal of textile dyes, such as acid, reactive, and basic dyes. The efficiency of Co@CHBc in adsorbing textile dyes is impacted by several factors, including the dye concentration, contact duration, pH, temperature, and the existence of additional contaminants in the wastewater. This study presents a novel and sustainable approach for the synthesis of Co@CHBc. It is an efficient adsorbent for the simultaneous removal of mixed cationic dyes, specifically Rh-B and MG dye, from aqueous solutions. Conventional studies focus on single dye systems, this work emphasizes the treatment of a binary dye system, which more closely represents real industrial wastewater conditions.

The incorporation of cobalt into the biochar matrix significantly enhances the availability of active sites and improves adsorption performance through synergistic effects, including electrostatic interactions and surface complexation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Analytical-quality reagents were used without any purifying procedures. The chemicals were sourced from different suppliers; Rhodamine B (C28H31ClN2O3) and Malachite Green (C23H25ClN2), were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemicals in India, with molecular weights of 479.02 g/mol and 364.9 g/mol, respectively. CH was obtained from the nearby Rajpura market in Punjab. SRL provided Cobalt (II) acetatetetrahydydrate [Co(OAc)24H2O)], which has an MW of 249.08 g/mol, as well as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) with an MW of 40 g/mol and hydrochloric acid (HCl) with an MW of 35.5 g/mol. The experiment utilized 0.1 M HCl to maintain the solution pH at 6.0, and millipore water was used for all sample preparations. Dilutions were made after creating a standard solution with a concentration of 1000 mg/L as a starting point.

Synthesis of Co@CHBc

To prepare the CH for use, they were cleaned by rinsing and washing them several times with Millipore water to remove any accumulated debris or garbage. Next, they were dried for 48 h at 100 °C in a heating oven to get rid of any excess moisture. A solution of CH and [Co(OAc)2.4H2O] in distilled water was used, with both substances having a weight-to-weight ratio of 1:10. The mixture was left to soak for 24 h at room temperature after being combined. Following the initial 24-hour impregnation period, the sample was dried for another 48 h at 120 °C to create the impregnated sample. After that sample was placed in a muffle furnace for carbonization at 600 °C at a rate of ten °C per minute for 5 h [27]. The resulting carbonized sample was then washed with distilled water to minimize waste content and eliminate any impurities. Finally, the activated carbon was dried for 48 h at 100 degrees Celsius in a heating oven before being used for the batch studies.

Characterization of Co@CHBc

The Co@CHBc spectrum was analyzed using the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) technique with a Perkin Elmer spectrophotometer to study the functional groups present. The KBr pellet method was used to generate the spectrum within a wavelength range of 400–4000 cm-1. The crystalline structures of Co@CHBc (BRUKER) were investigated using X-ray diffraction analysis with Cu K radiation (λ = 1.5405), scanning from 10 to 80° at a rate of 15°/min. To examine the porous structure of Co@CHBc, a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) with model number SU8010 Series made by Hitachi Japan was utilized.

Adsorption experiments

The impact of different factors that affect the adsorption, such as duration of contact time, the initial level of dye concentration, temperature, and pH, were observed and the most favourable experimental conditions were determined. The adsorption kinetics were examined at a temperature of 27 °C. A solution containing an mixture of cationic dye was mixed with 600 mg of Co@CHBc in Stoppard conical flasks and shaken for varying periods of time on a shaker. After each time interval, the mixture was filtered and tested for dye concentration using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (BURKER).

The experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the mean results were utilized. The effect of pH on the equilibrium adsorption of dyes was analyzed across a pH range of 2–12, and the pH of the solution was adjusted using 0.05 M NaOH or 0.05 M HCl solutions. The batch adsorption studies were carried out by varying contact times (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min) and dye concentrations (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 mg/L) to determine maximum adsorption. Adsorption activity of mixture of cationic dye concentration

Effect of Co@CHBc dose

The adsorbent dose has an impact on the adsorption process. Increasing the amount of adsorbent from 150 mg to 900 mg led to a rise in the mixture of cationic dye removal rate from 37.15% to 99.24% as depicted in Fig. 4a. This can be attributed to the greater surface area of the adsorbent and the increased number of available adsorption sites. Another reason for this is that the increase in adsorbent dose at a constant mixture of cationic dye concentration and volume can prevent the saturation of adsorption sites during the process. The study was able to achieve a 98.07% removal rate for mixture of cationic dye with a concentration of 20 mg/L by using 600 mg of Co@CHBc adsorbent.

Effect of initial cationic dye concentration on the adsorption process

With variations in the mixture of cationic dye concentration ranging from 10 to 60 mg/L, the removal efficiency percentage shows a significant decrease, declining from 99.3% to 48.32%. As there were numerous sites available for mixture of cationic dye adsorption, initial quantities of the dye were effectively eliminated. However, with an increase in the mixture of cationic dye concentration, fewer unoccupied active sites were accessible for adsorption, as illustrated in Fig. 4b.

Effect of contact time

The duration of contact time, ranging from 10 to 60 min, between an adsorbent and a dye is a crucial factor that can affect the effectiveness of dye elimination from wastewater. Contact time pertains to the duration for which the dye molecules are permitted to interact with the adsorbent surface. Upon introducing Co@CHBc into the dye solution, the adsorption process is initially rapid, leading to a swift reduction in the dye concentration.

However, as the contact time increases to 30 min, the adsorption rate gradually slows down until it reaches equilibrium. Equilibrium is achieved when there is no further change in the dye concentration, signifying that all accessible adsorption sites on the biochar have been occupied by the dye molecules, as depicted in Fig. 4(c). The impact of contact time on dye adsorption was examined for a duration of 60 min at 27 °C, using initial dye concentrations of 20 mg/L in 100 mL of distilled water with a catalyst dosage of 600 mg shows a removal efficiency of 98.07%. The removal rate becomes almost constant after 60 min of contact time, according to the results. Therefore, 60 min was selected as the equilibrium time for adsorption.

Effect of pH and point zero charge (pHPZC)

The impact of solution pH on the adsorption process was investigated by determining the maximum adsorption at various pH values ranging from 2 to 12. The optimal pH range for adsorption was found to be pH 8, as shown in Fig. 5a. At pH 8, the electrostatic attraction between the Co@CHBc and dye molecules is particularly strong, leading to increased adsorption when the dye molecules are positively charged relative to the adsorbent surface.

(a) Effect of pH (b) zero-point charge on the mixture of cationic dye removal using Co@CHBc (reaction optimization: [Co@CHBc] = 600 mg in 100 mL; pH = 8.0; at room temperature = 25 °C; [mixture of cationic dye] = 20 mg/L)

The pH is an important factor in determining the adsorption mechanism. pHPZC studies were conducted to understand the surface charge, and Fig. 5b shows that the pHPZC of Co@CHBc is 7.3. If the value of (pHPZC > pH), then pHPZC is higher than the pH of biochar, the biochar’s surface will be positively charged, whereas the biochar surface will be negatively charged if the value of pHPZC (7.3) is lower than the pH of Co@CHBc is 8 (pH > pHPZC). Therefore, to optimize the adsorption of mixture of cationic dye by coconut husk biochar, it is very significant to adjust the pH of the solution to a value that is higher than the pHPZC of the biochar. This can create a negative surface charge on the biochar, which can attract the positively charged cationic dyes and enhance their adsorption [28].

Reusability and regeneration studies

The regeneration performance of the spent Co@CHBc was investigated using a chemical regeneration method with 0.1 M NaOH solution. After each adsorption cycle, the dye-loaded adsorbent was treated with the alkaline regenerating agent to desorb the retained mixture of cationic dye molecules, followed by thorough washing with deionized water until neutral pH and subsequent drying before reuse. By using a chemical regeneration technique with a 0.1 M NaOH solution, the regeneration ability of the spent Co@CHBc was examined. In each cycle of adsorption, the dye loaded Co@CHBc was thoroughly cleaned with deionized water until the pH was neutral, and in an oven dried. And then treated with the alkaline regeneration agent to desorb the residual mixture of cationic dye molecules. The adsorption efficiency after regeneration was evaluated over eight successive adsorption desorption cycles, as presented in Fig. 9b. The results demonstrate that chemical regeneration using 0.1 M NaOH was initially effective in restoring the adsorption capacity of the activated carbon.

In the first cycles, the Co@CHBc showed high removal efficiency of 97%, which indicates available adsorption sites on Co@CHBc. However, adsorption efficacy deteriorated as the number of regeneration cycles increased, and by the eighth cycle, the removal efficiency had dropped to 31%. There are several reasons for the decrease in adsorption efficiency. Such as excessive exposure to NaOH may cause strongly bound dye molecules to partially desorb, gradually blocking micropores and active adsorption sites. However, repeated alkaline treatment may change surface functional groups, all of which lead to a decrease of effective adsorption capacity over next cycles.

Proposed mechanism of mixture of cationic dye

The elimination of mixture of cationic dye from artificial effluent by Co@CHBc is a complicated process involving both physical and chemical relationships. The adsorption mechanism of mixture of cationic dye by Co@CHBc is a complex interaction. Because the conjugated framework of the mixture of cationic dye, consisting of aromatic ring molecules and multiple bonds, binds with the molecular framework of the Co@CHBc. First of all, the porous framework of coconut husk biochar provides a large surface area as well as many macro and micro-pores, making it easier to accommodate dye molecules by physical adsorption. Cobalt encapsulation increases the surface area and porosity, which improves adsorption capacity [30, 31].

Chemically, cobalt ions that are present on the biochar surface provide extra active sites for adsorption. These ions create coordinated complexes with mixture of cationic dye molecules, which enhance adsorption via coordination bonds. The surface chemistry of Co@CHBc, including different functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phenolic groups, improves its attraction for mixture of a cationic dye molecules through electrostatic interactions, π-π interactions and hydrogen bonding. These functional compounds create vacant sites for chemical processes, which assisting in mixture of a cationic dye adsorption. Sustainability and cost assessment

CH was chosen as the precursor material for this investigation due to its availability as an agricultural waste and low cost. To make cobalt-doped biochar, 10 g of CH was impregnated with 1 g of cobalt acetate tetrahydrate (1:10), then pyrolyzed in a muffle furnace. The utilization of agro-waste biomass substantially reduces raw material costs and favours waste valorisation. CH is abundantly available in many areas and frequently eliminated, making it an inexpensive and sustainable fuel for biochar synthesis [40, 41]. The cobalt precursor is the primary source of increased expense throughout the preparation procedure. However, the comparatively low cobalt loading minimizes the total rise in manufacturing costs. The synthesis approach consists of simple impregnation and pyrolysis phases that do not require sophisticated chemical treatments, making it appropriate for scaling up. Pyrolysis in a muffle furnace is a popular and scalable technology for producing biochar, and economies of scale can further lower costs per unit mass at bigger stages [42]. As a result, a combination of low-cost biomass feedstock, minimum chemical consumption, and a straight forward production process provides support that Co@CHBc is a sustainable and economically feasible adsorbent for wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusion

In this study, Co@CHBc was synthesized by simple pyrolysis at 600 °C. The Co@CHBc was characterized by FESEM, EDX, Mapping, FTIR, XRD and BET studies. The following parameters are examined to investigate the adsorption performance of Co@CHBc for mixture of cationic dye such as pH, contact time, temperature, initial mixture of cationic dye concentration, Co@CHBc dosage. As a result, the maximum removal of mixture of cationic dye is 99% which was optimized at room temperature, pH = 8, mixture of cationic dye concentration = 20 mg/L, contact time = 60 min, and Co@CHBc = 600 mg in 100 mL of water. The adsorption kinetics data on kinetic equilibrium followed Pseudo-second-order kinetics which shows that the chemisorption process takes place between the mixture of cationic dye and Co@CHBc. Also, the adsorption isotherm data is well suited for the Langmuir isotherm which clearly shows that the adsorption is monolayer. On the basis of these adsorption and kinetic models, the adsorption mechanism is purposed.

In order to assess the economic viability of generating Co@CHBc on a wide scale. Future research should focus on cost analysis that takes into account the availability of raw materials, regeneration costs and energy consumption. A complete life cycle assessment (LCA) is required to determine the adsorbent’s total environmental effect, from biomass gathering and synthesis to use and disposal. Further research should also look into pilot-scale and industrial scale applications, like long-term operational stability, regeneration efficiency throughout several cycles, and possible metal leaching, to assure environmental safety.
