Interim analysis, a tool to enhance efficiency of pharmacokinetic studies: Pharmacokinetics of rifampicin in lactating mother–infant pairs
Authors: Aida N Kawuma, Francis Williams Ojara, Allan Buzibye, Barbara Castelnuovo, Jovia C Tabwenda, Jacqueline Kyeyune, Christine Turyahabwe, Simon Peter Asiimwe, Johnson Magoola, Lubbe Wiesner, Ritah Nakijoba, Catriona Waitt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmacokinetic studies are important for understanding drug disposition in the human body. However, pregnant and lactating women are often excluded from primary pharmacokinetic studies and as such there is often limited dosing information regarding drug use in pregnant and/or lactating women. The objectives of this interim analysis were to define the transfer of rifampicin to a breastfed infant and to determine the area under the concentration–time curve of rifampicin in maternal plasma, breastmilk and infant plasma. Performing this interim analysis enabled us to substantiate whether prior assumptions we made on several study design issues including patient sample size and pharmacokinetic sampling times held and whether we needed to amend our protocol or not. We enrolled lactating mothers on treatment for tuberculosis with their breastfeeding infants (below 12 months of age), performed intensive pharmacokinetic sampling (0–24 h post‐dose) on plasma samples from both the mother, infant(s) and breastmilk samples from the mother on two separate occasions (once during the initiation phase and another during the continuation phase of tuberculosis treatment). The initial study design, including sampling times, was informed by a stochastic simulation and estimation exercise, with very limited prior breastmilk data. An interim analysis after recruiting 6 mother–infant pairs ascertained that our initial assumptions were ideal for achieving our study objectives and no amendments to the sampling times were necessary. Initial data from 6 mother–infant pairs show that rifampicin penetrates breastmilk with an approximate milk‐to‐plasma ratio of 0.169 and 0.189 on two separate visits. However, it was undetectable in most infants.

Scientists should consider using interim analysis as an option for the timely assessment of accumulating data from studies undertaken in circumstances where there is limited or no prior data, especially in understudied populations.

INTRODUCTION

Pharmacokinetic studies play a vital role in evaluating drug behavior within the human body, determining appropriate dosing regimens, and understanding reasons for variability within and between individuals. However, populations including neonates, pregnant, and lactating women are often systematically excluded from primary pharmacokinetic studies that inform drug dosing. As a result, once a drug is approved, there is initially a paucity of prescribing information for these understudied populations and therefore, almost no prior data on which to base assumptions for further studies. This is a global problem. However, it presents particular challenges in low‐resource regions of the world where breastfeeding is the only affordable, feasible, acceptable, sustainable and safe infant feeding option 1 and where conditions requiring drug therapy are prevalent in women of childbearing age. 2 Often for understudied populations, information on drug pharmacokinetics in these groups is usually derived from post‐marketing clinical trials or surveillance studies. In other instances, prior information (e.g., from a well‐studied adult population) coupled with expected/anticipated differences between the understudied group and the general population can inform power‐size calculations for specific trials. However, in some circumstances, no prior data exists and that is often the case for information on whether a drug transfers into breastmilk or not. In such situations, assumptions must be made based on several aspects. These include the physicochemical properties of the drug, preclinical and animal data and sometimes individual case studies. Ultimately, an optimal study design is chosen based on these assumptions together with logistical feasibility. The use of interim analysis for studies undertaken in such circumstances enables the timely assessment of accumulating data, with a particular focus on the number of participants and optimal sampling times relative to drug dosing, thereby ensuring that modifications based on results from an interim analysis allow for a study design that answers the research question with adequate statistical power. 3 , 4 It may also allow for the early identification of potential safety concerns associated with drug exposure/pharmacokinetic parameters.

Lastly, an interim analysis allows for the integration of emerging knowledge and technologies into pharmacokinetic studies. New information, including drug–drug interactions, genetic factors, or novel analytical techniques, can be incorporated during the study to enhance data interpretation and refine pharmacokinetic analyses.

This manuscript provides an example of incorporating an interim analysis into a pharmacokinetic study protocol in a scenario where there is limited prior information about plasma to breastmilk transfer of drugs used for the treatment of tuberculosis in a population of lactating mothers.

Appreciable cases of tuberculosis occur in women of childbearing age with an estimated 216,500 cases of active tuberculosis reported in pregnant women annually across the globe. 5 Rifampicin is a key component of regimens used to treat drug‐sensitive tuberculosis. Despite approval 52 years ago, the guidance on its use in lactating women comes from a solitary publication in 1969, which while innovative in that era, was limited by lack of detail with respect to study design or number of study participants. 6 The current recommendation is that rifampicin can be used for lactating mothers since only low levels are observed in breastmilk, and these are not expected to cause any adverse effects in breastfed infants. The passage of antituberculosis drugs through breastmilk to the breastfed infant 7 may affect the infant either beneficially or adversely.

Here, we report on an interim analysis of an observational pharmacokinetic study of rifampicin in lactating mother–infant pairs. We focus on rifampicin since it plays a key role in tuberculosis treatment because of its bactericidal and sterilizing activity, including for latent tubercle bacilli. It is used in both the initiation and continuation phases of treatment. The objectives of this study were to (1) define the transfer of rifampicin, to a breastfed infant, (2) to determine the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) of rifampicin in maternal plasma, breastmilk and infant plasma.

METHODS

Study design and population

Lactating Ugandan women undergoing treatment for drug‐sensitive tuberculosis taking the WHO‐recommended first‐line treatment regimen together with their breastfeeding infants were enrolled from the Infectious Diseases Institute clinic, College of Health Sciences (Makerere University) and supported Kampala Capital City Authority clinics. The WHO 6‐month treatment regimen comprises an initiation phase which consists of a combination of 4 drugs (rifampicin, isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide) taken daily for 2 months and a continuation phase which consists of rifampicin and isoniazid taken daily for an additional 4 months. Pharmacokinetic sampling for both mother and infant was performed on two separate visits. The enrollment visit was conducted within the initiation phase of tuberculosis treatment while the second visit was conducted within the continuation phase of tuberculosis treatment. In these drug regimens, rifampicin is dosed at 600 mg once daily within a fixed‐dose combination pill (manufactured by either Macleods or Lupin Pharmaceuticals). Further details of the study design have been reported previously. 8

Sample size

Since there is limited information about the transfer of anti‐tuberculosis drugs to breastmilk, our study design was largely informed by a stochastic simulation and estimation (SSE) exercise. This simulation exercise helped inform on the eventual sample size and sampling schedule (what timepoints to draw maternal plasma and breast milk samples). An SSE exercise is largely based on pharmacokinetic models for which one makes assumptions. SSE evaluates the ability of a pre‐conceived study design (dose and pharmacokinetic sampling schedule and study population) to deliver acceptably accurate and precise pharmacokinetic parameter estimates given an appropriate pharmacokinetic model.

Performing the simulations and estimation

Method

We performed an SSE in NONMEM version 7.5.0 with the aid of Pearl Speaks NONMEM. 9 We run 200 simulations of no more than 20 individuals each with 5 paired plasma and breastmilk samples. A pre‐dose, 2‐, 4‐, 6‐, and 8‐h post‐dose sampling schedule of (paired plasma and breastmilk) was tested. We did not simulate more than 20 individuals because based on our previous experience conducting studies on breastfeeding mothers, we were cautious that numbers above these would be difficult to recruit.

Model and assumptions

A previously published rifampicin model 10 was modified to include an effect compartment to describe the delay in drug transfer from the maternal plasma to the breast milk. The modified model is portrayed in Figure 2. An effect compartment is a theoretical concept that can be used to describe a delay in the distribution of a drug to the location where it causes its effect. In this instance, adding an effect compartment to an existing pharmacokinetic model allowed us to describe a delay in the distribution of rifampicin from plasma to breast milk. This involved two additional parameters. An accumulation factor (breast milk to plasma ratio) and an equilibrium rate constant (delay). For our simulations, the half‐life of the delay was fixed to 1 h and the breast milk to plasma accumulation ratio to 1.5. These were chosen to mimic breast milk concentrations reported by the available published data which outlined breastmilk concentrations after a single oral dose of rifampicin. 6 Reflecting the conduct of such studies at the time, in the late 1960s, this study was limited by a lack of detail regarding the design and participants. At the time of performing our SSE, there was no further literature reporting observed breastmilk concentrations of rifampicin.

Proposed population pharmacokinetic model to describe observed rifampicin plasma and breastmilk concentrations.

For both additional parameters, 30% between‐subject variability was added and 30% error in the breastmilk measurements was assumed. Results from the SSE showed that at least 15 participants were required to characterize all the typical values of the plasma pharmacokinetic parameters with precision of at least 11% relative standard error (RSE) and the two additional breastmilk parameters with a precision of 1.14% and 0.591% RSE respectively for the delay and accumulation ratio. Therefore, based on the results of the simulation and estimation exercise, the entire study was designed to require 20 mother–infant pairs, which would account for an attrition rate of about 10%. However, because this was based on assumptions, this interim analysis on the first 6 mother–infant pairs was incorporated into the study design from the outset. We were also mindful that the published model used to run the SSE exercise might not apply to our population.

Pharmacokinetic sampling schedule

On both visits, plasma and breastmilk samples were obtained prior to a directly observed dose and at 2‐, 4‐, 6‐, and 8‐h post‐dose. All sampling was performed after participants had been on medication for more than 2 weeks to allow for them to reach steady state. If logistics permitted (proximity of participant's residence to the research unit), participants were invited for a further sample at 24 h post‐dose. A heel prick sample was obtained from the breastfed infants at the maternal trough (before maternal dose) and at a random timepoint (once per infant) over the 8‐h pharmacokinetic sampling visit, to characterize concentrations of these drugs over an 8‐h dosing interval. This sampling procedure and further details of the study design have been reported previously 8 (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05840809).

Ethical conduct of the study

The study was reviewed and approved by the Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI) Regulatory Ethics Committee (REC) REF: IDI REC 039/2021, the University of Liverpool Research Ethics Committee and the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST): HS1948ES. The study is being conducted according to legal and regulatory requirements, as well as the general principles outlined in the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Participants and the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, the study is being conducted in accordance with the protocol, GCP guidelines, and applicable local regulatory requirements and laws. All participants are required to give written informed consent prior to any study activities being undertaken.

Rifampicin assay

Plasma and breastmilk rifampicin concentrations were determined with validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assays developed at the Division of Clinical Pharmacology, University of Cape Town, South Africa.

Plasma samples were processed with a protein precipitation extraction method using rifampicin‐d3 as the internal standard, followed by high‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry detection on a SCIEX 3000 instrument. Isocratic chromatography was performed on a Discovery C18 (5 μm, 50 mm × 4.6 mm) analytical column. The analyte and internal standard were monitored at mass transitions of the protonated precursor ions at 823.4 and 826.5 to the product ions at 791.4 and 794.4 for rifampicin and rifampicin‐d3, respectively. The assay was validated over the 0.117–30.0 μg/mL concentration range. The combined accuracy and precision (coefficient of variation) statistics of the low‐, medium‐, and high‐quality controls (three validation batches, n = 18) were between 101% and 107%, and 2.7% and 3.7%, respectively.

Breastmilk samples were processed with a protein precipitation and solid‐phase extraction method using rifampicin‐d3 as the internal standard, followed by high‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry detection on a SCIEX 4000 instrument. Isocratic chromatography was performed on an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC‐C18 (2.7 μm, 4.6 × 50 mm) analytical column. The analyte and internal standard were monitored at mass transitions of the protonated precursor ions at 823.5 and 828.5 to the product ions at 791.4 and 796.4 for rifampicin and rifampicin‐d3, respectively. The assay was validated over the 0.150–15.0 μg/mL concentration range. The combined accuracy and precision (coefficient of variation) statistics of the low‐, medium‐, and high‐quality controls (three validation batches, n = 18) were between 89.3% and 92.5%, and 2.1% and 3.4%, respectively.

Interim pharmacokinetic data analysis

Data exploration was performed using R software R (4.2.2) while non‐compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis (NCA) was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.4. With the NCA, we derived the following parameters: the maximum concentration of rifampicin in maternal plasma and breastmilk, the time to maximum concentration and the area under the concentration–time curve in both plasma and maternal breastmilk.

RESULTS

In this interim analysis, we report findings from 6 mother–infant pairs. At the time of enrollment, one mother was in the continuation phase of treatment (rifampicin and isoniazid only) while 5 mothers were in the initiation phase of treatment (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol). The mothers' baseline demographics at screening were as follows: A median (interquartile range) weight of 52.6 kg (49.8–59.8) and height of 163.5 cm (158.5–167.0). All infants were born at full term and at baseline had a median (range) age of 4.5 months (2–8) and weight of 6.6 kg (5.4–10). Sixty‐five paired plasma/breastmilk samples were measured (Figure 1). Eighteen plasma samples—9 at pre‐dose, 1 at 2 h post‐dose, and 8 at 24 h post‐dose—were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ, 0.117 mg/L in plasma); which accounts for 28% of the plasma samples across both visits. For breastmilk, 27 (41.5%) samples were below the LLOQ of 0.150 mg/L including 9, 2, 1, 5, and 10 samples at pre‐dose, 2‐, 4‐, 8‐ and 24‐h post‐dose respectively. Values below the LLOQ were set to LLOQ/2 for data analysis.

The mean plasma maximum concentration (C max) was 7.22 mg/L, and 6.34 mg/mL in visits 1 and 2, respectively while the mean time to maximum concentration (T max) was 2.4 and 4.0 h in visits 1 and 2, respectively for plasma. There is a lag in the distribution of rifampicin from plasma to breast milk with a mean T max of 3.20 and 4.67 h in visits 1 and 2, respectively for breast milk. From patients with complete plasma and breastmilk concentration–time profiles, the mean milk‐to‐plasma ratio calculated as AUC in milk divided by the AUC in plasma was 0.169 and 0.189 in visits 1 and 2, respectively. Participant 6 had a markedly higher plasma and breastmilk concentration than expected on the second visit compared to her first visit, especially at the pre‐dose sample. She is also the only participant whose infant had observable plasma drug concentrations. This infant had a quantifiable concentration of 2.93 mg/L at 6 h post maternal dose. No severe adverse events were reported for either the mother or infant in these first 6 participants. Additionally, there was good adherence to the study protocol with all 5 mothers enrolled during the initiation phase of treatment completing two pharmacokinetic visits. All 6 mothers returned to the study site for a 24‐h post‐dose sample. Therefore, the logistics around the sampling for both the mother and infant were workable.

DISCUSSION

It is ethically essential that drugs are studied in the populations where they are to be used. Moreover, it is equally ethically essential that this is done with high quality with a study design that answers our questions as efficiently as possible and maximizes the use of each precious sample. There is now increased awareness of drawbacks associated with routinely excluding pregnant and breastfeeding women from clinical trials. 11 As such, there are now efforts including those by the E21 ICH working group 12 to provide guidelines on how to safely include pregnant and breastfeeding women in clinical trials.

This ongoing study provides the first real‐world account of the plasma‐to‐breastmilk distribution of rifampicin and associated infant exposure. The results of our interim analysis show that our current study design with its sampling schedule is adequate to answer our study objectives and is feasible and acceptable in our setting. As expected, for rifampicin which has a relatively short half‐life of about 2.5 h; 13 most samples at 24 h post‐dose had concentrations below the LLOQ. This is similar to previous studies (in plasma) which have reported similar findings. 14 , 15 Considering that rifampicin is a lipid‐soluble drug, we would expect it to distribute into breastmilk and as shown in Figure 1, we observed measurable amounts of rifampicin in the breastmilk samples.

Our findings (maternal plasma) match what we would expect among adult females—given that most of the women were at least 6 weeks postpartum which is generally accepted to be the time after which a postpartum woman is expected to return to the ‘normal’ pre‐pregnancy physiological state. 16 Based on our sampling schedule, we observe a T max that is slightly longer than what has been reported previously when rifampicin is given without food (1.5–2 h). 17 This is consistent with the fact that our participants were dosed with rifampicin after a standardized breakfast. Considering they were lactating mothers and that most of them anecdotally admitted to not fasting following dosing, we thought it was not prudent to fast them. Food has been shown to delay the T max and lower both the C max and AUC of rifampicin. 17

We report a difference (almost twofold) of T max between visit 1 and visit 2. A possible explanation for this is the difference in formulations. During visit 1, participants were in the initiation phase of treatment which consists of a fixed‐dose combination pill of isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide. During visit 2, participants were undergoing the continuation phase of treatment which consists of a fixed‐dose combination pill of rifampicin and isoniazid only.

No participants were lost to follow‐up; all study visits were attended, and no mother declined sampling from her infant. This is consistent with our previous experience of high levels of engagement of this population in studies relating to medication use and safe breastfeeding, 18 and was likely supported by the portfolio of public engagement and involvement and participatory research established by the MILK study team which has built strong community trust and partnership.

We suspect that participant 6 inadvertently took a dose of her medication a few hours before her second pharmacokinetic visit. Therefore, in addition to the observed dose taken at the facility, the participant already had appreciable concentrations. This observation compelled us to review our participant education process to ensure that we communicated the dosing instructions before the pharmacokinetic visit clearly. Only one infant had quantifiable rifampicin concentrations. This is not surprising given that the bioavailability of oral rifampicin is estimated at 68% during steady state and decreases from about 93% because of an increased first‐pass effect caused by autoinduction. It is also not surprising therefore that the one infant who had quantifiable rifampicin concentrations belonged to participant number 6. This serendipitous finding suggests considerations that one might have to ponder when using high‐dose rifampicin in a lactating mother. High‐dose rifampicin (used at doses as high as 35 mg/kg/day) might mean considerable exposure in a breastfed infant. Although these concentrations might still be safe for an exposed infant, further evaluation is warranted and could use a similar methodology to that reported here.

Despite only one infant of the 6 mother–infant pairs having measurable plasma concentrations, we continued sampling the infants of the proceeding participants because this finding was not sufficient to rule out infant exposure completely, especially considering that concentrations in breastmilk showed appreciable variability among the 6 mothers. We continued to sample from the infant at maternal trough because we will subsequently analyze concentrations of isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide in all 20 participants. Infants of breastfeeding mothers with tuberculosis are routinely prescribed isoniazid prophylaxis, 19 and additional drug exposure through breastmilk may have additional prophylactic benefits. However, considering that many anti‐tuberculosis drugs are metabolized by the liver, there is concern that infants, with their immature liver function, might be at risk of drug accumulation or adverse drug events. However, based on our findings, we will not measure rifampicin in the infant trough sample. For this interim analysis, we focused on rifampicin because it was most feasible and clearer to focus on a single drug first. Also, the power calculations to advise on the sample size were based on a rifampicin model. Lastly, rifampicin is a key component of antituberculosis treatment and is utilized both in the initiation and continuation phases of treatment.

Considering the small sample size of 6 participants, for this interim analysis, we undertook a non‐compartmental analysis as opposed to a compartmental approach. Given the risks of small sample size and incomplete capture of the anticipated variability within the population, a population pharmacokinetic analysis at this point could be at best limited, and at worst, potentially misleading. However, once the recruitment of all participants is completed, we will carry out a compartmental modeling approach to develop a population pharmacokinetic model to describe the observed plasma and breastmilk concentrations. With NCA, data points below the LLOQ are only analyzed by setting them to a fixed value (as we did in our analysis). On the other hand, model‐based analyses are better at handling data below LLOQ as the distribution of data points below LLOQ is modeled. The sampling schedule used for the first 6 mother–infant pairs and proceeding participants should allow us to adequately describe the distribution and elimination of rifampicin both in the plasma and breast milk. Considering that there is a delay in the peaking of breastmilk samples compared to plasma samples, we propose to first develop a population pharmacokinetic model using plasma data only and then extend this to include an effect compartment for the breastmilk. This will allow us to estimate two extra parameters, that is, an equilibration rate constant (delay) and an accumulation factor (breastmilk to plasma ratio) to describe the transfer of rifampicin from plasma to breastmilk. Figure 2 shows the proposed model we will fit to the data.

One limitation of this analysis is the small sample size. However, considering that there was only very scanty prior literature on the transfer of rifampicin to breastmilk, this report represents a substantial contribution to the body of knowledge. Another limitation is the fact that all values below the LLOQ were fixed to LLOQ/2. This could result in an over‐estimation of AUC values for both plasma and breastmilk.

In conclusion, incorporating interim analysis into pharmacokinetic studies offers several advantages, including the opportunity to verify whether initial study design assumptions hold. This allows researchers to make data‐driven decisions ultimately leading to more efficient and effective treatment approaches. These preliminary results provide additional robust information that although there is transfer of rifampicin in breast milk, the concentrations are low and nursing infants have largely undetectable levels. This interim analysis demonstrates that our study design is adequate for the characterization of plasma‐to‐breastmilk transfer of rifampicin and quantification of infant exposure. Further enrollment of mother–infant pairs (target n = 20), coupled with pharmacometrics‐based data analysis, will enable the characterization of the dynamic plasma and breastmilk concentrations, identification of sources of interpatient variability and quantification of infant exposure.
