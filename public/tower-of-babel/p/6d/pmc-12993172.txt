Biomimetic de novo construction of hierarchically aligned and gradient-mineralized collagen for tendon-bone integrated regeneration
Authors: Miao Lei, Hao Luo, Luyi Sun, Songsong Shi, Zhuangri Zhang, Chengxuan Yu, Han Gao, Jun Chen, Changsheng Liu, Xue Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tissues spanning from tendon to bone exhibit a highly specialized extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture, characterized by hierarchical collagen alignment and a gradient mineral composition, which together enable efficient force transfer and guide spatially organized cellular phenotypes. However, recapitulating such complex multi-scale organization and compositional gradients to achieve integrated soft-hard tissue remains challenging. Here, we report the de novo construction of biomimetic collagen–mineral matrices that mimic both the hierarchical organization and mineral gradient distribution of the native tendon-to-bone ECM. Through synergistic electro-assembly and post-treatment, collagen molecules self-organized into aligned fibrillar matrices with multi-scale architecture, replicating tendon-side morphology while providing robust tensile mechanics. At the opposing end, intrafibrillar and interfibrillar minerals were spatially patterned to emulate the mineral gradient from tendon to bone. This structural and compositional continuum enables smooth mechanical transition across the soft-hard tissue interface and promotes region-specific regeneration of aligned tendon-like tissue, fibrocartilage and bone. In vivo studies in rabbit models confirm that these de novo constructed matrices support histological reconstruction of multiple tissues from tendon to bone at the rotator cuff, and significantly improve functional recovery. This work presents a bottom-up biomimetic strategy for engineering multiscale collagen-based scaffolds and demonstrates the therapeutic potential of de novo constructed matrices for multiple tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Each year, more than 1.71 billion musculoskeletal injuries occur globally, with approximately 50% involving tendons-collagen-rich connective tissues that transmit muscle forces to bone through their remarkable tensile strength and stiffness [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Re-establishing a stable tendon-to-bone connection is critical for restoring joint function in various surgical procedures, such as rotator cuff repair [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Graft implantation is commonly employed to support tendon healing and reattachment, yet autografts and allografts suffer from limitations including donor site morbidity, immune response, and mechanical degradation due to aging [5,10,11]. Synthetic tendon substitutes (e.g., Leeds-Keio) offer robust mechanical properties but lack sufficient bioactivity, leading to poor tissue integration and complications such as synovitis or graft rupture [12]. A persistent challenge in tendon-to-bone repair is the formation of disorganized scar tissue at the insertion, which lacks the aligned collagen architecture and mineral composition of the native tendon-bone insertion [13]. The resulting insertion displays compromised mechanical strength often one order of magnitude lower than native tissue and limited osseointegration. Compounding this issue, the bone adjacent to the repair site undergoes resorption due to altered mechanical loading and increased osteoclastic activity, contributing to high postoperative re-tear rates, reported up to 94% [6,14].

Histologically, the tissues from tendon to bone possess unique extracellular matrix (ECM) characteristics as shown in Scheme 1a. Highly aligned collagen fibrils in the tendon are hierarchically bundled into larger fibers and transition through a fibrocartilaginous zone with a gradient in mineral content, ultimately forming a mineralized matrix in bone with intrafibrillar and interfibrillar minerals (i.e., hydroxyapatite, HAp) [15]. This structural and compositional continuum ensures effective stress transfer and provides spatial biochemical cues critical for guiding cell phenotype and orchestrating tissue regeneration [8,[16], [17], [18]]. Despite increasing interest in biomimetic design, current fabrication strategies remain limited in their ability to replicate such multiscale architecture. Top-down approaches such as 3D printing and electrospinning have demonstrated success in generating aligned collagen-based scaffolds, but often fail to recapitulate the highly ordered fibrillar organization across nanometer-to-millimeter multiscales [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. A promising and underexplored strategy is to build such hierarchical and gradient architectures from the bottom up, starting from molecular collagen assembly. Unlike top-down techniques, bottom-up fabrication offers the potential to reconstruct native tissue-like features through controlled self-organization of biopolymers, mimicking natural development and allowing fine-tuned spatial resolution at the nanoscale [26,27]. Such “de novo” strategies can provide more continuous transitions in both structure and function, and allow direct modulation of mineralization processes such as intrafibrillar minerals deposition, which is critical for bone integration [[28], [29], [30]]. However, achieving precise control over alignment, hierarchy, and mineral gradient in collagen systems via bottom-up assembly remains a major challenge.

In this study, we present an electrofabrication-guided approach to construct hierarchically aligned collagen matrices with spatially controlled mineral gradients, mimicking key structural and compositional features from tendon to bone. By applying low-voltage electrical signals (<9 V), we induce collagen assembly into a molten fibril state composed of 10 nm fibrils connected via reversible interactions [[30], [31], [32]]. Subsequent mechanical stretching and incubation in phosphate-buffered saline guide the transformation of molten fibrils into aligned fibers with multiscale organization resembling native tendon. At one end of the matrix, we introduce nanoscale intrafibrillar and interfibrillar HAp mineralization to replicate the enthesis gradient and create a hybrid interface supportive of bone ingrowth (Scheme 1b). This platform allows for the de novo reconstruction of a tendon-to-bone-like “ECM” with continuous structural and compositional features, offering both mechanical and biochemical cues for multiple-tissues integration (Scheme 1c). We further evaluated the performance of these de novo constructed biomimetic collagen-mineral matrices in promoting histological regeneration and restoring shoulder function in rotator cuff repair using a rabbit model. This study demonstrates a bottom-up strategy for creating multiscale biomimetic scaffolds and provides a promising platform for integrated soft-to-hard tissue regeneration.

Experimental section

Materials

Type I collagen of porcine skin origin, with its telopeptides enzymatically removed to minimize immunogenicity, was obtained from Shanghai Haohai Biotech Co., Ltd. Hydrogen peroxide, Na2HPO4·12H2O, NaCl, NH4HCO3, and ethanol were procured from Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Acetic acid and glutaraldehyde were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Prior to use, the collagen was purified to remove insoluble impurities and dialyzed (molecular weight cutoff = 7 kDa) against ultrapure water (Millipore Milli-Q Plus purification system) to eliminate soluble salts.

Electro-assembly of collagen

A 10 mg/mL Type I collagen solution was prepared by dissolving the purified collagen in ultrapure water, followed by dialysis against 0.5 M acetic acid for 24 h. The electrolyte for electro-assembly was formulated by adding hydrogen peroxide (30% w/v solution) to the dialyzed collagen solution to achieve a final concentration of 10% (v/v), with thorough stirring. Electro-assembly was performed using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation configured with a three-electrode system. The titanium sheet (2 cm × 3 cm) was used as the working electrode (cathode), platinum wire was used as the counter electrode (anode) and the Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode. The electrodes were immersed in the acidic collagen electrolyte (pH = 3.5). Under constant-current conditions (current density: 6.67 mA/cm2; duration: 3600 s), positively charged collagen molecules migrated towards the cathode and accumulated on its surface. Following assembly, the resulting collagen hydrogel matrix on the titanium substrate was rinsed extensively with ultrapure water. The coated titanium sheet was then immersed in anhydrous ethanol for 12 h. Finally, the collagen matrix was carefully peeled off the titanium sheet and stored in ethanol for subsequent processing.

Preparation of HA-Col

The electroassembled collagen hydrogel matrix (initial length: 10 mm) was subjected to uniaxial tensile strain (50-200%) using an ElectroForce 3200 testing instrument (TA Instruments, USA) to induce fibril alignment. To temporarily stabilize the aligned microstructure, strained matrices were immersed in anhydrous ethanol for 12 h. Subsequently, samples were transferred to a physiological buffer (0.1 M NaH2PO4, 0.14 M NaCl, pH 4.8) for 2 h while maintaining applied strain to preserve alignment, with continuous mechanical loading applied to both ends of the matrices. Fibrillogenesis and D-band formation were then induced by exposing the strained matrices to ammonia vapor for 24 h under sustained tension. Samples were air-dried under constant load. Dried collagen matrices were crosslinked in 1% (w/v) glutaraldehyde/90% (v/v) aqueous ethanol (30 min), followed by extensive washing in ultrapure water to remove residual crosslinker. Finally, crosslinked samples (i.e., HA-Cols) prepared at varying tensile strains (50%, 100%, 150%, 200%) and non-stretched RF-Col controls were obtained for structural and mechanical characterization.

Preparation of HAM-Col

This study compares the mineralization efficacy of five formulations (Table S1) on 200%-strained HA-Col, based on established methodologies. Specifically, HA-Col samples (200% strain) were immersed in mineralization solutions of distinct formulations (Table S1) and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. Throughout mineralization, solutions were replaced with fresh aliquots every 12 h. Post-mineralization, all materials were rinsed with ultrapure water to remove unbound minerals and dried overnight at 37 °C. The resulting mineralized samples (HAM-Col) were characterized for mineral composition and morphology to identify the optimal formulation.

Preparation and characterization of HAGM-Col

The mineralization precursor solution was formulated according to the optimized composition (Table S1). To establish a spatial mineralization gradient, the HA-Col matrix was vertically anchored at the base of a beaker. The precursor solution was continuously infused through a syringe pump at 0.5 mL/h, generating a diffusion-controlled concentration gradient along the longitudinal axis during 7-day mineralization at 37 °C. Solution replacement every 12 h maintained reaction dynamics. Post-mineralization, samples were rinsed and dried (37 °C) to yield the gradient mineralized collagen material (HAGM-Col).

Characterization of materials

The samples were freeze-dried until completely dry and then analyzed for surface morphology using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Helios G4 UC, USA). Specifically, the samples were mounted on flat stubs with conductive tape and sputter-coated with gold using an ion sputtering instrument for 30 s at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. High-resolution surface scanning was performed using an atomic force microscope (AFM, MFP-3D, UK). During the scanning process, a microprobe was used to detect the surface topology and mechanical properties, recording the surface height and modulus information at each location. The fiber morphology and mineral distribution within the samples were observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100, Japan). The samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in Eponate12 resin (Ted Pella, Redding, CA), and sectioned into thin slices with thicknesses of 60-90 nm, which were placed on a bare copper grid. Imaging was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. Polarized light microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Germany) and small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS, BL19U2, China) were used to investigate the orientation structure of the samples. For polarized light microscopy, images were taken in cross-polarization mode with the polarizer (P) and analyzer (A) perpendicular to each other, keeping the sample strain axis at a 45-degree angle to both the analyzer and the polarizer. SAXS data were obtained by illuminating the sample with an X-ray beam at a sample-to-detector distance of 1950 mm, with a 60-s exposure time to acquire scattering signals. The resulting two-dimensional SAXS data were processed using Fit2D software to average the one-dimensional scattering intensity curve as a function of the azimuthal angle. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG209F1, Germany) to determine the residual mass and calculate the mineral content in different samples. The heating process was conducted under a nitrogen flow of 20 ml/min, with the temperature raised from 20 °C to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The crystal structure of minerals in the mineralized samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Max2550VB, Japan). X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra of the samples were obtained, and the mineral crystal structure was analyzed using Jade software based on the XRD spectra. By comparing the results with standard HAp PDF cards, the crystalline phases present in the samples could be identified.

The crystallinity of mineral composition in different samples was calculated by Jade software. XRD analysis was performed using Cu Kα radiation (wavelength = 1.5406 Å), with the voltage and current set to 40 kV and 40 mA, respectively. The scanning range was 10° to 60°, and the scanning rate was 10°/min. The chemical composition and structure of samples were characterized by attenuated total reflection Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, Nicolet iS50, USA). The sample was placed directly on the ATR crystal, and infrared light was directed onto the sample surface. The area in contact with the ATR crystal absorbed the infrared light, producing specific absorption signals. The ATR-FTIR scanning range was 4000-400 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1. The static tensile properties of the samples at room temperature were measured using a dynamic biomechanical testing system (Electro-3220, USA). The materials were cut into rectangular strips (10 mm × 30 mm), and the thickness was measured by an electronic thickness gauge. For static mechanical testing, the tensile rate was set to 0.05 mm/s (0.17%/s). The strain of the sample was calculated by dividing the change in length at the break by the initial distance between the grips. The stress was calculated as the force applied to the sample divided by the initial cross-sectional area. The tensile fracture strength and elongation at break of samples at different stretching strains were determined from the stress-strain curves. Young's modulus was calculated from the slope of the initial linear region of the stress-strain curve. For Alizarin Red Staining, samples were immersed in 40 mM Alizarin Red solution (3 h), rinsed with ultrapure water, and air-dried for digital photographic documentation. For Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS), elemental distribution (Ca, C) and Ca/(Ca + C) atomic ratios were quantified at defined positions along the macro-axis. For Contact Angle Measurement, hydrophilicity was assessed by depositing 10 μL ultrapure water at gradient positions and measuring contact angles (JC2000D3 contact angle meter, China).

Cell culture in vitro

TSPCs adhesion

Samples were cut into 10 mm diameter circles and sterilized by irradiation for 48 h in 48-well plates. TSPCs were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/well onto the samples and cultured for 1 day at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 atmosphere. Then the seeded materials were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized in 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min. Cells were stained with Rhodamine-Phalloidin PBS solution for 20 min to visualize the cytoskeleton, followed by DAPI staining solution for 15 min to label cell nuclei. Each step was followed by washing with PBS for 10 min. Cell morphology was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Nikon A1R, Japan). Image J software was used for semi-quantitative the cell aspect ratio and area. Image J software was used to measure the orientation, area, aspect ratio and roundness of cells.

Differentiation of TSPCs into tenocytes

TSPCs were separately cultured on RF-Col and area III sample cut into 10 mm diameter circles and affixed to the bottom of 48-well plates (8.0 × 103 cells/well). For tenogenic differentiation, tendon-derived stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 (R&D Systems). After 14 days of culture, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin solution, and mRNA was extracted by TRIzol reagent (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA by test kit (iScript1TM cDNA Bio-Rad kit USA), and RT-PCR was performed by using a TB Green test kit (iTaq Universal SYBR Green, Bio-Rad, USA) as per the manufacturer's protocol (n = 3). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR, CFX Opus 384, USA) was used to analyze the expression of tendon differentiation-related genes (Col 1a1, Scx, Tnmd, Dcn, and Tnc). The primer sequences are detailed in Table S2.

Cell viability assay of BMSCs

Samples were cut into discs and placed in 48-well plates, then irradiated for 48 h. BMSCs were seeded at a density of 5.0 × 104 cells/well onto the samples and cultured for 7 days at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. After culturing, cells were stained with a Live/Dead staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) to observe cell growth states using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Nikon A1R, Japan). Additionally, a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to quantify LDH levels in BMSCs cultured on different sample surfaces for seven days to indicate cell viability.

Animal experiment

Establishment of rabbit tendon-bone defect model

Adult New Zealand white rabbits were used in this experiment. All rabbits and related anesthetic drugs were provided by the Shanghai Jiaotong University Agricultural and Life Sciences Practice Field Co., Ltd. All procedures and postoperative care protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and strictly followed the guidelines for the use and care of animals in research. A total of 96 adult male New Zealand rabbits weighing 2.8 ± 0.2 kg were randomly divided into four groups: Control group implanted with PET, RF-Col group, HA-Col group, and HAGM-Col group (10 mm in length, with a 7 mm non-mineralized III rea and a 3 mm mineralized II-b/II-a/I areas). Initially, a mixture of tiletamine hydrochloride and xylazine hydrochloride in a ratio of 8:1 was prepared as the anesthetic agent for rabbits, administered at 0.3 ml/kg body weight. After successful anesthesia, the fur was carefully shaved at the shoulder joint marking site on the left forelimb and disinfected with iodine solution. After laying surgical drapes, the acromial landmark of the rabbit was identified, and the position of the infraspinatus muscle was determined. A longitudinal incision was made along the infraspinatus muscle axis, then the skin was sequentially dissected. Subcutaneous tissue was freed using surgical operation to expose the tendon and its insertion point on the humeral head. The tendon was dissected from surrounding soft tissues using forceps and cut near the greater tubercle of the humeral head to create a 7 mm × 4 mm tendon defect. The remaining tendon ends were trimmed, and residual tendon and cartilage tissues at the insertion point of the infraspinatus tendon on the humeral head were further scraped off with a blade until the subchondral bone was exposed (depth of 3 mm). The collagen matrix materials were sutured and fixed to the remaining tendon end. Two parallel tunnels were created at the insertion area of the tendon on the humeral head's outer side. The suture at the distal end of the patch was passed through the tunnels and tied off, securing the patch to the insertion area of the tendon on the humeral head. The operation site was rinsed with saline, and the dissected muscle layers and skin were sutured layer by layer. Three disinfection procedures were implemented at the skin incision site. To prevent postoperative infection, rabbits were intramuscularly injected with penicillin for three days postoperatively. After surgery, rabbits were allowed free movement in cages, with free access to water and food.

Histological analysis in the rabbit model

Experimental rabbits were euthanized at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-surgery. Specimens were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 48 h and decalcified in EDTA solution for one month. Fully decalcified specimens were dehydrated in a gradient ethanol series (30%-100%), infiltrated with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut along the tendon-to-bone direction. Histological scoring was performed to assess the tissue quality in each group. Tissue sections were subjected to H&E staining, safranine-o-fast green staining, and immunohistochemical staining for type I collagen and type III collagen. Immunohistochemical staining for type I collagen and type III collagen was performed to observe the expression of collagen proteins in the repair tissue, evaluating the maturity of regenerated tendons. Stained sections were observed under an optical microscope, and images of tissue pathology were captured by digital camera. The proportion of fibrocartilaginous tissue at the tendon-bone interface was measured and calculated based on safranine-o-fast green staining results by Image J. Additionally, Image J software was used to measure and calculate the percentage of positive areas in immunohistochemical staining micrographs for type I collagen and type III collagen. Samples were dehydrated and embedded in methyl methacrylate, and sectioned at 500 μm to make Tissue sections. After grinding and polishing, the sections were stained with BMP 2 of osteoblasts, Aggrecan of chondrocytes and Tenascin C antibody of tendon cells by immunofluorescence (JiaJun Bio, China).

Micro-CT evaluation in the rabbit model

Specimens of the shoulder cuff tendon-humerus composite from each group were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Following fixation, samples were scanned and imaged by Micro-CT (Zeiss 520 Versa). Imaging was conducted at 4 weeks and 12 weeks post-implantation. Three-dimensional reconstruction, screenshots and data measurement were performed from both sides of the infraspinatus tendon of the humerus head to the lateral humerus head. The analysis parameters included bone mineral density (BMD, g/cm3), bone volume/total integral number (BV/TV, %) and trabecular number (Tb.N, mm−1) (n = 4). (defined ROI region as shown in Fig. S9). For high-resolution Micro-CT scanning and imaging, each composite was initially sectioned into small blocks of 5 mm per side using an electric saw. Sections were then fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by a 5-h rinse in ultrapure water. Subsequently, samples were immersed in Lugol's iodine solution for 48 h to enhance soft tissue contrast, rinsed in 70% ethanol, and stored in anhydrous ethanol until Micro-CT scanning. Data collected from Micro-CT scans were reconstructed using the XMR Reconstructor software provided with the scanner, followed by manual center-offset correction.

Biomechanical testing in the rabbit model

All biomechanical tests were conducted using a materials testing system (Instron-5566, USA). Four specimens of the rabbit shoulder cuff tendon-humerus composite were tested per group. Specimens prepared for biomechanical testing retained the upper limb bones and the tendon for clamping with other soft tissues removed. Prior to testing, specimens were placed at room temperature for 1 h. To minimize slippage, the humeral head and upper limb bones were secured in a custom mold with dental stone added to enhance grip. The tendon was sutured with medical gauze to increase friction, and then secured for testing. A preload of 1 N was applied at a testing speed of 5 mm/min (strain rate of 0.8%/s) before subjecting the shoulder cuff specimens to 20 cycles of preconditioning. Ultimate failure load (N), ultimate failure stress (MPa), and stiffness (N/mm) were recorded and calculated. Stiffness (N/mm) for each sample was calculated from the linear region of the load-deformation curve between the maximum load and failure point.

Motor ability tests in the rabbit model

At 12 weeks post-surgery, gait analysis was performed on all groups of experimental rabbits by using an animal gait analysis system. The system consists of a glass runway equipped with a high-definition camera on one side to capture and record the gait of the test animals. Adjacent to the apparatus, a feeding box encourages the animals to traverse the glass runway quickly which facilitates the experiments. Before gait analysis testing, animals were trained for one week to eliminate environmental and habituation factors that could interfere with their gait. The gait analysis system automatically identifies, analyzes, and calculates parameters such as posture and speed of rabbit footprints.

Statistical analysis

All data were represented as mean standard deviation (SD), and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc tests. Significance was set at p < 0.05 (∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05), while p > 0.05 indicated no statistical significance (N.S.)

Results and discussion

Synergistic electroassembly and post-treatment guide hierarchical collagen alignment with strong mechanics

Here we report a bottom-up strategy for the de novo fabrication of hierarchically aligned fibrillar collagen matrices starting from collagen molecules, aiming to better recapitulate the native microstructure of tendon tissue. As described previously, an externally applied electric field drives positively charged collagen molecules (in solution at pH 3.5) towards the cathode, where they accumulate. The localized increase in pH near the cathode (due to electrochemical generation of OH− ions by electrolyzing the additionally added hydrogen peroxide in the electrolyte, 2H2O2 + 4e− → 4OH−) induces the accumulated collagen molecules to self-assemble into nanofibrils (∼10 nm diameter). These nanofibrils further organize through reversible interfibrillar interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions) to form a free-standing hydrogel matrices (see Fig. S1a), which is stabilized by entanglement of high-density microfibrils, thereby forming a water-insoluble network [3,30]. Importantly, the electro-assembled collagen nanofibrils matrices, characterized by partial alignment and assembly with reversible interconnections, allows for the induction of further high-degree alignment and hierarchical assembly by modulating intermolecular interactions. Ionic strength is a key parameter governing collagen intermolecular interactions. We found that briefly treating the electro-assembled collagen hydrogel matrices with a solution containing sodium chloride (0.14 M) and sodium phosphate (0.1 M; pH adjusted to ∼4.8, near collagen's isoelectric point) initiated further hierarchical assembly, including the development of characteristic D-periodicity and fibrils thickening. During this critical assembly phase, continuous external traction was applied to maintain nanofibril alignment within the hydrogel. Subsequently, the hydrogel film was dried to densify the microstructure and then covalently crosslinked (1% w/v glutaraldehyde) to permanently fix the hierarchically aligned fibrillar architecture, yielding the final collagen matrices (designated as “HA-Col”), as shown in Fig. 1a. The mechanical stretching applied during preparation, which induces plastic deformation, directly modulates the fibrillar organization of HA-Col. It should be noted that this irrecoverable strain arises from the sliding and re-anchoring of adjacent fibrils through dynamic breaking and regeneration of non-covalent interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions). To investigate this, a series of HA-Cols was fabricated under varying levels of applied strain (50%, 100%, 150%, 200%), while collagen films prepared identically but without mechanical stretching (i.e., 0% applied strain) served as the control (designated “RF-Col” for randomly arranged fibrous collagen). All dry samples appeared milky white and opaque, with elongation increasing with strain (see Fig. S1c). The microstructural evolution across length scales (millimeters to nanometers) was characterized using three complementary techniques. Polarized optical microscopy (POM) images (Fig. 1b) revealed significant birefringence in all HA-Cols, with interference color intensity increasing with applied strain, indicating enhanced uniaxial orientation of collagen along the stretching direction. In contrast, RF-Col showed weak birefringence. Low-magnification SEM imaging (Fig. 1c) demonstrated that the film surfaces developed a fibrous morphology dependent on strain. Notably, the 200%-strained HA-Col exhibited highly aligned micron-scale fibers (∼10 μm diameter) along the stretch direction (white arrows). RF-Col displayed a relatively random fibrous surface. High-magnification SEM (Fig. 1d) further revealed that these surfaces were composed of nanoscale fibrils exhibiting D-periodicity (as shown in the inset at the lower left corner). Crucially, fibril packing density and alignment (red arrows indicate direction) within the fibers progressively increased with strain. The 200%-strained HA-Col consisted of densely packed, highly aligned nanofibrils with clear D-banding. Conversely, RF-Col was formed by loosely and randomly arranged fibrils [See Fig. S1d for Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) surface topography]. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) provided quantitative structural insights. The 2D SAXS pattern of RF-Col (Fig. 1e) showed a nearly isotropic ring, confirming its random structure. HA-Cols exhibited increasingly anisotropic scattering patterns (elliptical/arc-like features) with strain, consistent with progressive fibril alignment. The degree of orientation was quantified by the Herman's orientation parameter (ƒc) [30,33,34], calculated from azimuthal intensity distributions (Fig. S1e and equations Equation 1, Equation 2) in Supplementary Information). ƒc ranges from −0.5 (perfect perpendicular uniaxial alignment) to 1 (perfect parallel uniaxial alignment), specifically while ƒc = 0 indicates isotropic (random) orientation. RF-Col had a low ƒc (0.18), while ƒc for HA-Cols significantly increased with strain, reaching a maximum of 0.94 for the 200%-strained sample (values inset in Fig. 1e). Besides, polarized light microscopy was performed to evaluate the uniformity of fiber alignment across HA-Col after mechanical stretching, and representative images from different regions are shown in Supplementary Information of Fig. S1f. Furthermore, both the 2D SAXS patterns and the corresponding 1D intensity profiles (Fig. 1f, q-range: 0.2-1.2 nm−1) for HA-Cols and RF-Col exhibited a characteristic scattering ring/peak corresponding to a D-periodicity of 64.6 nm (calculated via the Bragg equation (3) in Supplementary Information), matching the 64-67 nm periodicity characteristic of native tendon collagen.

Uniaxial tensile testing confirmed that the aligned microstructure translated to enhanced mechanical properties along the alignment direction. Representative stress-strain curves (Fig. 1g) and quantitative analysis of tensile strength and Young's modulus (Fig. 1h) demonstrated a significant positive correlation between mechanical properties and the degree of alignment (i.e., applied strain). The HA-Col fabricated at 200% strain exhibited the highest dry-state tensile strength (95.97 ± 1.6 MPa) and Young's modulus (5.06 ± 0.07 GPa), values well within the range reported for native tendon tissues (55-120 MPa) [[35], [36], [37]]. Based on its superior microstructure characterized and related-properties by the highest fibril alignment, densely packed and uniformly oriented fibrils, and well-preserved D-periodicity, combined with its enhanced mechanical performance, the 200%-strained HA-Col was selected for all subsequent studies.

HA-Col biomimetic mineralization further enhances mechanical properties of hierarchically aligned collagen matrices

Mineralization represents a sophisticated functionalization strategy in nature, where the organized interplay between organic templates and inorganic minerals yields exceptional material properties. For instance, the hierarchical integration of collagen and HAp platelets in bone tissue underpins its load-bearing capacity and regenerative potential. Biomimetic mineralization aims to replicate this natural process in vitro by infiltrating collagen with amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) precursors, facilitating intrafibrillar nucleation and crystallization, ultimately forming collagen-HAp composites resembling native bone [38,39]. Herein, we applied biomimetic mineralization to HA-Col to enhance its mechanical compatibility with bone tissue and potentially confer biological functions conducive to tendon-to-bone regeneration [40].

Fig. 2a schematically illustrates the biomimetic mineralization process, depicting ACP infiltration into collagen fibrils and subsequent intrafibrillar minerals crystallization. While collagen fibrils/fibers serve as natural templates for HAp deposition, type I collagen itself, primarily composed of glycine (Gly), proline (Pro), and hydroxyproline (Hyp) residues [41,42], possesses limited intrinsic capacity to induce ACP mineralization due to the neutral and inert nature of these dominant amino acids [[43], [44], [45]]. To overcome this limitation, a strategy leveraging the polymer-induced liquid-precursor (PILP) process, which uses polyelectrolytes to stabilize ACP as a fluidic phase capable of infiltrating collagen's gap zones prior to crystallization. Additives such as polyaspartic acid (pAsp), citrate, or low molecular weight polyacrylic acid (PAA) are typical examples. These molecules provide carboxyl groups that act as nucleation sites, reduce the ACP-collagen interfacial energy, promote ACP infiltration into fibrils, and enhance crystallization, thereby boosting the degree of mineralization [46,47]. Consequently, identifying an optimal mineralization formulation is crucial. We systematically evaluated several literature-reported formulations [28,[48], [49], [50], [51]], (summarized in Fig. S2a and Table S1 in Supplementary Information). Briefly, HA-Col samples were immersed in these solutions for designated period, enabling ACP infiltration and crystallization to yield mineralized constructs designated ”HAM-Col”. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. S2b–c) and Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Fig. S2d–e) were used to assess mineral content and crystallinity, respectively. Specific formulation (i.e., V in Fig. S2a and Table S1) was selected for subsequent studies based on its superior mineral yield and well-defined relatively high crystallinity. Structural characterization confirmed successful mineralization while preserving alignment. Polarized optical microscopy (Fig. 2b) revealed retained strong birefringence in HAM-Col, indicating persistence of long-range collagen alignment. SEM imaging (Fig. 2c) visually confirmed the localized mineral formation (blue box). Besides, unstained TEM cross-sections of HAM-Col (Fig. 2d) exhibited abundant electron-dense regions, potentially attributed to the significant intrafibrillar deposition of HAp (high electron scattering mass) [52,53]. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Fig. 2e) showed characteristic collagen amide peaks (green squares) in both HA-Col and HAM-Col, while HAM-Col additionally displayed distinct minerals peaks, notably the PO43− ν4 bending mode at 713 cm−1 (blue square). The XRD analysis (Fig. 2f) further confirmed that the minerals present in HAM-Col exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks similar to those of HAp, with clear characteristic diffraction peaks absent in the smooth XRD profile of HA-Col. Biomimetic mineralization also significantly altered surface properties: HAM-Col exhibited markedly improved hydrophilicity (contact angle: [42.83 ± 2.02]° vs [83.67 ± 3.26]° for HA-Col; Fig. 2g) and increased surface stiffness (reduced modulus: [15.10 ± 0.31] GPa vs [7.17 ± 0.28] GPa for HA-Col; Fig. 2h). The improvement in mechanical properties of HAM-Col upon mineralization is intuitive, and the enhanced hydrophilicity of HAM-Col is primarily potentially attributed to two synergistic factors: First, the introduction of HAp minerals enriches the collagen surface with hydrophilic phosphate and hydroxyl groups (Fig. 2e and f), which promote hydrogen bonding with water [54]; and second ACP infiltration and intrafibrillar crystallization induce nano-micro-scale topographic roughening that reduces water contact angle via capillary effects [55].

Most significantly, mineralization profoundly enhanced the bulk mechanical properties. Representative tensile stress-strain curves (Fig. 2i) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 2j and k) demonstrated that HAM-Col achieved substantially higher tensile strength compared to HA-Col, without a significant compromise in failure strain. This combination (increased strength without pronounced embrittlement) is highly desirable for organic-inorganic composites and is likely attributable to the nanoscale integration of HAp within the collagen fibrils, which mitigates stress concentration at the organic-inorganic interface. Furthermore, biomimetic mineralization significantly boosted both the Young's modulus and toughness of HAM-Col (Fig. 2k). The Young's modulus of HAM-Col reached about 7 GPa, approaching values characteristic of human cancellous bone (typically ranging from 0.1 to 3 GPa, highly dependent on density and location) [4], and suggesting enhanced mechanical compatibility with bony regions. In summary, the results presented in Fig. 2 demonstrate that biomimetic mineralization of HA-Col successfully preserves its critical hierarchically aligned fibrillar architecture while generating a nanoscale collagen-minerals (similar with HAp) composite (HAM-Col). This integration synergistically maximizes key mechanical properties (strength, modulus, toughness) and surface characteristics, potentially emulating physicochemical attributes relevant to native bone tissue.

Gradient biomimetic mineralization induces spatially varied physicochemical properties in HA-Col

The gradient in mineral content is fundamental to the seamless integration of natural tendon-to-bone interfaces. This gradient enables a continuous spatial transition in mechanical stiffness, mitigating stress concentrations and facilitating efficient mechanical load transfer between soft and hard tissues. Replicating such mineral gradients within implants offers the dual benefit of accommodating the native stiffness transition and providing heterogeneous physicochemical cues (e.g., stiffness, composition) to guide spatially organized cellular phenotypes for tendon-to-bone reconstruction. Controlling the spatiotemporal infiltration of the mineralization solution into scaffolds presents a viable strategy for generating mineral gradients within HA-Col [56,57].

To implement this strategy, HA-Col strips (10 mm length x 3 mm width) were vertically immersed in a container, aligned parallel to their primary fibril orientation. Mineralization solution was continuously infused into the container at a constant rate of 0.5 mL/h using a syringe pump over 7 days, causing the liquid level to rise progressively. Consequently, the bottom ∼7 mm segment of each HA-Col scaffold experienced varying durations of exposure to the solution: maximum exposure at the bottom and minimum exposure at the ∼7 mm height point. The top ∼3 mm segment remained unexposed, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Since mineral deposition correlates positively with exposure time, this setup generated a continuous gradient in mineral content along the scaffold's length (alignment direction), yielding gradient mineralized HA-Col (designated as “HAGM-Col”).Fig. 3Gradient biomimetic mineralization induces spatially varied physicochemical properties of Hierarchically Aligned Collagen. The presence of a mineral composition gradient in HAGM-Col was first confirmed visually using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, which forms a red complex with calcium ions (Fig. 3b). HAGM-Col exhibited a clear gradient in red intensity along its length, indicative of decreasing calcium/mineral content from bottom to top. Based on the distinct ARS staining profiles, three representative regions were defined for detailed analysis: Region I (bottom ∼3 mm, intense uniform red, high mineral), Region II (middle ∼4 mm, gradient red, gradient mineral), and Region III (top ∼3 mm, unstained, unmineralized).

Multi-scale characterization confirmed the gradient structure. Low- and high-magnification SEM (Fig. 3c and d) revealed stark differences: Region I surface was densely covered with clustered mineral deposits; Region II displayed a visible mineral gradient, with abundant crystals near Region I (II-a, orange box/arrows) diminishing towards Region III (II-b, yellow box/arrows); Region III showed a smooth, unmineralized surface with aligned fibrillar morphology. Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 3e) and quantitative line scans (Fig. S3) confirmed decreasing calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) content from Region I to Region III, with a clear gradient within Region II. The Ca/P ratio was ∼1.67, which is very close to that of hydroxyapatite. Crucially, the Ca/(Ca + C) atomic ratio (reflecting relative mineral to organic content) decreased progressively from ≈80% at the bottom to undetectable levels at the top (Fig. 3g), unequivocally demonstrating a compositional minerals gradient.

Given the established link between local surface stiffness and stem cell behavior [56,[58], [59], [60], [61]], we quantified the spatial variation in surface mechanical properties using atomic force microscopy (AFM). AFM mapping (Fig. 3f) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 3h) revealed a significant gradient in surface modulus (e.g., reduced modulus), decreasing from approximately 15 GPa in highly mineralized Region I to ∼7 GPa in less mineralized areas, closely tracking the mineral content gradient. Contact angle measurements (Fig. 3i) also demonstrated a wettability gradient, with increased hydrophilicity correlating with higher local mineralization. Cyclic tensile testing of HAGM-Col for 1000 cycles from 0 to 60 MPa confirmed its elastic behavior and excellent fatigue resistance, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S11.

In summary, comprehensive characterization (ARS, SEM, EDS, TEM, AFM, contact angle) consistently verifies the successful creation of a continuous gradient in mineralization content, distributed both superficially and intrafibrillarly within HAGM-Col. This engineered compositional gradient directly translates into concomitant gradients in key surface properties, namely stiffness and wettability, mimicking essential physicochemical aspects of the native tendon-to-bone transition. Importantly, this gradient mineralized architecture also confers superior mechanical performance compared to biphasic mineralized controls, as demonstrated in Fig. S4 of the Supplementary Information. And, the in vitro degradation of collagen matrices with varying mineralization degrees is presented in Fig. S5 of the Supplementary Information.

We then assessed cellular responses on the distinct sample regions. Non-mineralized Area III, resembling native tendon in composition and possessing a biomimetically aligned fibrous morphology, is anticipated to provide a conducive microenvironment for tendon repair. To demonstrate the specific advantage of its aligned morphology, RF-Col with randomly arranged fibrous structure (prepared as in Fig. 1) served as the control. We first evaluated initial cell adhesion on Area III and RF-Col. Rat tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) were seeded onto both surfaces. Confocal imaging (Fig. 4b and c) revealed distinct TSPC morphologies after 24 h: TSPCs on RF-Col displayed a star-shaped, isotropic morphology, whereas those on Area III adopted an elongated, spindle-like shape. Quantitative analysis of actin cytoskeleton orientation (Fig. 4b) further demonstrated this difference: TSPCs on RF-Col showed no preferred directionality, while those on Area III exhibited a highly aligned orientation, predominantly within 110°-130°. This confirms that TSPCs on Area III achieved significant directional alignment. Cell elongation was further quantified by measuring aspect ratio and area using actin-stained cytoskeletons. TSPCs on Area III achieved a significantly higher aspect ratio (∼14) compared to those on RF-Col (∼1.85) (Fig. 4d). Correspondingly, both cell adhesion area (Fig. 4d) and roundness (Fig. S6b) were significantly lower on Area III than on RF-Col. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TSPCs sense and rapidly respond to the aligned fibrillar morphology of Area III upon initial attachment, adopting a characteristic elongated shape.

Cell shape is an established intrinsic regulator of stem cell differentiation [31,62,63]. Notably, in this study we focused on whether elongated cell morphology contributes to tenogenic differentiation. While elongated morphology has also been associated with alternative lineage commitments (e.g., osteogenic differentiation) in certain contexts, this divergence in differentiation outcomes can be attributed to cell type and the distinct mechanotransduction pathways involved [[64], [65], [66]]. In tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs), elongation activates ROCK/TGF-β signaling, which promotes tenogenic differentiation [[67], [68], [69]].

To investigate the impact of cell shape (induced by Area III vs. RF-Col) on TSPC tenogenic differentiation, we quantified tendon-related gene expression via qRT-PCR after 14 days of culture. We analyzed key tendon markers: structural collagens (Col1a1, Col3a1) [51,70], transcription factors essential for development (Scx, Tnmd, Mkx) [71,72], and proteoglycans regulating collagen organization (Dcn, Tnc) [51,70]. qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4e) revealed significantly higher expression of these tendon-related genes in elongated TSPCs on Area III compared to RF-Col after 14 days. Specifically, Col1a1, Scx, Tnmd, Dcn, and Tnc expression was upregulated 4- to 10-fold in the Area III group. These gene expression results confirm that the aligned fibrous structure of Area III promotes TSPC tenogenic differentiation, likely mediated by the induced elongated cell morphology. The mineralized regions of HAGM-Col anticipated to provide a conducive physicochemical microenvironment for chondrogenic and osteogenic cells, supporting fibrocartilage and bone repair. To assess the impact of regional mineralization on BMSC behavior, rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were seeded onto the distinct mineralized regions. Following 7-day culture in proliferation medium, cell viability was assessed via live/dead staining. Results revealed high confluency of viable cells (green) on all surfaces, with minimal dead cells (red) detected. Quantitative analysis confirmed consistently high BMSC viability across all regions after 7 days(Fig. S6c–d).

Further investigation was conducted on the effects of different areas of HAGM-Col on the specific differentiation of BMSCs by seeding cells on the surfaces of different samples. After culturing the cells for 14 days, chondrogenic and osteogenic markers were detected (note: Chondrogenic or osteogenic induction media was incorporated in the cultivation process to provide the background cues for inducing differentiation, and the differences in the differentiation effects of cells in each group were observed), as shown in Fig. 4f. Results showed that the highest expression of the chondrogenic marker glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and the osteogenic marker alkaline phosphatase (ALP) occurred when BMSCs were seeded on the surfaces of Area II-b and Area I, respectively. Specifically, in Alcian Blue (AB) staining (AB can form a blue complex with GAG), the deepest blue was observed on the surface of Area II-b, indicating the strongest effect on promoting chondrogenesis under chondrogenic induction media (Fig. 4g). Meanwhile, ALP staining results indicated that Area I had the greatest effect on promoting osteogenesis under the osteogenic induction media culturing (Fig. 4h). The corresponding semi-quantitative data below AB staining and ALP staining further indicate that the gradient mineralized area of HAGM-Col has the ability to induce BMSCs to express GAG and ALP in a gradient manner. To further validate the spatially guided differentiation capacity of HAGM-Col in a more clinically relevant format using intact continuous scaffolds, BMSCs were seeded onto undivided HAGM-Col scaffolds, and the results also confirmed region-specific differentiation, as shown in Fig. S10 in Supplementary Information. As the degree of mineralization increases, the gradient of GAG expression decreases, and instead, the expression of ALP increases. The quantitative analysis of ALP activity shown in Fig. S6e further confirmed the gradient osteogenic differentiation effect of BMSCs in different mineralized areas on HAGM-Col. We next quantified chondrogenic (Sox9, Aggrecan, Col2a1) and osteogenic (Ocn, Runx2, BMP2) gene expression across the mineralized gradient regions (Areas I, II-a, II-b) of HAGM-Col. Gene expression exhibited a mineralization-dependent gradient: chondrogenic markers decreased while osteogenic markers increased from low-mineralized (Area II-b) to high-mineralized (Area I) regions (Fig. 4i), demonstrating HAGM-Col's capacity for spatially guided chondrogenic or osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Interestingly, all three chondrogenic markers showed slightly elevated expression in Area I compared to Area II-a, which may be attributed to the transitional nature of the highly mineralized region. This modest elevation could reflect an early endochondral ossification-like state, in which cells transiently maintain certain chondrogenic markers before fully committing to osteogenesis [73,74]. Nevertheless, the chondrogenic markers remain highest in Area II-b and progressively decrease toward Area I. Under lineage-specific induction media, BMSCs on low-mineralized surfaces (Area II-b) exhibited enhanced chondrogenesis, whereas increasing mineralization progressively weakened chondrogenic potential and strengthened osteogenic capacity. The mineralized components recapitulate native tissue's biophysical/biochemical microenvironment to direct cell fate. This is mechanistically supported by established principles: Low substrate stiffness favors chondrogenesis [[75], [76], [77], [78]], while high stiffness and elevated calcium/phosphate promote osteogenesis [79,80]. Potentially, the gradient intrafibrillar/interfibrillar mineralization in HAGM-Col provides region-optimized stiffness and bioactive ion release (e.g., Ca2+/PO43−) to drive lineage-specific differentiation. We have conducted additional experiments to quantify the release of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ from the scaffolds and to evaluate their potential cytotoxic effects. Experimentally, for ion release analysis, samples from separated the three distinct regions (I, II-a, and II-b) were incubated in PBS at 37°C to simulate physiological conditions. We observed that the net release of PO₄³⁻ increased over time, with region I showing the highest cumulative release (∼185 mg/L, equivalent to ∼1.95 mM as PO₄), followed by region II-a and then region II-b. In contrast, the net release of Ca²⁺ remained consistently low across all time points (maximum ≤ 0.7 mg/L, equivalent to ≤ 0.02 mM). These values fall within the typical low-millimolar ionic range of standard cell culture media. Quantitative LDH assay results showed no significant differences among the groups (cells cultured with extracts from regions I, II-a, II-b, and the control group with standard culture medium). The observed ion release levels are well below concentrations reported to cause cytotoxic effects. Previous studies have shown that extracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations up to 6–20 mM can promote mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and migration without reducing viability [81], which is substantially higher than the ≤0.02 mM net Ca²⁺ release detected in our study. Similarly, significant phosphate-induced cytotoxicity has typically been associated with inorganic phosphorus concentrations ≥40 mM [82], whereas the maximum net PO₄³⁻ release in our study was approximately 2 mM. The results of Fig. S6f–i in the Supplementary Information present the cumulative release profiles of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ from the three scaffold regions (I, II‑a, II‑b) over 7 days, along with the corresponding LDH cytotoxicity assay data.

In summary, the results in Fig. 4 indicate that different regions of HAGM-Col exhibit well biocompatibility, supporting the adhesion and sustained proliferation of cells related to tendon and bone healing. Additionally, these results may suggest that HAGM-Col allowed to provide different local physicochemical environment that can spatially and selectively promote cell differentiation toward tendon, cartilage or bone, potentially reconstructing the gradient cell phenotype of the tendon to bone interface tissue.

Histological assessment focused on tendon defect repair. H&E staining (Fig. 5b) showed implants in all groups remained largely intact at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery ("M" in low-mag images), indicating incomplete degradation. At 4 weeks, the PET group exhibited minimal new tissue formation. In contrast, all three collagen-based material groups (RF-Col, HA-Col, HAGM-Col) displayed greater amounts of newly formed tissue. Critically, newly formed tendon adjacent to HA-Col and HAGM-Col implants showed significantly more regular morphology and better alignment (parallel to yellow arrows) compared to the disorganized tissue near RF-Col implants. By 8 weeks, this aligned tissue within the HA-Col and HAGM-Col groups appeared denser, while tissue in the RF-Col group remained irregularly arranged. Immunohistochemical staining for collagen types I and III (Fig. 5c and d) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 5f and g) assessed newly formed tendon maturity. Reflecting typical tendon healing, collagen III expression dominated at 4 weeks across all groups. By 8 weeks, collagen III significantly decreased in the RF-Col, HA-Col, and HAGM-Col groups, concomitant with increased collagen I expression. Notably, both HA-Col and HAGM-Col groups exhibited significantly higher collagen I levels at 8 weeks compared to the RF-Col and PET groups (Fig. 5f), suggesting enhanced maturation. Immunofluorescence for Tenascin-C (TNC), a mature tendon marker, provided further evidence (Fig. 5e). Weak TNC signal was present in collagen groups at 4 weeks, exceeding negligible levels in the PET group. By 8 weeks, robust TNC accumulation was evident within the aligned newly formed tendon of HA-Col and HAGM-Col groups, quantitatively confirmed to be significantly higher than in other groups (Fig. 5h).

Finally, newly formed tendon maturity was scored using established criteria (Table S4) integrating all histological parameters (H&E morphology/inflammation, collagen I/III ratio, TNC expression). Consistent with other findings, the HA-Col and HAGM-Col groups achieved the highest maturity scores at 8 weeks, significantly surpassing both the PET and RF-Col groups (Fig. 5i). [Histological results shown in Fig. S7 at 12 weeks confirmed sustained morphology and maturity levels comparable to 8 weeks].

Overall, these results demonstrate that collagen-based matrices promote regeneration within the tendon defect. Crucially, the preserved aligned fibrillar architecture within HA-Col and HAGM-Col implants directly facilitated the deposition of well-organized, aligned fibrous ECM in the newly formed tendon, thereby accelerating its maturation compared to randomly structured collagen (RF-Col) or the synthetic polyethylene terephthalate graft (PET).

HAGM-Col enhances functional recovery of shoulder movement in a rabbit rotator cuff injury model

Finally, kinematic analysis and biomechanical tests were used to further evaluate the functional recovery of the rabbit injured shoulder joint and the bonding strength of the tendon-bone insertion in rabbits 12 weeks post-surgery. The kinematic analysis of rabbits was conducted by evaluating their movement behavior on a treadmill. Specifically, the real-time movement process of the rabbits was recorded, and their motion was continuously captured using a high-speed imaging system. Qualitative and quantitative data analyses were performed based on the movement trajectories of the rabbits' left forelimbs, including the shoulder joint and other key motion-related parts. Fig. 8a visually presents representative photographs of the animals' movement processes in different groups (Supplementary Movie, in Supplementary Information). It was observed that while rabbits in all groups were able to perform basic movements on the treadmill 12 weeks post-surgery, there were differences in their motor abilities during the exercise. Among them, animals in the control group implanted with PET moved along the track without significant off-ground jumping behavior. In contrast, animals implanted with RF-Col, HA-Col, and HAGM-Col were able to perform off-ground jumps on the treadmill, though there were differences in the range of motion between groups. Fig. 8b further illustrates the relative positions of the shoulder cuff (marked with yellow dots) and other key parts related to forelimb movement (i.e., ankle, elbow, toes and neck, as shown in Fig. 8c) through continuous motion capture (each interval being one frame). The results showed that animals in the Control group exhibited relatively dense motion trajectories between consecutive intervals with movement at the lowest altitude space, indicating relatively slow movement speed and weaker jumping ability. In contrast, the three groups implanted with RF-Col, HA-Col and HAGM-Col showed relatively loose motion trajectories with movement at higher altitude spaces, indicating enhanced movement speed and jumping ability in these animals. Among them, the group implanted with HAGM-Col observed the best recovery in motor ability. The quantitative analysis of animal movement speed (Fig. 8d) and the highest body height during movement (Fig. 8e) further confirmed these results. The results showed that the group implanted with HAGM-Col achieved the maximum range of angular changes of the shoulder cuff relative to the neck and elbow during movement, confirming the best recovery of shoulder joint flexibility. Conclusion

In summary, we report the de novo construction of biomimetic collagen-mineral matrices that mimic both the hierarchical organization and mineral gradient distribution of the native tendon-to-bone ECM, aimed at reconstruction from tendon to bone. Firstly, this material has a hierarchically aligned fiber structure highly consistent with natural tendon tissue, exhibiting excellent mechanical properties and providing topographical guidance for directional cell growth and alignment, promoting tenogenic differentiation of TSPCs. Additionally, the gradient distribution of intra- and inter-fibrillar HAp in the material potentially simulate the layered spatial distribution of minerals in bone, ensuring a smooth transition of mechanical properties from relatively flexible soft tissue to stiff hard tissue. This also provides a gradient biochemical and biophysical microenvironment that spatially selectively promotes the differentiation of BMSCs into cartilage or bone, facilitating the reconstruction of the gradient cellular phenotypes of native insertion tissue. This biomimetic matrices achieved synchronous regeneration from tendon to bone in an in vivo rabbit model. The newly formed tendon tissue exhibited mature collagen expression and tissue alignment, while the reconstructed insertion tissue formed mature fibrocartilage, demonstrating a permeable and tight connection with the regenerated bone tissue. More encouragingly, the implantation of this material significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of the healed injury site and the recovery of animal motor function. Notably, the biomimetic collagen with hierarchical structure and a gradient in mineral content developed in this work probably not limited to tendon-bone interface repair. Given that native osteochondral interfaces also exhibit a continuous mineral gradient and hierarchical structural transition from non-mineralized cartilage to mineralized cartilage and subchondral bone, the method to fabricate hierarchically aligned, gradient-mineralized collagen matrices potentially offer a universal biomimetic technological path to recapitulate the extracellular matrix features of the osteochondral niche.

Despite these encouraging results, this study has several limitations. First, although the current fabrication strategy enables gradient mineralization and hierarchical alignment, it still relies on stepwise assembly and time-consuming mineralization steps, which may limit its scalability. Moreover, while this study establishes the feasibility of a gradient-mineralized scaffold for enthesis regeneration, the gradient mineralization technique requires further optimization to more precisely recapitulate the physiological sub-millimeter scale of mineral gradients at native entheses, while preserving its mechanical and inductive properties. Future work will focus on refining the fabrication process to achieve this scale and on evaluating the scaffolds' performance when the gradient is compressed to physiological scales in a large-animal model to confirm their translational potential. Although this work demonstrates the beneficial effects of biomimetic extracellular matrix physicochemical features on promoting tendon-to-bone healing, given the requirement of long-term stability of the gradient interface under physiological loading for achieving high-quality healing outcomes, it remains necessary to incorporate additional pro-healing biological cues (e.g., promoting vascularization to enhance the speed and efficacy of tendon-bone integration). Such efforts will further advance the clinical translation of this biomimetic platform material. While the current study demonstrates that the mineralization gradient induces region-specific differentiation, the underlying mechanotransduction mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Future work should systematically investigate how gradient-mediated changes in substrate stiffness and mineral exposure regulate key signaling pathways, including YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation and integrin-FAK activation, thereby providing mechanistic insight into how cells sense and respond to the graded cues. Besides, the potential immunomodulatory role of this gradient-mineralized aligned collagen matrix constitutes an important direction for future research. While the current study focused on tissue regeneration outcomes, subsequent work should incorporate quantitative assessment of macrophage polarization markers (e.g., CD86 for M1, CD206 for M2) at the tissue-implant interface, as well as in vitro co-culture models to elucidate how specific gradient regions influence local immune behavior. Such studies would provide deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying long-term scaffold integration and guide further optimization of gradient biomaterials for enthesis repair. Although this work demonstrates the beneficial effects of biomimetic extracellular matrix physicochemical features on promoting tendon-to-bone healing, given the requirement of long-term stability of the gradient interface under physiological loading for achieving high-quality healing outcomes, it remains necessary to incorporate additional pro-healing biological cues (e.g., promoting vascularization to enhance the speed and efficacy of tendon-bone integration). Such efforts will further advance the clinical translation of this biomimetic platform material. Overall, this work presents a bottom-up biomimetic strategy to engineering multiscale collagen-based scaffolds and demonstrates the therapeutic potential of de novo constructed matrices for multiple tissue regeneration.
