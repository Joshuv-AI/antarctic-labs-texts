Effects of ultrasound and microwave pretreatments of carrot slices before drying on the color indexes and drying rate
Authors: Fakhreddin Salehi, Kimia Goharpour, Helia Razavi Kamran
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Microwave-based drying is a promising method that can efficiently dry the products in a shorter duration. In addition, compared to other new methods, this process is quite simple and very energy efficient [1]. A microwave pretreatment (MWP) accelerates the water transfer in the drying procedure and can decrease the drying time (DT) of fruits and vegetables [2], [3], [4]. The microwave method has been used for pretreatment and drying of various agricultural products, including apple [5], [6], carrot [7], garlic cloves [4], ginger [1], green olives [8], Myrtus fruit [9], onion [1], pomegranate arils [10], pumpkin [11], sprouted lentils [12], and white sweet cherry [13]. A group of researchers reported that using MWP preserves the color and antioxidant activity of the final product [14]. The effects of thermal extrusion and microwave vacuum drying on fatty acids profile during fish powder preparation were examined by Ahmad et al. [15]. The results showed that the thermal extrusion and microwave vacuum drying have the ability to improve the bioactive fatty acids, peroxide value, and thiobarbituric acid reactive substance value of dried fish powder.

Of all the available pretreatment approaches, ultrasound is propounded as one of the best promising processes for the treatment of fruit and vegetable slices before drying [16], [17]. Wang et al. [18] used ultrasonic pretreatment (USP) to improve the drying rate of kiwifruit slices. Their results confirmed that the USP method can improve the drying process and preserve a high amount of phenols. The effects of ultrasonic waves on several qualitative properties of carrot juice were studied using response surface methodology by Tavakoli-Dakhrabadi et al. [19]. The results of this work exhibit that the qualitative properties of sonicated carrot juice are better than those of nontreated samples, and this finding indicates that sonication is a suitable alternative to conventional heat treatment. Tayyab Rashid et al. [20] tested the effects of ultrasound frequency (20 kHz) and glucose pretreatments alone or combined with drying of sweet potato slices (3 mm) using a hot air dryer at 60 °C to study the kinetics modeling, phytochemicals, antioxidant capacity, and functional and textural changes of the final dried product. Their results confirmed that the total phenolic content and total flavonoid content were significantly higher in glucose-pretreated samples while antioxidant activity was higher in samples pretreated by ultrasound and glucose.

Carrot (Daucus carota L.) ranks as one of the 10 most cultivated crops in the world with a worldwide annual production of 40–44 million tons and a total cultivation area of 1.12 million hectares [21], [22]. This crop is one of the most consumed vegetable in the world in all seasons that rich in vitamins, phenolic compounds, carotenoid, and dietary fiber, possess therapeutic characteristics for example being anti-anemic, anticancer, antioxidant, and along with corresponding amounts of several other beneficial compounds [23], [24]. Carotenoids are one of the most important phytochemicals in carrots, of which α-carotene and β-carotene are the most abundant, accounting for 80–90 % of the total carotenoid content in most carrot genotypes. Also, carotenoids composition is greatly influenced by root color. For example, phytoene and phytofluene are commonly found in white color carrots, while α-carotene and β-carotene are dominant in orange carrots [22], [25].

We found no report on the effects of MWP and USP on the drying kinetics, Deff, rehydration rate, color parameters, and shrinkage of carrot slices in the literature. So, the aim of this work was to examine the impacts of MWP and USP methods on the DT, mass transfer rate, Deff, rehydration ratio, color index (L*, a*, b*), and surface shrinkage of carrot slices. In addition, we modeled the changes in moisture content during drying of carrot slices.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

All methods were included in this study are in accordance with the relevant guidelines. For each experiment, fresh carrots (Daucus carota L.) were cut into cylinder-shaped slices of 5 mm thickness using a slicer (model AF-23, Girmi, Italy) and then directly placed into the microwave and ultrasound devices (Fig. 1). Moisture content (MC) of fresh carrot slices was determined using a laboratory oven at 105 °C for 5 h (Shimaz, Iran).Fig. 1Schematic of microwave and ultrasound pretreatments, drying and rehydration process of carrot slices.

Schematic of microwave and ultrasound pretreatments, drying and rehydration process of carrot slices.

Microwave pretreatment (MWP)

A microwave oven (Delmonti, voltage = 230 ∼ 240 V/50 Hz, output = 900 W, input = 1400 W, model: DL740, Italy) was used under atmospheric pressure to apply MWP to carrot slices. In this study, the impact of the MWP time at five levels of 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 s on the carrot slices was examined [26].

Ultrasound pretreatment (USP)

Ultrasound is a mechanical cell disruption process in which ultrasound waves are generated, causing shear forces that leading to cell disruption [27]. In this work, an Iranian Becker model vCLEAN1-L6, 150 Watt, 40 kHz frequency ultrasonic generator was used. The impact of the USP time at five levels of 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min on the carrot slices was investigated.

Convective drying of carrot slices

Following MWP and USP process, the carrot slices were dehydrated in a laboratory oven at 70 °C (Shimaz, Iran). The dehydration procedure was stopped when the weight of the carrot slices was remains constant.

Drying kinetics

The drying kinetics of carrot slices has been modeled using 9 different mathematical drying models (Henderson and Pabis, Logarithmic, Midilli, Newton, Page, Quadratic, Two-term, Verma, and Wang and Singh) [26], [28]. Regression analysis was done using Matlab software (version R2012a) for estimation models constants [29]. The r (coefficient of determination), SSE (sum of squared error), and RMSE (root mean squared error) values were the three criteria utilized to estimate the fit of the experimental data. When the highest r value is combined with the least amount of SSE and RMSE values, a satisfactory fit between the actual data and the correlations is established.

Rehydration rate

To test the water absorption (rehydration ratio) of dried carrot slices, the dried slices were first weighed and were placed in water bath (at 50 °C). A 1:50 wt ratio of carrots to water was assumed. Then, after 30 min, it was taken out from the water and weighed with a balance with a precision of 0.01 g. The rehydration tests were performed in triplicate and the mean values were recorded. Water absorption was calculated by Eq. (6).(6)Rehydration ratio=MrMd×100In this formula, Mr is the weight of the carrot slice after reabsorbing water and Md is the weight of the dry carrot slice.

Color indexes and shrinkage

In the L*a*b* space, L* is lightness/darkness that ranges from 0 to 100, a* is redness/greenness that ranges from −120 to 120 and b* is yellowness/blueness that ranges from −120 to 120 [30]. Carrot slices were photographed using a scanner (HP Scanjet-300). The image of each slice was converted from RGB to L*(lightness), a*(green/red), and b*(blue/yellow) indexes using an Image J software (V.1.42e, USA) [31], [32].

The surface shrinkage values of the carrot slices during dehydration process and after rehydration were determined using Image J software (V.1.42e, USA). The surface shrinkage (ΔS) values of the carrot slice were calculated using Eq. (7):(7)ΔS=S0-StS0×100where ΔS is the surface shrinkage (%), S0 and St (cm2) are the areas of fresh and dried carrot slices, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were conducted by triplicate and a completely randomized design was used for statistical analysis [33]. The dehydration tests of carrot slices were carried out in a factorial design and the means were compared via Duncan’s multiple range test using an alpha level of 0.05 for significant effects, using SPSS Version 21 (N = 3).

Results and discussion

Drying time (DT)

In this work, the average MC of fresh carrot slices was 90.77 % (wet basis). Statistical analysis of empirical results confirmed that the pretreatments type (MWP and USP), pretreatments time, and its interactions, have a considerable effects on the DT of carrot slices (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Fig. 2 presents the impact of pretreatment type (MWP and USP) and time on the DT (%) of carrot slices. The DT of pretreated carrot slices was lower than the nontreated samples under all conditions. The average DT of nontreated, microwave-treated, and ultrasonic-treated carrot slices were 225.0, 158.8, and 193.8 min, respectively. The DT of carrot slices reduced by increasing the MWP and USP time. With prolongation the MWP time from 15 to 60 s, the DT of carrot slices inside the dryer was decreased from 190.0 to 130.0 min (p < 0.05). In line with the results of this research, Motevali et al. [10] reported that the use of MWP to dry pomegranate arils before convection drying reduces the samples DT compared with convection drying without pretreatment. Motevali and Hedayati [5] research results also indicated a reduction in apple DT through the application of MWP. Ultrasonic waves propagate longitudinally through liquids and gases, alternating between compression and expansion phases, much like a spring stretches and then relaxes. The pressure is higher than normal where the molecular layers of the medium are compressed, and the pressure is lower in areas of low density. Changes in ambient pressure create cavities and form air bubbles. This process repeats compression and expansion multiple times until the bubble reaches its maximum volume. Eventually, a powerful explosion or bubble collapse occurs (cavitation process) [17], [19]. The influence of MWP and USP time on the water loss rate of carrot slices during drying in the dryer is shown in Fig. 3. As seen in this Figure, the application of MWP and USP have increased water removal from carrot slices and, as a result, improved the drying rate of the samples. Similar results were also reported for papayas [35], apple [34], carrot [36], and kiwifruit slices [18] after the USP. Effective water diffusivity coefficient (Deff)

Pretreatment and drying conditions can greatly influence the water diffusivity of fruit and vegetable slices [17]. The impacts of pretreatment type and time on the Deff values of carrot slices are presented in Fig. 4. The Deff values of pretreated samples by 15–60 s microwave and 20 min ultrasound were significantly more than the nontreated carrot slices (p < 0.05). The average Deff value of nontreated carrot slices during drying in the dryer was 6.71 × 10−10 m2 s−1. The Deff values were varied from 8.69 × 10−10 to 10.96 × 10−10 m2 s−1, and from 7.56 × 10−10 to 9.39 × 10−10 m2 s−1, for samples pretreated by microwave and ultrasound, respectively. Fernandes et al. [35] used the ultrasound process to improve the mass (water and sugar) loss of papayas before hot-air drying. The results presented that the Deff was increased after treatment by ultrasound causing a decrease of about 16 % in the dehydration duration of papayas. Results obtained by Karimi et al. [9] for myrtus fruit and by Rostami Baroji et al. [36] for carrot showed that MWP applied before drying influenced mass transfer rate and Deff values in comparison with dehydrated nontreated samples. In another study, Özkan-Karabacak et al. [37] used MWP before vacuum-drying of orange slices. Their results indicated that the use of MWP reduced the DT of samples and enhanced the dehydration rate, Deff, and rehydration capacity.Fig. 4Effect of microwave and ultrasound pretreatments on the effective water diffusivity coefficient (Deff) of carrot slices (NT = nontreated; M = microwave; U = ultrasound). Rehydration rate

Statistical analysis of the test results confirmed that the pretreatment type (MWP and USP) and its interaction with pretreatment time had a considerable influence on the rehydration rate of carrot slices (p < 0.01) (Table 3). But, pretreatment time does not significantly affect the rehydration rate of carrot slices (p > 0.05). The impacts of pretreatments type (MWP and USP) and time on the rehydration ratio of carrot slices are reported in Fig. 6. The rehydration values of pretreated carrot slices by microwave were more than the nontreated slices under all conditions (p > 0.05). But, the rehydration values of pretreated carrot slices by ultrasound were significantly lower than the nontreated samples under all conditions (p < 0.05). The average rehydration ratio of nontreated, microwave-treated, and ultrasound-treated carrot slices were 432.3 %, 449.2 %, and 360.9 %, respectively. The rehydration ratio of carrot slices increased by increasing the MWP time. But, it was decreased by increasing the USP time. With increasing the MWP time from 15 to 60 s, the rehydration ratio of dried samples was increased from 438.8 % to 465.1 % (p > 0.05). But, by prolongation the USP time from 5 to 20 min, the rehydration ratio of samples was decreased from 382.1 % to 346.7 % (p > 0.05). The effect of MWP on the rehydration rate of dried sweet cherries was examined by Salehi et al. [26]. Surface color indexes and shrinkage

The impacts of pretreatments type (MWP and USP), and time on the surface color indexes (L*, a*, and b*) of carrot slices are presented in Fig. 7. The lightness index (L*) of pretreated carrot slices by microwave was lower than the nontreated sample under all conditions. But, the lightness index of pretreated carrot slices by ultrasound was higher than the nontreated sample under all conditions. In addition, the lightness values of carrot slices decreased by increasing the MWP time. But, it was increased by increasing the USP time. The finding of Liu et al. [38] confirmed that the USP can improve the color (higher lightness) of dried cranberries. Color changes in fruit products caused by ultrasound waves are ascribed to be due to cavitation that occurs during processing [39], [40]. The redness index (a*) values of pretreated carrot slices by microwave and ultrasound were significantly more than the nontreated slices under all conditions (p < 0.05). With increasing the MWP time from 15 to 60 s, the redness values of dried samples were increased from 37.52 to 40.74 (p > 0.05). Also, by prolongation the USP time from 5 to 20 min, the redness values of samples were increased from 37.92 to 41.28 (p > 0.05).

Oxidation and temperature can influence the color of processed food products, high temperatures and oxidation make them darker while Millard reaction can speed up the production of yellow pigment [39]. The yellowness index (b*) of pretreated carrot slices by microwave were higher than the nontreated slices under all conditions (p > 0.05). But, the yellowness index values of pretreated carrot slices by ultrasound were lower than the nontreated slices under all conditions (p < 0.05). In addition, the yellowness values of carrot slices increased by increasing the MWP time. But, it was decreased by increasing the USP time. The average yellowness values of nontreated, microwave-treated, and ultrasound-treated dried carrot slices were 39.69, 41.43, and 32.00, respectively.

The impacts of pretreatments type and time on the surface shrinkage of carrot slices are reported in Fig. 8. The surface shrinkage of pretreated carrot slices by microwave were higher than the nontreated samples under all conditions. But, the surface shrinkage of pretreated carrot slices by ultrasound were significantly lower than the nontreated slices under all conditions (p < 0.05). In addition, the surface shrinkage of carrot slices increased by increasing the MWP time. The mean surface shrinkage of nontreated, microwave-treated, and ultrasound-treated dried carrot slices were 71.80 %, 75.61 %, and 52.38 %, respectively. Sharifian and Hosseini Ghaboos [11] used an osmotic–microwave dryer for drying of pumpkin slices. Their results showed that the lowest of shrinkage was associated with pumpkin slices dried at 100 W (71.09 %) and significant differences were observed between samples. Conclusion

MWP and USP processes can accelerate water transfer in the drying procedure and decrease DT for food products. In this work, the influence of MWP and USP and their treatment time on the thin-layer drying characteristics of carrot slices was studied. Our results showed that the pretreatments type (MWP and USP) and time influenced on the water loss rate, Deff, rehydration ratio, color parameters and surface shrinkage of dried carrot slices. This study showed that MWP and USP can be a useful pretreatment methods for carrot slices which decreased the DT of fresh samples. The DT of nontreated carrot slice was longer than the treated slices. The Deff values of pretreated samples by microwave and ultrasound were higher than the nontreated samples. The water transfer rates of carrot slices were described by the Page and Fick models, in which the Page's equation was shown to be the most suitable. The lightness index of pretreated carrot slices by microwave were lower than the nontreated samples under all conditions. While, the redness, yellowness, and surface shrinkage parameters of pretreated samples by microwave were higher than the nontreated slices. The lightness and redness parameters of pretreated carrot slices by ultrasound were higher than the nontreated samples under all conditions. It is anticipated from the outcomes that the research will be helpful to prepare value-added dried agricultural products in future researches. Also, ultrasound and microwave pretreatments will be more promising methods for carrot slices pretreatment before drying procedure in food industries.
