Association between Proton Pump Inhibitors, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, and Acute Kidney Injury: A Nested Case–Control Study
Authors: Chinami Yamawaki, Shunsaku Nakagawa, Keiko Ikuta, Yurie Katsube, Natsuki Imayoshi, Yuki Shigetsura, Daiki Hira, Shinya Yamamoto, Takeshi Matsubara, Motoko Yanagita, Tomohiro Terada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Points This is the first study to use a large database of patients with cancer to examine the association between the studied drugs and AKI.The use of either proton pump inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors was associated with an increased risk of AKI.However, their additive interaction was not identified. BackgroundAn association between proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use and an increased risk of AKI has been confirmed. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of PPI use on the risk of AKI in patients with cancer who were administered immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a class of drugs used in cancer treatment, and in those who were not.MethodsWe used a database provided by the Health, Clinic, and Education Information Evaluation Institute, which included demographic data, diagnoses, prescriptions, and laboratory results. We conducted a nested case–control study of 38,930 patients with cancer who were new PPI or ICI users and had no history of AKI before cohort entry. The odds ratio (OR) for AKI was estimated using conditional logistic regression models.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 8.3 months, 5870 cases of AKI were identified (incidence rate, 21.9/100 person-years). Compared with never or past PPI use without ICI use, the adjusted ORs of AKI for current PPI use without ICI use, past or never PPI use with prior ICI use, current PPI use with prior ICI use were 2.20 (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.01 to 2.40), 1.72 (95% CI, 1.37 to 2.17), or 2.62 (95% CI, 1.75 to 3.93), respectively. The risk of AKI in patients treated with both PPIs and ICIs was not higher than the additional or multiplication of the risks in those who were treated with PPIs or ICIs alone.ConclusionsThis study reinforces the association between PPI and ICI use and the increased risk of AKI. Although the interaction between the two drug classes was not detected, these findings highlight the need for careful monitoring and evaluation of kidney function in patients treated with PPIs and ICIs.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are among the most commonly used drugs. They are widely used to treat gastric ulcers and gastroesophageal reflux disease. However, despite their high efficacy, it has been recognized that the use of PPIs can lead to various adverse events.1 AKI is one such event associated with PPI use.2–4 In a systematic review, it was reported that the direction of the effect of PPI use on the risk of AKI were consistent across the studies: The relative risk of AKI onset in the PPI user compared with the non-PPI user was 1.44 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.08 to 1.91).5

Patients using PPIs often concurrently use other medications. Therefore, it is important to clarify whether concomitant drugs can modify the risk of AKI in PPI users. However, such studies have not yet been conducted. Recent studies examining the association between immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and AKI have reported that PPI use is a risk factor of AKI in ICI users.6–10 In addition, another study that used a database that included information on adverse drug reactions through spontaneous reports indicated a higher propensity for reporting nephritis when ICIs and PPIs were used concomitantly than the propensity when each was used alone.11 ICIs are known to cause systemic adverse events called immune-related adverse events (irAEs). AKI caused by acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) is the major type of ICI-related renal irAE, yet AIN is also recognized to be triggered by PPIs.12–14 It was reported that AIN is the most common pathological finding in kidney biopsies of patients with ICI-related AKI who also took PPI during ICI therapy.14 Based on these findings, it has been hypothesized that PPI use may cause ICI-related AKI through loss of tolerance via activation or reactivation of drug-specific T cells.15 A second-hit model has also been proposed, in which ICI use increases sensitivity to other drugs, including adverse events other than nephrotoxicity.16 Given these findings, PPI and ICI use may influence each other, increasing the risk of AKI. To verify this hypothesis, further studies should elucidate whether there is an interaction between these drugs or whether the effects of ICIs and PPIs on the risk of AKI are independent. To address this issue, we aimed to evaluate the effects of PPI use on the risk of AKI in patients with cancer who had been administered ICIs and those who had not, using a nested case–control design. Moreover, we assessed whether there were any interactions between the drugs.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study design was a nested case–control study in a cohort of adults aged 18 years or older who were prescribed a PPI or ICI at least once between September 2014 and July 2022 and were diagnosed with cancer in Japan. We used this design because of the time-varying nature of drug use and because the odds ratio (OR) obtained from the nested case–control study closely approximates the rate ratio obtained from a cohort study.17 The outcome of interest was AKI. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine (R3447).

Data Source

The medical information database provided by the Health, Clinic, and Education Information Evaluation Institute was used.18 This database includes information on approximately 20 million individuals from approximately 200 Japanese medical institutions. The information included demographic data, diagnoses, prescriptions, and laboratory results from both out-patient and in-patient encounters. Patient records were maintained by assigning unique identifiers to each patient that were valid only within the same institution. Diagnoses were recorded as International Classification of Diseases 10th version codes, and prescription records were stored in Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes. This database is widely used in clinical epidemiology and pharmacoepidemiology studies.19–21

Study Cohort

Patients were eligible if they had been prescribed PPI or ICI at least once between September 2014 and July 2022 and had a cancer diagnosis. Cohort entry was the date of the first PPI or ICI prescription. ICIs became available in Japan after September 2014, and this allows us to assume that the study patients did not receive ICIs before cohort entry. Patients were also required to be 18 years of age or older on cohort entry and to have a record of cancer diagnosis or treatment (Supplemental Table 1), for which the ICI could be administered, between 6 months before and 1 month after the entry. Exclusion criteria were (1) their medical histories in the database before the cohort entry were <6 months, (2) they did not undergo blood examinations of serum creatinine at least once a year after the cohort entry, and (3) they had a history of AKI or eGFR < 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2 before the cohort entry. Patients included in the study cohort were followed up for up to 1 year from cohort entry and were censored at the earliest of the following: 1 year from cohort entry, development of AKI, death, or the last recorded day in the database. Cases were individuals who developed AKI. Controls were those present in the cohort at the case index date and had not developed AKI.

Definition of AKI

Cases were defined as the patients who developed stage 1 or higher AKI. The criteria for AKI used in this study were based on those used in previous studies,22,23 which roughly correspond to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes creatinine criteria.24,25 We defined the following criteria for AKI stage 1 or higher. Criterion 1 was defined as an increased serum creatinine level at least 1.5 times higher than the median of all creatinine levels 8–365 days previously. Criterion 2 was defined as an increase in serum creatinine level to at least 1.5 times that of the minimum value within 7 days. Criterion 3 was defined as an increase ≥0.3 mg/dl within 2 days. In addition, we defined stage 2 or higher AKI as serum creatinine elevation greater than or equal to twice the reference level. The date of AKI incidence was defined as the index date for each case. Recovery from AKI was defined as a return of serum creatinine to within 20% of the reference value within 3 days of the index date.26 Partial recovery was defined as a serum creatinine level that remained higher than 20% of the reference value, without meeting any of criteria 1–3 within 3 days of the index date. Persistent AKI was defined as stage 1 or higher AKI that persisted for at least 3 days after the index date.6,27

Selection of Controls

Up to four controls were randomly selected using risk set sampling and matched to each case on the birth year (±10 years), sex, follow-up period (±90 days), exposure to potentially nephrotoxic anticancer drugs within 180 days before the cohort entry, and frequency of serum creatinine monitoring (Figure 1). Matching previous exposure to potentially nephrotoxic anticancer drugs aimed to reduce confounding by indication (e.g., patients receiving an ICI are more likely to have received other anticancer drugs). The definitions of the potentially nephrotoxic anticancer drugs are presented in Supplemental Table 2. Matching the frequency of serum creatinine monitoring aimed to reduce detection bias. Potential controls were required to undergo at least two creatinine measurements within 365 days before the index date, at least one of which had to be within 90 days before the index date. When fewer than four potential controls were available, only the available controls were analyzed.

Measurement of Exposure

The drugs of interest included PPIs (esomeprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole, and vonoprazan) and ICIs (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab, and ipilimumab). We assumed the class effects of these drugs, regardless of the daily dose or formulation type. In addition, we assumed that the drug administration began on the day of dispensing and continued for the number of prescription days recorded in the database. The exposure status of PPIs was classified into three categories: current use, referring to drug use within 30 days before the index date; past use, referring drug use after cohort entry, but not within 30 days before the index date; and never use, referring to no PPI use before the index date. This is because our previous studies showed an increased risk of AKI during the period of PPI use and within 30 days thereafter, compared with other exposure status.4,23 The exposure status of ICIs was classified into two categories: prior use, referring to ICI use at least once before the index date, and never use, referring to no ICI use before the index date. This approach was chosen because we assumed that the effects of ICI on the kidneys persisted for at least 1 year after administration. It has been suggested that irAEs may occur several years after ICI administration.28,29 The development of ICI-related kidney injury has been observed for 6–12 months after the initial drug administration.30

Potential Confounding

In addition to matching factors, we assessed confounders that may be associated with exposure to PPIs or ICIs and may influence the risk of AKI. These included baseline renal functions (eGFR and history of grade 2 or higher proteinuria), concomitant use of potentially nephrotoxic drugs, and comorbidities. Baseline eGFR was defined as the lowest value at the nearest time point to cohort entry. The history of proteinuria was assessed based on records before cohort entry. We defined potentially nephrotoxic drugs according to the drug formulary described in the Clinical Practice Guidelines for Drug-induced Kidney Injury in Japan 2016 published by the Japanese Society of Nephrology (Supplemental Table 3). Exposure to concomitant drugs was defined as the duration of drug use and 1–30 days thereafter. We identified records of hypertension and other comorbidities (peptic ulcer, liver disease, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, myocardial infarction, diabetes, pulmonary disease, connective tissue disorder, cancer, and renal disease) from 6 months before and including the month of cohort entry. The classification of diseases using International Classification of Diseases 10th codes (Supplemental Table 4) and calculation of the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were performed according to previous reports.31,32

Statistical Analysis

Because all cases available in the database were included and the sample size was determined by disease incidence, a formal power calculation was not performed. All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Pro software (version 16, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A conditional logistic regression model was used to compute the OR and 95% CI, adjusted for matching variables to account for residual confounding (age at the index date, sex, use of potentially nephrotoxic anticancer drugs before cohort entry). The model included an interaction term (the product of two dummy variables indicating exposure to PPI and ICI), but we did not consider interactions between PPI or ICI exposure and other covariates. The adjusted OR was estimated by adding potential confounders (baseline eGFR; history of grade 2 or higher proteinuria before cohort entry; current use of anticancer drugs, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs], H2 blockers, or antibiotics; concurrent hypertension; and CCI) into the model. In the main analysis, never or past PPI users with never ICI use were defined as the reference group and compared with other patients. In addition, to investigate the interaction between PPIs and ICIs, the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion of interaction (AP), and synergy index (S) were determined as follows:RERI=ORCurrent PPI use with prior ICI use−ORCurrent PPI use with never ICI use−ORNever or past PPI use with prior ICI use+1AP=RERI/ORCurrent PPI use with prior ICI useS=(ORCurrent PPI use with prior ICI use–1)/(ORCurrent PPI use with never ICI use+ORNever or past PPI use with prior ICI use−2)

The CIs for these indicators were calculated according to previous reports.33,34

Additional and Sensitivity Analyses

We repeated the analyses, including only cases with stage 2 or higher AKI, excluding patients who died within 30 days, further matching cases and controls on cancer type diagnosed at baseline, including only patients newly prescribed with PPIs after ICI initiation, or comparing current ICI use with never or past ICI use. We determined the person-years at risk for drug use by summarizing the dispensing data in accordance with the method described in previous reports.4,35 In addition, we repeated the analyses by varying the width of the exposure time window and the grace periods (30, 60, 90, 180, and 365 days). Finally, we repeated the analyses separating PPIs or ICIs into specific drugs.

Results

Of the 119,950 individuals who were age 18 years and older, started prescription of PPI or ICI between September 1, 2014, and July 31, 2022, and were diagnosed with cancer, 38,930 were eligible for this study (Figure 2). Of them, 5870 patients experienced stage 1 or higher AKI events during the 26,864 person-years of follow-up. The highest severity was stage 1 in 3528 cases, and stage 2 or higher in 2342 cases. Recovery from AKI within 3 days of onset was observed in 18.3% of cases, and persistent AKI was observed in 13.9% of cases. However, because of missing serum creatinine values, recovery or persistence of AKI could not be assessed in approximately half of the cases (Supplemental Table 5). The incidence rate of AKI was 21.9 (95% CI, 21.3 to 22.4) per 100 person-years. In total, 23,443 controls were included in the study cohort. Characteristics of the cases and controls are summarized in Table 1. Cases and controls had equivalent eGFR at baseline but different in the history of grade 2 or higher proteinuria before the cohort entry. Cases were more likely to have hypertension and other comorbidities. Furthermore, the cases differed from the controls in terms of the current use of potentially nephrotoxic anticancer drugs, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, NSAIDs, H2 blockers, and antibiotics. Notably, the proportion of NSAID and antibiotic users was high at 47.2% and 54.1% among cases and 25.2% and 26.0% among controls, respectively.

We initially assessed whether the use of PPIs alone, ICIs alone, or both PPIs and ICIs was associated with an increased risk of AKI (Table 2). Compared with never or past PPI use without ICI use, the estimated ORs of AKI for current PPI use without ICI use, past or never PPI use with prior ICI use, current PPI use with prior ICI use were 3.34 (95% CI, 3.07 to 3.62), 1.96 (95% CI, 1.57 to 2.44), or 4.87 (95% CI, 3.31 to 7.16), respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, the estimated ORs for AKI were 2.20 (95% CI, 2.01 to 2.40), 1.72 (95% CI, 1.37 to 2.17), and 2.62 (95% CI, 1.75 to 3.93). The risk of AKI in patients exposed to both PPIs and ICIs was no higher than that in patients who were exposed to PPIs or ICIs alone (RERI, −0.29 [95% CI, −0.99 to 0.41]; AP, −0.11 [95% CI, −0.40 to 0.18]; and S, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.56 to 1.28]).

The results of the main analysis may be influenced by the specificity in detecting AKI, the effect of severe diseases associated with drug use, or confounding by indications for the use of ICI. However, the results of sensitivity analyses (including only cases with stage 2 or higher AKI [Supplemental Table 6], excluding cases who died within 30 days [Supplemental Table 6], further matching cases and controls on cancer type diagnosed at baseline [Supplemental Table 7], and including only patients newly prescribed PPIs after the initiation of ICI [Supplemental Table 8]) were consistent with the main analysis. Supplemental Table 9 shows the frequency of days between the last use of PPIs or ICIs and the index date. Supplemental Table 10 presents the distribution of current, past, and never users of PPIs and ICIs among cases and controls. Most of the cases and controls who ever used PPIs were current users, with a higher frequency in cases (84%) than in controls (65%). Approximately 70% of cases and controls who ever used ICIs were current users, more frequently in controls (77%) than in cases (56%). We also repeated the analysis under the assumption that only current use is the active exposure status (Supplemental Table 11). The association between ICI use and an increased risk of AKI was weakened, but an association between PPI use and an increased risk of AKI was observed. An interaction between ICI use and PPI use was not detected. Furthermore, we repeated the analyses by varying the widths of the exposure time windows and grace periods (30, 60, 90, 180, and 365 days) (Supplemental Table 12). For PPIs, the point estimates of OR were lower in the 60- to 365-day time window than those in the 30-day time window. By contrast, for ICIs, the point estimates of the OR were lower when the time window was shorter than 365 days. This was probably due to increased misclassification of exposure, thus supporting the choice of a 30-day time window for PPIs and 365 days for ICIs. The results of the main analysis were also corroborated by the calculated incidence rates presented in Supplemental Table 13.

In addition, we evaluated the association between specific PPI or ICI and AKI risk (Table 3). Compared with no PPI use, the adjusted ORs for AKI with the use of esomeprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole, vonoprazan, and two or more PPIs were 1.96 (95% CI, 1.73 to 2.23), 2.18 (95% CI, 1.84 to 2.57), 5.38 (95% CI, 3.93 to 7.37), 2.12 (95% CI, 1.76 to 2.55), 1.77 (95% CI, 1.52 to 2.06), and 3.79 (95% CI, 3.05 to 4.70), respectively. Among the ICIs, our analysis identified nivolumab and pembrolizumab to be associated with an increased risk of AKI. The adjusted ORs were 1.58 (95% CI, 1.15 to 2.18) for nivolumab and 1.55 (95% CI, 1.23 to 1.96) for pembrolizumab. However, only a few patients were exposed to the other ICIs (avelumab, n=5, no case; durvalumab, n=107; and ipilimumab, n=2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study that simultaneously evaluates the effects of PPI use on the risk of AKI in patients who had been administered ICIs and those who had not, using a large database of patients with cancer. The results of this study confirmed that the use of PPIs or ICIs was associated with an increased risk of AKI; however, their interactions were not identified. The results remained consistent even after adjusting for confounding factors and performing sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, our results suggested a class effect of PPIs on AKI risk. However, we could not verify whether there were any variations in the effect of ICIs on AKI. Further studies are required to confirm these findings.

Recently, there has been growing interest in whether the combined use of PPIs and ICIs could potentially increase the risk of AKI compared with when they are used individually. An interaction based on biological mechanisms has also been hypothesized.15,16 Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether ICI use modified the risk of AKI associated with PPI use. Our study has two advantages over existing studies. First, we evaluated the association between PPI use and increased risk of AKI in both patients with and without ICI use. Second, we attempted to quantify the interaction by calculating RERI, AP, and S for a combination of PPI and ICI. These measurements quantify the risk of drug–drug interactions on an additive or multiplicative scale for dichotomous factors.33,34 RERI > 0, AP > 0, or S > 1 indicates that the combined effect of two exposures is larger than the sum or product of the individual effects. In our analysis, the RERI, AP, and S did not significantly exceed the reference values. This suggests that PPIs and ICIs contribute independently to the development of AKI, potentially through distinct mechanisms. The study showed that all five drugs classified as PPIs (esomeprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole, and vonoprazan) were associated with an increased risk of AKI. Although vonoprazan and traditional PPIs inhibit proton pumps, they differ in their binding sites and pharmacokinetic properties.36 Therefore, the mechanism by which PPIs cause renal injury may involve changes in physiological functions due to gastric acid suppression. Acid suppression by PPIs eventually affects the gut microbiota and absorption of minerals.1,37–40 Future studies should focus on these factors to elucidate mechanisms underlying PPI-induced renal injury.

This study had several limitations. First, the number of patients exposed to ICIs was limited. Second, we could not obtain biopsy information to ascertain the type of AKI. Third, we could not account for over-the-counter use of the study drugs. However, the PPIs and ICIs of interest in this study are not available as over-the-counter drugs in Japan. Fourth, we were unable to collect the medical data of patients before enrollment in the database. Some patients were diagnosed with renal disease and were treated with PPIs or ICIs before entry into the database. Fifth, the timing of drug administration was based on the prescription records. Therefore, the actual timing of the drug administration may differ. In addition, medication adherence is unknown. Sixth, differences in the type or severity of cancer, potentially associated with an increased risk of AKI, might exist between the groups. The robustness of the results was validated through a sensitivity analysis that matched cases and controls on cancer type. However, we could not measure the severity of cancer. Finally, unmeasured confounding factors could have influenced the results. However, even after adjusting for the CCI (assuming that the CCI could capture some of the unmeasured confounding factors), an association was identified between PPI and ICI use and AKI.

Our study provides evidence that PPI and ICI use increase the risk of AKI and that their interaction is NS. In the study cohort, more than 80% of patients who received an ICI had a history of PPI prescription. Therefore, these drugs are often administered concomitantly. Caution is advised when PPIs are prescribed concurrently with ICIs because the use of each drug may additively increase the risk of AKI. The findings highlight the need for careful monitoring and evaluation of kidney function in patients treated with PPIs and ICIs to improve their outcomes.
