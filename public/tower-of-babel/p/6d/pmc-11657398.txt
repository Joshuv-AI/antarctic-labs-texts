A rare presentation of pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Nazek Abuhalaweh, Stephen John Wort, John Dunbar, Alex Kempny, Michael Brown
Published: 2024

A 57-year-old lady presented to her general practitioner with breathlessness on exertion. She described no associated chest pain, cough or syncope. Her past medical history included pulmonary vein isolation in 2018 for atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes, asthma, chronic migraine, hypothyroidism and two miscarriages. She was a current smoker and worked for the ambulance service.

On examination, auscultation of her chest was unremarkable, and her saturations were 95 % on air. She had a soft, pan-systolic murmur at the left sternal edge and bilateral pitting oedema to above her ankles. She had no telangiectasia, nail clubbing or nail bed infarcts.

A general practitioner with a specialist interest in cardiology performed a transthoracic echo which indicated significant pulmonary hypertension and she was referred to our pulmonary hypertension clinic.

In clinic her right ventricle was dilated with impaired right ventricular function and her maximum tricuspid regurgitant velocity (TRV) was 4.95 m/s. Interventricular septal flattening was seen throughout the cardiac cycle. There was no evidence of left atrial dilatation or left ventricular dysfunction. A NT-pro BNP was elevated at 2556 pmol/L (normal range 20–200). A HIV test, antiphospholipid screen, connective tissue and vasculitis screen were all negative. Complement levels were normal. Her ESR was raised at 97.

A CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) (see Fig. 1) demonstrated bilateral pulmonary artery stenosis (PAS) with features of right ventricular pressure overload that included a flattened interventricular septum and a dilated right atrium. There was no evidence of thromboembolic disease to a segmental level. Comparing this to a CT coronary angiogram from 2018 it demonstrated the pulmonary artery stenosis was acquired and right ventricular changes new, which was subsequently corroborated on review of an echocardiogram from 2022.

A positron emission tomography (PET)-CT showed moderate FDG activity (SUV max 4.2) at the site of both pulmonary arterial stenoses with no evidence of external compression of the pulmonary arteries and no evidence of PET avid disease elsewhere.

The patient was then referred to the Royal Brompton Hospital for cardiac catheterisation and a pulmonary angiogram. Pre intervention, the mean pulmonary artery (MPA) pressure was 40mmgHG (95/12), the left pulmonary arterial pressure (LPA) was 18mmHG (24/12) and the right pulmonary arterial pressure (RPA) was 17mmHG (21/12). A pulmonary angiogram demonstrated significant stenosis in the proximal LPA with a minimum diameter of 6.1 mm and a stenosis in the RPA with a diameter of 3.3 mm. There was no evidence of thromboembolic disease elsewhere. A stepwise balloon dilatation of the LPA and then RPA was performed with a reduction in the systolic main pulmonary artery pressure to 59mmHG. The residual stenoses were then stented and following this more proximal stenoses were balloon dilated. This led to a significant improvement with the mean PA pressure reducing to 17 mmHg (39/2) (see Fig. 2).

Following her intervention her functional status improved from III to I and her NT-pro BNP reduced to 249 pmol/L. An echocardiogram 2 months post intervention showed improvement in right heart structure and function.

Summary

Pulmonary Artery Stenosis (PAS) associated with systemic congenital diseases such as Williams Syndrome and Alagille syndrome is well described. However, acquired PAS is less common and bilateral PAS seldom described [1].

Tamura Y et al. [2] recently described a cohort of 44 patients, median age 39 with PAS from 20 Japanese centres. Breathlessness on exertion was a common presenting symptom in 80 % of patients and all had severe pulmonary arterial hypertension with an average mean PAP of 44 mmHg and a mean PVR of 9 WU. Pulmonary angiography identified main pulmonary artery involvement in 20 % of these cases and the majority of stenoses were seen in the segmental and peripheral pulmonary artery territories. 84 % of all case demonstrated wedge shaped defects on lung perfusion scintigraphy [2]. A separate cohort study by Lock J et al. [1] supports Tamura's findings with dyspnoea and fatigue on exertion a universal presenting complaint. Cardiac catheterisation of this cohort demonstrated severe pulmonary arterial hypertension in all cases. In Tamura's cohort, idiopathic PAS was the predominant cause. Alternate diagnoses in association with PAS included extrapulmonary vascular lesions associated with Moyamoya disease (18.2 %) and fibromusculardysplasia (4.5 %). None were felt to have a systemic vasculitis suggestive of Takayasu's arteritis (TA).

PAS with associated pulmonary hypertension is a recognised complication of Takayasu's arteritis and has been described in the literature [3]. Zhi-Hui Zhao et al. describe a case similar to ours of bilateral PAS secondary to a diagnosis of type V TA based on the American College of Rheumatology 1990 criteria. In this case pulmonary arterial stenting was performed on the right PA and left PA a month apart with an associated reduction in MPA from 41 to 31mmgh.

Careful differentiation between disease processes driving PAS is required. Investigations to screen for the more common causes of pulmonary arterial hypertension must be performed. Pulmonary angiography may be used to highlight intimal irregularities and webs of thrombi more commonly seen in chronic thromboembolic disease or demonstrate multiple or focal stenoses associated with idiopathic PAS or a large vessel vasculitis. Optical coherence tomography evaluation has been described to differentiate between the arterial phenotypes of chronic thromboembolic disease and alternate diagnoses. Pulmonary artery sarcoma usually presents with unilateral large defects and PET here is useful in providing further diagnostic information. As described above, PAS in adulthood may be due to a systemic vasculitis such as TA or Behcet's disease.

In this case a diagnosis of acquired bilateral pulmonary artery stenosis secondary to a probable large vessel vasculitis with investigations favouring Takayasu's arteritis was made following discussion at pulmonary hypertension and rheumatology MDTs. A CT aorta also demonstrated soft tissue cuffing around the aorta in addition to known atheromatous burden in the area suggestive of previous aortitis. A subsequent PET scan post procedure showed persistent avidity of the pulmonary arteries. Immunosuppression was commenced with oral prednisolone and a steroid sparing agent will be introduced.

Takayasu arteritis is a chronic inflammatory vasculitis of unknown aetiology mainly affecting large vessels, especially the aorta and its main branches, as well as coronary and pulmonary arteries [9]. Clinical manifestations vary and depend on the site and severity of the involved arteries. Patients with pulmonary artery involvement more frequently developed dyspnoea, haemoptysis, and lower limb oedema [[4], [5], [6]]. They also had a higher incidence of bilateral pulmonary artery involvement and higher mortality rate if they develop pulmonary hypertension as described in He Y et al. [6].

We describe here treatment with bilateral percutaneous pulmonary angioplasty and stenting with successful reduction in haemodynamic pressures and an objective improvement in the patient's exercise capacity. This treatment modality has previously been described [1] and reported to improve the haemodynamic of patients with pulmonary hypertension due to pulmonary artery stenosis [[6], [7], [8],10].
