Hinge-Knee Megaprostheses Components Wear and Corrode: A Retrospective Study of 40 Devices
Authors: Abigail E. Tetteh, Tabitha Derr, Michael A. Kurtz, Gregg R. Klein, Nicolas S. Piuzzi, Arthur Malkani, Michael A. Mont, Steven M. Kurtz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Hinge-knee megaprostheses are frequently used in complex revision surgeries and are known to have higher complication and revision rates than primary total knee arthroplasties. However, the in vivo damage to the metal and polymer components of megaprostheses remains poorly understood. We, therefore, investigated the damage modes, their severity, and how they varied by implant manufacturer.Method:Forty megaprostheses and associated clinical data were collected from an institutional review board–exempt retrieval program, including implants from three manufacturers. We semiquantitatively scored the in vivo damage modes on polymer and metal megaprostheses components using the Kahlenberg method. The Goldberg score was used to classify corrosion for metal-on-metal interfaces. We then analyzed the differences between damage types and device manufacturers using nonparametric statistics.Results:We identified and classified scratching, pitting, and burnishing as major damage modes on polyethylene inserts and abrasion, delamination, surface deformation, and embedded debris as minor damage modes. Minor damage modes markedly differed between implant cohorts (P < 0.001), whereas major damage modes did not (P > 0.05). On metal components, scratching was more prevalent than burnishing and discoloration (P < 0.001). Damage to metal tibial components differed by manufacturer (P < 0.01) and corrosion was present in varying degrees across cohorts.Conclusion:In vivo damage occurred on both metal and polyethylene megaprostheses components. Within modular taper junctions, mechanics combined with chemically based processes generated corrosion. We found minimal differences between manufacturers, except for minor polyethylene damage and tibial component damage. The rotating hinge may release metal debris when unprotected by a polyethylene bumper evidenced by burnishing.

Hinged total knee implants are used in complex cases involving severe knee instability where standard total knee arthroplasty (TKA) implants would be inadequate. Primary TKAs with varying levels of constraint, such as cruciate-retaining, posterior-stabilized, and condylar-constrained knee prostheses, can be used to address severe coronal deformities.1 However, these options may be insufficient in the absence of both collateral ligaments and when there is inadequate bone to support the implant.2 Hinged TKA are a viable treatment option for patients with insufficient collateral ligaments, severe bone loss in the tibial plateau or femoral condyle, drastic valgus/varus deformities, extensor mechanism disruption, rheumatoid arthritis, and periprosthetic fracture.3-6 Hinged knee megaprostheses (HKM) are larger than standard hinged implants and are used to treat diseases or trauma that extend beyond the joint and into the bone shaft. Although they were historically used for oncological purposes, HKMs are now increasingly implanted in patients after multiple revision surgeries, often as a last option before amputation.4,7-13 They provide early weight-bearing, immediate fixation, and mobilization.4,8-10,14

Hinged TKA and HKM differ markedly from standard TKA implants. Although standard TKA relies on intact or partially preserved ligaments to retain stability, hinged implants provide stability in the absence of ligaments through fixed mechanical hinges.3 Later designs incorporate a rotating hinge that allows internal and external rotation, in addition to the flexion and extension movements that the previous hinges offered.15,16 Earlier iterations used metal-on-metal (MoM) articulations with a central axle, but later evolved to metal-on-polyethylene articulations using polyethylene bushings, bumpers, and sleeves. The combination of polyethylene with metal parts enabled the transfer of torsional and bending loads, offering more than just the limited flexion and extension provided by MoM designs.4,7 In addition, metal-on-polyethylene was introduced in an attempt to reduce the wear typically observed in MoM articulation surfaces.17 Despite these improvements, failure of hinged implants, especially HKM, continues to be of concern.

Megaprostheses have higher complication rates than standard and hinged TKA implants due to their size, complex design, challenging implantation process, and longer surgical times.4,14 Patients implanted with these devices face an increased risk of infection and loosening.7-9,13,14 The large size of megaprostheses not only heightens the risk of bacterial adhesion and persistence, making infections harder to eradicate, but also creates greater bending stresses at the implant-bone interface, leading to loosening and prosthetic fractures.8,10,14,16,18 Although the risk of infection may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as history of previous infection, the megaprosthesis size, extended surgery duration, and extensive soft-tissue exposure also present additional risks.8,10,14 For cancer patients, prior administration of immunosuppressive chemotherapy may exacerbate these issues.8 Other reported complications include tumor progression, bone resorption, and poor fixation.3,5,7-11,14,18,19 These challenges translate into in vivo complication rates as high as 40%.20 Although manufacturers attempt to design around these problems using strategies such as osseointegration surface coatings, complications persist, leading to increased revision rates.8

Damage modes previously identified on standard TKA retrievals included scratching (promoted by normal articulation, third-body wear, or adhesive wear), pitting, embedded debris, abrasion, and burnishing on polyethylene inserts and corrosion on metal components.5,7,21 Polyethylene insert damage often occurs when fragmented bone cement particles or metal debris transport to bearing interfaces, where they may embed in the polyethylene and engage in wear as third-body particles.22 Because of their size and high modularity, HKM devices may experience similar and more severe in vivo damage under normal and adverse conditions. Micromotion between the various components in the HKM may act as an additional source of wear.16 The release of wear debris into the biological environment can lead to adverse local tissue reactions, bone resorption, and osteolysis.23

Previous studies documented the revision reasons and survivorship of megaprostheses.10,13,19,24 Others investigated megaprostheses within the context of polyethylene damage, clinical outcomes, and case reports.4,11,25 Fewer studies investigated megaprostheses and device design, which may differ depending on the manufacturer.17 Therefore, the damage modes occurring in vivo on metal and polymer components from the same megaprostheses system remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the damage modes on the polyethylene inserts and the metal components, including articulating surfaces and modular junctions of HKM. We asked (1) what damage modes occur in vivo; (2) how severe is the damage; and (3) does component damage vary by implant manufacturer?

Methods

Implant Retrieval, Device Design, and Manufacturer

A retrospective study was conducted on HKM implants collected at an institutional review board–exempt implant retrieval program. The inclusion criteria were retrieved knee megaprostheses with a hinge mechanism. We excluded hinge knee TKAs and other constrained TKAs that did not replace both the epiphysis and metaphysis. Implants (n = 40) identified based on our inclusion criteria from three manufacturers with revision dates from 2008 to 2019 were analyzed. The manufacturers included Stryker-Howmedica with the global modular replacement system (MRS), and modular rotating hinge designs; Biomet with the orthopedic salvage system designs; and Zimmer with segmental system designs. It is important to note that the hinge mechanism differed for each design. The global modular replacement system and MRS systems connect the tibial and femoral components using an axle that passes through polyethylene bushings in the femoral condyles. This assembly is secured by press-fit, allowing for rotational movement, and an anterior bumper attached to the tibial baseplate controls the range of rotation. The orthopedic salvage system also uses an axle to connect the tibial and femoral components, passing through a bushing in the tibial baseplate into bushings on either side of the femoral implant. This assembly is also secured by press-fit but has no anterior bumper. The segmental system uses an axle that screws into the femoral implant, passing through a tibial bushing and a polyethylene piece inserted in the femoral implant recess (between the medial and lateral condyle) and does not include a bumper. The implants were grouped into the three manufacturer cohorts for analysis (groups A to C).

Patient Data

Patient and clinical data, including redacted postoperative notes and radiographs, were extracted and analyzed. All patients underwent distal femur-to-proximal tibia reconstruction. Of the 40 implants, n = 16 (40%) were revised for infection, n = 15 (37.5%) for loosening, n = 7 (17.5%) for other reasons (extensor mechanism disruption, prosthetic patellofemoral dislocation, wound dehiscence, hematoma, and drainage), and n = 1 (2.5%) each for pain and instability. Infection was the standardized revision reason in group A (n = 13/22), whereas aseptic loosening was the standardized revision reason in groups B (n = 5/9) and C (n = 4/9). The age of patients at implantation, their body mass index, and implantation time did not differ across the three cohorts (P > 0.05). Assessment and Scoring of Hinge Knee Megaprostheses Implants

A total of 156 implant components were analyzed (Table 2). According to manufacturer stickers in surgical notes, all polyethylene components were conventional ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene. Metal components were identified as cobalt chrome alloy using a Niton XL3t radiograph fluorescent analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Because of variability in implant design, other polyethylene accessories, such as bushings, tibial sleeves, and bumpers, were excluded from the analyses. Damage was assessed visually and with a Light Source LED-312 stereo microscope (Amscope) at ×10 magnification. Representative images of polyethylene and metallic damage modes were captured using digital optical microscopy (VHX-7000, Keyence OP-88338).

Polyethylene Component Scoring

Polyethylene inserts were semiquantitatively scored following the Kahlenberg method, derived from the Hood method.7,26 Seven damage modes (scratching, pitting, burnishing, abrasion, delamination, surface deformation, and embedded debris) were scored on a zero to three scale on 14 zones. Damage was classified as zero for no damage, one for damage covering less than 10% of the surface, two for damage covering 10% to 50%, and three for damage covering more than 50% of the surface (Table A1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A432). Damage on the superior (10 zones) and inferior (four zones) surfaces of the polyethylene inserts was combined and assessed to identify the most common types of damage (Figure 2, A and B). The maximum possible score for each damage mode was 42 (14 zones × 3 maximum score). The most frequently occurring and highest-scoring damage modes were classified as major, whereas the remaining damage modes were classified as minor.

Images explaining semiquantitative scoring of implant components. Polyethylene inserts (A and B) were scored using the Kahlenberg method; damage on the superior surface was calculated by summing up zones 0 to 9, whereas damage on the inferior surface was calculated by summing up zones 15 to 18. Metal femoral components (C), tibial components (D), and axles (E) were scored using the Hood method. Goldberg corrosion scoring was applied to two regions: Region 1 (F), comprising the femoral taper and femoral stem, and region 2 (G), consisting of the axle and tibial post.

Metal Component Scoring

Metallic components were semiquantitatively scored using two methods. Using the Hood method, metallic components were assessed for scratching, burnishing, and discoloration26 (Table A1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A432). Because of differences in implant design, scored areas of the metal components were grouped by location and normalized for analyses. Groups included the femoral components (Figure 2C), the tibial components (Figure 2D), and the axle (Figure 2E). Corrosion between modular metal junctions was scored using the Goldberg method27 (Table A1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A432). The scored modular junctions were initially evaluated and subsequently categorized into two regions for analyses: region 1 (femoral taper and femoral stem, Figure 2F) and region 2 (axle and tibial postarticulation site, Figure 2G). This was done to assess the effect of design variations across manufacturer cohorts.

The maximum overall damage score for areas scored with the Hood method was 24 (8 areas × 3 maximum score). The maximum overall corrosion damage scored with the Goldberg method was 16 (4 regions × 4 maximum score). Total scores for all metallic component groups were converted into percentages for analysis and comparison.

Data Analyses

Statistical analyses were done with GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 software. All analyses were done with nonparametric tests after confirmation of non-normality with Shapiro-Wilk tests. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to evaluate the following: differences in patient demographic data, differences in the overall damage modes on the polyethylene inserts to identify major and minor damages, differences in the major and minor polyethylene damage across the three cohorts, differences in the overall damage modes on all metallic components as well as total damage on femoral, tibial, and axle components across cohorts, and differences in the corrosion damage of metallic components at modular junctions across cohorts. In addition, the Mann-Whitney U-test was used to compare the overall corrosion damage on the metallic component regions to determine which region had the most damage. The Friedman test with Dunn multiple comparisons was used to assess the total polyethylene damage modes for each implant cohort when all the data were considered together. Finally, the Spearman correlation was used to analyze the relationship between implantation time and component damage. An α value of 0.05 was used to determine significance.

Results

Damage Modes and Severity on Polyethylene Inserts

Following scoring, major damage modes (most frequent and highest scoring) on polyethylene inserts included scratching, pitting, and burnishing (Figures 3 and 4A). In addition, we classified abrasion, delamination, surface deformation, and embedded debris as minor damage modes (Figure 4A). Although we identified notable differences between scores for major damage types and minor damage types (P < 0.001), no statistical differences existed within the group for the major damage modes (P > 0.05) nor minor damage modes (P > 0.05, Figure 4, A and D) for all cohorts.

We did not find statistically significant differences when stratifying major damage type by manufacturer (Figure 4B). However, when we separated the minor damage types into implant cohorts, we measured a markedly higher amount of minor damage in group B than in group A (P < 0.001) and group C (P < 0.0001, Figure 4C). We also found that longer implantation time corresponded to more notable total polyethylene damage in group B (P < 0.05, r = 0.86) and group C (P < 0.05, r = 0.86; Figure A1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A430).

Micrographs show scratching, burnishing, and discoloration on the femoral components, tibial components, and axles (Figure 5). Scratching, burnishing, and discoloration scores differed markedly from each other (P < 0.001), with scratching scoring the highest of the three damage modes (Figure 6A). For femoral components and axles, the combined damage did not differ across implant cohorts (Figure 6, B and C). However, group C exhibited less damage to the tibial components than groups A and B (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Surgeons increasingly use hinge knee megaprostheses following multiple revision TKA for skeletal defects and salvage procedures.8,12 However, their complication rates remain high, and research on these devices remains limited compared with primary TKA devices.4,14 In this study, we characterized and semiquantitatively scored damage on polyethylene and metal components of megaprostheses systems, comparing three manufacturers.

We found that infection and aseptic loosening were the primary revision reasons for all the implants, consistent with other megaprostheses retrieval studies.10,14,17-19,24 The large size of megaprostheses may translate into increased surface area for bacteria adhesion and persistence.8,12,14 Previous studies indicated that the length of megaprostheses compared with conventional TKA could cause increased bending stresses at the implant-bone interface, leading to aseptic loosening.16,18

All three cohorts demonstrated substantial polyethylene wear, evidenced by the comparable severity of the major damage modes. The major damage modes on the polyethylene inserts, which were scratching, pitting, and burnishing, suggest the presence of volumetric wear, as observed in another study that assessed wear on MoM rotating hinge megaprostheses.17 Although modern designs with bushing, bumpers, and sleeves may minimize MoM articulation and enhance stability,16,28 wear at the polyethylene bearing interface remains a concern, supported by the findings in this study and others.5,7,25 Polyethylene wear could occur when there is micromotion between components, and the likelihood is higher for HKM devices due to their high modularity.16 The wear debris from polyethylene may induce undesirable tissue reactions, which may also be connected to the higher incidence of aseptic loosening in this study.23,29

The main drawback of megaprostheses is the increase in exerted stresses due to the potential loss or reduction in integrity of muscular attachments.14,28 The tendency for frictional wear is heightened due to the articulation mechanism involving the screw-home movement during the termination of the metallic femoral condyle (upper weight-bearing surface) on the polyethylene insert (lower weight-bearing surface) in the megaprostheses assembly.23,28 Older patients tend to experience reduced lubrication from the synovial fluid due to lower hyaluronic acid levels, which decrease viscosity and impair boundary lubrication, potentially increasing friction and wear.23,30 The shearing forces on polyethylene during normal gait and the lack of boundary lubrication in elderly patients may further exacerbate wear.30 Because of the advanced age of the patient demographic at the time of implantation in this study, age may be a contributing factor to the polyethylene wear observed.

In addition, studies reported that the presence of bacteria adversely affected polyethylene wear and friction by altering the composition and concentration of proteins in serum lubricants used in wear simulations.31-33 Wimmer et al showed that the effect may be comparable to the influence of polyethylene type, protein concentration, and contact stress on polyethylene wear.33 Therefore, the prevalence of infection in this study and others may be considered a contributing factor in the observed wear-related damage to polyethylene inserts.

Manufacturers have modified their processing and treatment methods over the years to improve polyethylene wear-resistance and performance. These modifications include crosslinking, gamma irradiation sterilization, and the addition of vitamin E.25,34 Although there have been reports of improved polyethylene performance with such processing improvements, others show that polyethylene wear persists.5,12 In a retrospective analysis of polyethylene retrieved from 224 patients who underwent distal femur rotating HKM implantation from 1993 to 2015, Belzarena et al25 observed wear in both air-sterilized, machined, ram-extruded polyethylene and molded, gamma-radiated, argon, processed polyethylene. Our study identified polyethylene inserts across all cohorts as conventional ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene without crosslinking. The damage modes we observed were similar to those reported on highly crosslinked polyethylene and vitamin E–stabilized highly crosslinked polyethylene bearing surfaces in primary TKA, rotating hinge TKA, highly constrained TKA, and hinge knee megaprostheses.5,7,25,34 However, we cannot conclude whether the extent of damage observed here would differ if the polyethylene inserts were treated or highly crosslinked.

Megaprostheses, unlike primary TKA devices, may include MoM bearing surfaces, where the femoral and tibial components rotate about an axle. These bearings enable the incorporation of a hinge to reconstruct bones that a conventional TKA could not replace.15,35 However, one potential downside for MoM constructs may be increased metal ion release compared with metal-on-polyethylene.36 Consequences of elevated metal ion levels include adverse local tissue reactions, pseudotumor, osteolysis, and aseptic loosening.17,21,27,35,37,38 The predominance of scratching on metal articulating surfaces and high burnishing levels on axles across all cohorts in this study indicates the persistence of metal wear even in metal-on-polyethylene constructs. Also, the differences in megaprostheses design across cohorts, particularly in the implemented hinge mechanism, may have influenced the damage variations observed in tibial components.

We identified the metal components as cobalt-chromium alloys, and studies posit that cobalt and chromium ions may impair macrophage response to infection by altering their phenotype, reducing phagocytic function, and hindering migration.39,40 Consequently, there could be an increase in the susceptibility of patients to infection due to the altering of infection-associated surface markers from the influx of cobalt ions.40 This may be another reason for the prevalence of infection in this study. However, although in vitro testing provides evidence for this hypothesis, additional testing is required.

In addition to the evidence of metal wear due to high scratching levels, we found that corrosion on the femoral modular junctions was more severe than on the tibial posts and axles across implant cohorts. This provides further evidence that metal wear occurred in these devices in vivo. Knee articulation does not occur in a vacuum. Synovial fluid acts as an electrolyte, maintaining an electrical connection between metal bearings. Consequently, as the axle rotates or undergoes cyclic loading, asperities disrupt the passive oxide radiograph, likely promoting tribocorrosion.41,42 It remains unclear whether crevice corrosion at the modular taper junctions is more severe than the tribocorrosion hypothesized to occur at the tibial post-axle interfaces. Although we report a higher level of damage at modular junctions in this study, the cement mantle covering the taper interface likely limits the transport of ions. In comparison, tribocorrosion at the tibial post-axle interface may directly release ions into the synovial fluid. Case reports in the literature link wear around the axle of rotating hinge-knee implants and corrosion at distal modular tapers to adverse local tissue reactions in vivo.36,38,43 As a result, despite a lower Goldberg score, wear and corrosion at this interface may have increased clinical relevance.

The megaprostheses system, regardless of manufacturer, has seen minimal advancements in recent years. With their growing clinical use in salvage procedures after multiple failed TKAs, concerns still exist about the wear and failure of these devices. The findings in this study did not favor any specific device system; therefore, surgeons should choose a system based on patient needs and the intraoperative modularity required to repair defects.

There were some potential limitations in this study. We investigated surface damage modes using Kahlenberg and Goldberg scoring. Although semiquantitative scoring does not quantify the volumetric wear that occurred in vivo, it remains a common and valuable practice in assessing and categorizing damaged retrievals.7,21,34 Our study did not evaluate serum metal ion levels, as we retrospectively analyzed retrieved devices stored in an implant repository. Higher metal ion exposure has previously been observed for megaprostheses compared with standard rotating-hinge devices.36,37 Therefore, evaluating serum metal ion levels in future studies would be particularly relevant for comparing HKM devices from different manufacturers. In addition, implants in this study consisted of various components, either due to differences in implant designs or the retrieval of only specific components of a whole device system. These discrepancies mostly affected metal components, as the device systems comprised several metal components and one polyethylene insert. To address this, metal components were scored individually before they were grouped by location for normalization. We achieved the goal of comparing damage among manufacturer cohorts by normalizing the damage scores assessed on the metal components to the possible maximum score. In addition, we had a limited sample size, with 22 implants in group A and nine implants in groups B and C. Therefore, our findings may not accurately represent results from a larger sample size and could potentially affect the conclusion we draw in this study. Finally, the megaprostheses analyzed in this study were retrieved between 2008 and 2019. Current models may not exhibit the same damage patterns observed in this study due to potential design changes.

Conclusion

In our retrieved hinge knee megaprostheses collection, infection and aseptic loosening were the most common revision reasons. The polyethylene inserts primarily exhibited scratching, pitting, and burnishing, with a similar damage severity in all implant cohorts. However, we noted differences in the minor polyethylene damage, which could be as a result of differences in the device designs. Scratching was more prevalent than burnishing and discoloration on metal components. We also found that minor damage modes on polyethylene inserts and tibial component damage differed across the manufacturer cohorts, likely due to design differences affecting the implementation of the hinge mechanism. Our findings highlight that both metal and polyethylene megaprostheses components are susceptible to in vivo degradation, evidenced by the notable wear-related damage we observed on all components across the different manufacturer cohorts. Historically, some of the older generation implants had MoM articulation in the bearings, but newer generation implants with modified articulations may show different wear patterns. Given the ubiquity of wear and corrosion damage in retrieved megaprostheses, it is essential to prioritize efforts to mitigate the damage on future devices.
