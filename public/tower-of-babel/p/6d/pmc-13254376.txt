Radiation therapist research engagement and culture: findings from the ESTRO radiation therapist workshop
Authors: Yat Tsang, Siret Kivistik, Lynsey Devlin, Valbona Bali, Erica Bennett, Jenna Dean, Hannah Dyer, Cynthia L Eccles, Laurent Marmy, Melissa Martel, Kenton Thompson, Prakash Umbarkar, Michael Velec, Mark Warren, Aileen Duffton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeDespite widespread recognition of the value of research among radiation therapists (RTTs), sustained engagement remains low across international settings. This study aimed to explore RTT perspectives on research engagement, characterise the cultural conditions shaping participation, and generate structured recommendations for strengthening RTT research culture.Materials and MethodsA qualitative, workshop-based study was conducted through the ESTRO RTT workshop series. A sixteen-member Research Engagement Expert Reference Group (RE-ERG), drawn from ten countries, participated in two virtual sessions. The cultural web framework was applied in Workshop 1 to map research culture across six organisational elements. In Workshop 2, participants validated findings and developed recommendations structured around three strategic pillars: Preserve, Adapt, and Innovate (PAI). Real-time polling and recorded transcripts were analysed using deductive thematic analysis informed by the cultural web framework.ResultsTwo cross-cutting tensions emerged: a persistent recognition-to-practice gap in which strong endorsement of research coexisted with structural barriers to participation, and a reliance on individual commitment rather than systemic support, with research frequently described as “hidden work” conducted outside rostered hours. 15/16 participants (94%) agreed that RTTs could make a significant contribution to research. The Preserve pillar identified five foundational strengths including collaboration, mentorship, visibility, organisational support, and patient-focused research. The Adapt pillar generated recommendations addressing education, governance simplification, career pathway clarity, and protected time. The Innovate pillar emphasised research champion roles, graduated fellowship schemes, digital collaboration platforms, and embedding research within departmental objectives and international networks.ConclusionRTTs collectively articulate a clear and actionable vision for research culture development, yet realising it requires coordinated action from departments, national professional bodies, and international organisations. Applying the cultural web framework alongside the PAI pillars produced practitioner-grounded recommendations that move beyond barrier identification towards infrastructures that make research participation an expected and enabled dimension of RTT professional practice.Keywords: Radiation therapist, research culture, research capacity building

Introduction

Modern radiation therapy practice is grounded in person-centred care, emphasising the need for skilled practitioners who can deliver complex treatments personalised to individual patient needs [1]. Radiation therapists (RTTs) play an essential clinical role in cancer patients' pathway, as well as in providing education and reassurance to patients during what can be a vulnerable and frightening time [2], [3]. As radiation therapy has become more individualised and technology has advanced, RTTs are increasingly required to interpret complex clinical information, adapt treatment approaches, and apply emerging evidence in day-to-day practice [3], [4], [5], [6]. Modern practice incorporates advanced techniques such as adaptive radiotherapy and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy, where real-time treatment modifications demand complex decision-making skills [4], [5], [6]. This context makes RTT engagement in research especially important, not only to support evidence-based care, but also to ensure that innovation is informed by clinical practice and patient experience [4].

Despite this, research participation among RTTs remains limited. International studies suggest that RTTs generally hold positive attitudes towards research and recognise its importance for professional development, yet reported involvement in research activity remains low across settings [5], [6], [7], [8]. A large survey of medical imaging and RTT professionals in the United States reported that 72.8% of respondents had no or only slight knowledge of the research process, despite 65.9% rating research as very important for advancing the profession [9]. More recently, an international survey of diagnostic radiographers and RTTs has reinforced these patterns, highlighting persistent gaps between interest in research and actual participation [10]. Across studies, the most commonly reported barriers include lack of protected time, limited mentorship, insufficient research training, unclear career pathways, restricted funding, and workplace cultures that prioritise clinical productivity over RTT research activity [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. RTTs are well placed to identify practice-relevant research questions, contribute to service improvement, and lead innovation in person-centred care. However, opportunities to develop and sustain research involvement are uneven across departments and healthcare systems [9]. The issue is not whether RTTs value research, but how research culture can be strengthened so that participation becomes realistic, supported, and sustainable within routine practice.

International professional organisations are responding to these challenges through targeted initiatives. The European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) RTT Committee has examined research engagement through collaborative workshops and professional development programs [4], [6]. Building on this work, there is a need to move beyond describing barriers to explore how research culture can be strengthened across departmental, national, and international contexts. Bringing together RTT leaders, educators, and researchers from diverse practice environments offers an opportunity to identify both shared challenges and practical strategies for change. This recognition led to the ESTRO RTT workshop held in December 2024, entitled Current and Future Landscape of Radiation Therapist Research Practice. The workshop aimed to explore RTT perspectives on research engagement, identify barriers and facilitators influencing participation, and examine how research culture might be strengthened across departmental, national, and international levels. Specifically, it considered what aspects of current research culture should be preserved, what practices should be adapted, and what innovations may be needed to support more sustainable research engagement within radiation therapy.

This manuscript presents findings from the workshop, using cultural web mapping to examine RTT research engagement and to generate practice-informed recommendations for developing sustainable research cultures within radiation therapy services.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a qualitative workshop-based study that employed the cultural web framework to systematically examine research engagement patterns within the RTT profession. This theoretical framework provided structure for mapping qualitative findings against six cultural elements including organisational structures, power relationships, control systems, symbols, rituals and routines, and stories and myths [11], [12]. Thematic analysis was informed by the cultural web framework [11] and was used to organise and interpret findings, with the six cultural elements providing a deductive structure for analysis (Fig. 1).

Recruitment of participants

The workshop built on established ESTRO RTT workshop series', which has successfully facilitated scientific collaborations and professional networking within the RTT community [4], [5], [6]. Following the success of previous workshop frameworks, recruitment was conducted through an open call on the ESTRO webpage and social media channels, where interested participants were asked to submit their application and motivational statements. Workshop co-chairs (AD, SK and YT) reviewed applications to form a Research Engagement Expert Reference Group (RE-ERG), applying established selection criteria while seeking to attract RTTs across the research experience spectrum [6]. Applications were assessed to achieve balanced representation across experience levels and geographical regions, ensuring diverse perspectives from different healthcare systems.

Workshop design and implementation

Pre-workshop preparation included distribution of materials focused on research culture and capacity building, accompanied by questions about participants' current research activity and environments. Participants were also invited to reflect on personal and departmental research practices, including barriers e.g. time, funding, skills, leadership; and enablers e.g. journal clubs, research meetings. For workshop 1, they were asked to consider departmental culture, examples of successful or unsuccessful projects, the role of key individuals, and the influence of external drivers such as guidelines, funding, and professional standards. This approach encouraged thoughtful preparation for the collaborative sessions ahead.

Two virtual sessions (workshop 1 and 2) were conducted (3rd December 2024 and 11th March 2025), each lasting two hours. The extended timeline allowed co-chairs to analyse the initial session data, and present synthesised findings to participants during the second session for validation and development. Mentimeter (Mentimeter AB, Stockholm, Sweden), a cloud-based interactive platform was used for real-time visual display of participant responses during sessions. Real-time responses and chat function were visible to all participants in the session, to stimulate verbal discussion. Responses were anonymous following the session.

The first session (workshop 1) centred on cultural web mapping, where RE-ERG members identified cultural elements within their departments. Participants examined organisational structures, leadership approaches, research symbols, routine practices, and shared narratives influencing research engagement in their practice environments.

In preparation for the second workshop, participants were asked to prepare concise written reflections across three key areas 1. Research strengths to be preserved, 2. Adaptations to improve existing practices, and 3. Innovations to enhance research culture. For each area, participants were to consider examples at departmental (local practice), national (professional bodies and networks), and global international levels. A summary of Workshop 1 discussions was circulated in advance to support preparation, and Mentimeter was again used during the session to capture and collate responses.

The second session (workshop 2) opened with the presentation of analysed findings from session one. This enabled participants to validate interpretations and provide contextual understanding before developing practical recommendations for building research capacity within the profession.

Data collection and analysis

Data were anonymously recorded, and included real-time responses, recorded transcripts, and workshop discussions. Co-chairs facilitated and moderated the sessions discussions. These were recorded along with chat transcripts captured for analysis. Following each workshop, co-chairs independently reviewed recordings and transcripts, identifying recurring themes and patterns before meeting to reach consensus through structured discussion. Where disagreements arose during independent review, co-chairs engaged in structured discussion to reach resolution. In cases where consensus could not be reached through discussion alone, the relevant excerpts were returned to the broader RE-ERG during member-checking for contextual clarification, ensuring that final interpretations were grounded in participant intent rather than co-chair inference. This dual-review approach ensured comprehensive capture of participant perspectives whilst maintaining analytical rigour. Between sessions, detailed summaries of key themes were distributed to all RE-ERG members, providing opportunities for clarification and additional context. This member-checking process strengthened validity whilst minimising potential co-chair bias in interpretation. This ensured the finding represented participant perspectives. As co-chairs who are also practising radiation therapists with established research profiles, the team acknowledged the potential for confirmation bias in interpreting participant responses. This was mitigated by the dual independent review process, anonymous data collection, and member-checking across both sessions.

Ethical considerations

Participants were informed in advance, and at each session that the workshops would be recorded, and transcript used for analysis. Participation was accepted as consent by all participants, and they were informed of their right to leave at any point. During analysis all comments were anonymised, and no direct named quotes were used. This study did not require formal ethical review as it involved professional participants in a voluntary capacity, with no patient data collected.

Results

Participant characteristics and workshop attendance

Sixteen RTTs were selected to join the RE-ERG and participated in both sessions of the workshop held on 3rd December 2024 and 11th March 2025. The RE-ERG comprised individuals from ten countries: Canada (1), United Kingdom (5), Australia (3), New Zealand (1), Ireland (1), the Netherlands (1), India (1), Vietnam (1), Hong Kong (1), and Albania (1). Both workshop sessions were attended by all sixteen members.

Role distribution within the group was diverse. Six participants identified as advanced practice RTTs, three as research RTTs, three as educators and four managers. Participants represented varied practice contexts, from academic cancer centres with established research infrastructures to departments where research capacity was at an early stage of development.

Workshop 1: cultural web mapping and analysis

In the first session (3 December 2024), RE-ERG members characterised the research culture within their respective departments using the cultural web framework. 15/16 respondents (94%) agreed or strongly agreed that RTTs could make a significant contribution to research; the remaining respondent selected a neutral response, indicating neither agreement nor disagreement. All participants indicated willingness to support colleagues' research activities.

Analysis of the real-time polling and breakout discussions revealed distinct patterns across the six cultural elements. These findings are summarised in Table 1, with representative participant perspectives drawn directly from Mentimeter responses and session discussions.

Two tensions emerged consistently across multiple cultural web elements, cutting across individual categories rather than being confined to a single domain. First, a gap between recognition and practice was apparent throughout the mapping exercise. While RTTs broadly acknowledged the value and importance of research, structural conditions (most notably clinical workload demands and performance frameworks focused on throughput) were described as the primary obstacles to sustained participation. Second, individual commitment rather than systemic support was the predominant driver of whatever research activity did occur. Multiple participants described research as “hidden work” conducted outside contracted hours, which was highly dependent on personal motivation. These cross-cutting tensions informed the development of recommendations in Workshop 2.

Workshop 2: validation and strategic recommendations

In the second session (11 March 2025), synthesised findings from Workshop 1 were presented to all RE-ERG members for validation before the group proceeded to develop structured recommendations. Participants confirmed that the thematic analysis accurately reflected their workshop contributions and provided additional contextual detail where relevant. Recommendations were subsequently organised around three strategic pillars: Preserve, Adapt, and Innovate (PAI), and structured across departmental, national, and international levels. Preserve - sustaining core research strengths

Participants identified five inter-related strengths as foundational to sustainable research engagement. These included research promotion and visibility, collaboration and professional networks, strategic and organisational support, mentorship and capacity building, and patient-focused research, recognised across all three system levels (Table 2, Fig. 2). At departmental level, structured research forums, formally designated leadership roles, and mentorship spanning all career stages were consistently described as enabling conditions that normalise research dialogue within clinical settings. Patient-focused research was specifically highlighted as a source of motivation that links research activity to visible clinical outcomes. At national and international levels, professional society platforms and collaborative grant structures were identified as essential for sustaining visibility and creating well-defined research career pathways, with considerable variation in international practices noted as a driver for greater global coordination.

Adapt - modifying research practices across system levels

The RE-ERG identified practical adaptations needed to reduce barriers to participation across all three levels, with six themes emerging: education and training, research pathways and roles, networks and collaboration, participation and capacity building, time and resources, and processes and protocols (Table 2, Fig. 2). At departmental level, multi-timepoint study designs were described as a particular challenge for practitioners working in clinical settings. Simplified protocols with clearly defined participant roles, embedded research and quality improvement competencies in induction programmes, and structured clinical–research fellowships with joint supervision were proposed as concrete adaptations. The research governance approval process was framed as a shared challenge across cancer services. Early patient and public involvement (PPI), proactive ethics engagement, and clear local guidance were identified as pragmatic means to reduce delays and streamline participation. At national and international levels, development of clear clinical academic career route guidance, PI and CI training, postdoctoral pathway exemplars, stronger clinical–university links, and multicentre study designs with shared governance protocols were prioritised as mechanisms to expand RTT research capacity across healthcare systems.

Innovate - embedding research as routine professional practice

Participants identified the need for structures and infrastructures that embed research into all levels of RTT practice, rather than relying on individual enthusiasm or ad hoc participation. Five themes characterised effective innovation: leadership and roles, visibility and recognition, culture and engagement, strategy and planning, and digital and collaboration platforms (Table 2, Fig. 2). At departmental level, formally appointed Research Champion roles were identified as the highest-priority structural innovation. A Research Champion was described as a clinical RTT with designated protected time, responsible for mentoring colleagues, convening research meetings, and maintaining links with academic partners. Writing groups, journal clubs, research rotations, and dedicated RTT research days were proposed as complementary mechanisms to make research a routine part of professional practice. Participants stressed that sustained integration of research into departmental objectives, appraisal frameworks, and resource allocation was essential, reinforcing the broader finding that systemic structures rather than individual commitment are the necessary foundation for cultural change. At national level, inclusion of RTTs on grant review panels and clinical guideline committees were proposed as a connected pathway from initial research exposure to independent leadership. In addition, graduated fellowship schemes structured from internship through to academic appointment, and the promotion of successful RTT researchers as visible role models were recognised as important (Fig. 2). At international level, the establishment of RTT research networks, visiting professorships, multicentre studies with embedded mentorship, and PPI were described as strategies for simultaneously expanding global collaborative capacity and increasing the number of RTTs in PI roles. Authorship agreements that explicitly recognise RTT leadership were highlighted as inconsistent and should ideally applied prospectively.

Discussion

This study applied the cultural web framework through an international ESTRO RTT workshop to examine the organisational conditions shaping RTT research engagement, and to generate a structured set of recommendations for strengthening research culture across departmental, national, and international contexts as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The cultural web mapping showed that research success stories function as powerful cultural symbols, validating RTT capability and challenging the myth that “real research” belongs exclusively to medicine or academia. At the same time, RTTs face structural barriers, most notably workload pressures and performance frameworks that prioritise clinical throughput. This limits meaningful participation, and results in research activity that depends more on individual commitment than on supportive systems [13]. These narratives coexist with anecdotal accounts of stalled projects due to resource constraints, highlighting the fragility of research culture when organisational support wavers [14], [15]. This picture is consistent with surveys that indicated a minority of RTTs report active involvement in research, and that many perceive limited research impact on daily practice, despite strong endorsement of research as professionally important [7], [8], [9], [10].

The RE-ERG recognised that collaboration and multidisciplinary networks amplify individual capability, and provide access to expertise, methodologies, and opportunities beyond what isolated practitioners or departments can achieve. The variation in research practices observed across international settings within the RE-ERG is consistent with patterns documented in advanced practice development more broadly [5]. Existing international platforms, including the ESTRO RTT track, focus groups, and collaborative grant structures, were highlighted as demonstrating that global alignment is achievable and should be actively leveraged rather than treated as aspirational. By explicitly asking participants what should be “preserved”, “adapted”, and “innovated”, the workshop moved beyond simply cataloguing barriers. This extends survey-based work identifying barriers and enablers at population level [10] by capturing how RTTs themselves conceptualise how change should be approached and prioritised across system levels.

The strengths identified under the preserve pillar align with earlier ESTRO workshop outputs that emphasise professional identity, networking, and supportive departmental cultures [4], [5], [6]. The present findings extend this literature by showing how RTTs conceptualise the conditions in which collaboration, visibility, and mentorship are embedded in practice, and what places them at risk when organisational priorities shift, generating practitioner-grounded recommendations rather than externally imposed frameworks. Consistent with previous studies, our findings described a familiar pattern in which positive attitudes towards research coexist with practical barriers such as limited time, lack of mentorship, and uncertainty around research pathways [8], [9], [10], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19].

Adaptation of existing research practices were viewed as necessary to address the mismatch between clinical roles and traditional research systems. Supported by the findings from Piro et al., the RE-ERG highlighted challenges associated with complex study designs and research governance processes, particularly for RTTs working primarily in clinical settings [7]. Suggestions to simplify protocols, clarify roles, and integrate research skills earlier in career development, all align with broader discussions on how organisational culture and systems influence participation in educational and research activity. Participants proposed pragmatic adaptations, such as low-burden quality improvement projects, clearer local guidance on approvals, and embedding research expectations into job plans. These would align governance processes with clinical commitments rather than adding parallel workloads. At national level, participants emphasised that fellowship schemes at all career stages should incorporate protected time, interdisciplinary supervision, and training across methodology, ethics, grant writing, and dissemination, components largely absent from current RTT professional development structures.

Innovation focused on embedding research more firmly within routine RTT practice. Participants described the need for clearer role recognition, protected time, and structured career pathways to support long-term engagement, consistent with discussions in the literature on advanced and extended practice roles where sustainability is closely linked to role clarity, institutional support, and alignment with service priorities [3], [5], [20]. Layered developmental pathways, from initial exposure through research internships to independent leadership via clinical academic positions, reflects international evidence that RTT research leadership develops progressively when infrastructure and role recognition are in place [18], [19], [21]. That such structures are achievable in practice is demonstrated by Marr et al., who reported 445 RTT-authored publications across three international clinical academic centres over a decade, with research output increasing proportionally as dedicated research RTT posts grew; at the Canadian centre alone, full-time equivalents rose from 3.0 to 5.4, corresponding to an increase in annual publications from 21 to 34 [19]. Research champion roles, digital platforms for collaboration, and visible recognition of research outputs within appraisal frameworks were highlighted in our study as additional levers for shifting culture beyond reliance on individual motivation.

Beyond structural enablers, the findings highlight the particular importance of PPI in RTT-led research. Participants identified patient-focused research as a core motivational anchor, noting that demonstrable links to patient outcomes sustain clinical engagement where abstract research goals do not. RTTs are well positioned to lead PPI as a genuine driver of equitable, person-centred research design, given their proximity to patients during treatment and their sustained therapeutic relationships. This positions them as natural advocates for diverse patient voices including those historically under-represented in cancer research [22]. Explicitly elevating PPI within RTT research frameworks at all system levels offers both an ethical foundation and a practical motivator for broadening participation.

Unlike survey-based studies, the profession-led and internationally diverse design of this study enabled practitioners from varied healthcare systems to generate contextually grounded recommendations collaboratively. The iterative two-session design with member-checking strengthened analytical credibility, and the cultural web framework provided a theoretically structured lens not previously applied to RTT research culture.

Several limitations should be noted. Participants may represent RTTs with a pre-existing interest in research, potentially reflecting more optimistic perspectives than would emerge from less research-engaged colleagues. The workshop format did not permit detailed exploration of how recommendations would translate into practice, and the design does not allow causal inferences about the impact of specific strategies on research outputs, nor does it capture patient or managerial perspectives which may influence feasibility and acceptability.

Future work should prioritise implementation research examining whether centres adopting these recommendations achieve anticipated outcomes. Comparative effectiveness studies would determine which capacity-building initiatives deliver greatest impact relative to investment, and longitudinal cohort designs following RTTs through structured development pathways would illuminate the enabling factors that sustain research engagement over time.

Conclusion

RTTs collectively articulate a clear and actionable vision for research culture development, grounded in the recognition that sustained participation requires systemic support rather than individual exceptionalism. Applying the cultural web framework alongside a Preserve, Adapt, and Innovate structure produced a structured set of recommendations, presented in Fig. 2, that are rooted in practitioner experience and transferable across diverse healthcare contexts. Translating these recommendations into practice requires coordinated action from departments, national professional bodies, and international organisations. The profession now faces a choice of continuing to rely on exceptional individuals navigating unsupportive systems, or to deliberately build infrastructures where research participation becomes an expected and enabled dimension of RTT professional practice and a visible contributor to patient-centred, evidence-based care.
