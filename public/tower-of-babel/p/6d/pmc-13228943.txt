Assessing antimicrobial resistance in Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli O157 isolates from human clinical cases in the United States, 2010–2021
Authors: Tarjani Bhatt, Csaba Varga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo characterize antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns and temporal trends among Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli O157 (STEC O157) clinical isolates from humans in the United States.Patients and methods1,995 STEC O157 isolates collected from cases between 2010 and 2021 were analyzed. Resistance to antimicrobials was assessed, and temporal trends were evaluated using the Mann-Kendall test. Associations between AMR and demographic factors, including age and region, were examined using logistic regression models.ResultsResistance was most common to tetracycline (12.4%) and sulfisoxazole (9.9%), with temporal increases observed in resistance for these agents, along with chloramphenicol and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Mann-Kendall tau = 0.69–0.87, p < 0.001). Regional differences in AMR were noted for tetracycline, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and chloramphenicol. Cases aged 20–39 had higher odds of resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole compared to those aged 0–4.ConclusionsSince antimicrobials are not recommended for treating STEC O157 infections in humans, the sources of resistant strains could be foodborne, direct animal contact, the environment, or treatment for bacterial coinfections. The increase in the prevalence of AMR among STEC O157 strains in humans warrants investigation into the factors driving the emergence of resistant isolates. These findings call for an “One Health” approach for antimicrobial stewardship to mitigate AMR risks.

Introduction

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli O157 (STEC O157) is a main enteric pathogen, transmitted via contaminated foods, direct contact with infected livestock, mainly ruminants, their feces, or their contaminated environment [1]. Infections with STEC O157 can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) with symptoms including abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, and bloody or watery diarrhea, or in severe cases, kidney failure, coma, and death [2,3]. Worldwide, STEC O157 causes approximately 2.8 million acute illnesses annually, along with 3,890 HUS cases, 270 end-stage renal disease cases, and 230 deaths worldwide [4]. In the United States of America (U.S.), STEC O157 affect approximately 97,000 persons, causing 3,270 hospitalizations and 30 fatalities annually [5]. It was reported that between 2009 and 2018, an average of 0.9 STEC O157 outbreaks per 10 million population occurred annually in the US [6].

The majority of STEC O157 outbreaks are associated with contact with livestock, including cattle, small ruminants, pigs, and poultry, and with the consumption of their contaminated food products, including beef, raw/undercooked meat, and raw dairy products [7,8]. Lately, several STEC O157H7 outbreaks were linked to the consumption of contaminated leafy greens, such as lettuce and spinach, that were contaminated by cattle feces or irrigation water tainted with animal waste [7,9–11].

STEC O157 infections are most common in young children, specifically those under 5 years of age, and they are at higher risk of developing HUS [12]. An analysis of U.S. outbreak data from 2010–2017 revealed that children <5 years old showed the highest proportion of STEC infections [13]. Similar age-specific susceptibility was also supported in a research study, where STEC-associated diarrhea and HUS were prevalent among children under age 5 [14,15].

Regional variation in STEC prevalence was noticed across the U.S, with culture-confirmed incidence in 2016 exceeding the national average in at least half of the states in the Midwest and West [16]. A surveillance study from Argentina reported that STEC-associated diarrhea was not evenly distributed across the country; the highest rates occurred in southern regions [17].

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a threat to both human and animal health, as infections with antimicrobial-resistant enteric pathogens increase morbidity and mortality and treatment costs [18]. One of the main drivers of the emergence of AMR is the selective pressure exerted by antimicrobial use in human medicine, livestock and poultry production, and food technology [19,20].

To mitigate the increase of AMR in livestock and poultry production in the U.S., the use of medically important antimicrobials in feed and water has required veterinary oversight since 2015 [21]. This regulation aims to reduce unnecessary antimicrobial use in livestock, decrease selection pressure, and limit the development of multidrug-resistant bacteria. It is expected that these measures will reduce the antimicrobial-resistant bacterial load in the food chain [22].

Despite official recommendations against antibiotic use for STEC infections due to the potential risk of worsening symptoms and an increased risk of developing HUS, antibiotics are prescribed for severe cases or for bacterial co-infections [23–26]. In Michigan, recent surveillance of enteric cases identified a high prevalence of AMR in STEC O157 isolates [27]. In addition, in a recent analysis of the surveillance of STEC O157 isolates from human cases in England between 2016 and 2020, frequent resistance to tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and sulfonamides was identified [28].

The emergence of AMR in STEC O157 is a public health threat, yet it has received limited focus in AMR research. In the US, surveillance programs are monitoring AMR in STEC O157 [29]. However, there is a gap in longitudinal studies that track temporal trends in AMR, as well as assessing regional and age-related variations in AMR across the U.S. This study aims to fill this gap by analyzing publicly available data on AMR in STEC O157 clinical isolates of humans collected by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) through the National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System (NARMS) program across the US between 2010 and 2021. Identifying emerging AMR patterns, age groups at higher risk for AMR, and regions with elevated AMR will provide information that can be used by public health stakeholders when designing antimicrobial stewardship programs.

Materials and methods

Data source

We obtained publicly available data on AMR in clinical human STEC O157 isolates collected between 2010 and 2021 by the CDC NARMS program [30]. Public health laboratories across all 50 states and Washington, D.C., send every 20th STEC O157 isolate from sporadic cases of illness, as well as all isolates linked to multistate outbreaks, to the CDC NARMS laboratory for antimicrobial susceptibility testing [31].

The CDC reports regional AMR data using the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) regional framework [32], which divides the U.S. into ten administrative regions, that includes States, as follows: Region 1 - Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont, Region 2 - New Jersey, New York, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands, Region 3 - Delaware, District of Columbia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and West Virginia, Region 4 - Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Tennessee, Region 5 - Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Ohio, and Wisconsin, Region 6 - Arkansas, Louisiana, New Mexico, Oklahoma, and Texas, Region 7 - Iowa, Kansas, Missouri, and Nebraska, Region 8 - Colorado, Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Utah, and Wyoming, Region 9 - Arizona, California, Hawaii, Nevada, American Samoa, Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, Federated States of Micronesia, Guam, Marshall Islands, and Republic of Palau, Region 10 - Alaska, Idaho, Oregon, and Washington.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by the CDC NARMS laboratory using the broth microdilution method to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for each isolate [31]. We included the following antimicrobial agents: ampicillin (AMP), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AUG), ceftriaxone (AXO), chloramphenicol (CHL), ciprofloxacin (CIP), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (COT), sulfisoxazole (FIS), cefoxitin (FOX), gentamicin (GEN), nalidixic acid (NAL), and tetracycline (TET). Resistance was interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) breakpoints.

Descriptive statistics and data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (Version 4.5.1) within the RStudio platform (Version 1.4.1106).

Temporal trends in AMR

To assess temporal trends in AMR, the Mann-Kendall test (MKT) was used to evaluate the statistical significance of changes in resistance to each antimicrobial over the study period. We also applied the LOESS (Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing) method, which fitted multiple weighted least squares regressions using 75% of the data points within each local regression (span = 0.75). The resulting smoothed trend lines were visualized to illustrate the overall resistance trends while accounting for fluctuations in the data.

Multivariable logistic regression models

We constructed separate multivariable logistic regression models for each antimicrobial to evaluate the associations between the resistance status of an STEC O157 isolate (resistant = 1 / susceptible = 0) and predictor variables. These predictors included year (categorical, with 2013 as the reference, to allow for comparisons of resistance probabilities across subsequent years), region (categorical, the region with the lowest prevalence as the reference), and age group (categorical, with the 0–4 age group as the reference, due to their higher risk for severe infections). The final models reported odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant based on the Wald chi-square test. Odds ratios greater than 1 (OR >1) indicate a higher likelihood of resistance, while ORs less than 1 (OR <1) suggest a lower likelihood of resistance in relation to the reference categories, with all other predictors held constant at their reference levels.

Results

Prevalence of AMR in STEC O157 isolates

Among 1995 human clinical isolates of STEC O157, 916 (81.80%) were susceptible to all the tested antimicrobials. The highest proportion of resistance was observed to tetracycline (n = 247; 12.38%; 95% CI: 10.96–13.90) and sulfisoxazole (n = 197; 9.87%; 95% CI: 8.60–11.26) (Figure 1). In contrast, low and very low resistance were observed to ampicillin (n = 75; 3.75%; 95% CI: 2.96–4.68), nalidixic acid (n = 50; 2.50%; 95% CI: 1.86–3.29), ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin (n = 13; 0.65%, 95% CI: 0.34–1.11), respectively. For tetracycline, compared to 2010, the odds of an STEC O157 isolate being resistant were higher in 2015 (OR = 2.48; 95% CI: 1.06–5.79; p = 0.037), and the odds of resistance remained elevated in subsequent years, while holding the other predictor variables constant. Among age groups, the 20–39 age group had higher resistance odds (OR = 2.14; 95% CI: 1.07–4.27; p = 0.031) compared to the reference age category of 0–4 years, holding covariates constant.

Discussion

This study assessed temporal, regional, and demographic differences in AMR of STEC O157 infections in human cases across the United States between 2010 and 2021, reported to the CDC via the NARMS. Among the 1995 STEC O157 isolates, 81.80% were susceptible to all the tested antimicrobials. Tetracycline (12.4%) and sulfisoxazole (9.9%) exhibited the highest resistance prevalence, followed by chloramphenicol (7.8%) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (5.1%). However, since these drugs are not recommended for treating STEC O157 infections, their resistance may not have significant clinical relevance. Temporal patterns revealed a significant increasing trend in resistance for tetracycline, sulfisoxazole, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Mann-Kendall tau = 0.69–0.87, p < 0.001). In addition, regional differences in the odds of AMR were observed for tetracycline, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and chloramphenicol. For all antimicrobials tested, except trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, no significant (p ≤ 0.05) age differences in the odds of resistance were observed in the multivariable logistic regression models. For trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, individuals in the 20–39 age group had the highest odds of resistance compared to the reference age group (0–4 years old).

The increasing trend in AMR observed in our study is consistent with current literature describing the emergence of AMR in human STEC O157 clinical isolates, particularly those associated with foodborne infections. For example, a surveillance study from England found that 5.8% of human STEC O157 isolates exhibited resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, trimethoprim/sulfonamide, and tetracycline [28]. Furthermore, a recent genomic analysis of human STEC O157 isolates in England (2016–2020) reported that 14.7% of isolates carried at least one AMR gene, with the highest resistance observed against tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and aminoglycosides. The study emphasized the zoonotic transmission risk of STEC via contaminated food or contact with infected animals [33]. Lastly, the increasing AMR trends identified in our study from 2016 to 2021 among tetracycline, sulfisoxazole, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and chloramphenicol agree with a Michigan-based surveillance study conducted in a similar timeframe [27].

In our study, the presence of resistance to tetracycline and sulfonamides in clinical STEC O157 isolates cannot be attributed to treating STEC O157 infections, as antibiotics are not recommended due to the potential risk of exacerbating Shiga toxin production and causing HUS. Also, co-infected individuals may receive antimicrobial treatment for other infections, which could exert selection pressure on STEC O157 isolates, promoting the emergence of AMR. Additionally, a potential source of these resistant strains may include contracting from infected animals, contaminated food, or the environment. The transmission of antimicrobial-resistant STEC O157 strains from animal reservoirs, where antibiotics are commonly used in agriculture, to humans via the food chain is a well-established route, and the presence of resistance in human clinical isolates could reflect this pathway [34].

The use of antimicrobials in livestock has been described as a cause for the selection of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria, including STEC O157 [35]. The antimicrobials that showed resistance in our study, tetracyclines and sulfonamides, are commonly used in U.S. livestock farms for both therapeutic and preventative purposes [36]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) banned the use of antimicrobials as growth promoters in food animals, and since 2017, requires veterinary oversight for the use in livestock and poultry production of medically important antimicrobials administered via feed or water [37]. The rationale for these regulatory changes was to reduce non-therapeutic antimicrobial use in livestock to mitigate selective pressure for resistance, and thereby curb the emergence and spread of resistant bacteria in both animals and humans [38]. However, our findings present a paradox: rather than a decline, we observed a significant increasing trend in resistance in human clinical isolates of STEC O157, especially for tetracycline, sulfisoxazole (a sulfonamide), chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole. This trend persisted across 2010–2021, crossing the pre‑ and post‑implementation period of the FDA rule. This finding might have several explanations. Resistance genes and resistant STEC O157 strains established in animal reservoirs before 2017 may persist in the food production chain for years, maintaining or even expanding their prevalence despite reduced antimicrobial use. Also, it might be that the use of medically important antimicrobials is still permitted for disease treatment, control, or prevention under veterinary oversight [39], and in intensive livestock production where disease risk remains high (crowding, suboptimal sanitation, stress), these antimicrobials may still be used frequently, sustaining their selective pressure on the bacterial communities of the gastrointestinal tract [40]. In addition, it could be due to the continuous selection pressure of these drugs, as the sales of medically important antibiotics dropped initially after the 2017 ban; however, their use has not declined across all animal sectors, and in cattle and swine production, remained stable or even increased post-2017 [41]. Moreover, resistant STEC O157 strains may spread through environmental contamination and water [19], wild birds [42], wild deer [43], and consumption of vegetables [9], not solely via direct contact with infected animals or their contaminated food products, suggesting that preventive efforts should address all routes of maintenance and dissemination of AMR. Lastly, antimicrobial use to treat bacterial co-infections in patients may contribute to the emergence of AMR in STEC O157 isolates.

In our study, regional differences in AMR among STEC O157 were detected, underscoring the need for geographically adapted prevention and control strategies to mitigate the STEC burden regionally. The differences in STEC prevalence have also been described in previous studies from Argentina [17], the United Kingdom [44], and Canada [45,46], where they were attributed to the circulation of different strains in the local cattle populations [44], as well as socioeconomic and demographic factors within local populations [45,46].

The increasing trend in resistance to sulfonamides, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol observed in our study suggests that these STEC strains may exhibit resistance to multiple antimicrobial classes simultaneously [22]. This could be a result of co-selection of resistance mechanisms, where the use of one antimicrobial agent selects for resistance to others. Co-selection occurs when the resistance genes for sulfonamides, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol are located on the same mobile genetic elements, such as plasmids or transposons, which can be transferred between bacteria [19].

Before interpreting the results of our study, some limitations should be considered. This study relied solely on phenotypic resistance data; future research should incorporate Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) to identify AMR genes and explore the genetic determinants of AMR emergence and co-resistance patterns.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified an increasing trend in resistance to sulfisoxazole, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol among STEC O157 clinical isolates from human cases across the United States between 2010 and 2021. In addition, regional variations and age-related differences in AMR were identified. As the use of antimicrobials to treat STEC O157 in humans is not recommended, the multidrug-resistant strains are transmitted to humans, likely through foodborne routes, contact with infected animals, the environment, or even AMU in humans to treat co-infections. To address the emergence of AMR in STEC O157, a “One Health” approach is needed that should include integrated surveillance across human, animal, and environmental reservoirs, enhanced antimicrobial stewardship, and targeted interventions. Lastly, multi-faceted strategies aimed at reducing selective pressure on pathogens, improving biosecurity practices, and promoting alternative disease control methods will be important in combating AMR in foodborne pathogens.
