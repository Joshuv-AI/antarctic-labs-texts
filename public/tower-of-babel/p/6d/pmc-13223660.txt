What is the burden of fractures for a nation? An analysis of statutory health insurance cases in Germany
Authors: Dominik Szymski, Nike Walter, Susanne Baertl, Volker Alt, Markus Rupp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFractures represent a major clinical and socioeconomic burden; however, existing epidemiological data in Germany are largely based on inpatient cohorts and may underestimate the true incidence by omitting conservatively managed cases. This study aimed to quantify the national burden of fractures using nationwide outpatient statutory health insurance data.MethodsA retrospective analysis of nationwide outpatient billing data from the Central Institute for Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (ZI) was conducted, covering approximately 90% of the German population. All ICD-10 fracture diagnoses recorded between 2015 and 2022 were included. To avoid double-counting, repeated documentation of identical diagnoses within four consecutive quarters was excluded. Incidence rates were calculated per 100 000 inhabitants using population data from the Federal Statistical Office. Fractures were analysed by anatomical site, age and sex using descriptive epidemiological methods.ResultsA total of 18 171 815 fractures were identified between 2015 and 2022. In 2022, 2 400 606 fractures were recorded, corresponding to an incidence of 2845.71 per 100 000 inhabitants. Overall fracture incidence increased by 7.5% during the study period, with a temporary decline in 2020 followed by a rebound in subsequent years. The most frequent fracture sites were the distal radius, toes, fingers, ribs and lumbar spine. The largest relative increases were observed for distal femur (+38.7%), pelvic ring (+37.1%) and cervical spine fractures (+36.9%). Fracture incidence was higher in females than in males and increased markedly with age, particularly in individuals aged ≥65 years.ConclusionsThis nationwide analysis demonstrates a growing fracture burden in Germany, particularly among older adults. Outpatient data provide a comprehensive perspective on fracture epidemiology beyond surgically treated cases. These findings highlight the need for improved strategies in fracture prevention and outpatient care, and underline the importance of integrating inpatient and outpatient data for future health policy planning.

Introduction

Fractures continue to be a significant clinical and socioeconomic challenge across healthcare systems worldwide.1 As a common result of trauma and degenerative conditions, fractures are among the main reasons for surgical intervention in orthopaedic and trauma surgery. The first surgical procedures for fracture treatment were described as early as 1843.2 In Germany, the demographic shift toward an increasingly ageing population is expected to lead to a higher rate of fragility fractures, which will have significant implications for healthcare planning and resource distribution.3

Despite its high clinical and economic importance, there is currently no comprehensive nationwide data on the total burden of fractures in Germany. Although surgically treated fractures are relatively well-documented due to hospital-based reporting systems, a significant portion of fractures, especially those managed conservatively in outpatient settings, are still under-reported.4

Existing epidemiological data on fractures in Germany mainly come from inpatient or hospital-based datasets, which show an increasing burden of fractures, especially among older adults and for major osteoporotic fracture types like hip and vertebral fractures.4 5 However, these datasets naturally focus on more severe cases that often require surgery, providing only a partial view of fracture epidemiology. This approach systematically underestimates the true burden of fractures, as a substantial proportion, particularly minor trauma and many fragility fractures, are managed entirely in the outpatient setting. In the German healthcare system, outpatient care also plays a central role in the follow-up of fractures treated in hospital, making it a key point of contact for nearly all fracture patients. While many fractures, including minor injuries and many fragility fractures, are managed conservatively in outpatient settings and are not included in hospital-based data.6 7 Additionally, outpatient care is the main setting for follow-up after inpatient fracture treatment in the German healthcare system. As a result, outpatient billing data offer a more comprehensive, population-based view, capturing the entire range of fracture care and allowing for a more accurate estimation of the national fracture burden.

Therefore, nationwide outpatient billing data offer a unique and more comprehensive perspective on fracture epidemiology at the population level. The use of such data is essential to capture the full spectrum of fracture care and to provide a more accurate estimation of the national fracture burden.

The present study aims to address this knowledge gap by analysing statutory health insurance data from Germany to assess the national burden of fractures. The estimation indicates that nearly every fracture undergoes follow-up investigation in the outpatient sector by general practitioners or outpatient orthopaedic surgeons, regardless of whether it is treated surgically or conservatively and whether the case is inpatient or outpatient. Most fractures are diagnosed in an emergency room setting.6 Depending on the future treatment, some fractures are initially treated inpatient, while others are treated on an outpatient. However, in Germany, follow-up care after fracture treatment is predominantly performed in the outpatient sector, and most patients with fractures are expected to have at least one outpatient consultation during the treatment course—either for diagnosis or follow-up.

Based on these follow-ups, we estimate a comprehensive overview of fracture epidemiology in Germany using statutory health insurance data. Specifically, the objectives of this study are: (1) to provide a nationwide overview of fracture incidence in Germany between 2015 and 2022 and (2) to analyse changes in the male and female populations.

Materials and methods

This study retrospectively examined patients who experienced any type of fracture in Germany from January 2015 to December 2022. The analysis included all ICD-10 codes related to fractures for patients (see table 1). Outpatient data from the Central Institute for Statutory Health Insurance Physicians in Germany (ZI) were analysed for this study. The ZI database includes nationwide outpatient billing data according to §295 of the German Social Code (SGB). It comprises statutory health insurance patients who had contact with an outpatient doctor with a fracture diagnosis, identified by coding a diagnosis code (see online supplemental table 1) during the incident quarter. Similar fracture codes were excluded within four subsequent quarters after the incident quarter for the same patient. Because outpatient diagnoses in the statutory health insurance system are reported on a quarterly basis, repeated documentation of the same fracture diagnosis may occur during follow-up visits. To avoid multiple counting of the same fracture event, identical ICD-10 fracture codes recorded for the same patient within the four subsequent quarters after the initial diagnosis were excluded. This approach allowed us to count each fracture event only once while minimising potential overestimation due to follow-up consultations.

Fracture cases are recorded in the database through ICD-10 diagnostic coding submitted for reimbursement by outpatient physicians. Statutory health insurance covers 74.26 million people, which represents 90% of the German population in 2023.8 The remaining residents are mainly covered by private health insurance and are not included, including civil servants, self-employed individuals and employees earning above the mandatory insurance threshold. Since comprehensive outpatient billing data from the private insurance sector are not centrally available, these patients were not included in this analysis. Age and sex stratifications were analysed according to the variables provided within the ZI dataset. Age refers to the patient’s age at the time of the documented fracture diagnosis within the respective reporting year. Sex was recorded according to the classification used in statutory health insurance billing data.

The dataset was provided in aggregated nationwide form and did not include patient-level identifiers or regional stratification variables. Therefore, analyses were limited to national-level descriptive statistics. Data were provided and are available on request from the Central Institute for Statutory Health Insurance Physicians in Germany (ZI).

Assuming that every patient with a fracture - regardless of whether they were treated as an inpatient or outpatient - undergoes subsequent outpatient follow-up, we considered this dataset to provide an almost complete representation of all fractures within the study period.

The analysis was based on aggregated nationwide outpatient billing data from the Central Institute for Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (ZI), covering patients insured through the statutory health insurance system across Germany. Data were available as annual case numbers for the period between 2015 and 2022. Absolute frequencies refer to the total number of fracture cases recorded in the dataset for a given year or anatomical location. Relative frequencies were calculated as incidence rates per 100 000 population using annual population data obtained from the Federal Statistical Office of Germany (Destatis). The study was designed as a descriptive epidemiological analysis. Therefore, no inferential statistical tests were performed. Instead, temporal trends were evaluated using descriptive statistics, including incidence rates and growth rates across the study period. Prior to analysis, the dataset underwent a structured data cleaning process. All ICD-10 codes corresponding to fractures were extracted from the ZI database and included in the initial dataset. To avoid double-counting of the same fracture event during follow-up consultations, repeated documentation of identical fracture codes within the four subsequent quarters after the index diagnosis was excluded.

Undifferentiated fracture codes (eg, S92.9, fracture of foot, unspecified) were included in the overall fracture count but were excluded from detailed anatomical subgroup analyses to ensure accurate classification of fracture locations.

All analyses were conducted using anonymised and aggregated national-level data provided by the ZI database. Data collection was performed using Microsoft Excel V.16.85 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA), and figure design was performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct or dissemination plans of this study. Data were provided by nationwide outpatient billing data from the Central Institute for Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (ZI), covering patients insured through the statutory health insurance system across Germany.

Results

In this study, 18 171 815 fractures from 2015 to 2022 were included in the analysis. In 2022, a total of 2 400 606 fractures were reported, with an incidence of 2845.71 fractures per 100 000 population. Over the entire period, the growth rate for all fractures in Germany was 7.46% (figure 1). The most common fractures reported in the outpatient sector were distal radius fractures (210.76 per 100 000 population), toe fractures (193.73), finger fractures (186.1), rib fractures (156.51) and lumbar spine fractures (127.76) (figure 2). The largest increases were observed in distal femur fractures (+38.7%, incidence from 7.9 to 11.0), pelvic ring fractures (+37.1%, incidence from 29.6 to 40.5) and cervical spine fractures (+36.9%, incidence from 7.9 to 10.8) (table 1, online supplemental table 2).

In the overall population, the incidence of fractures was higher in females (3189.88 per 100 000 population) than in males (2491.26 per 100 000 population), with a more significant increase observed in females (+9.3%) compared with males (+5.2%) (figure 3). The largest increases in fracture incidence among females were seen in cervical spine (+38.4%), pertrochanteric femur (+39.0%) and distal femur (+40.9%). Conversely, several fracture sites, including sternum, combined distal radius and ulna and os sygomaticus and upper jaw fractures, showed a declining trend over time, especially in males, with decrease rates of -12.2%, -7.7%, and -3.1%, respectively (online supplemental table 3).

In the age-stratified analysis, fracture incidence growth was generally higher in patients under 65 years compared with those 65 and older. The most notable increase in the younger group (<65 years) was seen in fractures of the distal femur (+44.6%), distal tibia (+26.7%) and ossa tarsi (+26.6%). Conversely, several fracture types decreased in the older population (65+ years), most notably combined distal radius and ulna (-20.3%), ulna shaft (-5.5%), and finger (-5.3%) (online supplemental table 4, figure 4).

Discussion

This nationwide analysis of statutory health insurance data provides a comprehensive view of the fracture burden in Germany from 2015 to 2022. More than 18 million fractures treated in outpatient follow-ups were included in the analysis over this 8-year period. The findings reveal several important insights into the epidemiological and socioeconomic aspects of fracture incidence and treatment across anatomical regions, age groups and sexes.

One of the most notable findings is a 7.46% increase in total fracture cases during the study period, with more than 2.4 million fractures recorded in 2022, resulting in an incidence of 2845.71 fractures per 100 000 people annually. This trend highlights the growing burden of musculoskeletal trauma on the German healthcare system. For all inpatient-treated fractures in Germany between 2009 and 2019, a 14% increase was observed.4 9 In comparison with international data, similar trends have been reported. For example, registry-based data from the Netherlands demonstrated a 26% increase in fracture incidence over a 9-year time period.10 These consistent findings across healthcare systems support the assumption that demographic changes and population ageing are key drivers of rising fracture rates.

The distal radius was the most common fracture site. Additionally, fractures of the toe, finger and rib were among the most common fractures. There is a well-known, age- and sex-specific, as well as mechanism-specific peak for distal radius fractures. On the one hand, there is a peak among young male patients with sports injuries, and on the other hand, among elderly female patients with low-energy trauma.11 Single fractures of the toe, finger or rib are often managed conservatively and are typically under-represented in inpatient datasets.12 13 Their inclusion underscores the added value of outpatient data in providing a comprehensive picture of fracture epidemiology.

The data show a significant increase in fractures related to ageing and fragility, including cervical spine (+42.6%), pertrochanteric femur (+39.9%), distal tibia (+29.8%) and pelvic ring (+28.5%). These trends are likely linked to demographic changes, including longer life expectancies and an expanding elderly population, which heighten the risk of osteoporosis and fall-related injuries.14 15 This is further supported by the higher fracture rate in the 65+ population, where the incidence exceeded 5100 per 100 000 people—more than twice the rate seen in those under 65 in our study.

Amin et al identified in an investigation on residents in Olmsted County in Minnesota, USA, an increased prevalence of fractures by 11% in a 20-year period for residents aged over 50 years.16 An increasing rate of fractures in elderly patients, especially for osteoporotic fractures was already reported in previous literature.16 17 In particular, for the elderly population fractures are a severe injury with a long rehabilitation time, significant reduction in quality of life, mobility and a high rate of concomitant diseases.18 19 Although only 40% of all fracture patients in Germany were 65 years or older, the incidence was more than twice as high. Osteoporotic fractures often occur in this population and remain a high burden, in particular because of a 47% risk of secondary fractures within the first 2 years.20 When examining the localisation of the most frequent fractures among elderly patients, several characteristics were observed. The most frequent were fractures of the lumbar spine, with an incidence of 427 per 100 000 population, followed by distal radius (409/100 000), rib (317), proximal humerus (291) and thoracic spine (246). A similar pattern was identified in Japan, with an estimated 1–1.5 million vertebral fractures annually and a 10-fold increased risk compared with femoral neck fractures.17

Sex-specific differences were also evident, with higher fracture incidence observed in females, particularly for osteoporotic fracture sites such as the proximal humerus, lumbar spine and pelvic ring. These findings are in line with known biological differences in bone density and the increased risk of osteoporosis after menopause.21 The observed sex-specific patterns further support the need for targeted prevention strategies in high-risk populations. However, the present dataset does not include detailed clinical information on potential confounders such as comorbidities, medication use, bone mineral density or trauma mechanisms. Therefore, the observed age-related differences should be interpreted cautiously, as multiple factors may contribute to these epidemiological trends.

Temporal analysis revealed a distinct pattern during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a temporary decline in fracture incidence in 2020, followed by a rebound in subsequent years. This trend is likely explained by reduced mobility, decreased participation in sports, and lower exposure to occupational and traffic-related risks during lockdowns.2224 In contrast, fragility fractures appeared less affected by these external factors, supporting the hypothesis that these injuries are primarily driven by intrinsic patient-related risk factors rather than by environmental exposure.

From a healthcare planning perspective, these findings have important implications. First, the rising rate of fragility fractures underscores the need for targeted fall-prevention and osteoporosis-management programmes, especially for older women. Several fall-prevention strategies are documented in the literature and have been shown to significantly reduce falls and related injuries.25 For example, Dykes et al reported a 15% reduction in falls with a nurse-led fall-prevention toolkit.26 Fall prevention education was also shown to be a cost-effective and efficient way to reduce falls.27 Alongside these mechanism-based prevention tools for the elderly with a high rate of fragility fractures, preventing osteoporosis remains an important aspect.28 Patients with osteoporosis and a vertebral fracture are reported to have a fivefold increased risk of subsequent vertebral fractures and a twofold to threefold increased risk of fractures elsewhere.29 Current guidelines recommend osteoporosis screening for female postmenopausal patients and male patients over 50 years old. Basic therapy, including vitamin D and calcium should be started, along with physical exercise and proper nutrition. In cases of high secondary fracture risk, specific osteoanabolic medications should be discussed with the patient.30 31 Based on gender- and age-specific trends, a valuable framework for resource distribution and preventive strategies tailored to at-risk populations should be provided.

Beyond preventive strategies, the observed shift in fracture patterns has substantial health-economic implications. The marked increase in fragility-related fractures—particularly pelvic ring, distal femur and cervical spine fractures - suggests growing demand for resource-intensive care, including longer hospitalisations, rehabilitation and long-term outpatient follow-up. These fractures are typically associated with higher direct medical costs and indirect costs from loss of independence and the need for social care.32 In contrast, the decline in minor fractures, such as toe or finger injuries, may have limited economic impact because of their generally low treatment costs. The increasing proportion of elderly patients further amplifies the economic burden, as this population often requires multidisciplinary management and faces higher risks of complications and secondary fractures. From a healthcare system perspective, these trends indicate a shift from high-volume, low-cost injuries to lower-volume but high-cost fragility fractures. Consequently, future resource allocation should focus not only on prevention but also on optimising care pathways, strengthening outpatient structures and integrating fracture liaison services to reduce re-fracture rates and associated costs.33

Limitations

This study’s major strength lies in its use of nationwide, standardised health insurance billing data, which captures a large and representative segment (approximately 90%) of the German population. However, the analysis is limited to patients insured through the statutory health insurance system, and individuals with private health insurance were not included due to the absence of comparable nationwide outpatient datasets. As privately insured individuals may differ in demographic or socioeconomic characteristics, a certain degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Given the large population coverage of the statutory health insurance system and the standardised nationwide reporting structure, the dataset is still likely to provide a reliable representation of national fracture epidemiology. A further limitation is that the dataset includes only outpatient billing data and does not allow direct linkage with inpatient hospital records. Although many patients treated surgically in hospitals are subsequently followed in the outpatient sector and therefore captured in this dataset, the integration of inpatient and outpatient data would provide an even more comprehensive assessment of the national fracture burden. Therefore, fracture prevalence may be underestimated. Nevertheless, we assume that every inpatient treated patient is in some form of outpatient follow-up with their family doctor or general practitioner or orthopaedic surgeon. Another important limitation is the reliance on ICD-10 coding within administrative billing data, which may be subject to miscoding and misclassification bias. Diagnoses in this database are primarily documented for reimbursement purposes rather than for research, so inaccurate, incomplete or non-specific coding cannot be fully excluded. In particular, anatomically nonspecific fracture codes may have led to an underestimation of specific fracture subtypes, while recurrent, concomitant or complex fractures may not always have been captured with complete precision. Although the large nationwide dataset likely reduces the influence of isolated coding errors on overall epidemiological trends, these factors should be considered when interpreting subgroup-specific results. Another limitation is that the dataset does not include detailed patient-level information on potential risk factors such as comorbidities, medication use, lifestyle factors or socioeconomic status, nor does it include outcome or complication data. These variables were not available within the aggregated nationwide outpatient billing data used in this study.

Conclusion

The results of this study underscore the growing burden of fractures in Germany, especially in an ageing population. These findings highlight the urgent need for coordinated national strategies in fracture prevention, early detection, and both inpatient and outpatient treatment optimisation. Future studies integrating both ambulatory and hospital data will be essential to paint a complete picture of the fracture landscape and its impact on the German healthcare system.
