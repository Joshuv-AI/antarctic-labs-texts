Gastric cancer hospital-based registry: real-world gastric cancer data from Latin America and Europe

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer has a high incidence and mortality rate worldwide. Epidemiological, clinical, and molecular features significantly impact patient outcomes. In regions lacking a national gastric cancer registry, hospital-based registries can provide crucial data that may aid in planning therapeutic strategies for the disease.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort design was carried out in European Union (EU) and Latin American (LATAM) countries participating in the LEGACy project. Survival estimates were determined using actuarial Kaplan–Meier curves. Comparison was carried out with the log-rank test, and differences were considered statistically significant for P values ≤0.05.ResultsA total of 689 patients diagnosed with gastric cancer from November 2018 to November 2019 were included. Both cohorts had the body as the most common site reported (34.4% for EU and 51% for LATAM). The most used method for staging was computed tomography for both cohorts, although 6.9% of the LATAM population had positron emission tomography/computed tomography instead. Intestinal histological subtype was the most common (41.9% and 46.3% reported by EU and LATAM), while diffuse subtype was 44.9% for the LATAM and 21.3% for the EU population. Among patients tested for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), 12.5% were positive in the EU cohort and 13.8% in the LATAM cohort. For both cohorts, the most common site of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positivity was the gastroesophageal junction. Systemic treatment with curative intention was indicated in 50.7% in the EU cohort and 46.4% of the LATAM cohort. The most frequent scheme indicated for both the localized and the advanced setting was platinum-based (42.6% and 84.8% for EU and LATAM). Considering both cohorts, only 14.4% of the patients received second-line treatment, and 3% received a third-line treatment. After using Cox regression analysis, no difference in overall survival was reported, with a median of 10.9 months.ConclusionsDespite the limitations of hospital-based registry analysis, our study has provided valuable insights into clinical characteristics and treatment approaches of EU and LATAM populations.

Introduction

A lethal disease with poor overall survival, gastric cancer (GC) poses a significant global health challenge.1 GC ranks as the fifth most common cancer diagnosed and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.2 While 60% of cases are identified in Asia, there are countries in Latin America (LATAM) and Europe Union (EU) with an intermediate incidence rate, such as Chile, where GC is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among males.3

Several risk factors are associated with the development of GC. Among these, Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection and unhealthy lifestyle choices, such as obesity, smoking, alcohol, and processed meat consumption, are the most strongly linked. Additionally, atrophic gastritis, partial gastrectomy, and inherited genetic predispositions are recognized as significant factors associated with GC onset.4

Although GC is often considered one unique entity, it can be classified based on the anatomical subsites and comprises distinct histological entities and molecular subtypes.5 This subclassification has a direct impact on prognosis and treatment choices.6 Furthermore, molecular features, histological type, location, and genetic makeup of GC vary widely among patients worldwide.

Patients with GC often report a variety of symptoms that are challenging to interpret, such as indigestion, abdominal pain, changes in bowel movements, weight loss, and fatigue. Consequently, by the time of diagnosis more than half of cases have already metastasized to regional lymph nodes or distant organs. In the advanced stages of the disease, systemic treatment exhibits limited efficacy, with a median overall survival (OS) for patients of 11 months.6 Survival rates are only slightly improved if other therapies, such as targeted therapies or immunotherapy, are incorporated into the treatment regimen.7, 8, 9, 10

As 60% of cases are reported from Asia, particularly in Japan, China, and Korea, most extensive studies have been conducted in this population, leading to improved prevention and screening strategies.11 In western populations, clinical trials on GC have primarily taken place in North America and Europe, more extensively in Germany. Thus, despite the prevalence of GC in LATAM, especially in Chile, and in Eastern and Southern Europe, the populations of these countries remain insufficiently characterized. Therefore, comprehensive understanding of this disease necessitates characterization in these regions.

This study is part of the LEGACy project, which aims to collect real-world GC data from EU and LATAM populations. Through this ambitious initiative, our goal is to enhance our comprehension of clinicopathological details, molecular features, and risk factors in GC. Moreover, we aim to establish vital collaborative infrastructures, recognizing their pivotal role in the global pursuit of delivering precision medicine to patients.

As a potential tool to plan prevention and treatment strategies, the central goal of this study is to describe the epidemiology, characteristics, and treatment of GC using real-world data. To that end a hospital-based registry was implemented to describe and compare these populations. Hospital registries play a crucial role as valuable data sources for research studies. They aimed to enhance patient care quality, patient follow-up, serve educational purposes, and meet administrative needs. Multi-institutional cancer registries could significantly contribute to establish characteristics of reported GC within a specific population.

Methods

Design and patients

This is a multicenter prospective descriptive sub-study of the LEGACy project. Adult patients with gastric and gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) (Siewert type I, II, and III tumors) cancer, diagnosed between November 2018 and November 2019, from seven different countries in Latin America (Argentina, Chile, Mexico, and Paraguay) and Europe (Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain) were included.

Data was collected from the respective institutional medical records. Key data included patient demographics, GC risk factors, socio-economic factors, clinical characteristics (symptoms, disease staging, etiology), therapy, surgery, and outcomes. Description of all detailed variables is included as Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100088. Pathology characteristics were evaluated locally.

Institutions and partners

The clinical sites were selected based on their interest, expertise, and geographical location, as well as the recruitment potential. Eight reference centers (Instituto Investigación Sanitaria (Spain, INCLIVA), Instituto Nacional de Cancerología (Mexico, INCAN), Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile (Chile, PUC), Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology (Spain, VHIO), Institute of Pathology and Molecular Immunology of the University of Porto (Portugal, IPATIMUP), Laboratorio Gen Pat (Paraguay, GenPat), Stichting VU University Medical Center, (Netherlands, VUMC) and Instituto Alexander Fleming (Argentina, IAF) participated.

Patient recruitment lasted 1 year and was carried out by coordinating a multidisciplinary team in each center including gastroenterologists, pathologists, surgeons, and medical oncologists. To participate in each of the sub-studies, informed consent was collected from all patients and the procedures approved by each Institutional Research Board.

This study was funded by the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research innovation program, grant number 825832.

Statistical analysis

For interpretation, categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous data are described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), as data violate normality test assumptions. Categorical and numerical variables were compared using χ2 and Student’s t-test. Survival estimates were determined using actuarial Kaplan–Meier curves. Comparison was carried out with the log-rank test, and differences were considered statistically significant with a P value <0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 689 individuals diagnosed with GC between November 2018 and November 2019 were included in the study. Participant distribution was as follows: Argentina (69), Chile (112), Spain (124 in total, 43 from Valencia, and 81 from Barcelona), Mexico (70), Netherlands (159), Paraguay (31), and Portugal (124).

The median age at diagnosis for the entire population was 67 years (IQR, 57-75 years). When comparing the EU cohort to the LATAM cohort, however, the median age was 70.50 years (IQR 61.00-78.00 years) and 63.00 years (IQR 53.00-71.00 years), respectively. The diagnosis was more prevalent among males in both cohorts, 59.9% in the EU and 59.1% in LATAM.

Considering both cohorts, almost half (48.3%) had a family history of cancer, and 11.8% of the patients with GC had another diagnosed tumor.

In both cohorts, the stomach body emerged as the most frequently reported primary tumor site, accounting for 34.4% (n = 125) in the EU cohort and 51% (n = 125) in the LATAM cohort, whereas GEJ and antral pyloric presentation was similar. Within the LATAM cohort, the GEJ was the second most prevalent primary tumor site, presenting in 26.1% (n = 64) of patients, while the antral pyloric area was the least common site, found in 22.9% (n = 59) of cases. In the EU cohort, the antral pyloric site was the second most common at 29.8% (n = 105), with the GEJ being the least frequent at 27.5% (n = 100).

The predominant symptom in both cohorts was dysphagia, presenting in 42.4% of individuals. The GEJ emerged as the primary tumor site most associated with this symptom, manifesting in 66.7% of cases. Considering both cohorts, the most used method for staging was computed tomography (CT). It is noteworthy that 6.9% of the LATAM population alternatively had positron emission tomography (PET)/CT-[18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG). In addition to CT or PET/CT, 62.7% of patients in the EU cohort and 68.2% in the LATAM cohort underwent an endoscopic ultrasound staging (EUS).

The diagnosis at stage IV occurred in 44.8% (n = 111) of the LATAM cohort and 43.9% (n = 169) of the EU cohort. Stage I for LATAM was considerably lower than for the EU population (2.8% and 17.4%, respectively).

Considering the Lauren histological classification, intestinal histological subtype was the most common (41.9% and 46.3% reported by EU and LATAM), however, diffuse subtype was 44.9% for the LATAM and 21.3% for the EU population.

Among patients tested for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), 12.5% were positive in the EU cohort and 13.8% in the LATAM cohort. For both cohorts, the most common site of HER2 positive was the GEJ with 21.2%.

Treatment in the localized setting

Surgery was reported in 43.4% of the EU population and 48.9% in the LATAM cohort, with a palliative intention in 14.7% and 12%, respectively. Only 8.8% of patients who underwent surgery progressed during the duration of the study. Systemic treatment with curative intent was indicated in 50.7% of the EU cohort and 46.4% of the LATAM cohort. Regardless of the region, neoadjuvant treatment with chemoradiotherapy was the chosen treatment in GEJ cancer, reaching up to 66.7% in stage III.

Treatment in the advanced setting

The predominant regimen prescribed was platinum-based, encompassing a fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel (FLOT), FOLFOX, and capecitabine–oxaliplatin (CAPOX), constituting 42.6% and 84.8% for EU and LATAM, respectively. Immunotherapy was administered in 14.7% of EU cohort patients and 12% of LATAM cohort patients. A protocol-driven approach was chosen by 3% of the EU cohort and 1.1% of the LATAM population.

Considering both cohorts, only 14.4% of the patients received a second line of treatment, and 3% of the patients received a third line (Figure 1). The median OS for individuals aged ≥67 years was 15.9 months [95% confidence interval (CI) 12.8-22 months], contrasting with a median OS of 25.2 months (95% CI 17-33.6 months) for those aged <67 years (Figure 2). Discussion

In our study, the male predominance (59%) of patients in this study is consistent with the higher incidence of GC in men reported previously. The literature has observed that the incidence of GC rate increases with age and reaches a plateau between 55 and 80 years, with a median age at diagnosis being 67 years,12 aligned with this, the median age at diagnosis considering both cohorts in our study was 67 years old (IQR, 57-75 years). When analyzed separately, however, the LATAM cohort showed a younger onset with a median age of presentation of 63.00 years (95% CI 53.00-71.00 years), compared with a median age of 70.50 years (95% CI 61.00-78.00 years) in the EU cohort (P < 0.001).

According to our results, EUS was carried out in 66.4% of the patients, reflecting a higher rate of early-stage diagnosis compared with other studies.13 This may be explained by the fact that the clinical sites where the data were collected are centers of reference. For example, 51.3% of the patients were diagnosed at an early stage from the IPATIMUP in Portugal. Regarding the use of PET/CT, although the ESMO guidelines do not consider this study mandatory, due to the lack of positivity in diffuse-type and mucinous GC,6 6.9% of the LATAM population had used PET/CT alternatively to TC.

In respect of histology, while the World Health Organization provides guidelines for a more detailed classification of GC into distinct subgroups,14 it is noteworthy that the widely accepted Lauren classification continues to be dominant in use in EU and LATAM. The Lauren histological classification delineates two primary subtypes of GC: the intestinal and diffuse types. This classification not only underscores microscopic and macroscopic variances, but also highlights differences in cancer behavior and prognosis. Existing literature posits that intestinal-type GC is often linked to chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, while diffuse types typically originate from normal gastric mucosa.15,16 The distribution of intestinal and diffuse types exhibits notable variations across countries and continents. In this study, we observed that the EU cohort displayed a more pronounced disparity between diffuse and intestinal types (21.3% and 41.9%, respectively) compared with the LATAM cohort (44.9% and 46.3%, respectively) (P <0.001). Even though diffuse histological subtype has a poor prognosis, in our data the difference in histology did not translate in survival difference.

The recommendation for first-line therapy in GC is a two-drug regimen of fluoropyrimidine and platinum, with oxaliplatin mostly preferred over cisplatin due to lesser toxicity and similar effectiveness.6 For both cohorts the treatment choice aligns with guidelines recommendation. Remarkably in this registry, only a small rate of patients, 3% of the EU cohort and 1.1% of the LATAM population, could access clinical trials.

The proportion of patients reported to undergo second-line treatment varies between 20% and 30%.17 Tjulandin et al.,18 reported an occurrence of 85% in a real-world study conducted in Russia. Similarly, in Japan, the vast majority of patients diagnosed with metastatic GC undergo second-line therapy, with >50% of individuals receiving three lines of treatment.11 In contrast, when examining both our EU and LATAM cohorts, only 14.4% of stage IV patients by the time of the study received a second-line treatment, and only 3% received a third-line treatment. This might be related to the fact that 22.1% in the EU cohort and 19.9% in the LATAM cohort had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) ≥2 at diagnosis, and normally this population is underrepresented in clinical trials.

Before immunotherapy proved its benefit in GC, the prognosis of advanced GC was very poor, with a median OS of only 11 months.19 Our data coincide, with a median OS of 10.9 months for the advanced setting. To note, our data collection occurred before the approval of immunotherapy, and consequently, access to such treatments was primarily through participation in clinical trials. When considering all populations, the median OS for the LATAM cohort was 25.2 months and for the EU cohort 19.1 months, with no significant difference.

Patients with advanced GC have a poor prognosis with a 5-year relative survival rate of 6-8%. The aggressiveness of this disease should be considered together with the consequential fragility of this patient population.3 This is evidenced in our data where 46.3% mortality was reported.

Real-world hospital-based registries analyses have inherent limitations. They are commonly associated with retrospective investigations utilizing data acquired through patient chart reviews across multiple sites. This can lead to missing information and discrepancy between the centers and how they report data.

Conclusions

This hospital-based cohort of 689 patients framed in the LEGACy project is a sampling epidemiologic study of patients with GC conducted through an EU and LATAM partnership. This collaborative effort involved four organizations in LATAM and seven in the EU.

Despite the limited sample size, this LEGACy sub-study has highlighted differences and similarities between the EU and LATAM populations. We highlight the late diagnosis of the disease, and therefore the need of complementary diagnosis and staging methods. Furthermore, the low access to clinical trials is an important point to work on.

This analysis allows us to know the clinical practice of the centers participating in the LEGACy project, which could be a source of generating new hypotheses and future research.
