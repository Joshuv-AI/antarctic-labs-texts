A secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor-derived small peptide coating on a titanium surface enhances osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and reduces bacterial adhesion
Authors: Watchara Thepsupa, Nattikan Thongjui, Wannapat Chouyratchakarn, Onnicha Srisopar, Kritsana Kanthasap, Phornsawat Baipaywad, Wattanased Jarisarapurin, Jantipa Jobsri, Chayarop Supanchart, Sarawut Kumphune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstablishing strong osseointegration and preventing infection are essential for the success of titanium implants. Previous studies demonstrate that the secretory Leukocyte Protease Inhibitor (SLPI) enhances osteoblast adhesion on titanium surfaces, highlighting its potential clinical applications in orthopedics or dental implantation. This study examined SLPI-derived small peptides (SDSPs) as innovative coatings aimed at improving the biological and antibacterial characteristics of titanium (Ti). SDSPs were generated via in silico digestion of human SLPI sequence and subsequently synthesised. In this study, evaluation using the human foetal osteoblasts (hFOB 1.19) revealed that SDSP fragments (P1, P7, P8) notably enhanced cell adhesion by more than 50% in addition to improving spreading and proliferation on Ti surfaces wthout cytotoxic effects. The coatings demonstrated a notable increase in calcium deposition when compared to uncoated Ti. Analysis using XPS and profilometry validated the presence of peptides, and the P7 coating notably enhanced surface hydrophilicity. The anti-bacterial adhesion assays revealed that P7 effectively decreased the presence of both Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus on plastic surfaces. Interestingly, P7 fragments effectively prevented the adhesion of Gram-negative Escherichia coli on Ti, in a concentration-dependent manner. The findings indicate that SDSPs, especially P7, have the potential to enhance osteoblast activity while simultaneously decreasing bacterial adherence. Introduction

Biological Surface Science (BioSS) represents a dynamic, interdisciplinary field focused on the complex interfaces where synthetic materials meet biological environments. By investigating these unique boundary properties and fabricating biofunctional surfaces, researchers can engineer materials that seamlessly interact seamlessly with the body1. A prime example of this field’s clinical impact is the long-standing success of medical implants, which have significantly enhanced the quality of life for hundreds of thousands of patients. Each implant application presents a unique challenge, requiring specific biological and clinical tailoring to ensure compatibility and functionality within the human body1.

The main goal of modern implantology research is to develop devices that provide regulated, directed, and expedited healing. Besides enhancing normal wound healing processes, endosseous implants should promote the development of a distinctive interfacial layer and bone matrix with sufficient biomechanical qualities2. Titanium implants are widely used in orthopaedic and dental surgery due to their excellent biocompatibility, high strength, and corrosion resistance3. However, a major challenge to their long-term success is achieving robust osseointegration, the process by which bone tissue directly integrates with the implant surface4. Insufficient osseointegration can lead to implant failure, causing complications such as abnormal movement, inflammation, and bone resorption, which can even contribute to conditions like osteoporosis5. In addition, device-associated infections continue to be a significant factor in implant failure, primarily attributable to biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance6. To address this, researchers have focused on surface modification techniques to improve the biological interaction between the implant and bone tissue. A promising strategy involves coating titanium surfaces with proteins or peptides that can promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation6–8. A Previous report by Pelsőczi et al., examined the structural properties of layer-by-layer (LbL) self-assembled polypeptide films on titanium and glass substrates, which is particularly relevant for enhancing the biocompatibility of polypeptide in surface modifications for medical implants9.

Among the candidate biomolecules, Secretory Leukocyte Protease Inhibitor (SLPI) stands out due to its diverse pharmacological properties. SLPI is a serine protease inhibitor that has an anti-inflammatory effect via inhibiting the NF-KB signaling pathway, an antioxidant effect via activation of glutathione synthesis, an antibacterial effect, and an antiviral effect8,10–21. SLPI is also known to promote wound healing and enhance bone cell proliferation and differentiation7,8. Previous research has shown that coating titanium with recombinant human SLPI (rhSLPI) at concentrations of 10 and 100 µg/ml significantly enhanced human bone cell adhesion without causing toxicity12. These findings suggest that SLPI could be an ideal candidate for implant coatings, offering additional benefits beyond simple osseointegration, such as reducing inflammation and promoting osteoblast mineralisation22,23.

Despite the potential of full-length SLPI, its application may be limited by drawbacks such as a short half-life and susceptibility to degradation by protease enzymes10,24. This has spurred interest in developing small peptides derived from SLPI (SLPI-derived small peptides or SDSPs). These smaller molecules could potentially overcome the limitations of the full-length protein, due to the stability and capable of cellular internalization, while retaining its key therapeutic properties25.

This study therefores explore the potential of SLPI derived small peptides (SDSPs) as novel coating agents for titanium implants. SDSP coatings could enhance osteoblast adhesion, stimulate osteoblast proliferation, mineralization, upregulate osteogenic gene expression, reduce bacterial attachment on titanium surface, which, ultimately resulting in superior outcomes for orthopaedic and dentistry applications.

Results

Investigation of trypsin-digested SDSPs coating effects on osteoblast adhesion in culture wells

The hFOB 1.19 cells were seeded into 96-well plates, which was coated with SDSPs at concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM. The results of trypsin-digested SDSPs coating on osteoblast adhesion demonstrated that T2-coated Ti significantly enhanced cell adhesion at concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM (198.40 ± 10.03%, 210.20 ± 11.11%, and 150.60 ± 28.03%; p < 0.01) compared with the control group (approximately 100%) (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the T4-coated Ti significantly increased cell adhesion at 0.001 mM (208.60 ± 32.96%; p < 0.01) relative to the control (approximately 100%) (Fig. 1D). In addition, the T5-coated Ti significantly enhanced cell adhesion at concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM (178.80 ± 27.39%, 240.40 ± 18.50%, 192.20 ± 9.71%, and 165.60 ± 11.84%; p < 0.01) compared with the control group (approximately 100%) (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the T6-coated Ti significantly increased cell adhesion at 0.01 and 0.1 mM (148.20 ± 14.54% and 158.20 ± 5.80%; p < 0.01) relative to the control (approximately 100%) (Fig. 1F). However, T1, T3, T7, T8, and T9 showed no significant difference in cell adhesion compared to the respective control groups (Fig. 1A, C, G, H, and I). These results indicate that certain trypsin-derived peptide fragments can enhance cell adhesion.

The coating of SDSPs candidates improved osteoblast adhesion on the titanium surface

From the previous findings, Ti surfaces coated with SDSPs (T2, T4, T5, T6, P1, P4, P6, P7, P8, and P9) exhibited potential to enhance human osteoblast adhesion on culture well surfaces. Therefore, these peptide fragments were selected for further evaluation of osteoblast adhesion on Ti surfaces. The selection criteria were based on their ability to significantly increase cell adhesion compared with the control group at specific effective concentrations, as well as differences in peptide type and optimal concentration. The adhesion results on Ti surfaces were subsequently expressed as relative percent cell adhesion. The results demonstrated that coating the Ti surface with T4, T6, P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, P8, and P9 had significantly higher adhesion compared to the control group (111.80 ± 3.43%, 112.50 ± 3.43%, 158.80 ± 14.18%, 127.30 ± 7.96%, 144.40 ± 9.799%, 166.20 ± 12.11%, 154.70 ± 8.71%, 145.50 ± 7.70% vs. 100.00 ± 5.70%; p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A, B, D, E, F, G, H, I, and J). Furthermore, based on the results of cell adhesion on titanium surfaces, peptide fragments exhibiting relative cell adhesion values exceeding 150% were considered the most effective and selected for further evaluation. Cytotoxicity assessment using the MTT cell viability assay revealed that Ti surfaces coated with P1, P7, and P8 peptides showed no significant difference in cell viability compared with the control group (approximately 100%) (Fig. 3K), indicating that the peptide-coated Ti surfaces were non-toxic to hFOB 1.19 cells.

The effects of SDSPs on the morphology of human osteoblasts on titanium surfaces

The morphology of adhered hFOB 1.19 cells was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in the SEM images, hFOB 1.19 cells on uncoated Ti surfaces appeared small and predominantly spherical after 20 min of incubation (Fig. 4). After 60 min of incubation, SEM images revealed morphologies predominantly spherical with a greater number of cells were attached to the titanium surface compared to 20 min incubation (Fig. 5). To determine cell spreading ability, the cell surface area was determined. The results showed that there was an increase in relative percentage of cell surface area in P1, P7, and P8 groups (165.00 ± 11.66%, 124.90 ± 7.34%, and 126.30 ± 12.17%; p < 0.01) compared to the control group (approximately 100%) (Fig. 6). In addition, cells on Ti surfaces coated with P1, P7, and P8 peptide showed cytoplasmic extensions spreading outward, as indicated by the red arrows (Fig. 5, red arrow). These extensions suggest actin cytoskeletal reorganization, leading to actin polymerization at the cell periphery. This observation suggests that cells in these groups underwent more extensive spreading relative to the control condition.

The effects of SDSPs coating on bacterial attachment

The effect of SDSPs coating on the adhesion of Escherichia coli (E. coli) (Fig. 10A–C) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) (Fig. 10D–F) was evaluated using three SDSPs (P1, P7, and P8). In E. coli, coating with P1 and P8 showed no significant difference in bacterial adhesion at all concentrations (Fig. 10A and C). For P1, the relative bacterial adhesion was 104.80 ± 5.70%, 106.00 ± 7.05%, 88.04 ± 2.89%, and 95.12 ± 4.40% at 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM (p < 0.05). Similarly, P8 showed relative adhesion of 101.70 ± 2.89%, 85.22 ± 5.46%, 84.25 ± 5.09%, and 95.27 ± 8.21% at concentrations of 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, respectively (p < 0.05). In contrast, coating with P7 (Fig. 10B) showed a significant reduction in E. coli attachment, including 0.01 mM (65.81 ± 1.13%) and 0.1 mM (34.97 ± 2.51%) compared to the control (100% ± 2.88%) (p < 0.05). For S. aureus, the results of P1 and P8 showed similar trends to E. coli, with no significant reduction in bacterial adhesion (Fig. 10D and F). The relative bacterial adhesion of P1 was 101.10 ± 6.49%, 104.10 ± 6.28%, 111.00 ± 3.12%, and 93.67 ± 2.16% at 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, respectively (p < 0.05). A similar pattern was observed for P8, which had relative bacterial adhesion of 106.00 ± 3.44%, 98.74 ± 5.80%, 100.70 ± 3.17%, and 117.50 ± 9.04% at 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, respectively (p < 0.05). However, P7 (Fig. 10E) showed an interesting effect, which is significantly reduced S. aureus adhesion of 83.11 ± 3.49%, 62.39 ± 2.00%, and 57.02 ± 4.99% at 0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01 mM, (p < 0.05). Based on the adhesion finding, P7 demonstrated the remarkable effect of reducing both E. coli and S. aureus adhesion on plasticware. Thus, the P7 was selected as a candidate for a coating agent on the titanium surface.

The effect of SDSPs coating on titanium on bacterial attachment

The effect of P7-coated Ti surfaces on bacterial adhesion was evaluated against both E. coli and S. aureus at four different concentrations, which are 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, by using the MTT assay. The results showed a significant difference between the two bacterial species when exposed to the P7-coated Ti. In E. coli, P7 showed a significantly reduced E. coli adhesion of 80.55 ± 1.40%, 75.72 ± 1.68%, and 69.47 ± 3.93% at concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, (p < 0.05) compared to the control group (100.00 ± 0.92%) (Fig. 11A). Furthermore, these quantitative results are consistent with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images showing that P7 at 0.001–0.1 mM could reduce E.coli adhesion on Ti surface (Fig. 12A). The images showed that control and low-concentration groups (0.0001 mM) exhibited a high density of E. coli cell adhesion on the Ti surface. However, increases in P7 concentration from 0.0001 to 0.1 mM showed a significant decrease in E. coli cells on the Ti surface. In contrast, the effect of P7 on S. aureus adhesion showed no significant difference at all concentrations compared to the control (100% ± 3.70%), which are 89.59 ± 5.77%, 92.94 ± 5.83%, 97.84 ± 3.13%, and 97.94 ± 3.79% at concentrations of 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM, respectively (p < 0.05) (Fig. 11B). Moreover, the SEM images are consistent with the MTT result, showing that all concentrations of P7 on the Ti surface exhibited the S. aureus adhesion forming clusters or microcolonies, similar to control group. No differences were found in SEM images across all concentrations (Fig. 12B). These results demonstrated that P7 selectively inhibited the adhesion of Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli), while showing no significant effect on Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus) on the Ti surfaces.

Discussion

Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) has been shown to inhibit a range of inflammatory serine proteases. It plays several biological roles as a potent antimicrobial and antiviral peptide inhibitor. Recently, our previous study showed that nanoparticle delivery of recombinant human SLPI could enhance osteoblast adhesion and proliferation. Then, using SLPI to coat the Ti surface also presents the similar results on human osteoblasts. However, the half-life of SLPI in circulation is short (10–120 min)10. Therefore, improving the stability and efficiency of SLPI by alternatively using SLPI-derived short peptides (SDSPs) could be possible. Bioactive peptides are low molecular weight protein fragments of 2–20 amino acid residues that are considered to be a potent future pharmaceutical drug of the future26. As intrinsic signalling molecules for many physiological functions, peptides present an opportunity for therapeutic intervention that closely mimics natural pathways27. Moreover, small therapeutic peptides also have several advantages, such as their small size, cellular membrane penetration, high activity, specificity, affinity, stability, and less immunogenicity.

Osteoblast adhesion represents the preliminary interaction between the cell and the biomaterial. Osteoblast adhesion is typically limited on smooth surfaces (Sa < 500 nm) compared to moderately rough surfaces. Previous studies demonstrate that fewer cells initially adhere to these smoother surfaces due to the absence of micro-irregularities that facilitate mechanical interaction at the cellular level. Although a reduced number of cells may adhere, those that do typically exhibit more spreading on smooth surfaces28. To enhance osteoblast adhesion on titanium surface, surface modification is necessary to enhance osteoblast adhesion on titanium surfaces.

This current work is the first study to investigate the effects of SLPI-derived small peptides (SDSPs)-coated titanium, with the objective of evaluating its ability to enhance osteoblast adhesion and proliferation, as well as to reduce bacterial adhesion. From the finding, the SDSPs T2, T4, T5, T6, P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, and P9 exhibit the potential for enhanced human osteoblast cell adhesion on plasticware. Therefore, T4, T6, P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, P8 and P9 were therefore evaluated for efficacy on the Ti surface. However, the results demonstrated that certain peptide sequences, T2, T4, T5, and T6, were less effective in promoting cell adhesion. This reduced efficacy may be attributed to differences in the amount of peptide immobilised on the titanium surface. In addition, variations in peptide physicochemical properties, such as molecular size, amino acid composition, and surface charge, may influence peptide adsorption efficiency and orientation on the Ti surface, potentially limiting the accessibility of their bioactive domains for cell–surface interactions29. The experimental results indicate that coating with SDSPs peptides demonstrate greater affinity for plastic substrates than for titanium. This can be attributed to the enhanced hydrophobic interactions between the peptide and the polymer surface. The elevated surface energy and negative charge of the TiO₂ layer on titanium may promote the adsorption of competing proteins, potentially sterically hindering the SDSPs and obscuring their functional domains. The adhesion assay on the Ti surface was a candidate for further studies on physical and chemical characterisation and human osteoblast activities. P1, P7, and P8 were revealed to be biocompatible with the human osteoblast cell. The changing of cell morphology suggests the increasing of cell spreading. The enhanced cell adhesion and spreading on SDSPs-coated surfaces provided a larger contact area, which is essential for triggering downstream cell proliferation and differentiation30. Moreover, the increased cell spreading may suggest that the cells can detect their surroundings and activate earlier, leading to faster extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis and calcium deposition31. Previous studies have report that SLPI exhibits a boomerang-like structure consisting of two domains, the N-terminal and C-terminal, which are linked by hydrogen bonds. Additionally, SLPI possesses cationic properties that contribute to enhanced surface hydrophilicity19. Studies involving SDSPs coatings on titanium surfaces have demonstrated a reduction in surface hydrophobicity. Based on these findings, the present study proposes that SDSPs may contribute to enhanced surface hydrophilicity when coated onto titanium. Although SDSPs are considerably shorter than the full-length SLPI protein, certain peptide fragments retain positively charged amino acid residues, which may partially preserve the cationic characteristics of SLPIs. These cationic residues could promote electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with water molecules, thereby improving the hydrophilic properties of the Ti surface12. In particular, the successful coating of P7 is further supported by the electrostatic complementarity between the peptide and the Ti surface. At physiological pH, the TiO2 surface typically exhibits a negative zeta potential (pI ~ 5.0–6.0)32. Consequently, if P7 possesses an isoelectric point (pI) that is higher than the working pH, the resulting net positive charge facilitates strong electrostatic attraction to the Ti surface. This synergistic effect, combined with the established hydrogen bonding, not only stabilises the peptide layer as confirmed by XPS but also significantly enhances the surface’s hydrophilic character by promoting favourable interactions with surrounding water molecules33. Although the chemical composition analysis suggests the presence of SDSPs on titanium, the small molecular size of these fragments was found to often result in XPS peak intensities that are comparable to or lower than the control group. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the XPS sampling depth (~ 10 nm) exceeding the ultra-thin peptide layer, causing the substrate signal to overshadow the organic components33. Furthermore, the variation in signal intensity reflects the experimental design, in which the optimal concentration for each fragment was selected to achieve its specific surface saturation point33. While this led to variations in the absolute amount of adsorbed peptide, it ensured that each group was evaluated at its peak functional performance. Such differences in adsorption density and molecular orientation are critical factors that influence the final chemical signature detected by the XPS33. In the current investigation, the SDSPs groups demonstrated increased cell proliferation relative to the control group. The improved cell adherence and spreading shown on SDSPs-modified surfaces may explain the greater proliferation seen in these groups. More effective interactions between cells and materials make it easier for the integrins to connect and focal adhesions to form. These are well recognised to control the growth of cells and the advancement of the cell cycle. The increased cell count in the SDSPs groups may correlate with higher cytoskeletal organisation, improved focal adhesion formation, and a stronger attachment of cells to the changed Ti surface, which improve cell survival and proliferation34. Differentiation experiments also showed that SDSPs surfaces greatly stimulate calcium deposition, which could be because more cells were growing. The upregulation of important osteogenic gene markers such as ALP, RUNX2, COL1A1, and OCN further supports the notion that certain changes to SDSP might effectively encourage osteoblastic development. These results highlight the potential of SDSPs surfaces to create a conducive environment for cellular development35.

A study was conducted on the effects of coating SDSPs (P1, P7, P8) on plastic surfaces regarding the adhesion of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) by using the MTT and scanning electron microscope (SEM). The findings revealed that P7 exhibited the most effective reduction in both gram-negative and gram-positive bacterial adhesion. The inhibition effect of E. coli, a gram-negative bacteria adhesion, was observed at concentrations of 0.01 and 0.1 mM, while in S. aureus that was observed at concentrations of 0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01 mM. This shows that P7 more effectively prevent adhesion than P1 and P8. This difference suggests that P7 must have a suitable amino acid composition or secondary structure and higher cationic amphipathicity, which are important for its antimicrobial activity36,37. Furthermore, it has been shown that P7 performs most effectively on Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, where it has a significantly stronger concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on Gram-positive bacteria such as S. aureus. The primary mechanism is the intense electrostatic attraction between the potentially positively charged and the highly negatively charged lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on the outer membrane of E. coli38. The dense and complicated cell wall of S. aureus may make it difficult for P7 to access the cell membrane, which would make it less effective against Gram-positive bacteria38. SEM imaging on titanium confirmed that, despite the P7 coating, S. aureus cells were still able to form dense microcolonies. In contrast, E. coli exhibited a significant decrease in cell count as the concentration increased (Fig. 11C and D). Additionally, it was interesting to observe a U-shaped dose–response pattern in S. aureus was observed, where the inhibitory effect was significantly reduced at the highest concentration of 0.1 mM. This phenomenon is like the “Eagle Effect” or paradoxical effect, which has been seen in many bacteria, such as S. aureus, when they are exposed to too high doses of antimicrobial agents that break down the cell wall or membrane barrier mechanisms39,40. The mechanism that fits with this experimental result could be that the high levels of peptides interfering with the natural process of cell lysis (autolysis), which could make the antimicrobial agent ineffective by inhibiting the growth of cells or interfering with the function of autolysins (murein hydrolases)41. In addition, the reduction in efficacy at high concentrations may be due to the self-aggregation of P7 molecules into micelle structures that reduce the number of free active molecules36. In contrast, E. coli does not exhibit this phenomenon, possibly because the complex LPS structure may help maintain peptide binding even in aggregation conditions36.

Comparing the results between coatings on plastic and titanium surfaces indicates that the substrate plays a crucial role in regulating peptide activity. Although P7 exhibits activity against E. coli on both substrates, its efficiency is reduced when coated on titanium. This experimental result can be explained by the fact that the peptide interacts with the substrate in a specific way. This experimental result can be explained by the fact that peptides interact with the surface in a specific way. The titanium surface, which has an oxide layer (TiO2), is hydrophilic, unlike the hydrophobic plastic surface42. This difference results in the orientation or structure of the P7 molecule on the titanium surface not being as suitable as on the plastic surface36,43, which reduces the ability of the active domain to contact adhering bacterial cells. Meanwhile, the roughness on titanium becomes a niche for bacteria, especially S. aureus, which can allow them to avoid direct contact with the peptide and increase their adhesion44. These factors could explain a significant decrease in the efficacy of P7 against S. aureus the material was changed from plastic to titanium.

Although this study provides promising preliminary evidence regarding the effects of SDSPs-coated titanium on human osteoblasts and its anti-adhesion properties, several limitations must be addressed in future research. A primary constraint is the inability to quantitatively determine the exact amount of SDSPs on the titanium surface, coupled with the reliance on physical adsorption, which may compromise coating stability and lead to peptide detachment over time. To address these issues, future studies should utilise advanced surface modification techniques, such as plasma treatments or chemical linkers, tin order to create stronger covalent bonds and enhance durability. As this is the first study to investigate the bioactivity of these specific SLPI-derived small peptides (SDSPs) on titanium surfaces, our primary objective was to establish their initial efficacy in enhancing osteoblast activity and reducing bacterial adhesion. While we acknowledge that physical adsorption may lead to potential peptide detachment over time, the current results provide the necessary biological justification to move forward with more advanced stabilization techniques. To rigorously address the stability of the peptide coating in future research, a multi-faceted evaluation of the release kinetics and mechanical durability is planned. Quantitative assessment of coating longevity will be conducted using fluorescence-labeled SDSPs to establish a precise elution profile in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and simulated body fluid (SBF) over an extended 28-day period. To overcome the inherent constraints of physical adsorption, subsequent studies will investigate advanced surface modification strategies, such as oxygen plasma treatment to increase surface energy or the application of chemical linkers like polydopamine (PDA) and silanization to facilitate robust covalent bonding between the SDSPs and the titanium oxide (TiO2) layer. Furthermore, the mechanical stability of the immobilized peptides will be tested using standardized shear stress or ultrasonic vibration assays to simulate the forces encountered during clinical implant insertion. Finally, the resistance of the SDSP coating to proteolytic degradation will be evaluated by incubating the modified surfaces with inflammatory proteases, ensuring that the bioactive domains remain functional within the complex biological environment of a healing surgical site. Furthermore, while Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus are a decent representation of the basic mechanisms for inhibiting adhesion by Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, they do not fully show the complex microbiological conditions in real life, especially for the development of SDSPs peptide coatings for dental implants. These bacteria are not the primary causative agents of peri-implantitis. Therefore, future studies need to focus on oral biofilm-forming bacteria, especially the “red complex” group, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Tannerella forsythia. Finally, more comprehensive evaluations involving gene expression analysis via RT-qPCR to assess osteogenic differentiation, as well as in vivo studies, are essential to validate these findings and support their potential clinical application in dental implants.

In conclusion, the current study is the first to report the advantageous outcomes of coating SLPI-derived small peptides (SDSPs) on titanium to promote the effects of osteogenic adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and reduction of bacterial adherence. Thus, the outcome of this study can be applied to further investigations through an in vivo model to evidence the clinical potential of the technique.

Materials and methods

Chemical and reagents

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Media (DMEM), foetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin were purchased from Gibco (Gibco BRL, Life Technologies, Inc., NY, USA). All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). LB Broth, Lennox (Luria–Bertani Broth) was purchased from CulGenex™ (CulGenex Inc., USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablets were purchased from Medicago AB (Uppsala, Sweden). Grade 5 titanium discs (Ti) were cut to 5.5 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm in thickness for the 96-well plate.

Cell type and cell culture

Human foetal osteoblast 1.19 (hFOB 1.19) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC CRL-11372TM). Cells were cultured with a 1:1 mixture of Ham’s F12 medium and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin: streptomycin without phenol red.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial species, represented by Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 12600), were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and used for bacterial adhesion assays in LB broth culture medium. For each experiment, a single colony of each strain was inoculated into 5 mL of fresh LB broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C with agitation at 90 rpm.

Generation of small peptides derived from hSLPI

To hydrolyse human secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (hSLPI) in silico with porcine pepsin and trypsin, the ExPASy-PeptideCutter tool from the SIB Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics in Lausanne, Switzerland, was utilized. The Fmoc-solid phase synthetic method (GL Biochem, Shanghai, People’s Republic of China) was employed to create small peptides derived from the human secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (hSLPI), specifically P1-P11 and T1-T9, in anticipation of further in vitro evaluations. Following this, the produced lyophilized SDSPs were dissolved in solvents and preserved in a freezer at a concentration of 1 mg/ml at − 80 °C. The stock concentration of each peptide was calculated to mM unit, which then used for tenfold dilution before coating on the surface.

Screening of SDSPs that enhanced human osteoblast cell adhesion on plasticware

The osteoblast cell adhesion was performed using the MTT cell viability assay. The hFOB 1.19 cells, at a concentration of 2.5 × 105 cells/ml, were seeded on a 96-well plate coated with SDSPs at concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM. Cells were seeded and incubated for 20 min, then the media was removed and replaced with new fresh media and incubated for a further 1 h. After incubation, the number of adherent cells was indirectly determined using the MTT assay.

Coating titanium surface with SDSPs

The 5.0 mm-Ti discs (MEDI US company limited, Bangkok, Thailand) were placed into wells of the 96-well plate. Various concentrations of SDSPs were then diluted in ultrapure water to final concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM and were added into each well on the Ti surface and incubated at 4˚C for 24 h. The SDSPs solution was aspirated the following day and was used for the osteoblast adhesion assay.

Human osteoblast cell adhesion on Ti surface

The osteoblast cell adhesion was performed using the MTT cell viability assay. The hFOB 1.19 cells, at a concentration of 2.5 × 105 cells/ml, were seeded on the Ti discs coated with 1 mM of SDSP-P1, P7, P8 and incubated for 20 min. The media was removed and replaced with new, fresh media. The cells were then cultured for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. After incubation, the number of adherent cells was indirectly determined using the MTT assay.

Cytotoxicity of SDSPs-coated on Ti

The cytotoxicity of SDSPs coated on Ti was determined by the MTT cell viability assay. The hFOB1.19 cells, at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/ml, were seeded onto the Ti in a 96-well plate for 200 µL and incubated at 37˚C with 5% CO2 and 95% air for 24 h. After incubation, cells were treated with SDSPs at a concentration of 1 mM in culture media for 24 h at 37˚C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air. After incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and cell viability was measured by the MTT cell viability assay.

Determination of cellular morphology by scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The hFOB 1.19 cells were seeded on the Ti discs (2.5 × 105 cells/ml) coated with 1 mM SDSP-P1, P7, P8 and incubated for 20 min, or. 60 min for determination of cell spreading. During the post-incubation period, the culture medium was aspirated. Cells were fixed by 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 30 min. The Ti discs were carefully removed from the well plate and placed in the oven to dry. Top-view pictures were taken at 150X and 3000X magnifications using the SU3800 Benchtop Scanning Electron Microscope.

To determine cell surface area (CSA), six images of single cells on titanium surface were randomly pick up and subject to image analysis by using the National Institute of Health’s ImageJ software. After delineating the scale bar in image, then the calibrated images were converted to 8-bit greyscale and apply a Gaussian Blur filter to diminish background noise. Utilise the Threshold tool and sliders to refine the cells from the background and utilise Watershed to autonomously delineate separation lines between adjacent cells. The Particle Analysis was performed after to configuring measurements to yield the intended outcomes. The calculated surface area of each group was recalculated to express the relative percentage of cell surface area.

Determination of surface hydrophilicity

The hydrophilic properties of the titanium surface were assessed using the sessile contact angle method. A volume of 5 μL of deionized water was applied onto the Ti surface under two conditions: coated with 1 mM of SDSPs and non-coated in the presence of the SDSPs coating. The captured images in JPEG format, obtained using the camera, were subsequently imported into ImageJ for analysis. The angles of interest were then identified utilizing the angle tool feature provided by the software. The angles were determined by computing the mean of three observations obtained from distinct places.

Determination of surface roughness

The surface roughness was determined by using a 3D laser scanning microscope (VK-X3000; Keyence, USA). The surface roughness was represented as average roughness (Sa).

chemical property by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyse the surface chemical composition of the Ti surface. The XPS measurements of the scanned areas were conducted at 100 × 100 μm, with a pass energy of 224.0 eV, and the binding energy range was set from 0 to 600 eV.

Determination of osteoblast mineralization

The differentiation of osteoblasts was induced using osteogenic differentiation media (OM). Firstly, the 12 mm Ti discs were placed into a 24-well plate, and 1 × 105 cells/mL of hFOB 1.19 cells were seeded on Ti discs and cultured overnight at 37˚C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Then the culture media was removed, and the OM (100 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM sodium β-glycerophosphate, and 100 µg/mL ascorbic acid) was added. The cells were cultured for 21 days and renewed with new osteogenic differentiation media every three days. After induction, the induced cells were further observed for mineralization, which is alizarin red S staining. The Ti samples were moved to a new 24-well plate and fixed with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Then the cells were stained with 2% (w/v) alizarin red S staining (ARS) solution for 20 min. After incubation, the solution was discarded, and the cells were washed with 1X PBS. Finally, the strain was solubilized with a 10% (w/v) methylpyridinium chloride monohydrate solution. The absorbance was measured at O.D. 550 nm by using a microplate spectrophotometer.

Determination of osteoblast differentiation gene expression

On day 0, the hFOB 1.19 cells were seeded on SDSPs-coated Ti-discs in 24-well plates with 1 mM P1, 1 mM P7, and 0.001 mM P8, and were incubated at 37 ˚C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air overnight. Then, the culture media was removed, and osteogenic differentiation media (OM) containing 50 µM α-ascorbate-2-phosphate, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 0.1 µM dexamethasone was added at 37 ˚C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air for 7 days. The osteogenic induction medium was removed and replaced with fresh osteogenic induction medium every 48 h. At the end of incubation period, cells were harvested for total RNA extraction.

Total RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit (P RNA Mini Kit, Ambion). Prior to reverse transcription, the concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop, Thermo) by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm. The 260/280 absorbance ratio was used to evaluate the purity of the RNA. cDNA synthesis: the mRNA was converted to cDNA by using a TetroTM cDNA Synthesis kit (Bioline). Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR): The expression of ALP, OCN, RUNX2, and COL1A1 genes was determined by qRT-PCR using a QuantiNova® SYBR® Green PCR Kit (QIAGEN) with the primers listed in Table 1.

Determination of the effect of SDSPs coating on titanium on bacterial adhesion

The anti-adhesion activity of SDSPs-coated titanium surfaces against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus was evaluated using a modified MTT assay. Sterile titanium discs were first placed into individual wells of 96-well plates, followed by the addition of SDSPs coating solutions prepared at four concentrations (0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 mM) in PBS. The plates were incubated for 24 h to allow proper SDSPs coating on the titanium disc surfaces. Bacterial strains were cultured overnight in LB broth at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 90 rpm, then transferred to Erlenmeyer flasks for further cultivation until reaching an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 0.1. Following, 100 μL of the adjusted bacterial suspension was seeded into each well containing the SDSPs-coated titanium discs and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After the adhesion period, non-adherent bacteria were removed by gentle washing, and 100 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well. The plates were further incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to each well to solubilize the formazan crystals, and the plates were gently agitated for complete dissolution. After that, 100 μL of the dissolved formazan solution was transferred to a new 96-well plate for absorbance measurement at 565 nm using a microplate reader, with bacterial adhesion expressed as relative percentage compared to untreated titanium disc controls.

Statistical analysis

Data was processed using GraphPad Prism version 12 and reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All comparisons were assessed for significance using ANOVA, followed, when appropriate, by the Tukey–Kramer test. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗ p < 0.05, ∗ ∗ p < 0.01, and ∗ ∗ ∗ p < 0.001. Comparisons between 2 group including Ti and SDSPs + Ti groups were performed by the unpaired t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
