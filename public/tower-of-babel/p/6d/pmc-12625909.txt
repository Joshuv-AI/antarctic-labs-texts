Creating a large designer cellulosome in yeast to boost ethanol production
Authors: Zeba Khatoon, Marimuthu Anandharaj, Tzu-Ho Chen, Chi-Chia Wu, Jan-Fang Cheng, Tsui-Ling Hsu, Hsiao-Ching Lin, Jui-Jen Chang, Wen-Hsiung Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceConverting cellulosic biomass into biofuels or biochemicals remains challenging because of the recalcitrant structure of cellulose. Natural cellulosomes offer a blueprint for overcoming this challenge through enzyme assembly but creating enzyme complexes in yeast has been limited by scaffoldin size and lack of enzyme positioning control on the scaffoldin. We constructed the largest designer cellulosome scaffoldin to date in Kluyveromyces marxianus. It includes nine distinct cohesins, allowing the precise positioning of nine enzymes on the scaffoldin to achieve high enzyme proximity-effect and synergy. Our system increased ethanol yield by fourfold or more compared to previously constructed yeast cellulosomes. Our approach opens possibilities for assembling enzyme complexes to degrade lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose in a single consolidated process.

The sustainable production of biofuels and bio-based chemicals has been considered a promising approach to reduce the use of fossil fuel (1). Cellulosic biomass, due to its abundance, presents an attractive resource, but its complex structure necessitates innovative bioconversion strategies (2). In recent years, cellulosomes have emerged as a promising solution for efficient enzymatic degradation of cellulose (3, 4). In this study, we constructed and functionally validated a designer cellulosome in an industrial yeast that greatly boosts ethanol production.

Cellulosome, known as nature’s finest cellulolytic enzyme complex, was first identified in Clostridium thermocellum (5, 6). The largest known C. thermocellum cellulosome is composed of seven copies of a nonenzymatic scaffoldin known as “cellulosome integrating protein A” (CipA) (7, 8). A CipA contains nine type I cohesins and an integral cellulose-binding module (CBM), which binds to cellulosic substrate (9–11). Each cellulosomal enzyme contains a type I dockerin, which binds to the type I cohesin of CipA (12, 13). These cohesin–dockerin interactions on CipA promote the spatial proximity of cellulases (enzyme proximity effect) and the cellulose-binding capacity of CBM augments cellulose utilization (14). Taking advantage of cellulosome, one can develop a novel strategy to boost the saccharification efficiency in biomass refinery (15–17).

To date, researchers have identified several cellulosome-producing bacteria from various sources (18–23). Notably, the cohesin–dockerin interaction was species-specific in some cellulosome-producing bacteria (24, 25), and this finding was utilized to develop a “designer cellulosome” with distinct cohesins and dockerins from different cellulosome-producing bacteria (26–29). Through permutation and combination, an optimal array of desired enzymes on CipA may be achieved for proximity effect (30, 31).

Several research teams have developed smaller versions of designer cellulosomes in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to increase cellulose degradation and ethanol production (32–36). Nevertheless, the enzyme synergism is still far from ideal due to limited numbers of cohesins in the designed CipA and thus also limited choices of enzymes in the complex. Given that nature has diverse classes of cellulolytic enzymes (37), we followed our previous work (38) to engineer the yeast Kluyveromyces marxianus to possess a large designer cellulosome. Here, we constructed the largest ever designer CipA (named DCipA2B9C). It contains nine distinct cohesins selected from nine different cellulosome-producing bacteria and two CBMs. The DNA synthesis of this DCipA2B9C gene was highly challenging but was overcome by a special technique (Materials and Methods). Three of the nine cohesin–dockerin pairs used here were newly reported for binding interactions. Also, our DCipA2B9C contains two CBMs, which has been found to increase the saccharification efficiency of cellulose compared to a CipA containing only one CBM (38). This DCipA2B9C paved the way for increased enzyme numbers and types within a single cellulosomal complex, allowing the control of both enzyme number and location on the DCipA2B9C.

The conversion of cellulose into simple sugars requires at least three types of enzymes: endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and beta-glucosidases (39). Moreover, a new class of oxidative enzymes, called lytic polysaccharide monooxygenase (LPMO), has recently been reported (40). LPMO can decompose crystalline cellulose structure efficiently and increase the soluble sugar release by 2.6-fold, so it was hailed as “cellulase booster” (41). LPMO requires an enzyme-copartner, called cellobiose dehydrogenase (42), which donates the electron required by LPMO to exert its activity. Here, we selected nine fungal cellulases each fused with a distinct dockerin and selected their positions on the DCipA2B9C based on their expression ratios and overall synergism (43–46).

We engineered the designer cellulosome into an unconventional yeast K. marxianus. This yeast has several advantages over S. cerevisiae—it is thermotolerant, fast growing, and Crabtree-negative (47, 48). In addition, it has the ability to ferment various sugars including pentose (e.g., arabinose, xylose, and inulin). Furthermore, K. marxianus has a relatively higher secretory capacity than S. cerevisiae, because it has a proper glycosylation ability (49). The complete genome sequence of K. marxianus is available and good synthetic biology tools have been developed to engineer K. marxianus (50–53). Thus, K. marxianus is an emerging host for biotechnology (54). As will be seen later, constructing our designer cellulosome into this yeast greatly boosts ethanol production.

Results

Designing and Synthesis of Cellulosomal Components.

Nine type I cohesins each with a specific matched dockerin from nine different cellulosome-producing bacteria were selected to develop a designer scaffoldin protein A (DCipA2B9C). These nine bacterial species (cohesin) are C. thermocellum (cohT), Bacteroides cellulosolvens (cohB), Acetivibrio cellulolyticus (cohA), Clostridium cellulolyticum (cohC), Ruminococcus albus (cohR), Ruminococcus flavefaciens (cohF), Ruminococcus champanellensis (cohM), Clostridium clariflavum (cohL), and Clostridium josui (cohJ) (SI Appendix, Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis of the cohesin sequences from these nine bacteria indicated that most of these cohesins are evolutionarily well separated (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the multiple sequence alignment of the dockerin domains confirmed the differences in amino acid sequences among the nine cellulosome producing bacteria (Fig. 1B). Generally, dockerin domains exhibit an internal twofold symmetry, consisting of two duplicated F-hand motifs (a calcium-binding loop preceding a helix) (55), which reveals four residues that may account for the species specificity (denoted by “S” in Fig. 1B). Moreover, 6 highly conserved calcium-binding residues in each motif were identified (denoted by “C” in Fig. 1B) and almost all residues have been conserved in each of the selected dockerin domains. These conserved residues play a critical role in the high-affinity calcium-dependent cohesin–dockerin interaction (56). Our DCipA2B9C was developed by replacing the native cohesins (CohT) of CipA (38) with the ones from the other eight cellulosome producing bacteria (Fig. 1C). A native CipA contains one single CBM, which leads the cellulosome complex to bind to cellulose. To increase saccharification efficiency (38), we included two CBMs in our DCipA, thus the name DCipA2B9C in which “B” denotes the number of CBMs and C the number of cohesins. To demonstrate the specificity among the nine cohesins–dockerin pairs, nine monovalent scaffoldins were synthesized, each of which contains a given type I cohesin and a CBM domain (Fig. 1D). The use of monovalent scaffoldins offers a simplified, one-to-one interaction framework that eliminates confounding multivalent effects. In this design, the CBM serves as a stable carrier protein for cohesin modules, ensuring structural integrity and stability. Note also that a CBM enhances the solubility and expression of cohesin containing fusion proteins in Escherichia coli (57). On the other hand, an anchoring protein plays an important role for the attachment of the cellulosome onto the yeast cell surface. We previously synthesized a cell surface anchoring protein OlpB that harbors seven type II cohesins, fused with S. cerevisiae GPI domain (ScGPI) (38). OlpB-ScGPI was used to express the cellulosome complex on the cell surface (Fig. 1G). After designing the cellulosomal genes, their codon usage was optimized to suit for expression in K. marxianus and then synthesized by a high-fidelity platform.

Each of the nine dockerins was fused to xylanase T6 from Geobacillus stearothermophilus, a protein known for robust expression in E. coli and each cohesin was fused to a CBM (57). The result showed that each of the dockerins bound to their cognate monovalent scaffoldin, as indicated by a band shifting in the native-PAGE because of protein–protein association. In addition, SDS-PAGE of the same sample showed two distinct bands, representing the individual xylanase-fused dockerin and the monovalent scaffoldin and confirming successful complex formation.

Specificity Profiling of Cohesin–Dockerin Interactions.

To demonstrate the specificity of cohesin and dockerin interaction, affinity-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed using monovalent scaffoldins and xylanase-fused dockerins (Fig. 3 A–I). The results showed that each dockerin specifically binds to its cognate monovalent scaffoldin, confirming species specificity. When Scaf T was immobilized on the wells of the ELISA plate, only xylanase-fused Doc T showed binding across incremental concentrations while other xylanase-fused dockerins showed minimal binding (Fig. 3A). Doc T binding showed a substrate concentration-dependent increase, with OD450 values rising from 0.62 at 1 nM to 1.87 at 100 nM, indicating its strong affinity for the target. In contrast, other dockerins (XynT6- b, -a, -m, -j, -f, -c, -r, and -l) displayed low OD450 values across all concentrations, suggesting their weak or negligible binding with Scaf T.

A similar specificity trend was observed for the other scaffoldins. When Scaf B, Scaf A, Scaf M, Scaf F, Scaf R, and Scaf L were immobilized as the targets, their corresponding xylanase-fused dockerin partners (XynT6-b, -a, -m, -f, -r, and -l) displayed strong and specific binding (Fig. 3 B–D, F, H, and I). Noncognate dockerins again showed little to no interactions.

Interestingly, minor cross-reactivity was observed in a specific case involving C. cellulolyticum and C. josui. This was likely due to the high sequence similarity between their cohesin and dockerin domains (Fig. 1 A and B). Doc C exhibited slight interaction with Scaf J and Doc J showed slight binding to Scaf C. Nevertheless, this cross-reactivity was considerably lower than the robust binding observed between their cognate partners, Doc J–Scaf J and Doc C–Scaf C (Fig. 3 E and G).

Construction of Chimeric Cellulases.

To assemble the designer scaffoldin with the functional enzymes, a suitable dockerin module was fused to each fungal cellulase to facilitate cellulosomal integration. In this study, we assembled a tailored panel of five fungal cellulases/cellulase booster from our previous work (38), including an endoglucanase from Trichoderma reesei (EgIII), a synthetic exoglucanase (CBHII), a β-glucosidase from Neocallimastix patriciarum (BGS), and the cellulase booster from Thermoascus aurantiacus (LPMO) and its enzyme-copartner from Myceliophthora thermophila (also known as Thermothelomyces thermophila) [cellobiose dehydrogenase (CDH)]. Additionally, to expand the exoglucanase repertoire, we recruited an additional exoglucanase enzyme from the white-rot fungus Trametes orientalis (EXG2) into the existing enzyme lineup. Collectively, 9 different kinds of dockerin fusion plasmids were designed based on the type I dockerins from nine different bacterial species (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–I and Table S1). The dockerin module was fused at the C-terminus of each individual enzyme. A 36 bp glycine-rich linker was inserted between the dockerin module and the catalytic domain to reduce the conformational constraint and steric hindrance between cellulase enzymes (41, 58). An 8xHis tag was fused to each cellulase for purification and detection purposes. For the efficient secretion of each cellulase into the medium, the secretion signal peptide of the K. lactis alpha factor (prepro-leader sequence) was employed in these constructs and fused at the N terminus.

To degrade cellulose, cellulases act sequentially and synergistically in which endoglucanases randomly cleave the backbone of cellulose fibers and then cellobiohydrolases (exoglucanases) act on reducing and nonreducing ends of polysaccharide chains to release cellobioses. Next, β-glucosidase cleaves cellobiose to produce free glucose units. To ensure the efficient cellulose hydrolysis, the ratio of cellulase types and copy number of individual cellulase are crucial parameters (43–45). Hence, we premeditatedly fixed the enzyme positions on the designer scaffoldin (DCipA2B9C) to increase the synergistic and proximity effect. To achieve an optimal enzyme synergism, each cellulase was fused with a distinct dockerin and transformed into K. marxianus for performance evaluation. After evaluating their enzymatic activities, the most effective variants were selected. That is, EgIII exhibited high activity when fused with dockerins a, m, and c, but poor activity when fused to other dockerins; therefore, EgIII-a, EgIII-m, and EgIII-c were chosen (Fig. 4A). Similarly, CBHII demonstrated high activity with dockerin j and EXG2 with dockerin r, leading to the selection of CBHII-j and EXG2-r (Fig. 4 B and C). Based on our previous study (38), where the activity of LPMO with dockerin T was demonstrated, the CDH was fused with dockerin B, because CDH serves as an electron donor for LPMO, and we then confirmed their enzyme activities (Fig. 4D). Finally, BGS was fused with dockerin F and dockerin L, separately, and confirmed their enzymatic activities (Fig. 4E). The final order of the enzymes on the designer scaffoldin is designed as follows (Fig. 4F). First, the cellulase boosters (LPMO-t and CDH-b) are placed adjacent to each other, so that the electron transfer from CDH-b to LPMO-t will not be interfered by other enzymes (Proximity 1 in the complex in Fig. 4F). Second, to synergistically and sequentially digest the cellulose, we positioned two endoglucanases (EgIII-a and EgIII-m, two identical enzymes with different dockerins) close to CBM1 followed by one exoglucanase (CBHII-j) and one β-glucosidase (BGS-f) in sequential manner (Proximity 2 in complex). Third, the remaining three positions on DCipA were then filled with a second set of cellulases similarly arranged with one endoglucanase (EgIII-c) adjacent to CBM2 followed by one exoglucanase (EXG2-r) and one β-glucosidase (BGS-l) (Proximity 3 in the complex). The protein expression of the dockerin-fused cellulases was confirmed by western blotting, in which all the designated proteins were traceable (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–F). A few proteins (BGS-f, -l and CBHII-j) exhibited higher molecular weights than expected, suggesting hyperglycosylation, which was further supported by in silico glycosylation prediction (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). The three-dimensional structure of a dockerin-fused chimeric protein was modeled using homology modeling software, whereby the substrate access to enzymes was not hindered by the dockerin fusion (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–I).

Chromosomal Integration of the Scaffoldin Gene.

To study the effect of DCipA2B9C on cellulose degradation, the scaffoldin gene was transformed and integrated into the LAC4 region of the K. marxianus genome using the integrated-plasmid pKLac2. We named it the scaffoldin host (SH) (Fig. 5A). The chromosomal integration was verified by PCR (Fig. 5B) and their protein expression was confirmed by Western blot (WB) analysis using anti-strep antibody. All of the DCipA2B9C transformants contain the secretion signal peptide of the K. lactis alpha factor (prepro-leader sequence) and their expressed scaffoldin proteins were secreted out of the cell. The WB results of DCipA2B9C showed an apparent band at >207 kDa (Fig. 5C), confirming the expression and secretion of full size DCipA2B9C. The higher molecular weight than the predicted (207 kDa) is attributed to glycosylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).

Engineering the Designer Cellulase Host and the Cellulosome Host.

Two approaches were employed to test cellulose degradation: 1) A coculture where the enzyme host (EH) and the scaffoldin host (SH) work synergistically and 2) a single-host system, where all enzymes and the scaffoldin (DCipA2B9C) are expressed by a single host. The EH host, which carries the nine enzymes, was developed in two steps (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). First, five genes, ie., LPMO-t, CDH-b, EgIII-c, EXG2-r, and BGS-l, were integrated into the wild-type K. marxianus host and named DCH5. This DCH5 was then used as the recipient host for the second step, in which the remaining four dockerin-fused enzyme genes (EgIII-a, EgIII-m, CBHII-j, and BGS-f) were integrated to obtain a 9-EH, named DCH9. Numerous transformants were screened by PCR using gene-specific primers, and the transformants with the nine genes were selected (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–I). These transformants were then subjected to a second-stage screening to select the one with the highest capability of releasing reducing sugar in the presence of avicel. DCH9-2 was finally selected as the EH (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Next, to construct the single-host system, the scaffoldin (SH) host was employed as the recipient host and transformed with six genes (LPMO-t, CDH-b, EGIII-a, EGIII-m, CBHII-j, and BGS-f), resulting in the cellulosome-6 host. This host was then used as the recipient host to introduce the other three genes, EGIII-c, EXG2-r, and BGS-l, leading to the development of the cellulosome-9 host (Fig. 6B). The successful integration of all nine dockerin-fused genes along with DCipA2B9C was confirmed by PCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S6J). The relative copy number of each gene in DCH9-2 and the cellulosome-9 host were determined by qPCR using the actin gene as an internal reference (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). RNA expression analysis using RT-qPCR finally confirmed that all inserted genes were well expressed, with significantly higher expression levels at 48 h than at 24 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D).

The effect of scaffoldin on cellulose degradation was analyzed by the estimation of reducing sugar release, using avicel. The addition of the scaffoldin host (SH) to the EH (DCH9-2) significantly increased the reducing sugar release from avicel (37.5 mM) (Fig. 6C), which was slightly higher than the amount of reducing sugar (36.2 mM) released by the cellulosome-9 host, suggesting a better performance of coculturing the EH-DCH9-2 and the scaffoldin host (SH) (Fig. 6D). However, it could be partly due to that the expression levels of the genes in the cellulosome-9 host tended to be lower than those in DCH9-2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D).

Cellulosome Complex Formation Analysis.

The complex formation between scaffoldin and dockerin-fused enzymes was evaluated using concentrated supernatants from the SH host and the EH DCH9-2. After allowing the enzymes and scaffoldin to form a complex, microcrystalline cellulose was added to bind with the DCipA2B9C and designer enzyme complex. The resulting protein complex was recovered from the cellulose pellet and subjected to Native-PAGE analysis, confirming the cellulosome complex formation (Fig. 7A). Subsequent SDS-PAGE analysis revealed eight visible bands corresponding to DCipA2B9C and the dockerin-fused enzymes, consistent with their expected molecular weights (Fig. 7B). Eight instead of nine bands because some proteins such as BGS-f, -l and EgIII-a, -m, -c have similar molecular weights and cannot be resolved into distinct bands on SDS-PAGE. Additionally, Native and SDS-PAGE of unbound and bound fractions were followed by WB analysis using anti-His tag antibody, which further confirmed their presence in the eluted protein complex (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). We subsequently performed in-gel trypsin digestion followed by Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis to identify each visible band in SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C) and successfully identified all proteins along with their respective Mascot scores. However, due to high sequence similarities, BGS-f and -l were not distinguishable with each other in band 2 by LC-MS analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). Notably, BGS-f, BGS-l, CBHII-j, and DCipA2B9C exhibited higher molecular weights than expected and a similar pattern was observed in the WB analysis of a single gene host expressing BGS-f, BGS-l, CBHII-J (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C, D, and F), or DCipA2B9C (Fig. 5C). In-silico glycosylation prediction supported that these proteins were glycosylated in the host (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–D).

Ethanol Production Analysis.

Ethanol production by free designer cellulosome in a host consortium.

To evaluate the bioethanol production, both the EH and the scaffoldin host (SH) were cocultured at ratios (EH:SH) of 2:1, 5:1, or 10:1 (Fig. 8A). The ethanol production analysis was estimated under 1% avicel as the sole carbon source. The highest ethanol production was achieved when EH and SH were cocultured at the ratio of 5:1. This observation suggests that when EH:SH was 10:1, SH did not provide enough cohesins to match the dockerins on the enzymes, so that there were too many free cellulases and the enzyme synergism was low. On the other hand, when EH:SH was 2:1, the overall ethanol production was lower than at higher EH:SH ratios. Growth measurements indicated that SH accumulate more biomass than EH (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), suggesting diversion of sugar toward growth rather than ethanol production. The cellulosic ethanol production for the EH (DCH5 or DCH9) along with the scaffoldin host (SH) was investigated to probe the influence of the designer scaffoldin on enzyme–substrate complex synergism. As shown in Fig. 8B, a high ethanol productivity (14.29 g/L) was achieved in the coculture of the EH and the scaffoldin host (SH), as opposed to EH alone (2.5 g/L). Similarly, the ethanol production was significantly higher (6.26 g/L) in the coculture of DCH5 and SH, than in DCH5 alone (1.43 g/L). This result indicated that our designer scaffoldin DCipA2B9C significantly enhanced the enzyme proximity effect and hence the ethanol production.

Ethanol production by the anchored designer cellulosome in a host consortium.

The anchoring protein (OlpB) serves two key functions: i) its seven type II cohesin domains enable the assembly of multienzyme complex of up to 63 enzymes and ii) its GPI domain anchors the assembled complex to the yeast cell surface. To show the influence of this huge complex on enzyme–substrate–microbe complex synergism, the cellulosic ethanol production for the combination of an EH (DCH5 or DCH9-2) along with the scaffoldin host (DCipA2B9C) and the anchoring host (OlpB) in a ratio of 5:1:1 was investigated. The highest ethanol production of 8.75 g/L was achieved in the consortium of the DCH9-2, 2B9C, and OlpB host triad when compared to that of DCH9-2 alone (2.56 g/L) (Fig. 8C). Similarly, the ethanol production was as high as 6.26 g/L with DCH5 in place of DCH9 in the triad combination as opposed to that of DCH5 alone (1.5 g/L). These results indicated that the designer scaffoldin effectively enhances the enzyme proximity effect, thereby improving cellulose degradation and bioethanol production. However, the triad (63-enzyme complex) produced less ethanol (8.75 g/L) compared to the coculture (9-enzyme complex) of DCH9-2 and the scaffoldin host (14.29 g/L), suggesting that while anchoring improves enzyme synergy, the addition of the OlpB host may consume more metabolic resources and also restricts the movement of the cellulosome complex.

Ethanol production by free designer cellulosome in a single host.

Bioethanol production of a single host expressing both enzymes and DCipA2B9C was evaluated. The results showed that the amounts of ethanol production in the strains cellulosome-9 and cellulosome-6 are 10.15 g/L and 5.2 g/L, respectively (Fig. 8D). These results indicated that a single host (cellulosome-9 or cellulosome-6) produced less ethanol than the coculture of an EH and the scaffoldin host (SH), which produced 14.29 g/L (Fig. 8B). It might be because the integration of too many genes and/or multiple antibiotic selection markers could lead to a larger metabolic burden to the host, especially under high sugar accumulation. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that the microbial consortium is superior to a single host (3, 59–61).

Discussion

The proximity and synergistic effect of enzymes in cellulosome has been well documented in yeast (32–35). Wen et al. (62) were the first to develop a recombinant yeast host capable of expressing a cellulosome on the yeast cell surface. However, the yeast could only produce ethanol up to 1.8 g/L with PASC (phosphoric-acid swollen cellulose) as the substrate, which has a loose structure. Later, the same group, Liang et al. (63) expanded the capacity of cellulosome by incorporating a LPMO and a CDH to produce 1.56 g/L ethanol with avicel as the carbon source. Both studies used a single scaffoldin for cellulosome assembly and anchoring, which incurred a heavy metabolic burden on the single host and a burden on the secretion pathway. Moreover, using only one kind of cohesin–dockerin pairs might lead to uneven distribution of cellulases on the scaffoldin. Fan et al. (35) expressed a dual-scaffoldin cellulosome consisting of an anchoring scaffoldin with four cohesins to display the cellulosome on the yeast cell surface and a main scaffoldin carrying three distinct cohesins to recruit designer enzymes. This dual-scaffoldin cellulosome would theoretically be more efficient, but the number of cells displaying the anchoring scaffoldin decreased as its size increased, likely due to high metabolic burden and plasmid instability and so it produced only 1.4 g/L ethanol. Thus, it seemed to have a negative effect when the type II cohesin number on the anchoring protein was increased to more than two because all of the cellulosome components were expressed in a single yeast. Later, Fan et al. (60) explored the yeast consortium approach, to express the anchoring scaffoldin and main scaffoldin into one yeast and the cellulases in another yeast. Shifting cellulase production to a second yeast or to E. coli significantly increased the display level of anchoring scaffoldin on yeast surface by reducing metabolic burden on the hosts. Moreover, the yeast treated with E. coli lysates produces 1.14 g/L ethanol from microcrystalline cellulose within 4 d, comparable to the yield achieved using a single yeast host.

A yeast consortium approach provides the flexibility to adjust the distribution of cellulosome components on different hosts (3, 32, 33, 60, 64). Distributing the metabolic burden to more than one host reduces the metabolic burden on a host (27) and may allow for finer control over fermentation dynamics and higher product yields (59). In our previous study, we employed this consortium approach in K. marxianus using two different scaffoldins to mimic natural cellulosome architecture (38). The ethanol titer improved to 3.09 g/L using avicel. However, the use of a single type of cohesin–dockerin pairs limited the control of enzyme spatial arrangement. In this study, we constructed the largest designer cellulosome in K. marxianus using a host-consortium approach. In particular, by incorporating nine different species-specific cohesin–dockerin pairs, we significantly increased enzyme proximity, synergism, and overall cellulosome functionality. The engineered yeast consortium achieved efficient avicel degradation, yielding 14.29 g/L ethanol, which surpassed previously constructed yeast cellulosomes by fourfold or more (Fig. 8B and SI Appendix, Table S8). We noted further that when a single yeast host was constructed to express all components of the designer cellulosome, its bioethanol production was lower than that of the coculture of two separate hosts. It was probably due to the increased physiological burden on the single yeast host by the addition of one selection marker gene and the DCipA2B9C gene, which produces a large (>200 KDa) protein. Another reason may be because the exoglucanases CBHII-j and EXG2-r gene copies were fewer in the single host (cellulosome-9) than in the EH (DCH9-2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). Indeed, the RNA expression levels of cellulase genes in the single host (cellulosome-9) were lower than in the enzyme producing-host (DCH9-2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D).

The anchored cellulosome system facilitated enzyme localization and interaction with cellulose, leading to increased ethanol yields compared to the EH alone without DCipA. However, the maximum ethanol production achieved by the cell surface anchored system (8.75 g/L) was significantly lower than that of the free designer cellulosome (14.29 g/L) (Fig. 8B). A possible explanation for this difference is the restricted mobility of cell–surface–anchored cellulosome. In contrast to free cellulosomes, which can diffuse freely and access cellulose particles more efficiently, anchored cellulosomes are confined to the yeast cell surface. This spatial restriction may reduce substrate accessibility, thereby limiting overall efficiency (65). These arguments are consistent with the previous report (66) in which C. thermocellum strains with free-floating cellulosomes performed better than the anchored cellulosomes. Finally, we note that the addition of the OlpB host to a consortium would increase sugar consumption.

Since the optimal enzyme ratio and synergistic combinations had been reported previously (43, 44), our focus was directed toward constructing a designer scaffoldin containing nine distinct cohesins. The gene encoding this designer scaffoldin (DCipA2B9C) is long (6,171 bp) and contains extensive tandem repeats, making gene synthesis technically challenging. To overcome this, we conducted codon randomization to minimize sequence repeats without changing the amino acid sequence, and improve synthesis success. To systemically explore enzyme spatial arrangements without resynthesizing the long scaffoldin gene, we fixed the cohesin orders on the scaffoldin and then fused the enzymes to different dockerins to examine the performances of different arrangements of enzyme positions. This strategy allowed us to assess multiple enzyme arrangements while requiring synthesis of the designer scaffoldin gene (DCipA2B9C) only once.

In summary, our successful expression of the largest designer cellulosome in yeast represents a significant advancement in biorefinery technology. This work provides a promising platform for the efficient conversion of cellulosic biomass into bioethanol, with implications for sustainable energy production. While our system achieved 14.29 g/L ethanol from avicel, which is at least a fourfold improvement over other yeast cellulosomes, it remains below the 40 to 50 g/L benchmark considered necessary for commercial viability (67). Most high yielding noncellulosomal system rely on extensive metabolic engineering (68–70) and external enzyme supplementation (71). In contrast, our approach embeds enzyme synergy directly into the host offering a modular and potentially cost-effective alternative. Most previous studies used an episomal plasmid to express the cellulosomal components to achieve both higher copy number and enzyme expression. However, this advantage is often offset by the instability in the absence of selection pressure, a critical limitation for long-term industrial fermentations. Our results demonstrate that the expression of the large designer cellulosome in yeast can greatly boost ethanol production. It also means this enzyme complex technology has potential to convert the cellulosic biomass to other bio-based chemical bricks. In future, we aim to incorporate xylanases into the scaffoldin to enable hemicellulose degradation, extending the system to process raw biomass and boosting industrial relevance.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Media.

E. coli DH5α was used for plasmid assembly. E. coli GM2163 was used to demethylate plasmid and to ligate large DNA fragments. E. coli strains were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, containing 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L sodium chloride (NaCl) with the addition of 50 μg/mL ampicillin. The yeast K. marxianus 4G5 strain (72) was employed as a CBP host. This strain was sustained in YPG medium consisting of 1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% galactose and cultured at 30 °C under agitation at 300 rpm. Yeast transformants were cultured in YPG medium supplemented with G418, hygromycin B, or Zeocin at a concentration of 200 μg/mL.

Design and Construct of Cellulosomal Components.

The designer scaffoldin (DCipA2B9C) was constructed by replacing the native cohesins (CohT) of CipA from our previous study (38) with the cohesins of other eight-cellulosomal bacteria (SI Appendix, Table S1). The designer scaffoldin DCipA2B9C was designed by adding one more CBM domain between cohesins 7 and 8. The construct was fused at the N terminus with a K. lactis alpha-factor signal peptide for secretion along with a strep tag II and an 8× His tag for purification purposes. The anchoring protein (OlpB) used was from our previous study (38). All the constructs were codon-optimized and difficult-to-synthesize repeat sequences were avoided as much as possible, without changing the amino acid sequence, to facilitate DNA synthesis The construct was synthesized and inserted into the yeast integration plasmid pKLac2-α-Kan using AvrII and NotI.

Gene sequences for EgIII, BGS, CBHII (Gene Bank: KC311337), LPMO, and CDH were obtained from constructs reported in our previous studies (38, 53, 73, 74) while the EXG2 gene was cloned from the white-rot fungus Trametes orientalis in this study. To fuse the C-terminal dockerin, each cellulase gene was amplified with gene-specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S3) using High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (PCRBIO VeriFiTM Polymerase, PCR Biosystems, UK). The amplified fragment was digested with AvrII and SpeI and inserted into the yeast integration plasmid pKLac2-α-Kan-Dockerin kit (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), which carries a Type I dockerin from C. thermocellum (Doc T), B. cellulosolvens (DocB), A. cellulolyticus (DocA), C. cellulolyticum (DocC), R. albus (DocR), R. flavefaciens (DocF), R. champanellensis (DocM), C. clariflavum (DocL), or C. josui (DocJ). The pKLac2-α-Kan vector was constructed by modifying the K. lactis vector pKLac2 (75), in which the K. lactis alpha-factor secretion signal and a Kanamycin (G418) resistant marker were inserted. To enable integration, the pKLac2-α-Kan plasmid harboring the target gene was linearized using SacII/BstXI restriction digestion or PCR amplification. Our integration cassette is designed to enable repeated insertions at the same genomic locus (i.e., Within the Lac4 region of k. marxianus) (75, 76). This was achieved by including 5’ PLAC4 and 3’ PLAC4 homologous sequences that are retained after each integration event (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B).

Yeast Transformation and Clone Screening.

K. marxianus cells were cultured in 5 mL of YPG medium at 30 °C at 300 rpm for 16 h. Yeast competent cells were prepared according to protocol described by Chang et al. (53). Gene cassettes (1 µg) digested with SacII were electroporated into 40 µL of competent cells, using a 2 mm gap aluminum cuvette and the BioRad Gene Pulser Xcell™ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Electroporation was performed under the following conditions: 1.0 kV voltage, 400 Ω resistance, and 25 μF capacitance. Following, electroporation cells were recovered at 30 °C for 3 h and plated on YPG agar containing G418 or hygromycin B (200 μg/mL). Yeast colonies were treated with QuickExtract™ DNA Extraction Solution (EPICENTRE, Madison, WI) to remove the cell wall and the lysate was used as a template for PCR verification. Chromosomal integration of each gene was verified by PCR using gene-specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S3) and insertion stability was confirmed over five successive generations.

Cloning for Bacterial Expression of Cellulosomal Components.

The xylanase T6 (XynT6) gene from G. stearothermophilus was synthesized by TWIST Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA) and amplified by PCR using primer pairs containing BamHI and SpeI-EcoRI restriction site. The PCR product was digested with BamHI and EcoRI and subsequently ligated into pET28a(+) vector at the corresponding sites. The resulting plasmid enabled the insertion of nine different dockerins by digesting with BamHI and SpeI. The dockerin genes were PCR amplified from nine different dockerin kits using primers with BamHI and SpeI restriction sites. The resulting plasmid encoded a His-tagged xylanase fused with a dockerin at the C-terminal in pET28a(+) vector. All the monovalent scaffoldins (containing a cohesin and a CBM) were cloned into pET21b(+) vector at BamHI and NotI restriction sites. All the primer pairs used to clone genes into pET28a(+) and pET21b(+) vectors are listed in SI Appendix, Table S4.

Protein Expression and Purification.

To verify the cohesin–dockerin interaction and assess their species specificity, each xylanase-fused dockerin and monovalent scaffoldins were expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) strain and proteins were purified following the protocol described by Barak et al. (57) with slight modifications in the purification of xylanase-fused dockerin constructs. Briefly, Rosetta (DE3) cells were transformed with the desired pET-XynDoc plasmid and plated on kanamycin containing LB-agar plates. To prepare the culture, 4 mL of LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin was inoculated with a single colony of bacteria and grown overnight at 37 °C. Cells were subcultured into 25 mL LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin. Cells were further subcultured into 500 mL of LB medium [starting optical density at 600 nm (A600) of −0.15], supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin and 2 mM calcium chloride (CaCl2). The culture was incubated at 37 °C until it reached an optical density at 600 nm (A600) of ~1. Protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.1 mM, and the culture was grown at 16 °C for 16 h. Following incubation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 20 mL of binding buffer and the cells were disrupted by sonication (10 s on/off cycle at 40% amplitude for 5 min). The supernatant after sonication was loaded on the Nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resins pre-equilibrated with binding buffer. Recombinant proteins were purified and stored as described in ref. 57.

Nondenaturing PAGE.

Purified dockerin-fused xylanases were mixed with the purified monovalent scaffoldin at the equimolar ratio (4 to 8 μg) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) buffer containing 10 mM CaCl2 and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to facilitate complex formation. Following incubation, the sample loading dye (without SDS) was added to the complex and loaded on native-PAGE (stacking gel-4.3%; separating gel-9%) (77).

Affinity Based ELISA.

In this method, 3 nM monovalent scaffoldins (i.e., ScafT, ScafB, ScafA, ScafF, ScafC, ScafR, ScafL, ScafM, and ScafJ) were coated using a coating solution (0.1 M Sodium carbonate, pH 9) on a MaxiSorp ELISA plate (Nunc, A/S Roskilde Denmark) for overnight at 4 °C. The coating solution was discarded; blocking buffer (TBS, 10 mM CaCl2, 0.05% Tween 20, 2% BSA) was added and incubated for 1 h. The blocking buffer was discarded and the dockerin-fused xylanaseT6 (His-tagged), diluted in blocking buffer, was added to the coated plates. This mixture was incubated for 1 to 2 h to allow the dockerin to bind with their specific cohesins. Then the wells were washed three times with wash buffer (Blocking buffer without BSA) and incubated 1 h with anti-His antibody, followed by three washes with wash buffer. Next, horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody diluted (1:10,000) in blocking buffer was added. After 1 h of incubation, plates were washed four times. The interaction was analyzed by the addition of 100 µL/well chromogenic substrate, 3, 3’, 5, 5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) using 1-Step™ Ultra TMB-ELISA reagent, (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The color formation was stopped by the addition of 1 M H2SO4 (50 µL/well) and quantified using a spectrophotometer at 450 nm (57).

Quantitative PCR Analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from the transformants with the Gene-spin Total RNA Purification Kit (PRO TECH CAT# PT-RNA-MS-50, Taipei, Taiwan). Subsequently, first-strand complementary DNA was synthesized using the reverse transcription kit (SuperScript™ IV kit, Invitrogen, USA). RT-qPCR analyses were performed using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). The genomic DNA was extracted to perform qPCR to measure the gene copy number. Quantification of each gene was conducted, using gene-specific primer pair (with amplicon size of ~100 to 150 bp) on a LightCycler (LightCycler 480, Roche). Each data reflects the average of three replicates. The relative expression was determined relative to the actin gene using the formula 2–∆∆CT (78). The Primer pairs utilized for qPCR analysis are provided in SI Appendix, Table S5.

Enzyme Activity Assays.

The activity of dockerin-fused cellulases was assessed through various assays with specific substrates while Celluclast® 1.5L (cellulase from Trichoderma reesei, Novozymes, Copenhagen, Denmark) was served as a positive control. The activity of endo-glucanase was evaluated with dye-CMC (Megazyme, Wicklow, Ireland) as a substrate. The concentrated supernatant (45 μL) was mixed with 55 μL of buffer solution containing 0.4% dye-CMC, 50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5, in a total reaction mixture of 100 μL. The reaction mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, 250 μL of precipitation solution (composed of sodium acetate trihydrate, zinc acetate, ethanol, pH 5.0) was added to stop the reaction and vortexed 10 s. The mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min and the absorption was measured at 590 nm. Likewise, exoglucanase activity was evaluated using PASC as a substrate. The assay involved mixing 100 μL of supernatant with 900 μL buffer solution containing 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 5) and 0.4% PASC, followed by incubation in Thermal Shaker at 40 °C for 12 h. The amount of released reducing sugars was quantified by the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method and the sugar concentration was calculated based on the glucose standard (79). PASC was prepared as in ref. 80. The activity of β-glucosidase activity was evaluated by measuring the formation of p-Nitrophenol from the hydrolysis of the substrate p-Nitrophenyl-β-D-glucuronide (pNPG) with absorption recorded at 410 nm. Hydrogen peroxide release was quantified using the Amplex Red/HRP assay kit with fluorescence measured at 530 nm excitation and 590 nm emission using a spectrophotometer. All enzymatic assays were performed using concentrated culture supernatant as the enzyme source. Protein concentration was normalized using BSA as a standard. Enzyme assays were conducted at 40 °C.

Estimation of Reducing Sugar Production.

The concentrated supernatants were mixed at a 5:1 ratio (EH: Scaffoldin host) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h without shaking in the presence of 10 mM (CaCl2). Then, 20 mg/mL avicel was added followed by incubation in Thermal Shaker at 40 °C for 12 h. The amount of released reducing sugars was quantified by the DNS method and the sugar concentration was calculated based on the glucose standard (79).

Cellulosome-Complex Assembly.

One liter of the EH and the scaffoldin host culture were grown separately at 37 °C for 48 h at 300 rpm. Cultures were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm, 4 °C to pellet down the yeast cells and the resulting supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane. The filtered supernatants were then concentrated 20-fold using a 10 kDa MWCO Omega membrane centrifugal filter (PALL MAP010C37 Macrosep®, Cytiva Marlborough, MA, purchased from Omics Bio, Taipei, Taiwan). This process was carried out at 6,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. For complex formation, the concentrated supernatants were mixed at a 5:1 ratio (EH: Scaffoldin host) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h without shaking in the presence of 10 mM CaCl2 and 10% cellobiose. Cellobiose is known to block the partial binding of the enzymes with the cellulosic substrate. Subsequently, 10 g/L microcrystalline cellulose was added and the mixture was gently stirred at 4 °C for 2 to 3 h. The mixture was centrifuged (5 min at 4,000 rpm), the supernatants (unbound fractions) were carefully removed, and the cellulose pellet was collected.

Cellulose-Based Protein Purification.

Cellulose pellet was washed thrice with 45 mL TBS containing 1 M NaCl and three additional washes with 45 mL TBS. The protein complex/scaffoldin bound to the pellet was eluted using two volumes (12 mL each) of 1% (v/v) triethylamine and the eluates were neutralized with 0.6 to 1.2 mL of 1 M 2- N-morpholino ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer (pH 5.5). Both the eluted and unbound fractions were concentrated to 1 mL using a 10 kDa Omega membrane centrifugal filter and subsequently analyzed by WB, native PAGE (stacking gel: 4.3%; separating gel: 9%), and SDS-PAGE with a 10% polyacrylamide gel.

Protein Identification by LC-MS/MS.

The protein bands from 1D SDS-PAGE gel were manually excised, diced, and distained with 25% acetonitrile in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3). The gel pieces were reduced with 50 Mm DTE and alkylated with 100 mM iodoacetamide in 25 mM NH4HCO3 pH 8.5 at 37 °C and at room temperature (in dark), respectively. The gel pieces were then washed, dehydrated for 5 min and dried under vacuum. The pieces were rehydrated with 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio of Mass Spectrometry Grade Lys-C (Wako, 125-05061) in 25 mM NH4HCO3, pH 8.5, digested at 37 °C for 3 h. Following Lys-C digestion, the same amount of sequencing grade Trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added and digested at 37 °C for 16 h. Tryptic peptides were extracted twice with 50% acetonitrile/5% TFA, pooled, dried, desalted, and concentrated using C18-ZipTip (Millipore), and eluted with 50% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid.

NanoLC−nanoESI-MS/MS analysis was performed using a nanoAcquity UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA) coupled to a Linear Trap Quadrupole (LTQ) Orbitrap Velos hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Electron, Bremen, Germany) with a Thermo Nanospray Flex Ion Sources interface. Peptides were loaded onto a 75 μm × 25 cm C18 BEH column (1.7 μm, 130 Å) (Waters, Milford, MA). They were separated over a 30 min segmented gradient (5 to 25% solvent B in 27.5 min, then 25 to 35% in 2.5 min) at 300 nL/min at 35 °C. Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid in water; B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. MS survey scans (m/z 350 to 1,600) were acquired in the orbitrap at 60,000 resolution (m/z 400), and automatic gain control (AGC) target at 106. The 20 most intense ions were sequentially isolated for CID MS/MS fragmentation and detection in the linear ion trap (AGC target at 104) with previously selected ions dynamically excluded for 60 s. Ions with the singly and unrecognized charge state were also excluded.

The MS and MS/MS raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (v 3.2.0.450; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) and searched using Mascot server (v.3.1.0; Matrix Science, Boston, MA) against a custom protein sequence database and cRAP contaminate database. Search parameters included trypsin digestion, static modifications set as carbamidomethyl (C), dynamic modifications set as oxidation (M) and allowing up to 2-missed cleavage, mass accuracy of 10 ppm for the parent ion and 0.6 Da for the fragment ions mass tolerance. The master protein candidate found in the custom protein sequence database was included in the results. The data were filtered to a 1% false discovery rate at the peptide level using a target-decoy strategy.

Fermentation and Ethanol Production.

The cellulase host, scaffoldin host, and anchoring host were grown separately. Then, a total of 20 O.D. cells (A600) were harvested and washed twice with YP medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 10 mM CaCl2). Subsequently, resuspended in YP medium containing 0.042% Tween 80, and 1% Avicel. Fermentation took place in a 15 mL anaerobic serum tube with 15% volumetric headspace and incubated at 37 °C, 300 rpm rolling for several days (74). The ethanol concentrations were analyzed using Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) (an Agilent 8860 GC system coupled with a mass detector Agilent 5977B (MSD). A 100 μL aliquot of the culture medium was transferred to a 20 mL glass vial, and incubated at 50 °C on an agitator for 5 min. A 0.5 mL of the headspace gas was introduced to the GC-MS via a headspace autosampler with the syringe temperature set at 70 °C. A CycloSil-B capillary column (30 m length, 0.25 mm internal diameter, and 0.25-μm film thickness) with helium as carrier gas was used for separation. The GC run cycle had an overall runtime of 7.8 min. The initial temperature was 40 °C, held for 2 min, followed by a temperature ramp of 10 °C/min to 60 °C, and then a second ramp of 100 °C/min to 240 °C, held for 2 min. The MSD transfer line was maintained at 300 °C. For the MS detector, the electron multiplier (EM) voltage was set at 961 V. The MS source temperature was 230 °C, and the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C. Mass acquisition was performed in both SIM and scan modes, with the SIM mode specifically monitoring the ethanol signal at 46 m/z. Ethanol content in the yeast culture was quantified by integrating the area under the curve of the peak corresponding to ethanol. A calibration curve was generated using ethanol standards to determine the concentration of ethanol in a sample.

3D-Homology Modeling of Dockerin-Fused Enzymes.

To predict the 3D structures of each protein construct, all the protein sequences were sent to the Colab AlphaFold2 server for 3D structure prediction. Input sequences were aligned against pdb100 database for mining effective modeling templates, “mas mode” was set to mmseqs2_uniref_env for generating nonredundant cluster sets; “pairing mode” was set to the unpaired_paired method to pair the input within and across functional domains against the database.

The inputs were modeled under num_recycle=3 and relax_max_iterations=200 with “greedy” strategy to model against any taxonomically matching sequence subsets. There were five candidate models generated for a protein, and the one with lowest predicted aligned error across function domains was selected as the final predicted structure.

Statistical Analysis.

The experiments were conducted in triplicates and the results are presented as the mean ± SD. The two-tailed Student t test with unequal variance was applied to evaluate statistical significance.
