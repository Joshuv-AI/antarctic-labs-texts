Coronary artery calcification in Type 2 diabetes mellitus: biochemical and inflammatory insights from the Pakistani Population - a case-control study
Authors: Fasiha Fatima, Asher Fawwad, Abdul Basit, Eraj Abbas, Rahima Aftab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are at increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and coronary artery calcium score (CACS) serves as an important tool for cardiovascular risk assessment in this population. This study examines the correlation of CACS with biochemical and inflammatory parameters in individuals with T2DM.MethodsThis case-control study was conducted at Baqai Medical University and comprised 184 individuals with T2DM and 184 healthy people. CACS was assessed with cardiac non-contrast computed tomography. The Agatston method was used to determine CACS, while biochemical markers, such as lipid profiles and HbA1c, were analyzed using enzymatic colorimetric method and high-performance liquid chromatography. Inflammatory parameters, including hs-C reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF- α) were measured using immunoassays.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in median values of age (p < 0.0001), HbA1c (p < 0.0001), cholesterol (p = 0.001), triglycerides (p < 0.0001), hs-CRP (p < 0.0001), and IL-6 (p < 0.0001) in persons with T2DM compared to healthy subjects. Individuals with T2DM exhibited significantly higher CACS in both mild to moderate (1-100) and moderate to severe (> 100) categories compared with healthy controls (p-value < 0.0001). The CACS positively correlated with age (p < 0.0001), HbA1c (p < 0.0001), and low-density lipoprotein levels (p = 0.041). Binary logistic regression showed that elevated HbA1c and hs-CRP were independent predictors of coronary artery calcification in T2DM patients. Other factors, including age, BMI, family history, and lipid levels, were not significant after multivariate adjustment.ConclusionPoor glycemic control and systemic inflammation, reflected by elevated HbA1c and hs-CRP, are key independent predictors of calcified coronary arteries in patients with T2DM, underscoring importance of targeting hyperglycemia and inflammation to reduce cardiovascular risk.Trial registrationClinical trial no. not applicable.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a cluster of metabolic disorders characterised by persistent hyperglycemia, potentially leading to damage in several organs, notably the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels, ultimately resulting in dysfunction [1]. The latest IDF Atlas estimates that there are 33 million individuals living with T2DM in Pakistan, the third-highest diabetes population internationally [2]. T2DM is a well-established independent predictor of cardiovascular disease, with cardiac complications frequently observed in this patient group [3]. Individuals with diabetes are at a substantially elevated risk of developing hypertension and atherosclerosis; moreover, those with persistently raised blood glucose levels tend to exhibit more advanced coronary atherosclerotic disease compared to their non-diabetic counterparts [4]. Individuals with normal blood glucose levels who subsequently develop type 2 diabetes exhibit an elevated cardiovascular risk for years preceding the clinical onset of diabetes [5]. Premature mortality attributable to diabetes mellitus increased by 5% during the period 2000–2016 [6]. Notably, T2DM constitutes over 90% of all diabetes cases, and cardiovascular events in individuals with T2DM significantly contribute to the heightened risk of premature mortality, posing an escalating danger to global health. The involvement of the cardiovascular system in diabetes mostly stems from oxidative stress induced by inflammation, insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and various other risk factors [7, 8].

The evaluation of subclinical atherosclerosis, such as through the Coronary artery calcification (CAC) score assessment, may enable the timely detection of cardiovascular risk among individuals with type 2 diabetes who have yet to develop clinical symptoms [9]. CACS is a non-invasive imaging procedure designed to assess calcium deposits quantity in the coronary arteries and serves as a robust predictor of future cardiovascular events. CACS has emerged as a critical prognostic tool for diabetes patients with a coronary calcium score (CCS) ≥ 200 predicting poor outcomes [10]. However, the extent to which CACS correlates with various biochemical and inflammatory indices in clinically asymptomatic patients with T2DM has not been comprehensively established in the existing literature. This study investigates the association of coronary artery calcium score with biochemical and inflammatory markers in T2DM.

Methods

Study design

This case control study was conducted from May 2022 to January 2023 in the Department of Biochemistry, Baqai Medical University in collaboration with the Baqai Institute of Diabetes and Endocrinology. The study approved by ethics committee of Baqai Medical University (BMU-EC/01-2022). The study protocol was in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants volunteered, and were informed about the research protocol. Complete verbal and written informed consent were obtained.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated by using online open epi software (version 3.01) assuming a confidence interval of 95% and 80% statistical power. A reference study was used to calculate sample size by comparing two means and it is calculated as 184 in each group [11].

Study population

A total of 368 adults were recruited for the study after applying exclusion criteria. 184 eligible participants were adults (aged > 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for a minimum of one year [12], undergoing treatment with oral hypoglycemic agents and insulin therapy, and without any documented history of cardiovascular disease or symptoms indicative of coronary artery disease using convenience sampling; the remaining 184 participants were healthy persons recruited from same population source. Individuals with liver disease, Type 1 diabetes mellitus, infectious diseases, acute or chronic inflammatory conditions, renal disorders, pregnancy, steroid therapy, chronic smoking habits, autoimmune diseases, and those with a history of COVID-19 infection within the preceding three months were excluded from the study.

Biochemical analysis

All study participants were requested to come with 10–12 h of fasting for sample collection at the research laboratory of Baqai Institute of Diabetes and Endocrinology. After taking all aseptic measures, a trained phlebotomist collected 10 ml of venous blood from each participant. Biochemical parameters, including a lipid profile, were analyzed using Automatic Biochemistry Analyzer, Cobas (Roche, Germany). HbA1c measurement was performed with a Bio-Rad HPLC kit. Serum hs-CRP, Interleukin-6, and Tumor necrosis factor-α were measured using an ELISA kit. Coronary Artery Calcium score measurement.

A 64-sliced multi-detector CT scanner (Toshiba Scanner Aquilion One TSX-301) with high-resolution detectors imaged coronary artery calcification by certified technologist and radiologist. Image acquisition was performed using adjacent slices with a uniform thickness of 3 mm. A calcified lesion was defined as ≥ 3 contiguous pixels, with a peak attenuation of 130 Hounsfield units. Each lesion was then scored using the method developed by Agatston et al., 1990 [13]. The calcium score was derived by multiplying the total area of each plaque by a density-weighted cofactor based on the peak Hounsfield Unit (HU) value within the lesion: a cofactor of 1 was assigned for peak densities ranging from 130 to 199 HU, 2 for those between 200 and 299 HU, 3 for densities between 300 and 399 HU, and 4 for any lesion up to or exceeding 400 HU.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Baseline characteristics of the study participants were presents as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) as data were skewed. Kruskal-Wallis H and Mann-Whitney U test compared continuous variables, whereas the chi-squared test compared categorical variables between groups. Spearman correlation was used to check the relationship between variables. Multivariate analysis was performed after adjustment for potential confounding factors including age, BMI, glycemic parameters, lipid profile, and inflammatory markers. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the associations between different cardiovascular risk factors, T2DM and CAC scores. p-value < 0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical, biochemical and imaging characteristic of study participants are presented in Table 1. Individuals with T2DM were significantly older than healthy individuals with a median age of 50 years vs. 46 years in healthy subjects. The sex distribution was similar between groups with 192 males (52%) and 176 females (48%). Patients with T2DM had a positive family history of diabetes as compared to healthy individuals. Body mass index was significantly higher among T2DM than healthy individuals. No other differences were observed between T2DM and healthy individuals for marital status, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, and waist-to-hip ratio. Hb A1c, cholesterol, and triglyceride levels were higher among T2DM than healthy individuals. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between groups, in terms of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL). Regarding inflammatory parameter hs-CRP, IL-6 and TNF- α were significantly higher among T2DM than healthy individuals.

Table 2 reveals significant differences in CACS distribution between individuals with T2DM and controls. A higher percentage of controls (82.6%) had no CACS compared to 53.3% of T2DM subjects. The T2DM group exhibited a greater prevalence of mild to moderate CACS (1–100) at 28.8%, and moderate to severe scores (> 100) at 17.9%, compared to controls at 9.8% and 7.6%, respectively. Furthermore, the duration of T2DM is correlated with increased CACS severity, with median durations rising from 3 years in patients with no CACS to 10 years in those with moderate to severe CACS, showing a significant association (p < 0.0001) between diabetes duration and CACS severity.

Figure 1 demonstrates a statistically significant positive moderate correlation between CACS and age (p-value < 0.0001) whereas no significant association were observed with body mass index (p = 0.406, r = 0.044) and waist-to-hip ratio (p = 0.0595, r = 0.028). CACS was significantly but weakly correlated with HbA1c (p-value < 0.0001; r = 0.271), cholesterol (p-value = 0.045; r = 0.104), and low-density lipoprotein (p-value < 0.05; r = 0.041) in contrast (Fig. 2) and no correlation of CACS was found with triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein. Inflammatory parameters, including IL -6, TNF-α, and hs-CRP were also not correlated with CACS (Fig. 3).

To determine independent predictors of coronary artery calcification, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed after adjustment for age, BMI, glycemic parameters, lipid profile, and inflammatory markers.

In the adjusted model, hs-CRP emerged as a statistically significant independent predictor of CAC (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.09–1.72, p < 0.05), indicating that higher hs-CRP levels were associated with increased odds of coronary artery calcification after controlling for confounding factors.

In contrast, IL-6 and TNF-α did not demonstrate statistically significant independent associations with CAC in the multivariate model, suggesting that their crude associations were attenuated after adjustment for metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors.

HbA1c remained a strong independent predictor of CAC in the adjusted model (OR = 207, 95% CI: 41.8–1029, p < 0.05), highlighting the central role of chronic hyperglycemia in coronary calcification. These findings were considered statistically significant with p<0.05. (Table 3)

Discussion

CACS distribution, T2DM risk, and demographic associations

T2DM individuals had a 3.58-fold higher risk of presenting with significant CACS compared to healthy controls. The median CACS was significantly lower in the control group confirming the well-known association of T2DM and accelerated coronary atherosclerosis. Interestingly, patients with T2DM had a higher prevalence of mild to moderate coronary artery calcification (28.8%) compared with healthy controls (9.8%), suggesting a tendency for early calcification in the absence of apparent cardiovascular symptoms. This uniform pattern is in line with the longitudinal findings of Ke et al. [14] who noted a comparable relationship between diabetes duration and increasing severity of calcification. Previous studies have shown that subclinical coronary artery disease is an independent risk factor of T2DM [15].

A significant positive moderate correlation was seen between CACS and age, in agreement with Javaid et al. [16], who found that calcification was more prevalent and extensive in older persons, with a higher effect in males. Within our group, males had 1.86-fold greater odds of significant CACS compared with females, consistent with the well-established greater burden of premature coronary artery disease in males. These demographic associations are different to the studies of Takamura et al. [17] and Halon et al. [18]. The differences may be due to the ethnicity and age distribution of the study population and the inclusion criteria used; both studies were performed in non-South Asian populations where the baseline cardiovascular risk profiles are very different to those of Pakistani patients with T2DM.

Anthropometric parameters and CACS

Although patients with T2DM had significantly higher BMI than healthy controls — consistent with Alzamil et al. [19], in whom mean BMI exceeded 31 kg/m² — no significant association was found between BMI and CACS in our cohort. This contrasts with Wang et al. [20], and may be explained by the recognised inadequacy of BMI as a measure of visceral adiposity, which is the metabolically active fat compartment most strongly linked to atherosclerotic risk. In South Asian populations particularly, visceral adiposity is disproportionately elevated even at normal or overweight BMI values, meaning that BMI categories may lack the precision needed to capture cardiometabolic risk in this group. Similarly, no significant association was observed between waist-hip ratio and CACS, in contrast to Eun et al. [21]; however, that study was conducted exclusively in postmenopausal women, in whom estrogen deficiency is a specific mediator of coronary atherosclerosis — a mechanism not applicable to our mixed-sex cohort.

Lipid parameters and CACS

Serum triglycerides and total cholesterol were significantly higher in T2DM participants compared with healthy controls, in contrast to Lei et al. [22], and may reflect differences in dietary patterns, use of lipid-lowering therapy and population-specific metabolic characteristics between the two study cohorts. A significant positive correlation was found between CACS and total cholesterol (r = 0.104, p = 0.045) and LDL cholesterol (r = 0.041, p < 0.05), yet the magnitude of these associations was small and accounted for less than 2% of the variance in CACS, warranting cautious interpretation. In this context it is important to note that total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol were not independent predictors of CACS in the multivariate logistic regression model (Table 3). This finding further supports the interpretation that bivariate correlations of total and LDL cholesterol, albeit statistically detectable, are of limited independent clinical significance.

In addition, 46.7% of T2DM participants in our cohort had measurable coronary artery calcification (CAC > 0), suggesting that a considerable proportion of participants were already beyond the subclinical threshold. The directionality of the lipid associations is consistent with the findings of Mortensen et al. [23] who found that LDL-C levels were associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease events only among patients with established coronary atherosclerosis (CAC > 0; aHR: 1.18), but not among those without calcification (CAC = 0; aHR: 1.02). This suggests that LDL cholesterol may be a driver of the evolution of coronary atherosclerosis, once calcification is established, rather than an initial driver of calcification in asymptomatic patients with T2DM. The attenuated lipid-CACS associations in our study may also reflect the confounding influence of lipid-lowering therapy and dietary factors that were not fully controlled for in the analysis. Similar to Abd Alamir et al. [24], we found no association between hypertriglyceridemia and CACS in our cohort. This suggests that isolated elevations in triglyceride levels may not be an independent pathological risk factor for subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, but their role in broader cardiovascular disease is well established. The HDL cholesterol levels in T2DM patients were similar to healthy controls, as was shown by Xepapadaki et al. [25] and Zhang et al. [26], and did not correlate with CACS. This finding underscores the complex relationship between diabetes and HDL metabolism; while HDL-C concentrations may be preserved in T2DM, qualitative structural and functional abnormalities of HDL particles – not reflected by HDL-C measurement alone – may still contribute to residual atherosclerotic risk.

Inflammatory markers and CACS

IL-6 levels were markedly elevated in T2DM individuals compared to healthy controls, as indicated by Domazet et al. [27], and signify the persistent low-grade inflammatory condition linked to T2DM. Excessive IL-6 results in the dysregulation of cytokine signaling through the stimulation of the JAK-STAT pathway, leading to inflammatory and metabolic disturbances [28]. Polymorphisms of the IL-6 gene have been independently linked to coronary artery disease [29]. However, in our sample, we did not see any significant association between IL-6 and CACS, in contrast to Kraaijenhof et al. [30]. Simultaneously, TNF-α levels were markedly elevated in T2DM subjects, consistent with Alzamil et al. [19], although did not exhibit a correlation with CACS. A credible rationale for the absence of correlations between inflammatory markers and CACS in our cohort is the pharmacological influence of biguanide (metformin) medication administered to almost half of the T2DM subjects.

Indirect anti-inflammatory effects are mediated by effects on hyperglycemia, obesity and lipid profiles, which decrease systemic inflammation and its effect on atherosclerosis [31]. This interpretation is supported by evidence from the US Diabetes Prevention Program, which showed that biguanide therapy reduces CRP levels in subjects with impaired glucose tolerance [32]. Unlike the bivariate results, hs-CRP was found to be an independent predictor of CACS in the multivariate logistic regression model (Table 3), in agreement with Zeb et al. [33] and with the literature on the association between systemic inflammation and the severity of atherosclerosis in diabetic patients [34]. This apparent discrepancy between the bivariate and multivariate results for hs-CRP highlights the importance of adjusted analyses in teasing out independent predictors from correlated metabolic variables. That hs-CRP remained significant after adjustment for glycemic, lipid and demographic covariates suggests that systemic inflammation contributes to coronary artery calcification via pathways at least partly independent of glycemic control and dyslipidemia. In addition, other commonly prescribed pharmacological agents in patients with cardiovascular comorbidities such as beta-blockers have been shown to affect biochemical and oxidative stress markers such as gamma-glutamyltransferase and uric acid [35], further emphasising the need for a comprehensive documentation of medications in studies investigating biochemical correlates of coronary artery calcification.

Glycemic control and independent predictors of CACS

The independent association of HbA1c with CACS in the multivariate model is consistent with prior literature demonstrating poor glycemic control as an important contributor to the development and progression of coronary artery calcification [36]. Chronic hyperglycemia causes oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and vascular smooth muscle calcification through advanced glycation end-products and inflammatory mediators which provides a mechanistic basis for the observed HbA1c-CACS association. HbA1c and hs-CRP as independent predictors reveal the dual role of metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation in subclinical coronary atherosclerosis in patients with T2DM. In addition to static measures of glycemic control, a growing body of evidence suggests that sustained glycemic fluctuations and glycemic variability may exert additional deleterious effects on coronary vasculature through a number of molecular mechanisms including alterations in heart rate dynamics and dysregulation of long non-coding RNA pathways controlling vascular inflammation and macrovascular injury [37]. The findings of the present study are of particular significance in the context of cardiovascular risk in South Asia. Despite large multinational cohorts such as the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) providing substantial baseline CAC data across multiple ethnicities [11, 18], South Asians, particularly Pakistanis, are generally under-represented, and thus direct application of established risk thresholds to this population is not clinically appropriate. South Asians are known to develop cardiovascular disease at younger ages and lower BMI thresholds and exhibit disproportionate visceral adiposity, early-onset insulin resistance and a pro-inflammatory phenotype even in the absence of overt obesity [3, 8, 19, 27], features prominently reflected in our cohort. Other potential population-based contributors including diets high in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats, and genetic risk factors including high lipoprotein(a) and specific apolipoprotein polymorphisms, may also increase atherosclerotic risk beyond traditional risk models [7, 23, 24]. Together, these results highlight the urgent need for ethnicity-specific cardiovascular risk stratification tools for South Asian patients with T2DM and support the need to expand CAC research to geographically under-represented populations.

Recent studies by Shrivastav et al. show that T2DM patients with coronary artery disease have elevated glycation markers (CML) and inflammatory markers compared to non-diabetic patients consistent with findings in our cohort. T2DM also affects the pattern and severity of coronary stenosis in ways Western risk models may not adequately capture. These findings suggest that the inflammatory and glycation-driven mechanisms of diabetic heart disease in South Asian populations are distinct enough to warrant region-specific research, rather than relying on data from Western or East Asian cohorts [38, 39]. Further research in this area may explore microRNAs and MTHFR gene polymorphisms as additional diagnostic markers along with HbA1c and hs-CRP to identify high-risk T2DM patients at earlier stages. Likewise, the use of natural and plant-based therapeutic strategies for T2DM and its cardiovascular complications remain under-researched in South Asian communities where local dietary habits and cultural practices are significant contributors to disease progression and treatment adherence [40, 41].

Limitations

Some potential confounders such as medication history and duration of diabetes were not fully controlled, which could have affected the observed associations. Concomitant medication, including statins, antihypertensive agents and anti-inflammatory drugs, was not systematically collected from all participants and therefore could not be included as covariates in the multivariate regression model. This is a potential source of residual confounding, as these agents are known to affect lipid profiles, blood pressure and inflammatory markers. In addition, although some data were available on antidiabetic therapy (approximately half of the T2DM participants received biguanide (metformin) therapy), complete and standardised medication data were not available for all participants. Because biguanides are known to have indirect anti-inflammatory effects, the observed attenuation of the associations of inflammatory markers with CACS may partly reflect a pharmacologic effect rather than a true biological null association. Another limitation is the lack of formal interobserver variability testing but all scans were scored by one experienced reader using standardised software reducing intraobserver variability.

The extremely wide confidence interval for the HbA1c odds ratio (95% CI: 41.8–1029) in the multivariate model reflects statistical imprecision arising from the skewed distribution of HbA1c values, the presence of high-value outliers, and sample size constraints; this estimate should therefore be interpreted with caution. Lifestyle and dietary factors, known to influence cardiometabolic risk, were not completely assessed. Finally, the study provides important data from a South Asian population, but residual confounding and unmeasured variables cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

The study highlights that coronary artery calcification was associated with diabetes, age, and sex. This study demonstrates that among patients with T2DM, poor glycemic control and systemic inflammation, as indicated by elevated HbA1c and hs-CRP, are the most important independent predictors of coronary artery calcification. These results emphasize the vital need for early detection and personalised interventions based on these factors to effectively manage CAD risk. By closely monitoring individuals with diabetes and older adults, especially men, for early signs of CAD, the burden of cardiovascular events can be significantly reduced.
