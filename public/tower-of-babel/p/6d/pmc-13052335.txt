“They Align Themselves With Us, Whereas I'm Aligned With the Patient”: A Multi‐Site Qualitative Study of Resident Physicians' Experiences With Law Enforcement in the Emergency Department
Authors: Michelle Suh, Naomi Newton, Beatrice Torres, Nkele Davis, Josiah Rich, Dhara Amin, Bisan A. Salhi, Ambrose H. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLaw enforcement is a common yet complicated presence in the emergency department (ED). Studies have found emergency medicine (EM) attending physicians have varied interactions with and attitudes toward law enforcement. EM resident physicians are in a uniquely formative professional period. However, the experiences and perspectives of EM resident physicians have not been previously explored in depth.MethodsWe recruited EM resident physicians from three geopolitically distinct urban academic EDs in the United States to participate in semi‐structured interviews regarding their experiences with law enforcement in the ED. We analyzed the transcripts thematically, looking for unifying concepts across institutions and latent meaning. A codebook was created and iteratively refined based on review of transcripts, after which salient themes were developed through ongoing discussions with the research team.ResultsTwenty‐four resident physicians across three sites participated. Three salient themes emerged from our study: ambiguity of authority between emergency physicians and law enforcement, concerns about the impact of law enforcement presence on patients, and disruptions to the ED team workflow. First, as trainees learning professional norms in the ED, participants reported difficulty navigating situations in which law enforcement attempted to assert authority over patient care. Second, participants perceived negative impacts of law enforcement presence on patients in custody and non‐incarcerated patients of color receiving care in the ED. Finally, participants described frequent disruptions to the ED team workflow due to the presence of law enforcement. Participants relied on hospital security, rather than law enforcement, to ensure the safety of patients and staff.ConclusionEmergency resident physicians described ambiguous boundaries and complex interactions among law enforcement, patients, and themselves in the ED. Although our study found several concerning impacts of law enforcement in the ED, these represent opportunities to clarify roles and responsibilities in the emergency physician‐law enforcement relationship and improve patient care. Further research is needed, including eliciting perspectives of law enforcement and patients, particularly those with substance use disorders, trauma, and serious mental illness.

Introduction

Law enforcement officers are often present in emergency departments (ED) in the United States. Patients who are incarcerated (such as in jail, prison, and detention facilities) often access care through the ED, given standard medical procedures (e.g., regarding “medical clearance”) and limited access to primary care and specialist care within carceral health systems. Patients with substance use disorders, serious mental illness, and trauma also are commonly seen in the ED and interact with law enforcement. Law enforcement officers regularly accompany incarcerated patients and patients in police custody to the ED for the duration of the patient encounter. Given the diversity of patients' complaints, law enforcement officers may be present in any area of the ED including medical resuscitation or trauma bays, hallway stretchers, and/or waiting rooms. As a result, law enforcement officers regularly interact with hospital staff including emergency physicians, nurses, technicians, and medical students. For the rest of the paper, “officers” will refer to law enforcement officers.

The role of officers in the ED varies between patients and across settings. Officers may document injuries, conduct investigations, supervise patients in custody, and may even patrol ED waiting rooms [1, 2, 3]. Officers also may seek to gather patient‐specific information from both the patient and ED staff for judicial or investigative purposes. Additionally, officers often accompany patients in custody to ensure safety in the ED.

The care of patients who are incarcerated or in custody carries unique ethical and clinical nuances, in part due to the presence of officers. The constant presence of officers during the patient encounter can raise concerns for dual loyalty, defined as the perceived or real conflicts between physicians' ethical obligations toward their patients and competing responsibilities to third parties [4, 5]. Examination and treatment of patients who are incarcerated may also be hindered by the presence of shackles [6, 7]. The stigma of incarceration status itself can lead to potential bias in the healthcare setting, and this stigma and bias may be further reinforced by the presence of officers [8, 9].

Despite these complexities, literature has shown wide variability in emergency physician interactions, knowledge, and attitudes regarding law enforcement in the ED. Survey studies have shown uncertainty among emergency physicians regarding their interactions with officers in the ED, particularly regarding shackling and the necessity of law enforcement's presence at bedside [9, 10, 11]. One study of emergency physicians in Northern California noted their ED interactions with officers were mostly positive but marked by ill‐defined boundaries and interference with patient care [3].

Emergency medicine (EM) resident physicians may have a unique role and interaction with officers in the ED. Resident physicians are in a formative professional period, learning through structured residency didactics and direct patient care under supervision of attending physicians. Resident physicians have the simultaneous roles of being both early decision‐makers while ultimately deferring to attending physicians. In academic centers, resident physicians may interact more frequently with officers than supervising attending physicians, since resident physicians are the first‐line physicians in the ED. Additionally, resident physicians are often rotating across different institutions and thus more transitory than attending physicians and ED nurses [12, 13]. Thus, we sought to understand EM residents' perspectives and training regarding interactions with officers in the ED.

Methods

We conducted a multi‐institutional qualitative study of EM residents to identify unifying themes and compare institutions for variations in local practices. The study was approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB‐24‐1561). This research is reported using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Appendix S1).

Study Sites

Study sites were three academic emergency residency programs in urban, geopolitically diverse regions (Midwest, Northeast, and South) of the United States. All three sites are level 1 trauma centers and routinely see patients within the carceral system, either in police custody or incarcerated (in jail or prison). One site is a county hospital. Each site has a variety of officers' affiliations, some of whom are armed while in the ED (Table 1). We defined officers as police, detectives, sheriffs, university and hospital police; this definition was shared with participants at the beginning of the interview.

Recruitment and Data Collection

Study recruitment and interviews took place during the academic year 2024–2025. Participants at each site were recruited through announcements at residency didactics and resident listservs. EM residents were eligible to participate if they had interacted with at least one officer in the preceding 6 months. Subjects received a $50 gift card for participating.

The interview guide explored EM resident perceptions of and interactions with officers in the ED, as well as previous training regarding officers in the ED. The interview guide was created by the study team including emergency physicians with educational experience, administrative leadership, and firsthand clinical and research experience regarding officers. The guide's development was informed by a literature review and the study team's priorities. The guide was pilot tested with EM residents at a different residency program and further refined via iterative input by the study team.

Virtual interviews were conducted by members of the study team (MS, BT) with prior experience with semi‐structured interviewing. Interviews were recorded, transcribed by AI software (Otter.ai), manually proofread for accuracy by team members (MS, BT) and de‐identified for data analysis.

Data Analysis

The study team used an inductive analytic approach to uncover patterns and meanings to elucidate relevant themes [14]. Based on review of initial transcripts, we developed an initial codebook addressing power and authority, impact on physicians, impact on patients, aspects of patient care, ED care team workflow, and types of officers (Appendix S2). Subsequently, each transcript was coded independently by two members of the study team (MS, NN) with coding discrepancies resolved through discussions with the study team and iterative refinement of the codebook. After all the transcripts had been coded, the team identified and refined themes and subthemes during a series of team meetings. Authors returned to the original transcripts as a means of confirming and deepening themes and subthemes. Throughout our data analysis, we prioritized reflexivity—a process of continuous reflection on how the experiences, values, and preconceptions of the study team affect the interpretation of data [15, 16].

Results

Twenty‐four residents from three sites participated in this study. Although there were 170 residents across all three sites, it is unknown how many met eligibility criteria of interacting with at least one officer in the preceding 6 months. Seven participants self‐identified as men and 17 as women (Table 2). Nine participants had no prior interactions with officers outside of the ED; four participants had family and friends who were officers, and six participants reported prior personal interactions with officers regarding traffic stops and tickets; and four participants had negative personal interactions with officers or had close relationships with people who had negative personal interactions with officers (Table 2). One of the participants reported both positive and negative personal interactions with officers. Three participants each at three different sites reported receiving training and education about officers in the ED; 21 participants across the three sites stated that they did not receive formal training and education on officers. Interviews lasted on average 31 min, with a range of 21 min to 1 h. We conducted interviews until thematic saturation was reached, indicating adequate sample size. Three salient themes were identified from our study: ambiguity of authority between emergency physicians and officers, concerns about negative impact of officers' presence on patients, and disruptions to the ED workflow (Table 3).

Theme 1: Ambiguity of Authority Between Emergency Physicians and Law Enforcement

Participants reported difficulty navigating clinical situations involving a potential deference of power to officers. Participants expressed that the role of the healthcare team was to determine the appropriate care for each patient, but tensions may arise when officers are perceived to be asserting their authority in the clinical decision‐making process. Several participants described encounters where officers tried to assert authority in a clinical situation such as arguing about the necessity of a workup that would extend the patient's stay in the ED and demanding a disposition. One participant described an incident where she approached an officer in the ED who responded angrily to her question of when a patient in custody would be available for interviewing. When she debriefed the incident with her attending on shift, he explained that the officer may have viewed his own authority in the ED being challenged by her questioning. However, she reported viewing herself as the authority figure in the ED:“Law enforcement as well as medical staff, physicians, and nurses are used to having authority and control over situations…. As a physician, my goal is to keep the patient safe, and I am the one who's in charge of running the room [during the resuscitation], whether police are there or not.” ID 12, Site 2

“Law enforcement as well as medical staff, physicians, and nurses are used to having authority and control over situations…. As a physician, my goal is to keep the patient safe, and I am the one who's in charge of running the room [during the resuscitation], whether police are there or not.” ID 12, Site 2

Establishing authority in the ED seemed most salient during times of conflicting goals during the patient encounter. Participants perceived a mismatch between emergency physicians seeking to provide care for the patient and officers seeking to return the patient back to jail or prison prior to shift change:“I feel like [law enforcement] align themselves with us, whereas I feel like I'm aligned with the patient. I'm here to care for the patient, whereas I think maybe the officer in custody thinks, ‘Oh we're on the same team, like we're communicating.’ Not that there's teams [necessarily], but we're here to take care of the patient. It's not my goal to get the patient back to jail as quickly as possible. It's my goal to provide whatever care they need, and if that's getting admitted, if that's a procedure, then whatever they need.” ID 9, Site 1

Subtheme: Women Physicians Experienced Greater Resistance From Officers

Several participants across all three sites reported perceiving greater pushback from officers to women physicians compared to their male counterparts. The resistance from officers arose in situations where women participants tried to assert their authority as physicians, such as requesting repositioning of handcuffs of their patients or asking officers to step outside for a sensitive exam. Participants reported not seeing the same level of challenge from officers to their men physician counterparts; one man resident also reported encounters where the law enforcement officer argued with his senior woman resident physician more than with men physicians:“For most of the time, I'm mistaken as a nurse or a tech [by the officer]… I think your decision making is questioned more as a female. I say this having spoken to my male counterparts who don't get asked the same questions, don't have the same experiences. So I wouldn't be surprised if that's the issue. And I think a lot of times, it feels like there's ego at play.” ID 17, Site 2

Subtheme: Residents Had a Uniquely Challenging and Uncomfortable Position Navigating Situations as Physicians and Trainees

Participants reported higher levels of uncertainty while interacting with officers as junior residents. This uncertainty was often met with greater resistance from officers. In contrast, senior residents spoke more authoritatively, and their requests were more often accepted by officers:“When I first started, interns are always a little hesitant because we're doing this for the first time. I think the biggest thing is if [law enforcement] does give you pushback, or if they are a little snarky, you just have to stay on your ground and they will listen because they have to.” ID 10, Site 1

I think the biggest thing is if [law enforcement] does give you pushback, or if they are a little snarky, you just have to stay on your ground and they will listen because they have to.” ID 10, Site 1

Most participants described learning how to act more assertively in their interactions with officers by seeing their senior residents and attendings model similar behavior with success. However, in one notable instance, a resident witnessed officers assaulting their patient while receiving care within the ED. When the resident asked leadership how to respond, the only guidance was for the medical evaluation of the patient's injuries. Disturbed by the police brutality, the resident leveraged their personal connection with a public defender to learn how to file a report with the police department; upon review of the body camera footage of the incident, the police department suspended the officers.

Theme 2: Concerns About Negative Impact of Officers Presence on Patients

Subtheme: Patients Withholding Information in the Presence of Officers

Participants across all three sites reported concerns about the impact of officers' presence on the physician‐patient relationship. Participants perceived that patients were withholding information from ED staff when in the presence of officers, due to concern for discovery. The presence of officers during patient interviews was seen as a potential barrier to eliciting clinically important histories with relevant information:“Just the fact that [law enforcement] are sitting in the room can interfere with some types of questioning during the interview… [When interviewing trauma patients], we like to ask, what speed do you think you're going, or what speed was the road you were on, just to assess what the level of trauma was. And if the police are in their vicinity, they'll be a lot more hesitant to share that because I guess they're worried that the police are going to put that into the police report about what happened with the accident.” ID 22, Site 3

Subtheme: Concerns of Officers Infringing on Patients' Rights

Participants reported that despite taking actions to try to preserve patients' rights to privacy and thorough examinations, they frequently encountered resistance from officers. Multiple participants recounted attempts to remove or reposition shackles to conduct a physical examination of the patient that were refused by officers:“Sometimes it can lead to some friction between law enforcement and the medical team, especially if law enforcement doesn't feel that the person's medical concern is legitimate. For example, [a patient was] chained to the bed and actively seizing, so we needed to unshackle them in order to protect their airway and give them medications and get IV access. Subtheme: Concerns About Uniquely Adverse Impact of Officers Presence on Patients of Color Regardless of Incarceration Status

Participants also reported concerns about the impact of officers on other patients in the ED who were not in custody. Participants were concerned about the history of structural racism and policing in communities of color. Citing the antagonistic history between officers and communities of color in the United States, participants described concerns about the impact of officers' presence in the ED on patients of color who were not in custody. Participants explicitly described patients of color as vulnerable populations for whom the therapeutic environment of the ED may be threatened by the presence of officers:“I think a lot of [our patients] see themselves in Breonna Taylor as well as George Floyd. And I think that the community as a whole has an extremely negative interaction with law enforcement. So to come to a hospital where they're not feeling well or they're expecting to get help, I think [law enforcement in the ED] is beyond triggering for them, and the actions of the law enforcement, again, usually does not make things better.” ID 24, site 2

So to come to a hospital where they're not feeling well or they're expecting to get help, I think [law enforcement in the ED] is beyond triggering for them, and the actions of the law enforcement, again, usually does not make things better.” ID 24, site 2

Participants described seeing changes in patients' behavior around officers such as looking down more and being quieter. One participant reported seeing adult trauma drop‐offs by friends and family at the nearby children's hospital. After one such incident, the resident was told by nurses at the children's hospital that this is common practice to avoid interactions with officers, since the children's hospital is known to have less officers presence than the nearby adult level 1 trauma center.

Theme 3: Disruptions to the ED Care Team Workflow

Participants reported frequent disruptions to the typical ED workflow due to the presence of officers in the ED. These disruptions typically occurred when officers attempted to expedite or circumvent workups and treatment plans ordered by the emergency physician or when they repeatedly requested information about the patient's status, particularly regarding disposition and timing of discharge:“Sometimes it can get a little bit tense and a little bit touchy in those circumstances where it feels as though I'm trying to focus on the patient and the workup … and then you [the law enforcement] are just worried about how long you're going to be on the clock and if I can set you free from this case…. It's frustrating, because I have so many sick people and I'm worried about this, and I have other things that I'm also navigating in addition to that patient [in custody]. But then [the law enforcement is] just in my face, showing up multiple times, trying to press, trying to send the nurse to come after me to ask, can they go? Sometimes it can be a little bit frustrating and irritating.” ID 21, Site 3

Subtheme: Hospital Security, Not Officers, Acts as Part of the Care Team

Participants acknowledged the importance of ensuring the safety of patients and staff in the ED but relied more upon hospital security than officers for safety. In fact, without prompting from the study team, participants across all three sites often differentiated hospital security from officers. Hospital security was described as part of the care team and aligned in terms of care priorities and patient de‐escalation strategies:“Our [hospital] security are in the ER with us every day. We know them, we know their faces, so we have more of an interpersonal relationship with them, and they are directly there to protect us from combative patients. And they have really good training on de‐escalation. They start verbal, and then they'll go physical and hold the patient down. I have never witnessed them doing anything excessive to the patients. They keep us safe…. Once the situation is done, they're out of the room. They are not staying one second longer. They know: situation's done, clear out. Let them keep doing the medical stuff.” ID 16, Site 2

Discussion

Our multi‐site qualitative study of EM residents found conflicting roles and relationships with officers, particularly regarding authority in the ED, negative impacts on patients in custody and patients of color in the ED, and frequent disruptions to the typical ED care team workflow.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of resident physicians' experiences with officers across multiple institutions. Our findings are in line with prior EM studies demonstrating poorly defined roles and boundaries of officers in the ED [10, 11]. Although our participants sought to prioritize the care of the patient, they described multiple barriers and pressure from officers while in the ED.

The safety of patients and hospital staff is important, especially amidst rising rates of workplace violence against ED staff [17]. Some may argue the presence of officers is necessary for ensuring safety and required for legal purposes. However, our interviews suggest that officer presence may be triggering and threatening to other patients including those not in custody, and even ED physicians. Our study highlights an opportunity for officers and ED physicians to work together to find a path forward for maintaining the safety of the ED without inciting unnecessary fear or aggression.

Clearer interprofessional policies regarding the appropriate role and behavior of officers in the ED, explicit training in residency, and close coordination with law enforcement departments may help improve patient care. Although there have been professional guidelines for ED care of patients in custody and commentaries proposing resident education for patients in custody [12, 18], law enforcement‐specific ED policies may mitigate some of the disruptions and negative impacts described by our study participants. Policies may need to clarify the role of and expectations for officers in the ED to allow the ED care team to provide patient care and protect patient rights. Potential topics to address include the process of removing/readjusting handcuffs and shackles when medically necessary; ED staff being requested to participate in evidence collection and/or other law enforcement processes (legal blood draws, body cavity searches); acceptable areas and times for the usage of body cameras; appropriate disclosure of protected health information; which areas of the ED are accessible to officers including physician workspaces; and reporting mechanisms to address staff concerns. These policies will require close coordination of ED leadership and law enforcement leadership, as well as the input of communities of color and patients and families with lived experiences with the carceral system. Both ED staff and officers may benefit from training about these best practices.

Our study has several limitations. First, interviews were carried out at academic, urban residency programs serving a primarily adult patient population. Thus, the findings reflect the experiences of these residents and their particular training environments and may not be transferable to other programs (e.g., community programs), geographic locations (e.g., rural settings or other areas of the US), and/or clinical settings (e.g., pediatric EDs). Second, participants may have distinct experiences not shared by residents who did not participate in our study. Nevertheless, our study shares similarities with other studies of officers in the ED and suggests dynamics that may affect patient care and are worthy of continued research [3, 9]. Third, participants may have felt compelled to take part in this study, as recruitment was led in part by faculty members at each site. We were cognizant of the fact that residents are still physicians in training and may feel vulnerable in sharing their thoughts and opinions on officers in the ED, so we sought to account for this throughout this study and to minimize resident vulnerability whenever possible (e.g., by limiting interviewing to a trained non‐physician research assistant and deidentifying responses). Fourth, we carried out our interviews in the period after the Black Lives Matter protests of 2020 and before the changes in the scope of policing in the US (e.g., through expansion of US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and the deployment of the military and/or national guard to US cities). It is likely that these changes would have influenced our participants' responses and/or perceptions of officers in the ED, and including the perspectives of participants with Hispanic ethnicities is critical given the targeting of these communities by ICE [19]. In general, thoughts and feelings toward police officers are subject to personal experiences, broader political discourse, and other forces that may not have been immediately evident to our participants or readily identifiable in a semi‐structured interview format. Future studies are needed to better understand how the changes and expansion of policing contributes to residents' experiences, perception of officers, and patient care in the ED. At last, given the nature of qualitative research, we are only able to report participants' perceptions and lived experiences. Further work should include other methodologies, such as observed ethnographic work, may help characterize interactions. Although one single‐site ethnographic observed officers in the ED, results were focused on about location, chief complaint of patients, time of day and length of interactions [20]. Future studies should also explore perspectives of attending physicians, ED nurses, officers, and patients in custody.

Although our study found several concerning impacts of officers in the ED on patients and patient care, we argue these highlight a valuable opportunity to clarify roles and responsibilities in the emergency physician‐officers relationship as well as patient care.
