Comparison of the Hospital Burden Associated with Respiratory Syncytial Virus and Human Metapneumovirus Across All Age Groups in Scotland: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Durga Kulkarni, Richard Osei-Yeboah, You Li, Kate Templeton, Harish Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human metapneumovirus (hMPV) are commonly associated with respiratory tract infections (RTIs) in humans.MethodsUsing national hospital and laboratory data over six seasons (2017–2023), we characterised the epidemiology of RSV and hMPV-associated RTI hospitalisations in Scotland across all age groups. We examined age distribution and estimated annual incidence rate ratios comparing RSV and hMPV hospital incidence in different age groups. Clinical severity was assessed using ICD-10 codes, length of hospital stay, ICU admissions and in-hospital case fatality. Seasonality was evaluated over the study period.ResultsThis study included 13,807 RSV- and 2491 hMPV-associated RTI admissions. RSV-associated hospitalisations were generally higher than those for hMPV before RSV vaccine introduction, with the greatest differences observed in infants. Severity appeared to increase with age for both viruses and was highest in older adults, with broadly comparable outcomes between the two viruses. Before the coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) pandemic, RSV season generally started earlier and lasted shorter than the hMPV season. Both viruses demonstrated marked disruption of seasonal circulation during 2020/21, followed by a partial re-establishment of the winter peak.ConclusionshMPV and RSV both contributed substantially to RTI-related hospitalisations in Scotland before RSV vaccine introduction. RSV accounted for a greater number of admissions, while hMPV demonstrated comparable disease severity. Children bore the greatest incidence burden, whereas older adults experienced disproportionately severe outcomes. The findings suggest that temporal patterns in RSV and hMPV circulation may have important implications for respiratory virus surveillance and seasonal healthcare preparedness. Enhanced virus-specific testing and targeted immunisation in infants and older adults could improve surveillance and reduce hospital admissions and healthcare pressures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01402-8.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human metapneumovirus (hMPV), both members of the Pneumoviridae family, are closely related viruses. Both are commonly associated with human respiratory tract infections (RTIs) and contribute substantially to hospitalisations worldwide [1, 2]. RSV is commonly associated with severe disease in infants and young children and is increasingly recognised as an important pathogen in older adults and individuals with underlying conditions [3]. Since its identification in 2001, hMPV has also been associated with a similar spectrum of illness and most frequently affects young children, older adults and immunocompromised populations [4].

RSV vaccines for pregnant women and older adults (aged 75–79 years) were introduced in Scotland in September 2024 [5]. There is interest in whether RSV immunisation programmes may indirectly influence hMPV epidemiology through changes in RSV transmission dynamics and potential mechanisms of viral interference, although empirical evidence remains limited [6, 7]. Understanding the similarities and differences between RSV and hMPV epidemiology before RSV vaccine introduction can help assess the potential impact of RSV-targeted interventions on hMPV, guide clinical testing, and also provide evidence for the future evaluation of combination vaccines targeting both viruses [8]. Population-based analyses across all ages that directly compare RSV and hMPV within the same dataset can clarify their relative epidemiological impact and inform surveillance and immunisation policy.

In this study, we compared laboratory-confirmed RSV- and hMPV-associated hospitalisations across all age groups over six seasons at the national level in Scotland, examining age distribution, clinical presentation, severity, and seasonal patterns. By analysing both viruses within the same healthcare system and time period, we aimed to provide a comprehensive assessment of their epidemiological characteristics and relative burden on the healthcare system.

Methods

We utilised routinely collected data from Scottish national hospital registries for the period from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2023, for this retrospective analysis. We defined the annual respiratory season as the period between 1 July of one year and 30 June of the following year, to capture all hospitalisations during the winter period.

Hospitalisation data were obtained from the Scottish Morbidity Records 01 (SMR01), an episode-based patient record covering all in-patient and day-case episodes from non-obstetric and non-psychiatric specialities in Scotland [9]. We included all SMR01 records with at least one RTI-related ICD-10 diagnostic code (primary or secondary; up to six codes were available for each episode). The list of RTI ICD-10 codes is provided in Supplementary Material S1 [10]. Routine admissions were identified using the ‘type of admission’ variable, and admissions recorded as ‘routine’ were excluded because these represent scheduled admissions unlikely to be related to an acute RTI episode. We excluded day cases because these involve planned clinical care with clinician review and supervised recovery at the treatment facility without an overnight hospital stay. Inpatient admissions lasting <2 days (not involving an overnight stay) were, however, retained, as these may reflect short acute hospitalisations.

Laboratory data for hMPV and RSV were obtained from the Electronic Communication of Surveillance Scotland (ECOSS), a Public Health Scotland system that collects patient-level laboratory results from all National Health Service (NHS) laboratories in Scotland [11]. Only positive test results were available; data on negative tests or the total number of tests performed were not available. Testing was conducted as part of routine clinical care, and policies varied across health boards. In Lothian, respiratory virus testing using the LUMINEX Respiratory Virus Panel (including hMPV and RSV) was recommended for all RTI hospital admissions. In contrast, testing practices in other health boards were unclear. Laboratory-confirmed hMPV or RSV hospital episodes were defined as RTI hospitalisations with a positive test from a specimen obtained within 7 days before and up to 3 days following admission [12]. Co-infections with other respiratory viruses were not excluded but were not analysed separately. A 28-day washout period was applied at the patient level to laboratory tests so that multiple positive tests within 28 days were considered a single positive test result.

Length of stay (LOS) was defined as the difference between discharge and admission dates, and admissions with same-day discharge were assigned a LOS of 1 day.

Intensive care unit (ICU) or high-dependency unit (HDU) admission data were obtained from the Scottish Intensive Care Society Audit Group (SICSAG) [13]. ICU admission was defined as admission to either ICU or HDU within RSV or hMPV RTI hospital admission episodes. ICU admission rate was defined as the proportion of RSV or hMPV RTI admissions requiring ICU (or HDU) admission and was expressed as a percentage. The mortality data were extracted from the National Records of Scotland (NRS) [14]. The in-hospital case fatality rate (CFR) was defined as the proportion of in-hospital deaths among all RSV or hMPV RTI admissions.

Data from SMR01, ECOSS, SICSAG and NRS mortality records were linked using a pseudonymised unique patient identifier available across all datasets. Linkage was performed by the study team within the national Trusted Research Environment (TRE). Previous evaluations of Scottish national data linkage systems have demonstrated high linkage quality, with strong agreement between routinely linked datasets, suggesting minimal linkage error [15]. The data linking procedures for linking all datasets are illustrated in Supplementary Material S2.

Population data for the estimation of incidence rate ratios were obtained from the open-access NRS [16, 17]. For each season, we used the mid-year population estimates corresponding to the end of that season.

Ethical Approval

The Public Benefit and Privacy Panel for Health and Social Care (PBPP-HSC) approved data access to the required data in the National Safe Haven (Approval number: 2223-0019).

Patient Consent

This research examined pseudonymised data that did not require patients’ active consent. The data were collected routinely through NHS Scotland, with patients informed of potential use and their rights through various PHS and NHS Scotland privacy notices.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio (4.2.1). All analyses were conducted at hospital admission (episode) level. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) comparing RSV and hMPV were estimated using Poisson regression with a log link and population offset, stratified by age group and annual respiratory season. IRRs were obtained by exponentiating virus coefficients, with 95% confidence intervals calculated using profile-likelihood methods to account for sparse data. Strata with zero laboratory-confirmed RSV or hMPV RTI hospital admissions were excluded, as IRRs could not be estimated in such cases.

To account for potential undertesting outside Lothian, analyses were repeated using extrapolated counts. Age- and month-specific virus positivity proportions among RTI admissions in Lothian were applied to RTI admissions in other health boards to estimate virus counts, which were then combined with observed Lothian counts to generate national estimates. These age- and month-specific virus positivity proportions among RTI admissions in Lothian are provided in Supplementary Material S3. The extrapolation procedure is described in detail in Supplementary Materials S4 and S5. IRRs were recalculated using the same modelling approach mentioned above.

To provide context for the interpretation of regional estimates, overall population characteristics for Lothian and Scotland were described separately and are presented in Supplementary Material S6. In addition, as a sensitivity analysis, laboratory-confirmed IRR estimates were reported separately for Lothian.

We also compared laboratory-confirmed RSV- and hMPV-associated RTI hospitalisations by clinical presentation and disease severity. Clinical presentation was described using RTI-related diagnostic codes, while severity was assessed using LOS, ICU admissions and in-hospital mortality.

First, we calculated the proportion of laboratory-confirmed RSV and hMPV hospital admissions across all ages with primary or secondary diagnostic codes for bronchitis/bronchiolitis, pneumonia, unspecified lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI), upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) and pathogen-specific diagnoses to describe the common clinical presentations of each virus (Supplementary Material S1).

Secondly, LOS was summarised descriptively for each virus by age groups. For each group, we reported the mean LOS with standard deviation and the median LOS with interquartile range (IQR). Because LOS distributions were typically right-skewed, both the mean and median LOS were reported. To further describe differences in prolonged hospitalisation between the two viruses, we also calculated the proportion of admissions with prolonged LOS within each age group. Prolonged LOS was defined as >5 days [18].

Thirdly, we estimated the proportion of laboratory-confirmed RSV and hMPV hospital admissions requiring ICU care (including HDU) and resulting in in-hospital death (CFR), expressed as percentages with 95% CI derived using non-parametric bootstrap resampling.

Owing to the rarity of these events, hospitalisations were aggregated across seasons, and broader age groups were used to improve estimate robustness for the severity analyses: young children (<5 years), older children and adolescents (5–17 years), young to middle-aged adults (18–59 years) and older adults (≥ 60 years). Owing to the small number of events (fewer than five in several groups) and variable testing policies across health boards, formal statistical comparisons between viruses were not performed for severity outcomes to avoid potentially misleading results. Instead, these outcomes were presented descriptively, using percentages with 95% CI to illustrate the observed burden.

We assessed temporal trends in monthly laboratory-confirmed hospitalisation episodes of RSV and hMPV. Seasonality was evaluated descriptively using time series plots of monthly case counts.

Seasons for each virus were defined using the Annual Average Percentage (AAP) method, which identifies the smallest set of months accounting for 75% of total seasonal cases [19]. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material S7. AAP analyses were additionally stratified by age group (<5 years and ≥ 60 years) as the highest overall burden (hMPV or RSV) was observed in these groups during the study period.

Except for the 2020/21 annual respiratory season, IRRs based on extrapolated viral counts were consistently >1 and statistically significant in the under-5-year population, indicating a higher observed burden of RSV-associated hospitalisations compared with hMPV in these age groups. A similar pattern was observed among older children (5–17 years), although IRR estimates were much closer to 1 and were statistically insignificant in the 2017/18 annual respiratory season. Among younger adults (18–49 years) and middle-aged adults (50–59 years), IRR estimates continued to approach 1, with some seasonal estimates slightly above or below 1 and occasionally not statistically significant. In adults aged 60–74 years, IRRs generally remained close to 1, occasionally <1 in certain annual respiratory seasons. However, among adults aged ≥ 75 years, IRRs were consistently >1 and statistically significant in all seasons except 2020/21, suggesting a greater contribution of RSV than hMPV to hospitalisations in the oldest age group.

The 2020/21 annual respiratory season displayed atypical patterns. No laboratory-confirmed RSV-associated RTI admissions were observed in the 18–49 years or 50–59 years in Scotland. As a result, IRR could not be estimated for these groups during this annual respiratory season. IRRs were <1 in all other age groups, indicating a higher hMPV incidence than RSV, with statistically significant differences observed in infants aged 6 months–1 year and adults aged ≥ 75 years.

Across all annual respiratory seasons except 2020/21, IRR estimates were most precise in age groups with higher hospitalisation counts of hMPV and RSV, particularly at the extremes of age. Conversely, lower incidence estimates in some strata, especially among younger and middle-aged adults, were associated with wider confidence intervals.

We observed that RSV-associated RTI admissions were more often characterised by RSV-specific ICD-10 codes and/or bronchiolitis/bronchitis, with pneumonia being relatively uncommon (Table 2). In contrast, hMPV infections were associated with a broad spectrum of respiratory illnesses, including pneumonia, bronchiolitis/bronchitis and unspecified LRTIs. An additional observation was the difference in pathogen-specific coding. RSV admissions more frequently included RSV-specific diagnostic codes (64%), whereas only 25% of hMPV admissions had hMPV-specific codes. Miscoding of pathogens was evident for both viruses but appeared more pronounced for hMPV (3% hMPV episodes had RSV-specific codes). The median LOS seemed similar for RSV and hMPV across all age groups and was highest in the older adult group for both viruses. (Supplementary Material S12). Similarly, the proportion of admissions with an LOS exceeding 5 days was highest among older adults for both viruses (Supplementary Material S13). Although LOS generally increased with age for RSV, hMPV showed a different pattern, with LOS increasing in older children and adolescents, declining in young- to middle-aged adults, and rising again among older adults. ICU admission rates and in-hospital CFRs for laboratory-confirmed RSV and laboratory-confirmed hMPV RTI hospital admissions are presented in Supplementary Materials S14 and S15. For ICU admission rates, the point estimates ranged from 0.12% to 13.68% for RSV and 0.34% to 19.66% for hMPV. Both viruses exhibited similar age-related patterns, with ICU admission rates increasing through the younger and middle-aged adult groups before declining in older adults. Regarding in-hospital case fatality rates (CFR), point estimates ranged from 0.01% to 2.82% for RSV and from 0% to 3.19% for hMPV. Similar to ICU admissions, both viruses followed comparable age-related trends; however, the CFR continued to increase progressively across all age groups, including older adults.

Across the AAP analyses, distinct seasonal and temporal differences were observed between RSV and hMPV during the pre-COVID-19 pandemic period (2017/18–2019/20). RSV seasons generally began earlier and had shorter durations, typically lasting approximately 3 months. In contrast, hMPV showed a later onset, most commonly in December, with peak activity in January and a longer season duration exceeding 4 months. These patterns indicate more prolonged seasonal circulation of hMPV compared with the more temporally concentrated RSV seasons.

Age-stratified analyses demonstrated a consistent age-related shift in RSV seasonality, with earlier onset and peak timing in children under 5 years of age compared with adults aged ≥ 60 years across both pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic periods. In contrast, hMPV showed a weaker and less consistent age gradient, with earlier onset in children observed in some seasons but no stable directional pattern across the study period.

The 2020/21 season showed marked disruption to the established seasonal patterns of both viruses compared with pre-pandemic seasons. hMPV circulation occurred earlier and persisted for longer than typically observed, while RSV activity was substantially delayed and extended, particularly in children, with peak activity occurring unusually late in June 2021.

In the subsequent 2021/22 and 2022/23 seasons, both viruses demonstrated a partial return towards typical winter-seasonal circulation patterns. However, variability in onset, peak timing and duration persisted between viruses and across age groups.

The results of these AAP analyses are provided in Supplementary Materials S16 and S17.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the epidemiology of RSV and hMPV hospitalisation concurrently across all age groups over multiple seasons in Scotland using real-world routinely collected data before the introduction of RSV vaccines. The findings demonstrate that laboratory-confirmed RSV hospital admissions concentrated in early infancy, and hMPV showed a broader age distribution. Despite these differences in incidence, both viruses exhibited consistent age-related increases in severity, with the highest burden observed in older adults.

This study shows that RSV contributed more substantially to hospitalisations than hMPV in infants and young children, with RSV peaking in the youngest infants (0–5 months) and hMPV peaking slightly later (6 months–1 year) during the study period in Scotland. This age pattern in children observed in this study is broadly consistent with the pattern from a USA-based study [6]. Our analysis further shows that in older children and adults <75 years, the relative observed burden of RSV and hMPV was more balanced, consistent with findings in the literature [20]. Among adults aged ≥ 75 years, RSV again appeared to drive a higher proportion of hospitalisations. Although RSV contributed more hospitalisations overall, the relative balance between RSV and hMPV in older children and adults under 75 years suggests overlapping epidemiological patterns in these groups.

Sensitivity analyses comparing Lothian-specific and Scotland-wide laboratory-confirmed estimates demonstrated broadly consistent age-specific patterns, but with higher incidence rate ratios observed in Scotland overall, particularly in younger age groups. This indicates some degree of heterogeneity in the magnitude of laboratory-confirmed estimates across health boards when compared with Lothian as a reference setting with more complete testing. When these estimates were compared with those derived from extrapolated counts, IRRs were attenuated and more closely aligned with Lothian-specific estimates. However, extrapolation did not substantially alter the overall age-specific or seasonal patterns especially in the pre-COVID-19 pandemic seasons. The consistency of these patterns across analytic approaches suggests stable underlying epidemiological signals despite variation in absolute magnitudes and incomplete testing. The disruption of respiratory virus circulation during the COVID-19 pandemic further complicated the interpretation of these patterns and likely influenced both healthcare utilisation and testing behaviour, particularly during the 2020/21 season.

Clinical presentation also differed between viruses, with hMPV associated with a broader spectrum of ICD-coded respiratory disease, including a higher proportion of pneumonia diagnoses, whereas RSV was more frequently coded as bronchiolitis and lower respiratory tract disease in infants. This pattern is consistent with previous studies showing higher proportions of pneumonia-coded hospitalisations in hMPV compared with RSV in both children and adults [6, 21]. However, the possible influence of differential testing practices on these findings cannot be ruled out. Despite these differences, the overall clinical syndromes overlapped substantially, reinforcing the limited reliability of syndromic diagnosis and the importance of virus-specific testing [22].

A higher proportion of hMPV admissions were miscoded as RSV (3%), whereas the reverse occurred in fewer than 1% of cases, suggesting differential misclassification when analyses rely solely on clinically coded data. The observed differences in pathogen-specific coding further suggest that reliance on routine ICD coding alone is likely to underestimate hMPV burden to a greater extent than RSV, likely reflecting differences in clinician awareness and testing practices.

Although median LOS was similar for both viruses, hMPV showed a tendency towards longer hospitalisations in selected age groups, particularly older children, adolescents, and older adults. ICU admission increased in younger and middle-aged adults but declined in older adults, likely reflecting clinical decisions to limit invasive interventions in very old patients due to frailty, comorbidities and lower expected overall benefit [23]. In contrast, in-hospital CFR was highest among older adults, reinforcing that the oldest experience the greatest severity. Across nearly all age groups (except 5–17 years), point estimates for ICU admissions (including HDU) and in-hospital CFRs were higher among hMPV than RSV. However, the severity estimates were based on small numbers in several strata, and confidence intervals frequently overlapped, with no formal statistical comparisons undertaken given the variation in and unclear testing policies.

The seasonal patterns of RSV and hMPV in our data align with reports from temperate settings, where RSV typically peaks earlier in winter, and hMPV circulates later, generally following RSV peaks [24]. The disruption observed during 2020/21 and early 2021/22 likely reflects the impact of COVID-19-related public health measures. The pattern of RSV season peaks occurring earlier in children than in adults in this study is broadly consistent with previous studies [25, 26]. In contrast, hMPV showed no consistent age-related pattern in our data, and there remains limited evidence in the literature supporting a stable age-gradient for this virus.

We acknowledge some limitations of this study. We extrapolated where possible to account for incomplete and variable testing across health boards. Lothian was selected as the reference for extrapolation as it had the most consistent and near-complete implementation of multiplex respiratory virus testing among hospitalised RTI admissions throughout the study period. However, differences in population structure, healthcare access, and localised outbreaks may have influenced RTI ascertainment, and residual bias in extrapolated estimates cannot be excluded, as described in previous analyses [12]. In addition, RSV may have been more consistently tested than hMPV, particularly in infants, while hMPV testing may have been more selective and focused on more severe or clinically complex cases in health boards other than Lothian. This may have introduced ascertainment bias, leading to under-ascertainment of hMPV burden. Regarding severity outcomes, selective testing of more severe hMPV cases may have inflated severity estimates. In contrast, incomplete testing may also have led to under-ascertainment of outcomes, making the overall direction of bias uncertain.

We did not examine underlying comorbidities in this analysis, which is an important limitation because comorbidities may influence both testing practices and risk of severe outcomes, including prolonged hospitalisation, ICU admission, and mortality. Previous studies have shown that comorbidity status is a key determinant of severe RSV outcomes, suggesting that risk stratification by comorbidity may be more informative than age alone for preventive strategies [27]. The impact of co-infections with other viral pathogens could not be assessed due to the lack of data on testing strategies and small numbers within each group.

Nevertheless, the age-specific patterns in burden and severity observed remain informative for public health planning. By describing hospitalisations, ICU admissions and CFR across age groups before RSV vaccination introduction, this study provides a baseline against which future immunisation impact can be evaluated and helps identify populations most likely to benefit from targeted prevention strategies.

Conclusions

RSV and hMPV both contributed substantially to hospitalisations for respiratory tract infections in Scotland before the introduction of RSV vaccination programmes. Children, particularly infants, appeared to drive the incidence burden, whereas older adults experienced a disproportionate share of morbidity and severe outcomes for both viruses. However, differences in testing intensity, diagnostic coding practices and potential under-ascertainment, particularly for hMPV, mean that these findings should be interpreted as reflecting laboratory-detected disease rather than true disease burden. Enhanced virus-specific testing would improve ascertainment and the reliability of routinely collected clinical and laboratory data.
