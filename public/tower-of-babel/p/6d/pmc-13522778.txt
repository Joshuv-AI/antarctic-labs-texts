Efficacy and biomarker exploration of anlotinib plus penpulimab in second-line ES-SCLC: Results from the phase 2 ALTER-L041 trial
Authors: Liang Zeng, Fang Tian, Cunji Wang, Qun Chen, Wenjuan Jiang, Juan Liang, Yanming Tan, Huan Yan, Jiao Huang, Chunhua Zhou, Haiyan Yang, Zhaohui Ruan, Zhe Huang, Jiacheng Dai, Yuanqing Feng, Yi Xiong, Li Liu, Chengzhi Zhou, Qinqin Xu, Yue Xie, Chao Zhang, Haoran Guo, Bing Zhang, Zhijun Wu, Nong Yang, Yongchang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTreatment options are limited for patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) after platinum-based therapy. This open-label, single-arm, multicenter phase 2 trial evaluates penpulimab plus anlotinib in 65 patients with first-line platinum-resistant ES-SCLC. The confirmed objective response rate by blinded independent central review is 41.5% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 29.2%–53.8%), and the disease control rate is 75.4% (95% CI: 64.6%–86.1%). Median progression-free survival and overall survival are 4.4 (95% CI: 3.5–5.3) and 10.5 (95% CI: 4.1–17.2) months, respectively. Grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events occurred in 23.1% of patients, with no new safety signals. Exploratory circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis suggests that lower baseline maximum somatic allele frequency is associated with improved survival, and decreased T cell receptor clonal diversity is observed at disease progression. Penpulimab plus anlotinib shows promising antitumor activity with a manageable safety profile and may represent a potential second-line treatment option for platinum-resistant ES-SCLC. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05001971).Keywords: small cell lung cancer, PD-1 inhibitor, penpulimab, anti-angiogenesis, anlotinib, combination therapy, biomarkers

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis. Approximately 80%–85% of patients are diagnosed at the extensive stage, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 7%.1,2,3 Therapeutic options remain limited after failure of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy, and effective second-line treatments are urgently needed. Before lurbinectedin became the preferred option for the second-line treatment of extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC), topotecan was the only approved therapy, with an objective response rate (ORR) of approximately 22%–24% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) of about 2–3 months.4 Although amrubicin and immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown some activity in relapsed SCLC, survival benefits remain limited.5,6,7,8,9 Therefore, combination strategies are needed.

Activation of angiogenic signaling fosters an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and targeting angiogenesis can restore or enhance antitumor immune responses. Accordingly, anti-angiogenic therapies, whether as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy or ICIs, have produced promising clinical results in several malignancies. Anlotinib, a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has demonstrated clinical benefit in relapsed SCLC and was approved in China for third-line treatment and beyond based on the ALTER-1202 trial.10,11,12,13,14 Penpulimab, an anti-PD-1 IgG1 antibody, has shown improved efficacy with a low incidence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs).15 The AK105-302 study demonstrated that penpulimab combined with paclitaxel and carboplatin provides high efficacy and a favorable safety profile in advanced squamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).16 Moreover, the combination of penpulimab and anlotinib has shown significant improvements in PFS and OS, with no new safety signals observed in numerous malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and gastric cancer.17,18,19,20

The rationale for combining PD-1 inhibition with antiangiogenic therapy is supported by their potential synergistic effects. Antiangiogenic agents like anlotinib may reverse the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) by normalizing tumor vasculature, enhancing T cell infiltration, and reducing the population of immunosuppressive cells.21 This reprogrammed TME could potentially sensitize tumors to the effects of PD-1 inhibitors like penpulimab. However, the application of this regimen in SCLC has not been extensively studied, and non-invasive biomarkers for selecting beneficiaries of PD-1/VEGF blockade in SCLC remain elusive.22,23 Emerging evidence indicates that plasma-derived circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) not only mirrors the genomic landscape of SCLC but also has been validated as a potential biomarker across multiple solid tumors through maximum somatic allele frequency (MSAF), blood-based tumor mutation burden (bTMB), and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) score.23,24 Meanwhile, peripheral T cell receptor (TCR) β-chain has emerged as a minimally invasive tool to track clonal evolution during checkpoint blockade.25 Whether these metrics predict sensitivity to combined immunotherapy and anti-angiogenesis in platinum-refractory ES-SCLC has never been prospectively tested.

Given the high unmet need in second-line ES-SCLC and the compelling scientific rationale for this combination, we conducted this multicenter phase 2 trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of anlotinib plus penpulimab as a second-line treatment for recurrent ES-SCLC. This study also aims to explore potential biomarkers that may predict treatment response, with the goal of guiding personalized therapy and providing insights into innovative treatment strategies for platinum-resistant ES-SCLC.

Results

Patients

Between January 11, 2021, and January 15, 2025, a total of 79 patients were screened for eligibility. Fourteen patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Sixty-five patients who had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy and no prior immunotherapy were enrolled (Figure 1). All enrolled patients received at least one dose of the study treatment. Baseline characteristics of the 65 patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 62 years (range: 57–71). The majority of patients were male (87.7%, 57/65), were younger than 65 years (53.8%, 35/65), had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 1 (81.5%, 53/65), and had a history of smoking (69.2%, 45/65). Brain metastases were present in 32.3% (21/65) of patients, liver metastases in 27.7% (18/65), and bone metastases in 15.4% (10/65). The interval since the last platinum-based chemotherapy was less than 6 months in 67.7% (44/65) of the patients.

Efficacy

As of the data cutoff date (April 15, 2025), the median duration of follow-up was 18.7 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 9.21–22.08). Among the 65 patients, the best overall response assessed by blinded independent central review was partial response (PR) in 27 patients (41.5%), stable disease (SD) in 22 (33.9%), and progressive disease (PD) in 16 (24.6%) (Table S1; Figures 2A and 2B). The confirmed ORR was 41.5% (95% CI: 29.2%–53.8%), and the disease control rate (DCR) was 75.4% (49/65; 95% CI: 64.6%–86.1%). Seven patients showed stable primary lesions but developed new liver (n = 5) or bone metastases (n = 2) (Figure 2A). Median PFS was 4.4 months (95% CI: 3.5–5.3), with a 6-month PFS rate of 39.1% (Figure 3A). Median OS was 10.5 months (95% CI: 4.1–17.2), with 12- and 18-month OS rates of 46.7% and 29.3%, respectively (Figure 3B).

Across the analyzed subgroups, the ORR was at least 11.9%, including those defined by age, gender, smoking history, ECOG PS, and the presence or absence of brain, bone, or liver metastases (Figure S1). In post hoc analyses, univariate Cox regression analyses were performed to explore potential factors associated with survival outcomes (Figures S2A and S2B). Compared with patients with liver metastasis, those without liver metastasis had a longer median PFS (6.2 vs. 1.6 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.10–0.55; p < 0.001; Figure S3A) and a longer median OS (14.3 vs. 6.4 months; HR = 0.40, 95%CI: 0.19–0.89; p = 0.004; Figure S3B). In addition, compared with patients with a shorter platinum-free interval (<6 months, n = 44), those with a longer platinum-free interval (≥6 months, n = 21) had a longer median PFS (7.1 vs. 3.2 months; HR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.33–0.94; p = 0.036; Figure S4A) and a numerically longer median OS (16.0 vs. 8.6 months; HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.29–1.00; p = 0.060; Figure S4B). In the univariable Cox regression analyses, no statistically significant associations with either PFS or OS were observed for age, gender, smoking history, brain metastasis, or bone metastasis.

Safety

The safety analysis included all 65 patients. Fifteen patients (15/65, 23.08%) experienced treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) of grade 3 or higher. The most common TRAEs were hypothyroidism (25/65, 38.5%) and increases in blood pressure (21/65, 32.3%) and serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (19/65, 29.2%). The most frequently reported grade 3 or higher TRAEs included increases in blood pressure (n = 3), ALT (n = 2), aspartate aminotransferase (n = 2), and blood glucose (n = 2); thrombocytopenia (n = 2); and immune-related pneumonia (n = 2) (Table 2). Seven patients withdrew from the study due to TRAEs, with one case each of rash, immune-related myositis, hand-foot syndrome, thrombocytopenia, hemoptysis, hyperbilirubinemia, and hyperglycemia.

Among irAEs, endocrine toxicities, including hypothyroidism (n = 25) and hyperthyroidism (n = 7), were predominantly grade 1–2 and were managed with hormone replacement or symptomatic therapy without requiring permanent treatment discontinuation. One patient with grade 3 hypothyroidism improved with L-thyroxine replacement therapy. Among the five patients with immune-related pneumonitis, three with grade 1–2 events were managed with temporary treatment interruption and achieved symptom resolution. Two patients with grade 3 immune-related pneumonitis, one patient with grade 3 immune-related myositis, and one patient with grade 4 rash improved after discontinuation of penpulimab and treatment with standard systemic corticosteroids. Overall, most irAEs resolved or were clinically controlled, and no fatal irAEs were observed during the study. The safety profile was consistent with the known toxicities of the individual agents.

Genomic landscape and correlation between mutation and survival at baseline

Among the 41 patients at baseline for next-generation sequencing analysis, gene mutations were detected in the majority of baseline samples (92%, 38/41). TP53 was the most commonly mutated gene, found in 82.9% (34/41) of patients. RB1 alterations were detected in 68.3% (28/41), followed by LRP1B (36.6%, 15/41), PTPRD (19.5%, 8/41), TP73 (17.1%, 7/41), and KMT2D (14.6%, 6/41). Mutations in KIT, KMT2C, and EP300 were detected in 12.2% (5/41) of patients. CREBBP, ERBB4, KMT2A, NOTCH1, TSHR, and GATA3 were mutated in 9.8% (4/41) of patients (Figure 4A).

No gene mutations were associated with best response (PR and SD/PD). Among the mutations in cell cycle genes, only PTPRT (2/41, 95% CI: 4.45–233.47; p < 0.001, adjust.p = 0.046) remained significantly associated with shorter PFS after false discovery rate correction (Figure S5A). Although EGFR, FGFR3, and RAD21 showed trends toward shorter PFS, and BRCA2 and FGFR3 showed trends toward shorter OS (Figure S5B), these associations were not statistically significant after correction. Mutual exclusivity of genetic mutations was dominant at baseline (Figure S5C). In pairwise co-alteration analysis, EP300-TP73 and LRP1B-MLL co-mutations (2/41, 4.9%) conferred the highest risk of disease progression (HR: 18.28, 95% CI: 3.26–102.40; p < 0.001, adjust.p = 0.035) (Figure S5D). Other co-alterations, including EGFR with TP53, LRP1B, or RB1; BRCA2 with RB1, TP53, or CREBBP; and ATRX/PTPRD co-mutation, were associated with inferior survival outcomes, but did not retain statistical significance after false discovery rate correction.

Baseline genomic biomarkers associated with response to anlotinib plus penpulimab therapy

The cohort had a median MSAF of 0.31 (range: 0–0.84) at baseline. Patients achieving PR (n = 16) had lower median MSAF than those with SD/PD (n = 25) (0.14 vs. 0.43, p = 0.067, Figure 4B). The median MSAF was used as a cutoff, classifying 21 patients as MSAF-high ([MSAF-H], MSAF ≥0.31) and 20 as MSAF-low ([MSAF-L], MSAF <0.31). The ORR was lower in the MSAF-H subgroup (23.8%, 5/21) than in the MSAF-L subgroup (55.0%, 11/20, p = 0.058, Figure 4C).

Spearman rank correlation analysis showed that continuous MSAF inversely correlated with both PFS (r = −0.41, p = 0.009, Figure 4D) and OS (r = −0.36, p = 0.019, Figure 4E). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed prognostic separation: MSAF-H patients experienced significantly shorter median PFS compared with MSAF-L patients (2.5 vs. 6.3 months, p = 0.001, Figure 4F). Similarly, median OS was significantly shorter in MSAF-H than in MSAF-L (7.7 vs. 14.5 months, p = 0.005, Figure 4G).

At baseline, the cohort had a median bTMB of 13.44 mutations per megabase (Muts/Mb) (range: 0–25.92 Muts/Mb). Patients achieving PR (n = 16) had numerically higher median bTMB than those with SD/PD (n = 25) (15.36 Muts/Mb vs. 13.44 Muts/Mb), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.78, Figure S6A). The median bTMB was used to stratify patients into high bTMB ([bTMB-H], bTMB ≥13.44 Muts/Mb) and low bTMB ([bTMB-L], bTMB <13.44 Muts/Mb) categories. Twenty-two patients were classified as bTMB-H and 19 patients as bTMB-L. The ORR was comparable between bTMB-H (59.1%, 13/22) and bTMB-L (60.0%, 12/20, p = 1.000, Figure S6B). Patients with bTMB-H experienced shorter median PFS and OS compared with those with bTMB-L, although this difference was not statistically significant (PFS = 3.1 vs. 4.8 months, p = 0.930, Figure S6C; OS 7.4 vs. 9.9 months, p = 0.270, Figure S6D).

The cohort had a baseline median HRD score of 6 (range 0–23). Median HRD score was comparable between patients with PR (n = 16) and patients with SD/PD (n = 25) (6 vs. 6; p = 0.370, Figure S6E). For subgroup analyses, patients were stratified based on the median HRD score as high (HRD-H; HRD ≥6) and low (HRD-L; HRD <6). Twenty-seven patients were classified as HRD-H and 14 as HRD-L. The distribution of patients with ORR was comparable in HRD-H (63%, 17/27) and HRD-L (57%, 8/14, p = 0.750, Figure S6F). Survival outcomes were comparable between patients with HRD-H and those with HRD-L (PFS = 4.4 vs. 4.8 months, p = 0.780, Figure S6G; 8.2 vs. 9.0 months, p = 0.350, Figure S6H).

Changes in TCR repertoire between baseline and after disease progression

To investigate immune escape mechanisms under VEGF plus PD-1 blockade, we performed β-repertoire of TCR sequencing on peripheral blood obtained at baseline (n = 43) and after disease progression with anlotinib plus penpulimab (n = 21).

The median raw sequencing depth (total productive T cell reads) increased from a median of 10,207,082 at baseline to 12,077,221 at disease progression (p = 0.62, Figure 5A). We performed rigorous computational rarefaction to downsample all repertoires to an equal depth, since clonotype counts are mathematically dependent on sequencing depth. After adjustment, the normalized richness declined from a median of 16,941 to 11,281 (p = 0.1, Figure 5B). Similarly, the normalized Shannon index (7.72 vs. 7.64, p = 0.43, Figure 5C) and evenness (0.82 vs. 0.82, Figure 5D) showed no significant changes. The representation of CD4+ T cells within the repertoire increased from 15.8% to 18.0% (p = 0.039, Figure 5E), while CD8+ clones remained stable (26.6%–26.7%, p = 0.890, Figure 5F), suggesting that VEGF-induced normalization of the TME preferentially recruits helper rather than cytotoxic T cell subsets during early immune escape.

To explore whether the baseline T cell repertoire architecture predicts response to anlotinib plus penpulimab, we compared the baseline TCR metrics between responders (PR, n = 17) and non-responders (SD/PD, n = 26). The total productive T cell count was numerically lower for non-responders than responders (9,778,213 vs. 17,371,464, p = 0.62, Figure S7A). The global baseline diversity metrics, including richness (14,973 vs. 17,857, p = 0.37, Figure S7B), Shannon index (7.6 vs. 8.1, p = 0.63, Figure S7C), and evenness (0.83 vs. 0.82, p = 0.93, Figure S7D), did not distinguish responders from non-responders. The CD4+ T cell fraction within the repertoire was 16% (SD/PD) and 17% (PR) (p = 0.21, Figure S7E); the corresponding CD8+ fractions were comparable (27%, p = 0.53, Figure S7F). No significant imbalance in helper vs. cytotoxic representation was observed. Collectively, these data indicate that static peripheral TCR metrics at baseline lack predictive value for subsequent radiological benefit, reinforcing the need for dynamic on-treatment sampling to capture immunological correlates of treatment response.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this multicenter phase 2 trial is the largest study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a PD-1 inhibitor combined with an anti-angiogenic agent as a second-line treatment for ES-SCLC. The study provides evidence supporting the efficacy and safety profile of the immunotherapy and antiangiogenesis combination, suggesting that this strategy may be a promising option for second-line treatment of ES-SCLC.

In this study, the median follow-up was 18.7 months. The ORR was 41.5%, with a DCR of 75.4%, a median PFS of 4.4 months, and a median OS of 10.5 months. Overall, adverse events were manageable. Collectively, these results suggest that this combination regimen may confer survival benefits comparable to those of existing second-line treatment options for ES-SCLC. Notably, we observed seven patients with stable primary lesions who subsequently developed new liver metastases (n = 5) or bone metastases (n = 2). This mixed response pattern, in which pre-existing disease remains controlled while new metastatic lesions emerge, may reflect intratumoral heterogeneity or distinct biological behaviors of metastatic clones and warrants further investigation in future studies incorporating spatial multi-omics analyses.

Topotecan, long regarded as a standard second-line option, is associated with an ORR of 21% and significant hematologic toxicity, including grade 4 neutropenia in 64% of patients, as well as high rates of anemia and thrombocytopenia.26 Another trial comparing berzosertib plus topotecan against topotecan alone for relapsed SCLC reported a median PFS of 3.9 vs. 3.0 months and a median OS of 8.9 vs. 5.4 months27 A phase 3 study of combined dinutuximab and irinotecan therapy found no improvement in survival outcomes or response rates compared with irinotecan or topotecan alone: median OS was 6.9 vs. 7.0 vs. 7.4 months (p = 0.313), median PFS was 3.5 vs. 3.0 vs. 3.4 months (p = 0.348), and ORR was 17.1% vs. 18.9% vs. 20.2% (p = 0.804).28 More recently, lurbinectedin was approved based on a phase 2 study demonstrating an ORR of 35.2% and a median OS of 9.3 months, with similar outcomes observed in a Chinese bridging study (ORR: 45.5%, median OS: 11.0 months).29 The LUPER study evaluating lurbinectedin combined with pembrolizumab reported an ORR of 46.4% and a median OS of 10.5 months, although 71.4% of patients experienced grade ≥3 TRAEs.30

Previous prospective phase 2 studies have suggested promising efficacy for immunotherapy combined with antiangiogenic therapy in the second-line or later setting. A study of sintilimab plus anlotinib (n = 42) reported a median PFS of 6.1 months, a median OS of 12.7 months, an ORR of 56.8%, a DCR of 89.2%, and 12- and 18-month OS rates of 55.4% and 39.4%, respectively.31 Another phase 2 trial investigating sintilimab plus anlotinib and nab-paclitaxel (n = 25) showed a median PFS of 6.0 months and a median OS of 13.4 months.32 Although these results are numerically better than our findings, they were derived from relatively small sample sizes. The PASSION study, a phase 2 trial of camrelizumab plus apatinib in the second-line setting, achieved an ORR of 34.0%, with median PFS and median OS of 3.6 months and 8.4 months, respectively.33 Additionally, an investigation of tarlatamab (an anti-DLL3 antibody) for relapsed SCLC reported an ORR of 23.4% and a median PFS of 3.7 months.34

Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent and stable antitumor activity of anlotinib plus penpulimab across all predefined subgroups, with most subgroups maintaining a high ORR, suggesting broad therapeutic potential across diverse baseline characteristics. For subgroups with a very small sample size (e.g., female patients, n = 8), we further provided gender-stratified survival analysis results, including median PFS, median overall survival (OS), and their corresponding 95% CIs for both male and female participants (Table S2). Although no statistically significant differences were observed, the ORR estimates in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution due to the wide confidence intervals.

In exploratory prognostic analyses, univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis showed that the absence of liver metastasis at baseline was associated with improved prognosis. This finding likely reflects the well-established adverse prognostic impact of liver metastasis. Compared with patients with a shorter platinum-free interval (<6 months), those with a longer interval (≥6 months) showed numerically longer median PFS and OS, suggesting that sensitivity to prior platinum-based therapy may still be associated with benefit from subsequent treatment. Given the single-arm design, definitive interaction effects cannot be established and should be validated in future randomized controlled trials. Moreover, because of the limited sample size and the small number of events in some subgroups, multivariable Cox models may be vulnerable to overfitting; therefore, only univariable results are reported, and these findings should be considered exploratory.

MSAF has been validated as a solid non-invasive predictive biomarker for immunotherapy in NSCLC, but has not been evaluated in SCLC.35 Previous research reported that TMB and HRD scores showed significant predictive value for camrelizumab plus apatinib and chemotherapy in first-line treatment in ES-SCLC.23 However, TMB is sensitive to sequencing depth, as it is estimated by calculating somatic mutations above a threshold frequency.36 Both TMB and HRD score may vary across panels due to differences in genomic regions covered by each assay. Furthermore, bTMB or HRD score inferred from ctDNA may not exhibit similar predictive functions compared with those calculated from tissue samples.23 Our results establish baseline MSAF as the strongest genomic predictor of PFS and OS, outperforming bTMB or HRD score. Mechanistically, low MSAF may limit neo-antigen drift and maintain tumor immunogenicity under PD-1/VEGF pressure.37 In our TCR sequencing analysis, we observed that despite an increase in raw total T cell reads at disease progression, the normalized clonal richness exhibited a decreasing trend. While detecting fewer clones in a larger pool of T cells suggests a biological contraction of the immune repertoire, this trend did not reach statistical significance after rigorous rarefaction (p > 0.05). Therefore, we cannot definitively conclude that oligoclonal dominance drives acquired resistance to PD-1/VEGF blockade in this cohort. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate whether this contraction of TCR diversity is a reliable pharmacodynamic surrogate for treatment failure. CD8+ clonotypes remained stable, while CD4+ representation slightly increased in patients with PD, suggesting selective enrichment of cytotoxic T cells with an exhausted phenotype. Insufficient CD8+ T cell infiltration is a key reason for the poor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.38 Notably, baseline TCR diversity did not predict response, underscoring the need for on-treatment kinetic monitoring rather than static snapshots.39 Given that both MSAF and TCR metrics can be obtained non-invasively, they are immediately translatable to adaptive trial designs. In summary, our findings suggest that baseline ctDNA and on-treatment TCR contraction may serve as non-invasive biomarkers that can inform adaptive randomized trials of PD-1/VEGF combinations in ES-SCLC.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-arm study lacking a randomized control group, and given its relatively small sample size, its findings require confirmation in larger phase 3 randomized controlled trials. Second, despite a moderate overall sample size, subgroup sizes were highly unbalanced, with only two to three patients in some co-mutation subgroups, thereby limiting the robustness of these analyses; therefore, these exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, this study had a notable gender imbalance (only eight female patients), which primarily reflects the higher epidemiological incidence of small cell lung cancer in men. Although we supplemented the analysis with gender-stratified analyses, the very small sample size of female patients resulted in wide confidence intervals for efficacy estimates, which may obscure true differences or yield spurious findings. Therefore, this study cannot draw reliable conclusions regarding treatment benefit in female patients, and the relevant results are reported only as exploratory findings. Third, biomarker analyses were performed in only a subset of patients. Some patients declined to provide additional samples for exploratory biomarker assessments; furthermore, a proportion of blood samples failed quality control due to insufficient volume, hemolysis, or missed collection time points, thereby precluding the generation of valid biomarker data. Consequently, the observed negative findings for biomarkers such as bTMB and HRD scores may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than a genuine lack of predictive value. These findings should be considered hypothesis generating and warrant further validation in larger, prospective studies. Moreover, given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the multiple hypotheses tested, there remains a potential risk of false-positive findings, despite the application of false discovery rate correction. Given the limited sample size for certain biomarker analyses, these exploratory results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, although platinum monotherapy remains used in real-world practice, the enrollment characteristics of this study limit the generalizability of the results to patients with relapsed ES-SCLC previously exposed to immunotherapy. Finally, biomarker-guided patient selection was not incorporated. Future randomized trials using biomarker-driven strategies are needed to confirm the efficacy signals observed here and to improve outcomes in this refractory disease.

In conclusion, this multicenter phase 2 trial demonstrates that the combination of a PD-1 inhibitor and an anti-angiogenic agent, penpulimab and anlotinib, is a viable second-line treatment option for patients with ES-SCLC. However, given the single-arm design, limited sample size, exploratory biomarker analyses in patient subsets, and the exclusive enrollment of immunotherapy-naive patients, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The promising efficacy and safety profile observed, along with the significant survival outcomes, supports further exploration of this treatment strategy in larger randomized controlled trials. Future studies are particularly needed in patients previously treated with first-line chemo-immunotherapy to better define the role of this regimen in contemporary clinical practice. Additionally, TCR repertoire dynamics may serve as potential biomarkers, highlighting the importance of personalized approaches in managing ES-SCLC.

Experimental model and study participant details

This single-arm, open-label, multicenter, phase II clinical trial was conducted at seven cancer centers across China. All patients provided written informed consent prior to any trial-related activities. The study was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each participating site before initiation and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hunan Cancer Hospital (Ethics Approval: 2020-IIT-079).

Patients

Inclusion criteria included: male or female patients aged 18–75 years; pathologically confirmed ES-SCLC according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALG) staging system; failure of prior first-line platinum-based chemotherapy; at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1); Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 0 or 1; previous receipt of at least one cycle of platinum-based chemotherapy; life expectancy of at least 3 months; and adequate organ function.

Exclusion criteria included: prior use of anlotinib hydrochloride or other antiangiogenic agents; previous immunotherapy; receipt of two or more lines of platinum-based doublet chemotherapy; radiographic evidence (either computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) of cavitary pulmonary lesions; or receipt of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other investigational anticancer therapies within 4 weeks before the first dose. Detailed criteria are provided in the study protocol.

Procedures

Eligible patients received anlotinib hydrochloride capsules (10 mg orally once daily, fasting before morning meals) on days 1–14 of each 21-day cycle, followed by a 7-day break. Penpulimab (200 mg) was administered intravenously on day 1 of each cycle. Patients continued treatment until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal from the study. Treatment was discontinued upon confirmation of progressive disease (PD) by blinded independent central review (BICR).

Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST v1.1 every two cycles (every 6 weeks) for the first 24 weeks, and every 12 weeks thereafter until PD. After treatment discontinuation, patients entered a follow-up phase and were contacted every 12 weeks via telephone (patient, family, or local physician) until death or loss to follow-up. Safety assessments were performed by monitoring adverse events (AEs) using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (NCI-CTCAE v5.0), beginning from the first dose until 30 days after treatment discontinuation. Safety data, including the treatment-related AEs (TRAEs), were summarized by grade of severity according to the protocol.

Method details

The primary endpoint was confirmed ORR, defined as the proportion of patients with confirmed CR or PR, as assessed by BICR per RECIST v1.1. Confirmation required repeat imaging at least 6 weeks after the initial response. Secondary endpoints included PFS, disease control rate (DCR), OS, and safety. PFS was defined as the duration from the first dose of study treatment to documented PD or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the duration from the first dose to death from any cause. DCR was defined as the proportion of subjects achieving the optimal overall efficacy was confirmed as CR or PR or SD (according to RECIST v1.1 criteria). Adverse events were graded according to the NCI-CTCAE v5.0.

Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS)

Peripheral blood was collected within 7 days prior to treatment initiation (baseline). For the progression-stage analysis, blood samples were collected within 7 days after the objective confirmation according to RECIST v1.1 of progressive disease (PD) and strictly before the initiation of any subsequent therapy. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantification was performed using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Libraries were prepared with 20–100 ng fragmented cfDNA (mean size 170 bp) using an in-house adapter-ligation protocol incorporating unique molecular identifiers. After polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based enrichment, libraries were hybrid-captured with a custom 1021-gene panel spanning 1.5 Mb (Table S3, GenePlus, Beijing, China). Captured libraries were sequenced on the Gene+Seq-2000 platform (GenePlus, Beijing, China) with 2 × 101 bp paired-end reads.43

Mutation calling

Raw reads were filtered with NCrealSeq v1.2.0 and NCfilter v2.0.0 to remove adapter contamination and low-quality bases (Q < 20). Clean reads were aligned to GRCh37 using BWA-MEM v0.7.15. Duplicate reads were marked with Picard v2.6.0. Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertion deletions (indels) were called using TNSCOPE v201808 and realDcaller v1.7.1. Hotspot variants were reviewed with NChot v2.7.2 and annotated with NCanno v1.1.3 against various databases, including gnomAD, COSMIC, ClinVar, and OncoKB. Only non-synonymous coding variants (±2 bp splice) with allele frequency ≥0.5% and molecular read count ≥3 were retained.

The bTMB was calculated as mutations per megabase of the genome covered by the targeted panel used (1.5 Mb).44 MSAF was defined as the highest mutant allele fraction among all qualified somatic substitutions.24,45 The HRD score was calculated by summing scores for each of the three features46,47: loss of heterozygosity (LOH),48 telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI),49 and large-scale state transitions (LST).50

TCRβ sequencing

High-throughput sequencing of complementary-determining region 3 (CDR3) of T cell receptor β chains was performed on the genomic DNA and amplified using multiplex PCR primers.51 The DNA library was sequenced on the Gene+Seq-2000 (GenePlus) with 2 × 101 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing data were processed and analyzed as follows: (1) sequencing reads of low quality or with missing primers for multiplex PCR were removed; (2) remaining high-quality paired-end reads were merged to obtain contigs using Pear; (3) merged reads were aligned to the V, D, and J germline sequences, and the CDR3 regions using MiXCR.52 To rigorously control for the confounding effect of variations in total sequencing depth on repertoire diversity, in silico rarefaction (downsampling) was performed. All baseline and progression TCR libraries were computationally downsampled to an equal number of total productive T cell reads using the vegan package in R. Normalized richness, Shannon index, and evenness were then recalculated based on these rarefied matrices.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This was a single-arm, single-stage phase II study using a Fleming single-stage design, with the sample size determined based on hypothesis testing for the primary endpoint, objective response rate (ORR). The sample size was estimated using the normal approximation method for a single proportion. Based on the historical ORR of 11.9% reported in the CheckMate032 trial, we hypothesized that combination therapy with anlotinib and penpulimab would achieve an ORR of 25% as the alternative hypothesis (null hypothesis ORR = 11.9%). Using a one-sample proportion test with a two-sided α of 0.05 and power (1–β) of 80%, the required sample size was calculated with PASS software (version 15.0) to be 59 evaluable patients. To account for a potential 20% dropout rate, the planned sample size was set at 73 patients. Ultimately, 65 patients were enrolled, constituting the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Although this number (n = 65) exceeded the minimum requirement of 59 and provided sufficient power for the primary endpoint analysis, it did not reach the initial target of 73 patients. This shortfall may marginally affect the precision of subgroup analyses but is unlikely to substantially impact the conclusions for primary efficacy, as the actual sample size met the pre-specified statistical power threshold.

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a nonnormal distribution as presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For independent group comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test) was used. For boxplots, the central horizontal line represents the median, the box limits represent the first and third quartiles (IQR), and the whiskers extend to 1.5× the IQR. Individual data points are overlaid as dots. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages). The Clopper–Pearson method was used to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for ORR and DCR, and ORR was compared with the historical control using an exact binomial test. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with exploratory subgroup comparisons performed using the log rank test. For PFS, patients without progression or death were censored at the date of their last tumor assessment; for OS, patients alive at the data cutoff (April 15, 2025) were censored at the date of last contact. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were <5.

As an exploratory post hoc analysis, univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to assess potential prognostic factors associated with survival outcomes. No formal futility stopping rules were prespecified. Patient safety was overseen throughout the study through protocol-defined clinical monitoring, regular safety assessments, and adverse event reporting procedures. Missing data were not imputed, and analyses were conducted using available cases. Spearman rank correlation was used to examine the correlation between continuous biomarkers (MSAF) and survival (PFS and OS). All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For the exploratory genomic biomarker analyses (e.g., correlations between specific gene mutations/co-mutations and survival outcomes), nominal p values from univariable models were further adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Statistical analyses were performed with R v4.3.1 and SPSS v27.0.

In all analyses, n refers to the number of patients unless otherwise specified. Exact values of n for each analysis, along with the specific statistical tests applied, are reported in the corresponding figure legends, figures, and results section.
