Patterns and perceived risks of personal auditory device use among medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Pranil Shah, Prashant Dave, Labhita Das, Utkarsh Shah, Niraj Bharadva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe World Health Organization estimates that 1.1 billion people are at risk of developing noise-induced hearing loss from the unsafe use of Personal Auditory Devices (PADs). The use of these devices has grown exponentially, placing young adults at high risk of recreational NIHL and other ear-related health problems. This study aimed to explore PAD usage patterns among medical students, identify perceived ear and hearing health problems, and analyse the association between usage patterns and these issues.Results92% of students reported using PADs. A majority used them daily, with an average duration of nearly five hours. Many participants reported listening at high volumes and perceived ear and hearing health problems as a result. Notably, almost half of the users felt unable to discontinue their PAD use despite acknowledging potential adverse effects. There was a significant association between the daily duration of PAD use and the perception of adverse ear and hearing health effects. The continuous, prolonged, and high-intensity use of PADs among medical students is a significant issue that could escalate into a public health concern regarding ear and hearing health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07541-4.Keywords: NIHL, Personal auditory devices, Hearing loss, Ear health, Medical students, Public health

Introduction

Personal auditory devices (PADs), including headphones and wireless earbuds have become integral to everyday life, serving as portable gateways for music, podcasts, and other media. While these devices offer convenience, they also carry significant public health concerns. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified unsafe listening as a major threat, estimating that over 1.1 billion adolescents and young adults are at risk of preventable noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) due to unsafe listening practices [1].

Listening safety can be evaluated in terms of volume intensity and duration of exposure. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) adapts occupational standards to recreational contexts, suggesting that daily exposure around 90 dB should not exceed 150 min [2]. To provide a more practical framework, Portnuff (2016) proposed the “80–90 rule” listening at ≤ 80% of maximum device volume for ≤ 90 min per day [3]. Despite such guidelines, evidence from multiple countries consistently shows widespread overuse and risky behaviour.

Epidemiological studies highlight that PAD use is nearly universal among young populations. For example, prevalence rates among university students reached 81.3% in India [4] and 93.8% in Nigeria [5], with many reporting daily use for 1–3 h. Beyond duration, listening volume is a critical risk factor. A New York study found that over one-third of adult PAD users routinely exceeded 75% of maximum volume [6]. Similarly, Sulaiman et al. (2014) in Malaysia objectively measured average listening levels at 83 dB, with some individuals reaching 96 dB, levels well above the safe threshold [7].

Importantly, listening environments strongly influence volume preferences. Hodgetts et al. (2007) demonstrated that individuals on public transit increased device intensity by 10–15 dB compared to quiet settings, a phenomenon linked to auditory masking [8]. Noise-cancelling headphones could mitigate this by reducing external noise, yet adoption rates remain relatively low [7]. Despite awareness campaigns, there is a persistent knowledge–behaviour gap. Studies have shown that young adults are conscious of the risks but still engage in unsafe practices [4]. This discrepancy is often explained by auditory optimistic bias, a psychological tendency to believe one is less vulnerable than peers [9]. Clinical consequences already appear among students and young adults: surveys from the USA found that 11.2% reported some degree of hearing loss and nearly 16% experienced tinnitus, an early warning sign of cochlear stress [10]. In addition to hearing risks, PAD use is associated with attentional hazards. Thompson et al. (2013) observed that pedestrians using earphones were significantly less likely to check for traffic before crossing, highlighting PADs as both a hearing health and safety concern [11].

The growing concern over PAD use behaviors, particularly among youth in low- and middle-income countries, underscores the need for closer examination. This study therefore seeks to assess the patterns of listening practices, levels of risk awareness, and their implications for public health interventions among medical students.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted between January and February 2023 among medical students enrolled in the MBBS program at a private university in Western India. The primary aim was to assess the patterns, prevalence, and health effects of Personal Auditory Device (PAD) use among medical students. The study population comprised of 600 students across four academic batches.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All students aged ≥ 18 years, irrespective of their PAD usage status, were eligible to participate. Students who declined to provide consent, or who reported having pre-existing diagnosed hearing impairment, chronic otologic disease, or psychiatric conditions affecting perception/response to the questionnaire, were excluded.

Pilot study and sample size determination

A pilot study was conducted in November 2022 with 102 students to test feasibility, refine the questionnaire, and assess reliability. The pilot indicated a PAD usage prevalence of 82%. Using this prevalence, the required sample size for the main study was calculated as 228, based on Cochran’s formula for cross-sectional prevalence studies, with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. After accounting for a 15% non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated to be 262. The pilot study data were excluded from the final analysis but used for tool validation and reliability testing.

Study instrument and reliability testing

The principal investigator, a faculty member within the university, conducted the data collection. Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of the university. The purpose of the research was explained to all participants, and it was made clear that their participation was voluntary and confidential. All participants provided valid written informed consent before the questionnaire was administered. Data was anonymized to protect participant privacy.

A pretested, self-designed questionnaire (appendix:1), distributed as a Google Form, was developed based on a review of existing literature. Its validity was ensured through consultation with subject experts. The questionnaire was structured to collect data on several key areas, Sociodemographic Details (Age, gender, and residence (hostel or home)), PAD Usage Patterns (Duration of use (in years), frequency of use (days per week), average daily duration, type of PAD, and purpose of use (e.g., leisure, study, driving)), Listening Habits (Self-perceived volume levels and frequency of receiving high-volume alerts from their devices), Perceived Health Issues (Experience with symptoms such as headaches, earaches, hearing difficulty, and tinnitus), Awareness and Attitudes (Beliefs about the side effects of PADs and their reversibility, and perceptions of safe listening durations).

Data were collected using a pre-tested, self-designed structured questionnaire developed after a comprehensive review of existing literature [1, 3, 4]. The tool included sections on sociodemographic details, PAD usage patterns (frequency, duration, device type, and fitting), listening habits (volume, environment), usage contexts (study, leisure, driving), hygiene practices (cleaning, sharing), awareness of risks, perceptions of safe usage, and self-reported adverse effects (e.g., headache, earache, tinnitus, insomnia).

Content validity was established through expert review by Public Health and Otorhinolaryngology specialists. Reliability testing using pilot data yielded an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.82, indicating good internal consistency. Sub-domain α values were: usage patterns/exposure duration (0.74), risk perception/awareness (0.79), and reported symptoms/adverse effects (0.81), all above the recommended threshold (≥ 0.70) [12, 13].

Sampling and data collection

A convenience sampling approach was employed to ensure representation across all academic years. From each of the four MBBS batches, 57 students were invited, leading to a total of 228 participants in the main study. Data collection was carried out in common university areas such as lecture halls, the library, and cafeteria. The principal investigator read each question and recorded responses in a Google Form in real time to minimize misinterpretation.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms into MS Excel for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated as means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., daily PAD usage duration vs. reported adverse effects) were assessed using the chi-square test. All tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Perceptions regarding safe use were varied and often misinformed (Table 5). About one-third (32.2%) believed that 1 h/day was safe, while another 31.6% considered 2 h/day acceptable. Although 67.8% acknowledged that PAD use could have adverse effects, only 52.6% believed these were reversible. Additionally, 50% of users reported that they could not stop or were unsure if they could, suggesting behavioural dependence. The majority of students reported PAD usage for leisure activities (music, videos, gaming, social media) (~ 80%). Academic reasons (listening to study material or lectures) were also common, reported by 64.2%.

Risk behaviors were frequent. 71% of students reported using their devices while walking, crossing the road, eating, or exercising—activities requiring situational awareness. Alarmingly, 22.6% admitted to using PADs while driving, a high-risk behavior.

There was little consensus on safe listening duration. While 33% considered 1 h/day as safe, 31% believed 2 h/day, and 13% perceived 3 h/day or more to be safe. A 13% of participants perceive 3 h per day of usage to be safe.

A mismatch was observed between device warnings (“Keep volume low”) and self-perception of listening volume. While a majority acknowledged receiving warnings, very few accepted that they regularly listened at a high volume—suggesting normalization and underrecognition of auditory risk.

Discussion

This study highlights the widespread use of personal auditory devices (PADs) among medical students, with 82.4% of respondents reporting regular use (Table 1). The prevalence observed in our sample is consistent with findings from both Indian and international contexts, where PAD usage among university students ranges from 81% to over 90% [14, 15]. More critically, the data reveal that this usage is not casual or occasional but rather a deeply ingrained daily habit. Nearly two-thirds of students (62.1%) reported using PADs every day, and almost half (46.1%) listened for an average of 1–3 h per day (Table 2). Furthermore, one-third (32.2%) admitted to continuous listening for 1–2 h in a single sitting. This sustained exposure is concerning in light of World Health Organization (WHO) estimates suggesting that individuals using PADs unsafely for five or more years are at significantly increased risk of developing noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) [1]. Alarmingly, a subset of respondents already reported listening durations (>3 h daily) that place them in a high-risk category.

Gender-based patterns of PAD use were evident, with male students more likely to engage in prolonged daily usage compared to females (Table 2), mirroring trends seen in prior studies [16]. However, our study did not find significant gender differences in volume preferences, diverging from earlier research [6]. This may reflect evolving cultural norms or population-specific behaviours.

A central finding of this study is the disconnect between risk awareness and behavioural change. While two-thirds (67.8%) of participants acknowledged the potential adverse effects of PADs (Table 5), and a majority reported experiencing symptoms such as headaches (45.5%), earaches (36.4%), or tinnitus (18.2%) with longer use durations (Table 3), few made meaningful changes to their listening patterns. This gap between knowledge and action is consistent with the concept of “auditory optimistic bias,” where individuals believe that the risks of loud listening apply more to others than themselves [17]. For example, one-third (32.2%) of respondents believed that daily exposure of one hour was safe, but another third (31.6%) considered two hours/day acceptable, and 13.2% perceived three or more hours/day as safe, despite expert recommendations like Portnuff’s “80–90 rule,” which suggests limiting listening to 80% of maximum volume for no more than 90 min per day [3].

Another troubling pattern is the normalization of high listening volumes. Despite frequent device-generated volume warnings, 58.6% of students admitted to “sometimes” listening loudly and 5.3% to “always” listening loudly (Table 5). This reflects perceptual adaptation, where sustained exposure recalibrates what listeners consider “loud,” potentially creating a dangerous feedback loop of increasing volume and accelerating cochlear damage. The widespread preference for in-ear and canal-type devices (71.7%) further compounds this risk, as these deliver sound directly to the tympanic membrane and increase exposure to harmful sound pressure levels [7].

Risk was also elevated by poor hygiene and unsafe behavioural practices. Only half (51.3%) reported regularly cleaning their devices, while one-third (32.9%) admitted to sharing them (Table 6), behaviours known to increase the risk of otitis externa and other infections [15, 18]. Furthermore, unsafe PAD use during high-risk activities was common: 72.3% while walking, 68.1% while exercising, 61.5% while crossing roads, and 23.7% while driving (Table 4). Such practices impair situational awareness by masking auditory cues like horns or sirens, reflecting inattentional blindness and posing serious public safety concerns [11].

The presence of self-reported symptoms such as headaches, earaches, and tinnitus (Table 3) aligns with Hoover and Krishnamurti’s findings in similar populations [10]. Although self-reported symptoms are not diagnostic, they are important early indicators of auditory stress and potential pathology. Associated complaints such as irritability or insomnia may also reflect tinnitus, a common early manifestation of auditory system stress and a precursor to long-term hearing loss [19].

Given the severity and complexity of the issue, a multi-pronged strategy is required. Educational institutions and public health bodies should develop targeted awareness campaigns that translate technical decibel thresholds into relatable terms—for example, comparing certain volumes to industrial noise sources. Campaigns should feature clear, actionable messages, such as the “80–90 rule” [3], and be supported by screening initiatives that provide baseline audiometric assessments for students. Furthermore, hearing conservation should be integrated into medical curricula to prepare future healthcare professionals to counsel patients on PAD-related risks.

On the policy front, government and regulatory agencies should implement the WHO and International Telecommunication Union (ITU) global standard for safe listening devices, which includes real-time monitoring of sound exposure and default volume limits [20]. Packaging for PADs should carry standardized labelling indicating maximum safe listening times at various volumes, similar to nutritional labels. National campaigns should also address risky behaviours like listening while driving or walking in traffic, leveraging social and mass media to engage young adults.

PAD manufacturers must also share responsibility. Devices should default to safe listening modes, requiring users to override settings with clear health warnings. Interfaces could include cumulative exposure indicators, akin to fitness trackers. Additionally, manufacturers should promote noise-cancelling technology more actively, as this allows users to listen at lower volumes in noisy environments.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies with larger, diverse populations to assess the long-term auditory impact of PAD use. Such studies should incorporate objective measures such as device-based dosimetry and extended audiometric testing to better establish dose–response relationships between sound exposure and hearing loss [21]. The widespread and often unsafe use of PADs among medical students represents a serious and preventable public health issue. The observed disconnect between awareness and action, normalization of risky behaviours, and early signs of auditory damage highlight the need for urgent and coordinated interventions across education, policy, and industry.

Limitations

There are certain limitations of this research that need to be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study conducted in a single private medical college, the findings may not be generalizable to all students in India or other populations. Medical students may also have a higher baseline knowledge of health risks, which could influence their responses, although our results suggest that such knowledge does not always translate into safe listening behaviour. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have underreported behaviours they perceived as undesirable, such as listening at high volumes or using PADs while driving. Third, the study did not employ objective measures, such as direct assessment of listening levels or audiometric testing, which would have provided stronger evidence of associations between PAD use and auditory health outcomes. Finally, participants were recruited using convenience sampling from common areas such as the library and cafeteria, which may have introduced selection bias. This could partially explain the high prevalence of PAD usage (92%) observed in our study, and therefore the findings may not be fully representative of the wider student population.

Conclusions

PAD use was found to be highly prevalent among medical students, with many engaging in prolonged listening and risky behaviours despite some awareness of potential health risks. Longer durations of use were significantly associated with adverse outcomes, including headache, earache, and tinnitus. These findings highlight the need for health education initiatives that not only raise awareness but also encourage behavioural change toward safer listening practices. Future research should incorporate objective auditory assessments, larger and more diverse populations, and longitudinal designs to better understand the long-term implications of PAD use and to inform evidence-based guidelines for safe listening among young adults.
