Association between LIV + 1 bone mineral density asymmetry and distal adding-on phenomenon in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis
Authors: Chaofan Han, Zihao Ding, Peng Yin, Yong Hai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDistal adding-on phenomenon remains a common complication following corrective surgery for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). This study aimed to characterize the spatial distribution of vertebral bone mineral density (BMD) across the scoliotic spine and to examine whether preoperative BMD asymmetry at the vertebra immediately distal to the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV + 1) may serve as a complementary biomechanical risk marker for postoperative distal adding-on within existing LIV-selection frameworks.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 90 patients with AIS who underwent posterior spinal fusion and had preoperative full-spine computed tomography scans. Patient-specific three-dimensional finite element analysis (3D-FEA) models were constructed to quantify bilateral vertebral BMD along simulated pedicle-screw trajectories. Patients were grouped by Cobb-angle strata and by postoperative adding-on status. ROC analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry. Multivariable and Firth penalized logistic regression analyses were performed to assess its association with distal adding-on.ResultsThe mean whole-spine BMD was 0.85 ± 0.14 g/cm2. No significant difference was observed between the overall concave and convex sides (p = 0.510), although region-specific asymmetry patterns were identified. Distal adding-on occurred in 15 patients (16.7%). Patients with adding-on showed greater LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry than those without adding-on (0.14 ± 0.06 vs. 0.11 ± 0.03 g/cm2, p = 0.006). LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.865, 95% CI 0.77–0.96), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.112 g/cm2. Based on the optimal cutoff, adding-on occurred in 44.8% of patients above this cutoff and 3.3% of patients at or below this cutoff (Fisher’s exact OR = 24.0, p < 0.001); the negative predictive value was 96.7%. The between-group effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 0.85; Hedges’ g = 0.85). In Firth penalized logistic regression, LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry remained associated with adding-on (OR = 1.338 per 0.01 g/cm2 increase, 95% CI 1.077–1.664, p = 0.009).ConclusionVertebral BMD in AIS exhibits a region-specific asymmetric distribution pattern. Increased preoperative LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry is associated with postoperative distal adding-on and may provide complementary, hypothesis-generating biomechanical information for risk stratification and distal fusion-level planning. These findings should be validated in larger cohorts before BMD asymmetry is used as a stand-alone determinant of LIV selection.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional spinal deformity characterized not only by abnormal curvature but also by substantial biomechanical remodeling of vertebral structures [1–3]. Chronic asymmetric loading along the scoliotic curve alters vertebral trabecular architecture and leads to regional variations in bone mineral density (BMD). These structural adaptations are clinically relevant because vertebral bone quality directly affects the mechanical competence of the spine and the stability of pedicle screw fixation during corrective surgery [4]. Understanding the spatial distribution of BMD within the scoliotic spine may therefore provide important insights into the biomechanical environment underlying deformity progression and surgical outcomes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with AIS often exhibit reduced overall BMD compared with healthy adolescents, and several investigations have reported associations between bone density and curve severity [5–7]. However, most prior work has relied on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) or Hounsfield unit–based measurements at limited vertebral levels, primarily within the lumbar spine. These approaches cannot adequately characterize the three-dimensional spatial distribution of bone density across the entire scoliotic spine, particularly within the thoracic region. As a result, how vertebral BMD varies along different curve segments and between the concave and convex sides of the deformity remains incompletely understood.

Beyond deformity progression, the mechanical properties of vertebrae adjacent to the fusion construct may also influence postoperative complications. One clinically important complication in AIS surgery is the distal adding-on phenomenon, defined as the postoperative extension of the curve beyond the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) [8]. Current LIV-selection strategies rely primarily on radiographic landmarks and alignment criteria, including the stable vertebra, the last touched vertebra, LIV or LIV + 1 deviation relative to the central sacral vertical line (CSVL), disc angle below the LIV, LIV tilt, and sagittal alignment [9]. These parameters are essential for surgical planning, but they primarily describe spinal geometry and do not directly capture the local biomechanical properties of the vertebra immediately distal to the fusion construct. The LIV + 1 vertebra represents the first mobile segment subjected to concentrated postoperative stress. If substantial structural asymmetry exists at this level, particularly in terms of BMD distribution, it may contribute to an imbalanced load-bearing environment that predisposes the distal junction to adding-on [10, 11].

Therefore, the present study aimed to integrate three-dimensional quantitative assessment of vertebral BMD with clinical outcome analysis in AIS. First, we sought to characterize the spatial distribution of vertebral BMD throughout the scoliotic spine using a CT-based three-dimensional finite element framework. Second, we investigated whether preoperative BMD asymmetry at the vertebra immediately distal to the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV + 1) is associated with postoperative distal adding-on. We hypothesized that increased LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry reflects an imbalanced biomechanical environment and may provide complementary information for identifying patients at increased risk of distal adding-on following posterior spinal fusion.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This retrospective cohort study included patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis who underwent posterior spinal fusion at Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital between 2020 and 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of AIS with a primary main thoracic curve (MTC); (2) availability of preoperative full-spine CT scans in DICOM format; (3) skeletal maturity or near maturity (Risser sign IV-V); (4) indication for posterior spinal fusion; (5) complete radiographic records; (6) high-quality CT data suitable for 3D modeling; and (7) absence of congenital vertebral anomalies or syndromic etiologies. The exclusion criteria were: (1) known metabolic bone disorders, including thyroid dysfunction; (2) syndromic scoliosis associated with abnormal bone metabolism, including neurofibromatosis-associated scoliosis; and (3) significant coronal or sagittal imbalance that would preclude standard LIV-selection assessment. A total of 90 patients were included in the final analysis. Because the cohort was restricted to Risser IV-V patients, the mean chronological age was higher than that typically expected in early-adolescent AIS cohorts; this near-mature population was considered appropriate for the present initial biomechanical analysis but limits generalizability to younger AIS patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical and radiographic assessment

Demographic data (age, sex, height, weight, and BMI) were collected. Preoperative standing full-spine radiographs were used to measure Cobb angle, thoracic kyphosis, and apical vertebral translation (AVT). AVT was defined as the horizontal distance from the center of the apical vertebra to the C7 plumb line (for proximal thoracic curves) or to the CSVL. Adding-on phenomenon was diagnosed at the 1-year follow-up if either of the following was present: (1) angulation > 5° at the disc space immediately below the LIV, or (2) lateral deviation > 5 mm of the LIV + 1 vertebra from the CSVL [12]. All patients underwent 64-slice spiral CT (1-mm slice thickness) for morphological assessment and DICOM file generation. For descriptive purposes, patients were stratified into operational Cobb-angle groups (40°–80°, 80°–120°, and > 120°); these strata were not intended to represent a formal Lenke-based mild, moderate, or severe classification. Traditional LIV-selection variables such as stable vertebra, last touched vertebra, LIV + 1-to-CSVL deviation, and disc angle below LIV were not prospectively recorded in a standardized manner for the full cohort, and therefore a formal incremental-prediction model comparing conventional LIV-selection variables with and without LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry could not be performed in this retrospective analysis.

3D finite element model reconstruction

DICOM files were imported into 3D Slicer (V5.10.0t, OPEN ACCESS) to generate initial surface models, which were then refined and exported as VTK files (Fig. 1). These were meshed into tetrahedral elements via ANSYS (V 15.0) to create 3D-FE models. Voxelwise HU values from CT were converted to BMD (g/cm2) through a three-step calibration process:1. ρ HA = 0.0026HU − 0.0829 (Converting HU values to hydroxyapatite density using a hydroxyapatite calibration model) 2. ρ asℎ = (ρ_HA + 0.09)/1.14 (Ashdensity defined as mass divided by the true bone-tissue volume) 3. ρ app = ρ asℎ/0.6 (apparent density expressed as wet mass divided by the total volume occupied by bone tissue) [13]. BMD values were assigned to each finite element using Bonemat (open source, V3.2) yielding patient-specific, BMD-embedded 3D-FE models (Fig. 2A). To evaluate measurement reproducibility, bone mineral density measurements were independently performed by two observers. Region of interest (ROI) definition and BMD measurement

The region of interest (ROI) was outlined on the selected cross-sectional plane to quantify local bone density. (Fig. 2B) First, a line of arbitrary length was drawn adjacent to the plane to create a scale bar for calibration. Next, two elliptical ROIs were manually placed on either side of the vertebral midline. The ellipses were oriented to the pedicle’s longitudinal axis and directed toward the anterior vertebral body, thereby approximating the intended screw trajectory and enabling assessment of cancellous bone density surrounding the pedicle screw. Ellipse size and area were determined using the scale bar; grayscale values corresponding to the within-ellipse color variations were then extracted and converted to bone density measurements. Finally, the mean bone density (g/cm2) within each ROI was computed as the total bone density within the ROI divided by the elliptical area.

The finite-element model was imported into the open-source visualization toolkit ParaView (v5.70-RC2) to perform selective planar rendering and virtual resection. (Fig. 3)The resection plane was specified as an oblique axial-pedicular plane that intersected the pedicle midpoint and was parallel to the superior endplate. This orientation was selected to establish a standardized reference for subsequent biomechanical evaluation of pedicle-screw purchase. Following the plane definition, the cross-sectional region was mapped according to local BMD using a grayscale window of 0–225 HU and a median threshold of 125 HU. The resulting surface was then exported in VTK format and transferred to MATLAB (MathWorks, R2017a) for final postprocessing. Whole spine bone mineral density measurement

BMD was measured at the apical vertebra (APEX) and averaged within the proximal thoracic curve (PTC), MTC, and thoracolumbar/lumbar curve (TLC). Concave and convex BMD values were compared within each curved region and across regions. The bone mineral density difference is the absolute value of the difference between the concave and convex sides. For the adding-on analysis, preoperative BMD values for the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) and distal to the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV + 1) were extracted according to the actual surgical constructs.

Statistical analysis

Primary statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Firth penalized logistic regression was conducted using the logistf package (version 1.26.0) in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to address potential small-sample bias associated with the low events-per-variable ratio. Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g were calculated using the effsize package (version 0.8.1) in R. Intraclass correlation coefficients were computed using SPSS. Continuous variables are presented as means ± SDs or medians (IQRs), and categorical variables are presented as n (%). Paired t tests were used to compare concave and convex BMD within regions. Independent t tests were used to compare BMD asymmetry between patients with and without adding-on. Pearson correlation was used to assess relationships between BMD asymmetry and clinical/radiographic parameters. ROC curves were used to evaluate the predictive performance of LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry for adding-on, with the Youden index used to determine the optimal cutoff. The AUC and 95% CIs were calculated using DeLong’s method. Patients were further stratified into high and low BMD-asymmetry groups according to the ROC-derived cutoff, and the incidence between groups was compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Cutoff-based diagnostic metrics were calculated after stratifying patients according to the ROC-derived cutoff. Standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d and Hedges’g), common-language effect size, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive and negative likelihood ratios were calculated to clarify the clinical meaning of the cutoff-based classification.

Because only 15 adding-on events were observed, conventional multivariable logistic regression was retained as an exploratory model for continuity with the original analysis, while the risk of overfitting was explicitly considered. As a sensitivity analysis, Firth penalized logistic regression was performed using the same covariates as the primary model (Cobb angle, AVT, BMI, and LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry) to reduce small-sample bias. Odds ratios (ORs) for LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry were expressed per 0.01 g/cm2 increase. All BMD measurements were assessed for intra- and interobserver reliability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% CIs.

The average thoracic kyphosis was 35.7° ± 21.8°, and the mean AVT was 62.9 ± 16.5 mm. Patients were stratified into three operational Cobb-angle groups (40°–80°, 80°–120°, and > 120°). Correlation analysis demonstrated that Cobb angle was moderately associated with BMD asymmetry both at the apical vertebra and across the main thoracic curve region (r = 0.505 and r = 0.632, respectively; both p < 0.01).

Cutoff-based risk stratification and small-event sensitivity analysis

The cutoff-based analysis suggested that LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry may be most useful as a binary risk-stratification marker rather than as an isolated continuous value. Patients with LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry above 0.112 g/cm2 had markedly higher adding-on incidence, whereas patients below the cutoff had a low observed risk and a high negative predictive value.

Firth penalized logistic regression

Because only 15 adding-on events were observed, we performed Firth penalized logistic regression as a small-event sensitivity analysis. In the Firth model including Cobb angle, AVT, BMI, and LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry, LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry remained significantly associated with distal adding-on (OR = 1.338 per 0.01 g/cm2 increase, 95% CI 1.077–1.664, p = 0.009) (Table 7). Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive analysis of vertebral BMD distribution in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis using a three-dimensional finite element-based approach. Three principal findings emerged. First, vertebral BMD demonstrated a region-specific mirror-pattern distribution along the scoliotic spine. Second, preoperative BMD asymmetry at the LIV + 1 vertebra was strongly associated with postoperative distal adding-on. Third, cutoff-based risk stratification, Firth penalized sensitivity analysis, and reliability testing supported the potential value and reproducibility of LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry as a complementary biomechanical risk marker. Together, these findings suggest that side-to-side bone-density imbalance may represent an important biomechanical feature of AIS and a potential marker of postoperative junctional vulnerability.

Bone mineral density in the scoliotic spine is strongly influenced by long-standing asymmetric mechanical loading. Previous studies have reported reduced overall bone density in AIS patients and demonstrated correlations between BMD and curve severity [14–16]. Consistent with these observations, the present study found a significant association between Cobb angle and the magnitude of bilateral BMD asymmetry at the apical vertebra and along the main curve. This pattern may reflect adaptive remodeling of vertebral trabecular bone under asymmetric compressive forces. Increased compressive loading on the concave side promotes trabecular densification, whereas the convex side experiences relatively reduced mechanical stimulation, leading to lower bone density [17, 18]. As curve magnitude increases, this imbalance in mechanical loading becomes more pronounced, resulting in greater concave–convex differences in vertebral BMD [19].

Beyond localized measurements, our three-dimensional analysis revealed a region-specific spatial distribution of vertebral BMD along the entire scoliotic spine. The concave side demonstrated a “low–high–low” pattern from the proximal thoracic to the thoracolumbar region, whereas the convex side exhibited the opposite “high–low–high” pattern. This mirror-symmetric distribution likely reflects the alternating mechanical environments created by the primary and compensatory curves. Importantly, these findings highlight that BMD variation in scoliosis is not uniform but rather follows predictable biomechanical patterns along the spinal axis. Mapping these spatial patterns may therefore provide new insights into vertebral load distribution and mechanical adaptation in AIS.

A key finding of the present study is the strong association between BMD asymmetry at the LIV + 1 vertebra and postoperative distal adding-on. The LIV + 1 segment represents the first unfused motion segment exposed to concentrated mechanical stresses after spinal instrumentation. When substantial differences in bone density exist between the concave and convex sides of this vertebra, the resulting asymmetry in structural stiffness and load-bearing capacity may disrupt coronal load transmission [20, 21]. Under postoperative mechanical loading, such an imbalance may contribute to asymmetric disc loading [11], altered load transfer, and progressive distal curve extension [22]. However, BMD asymmetry should not be interpreted as the sole causal driver of adding-on. It may also serve as a surrogate marker of long-standing asymmetric loading, curve severity, disc behavior, and broader paraspinal soft-tissue imbalance. Prior literature has reported asymmetry in paraspinal muscle morphology and composition in AIS, including multifidus-related changes; such muscular asymmetry may interact with vertebral remodeling and should be considered in future mechanistic studies [23–25].

These findings have important implications for surgical planning in AIS, but their clinical role should be framed cautiously. Current strategies for determining the LIV rely on radiographic landmarks such as the stable vertebra, last touched vertebra, LIV/LIV + 1 relationship to the CSVL, disc angle below the LIV, LIV tilt, and sagittal alignment [9, 26, 27]. These parameters remain central to LIV selection. The value of LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry is that it may provide complementary biomechanical information about the first unfused vertebra distal to the construct. In patients whose conventional radiographic parameters are borderline or discordant, high LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry may serve as a warning signal prompting closer review of distal alignment, disc wedging, curve flexibility, and the risk–benefit tradeoff of extending fusion. Because standardized traditional LIV-selection variables were not available across the full retrospective cohort, the present study cannot prove incremental predictive value beyond all established surgical criteria; this should be tested prospectively.

The clinical interpretation of the effect size also deserves caution. Although the adding-on group had statistically higher LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry than the non-adding-on group, the absolute difference in group means was modest. However, the standardized effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 0.85), and the ROC-derived cutoff of 0.112 g/cm2 appeared clinically useful as a binary risk-stratification threshold. Patients above this cutoff had substantially higher adding-on incidence (44.8% vs. 3.3%, OR = 24.0), whereas patients below the cutoff had a high negative predictive value (96.7%). Even when the cutoff is exceeded, BMD asymmetry should not automatically mandate a longer fusion. Rather, it may identify patients who deserve more careful evaluation of distal fusion level selection and closer postoperative surveillance.

The practical implementation of CT-based 3D-FEA in routine clinical workflows also requires consideration. The present approach provides detailed patient-specific information but requires CT data, segmentation, finite-element model construction, and postprocessing. These steps are more feasible in a research or complex-case planning setting than in routine screening. Future work should evaluate whether simplified Hounsfield-unit-based measurements, automated segmentation, or limited-level LIV + 1 assessments can approximate the same biomechanical signal with lower workflow burden.

Although a formal head-to-head incremental AUC comparison with stable vertebra, last touched vertebra, LIV + 1-to-CSVL deviation, or disc angle below LIV could not be performed in this retrospective cohort, the Firth sensitivity analysis suggests that the association between LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry and adding-on was not merely an artifact of small-event conventional logistic regression. The nonsignificant effects of Cobb angle and AVT should not be interpreted as evidence that conventional radiographic variables are unimportant; rather, they may reflect limited power, cohort characteristics, or the fact that local junctional biomechanics are only partly captured by global alignment measures.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and relatively limited sample size restrict generalizability. Only 15 patients developed distal adding-on, which limits the stability of multivariable models and increases the risk of overfitting. Although Firth penalized logistic regression supported the robustness of the primary association, the results remain hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Second, traditional LIV-selection variables, including stable vertebra, last touched vertebra, LIV + 1-to-CSVL deviation, disc angle below LIV, and detailed sagittal parameters, were not prospectively recorded in a standardized manner for the full cohort. Therefore, we could not perform a definitive incremental-prediction analysis comparing conventional LIV-selection criteria with and without LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry, nor could we identify formal clinical conflict cases based on complete conventional LIV-selection data.

Third, the cohort consisted primarily of skeletally mature or near-mature AIS patients (Risser IV-V) and had a relatively high mean chronological age. The findings therefore should not be generalized directly to younger AIS populations with substantial remaining growth potential, in whom both BMD remodeling and adding-on mechanisms may differ. Fourth, curve flexibility is highly relevant to arthrodesis-level selection; however, bending or traction radiographs were not systematically available for all patients in this retrospective dataset, and flexibility adjustment was therefore not included in the statistical models.

Fifth, patients with known metabolic bone disorders were excluded, but calcium, vitamin D, parathyroid hormone, and other bone-metabolism tests were not systematically collected for all patients. Therefore, subclinical metabolic factors affecting BMD could not be fully excluded. Sixth, although CT-based 3D-FEA provides detailed estimates of vertebral BMD distribution, it remains a computational approximation rather than a direct measurement of mechanical loading, tissue stiffness, muscle force, or disc behavior. Finally, BMD asymmetry may reflect a broader asymmetric biomechanical environment, including paraspinal muscle imbalance, chronic asymmetric loading, disc wedging, and deformity severity; causal inference cannot be established from the present retrospective observational design.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that vertebral BMD in AIS follows a region-specific asymmetric distribution pattern and identifies increased preoperative LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry as a reproducible marker associated with distal adding-on. Cutoff-based risk stratification and Firth penalized sensitivity analysis support the potential value of this parameter as complementary biomechanical information for distal fusion-level planning. Larger prospective studies incorporating standardized traditional LIV-selection variables are required before LIV + 1 BMD asymmetry can be used as a stand-alone determinant of surgical level selection.
