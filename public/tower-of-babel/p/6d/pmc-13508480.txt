Perspective on Scanning Electrochemical Probe Microscopy in Single-Cell Imaging
Authors: Jingjing Song, Yuheng Lv, Feixiang Fang, Xige Ren, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-cell analysis is essential for probing cellular heterogeneity and understanding biological and disease mechanisms. Cellular imaging plays a key role in this field by providing critical spatiotemporal and functional information. Scanning electrochemical probe microscopy (SEPM), a kind of electrochemical imaging technique using micro/nanometer-sized probes to scan cells, can provide information on cellular topography and various interfacial processes in a nondestructive and real-time manner with high spatiotemporal resolution, therefore becoming a powerful tool for single-cell analysis. In this perspective, we systematically review the advances of three SEPM techniques (including scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM), scanning ion conductance microscopy (SICM), and scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM) for single-cell analysis, including imaging cellular topography, membrane protein distribution, metabolic activity, surface charge, and mechanical property. We also discuss the current challenges in enhancing spatiotemporal resolution, detection sensitivity, and imaging throughput and realizing the multiparameter monitoring capability of SEPM for single-cell studies. Last, we highlight their promising development directions, such as high-performance imaging, multimodal integration, functionalized probes, 4D monitoring, and intelligent data analysis, with which SEPM can support more in-depth investigation in cell function studies.

Introduction

Deciphering functional heterogeneity at the single-cell level is fundamental to elucidating biological mechanisms and disease pathogenesis. Conventional cell analysis techniques, such as Western blotting and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, often mask individual cellular states and their transient dynamics due to ensemble averaging. To overcome this, single-cell analysis techniques (e.g., flow cytometry, optical microscopy, and single cell sequence) are essential for capturing individual cellular states, revealing functional heterogeneity, and enabling precise insights into biological processes and disease mechanisms. Among various single-cell analysis techniques, cellular imaging techniques such as optical and electron microscopies with high spatial resolution are essential for capturing the cell topological structure and functional information. However, they mainly provide “snapshot” images of cells and suffer from inherent trade-offs among spatiotemporal resolution, invasiveness, and physiological compatibility. For the current fundamental and applied biological studies of cells, the growing interest in probing the dynamic behavior of living cells under their physiological environment raises the need of new imaging tools for noninvasively probing living cells at their dynamic state.

Scanning electrochemical probe microscopy (SEPM), a kind of electrochemical imaging technique using a micro/nanometer-sized electrode as its probe to scan across or approach a cell surface, has micro/nanometer-scaled spatial and high temporal resolution and in situ monitoring capability of the multiparameter of cellular function under physiological conditions. Over the past 30 years, several SEPM techniques, such as scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM), scanning ion conductance microscopy (SICM), and scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM), have been developed and used for single-cell studies. For example, SECM, using an ultramicroelectrode (UME) as its probe to record faradaic currents of redox molecules (e.g., O2, H2O2, neurotransmitters), can real-time monitor metabolic fluxes, secretion dynamics, and interfacial electron-transfer kinetics across cells. SICM, utilizing a glass nanopipette as its probe to record ion current during approaching the cell surface, can provide living cell topography and cellular membrane property in a label-free manner at the nanoscale. SECCM, employing a droplet confined at the tip of a dual-barrel capillary to form a localized electrochemical cell upon gentle contact with the cell surface, allows highly confined electrochemical measurements with exceptional spatial resolution, facilitating super-resolution membrane protein imaging. These SEPM techniques have the advantages of imaging topography, functional studies of redox activities at a single living cell, and injecting/extracting biomolecules into a living cell, thus becoming a synergistic toolkit for single-cell studies. , They have been increasingly applied to investigate various cellular processes, including cellular metabolism, ion channel activity, intercellular communication, and single-cell nanobiopsy.

In this perspective, we begin by outlining how the three SEPM techniques image cells, including capturing cellular topographical features, providing spatially resolved maps of membrane protein activity, metabolic fluxes, surface charge, and mechanical properties (Figure ). We then discuss the key methodological bottlenecks, particularly in imaging speed and throughput that currently limit the wider applications of SEPM in cell studies, and highlight the emerging technical advances aimed at overcoming these limitations. Finally, we propose the promising avenues for SEPM development, with the emphasis on advanced probe designs, integrated multimodal detection, improved spatiotemporal resolution and data analysis capability, and expanded utility in live-cell bioanalysis. These would push SEPM forward in cell studies with offering more new insights in fields ranging from drug screening to mechanistic studies of disease.

Work Principles of SEPM

The three SEPM techniques are all electrochemical principle-based scanning probe microscopies using a micro- or nanoelectrode/nanopipette as the probe, while the working principles of each technique are different.

As illustrated in Figure a, SECM mainly employs a solid micro/nanoelectrode as its probe to record faradaic current/potential changes of redox mediators around samples in electrolyte solutions. It primarily operates in several modes, including feedback mode (positive/negative feedback depending on whether the substrate regenerates the redox mediator), generation–collection mode (where the tip detects redox species generated at the sample surface), and redox competition mode (where the tip and substrate compete for the same redox species). All these modes of SECM are suitable for spatial mapping of the localized electrochemical activity of samples.

Schematic illustrations of the work principles of (a) SECM, (b) SICM, and (c) SECCM (WE: working electrode, RE: reference electrode, CE: counter electrode, QRCE: quasi-reference counter electrode).

As shown in Figure b, SICM utilizes an electrolyte-filled nanopipette as its probe to record ion current between quasi-reference counter electrodes (QRCEs) inside the nanopipette and in the bulk electrolyte. As the nanopipette approaches the sample surface, the surface proximity partially blocks the flow of ions at the nanopipette tip, increasing the access resistance and thereby causing a decrease in ion current (distance-dependent resistance). This highly sensitive, distance-dependent effect provides precise feedback for tip positioning, enabling noncontact scanning. There are several operational modes of SICM, including direct current mode (steady-state ion current for topography imaging), modulation modes (bias-modulated and AC-modulated for enhanced sensitivity to charge and mechanics via oscillating signals), and hopping mode (retract-and-approach cycles with preventing tip crashing for imaging rough surfaces like cells).

The scheme in Figure c shows that SECCM confines the electrochemical measurement within a meniscus droplet protruding from a micro/nanopipette, which serves as a confined electrochemical cell and allows for nanoscale resolution in the investigation of single entities. As the droplet contacts the sample surface, a conductive pathway is established, enabling localized electrochemical measurement without submerging the entire sample. In SECCM experiments, tip positioning is controlled via ion conductance current upon droplet–surface contact with scanning performed in constant-distance or hopping modes to accommodate rough surfaces and prevent tip crashes, which is critical for fragile samples like cells to avoid probe damage or sample disruption. Common operational modes of SECCM include voltammetric (for current–potential sweeps), potentiometric (for potential measurements), and impedance modes (for interfacial kinetics).

In summary, SECM relies on faradaic processes for electrochemical activity mapping, making it ideal for detecting redox-active species and metabolic fluxes in the cellular microenvironment. SICM records ion currents for noncontact topography and charge/mechanics imaging, suited to soft living cells. SECCM utilizes a confined droplet to enable localized interfacial analysis on the sample surface, demonstrating distinctive capabilities in the studies of single-cell secretion and nanoscale biosensing.

Applications of SEPM in Single-Cell Imaging

Imaging of Cellular Topography

Cellular topography is a fundamental descriptor of cell state and function through topographical features such as membrane contours, dimensional profiles, and dynamic structures like ruffles and microvilli. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) has been widely used for imaging the topography of living cells. However, it often uses a contact-based mode, which requires direct physical interaction between the probe and the cell surface and inevitably introduces mechanical perturbation, leading to sample deformation and disruption of native cellular processes. In contrast, SECM with an UME to scan across the cell surface while recording faradaic currents that vary with probe–cell surface distance can invasively image the cell surface without contacting and provide dynamic topographical imaging. SICM utilizes an electrolyte-filled nanopipette to measure ion current between the nanopipette tip and the cell surface, where the current decreases as the nanopipette approaches the surface, allowing precise distance feedback for noncontact scanning. These two techniques achieve high-resolution imaging of delicate cell structures without mechanical contact, especially suitable for studying soft and highly dynamic biological surfaces.

For instance, Ding and colleagues used SECM with a 5 μm-in-diameter Pt UME as its probe to continuously track the topographical changes of T24 bladder cancer cells at nonphysiological temperatures over 156 min and observed the cellular early apoptotic events under heat stress (Figure a). Later, Matysik et al. used a 200–600 nm-in-radius Pt electrode as the SECM probe to record the topographic images of confluent rat kidney epithelial cell monolayers under physiological conditions with submicrometer resolution and selectively induced necrosis at a single cell through local hydroxide ion generation to elevate pH around the target cell, demonstrating SECM’s capability of noninvasive imaging and targeted chemical intervention. However, in the traditional feedback mode of SECM, the probe current depends on both tip–sample surface distance and heterogeneous interfacial kinetics around cells, which cannot deconvolute topographic information from local chemical reactivity. A shear force-based constant-distance mode has been developed to reduce the effect of cellular topography on recording chemical reactivity around cells in the constant-distance strategy.

Compared to SECM, owing to the nanometer-scaled spatial resolution based on the nanopipette as its probe, SICM is more suitable to image cell topography with providing the ultrastructure. For example, Shiku et al. utilized SICM with a nanopipette (inner diameter: 100 nm) as its probe to real-time capture the dynamic changes in the topography of A549 cells during epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), revealing a quantitative reduction in the number and size of membrane ruffles during EMT progress (Figure b). To overcome the issues of low imaging speed and low scanning throughput of the conventional SICM technique during cell imaging, several efforts have been reported to enhance the temporal resolution and throughput of SICM for single-cell studies. For example, considering that precise controlling of probe-to-sample distance is essential for obtaining accurate topographic maps, to accelerate imaging speed, Matsue et al. developed an intelligent scanning algorithm with optimizing probe hopping amplitude to dramatically increase the scan rate without compromising data quality. In detail, the developed hopping-based distance control algorithm applies a small vertical oscillation to the probe and monitors the ion current changes, achieving fast scanning at 15 s per frame (32 × 32 pixels) and visualization of rapid endocytosis processes without requiring full probe retraction (Figure c). Fukuma et al. implemented an automated region-of-interest mode with predicting cell locations to guide sequential imaging, thereby boosting the efficiency of time-lapse experiments. Beyond imaging speed, acquiring multiparameter information on cells in a single scan is also a key objective, which has been achieved using multichannel probes (e.g., double-barrel pipettes) to simultaneously map cell topography and local electrical impedance. Moreover, integration of SICM with other techniques, such as super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, microelectrode arrays (Figure d), and also SECM, can create more powerful correlative imaging platforms, enabling the simultaneous acquisition of topographical, chemical fluxes, and functional data of living cells with high enhanced throughput.

Beyond single-cell imaging, SICM has also been used for imaging cell–cell interactions. For example, Gorelik et al. introduced an “Opto-SICM” framework that combines SICM with cell-specific optogenetic stimulation to precisely investigate the dynamic interface between fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes, a process often challenging to resolve through optical microscopy (Figure e). Lazarenko et al. used SICM to real-time monitor the dynamic process of neutrophil transendothelial migration in a Staphylococcus aureus-simulated pyogenic lesion model. Recently, our group used SICM to track the formation of tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) and subsequent mitochondrial transfer between living pancreatic cancer cells, systematically visualizing the life cycle of these intercellular connections (Figure f). These examples underscore the capabilities of SECM and SICM to dynamically image elucidate complex, time-dependent topographic changes in living cells under physiologically relevant contexts with label-free and noninvasive nature.

Imaging of Cellular Membrane Proteins

Membrane proteins are essential mediators of cellular communication and signal transduction, regulating critical processes such as molecular transport, cell recognition, and disease progression through their nanoscale distribution and dynamic activity. However, conventional optical methods for studying these proteins are often constrained by limitations in interference from fluorescent labels and lack of functional activity information on membrane proteins. In this context, SEPM with nanoscale spatial resolution and functional detection capability has emerged as a indispensable tool for the direct investigation of membrane proteins in living cells.

Among three SEPM techniques, SICM can provide fine topographic information on membrane protein architecture under physiological conditions. For instance, Korchev et al. used a nanopipette with an inner diameter of approximately 13 nm as the SICM probe and successfully visualized protein complexes with diameters of ∼14 nm and ∼30 nm, along with their dynamic reorganization on the membrane of living sperm cells (Figure a). Beyond the imaging static structure, SICM can also monitor dynamic secretory events in cellular membrane proteins. For example, Matsue et al. applied SICM to directly observe the secretion of the von Willebrand factor from living endothelial cells, capturing the formation of filamentous structures (∼60 nm wide) and the opening/closing dynamics of secretion pores (∼500 nm in diameter) in real time (Figure b). These examples indicate SICM’s capability for in situ and nondestructive monitoring of membrane-associated processes.

Compared to SICM, SECM is more appropriate for quantitatively characterizing the functional activity of membrane proteins on cell surfaces through a prominent strategy of tagging proteins with an enzyme that generates an electroactive reporter. A representative work by Matsue et al. realized the quantification of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expressions on human epidermoid carcinoma (A431) cells and distinguished among different cell lines (Normal CHO, A431 and EGFR/CHO cells) with varying EGFR expression profiles by SECM using an antibody-conjugated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) to label EGFR (Figure c). Subsequently, development of the voltage-switching mode SECM further allows the simultaneous acquisition of topographical and electrochemical activity maps of membrane proteins. In this approach, a nanoelectrode is employed to sequentially collect topographic and electrochemical information at each measurement point through rapid potential switching, and the hindered diffusion-based feedback mechanism is used for precise distance control and topography mapping with a constant-distance mode. The applied potential is then switched to record faradaic currents corresponding to specific electrochemical activities at the identical location, which allows colocated imaging with high resolution. For example, Matsue et al. applied SECM with voltage-switching mode to concurrently perform high-resolution imaging of both surface topography and EGFR on A431 cell membranes, revealing the heterogeneous distribution of EGFR on the cell membrane (Figure d). These works demonstrate SECM’s power in correlating protein function with spatial location.

More recently, SECCM has also been used to map membrane protein distribution by confining measurements to a droplet cell. The pioneering work by Jiang and co-workers labeled carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) with an electroactive ruthenium bipyridyl complex ((Ru­(bpy)3 2+) and used SECCM to realize the visualization of CEA nanoclusters on fixed single cells with a spatial resolution of 160 nm. Their work achieves the high-resolution scans with a 20 nm step size and reveals a heterogeneous, peripherally enriched distribution of CEA at the cell periphery (Figure e).

Imaging of Extracellular Bioactive Molecules and Ions

Extracellular bioactive small molecules (e.g., oxygen and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species) function as essential messengers in cellular metabolism and signaling, for which spatiotemporal concentration profiles and dynamic fluctuations are critical for deciphering cellular functions, pathological pathways, and drug responses. However, real-time, in situ monitoring of these species remains technically challenging, owing to their low abundance, short lifetimes, and rapid diffusion. Conventional fluorescence-based approaches suffer from limitations in long-term tracking and quantitative accuracy due to photobleaching and calibration difficulties. In SEPM techniques, SECM offers a direct and quantitative alternative by measuring faradaic currents arising from redox reactions of target molecules around living cells, enabling dynamic, label-free monitoring of these molecules, specifically for metabolic activity of living cells.

An early work by Matsue et al. successfully used SECM with a constant-height mode to visualize oxygen consumption around a single HeLa cell based on the recorded oxygen reduction currents around cells. Compared with fluorescence viability assay results with no significant change until 40 min, their SECM results demonstrate a decrease in cellular respiratory activity within 5 min after exposure to 20 mM KCN. Upon treatment with 200 μg/mL antimycin A, the SECM results revealed a clear decline in oxygen consumption within 30 min, whereas fluorescence intensity remained unchanged over a 2 h period. This example demonstrates that the capability of SECM for assessing living cell respiration with monitoring early cellular responses. Moreover, to overcome the influences of the sample topography and oxygen reduction reaction at the SECM probe during chemical activity measurements with a conventional constant-height mode, Schuhmann and colleagues developed a shear force-based constant-distance mode integrated with a potential pulse protocol and time-dependent data acquisition, which realized precise quantification of respiratory activity in PC-12 and HEK293 cells (Figure a). , Recently, our group implemented a programmable pulse potential mode of SECM to simultaneously in situ image oxygen consumption and extracellular ROS around living neurons under different temperatures, and revealed dynamic changes in neuronal oxidative states under thermal stress (Figure b). He et al. also used a programmable SECM with potential waveforms and multiple redox mediators (oxygen, FcMeOH, and Ru­(NH3)6 3+) to achieve multiparametric electrochemical images of cells in a single scan (Figure c).

Besides small molecules, SECM can also permit quantitative imaging of ions relative to cellular physiology. For instance, He’s group applied SECM using a K+-selective ionophore-modified nanopipette as its probe with applying a constant bias voltage to monitor extracellular K+ levels of different cancer cell lines (MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and SK-BR-3 cells). They found that MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited the most pronounced K+ efflux upon electrical stimulation, suggesting cell-type specific activity of voltage-gated K+ channels (Figure d). The above examples demonstrate SECM as a uniquely powerful approach for resolving the spatiotemporal dynamics of reactive molecules and ions at the single-cell level, providing critical insights into metabolic regulation, redox signaling, and ion channel activity of cells.

Imaging of Cell Surface Charge

Cell surface charge serves as a critical biophysical parameter regulating essential physiological processes, e.g., intercellular communication, cell adhesion, growth, and division. The ability to visualize the local surface charge distributions on the cell surface and resolve its heterogeneity is vital for elucidating how surface charge influences cellular function. Through monitoring the ion current between the nanopipette and the bulk electrolyte solution near the charged cell surface, SICM can quantitatively map surface charge density of cells. The mechanism is that at low electrolyte concentration, the increased thickness of the electrical double layer (up to several nanometers in aqueous solutions) at the nanopipette tip induces surface charge effects and thus manifests ion current rectification (ICR), a polarity-dependent conductivity of the nanopipette.

The first work of the SICM-based approach to image the surface charge of the charged polymer using distance-modulated mode was reported by Unwin’s group. Subsequently, they extended this approach to image the cell surface charge with a developed bias-modulated SICM (BM-SICM) with applying a modulated bias potential between QRCEs in an electrolyte-filled nanopipette and bulk solution. Since negative cell surface charge induces ICR in the diffuse double layer, enhancing ionic current under negative bias (cation attraction) and suppressing it under positive bias (cation repulsion), the bias-dependent responses quantified at each pixel can be obtained. Based on this mechanism, BM-SICM enables simultaneously mapping cell topography and local surface charge (Figure a). Through combining current–voltage measurements with finite element modeling, they obtained the surface charge at Zea mays root hairs with a negative charge of −20 mC/m2 at the tip region of the root cell and a cell surface charge of about −15 mC/m2 on the AbSc cells. Later, to shorten single-point acquisition time and improve overall detection efficiency, they also developed pulsed-potential SICM to image the surface charge of living PC-12 cells in physiological media and observed previously undetectable nanoscaled charge heterogeneity on the cell surface under high ionic strength condition (Figure b). Recently, Gu and colleagues proposed a current difference-based SICM (ΔI-SICM) protocol, through which currents and probe positions were measured close to and far from the surface of cells, to monitor extracellular charge distributions and topography structures of hDPSCs cells.

Besides imaging cell surface charge, Unwin et al. also explored the applications of SICM to characterize bacterial surface charges. For example, they employed pulsed-potential SICM to map the extracellular charge environment of Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis, revealing that B. subtilis exhibits a higher negative surface charge (−350 to −450 mC/m2) than E. coli. Later, Shepard et al. developed a hopping mode SICM for rapid surface charge mapping of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and biofilms, quantifying charge densities of −2.0 ± 0.45 mC/m2 for P. aeruginosa and over −20 mC/m2 for biofilms. Compared to the traditional surface charge characterization methods (e.g., zeta potential measurements), SICM can offer detailed distribution and patterning information on the local surface charge of a single cell under physiological conditions, thereby addressing the limitations associated with ensemble averaging inherent in population-level analysis.

Measurement of Cellular Mechanical Properties

Cells possess the intrinsic ability to sense and respond to external mechanical stimuli, a process known as mechanotransduction, which regulates key biological events such as proliferation, differentiation, and migration. In situ characterization of the cellular mechanical property is essential for understanding various cellular processes like cell migration, cell durotaxis, embryogenesis, and cell differentiation. Similar to AFM for cell mechanics measurements, SICM can also be used to characterize the mechanical properties of living cells via applying a positive/negative pressure inside the pipette but in a noncontact way compared to AFM. After the pipette with an inner radius of around 15–1000 nm is positioned near the cell surface, the applied pressure causes electrolyte solution to flow out from or into the pipette, creating a significant hydrostatic pressure near the tip, therefore inducing cell membrane deformation without directly contacting the cell surface.

Korchev et al. demonstrated the first use of SICM to quantitatively map cell stiffness by applying a regulated hydrostatic pressure through a nanopipette (inner radius: 100–1000 nm), where the resulting flow-induced deformation of the cell surface allowed the calculation of local mechanical properties. Subsequently, Rheinlaender and Schäffer et al. used SICM to measure cell membrane stiffness with applying a controlled pressure through the rear of an SICM nanopipette, which induces cellular membrane stiffness relative cellular deformation. Based on this, they further used SICM (with nanopipettes of inner radii of 150–200 nm) to record cellular creep compliance under applied pressure to compute both stiffness and fluidity, simultaneously obtaining cell topography, membrane stiffness, and fluidity property. Later, they introduced electroosmotic flow (EOF) as the supplemental flow source of SICM (with nanopipettes of inner radii of 50–500 nm) and imaged Young’s modulus of living cell according to EOF-induced cellular membrane deformation through quantifying EOF mobility with a finite element model (Figure c). Recently, to further minimize mechanical perturbation on cell membrane during measurements, Fang et al. developed an SICM technique (with nanopipettes of inner diameters of about 15 nm) based on hydrodynamic resistance to image the apparent Young’s modulus of living cells, revealing the cytoskeleton-associated modulus distribution (6.27 ± 1.45 kPa) in migrating MCF-7 cells (Figure d). Although the lateral resolution of SICM (∼20 nm governed by the pipette aperture) is lower than the atomic-scale resolution of AFM, these studies demonstrate that SICM serves as a robust noncontact alternative for cell mechanics measurements, particularly by preserving cellular activity and structural fidelity.

Challenges and Future Perspectives of SEPM for Single-Cell Imaging

SEPM has become an important single-cell imaging technique with capabilities of providing real-time information on cellular topography, membrane protein distribution, secretion of bioactive molecules, surface charge, and mechanical properties. Despite these achievements, to become a central methodology for cell biology, several persistent technical challenges of SEPM still need to solve. Current limitations primarily lie in trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolutions, higher detection sensitivity, imaging throughput, and capacity for simultaneous multiparameter acquisition at the single-cell level. For example, the balance of spatial and temporal resolutions refers to the fact that enhancing spatial resolution (e.g., via smaller probes or larger sampling numbers) lengthens the imaging time at the expense of temporal resolution, whereas accelerating imaging generally reduces spatial detail. As a result, the spatiotemporal resolution of SEPM generally remains at the submicrometer level (e.g., approximately 30 nm for SICM, and 100 nm for SECM and SECCM). Acquiring a full image usually takes several seconds to tens of minutes according to the sample size and spatial resolution. Moreover, the intrinsic compromise among imaging speed, spatial resolution, and scan area makes large-area or multicell high-resolution mapping prohibitively slow, limiting statistical power for heterogeneous populations. Improving detection sensitivity typically relies on longer signal integration and stricter noise suppression to maintain an adequate signal-to-noise ratio, which further constrains imaging speed and throughput. According to these, we next list our thoughts about the technical challenges and raise future perspectives of SEPMs for cell studies.

Advances in Imaging Technology: Speed, Resolution, and Throughput

A fundamental challenge in current SEPM for direct cell observation is in the intrinsic trade-off among imaging speed, spatial resolution, and scan area. For example, the pursuit of high spatial resolution often forces a compromise on temporal resolution, rendering the technique inadequate for capturing rapid millisecond-scale cellular dynamics such as neurotransmitter release and vesicular fusion. Performing scans of large areas or multiple cells at high resolution becomes prohibitively slow, severely restricting the statistical power needed to analyze heterogeneous cell populations. Addressing this tripartite compromise is therefore a fundamental prerequisite for the technological progress of SEPM. Future efforts can be focused on innovations in probe engineering and scanning methodology. For instance, the development of multiplexed nanoelectrode arrays through advanced micro/nanofabrication integrated with high-speed data acquisition systems can offer a promising path toward concurrent nanoscale spatial and millisecond temporal resolution. In parallel, the implementation of adaptive or “smart” scanning protocols, which dynamically prioritize target regions with high cellular activity for detailed imaging, can maximize the information yield without sacrificing speed or field of view. Collectively, these advances can promote the technical foundation necessary to visualize fast physiological events in real time and to conduct robust high-throughput studies of single-cell heterogeneity.

Multimodal Integration and Multifunctional Probes: Toward a Correlative Information Network

Cellular function is influenced by a complex, interdependent network of chemical, electrical, and mechanical signals. A significant limitation of current SEPM is their typical operation in a one or two parameter mode, i.e., capturing isolated data on either topography or a specific redox mediator, obscuring the direct, spatiotemporal correlations between different physicochemical properties that are essential for a mechanistic understanding of cellular activity. This constraint arises from two principal challenges: the absence of fully integrated platforms for synchronous acquisition of electrochemical, topographical, and mechanical data streams and the limited functionality of most single-channel probes, which prevents the concurrent monitoring of multiple redox activities of living cells in situ.

Future progress can focus on the coordinated development of integrated multimodal platforms and next-generation multifunctional probes. For the platform part, achieving integrated hardware synchronization and software correlation between SEPM and complementary techniques, such as super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, AFM, Raman spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry, can be a strategy. This integration will enable the simultaneous recording of multiparameter correlation maps from the same cellular region. Moreover, integration of advanced single-cell analysis technologies into the SEPM platform would further expand the applications of SEPM in single-cell analysis. For example, combination of dielectrophoresis-based nanotweezers with the capability of precisely trapping biomolecules (e.g., DNA, RNA, mitochondria) inside living cells using nanopipettes , with SICM can achieve high-precision targeting and dynamic sampling of living cells. Moreover, such an integrated SEPM system can be coupled with downstream analytical techniques such as qPCR and sequencing techniques for single-cell nanobiopsy and genomic analysis at the single-organelle level. For the probe part, nanopipettes of SICM can serve as a single-cell manipulation platform, enabling precise nanoscale localization for extraction of cellular contents or targeted biomolecule delivery. − In addition, advancing micro/nanofabrication to create multichannel or multifunctional probes capable of parallel detection of several metabolites (e.g., O2, H2O2, pH, ions, neurotransmitters) is critical for understanding cellular functions under an in vivo microenvironment. The synergy of these parallel advancements will empower researchers to elucidate relationships between disparate cellular parameters. For instance, it will become feasible to correlate localized metabolic bursts with underlying cytoskeletal mechanics or to map the codistribution of specific membrane receptor clusters with the nanoscale domains of surface charge, thereby decoding the multifactorial logic of cellular signaling and responses.

Expanding Spatiotemporal Dimensions: From 2D Surface Mapping to 4D Physiological Dynamics

A prevailing limitation of conventional SEPMs is their confinement to two-dimensional surface mapping, which overlooks the fundamental three-dimensional and highly dynamic nature of critical cellular physiology. Processes such as the spatiotemporal evolution of chemical gradients in the extracellular space or within organelles and the continuous remodeling of cellular mechanics during migration or mitosis unfold in 4D (3D space + time) and remain largely inaccessible to conventional surface-scanning techniques.

To bridge this gap, the next frontier lies in advancing SEPM toward 3D imaging and long-term live-cell interrogation. Achieving 3D imaging of bioactive molecules of cells may involve strategies such as controlled depth profiling with specialized probes or the correlative integration of SEPM with optical sectioning methods (e.g., confocal or light-sheet microscopy) to reconstruct molecule distributions in a volume surrounding the cell. In parallel, enabling long-term monitoring over hours or days requires dedicated innovations in system environmental control, probe stability, and enhanced biocompatibility to minimize experimental perturbation. This capability is essential for tracking a single cell’s dynamic response to stimuli, such as drug administration or intercellular signaling events. The convergence of these capabilities will ultimately furnish a 4D analytical perspective, transforming SEPM from a surface snapshot tool into a platform for the continuous observation of integrated cellular physiological processes. This will open new windows for studying sustained and complex events, such as phagocytosis, intracellular vesicle transport, and the establishment of cell–cell communication networks in their native volumetric and temporal context.

Artificial Intelligence-Driven Data Analysis: From Data Mining to Predictive Therapeutic Efficacy

As SEPM evolves multimodal, high-throughput, and high spatiotemporal resolution imaging, it will inevitably generate vast, complex data sets that are intractable to conventional analysis. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) will transition from useful tools to indispensable cores of the data interpretation workflow. These algorithms will be essential for automatically extracting discriminative features from multidimensional data, including cellular topography, molecule concentration, and electrical and mechanic properties, to construct reference databases of single-cell “fingerprints”. These databases can systematically catalog signatures associated with the cell type, health status, and disease progression. Beyond feature identification, AI/ML’s deeper value lies in autonomously discovering hidden correlations within multiparameter space, for instance, revealing whether specific morphological motifs consistently coincide with distinct electrochemical or metabolic phenotypes. This capability will enable the automated characterization of complex spatial patterns, such as cellular protein aggregation sites, metabolically abnormal microdomains, or heterogeneous distributions of surface charge and mechanics. Ultimately, by training on imaging data, predictive models can be developed to forecast cellular behavior and drug responses. This shift from descriptive analysis to predictive modeling will establish a novel quantitative framework for clinical diagnostics, drug target discovery, and the in vitro prediction of therapeutic efficacy.

Together, the trajectory of cellular electrochemical imaging points toward a future that is intrinsically dynamic, intelligent, multiparameter, and 4D scanning (Figure ). By systematically overcoming the prevailing technical bottlenecks of conventional SEPM outlined above, the new technologies will evolve beyond their current role as advanced observational tools of cell studies. They are poised to become integrated platforms for the systematic, correlative, and real-time investigation of the chemical, physical, and structural states of living cells at the micro/nanoscale. This transformation will profoundly deepen their impact across fundamental and translational biomedical research, including studies of cellular heterogeneity, intercellular communication, drug screening, and the mechanistic elucidation of disease pathologies.

Challenges and future perspectives of SEPM for single-cell imaging. Overview of four major development directions toward the SEPM platform: (1) breakthroughs in imaging speed, nanoscale resolution and throughput; (2) multimodal and multichannel functional probes for correlated multiparameter detection; (3) transition to dynamic and 4D imaging and long-term tracking; and (4) AI-driven analysis for automated feature extraction, complex data interpretation, and predictive modeling.

Conclusion

In the past 30 years, three SEPM techniques (SECM, SICM and SECCM) with advantages of nanoscaled spatial resolution, monitoring chemical specificity capability, and inherent compatibility with living systems have been widely used for direct, in situ quantification of a diverse array of cellular physicochemical properties, from cellular topography, distribution of membrane proteins, dynamics of bioactive molecules to nanoscale variations in surface charge and mechanical stiffness. Therefore, SEPMs have firmly established themselves as indispensable functional imaging platforms for single-cell analysis by providing unprecedented insights into cellular structure–function relationships and precise operability for investigating and modulating living cell dynamics, uncovering heterogeneous landscapes that are often obscured by ensemble-averaging techniques. Despite these advances, the full potential of SEPM to decipher complex dynamic biological processes is constrained by several intertwined challenges. Key limitations include the persistent trade-off between spatiotemporal resolution and imaging throughput, the need for greater stability and specificity in physiologically complex environments, and the current inability to perform the real-time 4D monitoring of cellular events.

Overcoming these hurdles will necessitate concerted advancements in four critical areas: (1) the development of integrated, multimodal imaging platforms; (2) the design of next-generation, multifunctional nanoprobes; (3) the implementation of novel high-speed scanning methodologies; and (4) the adoption of AI-driven frameworks for multidimensional data interpretation. As innovations in probe engineering and imaging modalities continue to converge, SEPM is anticipated to become a cornerstone technology in frontier biological research, enabling studies of the tumor immune microenvironment, synaptic communication, and stem cell fate decisions. By providing high-content functional maps of single cells, SEPM can hold significant promise for translational applications, including high-throughput drug screening and precision diagnostic strategies. These evolutions of SEPM will shift SEPM from a tool for static surface mapping toward a dynamic, intelligent, and 4D analytical system, offering a robust and versatile toolkit for the next generation of single-cell research for a system-level understanding of cellular physiology.
