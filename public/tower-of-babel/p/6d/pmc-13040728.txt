Multimodal machine learning mobility assessment in Parkinson’s disease within supervised and unsupervised settings
Authors: Y Celik, H Kuduz, F V Engin, F Kacar, E Tarakci, L T Pearson-Noseworthy, J Das, S Stuart, W L Woo, A Godfrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMobility in people with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) is strongly context-dependent. Clinic-based assessments provide only brief snapshots under controlled conditions and may not reflect everyday functional capacity. Wearable sensors enable continuous free-living monitoring, yet most studies use unimodal signals and simple threshold-based walking detection. Multimodal sensing, combining surface electromyography (sEMG) with inertial measurement units (IMUs), can offer a more complete picture of mobility in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Therefore, developing a data-driven multimodal gait analysis pipeline is essential for accurately identifying walking episodes and deriving detailed gait characteristics of PD in unsupervised free-living settings.MethodsTen PwPD wore four thigh and shank sensors (IMU + sEMG) during supervised laboratory and outdoor walks, followed by unsupervised free-living recording on the same day. Video captured using eye-tracking glasses was used to annotate the supervised data, which were then used to train a machine learning (ML) model based on IMU-only and fused IMU + sEMG features. The trained model was applied to the unsupervised data to detect walking bouts, after which spatiotemporal, kinematic, and muscle-activation measures were computed.ResultsWalking-bout detection using IMU features alone achieved acceptable performance (F1 = 87.53%), with higher performance observed when sEMG features were included (F1 = 94.45%). Exploratory analyses suggested that unsupervised walking may be associated with slower pace, greater variability, and increased asymmetry compared with supervised walking. A reduction in knee flexion-extension (F-E) amplitude and an earlier occurrence of peak F-E were also observed during unsupervised walking. Additionally, higher lower-limb muscle activation during stance was noted, indicating potentially increased neuromuscular demand in free-living conditions. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and are intended to be hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.ConclusionThis pilot study successfully presents a multimodal, data-driven framework integrating neuromuscular and kinematic signals to assess mobility in supervised and unsupervised settings. The analysis of combined changes in muscle activation, joint kinematics, and spatiotemporal gait measures reveals interconnected gait adaptations that are otherwise missed by unimodal systems. These findings suggest the potential for novel. multimodal digital biomarkers that offer an ecologically valid, continuous monitoring of motor function in PwPD.

Introduction

Mobility impairments are among the most disabling features of Parkinson’s disease (PD), often emerging from the early stages [1]. People with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) typically show alterations in walking speed, rhythm, and balance, which can lead to instability and an increased risk of falls [2]. The fear of falling and the loss of confidence may further reduce independence and limit everyday activities. Over time, these difficulties contribute to lower physical activity levels and a decline in overall quality of life [3]. Addressing those challenges requires monitoring disease progression and optimising fall reducing strategies via personalised care which can utilise objective gait assessment as a key outcome.

In clinical practice, gait assessment in PwPD commonly relies on structured rating scales and observational tests [4]. In research and specialist laboratory settings, more detailed analyses are performed using instrumented treadmills, motion capture systems, and pressure-sensitive walkways to quantify gait characteristics (e.g., step time) with greater precision [5]. However, lab-based assessments capture only brief, highly controlled snapshots of gait that are influenced by examiner presence and environmental cues, and therefore may not represent daily mobility capacity [6]. Some clinically relevant events are also difficult to capture during routine clinical visits, such as freezing of gait, or fluctuations in response to dopaminergic treatment [7]. Consequently, these assessments may not reflect how PwPD walk in everyday settings, where gait is influenced by external factors like terrain, obstacles, and lighting, as well as internal states such as fatigue, motivation, and dual-tasking [8]. Addressing these challenges requires continuous, ecologically valid gait monitoring that captures how PwPD walk in their daily environments. Wearable sensors (e.g., inertial measurement units, IMUs i.e., accelerometers and gyroscopes) provide a practical solution to this need as they enable unobtrusive, long-term gait monitoring beyond the clinic [4]. Compared with lab based systems, wearables offer greater portability and suitability for prolonged use beyond clinics [9]. These advantages make them particularly well suited for capturing context-dependent variations in gait. Nonetheless, before gait characteristics can be meaningfully examined in unsupervised settings, it is essential to first detect walking bouts reliably within continuous sensor data.

Early unsupervised studies identified walking bouts from IMU signals using algorithmic detection methods based on periodicity, thresholding, or wavelet peak analysis [10–12]. However, such heuristic methods are sensitive to signal variability and placement errors and often fail to adapt to the heterogeneous nature of free-living data [13]. Data-driven approaches including supervised machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models can overcome these limitations by learning discriminative features directly from sensor signals [14]. DL architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and hybrid CNN-Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models, have shown very high accuracy on benchmark datasets by learning complex spatial and temporal features from IMU data [15, 16]. Their performance, however, depends on large volumes of labelled data, which are often scarce in neurological populations such as PD.

To mitigate this, recent studies have explored data augmentation techniques that artificially expand limited IMU datasets and can improve classification accuracy in clinical cohorts [17, 18], although they introduce greater computational demand and do not resolve the underlying issue of limited patient data [19]. As a result, both ML and DL models are frequently trained on data from healthy adults [20–23], despite the well-documented differences in gait characteristics between healthy individuals and PwPD, leading to reduced generalisability in clinical applications [24]. These generalisability issues indicate the need for walking-detection models developed using data from PwPD, so that walking episodes can be recognised accurately in unsupervised patient recordings.

Once walking periods are identified accurately, it becomes possible to investigate (within walking bout) gait characteristic performance in free-living, which offers several advantages over brief clinic-based assessments. Evidence from week-long monitoring with single-wearable devices suggests that spatio-temporal characteristics within longer walking bouts captured outside of clinics are more sensitive to Parkinson-related gait deficits than within short, structured clinic walks [11]. Beyond these sensitivity differences, home-based recordings also show that gait behaviour itself changes substantially outside supervised settings: unsupervised walking is consistently slower, more variable, and less stable than clinic performance [25]. These differences arise partly because everyday contexts impose demands that standardised tests do not capture. For example, PwPD may shorten their steps in narrow spaces, reduce speed while conversing, or hesitate when turning, context-driven behaviours rarely elicited under supervised conditions. Further studies show that real-world gait in PwPD often falls into two distinct patterns: typically slower gait and greater variability [26]. Occasionally, their speed increases to levels similar to those seen in the clinic, usually during longer walking bouts or periods when ON medication [27]. Such context-dependent adaptations can be large in magnitude, sometimes exceeding the changes observed following therapeutic interventions [7]. Therefore, recognising these differences is essential for developing objective and ecologically valid methods that link clinical observations with everyday mobility in PwPD, a need also emphasised by regulatory agencies [28].

Most of the previous work is based on unimodal IMU-derived characteristics, using either a single IMU or multiple IMUs. While these approaches offer useful spatiotemporal information, they are limited because kinematic data capture only one aspect of impaired gait. A multimodal framework that integrates surface electromyography (sEMG) derived neuromuscular activation and joint kinematics, has been shown to capture more nuanced gait deficits and reveal underlying motor mechanisms that would otherwise go unnoticed in unimodal systems [26]. Although several previous studies have explored multimodal approaches, their deployment has largely remained confined to laboratory settings [29] due to the need for tightly controlled protocols, precise electrode and sensor placement, and the synchronised acquisition of multiple high-bandwidth physiological signals [30].

Therefore, this pilot study presents a data-driven, multimodal pipeline focused first on accurate walking-bout detection (using a supervised model) and then on gait analysis in PwPD across both supervised and unsupervised settings. Building upon previous supervised methodologies, the proposed framework fuses IMU and sEMG signals to both improve supervised walking-detection performance and establish a complete pipeline for multimodal gait assessment. Specifically, this pilot study contributes by:

Developing a supervised, data-driven model that fuses IMU and sEMG features for accurate identification of walking bouts in unsupervised settings,

Developing a supervised, data-driven model that fuses IMU and sEMG features for accurate identification of walking bouts in unsupervised settings,

Utilising a multimodal fusion framework that integrates spatiotemporal, kinematic, and muscle-activation metrics from IMU and sEMG signals to enable comprehensive gait analysis across both supervised and unsupervised settings in PwPD.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sample of community-living ambulatory older adults with mild-to-moderate PD were recruited to participate in this study. Participants were identified through existing Northumbria University research databases and provided written, informed consent prior to participation. The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants aged > 50 years with a clinical diagnosis of Parkinson’s by a movement disorder specialist according to UK brain bank criteria (H&Y stage I-III) [31]; a minimum score of 21/30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment to be able to follow instructions and undertake the activities required to complete the study protocol with support from the researcher [32]; living independently, able to walk and stand without support or assistance from another person, and have adequate hearing/vision capabilities to allow participation in all aspects of study. Participants were excluded if they had a history of psychiatric co-morbidity (e.g. major depressive disorder as determined by geriatric depression scale [33], clinical diagnosis of dementia or other severe cognitive impairment). Participants with a history of stroke, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis or neurological disorders other than PD, any acute lower back or lower extremity pain, peripheral neuropathy, rheumatic and orthopaedic diseases, and unstable medical conditions including cardio-vascular instability in the past 6 months were also excluded.

Ten individuals with PD were recruited for this study (8 males, 2 females; mean age 65.3 ± 7.8 years; mean disease duration 3.2 ± 1.9 years). Participant characteristics included mean body mass of 88.8 ± 9.5 kg and mean height of 171.7 ± 4.5 cm. Clinical assessments were conducted by a trained physiotherapist while participants were ON their regular dopaminergic medication. Cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA [31, 34]), with participants demonstrating preserved cognition (mean score 27.5 ± 2.0). Motor symptom severity was assessed using the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale, part III (MDS-UPDRS III [32, 35]), yielding a mean score of 29.5 ± 19.8). Disease severity, determined using the Hoehn and Yahr (HY) scale [33], indicated mild-to-moderate disease progression: five participants at stage I, three at stage II, and two at stage III. Approval for the study was granted by the Northumbria University Research Ethics Committee (Reference: 23946).

Protocol

Data collection was carried out in two phases on the same day. The first stage took place at Northumbria University’s Coach Lane Campus. Participants walked a 20 m circuit for 2 min, followed by 2 min of sitting and 2 min of standing, before resuming walking. This sequence was repeated 5 times as part of the supervised indoor gait laboratory session and was followed by outdoor walks across asphalt (310 m straight), cobble (305 m straight), and grass (335 m straight) surfaces, taking approximately 10 min to complete. Both walking (~ 20 min) and non-walking (~ 20 min) data were collected for ML model development. These sessions were recorded using a Tobii eye-tracking device, which provided accurate visual labelling of walking periods for data annotation while remaining unobtrusive and not interfering with participants’ natural walking. In the second stage, participants left the campus and resumed their normal daily activities (e.g., at home, at work, or in the garden) while continuing to wear four Shimmer3 EMG sensors for up to 5 h of unsupervised data collection, without the use of the eye-tracking system. Participants were asked to go about their normal day as usual, without being given any specific tasks. This approach was intended to capture natural, habitual behaviour under real-world conditions. In the afternoon, a member of the research team visited participants’ homes to collect the sensors.

Although the sample size is modest, the extended data collection protocol generates a large volume of high-resolution multimodal data. Four wearable sensors were deployed per participant, each operating at 512 Hz and generating approximately 5 GB of data. This resulted in approximately 20 GB of multimodal recordings per participant. Across the full cohort of ten participants, the total dataset size was therefore approximately 200 GB. All participants completed the recording protocol as planned.

Data collection

All participants wore four Shimmer3 EMG wearables (24.9 cm³, 31 g) via straps on the lateral sides of the thighs (RT: right thigh, LT: left thigh) and shanks (RS: right shank, LS: left shank). Prior to data collection, the sensors placed on the shank and thigh were vertically aligned while the participant maintained a comfortable standing posture for 10 s to ensure more accurate estimation of the knee kinematics. Each sensor unit recorded three-axis acceleration, three-axis angular velocity, and two channels of sEMG data. Figure 1 (sensor placement) presents data from the right-thigh (RT) sensor. The same data collection procedure was applied across all sensors and for the left-side measurements. Comprehensive details regarding sensor placement are provided in [36]. Signals were recorded at a sampling frequency of 512 Hz, with the IMU (three-axis accelerometer and three-axis gyroscope) configured (16-bit resolution, ± 8 g, ± 500°/s). Data from the four sensors were synchronised using the proprietary ConsensysPRO software.

Sensor placement (right leg), supervised lab data collection and labelling with eye tracker glasses (segment 1), feature extraction and classification model development (segment 2), unsupervised free living data collection without eye tracker glasses (segment 3), detection of walking bouts in unsupervised data using the model developed and visual inspection (segment 4), multimodal gait analysis (segment 5)

Prior to sEMG electrode placement, the skin was cleaned using alcohol swabs to improve electrode-skin conductivity. Single-use circular Ag/AgCl (silver/silver chloride) surface electrodes were positioned bilaterally in line with SENIAM guidelines [37] on the rectus femoris (RF), biceps femoris (BF), tibialis anterior (TA), and gastrocnemius (GS), with a reference electrode positioned on the patella. For each wearable device (applied to both legs), channel 1 (ch1) was designated for the TA and RF muscles on the shank and thigh sensors, respectively, while channel 2 (ch2) corresponded to the GS and BF muscles on the shank and thigh sensors. The same devices were employed across all participants and placed consistently in identical anatomical locations to minimise variability or placement bias. After completing data collection, IMU and sEMG data were transferred to a Windows 10 workstation using ConsensysPRO proprietary software for further processing.

ML model development

We previously developed a feature-level fusion methodology combining IMU and sEMG data for activity classification [38], which was adopted in the present pilot study. For model development, data from the right and left thigh sensors (RT and LT) were used, as thigh-mounted IMUs have been shown to provide high accuracy in distinguishing walking from non-walking activities due to their sensitivity to changes in leg posture and movement intensity [39].

Raw IMU signals are typically noisy and therefore not directly suitable for HAR models. To address this, a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter was applied, with cut-off frequencies of 5 Hz for acceleration and 20 Hz for gyroscope signals. Surface sEMG signals underwent pre-processing using a zero-lag fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (20–250 Hz), rectification, and an additional zero-lag fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter at 6 Hz to minimise baseline drift and movement artefacts. Finally, all sensor data were segmented into non-overlapping 2-second windows for further analysis. A window length of 2 s was chosen to ensure that each segment captured at least one to two strides.

In total, approximately 1,200 ± 100 segments were generated per participant, consisting of around 600 ± 50 walking occurrences and 600 ± 50 non-walking occurrences (sitting and standing), with slight variations across individuals. Across all participants, this resulted in roughly 12,344 segmented occurrences. For each segmented window, a comprehensive feature set was generated based on characteristics previously shown to be informative for activity classification [40]. The extracted features included: (1) maximum fractal length, (2) simple square integral, (3) mean absolute value (MAV), (4) modified MAV, (5) log detector, (6) average amplitude change, (7) waveform length, (8) root mean square (RMS), (9) slope sign change, and (10) zero crossing. In addition, several statistical descriptors were computed, including (11) variance, (12) standard deviation, (13) mean absolute deviation, (14) interquartile range, (15) coefficient of variation, (16) skewness, (17) kurtosis, (18) average energy, and (19) area under the curve.

The features extracted from IMU and sEMG signals were subsequently used as inputs to Support Vector Machine (SVM) and k-Nearest Neighbour (kNN) classifiers, which were selected due to their previously demonstrated superior performance in activity recognition and gait classification tasks [38]. Model evaluation was performed using a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation strategy to ensure subject-independent generalisation. In each fold, all signal segments from one participant were reserved for testing, while data from the remaining participants were used for training. This ensured that every participant’s data was tested once on unseen subjects. Both classifiers were evaluated for their ability to classify walking and non-walking activities using IMU-only and IMU + sEMG feature sets.

The trained model was then applied to identify walking (activity) periods within the unsupervised data. To achieve this, the unsupervised recordings underwent the same preprocessing pipeline, followed by segmentation into 2-second windows. The same feature vectors were then computed and used as inputs to the developed model for activity classification. Each identified walking period was inspected by examining the repetitive peaks in sagittal plane angular velocity from shank and thigh level IMUs (Fig. 1, stage 4) to confirm complete, continuous gait cycles.

Multimodal gait analysis

The last stage of the pipeline involved performing multimodal gait analysis on the detected walking bouts of unsupervised free-living settings. Prior to analysis, the data from each sensor were segmented using the corresponding time stamps of the labelled events. Specifically, walking period time stamps identified from the right thigh (RT) sensor were used to extract the corresponding segments from all other sensors, and the same procedure was applied for the left thigh (LT) sensor. When walking periods detected by the RT and LT sensors occurred within a 2-second overlap window, they were considered to represent the same walking episode. This ensured temporal alignment and prevented duplicate labelling of the same walking bout. As a result, tri-axial acceleration, tri-axial angular velocity, and two-channel sEMG signals were segmented from all four sensors for the labelled unsupervised data. Following this, a previously proposed multi-layered fusion approach was applied [36] to analyse the data. Custom MATLAB® scripts (R2023b) processed the unsupervised labelled IMU and EMG data at the sample level to extract spatiotemporal features, knee kinematics, and muscle activity measures. Readers are directed to [36] for a comprehensive explanation of data processing. Broadly;

The signal was decomposed with a fifth-order coiflet wavelet (ten scales) to isolate approximation and detail components, reducing drift and artefacts. Time of mid-swing (tms) was identified as the global maximum, and IC-FC events were then detected as negative peaks within predefined windows [IC (tms + 0.25–2 s); FC (tms − 2 to − 0.05 s)]. Then, IC-FC moments were used to extract temporal characteristics.

All results for spatiotemporal gait characteristics are presented according to clinical domains of gait, including pace, rhythm, variability, and asymmetry [11]. To compare gait measures between supervised and unsupervised conditions, participant results (n = 10) were averaged within each setting and summarised using median and interquartile range due to the small sample and inter-individual variability. Differences were assessed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.05), and effect sizes (r) quantified their magnitude. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28).

Results

Classification models

For the IMU-only configuration, the SVM model achieved an accuracy of 87.52%, and F1-score 87.53%, while the kNN model achieved 85.62%, and 85.47%, respectively. Incorporating sEMG features substantially improved both models: the SVM reached an accuracy of 94.47%, and F1-score 94.45%, whereas the kNN achieved 90.95%, and 90.92%, respectively (Table 1). This pilot study utilised the multimodal IMU + sEMG configuration and SVM model for subsequent analyses due to its superior performance in detecting walking periods in unsupervised data.

TA showed higher activation in both stance and swing (p = 0.036 and 0.031, respectively), and GS likewise showed higher activation in stance and swing (p = 0.030 and 0.040, respectively) Fig. 2b.

Discussion

This pilot study addresses a methodological gap in assessing gait impairment in PwPD by demonstrating that a multimodal, data-driven approach can reliably detect walking bouts and characterise gait across supervised and unsupervised environments. Existing free-living gait analyses rely almost exclusively on IMU-derived kinematic measures, limiting their ability to capture the neuromuscular mechanisms that contribute to gait impairment in PwPD. By integrating sEMG with IMU signals, the present pilot study may enable richer interpretation of human movement, enabling improved walking-bout detection and enriched interpretation of gait characteristics across real-world contexts. These contributions are particularly important given the well-recognised discrepancy between clinic-based gait performance and everyday mobility [7], and the growing need for objective, scalable, and ecologically valid monitoring tools in PwPD [28].

In walking detection, the SVM-based fusion model achieved promising subject-independent performance F1 = 87.53%, which increased to 94.45% when sEMG features were added to IMU derived features. Similar trends were reported in healthy populations [38]. This gain may reflect that muscle activation signals carry complementary temporal and physiological cues (e.g. onset timing) [44], which help discriminate walking from non-walking even during slow, irregular, or transitional movements of PwPD. Similar improvements were observed elsewhere [45], where fusing sEMG with accelerometer data increased classification accuracy, likely due to the earlier onset of muscle activation, as sEMG captures pre-movement neural drive preceding IMU detected motion. Beyond classical ML approaches, several studies have demonstrated that deep learning architectures, particularly CNNs, LSTMs, and hybrid CNN-LSTM models, can achieve very high activity-classification accuracies using wearable sensors, including when multimodal inputs are available. For example, CNN-LSTM models have shown state-of-the-art performance by learning temporal representations directly from raw IMU signals [16]. Other studies reported strong results by converting sensor data into activity images or spectrograms for CNN-based classification [17, 18]. However, these methods require substantial preprocessing steps, such as generating activity images, Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)-derived images or constructing multichannel activity maps, which increases complexity and makes full automation challenging for free-living pipelines. In contrast, the ML-based fusion approach used here relies on a consistent set of time-domain features that can be applied directly to unseen data without additional transformations, simplifying integration into gait-segmentation workflows for PwPD. Although previous deep learning models reached similar or higher accuracies on healthy cohorts, their dependence on complex data preparation can pose challenges for scalability in real-world monitoring, where automation and computational efficiency are crucial.

Beyond classification, this pilot work adds exploratory insight beyond conventional unimodal gait assessment by providing information on muscle characteristics of gait impairment in PwPD. Conventional unimodal gait assessments in unsupervised settings typically quantify only spatiotemporal [11] or kinematic characteristics [25] and therefore overlook the neuromuscular processes driving these movements. For instance, the combined analysis of TA and GS activation reveals how coordinated muscle activity regulates foot contact and propulsion, while the interplay between RF and BF provides insight into knee F-E control across the gait cycle [46]. Hence, muscle activation profiles within specific sub-phases of the gait cycle may provide a physiological basis for the differences observed between supervised and unsupervised walking.

Supervised vs. unsupervised

In the unsupervised setting, pace (step velocity and step length) was significantly lower, with greater variability and asymmetry (Fig. 2a). These findings align with previous seven-day comparisons of supervised and free-living gait in PwPD and controls, which reported reduced pace and increased variability, rhythm irregularity, and asymmetry [11]. Gait-speed adjustability, the ability to shift between slow and fast modes, also declines in daily life, alongside shorter steps, greater double support, and higher asymmetry [26]. Although PwPD can occasionally reach clinic-level speeds at home, such bouts represent only about 3% of strides and occur mainly during long, continuous walking or shortly after medication intake [27]. Clinic-based gait metrics show weak correspondence with in-home mobility, indicating that controlled assessments fail to capture the adaptability and contextual influences of daily-life walking [25]. Our pilot results align with these patterns within the same participants, showing slower, more variable, and less regular gait outside clinical control. This difference likely reflects environmental demands, divided attention, and the lack of external cues supporting rhythmic gait. Large-scale comparisons across older adults and PwPD similarly report up to 40% differences between supervised and unsupervised conditions [7], suggesting that clinical assessments may overestimate functional ability while failing to fully capture real-world variability.

The knee F-E angle was smaller, more asymmetric, and occurred earlier in the gait cycle during unsupervised walking (Fig. 2c). This pattern may reflect a more cautious or effortful strategy, which in turn may increase double-support duration [26]. Although many wearable-sensor studies have validated joint kinematics against laboratory-based motion capture systems, the use of wearables to assess joint kinematics beyond clinical or controlled environments remains limited [47, 48]. One study validated a wearable goniometer for measuring knee F-E during gait in healthy adults; however, it did not include a within-subject supervised vs. unsupervised comparison [49]. Collectively, this pilot study presents preliminary evidence that knee F-E patterns differ significantly between supervised and unsupervised walking conditions. These environment-dependent changes in joint timing and range may help explain the greater variability and asymmetry observed in spatiotemporal gait characteristics during unsupervised walking [11, 26]. Future studies should verify these findings across larger and more heterogeneous cohorts to determine whether these environment-dependent changes in knee kinematics generalise across the broader PwPD population.

Muscle activation results provided additional insight into the context-related differences. Activation of the RF, BF, TA, and GS increased across both stance and swing phases in unsupervised condition, Fig. 2b suggesting potentially higher muscular demand and altered activation strategies to enhance stability during unsupervised walking. Altered muscle activation patterns may contribute to the observed joint kinematic differences, which in turn may contribute to the increased variability and asymmetry in spatiotemporal gait characteristics between supervised and unsupervised walking. Earlier investigations using inertial sensors suggest that walking in free-living environments imposes greater cognitive and biomechanical demands. For instance, studies have reported higher dual-task costs outdoors compared to indoors [50], and more pronounced gait deteriorations under combined cognitive-motor loads [51]. Although not always expressed in energetic terms, previous work using IMU-derived energy-expenditure estimates further supports that challenging walking conditions (e.g., slopes, variable speed) increase physiological cost [52]. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that unsupervised walking imposes greater physical and neural demands, or at least more effortful and less efficient neuromuscular control, during unsupervised walking. Consistent with this interpretation, our results revealed measurable differences in muscle activation patterns between supervised and unsupervised settings. Together, these outcomes highlight the potential value of multimodal wearable analysis for capturing the physiological complexity of gait in real-world conditions.

Limitations and future work

The generalisability of these findings is limited by the small sample size (n = 10), the predominance of mild-to-moderate disease severity, and the use of a single-day unsupervised recording. Differences in walking-bout duration across settings may further affect comparability, as shorter or fragmented bouts can alter gait dynamics [11]. Although the use of wearables enabled data collection in real-world settings, potential inaccuracies related to soft-tissue artefact, sEMG signal noise, and sensor placement may have influenced data quality [53]. Ground-truth validation in unsupervised free-living gait assessment is inherently challenging due to the absence of continuously labelled data, which limits direct validation of the supervised ML model under unconstrained settings. To partially address this, the model was trained using supervised data that included not only indoor laboratory walking but also outdoor walking across varied terrains (e.g., asphalt, grass, cobble, and uneven rocky surfaces), thereby approximating the movement variability encountered in free-living conditions. Future work may further mitigate this limitation through targeted in-home validation, ethical use of reference sensing, or semi-supervised learning approaches. While validated indoor algorithms were applied, gold-standard motion capture is not feasible in home environments, where wearable sensors currently represent the most practical and privacy-preserving alternative [54]. Future work should involve larger and more diverse cohorts, including individuals at more advanced disease stages and across ON/OFF medication states, with extended multi-day monitoring. Crucially, the proposed data analysis framework should be further validated analytically against established gold-standard references and clinically through known-groups validity, convergent validity with existing clinical measures, and sensitivity to intervention and medication related changes to establish the reliability and responsiveness of multimodal digital gait biomarkers.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, we introduced a data-driven, multimodal framework that integrates IMU and sEMG signals to characterise gait in PwPD across both supervised and unsupervised settings. By fusing IMU and sEMG signals, the framework achieved improved performance compared with an IMU-only configuration and provided a more holistic characterisation of gait impairment. Notably, multimodal analyses identified differences in knee joint kinematics and muscle activation patterns between supervised and unsupervised walking. These discrepancies may reflect adaptive yet potentially inefficient neuromuscular strategies, where altered muscle recruitment during unsupervised walking may be associated with changes in joint coordination and gait variability. The derived multimodal features thus highlight the potential of multimodal features as digital biomarkers that can complement traditional clinical assessments by capturing subtle, context-dependent fluctuations in motor function that may not be captured in standard laboratory assessments.
