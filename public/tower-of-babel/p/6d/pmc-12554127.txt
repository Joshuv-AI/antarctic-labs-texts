A luminescence-based biosensor to measure endogenous UBE3A activity
Authors: Lei Xing, Sophia U. Lamberti, Hannah C. Nourie, Trinity E. Rust, Wenxin Hu, Mark J. Zylka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLoss- and gain-of-function (LOF and GOF) mutations in the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBE3A cause distinct neurodevelopmental disorders. The AZUL domain of UBE3A binds with low nanomolar affinity to a 45 amino acid (aa) region of PSMD4. We fused this 45-aa sequence to Firefly luciferase to generate a luminescence-based biosensor, Firefly-45aa, that acts as an artificial UBE3A substrate and exhibits exceptional sensitivity in measuring UBE3A activity. Testing UBE3A variants using Firefly-45aa in HEK293T cells revealed distinct biosensor activity profiles, enabling the classification of LOF and GOF mutations based on maximum activity, inhibitory concentration-50, and activity per unit of protein. Some strong LOF mutations had dominant negative activity. In addition, Firefly-45aa can be used to quantify endogenous UBE3A activity in primary cells from Angelman syndrome and GOF mouse models. This biosensor reveals a mutational spectrum of UBE3A enzyme activity and is a sensitive tool for functional characterization and therapeutic development in UBE3A-related disorders.

Introduction

UBE3A is an HECT-domain containing E3 ubiquitin ligase that transfers ubiquitin to its substrate proteins for proteasome mediated degradation or functional modulation.1 Aberrant UBE3A activity is linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, including Angelman syndrome (AS), a severe disorder associated with UBE3A gene deletion and loss-of-function (LOF) mutations.2 The duplication of UBE3A is associated with Dup15q syndrome.3 Recent studies showed that elevated UBE3A activity through gain-of-function (GOF) UBE3A mutations causes additional neurodevelopmental phenotypes distinct from AS in humans and mouse models.4,5 It is thus important to determine if mutations cause loss or gain of UBE3A function to diagnose disease and deliver appropriate treatment.

The deletion of the maternal 15q11.2-q13 chromosome region accounts for about 80% of AS cases, while ∼10% of AS cases carry missense and indel mutations in UBE3A.2 Missense mutations in UBE3A cannot readily be classified as benign or pathogenic in the absence of functional data, especially given that some mutations reduce enzymatic activity while others increase enzymatic activity.4,6 Numerous UBE3A variants curated in the ClinVar database are classified as uncertain significance, likely benign or pathogenic, or conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity. Thus, there is a need to characterize the functional consequences of existing and newly identified UBE3A missense and indel mutations.

UBE3A binds and ubiquitinates components of the proteasome, including PSMD4, a component of the 19S cap.7,8,9,10,11,12,13,14 The N-terminal AZUL domain of UBE3A interacts with a minimal 45 amino acid (aa) long sequence located at the C-terminus of PSMD4.9,13,14 The loss of UBE3A in AS model mice resulted in elevated protein levels of PSMD4, while excessive UBE3A activity in Ube3aT503A mice (harboring an autism-linked GOF mutation) led to a significant reduction in PSMD4 protein levels.5 These data suggest that it might be possible to quantify UBE3A activity by generating an artificial UBE3A substrate based on PSMD4.

Multiple fluorescent protein and luciferase-based assays have been developed to measure levels of polyubiquitylation and proteasome activity by imaging approaches.15,16,17,18,19 Reporters for overall proteasome activity have been generated by tagging a degron, i.e., Ubiquitin-G76V or poly-ubiquitin, to GFP or luciferase.20,21,22 In this study, we fused the PSMD4 45aa peptide to the C-terminus of Firefly luciferase to generate a UBE3A biosensor (termed Firefly-45aa biosensor). We found that this modification rendered luciferase activity highly sensitive to UBE3A E3 ubiquitin ligase activity in cells, and to classify UBE3A LOF and GOF mutations.

Results

The 45aa PSMD4 peptide interacts with UBE3A through the AZUL domain in a ligase activity-independent manner

The N-terminal AZUL domain of UBE3A binds with low nanomolar (Kd = 13.8–22.6 nM) affinity to a 45 aa sequence (aa325-369, GeneBank: NP_001317621.1) located at the C-terminus of PSMD4.14 To validate this interaction and determine whether the interaction is affected by UBE3A activity, we synthesized a biotinylated version of this 45aa peptide and performed in vitro binding assays. We found that the 45aa peptide efficiently pulled down enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)-tagged WT and ligase-dead (LD, C820A) versions of UBE3A, but not EGFP alone (Figure 1). Moreover, the deletion of the AZUL domain (ΔAZUL) completely abolished the interaction, as expected. Firefly luciferase fused to a 45aa PSMD4 peptide serves as a biosensor of UBE3A activity

We hypothesized that it might be possible to render luciferase activity responsive to different levels of UBE3A by harnessing this strong interaction. To test this hypothesis, we fused this 45aa peptide to the C-terminus of Firefly luciferase (Firefly-45aa) and used unmodified Firefly luciferase as the control (Firefly-Control). The three known UBE3A isoforms show distinct cytoplasmic and/or nuclear localization patterns,23,24 which we confirmed in HEK293T cells (Figure S1). We found that the Firefly-45aa fusion protein, like the Firefly-control protein, was localized to the nucleus and the cytosol of HEK293T cells (Figure 2A). We first tested how the Firefly-45aa biosensor responded to the overexpression of three different UBE3A isoforms. We co-transfected HEK293T cells with an increasing amount of plasmid encoding EGFP-UBE3A-WT isoforms and a Renilla luciferase expression plasmid to normalize for transfection variability. Our result showed that all three UBE3A isoforms resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in Firefly-45aa signal with response kinetics that were saturable and fit a sigmoidal dose-response curve (R2 > 0.9). UBE3A isoform 1, the predominant isoform expressed in mammalian cells,24,25,26 reduced the Firefly-45aa signal to a greater extent relative to isoforms 2 and 3 (Figure 2B). Appending sequence to the N-terminus of isoforms 2 and 3 does not interfere with the interaction with PSMD4.7 The overexpression of EGFP-UBE3A-WT (isoform 1) did not affect the signal from the untagged Firefly luciferase control (Figure 2C). The overexpression of a ligase-dead (LD) version of UBE3A (EGFP-UBE3A-LD/C820A, isoform 1) had no effect on Firefly-45aa or Firefly control signal (Figure 2C). We noticed that stronger UBE3A overexpression at 100 ng/well reversed the response trajectory of the Firefly-45aa (Figure 2B), which may be caused by the inhibitory effect of overexpressed UBE3A on proteasome activity.10 This trajectory reversion was more evident when using the CAG promoter, a fusion of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) enhancer and the chicken beta-actin promoter, to drive high levels of UBE3A expression (relative to the CMV promoter in pCMV-Myc-UBE3A vectors, Figures S1B and S1C). Overall, these results suggest that Firefly-45aa functions as a UBE3A activity biosensor.

We and others previously quantified the activity of overexpressed UBE3A variants in HEK293T cells using a β-catenin/Wnt activity reporter (BAR) plasmid.4,10,27,28 When testing the same UBE3A isoform, promoter, and time post transfection, we found that 0.78 ng of EGFP-UBE3A plasmid was sufficient to detect a significant reduction in Firefly-45aa signal (Figure 2D). In contrast, the BAR reporter signal did not significantly increase until 200 ng of EGFP-UBE3A plasmid was transfected (Figure 2D). These data suggest that the Firefly-45aa biosensor is over 200× more sensitive than the BAR reporter at measuring UBE3A activity when this E3 ubiquitin ligase is overexpressed in HEK293T cells.

The Firefly-45aa biosensor is an artificial UBE3A substrate

We next co-transfected the Firefly-45aa biosensor or Firefly-Control into HEK293T cells along with EGFP-UBE3A-WT or EGFP-UBE3A-LD(C820A) and quantified protein levels of the Firefly-45aa biosensor and the Firefly-control by western blotting. An expression plasmid (mCherry) was co-transfected as the normalization control. Protein levels of the Firefly-45aa biosensor (Figure 3A), but not the Firefly-Control (Figure 3B), were significantly reduced in cells overexpressing EGFP-UBE3A-WT relative to EGFP-UBE3A-LD(C820A). UBE3A targets proteins for proteasomal degradation via ubiquitination.1 To determine whether the Firefly-45aa protein was polyubiquitinated by UBE3A, we fused a triple-HA tag (3XHA) to the N-terminus of the firefly luciferase coding sequence to allow for highly efficient capture of Firefly-45aa and Firefly-Control proteins by immunoprecipitation with antibodies against the HA-tag. The addition of the 3XHA-tag did not affect the sensitivity of the Firefly-45aa biosensor in cells overexpressing UBE3A (Figures S2A and S2B). We found that the poly-ubiquitin signal intensity in the 3XHA-Firefly-45aa immunoprecipitants, detected by antibodies against ubiquitin, was greatly increased in cells overexpressing UBE3A-WT relative to UBE3A-LD(C820A) (Figure 3C). Likewise, the 3XHA-Firefly-45aa signal, detected by antibodies against HA-tag, was shifted to the high molecular weight polyubiquitylated fraction in cells overexpressing UBE3A-WT relative to UBE3A-LD(C820A) (Figure 3C). Overall, these results indicate that the Firefly-45aa biosensor is polyubiquitinated by catalytically active UBE3A, similar to endogenous UBE3A substrates.6,10,29,30

Characterization of UBE3A variants using the Firefly-45aa biosensor

Given the greater sensitivity of the Firefly-45aa biosensor over the BAR reporter, and the ability to measure dose-response kinetics, we next evaluated the extent to which the Firefly-45aa biosensor could be used to characterize 53 UBE3A variants, including an AZUL domain-deleted mutation (ΔAZUL), C820A (LD), previously characterized LOF and GOF variants, variants of unknown significance, and two double mutations, T485A/C820A (T485A-LD) and T787A/C820A (T787A-LD). All variants were expressed as EGFP fusion proteins tagged to the N-terminus of UBE3A isoform 1, allowing us to measure EGFP fluorescent intensity as a surrogate for UBE3A protein levels.

We found that 75% (40 out of 53) of the UBE3A variants significantly increased or decreased biosensor activity compared to WT controls at one or more plasmid amounts (Table S2). Among these, the activity of 31 variants significantly differed from WT at the lowest transfection dose (1.56 ng/well) (Figure 4A), further demonstrating the exceptional sensitivity of the Firefly-45aa biosensor. We next examined the kinetics of the Firefly-45aa biosensor signal at different UBE3A plasmid amounts by a nonlinear regression (curve fit, GraphPad Prism 10.3.1) analysis. Of the 53 variants, 15 did not fit a typical inhibitory dose/response curve with a coefficient of determination (R2) <0.75. All 15 variants were strong LOF mutations at all UBE3A plasmid amounts tested (Table 1, see also Figure S3; Table S2). The remaining 38 variants exhibited a typical sigmoidal inhibitory dose/response curve with a high (>0.9) R2, including all 5 UBE3A-WT control replicates (Table 1; Figure S3). The near-perfect fit to a sigmoidal inhibitory dose/response curve allowed us to estimate the maximum activity of these UBE3A variants and UBE3A-WT controls on the Firefly-45aa biosensor and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) (Table 1). We found that these UBE3A variants could be distinguished based on their maximum activity and IC50 relative to UBE3A-WT (Figure S4A). Strong GOF UBE3A variants (T485A, G588E, G738E, and G738R) were associated with increased maximum activity and reduced IC50 relative to WT UBE3A. Mild GOF variants (e.g., T787A, K779T, and R780S) exhibited increased maximum activity, but comparable IC50 values with UBE3A-WT, while GOF variants K679T and N676D exhibited similar maximum activity levels but reduced IC50 values relative to UBE3A-WT. LOF variants (e.g., G20V, S143R, L237P, T106P, and C116R) displayed reduced maximum activity and an increase in IC50, while partial LOF variants I329T and G808D exhibited reduced maximum activity with similar IC50 values relative to WT. Unlike the sigmoidal dose-response curve for the Firefly-45aa biosensor, the protein levels of all UBE3A variants, measured as EGFP fluorescence intensity, linearly correlated to the amount of EGFP-UBE3A plasmid transfected (Figures S3B, S3D, S3F, S3H, and S3J). These data indicate that the protein levels of each UBE3A variant can be expressed as a linear function of plasmid amount. This allowed us to calculate the relative protein level of each UBE3A variant, as the ratio of the slope of a variant to the slope of UBE3A-WT (Table 1). We found that strong LOF variants (e.g., LD/C820A, R477P, M478I, R482P, and E550L) were associated with increased protein levels relative to WT, while other LOF variants (e.g., G20V, C21Y, T106 K/P, C117R, S143R, and D212V) were associated with reduced protein levels. Strong GOF variants (e.g., T485A, Q588E, G738 E/R) were associated with reduced protein levels, similar to previous studies on GOF mutations,4,5,6 likely due to enhanced self-ubiquitination. The overall total UBE3A activity for variants C60R, D122A, I130T, A178T, and E269G was similar to UBE3A-WT; however, the protein levels of these variants were greatly reduced compared to UBE3A-WT controls (Figure S3; Table 1). These data suggest that these variants possess UBE3A activity higher than UBE3A-WT. We previously classified some of these variants (T106P and I130T) as hyperactive self-degraders.6

To estimate the amount of UBE3A activity per unit of UBE3A protein, we divided the total UBE3A activity measured at a sub-saturation dose (6.25 ng) by the protein level of each variant. We found that UBE3A variants with complete or near complete loss of total UBE3A activity were associated with essentially no activity per unit of protein (Table 1; Figure S4B). Interestingly, variants showing elevated UBE3A activity per unit of protein can show reduced maximum UBE3A activity relative to WT (e.g., T106K/P, C117R, S143R, and D212V) or increased maximum activity relative to WT (e.g., T485A, Q588E, and G738E/R), or no change in maximum activity relative to WT (e.g., C60R, D122A, and E269G) (Table 1).

We next sought to determine if principal component analysis (PCA) could segregate variants into distinct classes in an unbiased manner. For PCA, relative EGFP intensity and Firefly-45aa activity, as measured at all plasmid amounts from each variant and all replicates, were included. These data were reduced to two main dimensions: dimension 1 (Dim 1) accounted for 57.2% of the variation, and Dim 2 accounted for 25.7% of the variation. Complete or near-complete LOF variants were co-clustered with LD(C820A) and other known LOF variants (i.e., E550L and M478I) (Figure 4B). All 14 UBE3A variants in this cluster have near zero total UBE3A activity, and their effects on the Firefly-45aa biosensor did not fit a sigmoidal inhibitory dose/response curve (Table 1, see also Table S2). Strong GOF variants T485A, Q588E, G738E and G738R were closely clustered. Common features for this group include increased maximum activity, decreased IC50, reduced protein levels and increased activity per unit of UBE3A protein. Partial LOF variants (e.g., G20V, C21Y, T106P, and D212V) were closely clustered and featured reduced total activity and reduced protein levels, but increased or near WT level of activity per unit of UBE3A protein. Variants T106K, D122A, I130T and E269G were clustered and had near WT maximum activity, reduced protein levels, and increased activity per unit of UBE3A protein. Although not well separated from WT controls, variants C60R, A178T, and K779T were closely grouped with near WT or mild increase in total UBE3A activity, but reduced protein levels and increased activity per unit of UBE3A protein. A nearby variant, T787A, also exhibited similar features but with a stronger increase in UBE3A total activity. Overall, these data suggest that the Firefly-45aa biosensor can be used to characterize UBE3A variants in conjunction with the measurement of UBE3A protein levels.

We noticed some discrepancies between our biosensor results and prior studies using the BAR assay.4 Using the Firefly-45aa biosensor, G738E and G738R mutations behaved like GOF variants, N676D and K679T had weak GOF activity, and N243K was equivalent to WT (all tested as N-terminal EGFP fusions to isoform 1). In contrast, these five variants were classified as LOF using the BAR assay (all tested as N-terminal Myc-tag fusions to isoform 2).4 This discrepancy is not likely due to an isoform mismatch, as the G738E and G738R mutations, when introduced into all three isoforms, showed greater activity relative to WT UBE3A when tested with the Firefly-45aa biosensor (Figures 5A–5C). Several of the complete LOF variants (e.g., R477P, M478I, E550L, and LD/C820A) caused the Firefly-45aa biosensor signal to increase as the amount of UBE3A plasmid transfected was increased (relative to no UBE3A control condition; Figure S3), suggesting they dominantly interfered with endogenous UBE3A. To test dominant-negative activity, we transfected two UBE3A LOF variants into cells at different plasmid amounts while holding the amount of UBE3A-WT constant (Figure 5D). We found that 4× more UBE3A-LD(C820A) and 8× more UBE3A E550L were sufficient to interfere with UBE3A-WT activity on the Firefly-45aa biosensor (Figure 5D). To model the expression of WT UBE3A in an AS patient with a ligase-dead version of UBE3A on the maternal allele (where WT levels can never go above 1:1 relative to the mutant allele if using an unsilencing approach), we also evaluated how increasing the amount of UBE3A-WT plasmid transfected affected the Firefly-45aa biosensor signal when the amount of each UBE3A LOF variant was held constant. To avoid potential confounds associated with weak EGFP dimerization,31 LOF mutations were expressed as EGFP-tagged proteins, and WT UBE3A was introduced as an mCherry-tagged protein. Our results showed that LOF UBE3A variants inhibited the function of WT UBE3A and resulted in an increase in IC50 compared to the EGFP control group at sub-saturating plasmid amounts (Figure 5E). These data suggest that the tested LOF variants have dominant negative activity, with clinical implications (see discussion).

Unlike mature neurons, where UBE3A is predominantly expressed from the maternal allele,26,35 UBE3A is biallelically expressed in mitotic cells, including fibroblasts.36,37 We cultured fibroblast cells from embryos of Ube3aT503A mutant, AS, or UBE3A-YFP mice and their respective littermate WT controls. UBE3A activity was measured following viral transduction with the Ef1α-AAV-Firefly-45aa biosensor vector. We found that the Ef1α-AAV-Firefly-45aa biosensor detected elevated UBE3A activity in fibroblast cells from Ube3aT503A mutant mice (Figure 6J), and decreased UBE3A activity in fibroblast cells from AS mice (Figure 6K) and from UBE3A-YFP mice (Figure 6L) relative to WT controls. Discussion

We developed a highly sensitive luminescence-based biosensor that can measure the activity of UBE3A variants when overexpressed at low levels in HEK293T cells. Importantly, this biosensor can also quantify changes in endogenous UBE3A activity in primary cells. This biosensor makes it possible to characterize disease-associated UBE3A LOF and GOF variants in ways that were not possible before. Previously, our lab pioneered the use of a luciferase-based BAR assay to classify UBE3A LOF and GOF variants when overexpressed in HEK293T cells.6,10 This assay was subsequently used to characterize UBE3A missense mutations on a larger scale.4 In vitro polyubiquitylation assays have also been used to evaluate how UBE3A mutations affect enzyme activity, to identify activators of UBE3A, and to evaluate recombinant UBE3A protein activity.38,39 However, none of these assays can quantify endogenous UBE3A activity in cells. Our studies with primary neurons and fibroblasts from mice harboring monoallelic Ube3a LOF and GOF mutations suggest the Firefly-45aa biosensor might also be capable of quantifying aberrant UBE3A activity in cells isolated from patients. Such an application could facilitate the biochemical diagnosis of neurodevelopmental disorders that are caused by loss or gain of UBE3A activity.

Most of the known LOF and GOF variants that we tested were correctly classified using the Firefly-45aa biosensor, consistent with prior studies (Table 1).4,6 However, we also noted classification discrepancies for a small number of variants relative to the BAR assay4 and in vitro polyubiquitination assays,40 with UBE3A mutations G738E and G738R being the most extreme examples. In contrast to the nearly complete LOF reported by the BAR assay,4 the Firefly-45aa reported strong GOF for both mutations. Our data suggest that such a discrepancy was not due to the use of different UBE3A isoforms. The difference in mechanisms underlying the Firefly-45aa and the BAR may be a contributing factor. The response of the Firefly-45aa relies on a direct interaction between the biosensor and UBE3A, followed by polyubiquitylation and, as our data suggest, biosensor degradation. We cannot rule out the possibility that polyubiquitylation alone disrupts Firefly-45aa luciferase activity. The BAR assay is an indirect readout—as it reports changes in β-catenin signaling when proteasome activity is inhibited by overexpressed UBE3A.10 High (100–200 ng) UBE3A plasmid amount caused a slight increase in the Firefly-45aa signal, likely due to the inhibition of proteasome activity by overexpressed UBE3A (Figures 2B and 2D). This increase in biosensor signal was further enhanced when UBE3A expression was boosted by using a stronger CAG promoter (Figures S1B and S1C). The Firefly-45aa biosensor directly responds to UBE3A activity, whereas it remains unclear how abnormally high levels of UBE3A activity affect the proteasome and hence the BAR reporter. As another difference, UBE3A was expressed as a fusion protein tagged with EGFP in our present study, so that protein levels could be directly measured as EGFP fluorescence. In contrast, the less structured short Myc-tag was used in previous BAR assays.4 We noticed that for a few UBE3A mutations, UBE3A protein levels reported by EGFP-tagged proteins were inconsistent with previous reports by western blot analyses against Myc-tag or overexpressed UBE3A proteins.6,40 Future studies will be needed to determine if the EGFP tag interferes with the function and stability of UBE3A and/or affects UBE3A-effector interactions.

We found that combining the measurement of UBE3A protein levels (via the attached EGFP tag) with the Firefly-45aa biosensor signal in a single assay provided a more efficient way to characterize UBE3A variants. Previously, the measurement of UBE3A activity and protein level was conducted separately, with the latter requiring more laborious western blot analyses.6,40 In addition, the high sensitivity of the Firefly-45aa biosensor allowed us to test multiple doses of each UBE3A variant in parallel in 96-well format. This allowed us to quantify the response kinetics of the Firefly-45aa biosensor to the overexpression of different UBE3A variants. Several unique features of each UBE3A variant could then be quantified, including the maximum activity and the IC50. In conjunction with the measurement of expression levels of UBE3A variants, the absolute UBE3A activity per unit of protein can be calculated. These measures allowed us to detect more subtle effects on UBE3A enzyme activity, where only one measure was affected by a mutation. As an example, the K170N and E766A mutations (among others) showed a steeper initial slope, as measured by a lower IC50 but no change in the maximum activity relative to WT. The C60R mutation showed no change in maximum activity or IC50 but appeared to have increased enzymatic GOF (higher activity per unit of protein), which was balanced by a reduction in protein levels. These mutations would otherwise have been missed with an assay such as the BAR assay, where only high/saturating amounts of UBE3A plasmid are tested.4,6 More recently, Weston et al. show that the UBE3A Q606E mutation, equivalent to human Q588E, had effects on brain development and behavior in mice that were similar to Ube3a loss-of-function.41 However, this Q588E mutation was classified as a GOF mutation in their previous study with the BAR assay4 and was one of the strongest GOF mutations as assessed with the Firefly-45aa biosensor (Figure 4A). This GOF mutation may be so strong that it targets itself for degradation (an intramolecular reaction) over the less kinetically favored enzyme-substrate (intermolecular) reaction.

Our biosensor identified other features of UBE3A that were not apparent in prior studies. Notably, we found that complete LOF UBE3A mutations can dominantly inhibit the function of WT UBE3A (Figure 5D). The restoration of UBE3A levels by gene replacement or unsilencing paternal UBE3A using ASOs, small molecule compounds, or gene editing is being developed as a potential treatment for AS.42,43,44,45,46,47,48 Our result suggests the effectiveness of these treatments may be compromised in patients carrying complete LOF missense or in-frame indel mutations on the maternal allele, even when the expression level of the WT paternal UBE3A allele is restored to the same levels of maternally expressed mutant proteins. In addition, complete LOF UBE3A mutations tend to have enhanced stability because they do not self-ubiquitinate, thus having protein levels higher than endogenous WT protein. This may further exacerbate the dominant negative effect of these complete LOF proteins. Thus, one might expect limited therapeutic benefits when treating patients with complete maternal LOF UBE3A missense mutations, unless UBE3A WT was introduced at an excess amount over the mutant protein, such as using a UBE3A gene replacement vector.46

Limitations of the study

Our experiments indicate that the response of the Firefly-45aa biosensor to UBE3A activity relies on the direct interaction between the biosensor and the AZUL domain of UBE3A. The Firefly-45aa biosensor should thus be sufficient to interrogate a broad range of UBE3A variants that cause changes in UBE3A activity by affecting the catalytic mechanism, protein stability, and/or interaction with the biosensor via the AZUL domain. All previously characterized UBE3A mutations with impaired ligase activity were correctly identified as LOF mutations by the Firefly-45aa biosensor (Table 1).4 UBE3A interacts with other substrates via additional domains.13,49,50 For example, biochemical studies showed that HERC2 interacts with UBE3A through a sequence downstream of the AZUL domain.13,50 Thus, Firefly-45aa may not report changes in UBE3A function caused by mutations that disrupt protein-protein interactions outside the AZUL domain. Some disease-linked missense mutations result in a redistribution of UBE3A from the nucleus to the cytoplasm.25,40 In fact, a large number of UBE3A LOF mutations associated with AS are mislocalized to the cytosol in contrast to the predominant nuclear localization of WT UBE3A.40 The Firefly-45aa biosensor is relatively evenly distributed in the nucleus and cytosol of HEK293T cells and hence may not correctly classify such mutations as pathogenic if they do not also affect enzyme activity or interaction with the AZUL domain. In addition, experiments involving UBE3A overexpression were conducted in HEK293T cells. Endogenous UBE3A protein expressed in HEK293T cells may affect the Firefly-45aa activity and polyubiquitination. Future experiments may need to be conducted in UBE3A-null cells, such as UBE3A knockout HEK293T cells,25 to further characterize UBE3A mutations. Our study highlights an overall approach that could be used to generate additional biosensors to interrogate UBE3A activity, to further examine the functional consequences of UBE3A variants, as well as to interrogate nuclear versus cytoplasmic UBE3A activities.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse models

Mouse procedures were performed according to established protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. All mice were maintained on a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle with water and food provided ad libitum in standard facilities. Wildtype (WT) C57BL/6J mice (#000664), Ube3a knock-out mice33 and UBE3A-YFP knockin mice34 were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and maintained in the lab. Ube3aT503A mice were generated in the lab as previously described.5 Ube3aOE2+ mice which carry two extra copies of human UBE3A transgenes, as described,32 were shared by Benjamin Philpot from UNC Chapel Hill. All mice were maintained on the C57BL/6J background. Timed matings were set up to obtain mutants and control embryos for dissection at embryonic day (E) 15.5. Male C57BL/6J mice were bred with Ube3a+/- females to generate Ube3am-/p+ and control embryos. Male C57BL/6J mice were bred with Ube3a+/T503A females to generate Ube3amT503A/p+ and control embryos. Male Ube3aOE2+ mice were bred with female C57BL/6J mice to generate Ube3aOE2+ and control embryos. Male WT mice were bred with female Ube3a-YFP mice to generate maternal Ube3a-YFP mutant and control embryos. In this study, sex of embryos was not individually determined. All embryos in a litter were dissected to harvest primary cortical neurons and fibroblast cells.

Cell lines

HEK293T cells (#CRL-3216) were purchased from ATCC and maintained in DMEM/FBS supplemented with 1X Pen/Strep.

Primary neuronal and fibroblast cultures

Mouse primary cortical neurons were dissected from E15.5 mouse embryos as described previously.51 Neurons were plated at a density of 50,000 cells/well in poly-D-lysine coated 96-well plates in minimum essential media (MEM, ThermoFisher, #11095080) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ThermoFisher, 10437028) and 1X Pen/Strep. Neurons were allowed to attach to the plate for 2 hours and then cell culture medium was changed to neurobasal medium with 1 X B27 supplement (ThermoFisher, #17504044), 1X GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher, #35050061) and penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics (Pen/Strep, ThermoFisher, #15140122) at 1,000 units/mL of penicillin and 1,000 μg/mL of streptomycin (1X) for long-term culture. To knockdown the expression of UBE3A, WT cortical neurons were treated with ASOs against mouse UBE3A (provided by Benjamin Philpot, ASO-2997) at indicated concentrations at DIV3 and collected on DIV7 to evaluate UBE3A protein levels or measure UBE3A activity.

Fibroblasts were harvested from skin tissues of E15.5 embryos using an established protocol52 and maintained in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM)/FBS supplemented with 1X Pen/Strep. Secondary fibroblast cells were plated in 96 well plates at 20,000 cells/well for experiments.

Method details

DNA constructs

To generated constructs expressing human UBE3A with EGFP-tagged at the N-terminus, DNA sequence encoding WT or existing variants (G20V, T106K, T106P, C117R, I130T, A178T, N243K, S194I, L273F, I329T, R477P, M478I, P482P, T485A, L502P, R506C, E550L,T787A, C820A (LD), T485A/C820A and T787A/C820A) were amplified from pCAG-MYC-UBE3A-ires-EGFP vectors.6 Forward, primer (listed as 5’-3’) GCTCAAGCTTCGATGAAGCGAGCAGCTGCAAA, GCTCAAGCTTCGATGGAGAAGCTCCACCAGTGT and GCTCAAGCTTCGATGGCCACAGCTTGTAAAAGATCA were paired with the reverse primer TCGCGGATCCTTACAGCATGCCAAATCCTTT to amplify human UBE3A isoform 1 (GeneBank: NM_130838.4), 2 (GeneBank: NM_000462.5) and 3 (GeneBank: NM_130839.5), respectively. DNA fragments were inserted into the HindIII and BamHI sites of pEGFP-C1 (Takara/Clontech). Additional UBE3A variants, G20V, R39H, R39C, I47V, C60R, E75Q, A80V, D122V, S143R, T151S, K170N, A174P, D212V, L237P, E269G, L390F, Q588E, G629R, N676D, K679T, G738E, G738R, R740Q, E766A, K779T, R780S, G795E, G796R, G808D and G850D were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using primers listed in Table S1. The AZUL domain of UBE3A was amplified using primers (GCTCAAGCTTCGATGAAGCGAGCAGCTGCAAA and CGGTGGATCCGCGGCCGCTTAGTCACAGAGTTTTGCATTAATC) and the AZUL-domain deleted UBE3A were amplified using primers (GCTCAAGCTTCG CCTCATCCCTCCAAGAAAGG and TCGCGGATCCTTACA GCATGCCAAATCCTTT). pmCherry-UBE3A (isoform 1) was generated by replacing the EGFP tag in pEGFP-UBE3A (isoform 1) with mCherry coding sequence from a pmCherry-C1 (Takara/Clontech) using NheI and HindIII site. The expression of all EGFP- and mCherry-tagged UBE3A variants was driven by the CMV promoter.

Renilla luciferase-expressing construct driven by herpes simplex virus HSV-thymidine kinase (TK) promoter (pRL-TK, #E2241) were purchased from Promega. Firefly-luciferase coding sequence was amplified from BAR-pGL3 vector10 be using primers TATAGGCTAGCCACCATGGAAGACGCCA and ATCTGCGGCCGCTTACACGGCGATCTTTCC, and inserted to the NheI and NotI sites of the pRL-TK vector backbone to generate Firefly luciferase-expressing control construct (named as pFirefly-TK, Firefly biosensor control). Firefly luciferase with the PSMD4 45aa peptide tagged at the C-terminus (pFirefly-45aa-TK, Firefly-45aa biosensor) were generated by splicing PCR using primers (firefly luciferase: TATAGGCTAGCCACCATGGAAGACGCCA and ATCCTCCTCCACGGCGATCTTTCCGCC; PSMD4 45aa peptide: ATCGCCGTGGAGGAGGATGATTACGACG and CGGTAGATCTGCGGCCGCTCAGTCCTTGGTGGCCTGGGA). DNA fragments were inserted into the NheI and NotI sites of pFirefly-TK. An HA tag was introduced to pFirefly-TK and pFirefly-45aa-TK constructs by PCR using primers CCATACGATGTTCCAGATTACGCTGGAGGCGGCGAAGACGCCAA and AGCGTAATCTGGAACATCGTATGGGTACATGGTGGCTAGCCTATAGTGAGT. HA vectors were further amplified using primers GGCTACCCATACGACGTCCCAGACTACGCTGGCTACCCATACGATGTTCCAGA and AGCGTAATCCGGAACATCATACGGGTACCCCATGGTGGCTAGCGGATCTGACGG to generate TripleHA (3XHA) tagged constructs (p3XHA-Firefly-TK and p3XHA-Firefly-45aa-TK) for polyubiquitination assay.

pAAV-hSyn1-EGFP vector (Addgene # 50465) was a gift from Bryan Roth. DNA fragments encoding Firefly luciferase and Firefly-45aa were amplified by PCR and an EcoRI site was introduced using reverse primers CCCATCGAATTCTCACACGGCGATCTTTCCGCCCT and CATCGAATTCTCAGTCCTTGGTGGCCTGGGA, respectively. DNA fragments were inserted into the pAAV-hSyn1-EGFP vector using NcoI and EcoRI sites. Fusion DNA fragments encoding an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) and-the Renilla luciferase were generated by splicing PCR. The IRES DNA fragments were amplified from pCAG-MYC-UBE3A-IRES-EGFP6 using primers CTTGCAGTTTACAACGGGCACAGACAG and TCGAAGTCATGGTTGTGGCCATATTATCAT. The Renilla luciferase encoding DNA fragments were amplified using primers GGCCACAACCATGACTTCGAAAGTTTATGA and TGATCCCGGGGCGGCCGCTTTATTGTTCA. The fusion DNA fragments were digested with SmaI and inserted into the EcoRV sites of pAAV-hSyn1-Firefly and pAAV-hSyn1-Firefly-45aa vectors to generate pAAV-hSyn1-Firefly-IRES-Renilla and pAAV-hSyn1-Firefly-45aa-IRES-Renilla transfer vectors. The Ef1α core promoter sequence was amplified from the pLentiCas9-Blast vector (Addgene #52962)53 using primers TGTGTCTAGATAGGTCTTGAAAGGAGTGGGA and TCTTCCATGGTGGCTAGCACCGGTCCTGTGTTCTGG. PCR products were digested and inserted to the XbaI and NcoI site of pAAV-hSyn1 vectors to generate respective pAAV-EF1α vectors.

PCRs were done using Fusion (New England Biolabs/NEB, # M0530) and Q5 (NEB, #M0491) high-fidelity DNA polymerases. All restriction enzymes were purchased from NEB. All DNA constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing or Plasmidsaurus whole plasmid sequencing.

Firefly/Renilla dual-luciferase assay

To quantify the responsiveness of the Firefly control and Firefly-45aa biosensor to the overexpression of UBE3A-WT or variants in HEK293T cells, a dual luciferase assay was performed with Renilla luciferase (pRL-TK) as the internal transfection control for normalization. The day before transfection, HEK293T cells were plated at 50,000 cells/well in 96-well plates. Transfection mixture was prepared using 300 ng of plasmid DNA mix and 9 μg of PEI transfection reagent (Serochem, #PRIME-P100, prepared in water at 1 mg/ml) in Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher, #31985070). The plasmid DNA mix contains 50 ng of pFirefly-TK or pFirefly-45aa-TK, 50 ng of pRL-TK, indicated amount of EGFP-UBE3A expression plasmid, and the rest supplemented with pCMV-Flag empty vector plasmid to a total of 300 ng.54

Firefly and Renilla luciferase activity was measured 16 to 20 hours after transfection using the Dual-Glo luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, #E2980). Dual-Glo® luciferase buffer was diluted with 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 1:1 ratio and used as the lysis buffer to extract cell lysates and reconstitute Firefly luciferase substrate. To enable the measurement of EGFP signals before the luciferase assay, cells were gently washed with PBS to remove residual phenol red from the cell culture medium. Cells were then incubated with 35 μl of lysis buffer on an orbital shaker for 10 minutes. The EGFP signal was measured using a CLAROstarplus plate reader (BMG Labtech). Then 35 μl of Firefly luciferase substrate was added and incubated for 10 min before measuring Firefly luciferase activity. After the measurement, 35 μl of Renilla luciferase substrate was added and incubated for 10 min to measure Renilla luciferase activity. Both Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a CLAROstarplus plate reader (BMG Labtech). Firefly luciferase activity was first normalized against Renilla activity to correct transfection variability. EGFP signal and relative Firefly luciferase activities were normalized against EGFP-UBE3A non-transfected (0 ng) samples for statistical analyses.

In vitro binding assay

N-terminally methyltetrazine-labeled peptide (EEDDYDVM(Nle)QDPEFLQSVLENLPGVDPNNEAIRNAM(Nle)GSLASQATKD) corresponding to the C-terminal 45aa sequence (aa325-369) of human PSMD4 (NCBI Reference Sequence: NP_001317621.1) was obtained from GenScript. In the synthesized peptide, methionine (M) residues were replaced with Norleucine (Nle) residues to avoid methionine oxidation which may result in changes in the chemical property of the peptide. Methyltetrizine-labeled peptide was incubated with TCO-PEG4-biotin (BroadPharm, #BP-23847) to obtain biotinylated peptides. Residual TCO-PEG4-biotin molecules were removed using SpinTrap G-25 columns (Cytiva) according to the manufacturers protocol.

HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmid expressing EGFP, EGFP-ΔAZUL (AZUL domain-deleted), EGFP-UBE3A-WT or EGFP-UBE3A-LD(C820A). Cell lysates were extracted using binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with 1X protease and 1X phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher, #78440) 20 hours after transfection. Cell lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 16,000 x g for 10 min at 4°C. Cell lysates were incubated with 1 μg of biotinylated peptide for 10 min and biotinylated peptides were enriched using 20 μl of Streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads (ThermoFisher, # 88816) for 10 min and washed three times with binding buffer at room temperature. Total protein lysates and magnetic beads were denatured in 2X Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, #161-0737) supplemented with 2% β-mercaptoethanol for western blot analyses.

Polyubiquitylation assay

To determine whether UBE3A overexpression affects Firefly-45aa protein levels, HEK293 cells were transfected with 1 ug of pEGFP-UBE3A-WT or pEGFP-UBE3A-LD(C820A) and 1 μg of pFirefly-TK or pFirefly-45aa-TK and 200 ng of pmCherryC1, which was used as the transfection control or protein level normalization. Cell lysates were extracted with 1 X radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5 % sodium deoxycholate and 0.1 % sodium dodecyl sulfate) supplemented with 1X protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Sonicated cell lysates were denatured in 2X Laemmli sample buffer supplemented with 2% β-mercaptoethanol for western blot analyses.

For polyubiquitylation experiments, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids expressing 3XHA-tagged Firefly luciferase (3XHA-Firefly) or Firefly-45aa biosensor (3XHA-Firefly-45aa) and pEGFP-UBE3A-WT or pEGFP-UBE3A-LD(C820A). 20 hours post transfection, cells were treated with MG-132 (Sigma, #474790) at 30 μM for 4 hours. Cell lysates were extracted with 1X RIPA buffer supplemented with 1X protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and 30 μM MG-132 and cleared by centrifugation at 16,000 x g at 4°C for 10 min. Cleared cell lysates were then incubated at 70°C for 30 min and centrifugated for 10 min at room temperature. Supernatants were incubated with anti-HA magnetic beads (ThermoFisher, # 88836) for 30 min at room temperature and washed 3 times with 1 X RIPA described above. Total protein lysates and immunoprecipitants were denatured in 2X Laemmli sample buffer supplemented with 2% β-mercaptoethanol for western blot analyses.

Antibodies and western blot analyses

Primary antibody dilutions used for Western blot analyses: rabbit anti-GFP (Novus, NB600–308; 1:2,000), rat anti-HA (Millipore-Sigma, 11867423001, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Firefly luciferase (Thermofisher, PA5-32209, 1:500), mouse anti-Ubiquitin (Invitrogen, 14-6078-82, 1:500) and goat anti-mCherry (Origene, TA150126, 1:5,000). Secondary antibody dilutions used for Western blot analyses are: IRDye 680RD-conjugated donkey anti-mouse polyclonal antibody (LI-COR, c6116–05; 1:10,000), IRDye 800CW-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit polyclonal antibody (LI-COR, c60712–05; 1:10,000), IRDye 800CW Donkey anti-Goat polyclonal antibody (LI-COR, 925-32214, 1:10,000).

Western blots were performed as previously described.5 Blots were scanned using an Odyssey CLx infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences) and analyzed in ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal imaging

Transfected HEK293T cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and rinsed with 1X PBS for three times. Cells were incubated in blocking solution (5% normal donkey serum, 0.3% Triton X-100, 2% DMSO, 0.02% Sodium Azide in 1X PBS) for 30min at room temperature, then incubated with mouse anti-Lamin A/C (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-376248, 1:2000) and rabbit anti-Firefly luciferase (Thermofisher, PA5-32209, 1:500) antibodies in blocking solution for 1 hour at room temperature. Following rinses with 0.1% Triton X-100/1 X PBS (PBS/T) three times, cells were incubated with donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 568 (ThermoFisher Scientific, A10042; 1:1,000) and donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (ThermoFisher Scientific, A32787; 1:1,000) secondary antibodies in blocking solution for 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were then stained with DAPI (1:1,000 in PBS/T) and rinsed with PBS/T three times for 10 min each. Coverslips were mounted onto slides (Fisher Scientific) using antifading polyvinyl alcohol mounting medium with DABCO (Sigma-Aldrich, 10981). Images were collected with a Zeiss LSM 710 scanning confocal microscopes. EGFP fluorescence was imaged without antibody amplification. To quantify fractions of Firefly-45aa and Firefly-Control signals in the nucleus, the signal of Lamin A/C, a marker for the nuclear envelope, was used to draw the mask to define the boundary between the nucleus and cytoplasm.

AAV packaging and transduction

AAV particles were produced and tittered according to a previously published protocol.55 AAV2/1 capsid/serotype plasmid (Addgene #112862) and a helper plasmid (Addgene, #112867, pAdDeltaF6) were gifts from James M. Wilson. AAV particles at 5,000 multiplicity of infection (MOI) were added to primary neurons at 4 days in vitro (DIV) or to secondary fibroblast cultures 24 hours after plating. Three days after AAV transduction, cells were subjected to Dual-glo luciferase assay as described above.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For luciferase assays in HEK293T cells and western blot analyses, n represents the number of experimental repeats. For AAV transduction experiments, n represents the number of animals used for experiments. All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 or Rstudio. Statistical details were reported in figure and figure legends. Results were represented as the mean +/− standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences were considered significant when p < 0.05. p values are listed over bar graphs or in the corresponding figure legends. PCA was performed using the factoextra R package56 and graphic panels were generated using the ggplot2 R package57 in R studio.
