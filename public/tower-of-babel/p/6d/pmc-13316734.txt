Astrocytic Regulation of Aberrant Perineuronal Net Formation in Mecp2‐Null Neocortex
Authors: Ashis Sinha, Angelica M Kowalchuk, Nasim Khatibi, Russell T Matthews, Jessica L MacDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRett syndrome (RTT), caused by mutations in MECP2, is a complex neurological disorder characterized by myriad physiological disruptions, including early closure of the critical period of developmental plasticity and precocious formation of perineuronal nets (PNNs). PNNs are lattice‐like substructures of extracellular matrix (ECM) that enwrap specific subpopulations of neurons. PNNs are essential in the modulation of neuronal plasticity and brain maturation, and their enzymatic disruption can partially restore plasticity in adults and improve memory. Although precocious PNN formation is well‐established in RTT, little is known of the cellular, molecular, or biochemical underpinnings of their precocious formation, or whether precocious PNN formation is due to cell‐autonomous or non‐cell‐autonomous mechanisms. While PNNs form on subsets of neurons throughout the brain, astrocytes secrete many ECM components that form PNNs, and they play a central role in controlling closure of the critical period. We find that Mecp2‐null mouse astrocyte conditioned media induces the expression of the key PNN component Hapln1 and causes enhanced PNN formation on wildtype mouse neurons in vitro, suggesting that Mecp2‐null astrocytes play a key role in the precocious formation of PNNs in RTT. Further, we identify increased expression of HAPLN1 and other PNN/ECM components in the developing mouse Mecp2‐null cortex, and demonstrate that PNNs are structurally and biochemically mature at an earlier developmental stage. These results provide essential insight into the mechanisms and structure of aberrant PNNs in Mecp2‐null cortex and identify potential new avenues for targeted rescue or reversal of the precocious closing of the critical period in RTT. Keywords: extracellular matrix, HAPLN1, neocortical development, neuron–glia interactions, Rett syndrome

Introduction

Rett syndrome (RTT), an X‐linked progressive neurodevelopmental disorder affecting ~1 in 10 000 live female births (Chahrour and Zoghbi 2007), is caused by mutations in the transcriptional regulator MECP2 (Amir et al. 1999; Chahrour and Zoghbi 2007). Girls with RTT develop relatively normally for 6–18 months, after which they undergo a period of rapid regression, with loss of motor skills, including purposeful hand movement, deceleration of head growth, and onset of repetitive, autistic behaviors (Hagberg et al. 1983; Rett 1966). Mecp2 mutant mouse models recapitulate many aspects of human RTT, with one notable difference being that male Mecp2 hemizygous null (Mecp2−/y; KO) mice survive into early adulthood (Guy et al. 2001) and they have been a commonly studied model (Ribeiro and MacDonald 2020). Studies from both mouse and human have found that MeCP2 regulates numerous genes and cellular pathways in a tissue‐ and cell‐type specific manner (Chao et al. 2010; Derecki et al. 2012; Lioy et al. 2011; Samaco et al. 2009; Sugino et al. 2014), and loss of MeCP2 function in defined CNS circuits results in distinct RTT phenotypes (Adachi et al. 2009; Alvarez‐Saavedra et al. 2007; Fyffe et al. 2008; Ward et al. 2011; Wither et al. 2013; He et al. 2014; Nguyen et al. 2013). This proves a significant challenge to the development of therapeutic treatments, and there is currently no effective treatment or cure for this devastating disorder.

While much research has focused on the role of MeCP2 in distinct neuronal subtypes, disruption of Mecp2 in astrocytes is sufficient to drive numerous RTT phenotypes, and Mecp2‐null astrocytes can alter the development and function of wildtype neurons in a cell non‐autonomous manner (Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Dong et al. 2020; Maezawa et al. 2009; Rakela et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2014). Astrocyte conditioned media (ACM) from Mecp2‐null astrocytes leads to reduced neuronal size and complexity of both mouse and human‐derived neurons and alters synaptic development (Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2014), and Mecp2‐null astrocytes lead to distinct transcriptional changes in wildtype neurons (Albizzati et al. 2024). While some differences in the factors secreted by Mecp2‐null astrocytes have been identified (Caldwell et al. 2022; Ehinger et al. 2021), how Mecp2‐null astrocytes alter neuronal development and function and contribute to RTT pathology is incompletely understood.

Astrocytes secrete many extracellular matrix (ECM) components that form perineuronal nets (PNNs) (Carulli et al. 2006; Giamanco and Matthews 2012), and they play a central role in controlling the closure of the critical period (Ribot et al. 2021). PNNs, unique honeycomb‐ or cobblestone‐like structures on the surface of specific subclasses of neurons surrounding synapses on the cell body, predominantly parvalbumin‐positive interneurons in the cortex (Celio et al. 1998; Hockfield and McKay 1983), form coincidently with closure of the critical period for developmental plasticity in the cortex and they are essential in the modulation of neuronal plasticity and brain maturation (Wang and Fawcett 2012). Early critical period closure and precocious formation of PNNs have been identified in multiple brain regions in Mecp2‐mutant mice (Carstens et al. 2021; Krishnan et al. 2017; Krishnan et al. 2015; Lau et al. 2020; Patrizi et al. 2020; Sigal et al. 2019) and postmortem human RTT brains (Belichenko et al. 1997; Carstens et al. 2021), supporting the clinical relevance of the altered PNN formation in Mecp2‐mutant mice. RTT has, in fact, been posited to be a disorder of early critical period plasticity (Picard and Fagiolini 2019).

Although precocious PNN formation and altered cortical plasticity are well‐established in Mecp2‐mutant mice, little is known currently about the molecular composition and structure of the aberrant PNNs formed in RTT, which is essential to develop strategies to specifically rescue their formation and elucidate mechanisms by which they disrupt plasticity. Further, whether the precocious PNN formation on neurons is due to cell‐autonomous or non‐cell‐autonomous mechanisms is not known. We tested the hypothesis that Mecp2‐null astrocytes are deficient in the ability to regulate the timing and structure of PNN development, leading to their precocious and altered formation in RTT. Determining how Mecp2‐null astrocytes alter PNN development in a cell non‐autonomous manner will have broad implications for mechanisms that regulate critical period plasticity, both in wildtype and the Mecp2 mutant contexts.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Syracuse University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Office of Animal Welfare Assurance Number D16‐00405) and adhere to NIH ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were group housed at a maximum of five mice per cage on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle and were given food and water ad libitum. CD‐1 timed pregnant female mice were purchased from Charles River. Female Mecp2 heterozygous mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (B6.129P2(C)‐Mecp2tm1.1Bird/J; RRID:IMSR_JAX:003890) and were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. Genotypes were determined by PCR on genomic DNA as follows: Mecp2 mutant mice, forward primer oIMR1436 5′–GGT AAA GAC CCA TGT GAC CC–3′; reverse primer oIMR1437 5′–TCC ACC TAG CCT GCC TGT AC–3′; reverse primer oIMR1438 5′–GGC TTG CCA CAT GAC AA–3′. Total # of mice used: 79 (Ns for each experiment indicated in figure legend).

Primary Cortical Neuronal Cultures

Timed pregnant CD‐1 (Charles River, 022; RRID:IMSR_CRL:022) dams were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation (1–2 mL per 500 mL enclosed chamber) in a sealed chamber (Piramal Critical Care Inc., Cat#: 66794‐013‐25) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Embryonic day (E)15.5 embryos were decapitated, and cortices were dissected out in dissociation medium (DM) containing MgKyn, glucose, AP‐V (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: A5282), penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen, Cat#: 15070063), and B27 supplement (Gibco, Cat#: 17504044). Brains were pooled and the harvested tissue was dissociated using cysteine (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: C9768) and papain (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: P3125) in OptiMem media (Gibco, Cat#: 51985034). Dissociated cells were plated on glass coverslips precoated with poly‐D‐lysine hydrobromide (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: P0899) at a density of 750 000 cells/mL. Cells were initially plated in Neurobasal based medium (Gibco, Cat#: 21103049) containing Glutamax (Gibco, Cat#: 35050061), fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, Cat#: 50253931), and penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Cat#: 15140122). After 4 h, the plating medium was removed, and growth medium containing Neurobasal (Gibco, Cat#: 21103049), Glutamax (Gibco, Cat#: 35050061), penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen, Cat#: 15070063), N2 (Gibco, Cat#: 17502048), and B27 supplements (Gibco, Cat#: 17504044) was added. Cells were treated with AraC (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat# C6645) from 1 DIV to 3 DIV to remove glia as required. A full media change was carried out on 3 DIV to remove AraC and subsequently, half media changes were carried out every 3 days. ACM treatment was started at 6 DIV with the addition of 20% ACM by volume to individual wells.

Primary Cortical Astrocyte Cultures

Cortical astrocyte cultures were prepared from P2–3 mice brains as described previously (Uliasz et al. 2011). Briefly, individual P2–3 mouse pups were placed in an ice filled container ensuring full contact of the pup with cold surfaces. The pups were allowed to remain on ice for 1–3 min and observed for loss of movement and a lack of response to gentle touch indicating unconsciousness. The pups were then rapidly decapitated, and the heads were transferred to a dish with 100% (v/v) sterile EtOH. Heads were swirled to clean them and subsequently transferred to a dish containing PBS to remove any EtOH. The PBS rinse was carried out one more time to remove any remaining traces of EtOH. Cortices were dissected out in cold dissociation media and meninges were removed. Individual brains were kept separate through dissociation and plating. Tissue was minced and digested using 0.025% Trypsin EDTA for 20 min. Cells were triturated using glass pasteur pipettes and plated on Primaria plates. A full media change was carried out 24 h after plating. Subsequently, cells were given half media changes every 3 days. Once astrocytes reached confluence, cells were treated with AraC (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: C6645) to inhibit microglial growth. 48 h after AraC treatment, cells were treated with 75mM L‐leucine methyl ester (LME; Sigma Aldrich, Cat#: L1002‐10G) to deplete microglia. Conditioned media from cells (ACM) were collected at 12 DIV and stored at −80°C freezer for future use.

Immunocytochemistry

Coverslips were fixed in cold 4% phosphate‐buffered paraformaldehyde (PFA) with 0.01% glutaraldehyde, pH 7.4. Cells were then blocked in screening medium (DMEM, 20% FBS, 0.2% sodium azide) for 1 h, before adding primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following day, Alexa‐fluor conjugated secondary antibodies in screening medium were added to the cells for 2 h before mounting the coverslips using FluoromountG (Invitrogen, Cat. No.: 501128966) containing DAPI for visualizing cell nuclei. Primary antibodies used were mouse anti‐NeuN (Millipore, Cat#: MAB377; RRID:AB_2298772), rabbit anti‐Gad1/Gad2 (Sigma, Cat#: G5163; RRID:AB_477019), mouse anti‐TenascinR (R&D Systems, Cat#: MAB1624; RRID:AB_2207001), goat anti‐HAPLN1 (R&D Systems, Cat#: AF2608; RRID:AB_2116135), rabbit anti‐ACAN (Millipore, Cat#: AB1031; RRID:AB_90460), mouse anti‐ACAN (Bio‐Rad, Cat#: MCA1454G; RRID:AB_10961289), and rabbit anti‐TUJ1 (Cell Signaling, Cat#: 5666S; RRID:AB_10691594).

Quantification

The number of cells positive for each PNN marker was counted from large scan images (1769 μm × 1769 μm) from 3 different areas for each coverslip using a Nikon Eclipse Ni upright microscope (Nikon; RRID:SCR_024835) with a Prime BSI Express sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics; Cat#: 01‐PRIME‐BSI‐EXP) under a 20× objective, equipped with an OptiScan XYZ motorized stage (Prior Scientific, Cat#: CS152DP) to allow for Z stacks, and NIS Elements (Nikon) software (Nikon; RRID:SCR_014329). Counts were performed blinded to condition and verified by an independent investigator.

Immunohistochemistry

Postnatal day (P) 16 pups were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (1–2 mL per 500 mL enclosed chamber) in a sealed chamber (Piramal Critical Care Inc., Cat#: 66794‐013‐25) followed by intraperitoneal injection of Avertin to ensure prolonged anesthesia (1.25% Tribromoethanol solution, dose: 0.04–0.06 cc/g of body weight). Transcardial perfusion was performed with ice cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were dissected and post‐fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, washed in PBS, and then either cryopreserved with increasing concentrations of sucrose in PBS (5%, 15%, 30%) until the brains settled and frozen in Tissue‐Tek O.C.T. Compound (Avantor, Cat#: 25608930) and cryosectioned in 30 μm thick coronal sections, or they were sectioned in 50 μm thick coronal sections on a vibrating microtome following fixation. For both, brain sections were then stored in PBS at 4°C until immunolabeling. As a broad PNN marker, Fluorescein labeled WFA ( Wisteria floribunda agglutinin) from Vector Laboratories Inc. (Cat#: FL‐1351‐2; RRID:AB_2336875) was used.

Biochemical Release Assay

P16, 21, 36, 56 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (1–2 mL per 500 mL enclosed chamber) in a sealed chamber (Piramal Critical Care Inc., Cat#: 66794‐013‐25) followed by intraperitoneal injection of Avertin (1.25% Tribromoethanol solution, dose: 0.04–0.06 cc/g of body weight). Anesthetized animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation followed by rapid decapitation. The neocortex was dissected out and tissue was homogenized in 150 mM sodium chloride and 50 mM Tris with EDTA‐free protease inhibitor tablets (Roche, Cat#: 11836170001; 1 tablet in 10 mL buffer), using a handheld homogenizer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then removed, the pellet washed once and then resuspended in 1 mL buffer. A BCA assay (Thermo‐Fisher, Cat#: 23227) was performed, and protein concentrations were adjusted to 2.0 μg/μL. Samples were treated with 2 μL chondroitinase ABC (0.01 U/ μL, Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: C3667‐5UN) per 100 μL of sample and/or 2.5 mM EDTA for 8 h at 37°C. Samples were centrifuged again at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to separate soluble release (R) fraction and insoluble pellet (P) fractions and subsequently prepared for Western blotting by adding sample loading buffer and heating to 95°C for 5 min.

Western Blotting

Cortical protein homogenates were collected by homogenizing snap‐frozen cortex tissue in homogenization buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1% NP‐40, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS) with protease inhibitors (Roche, Cat#: 11836170001). Samples were then centrifuged at 10000 rpm, 4°C for 10 min. Pellets were resuspended in homogenization buffer and treated with 2 μL chondroitinase ABC (0.01 U/ μL, Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat#: C3667‐5UN) per 100 μL of sample for 8 h at 37°C. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Thermo‐Fisher, Cat#: 23227) and adjusted to 2 μg/μL before being prepared for Western blotting by adding sample buffer and heating to 95°C. Protein samples were electrophoresed on 4%–20% gradient SDS‐polyacrylamide gels (Bio‐Rad, Cat#: 4561093) and transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes. Western blotting was conducted as previously described (Eill et al. 2020; Sinha et al. 2025). Briefly, blots were blocked in 5% milk in low salt TBST (50 mM Tris‐buffered saline pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Tween‐20) and then incubated in primary antibody overnight. Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti‐MeCP2 (Cell Signaling, Cat#: 3456; RRID:AB_2143849), mouse anti‐TenascinR (R&D Systems, Cat#: MAB1624; RRID:AB_2207001), goat anti‐HAPLN1 (R&D Systems, Cat#: AF2608; RRID:AB_2116135), and rabbit anti‐Brevican (B756, provided by Dr. R.T. Matthews). Blots were washed in TBST 3 times then incubated in HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and, after washing 3 times in TBST, were exposed using SuperSignal west pico chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific, Cat#: 34580) or incubated with IRDye 800CW, washed with TBST, and directly imaged (LI‐COR Biotech, Lincoln, NE, USA). Blots were imaged using the LI‐COR Odyssey Fc imaging system (LI‐COR Biotech; RRID:SCR_023227). Loading concentrations were judged by total protein using REVERT Total Protein Stain 700 (LI‐COR Biotech, Cat#: 926–11 015).

RNA‐Sequencing

E15.5 wild‐type neuronal cultures were treated with non‐conditioned media (NCM), Mecp2 WT ACM, and Mecp2 KO ACM and cultured to 12 DIV. Total RNA was extracted using a combined TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat#: 15596018) and RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat: 74104) protocol, as described previously (Ribeiro and MacDonald 2022). Samples were from 4 independent neuronal cultures. RNA samples were assessed for quality and concentration by NanoDrop (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific; RRID:SCR_016517) and Agilent Tapestation (Agilent Technologies; RRID:SCR_018435). Library preparation and RNA‐sequencing were performed by Azenta Life Sciences. Libraries were generated with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina following manufacturer's instructions (New England Biolabs, Cat#: E7770), and sequenced on an Illumina Hi‐seq Series (2 × 150bp) with 20 million paired‐end reads per sample. Sequence reads were trimmed to remove adapter sequences using Trimmomatic v.0.36 (RRID:SCR_011848) and were aligned to the Mus Musculus GRCm38 ERCC reference genome using the STAR aligner v.2.5.2b (RRID:SCR_004463). Gene hit counts were extracted using featureCounts (RRID:SCR_012919) from the Subread package v.1.5.2. Sequence reads underwent batch correction to remove unwanted variation in R using RUVseq ((Risso et al. 2014); RRID:SCR_006263) and differentially expressed genes were calculated using edgeR ((Robinson et al. 2010); RRID:SCR_012802). Genes were considered differentially expressed according to their false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05). Volcano plots were made using GraphPad Prism and gene ontology (GO) performed using ShinyGo 0.8 ((Ge et al. 2020); RRID:SCR_019213).

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software; RRID:SCR_002798) was used to carry out the statistical analyses. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those generally employed in the field. Our statistical tests consisted of two‐tailed t‐test, or two‐way ANOVA or mixed‐effects analysis with Tukey's multiple comparison, or Pearson correlation of multivariate analysis to determine statistical significance among groups. Data distribution was handled as if normal, but this was not formally tested (since potential differences in results would be minor). No outlier tests were performed, and no data points were excluded. All data shown represent mean ± SD. Sample size and statistical test are specified in each figure legend. Final images were assembled into figures using Adobe Photoshop and Adobe Illustrator.

Results

Precocious Formation of PNNs in Developing Mecp2 KO Neocortex

We first confirmed previous studies demonstrating precocious and enhanced staining of PNNs with Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA) on neurons within the male hemizygous null (Mecp2−/y; KO) cortex (Krishnan et al. 2015; Patrizi et al. 2020; Sigal et al. 2019). At postnatal day (P)16, WFA labeling is apparent throughout the Mecp2+/y wildtype (WT) somatosensory cortex, with abundant labeling within the barrel cortex (Figure 1A,A'). WFA labeling in the Mecp2 KO is more extensive throughout the somatosensory cortex with a higher overall intensity (Figure 1B,B'), and there is a significant increase in the number of WFA+ cells in the superficial layers (Layers II‐IV) with a trend to increased numbers in the deep layers (Layers V–VI; Figure 1E). Higher magnification images (insets) demonstrate similar overall structure of the PNNs; however, suggesting that the number of PNNs largely accounts for the increased WFA labeling.

In the female neocortex, there is extensive WFA labeling across the neocortex at P16 with a similar pattern between female Mecp2 WT (Mecp2+/+; Figure 1C') and Mecp2 heterozygous (Mecp2+/−; Het; Figure 1D'), and no significant difference in the number of WFA+ cells (Figure 1F). This difference from the male Mecp2 KO could reflect a different developmental trajectory in female or a sufficient compensation from the approximately 50% of cells that express the wildtype allele of Mecp2 in the female Het. Interestingly, we noted a significant difference in the number of WFA+ cells between the two hemispheres of the same brain in female P16 wildtype mice, which was completely absent in the Mecp2 Het brains (Figure 1G). Prior studies have identified a lack of laterality of PNN development in response to maternal learning tasks in Mecp2 Het brains, which occurs in WT mice (Lau et al. 2020); however, this loss of laterality in PNN formation has not previously been noted during the development of Mecp2 Hets, to the best of our knowledge.

Astrocytes Regulate the Timing of PNN Protein Accumulation on Neurons In Vitro

The precocious PNN formation in the Mecp2 KO neocortex could be due to the altered developmental trajectory of the PV interneurons on which the PNNs form (Patrizi et al. 2020; Rupert et al. 2023) or altered neuronal activity and interaction between the interneurons and excitatory neurons as PNN formation is activity‐driven (Moissidis et al. 2025). In addition to neurons, astrocytes play a role in controlling the closure of the critical period of plasticity (Ribot et al. 2021). Astrocytes secrete many extracellular matrix (ECM) components that form perineuronal nets (PNNs) (Carulli et al. 2006; Giamanco and Matthews 2012), and Mecp2 KO astrocytes disrupt neuronal development (Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Dong et al. 2020; Maezawa et al. 2009; Rakela et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2014). Further, Mecp2 KO astrocytes have a distinct secretome from WT astrocytes (Caldwell et al. 2022; Ehinger et al. 2021), with a significant increase in key ECM components, including HAPLN1, and ECM‐related cellular pathways found in Mecp2 KO astrocyte conditioned media compared to Mecp2 WT (Caldwell et al. 2022). This raises the question of whether altered PNN development in RTT is due to aberrant astrocytic regulation.

To investigate the complex interplay between astrocytes and neurons controlling the development of PNNs, we first examined the formation of PNNs on wildtype primary cortical neurons in vitro, in the presence of astrocytes in comparison to when astrocytes are inhibited (Figure 2). Our previous work indicated that the presence of astrocytes in neuronal cultures delays the formation of aggrecan‐expressing ECM accumulation on neurons (Giamanco and Matthews 2012). Aggrecan is made primarily by neurons and is a key activity‐dependent component of PNNs (Giamanco and Matthews 2012; Matthews et al. 2002; Rowlands et al. 2018), suggesting that astrocytes regulate the precise timing of PNN formation.

To test the developmental timing of PNN formation, astrocytes in E15.5 wildtype cortical cultures were either allowed to proliferate or they were inhibited by the mitotic inhibitor AraC from 1 to 3 days in vitro (DIV) in parallel experiments. We confirmed by immunohistochemistry that AraC treatment led to a significant reduction in the number of GFAP‐positive astrocytes but did not alter the number of neurons (data not shown). We examined the time‐course of expression of key PNN components, as well as their aggregation on the surface of subsets of neuronal cells (Figure 2). We confirmed that key PNN components HAPLN1 and ACAN were located on the surface of TUJ1‐positive neurons, and that they colocalized with WFA (Figure S1), a common marker of PNNs in vivo. However, expression of WFA in vitro occurs only with extended culture times on more mature neurons (Geissler et al. 2013; Hartig et al. 2022), and does not provide a good indication of the initial formation of PNN structures. Further, because the exact ligand bound by WFA is not known, we chose to focus our quantitative analyses on defined PNN components.

At 6 DIV, PNN/ECM components were not readily detected in either condition (data not shown). Starting by 8 DIV, PNN/ECM components were detectable in AraC treated cultures (Figure 2A–C) but not non‐AraC cultures (Figure 2A'–C'). By 12 DIV, there were significantly more WT neurons expressing the PNN/ECM components ACAN (Figure 2A″), HAPLN1 (Figure 2B″), and TNR (Figure 2C″) in cultures in which glial cells were inhibited, in comparison to those not treated with AraC. These data suggest that astrocytes play a key role in the developmental timing of PNN formation on neurons, likely preventing precocious formation of the PNNs.

Mecp2 KO ACM Induces Unique Transcriptional Changes in Wildtype Neurons

How astrocytes regulate the developmental timing of PNN formation on neurons is not known. One possibility is that this phenotype is not the direct result of availability of PNN components from the astrocytes but involves differential activation of signaling cascades in neurons by astrocytes. Unfortunately, the signaling pathways that are key in regulating PNN formation are poorly understood; we thus performed RNA‐seq on wildtype neurons treated with astrocyte conditioned media (ACM) in comparison to control non‐conditioned media (NCM). Based on the timing of PNN formation observed in these cultures, with differences noted with the presence of astrocytes between 8 and 12 DIV (Figure 2), we treated the neurons with ACM starting at 6 DIV and performed bulk RNA‐seq on the wildtype cortical neurons at 12 DIV (Figure 3A). Prior studies have demonstrated that conditioned media from Mecp2 KO astrocytes is sufficient to significantly disrupt the development of wildtype neurons (Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2014); thus we investigated whether Mecp2 KO ACM induces altered transcriptional responses in neurons that could contribute to the disrupted developmental timing of PNN formation.

Astrocyte Conditioned Media (ACM) from Mecp2 KO mice leads to transcriptome changes in cortical neuronal cultures compared to those treated with Mecp2 WT ACM. (A) Schematic of experimental design. We treated E15.5 CD‐1 wildtype cortical neurons with ACM collected from astrocytes cultured from P2‐3 Mecp2 WT or Mecp2 KO cortex, in comparison to non‐conditioned control media (NCM) from 6 to 12 DIV. We confirmed that Mecp2 KO ACM leads to decreased neuronal outgrowth of wildtype neurons by 12 DIV (Figure S2A–C) as previously reported (Albizzati et al. 2024; Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2014). ACM from both Mecp2 WT and Mecp2 KO cultures induced extensive transcriptome changes in neurons in comparison to non‐conditioned media (Figure 3B–D), similar to what was observed in co‐culture experiments (Albizzati et al. 2024). We focused, however, on the transcriptome changes observed with KO ACM in comparison to WT ACM (Figure 3D). We identified 94 differentially expressed genes when comparing neurons treated with KO ACM versus WT ACM, 49 of which were up‐regulated (Figure 3E,F and Table S1) and 45 of which were down‐regulated (Figure 3G,H and Table S1). Gene Ontology analyses identified significant up‐regulation of cytokine categories and GABAergic development (Figure 3F and Table S2), while transcript abundance of NeuN was down‐regulated along with gene ontology categories related to long‐term synaptic potentiation, neurotransmitter uptake, and synapse organization and plasticity (Figure 3H and Table S3). This decrease is consistent with the known disruption in neuronal maturation and synapse development with Mecp2 KO ACM, and the altered development within the Mecp2 mutant brain.

Increased expression of GABA synthetic enzymes and vesicular GABA transporter is also observed in the developing Mecp2‐null visual cortex, correlated with accelerated PV interneuron development and PNN formation (Krishnan et al. 2015). Importantly, reducing GAD67 expression restored WFA+ PNN levels to that of WT in that study, indicating the importance of GAD67 expression in the precocious PNN formation. We confirmed enhanced expression of GAD65/67 and reduced expression of NeuN with Mecp2 KO ACM. Interestingly, protein expression levels of GAD65/67 and NeuN are not significantly different in Mecp2 KO ACM‐treated neurons at 12 DIV (Figure S2D,F), when transcript levels were found to be altered in the RNA‐seq data; however, there is reduced expression of NeuN and enhanced expression of GAD65/67 by 16 DIV with Mecp2 KO ACM (Figure S2G–I), perhaps reflecting a delay in the protein translation. These data, however, align with the RNA‐seq findings and suggest that the neurons treated with Mecp2 KO ACM are altered in their developmental trajectory.

Importantly, Mecp2 KO ACM leads to enhanced expression of the key PNN component Hapln1 (hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1) by wildtype neurons. PNN formation in the early postnatal brain initializes with the upregulation of HAPLN1 and synthesis of the HA backbone and mice deficient in HAPLN1 display severely attenuated PNN structure and formation (Carulli et al. 2010; Kwok et al. 2010). Exogenous over‐expression of HAPLN1 accelerates maturation of CA1 PNNs and produces adult‐like memory phenotypes (Ramsaran et al. 2023). These data suggest that Mecp2 KO astrocytes might lead to enhanced expression of Hapln1 by neurons, contributing to the precocious PNN formation. It is important to note that increased HAPLN1 protein was also identified in Mecp2 KO ACM (Caldwell et al. 2022), suggesting that this important ECM component could be up‐regulated in the astrocytes themselves in addition to inducing the increased expression by neurons.

Mecp2 KO ACM Causes Enhanced HAPLN1 Expression and Precocious Accumulation of PNN Proteins on Wildtype Neurons

To investigate whether Mecp2 KO ACM leads to enhanced HAPLN1 expression and PNN formation in wildtype neuronal cultures, primary astrocyte cultures were prepared from P3 WT and KO cortex with ACM added to wildtype E15.5 cortical neuronal cultures from 6 to 12 DIV, as performed for the RNA‐seq experiments (Figure 3A). The cultures were treated with AraC to eliminate glia from 1 to 3 DIV before treating with ACM from 6 to 12 DIV, after which expression of HAPLN1, and the additional PNN/ECM components ACAN, and TNR was compared between NCM (Figure 4A–A″), WT ACM (Figure 4B–B″), and KO ACM (Figure 4C–C″). We again confirmed that PNN components were detected on TUJ1‐positive neurons and that they co‐localized with WFA in all conditions (Figure S3). Notably, the Mecp2 WT ACM did not significantly alter the number of neurons displaying HAPLN1 (Figure 4D), ACAN (Figure 4E), or TNR (Figure 4F) when compared to control non‐conditioned media (NCM). This could suggest that secreted factors from WT astrocytes do not drive the change observed in PNN formation found with astrocyte co‐culture (Figure 2), and astrocyte‐neuron interactions are needed. Mecp2 KO ACM, on the other hand, led to a significant increase in PNN‐expressing neurons, when compared to NCM and WT ACM, indicating that secreted factors from Mecp2 KO astrocytes can enhance PNN formation, possibly through the regulation of Hapln1 expression within the neurons. The number of HAPLN1 expressing neurons remains enhanced at 16 DIV with Mecp2 KO ACM (Figure S4). Together, these data suggest that Mecp2‐null astrocytes play a key role in the precocious formation of PNNs in RTT, providing essential insight into the mechanisms and structure of aberrant PNNs in Mecp2‐null cortex and the early closure of critical period plasticity observed in RTT.

HAPLN1 Expression Is Increased in Developing Mecp2 KO Cortex

We next investigated whether HAPLN1 expression is also increased in the developing Mecp2 KO neocortex in vivo. While precocious PNN formation and early critical period closure are well‐established in RTT, to date studies in Mecp2‐mutant mice have been limited to detection of PNNs by staining with Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA), a lectin considered to be a standard reagent to detect PNNs, but that does not detect all PNNs. PNNs are composed of the glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan (HA), chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), and secreted glycoproteins, but an understanding of their precise composition is incomplete (for review see (Lau et al. 2013)). We determined that HAPLN1 expression is significantly increased in the developing Mecp2 KO neocortex at P16, relative to WT littermates (Figure 5A–A'). We found that full‐length Brevican (BCAN), another key component of the neural ECM, and PNNs specifically, is also significantly increased while TenascinR (TNR) is not. The 80 kD proteolytic cleavage fragment of BCAN was variable and not significantly different in the Mecp2 KO cortex (Figure S7). In line with the lack of significant overall increase in WFA+ cells in the female Mecp2 Het neocortex at this age, we do not see an overall increase in expression of these ECM components at this age in the female Het (Figures S5 and S9).

Altered Developmental Trajectory of PNN Structure in the Mecp2 KO Neocortex

There are no significant differences in the overall expression of the three PNN/ECM components at P21 in the Mecp2 KO (Figure 5B'), perhaps reflecting the increased expression occurring in the WT as PNNs are forming. However, neural ECM, including the highly organized PNN structure, undergoes dynamic changes during development. PNN components are immobilized on the surface of the cell by two independent types of interactions; PNN formation initializes with the synthesis of a hyaluronic acid (HA) backbone and HAPLN1 expression (Carulli et al. 2010), followed by a Ca2+ dependent incorporation of other PNN components, including ACAN, TNR, and RPTPζ (Carulli et al. 2010; Eill et al. 2020; Sinha et al. 2023). Although TNR expression is not altered in the P16 KO cortex, as HAPLN1 and full‐length Brevican are, TNR is a key ECM organizer known to promote the assembly and incorporation of ECM molecules via the clustering of CSPGs (Bruckner et al. 2000; Haunso et al. 2000; Morawski et al. 2014; Suttkus et al. 2014). TNR associates with the cell membrane by its interaction with HA via its clustering of CSPGs and through Ca2+ mediated protein–protein interactions. Although the identity of the TNR membrane receptor has not been definitively identified (Liao et al. 2008; Sinha et al. 2023; Viapiano and Matthews 2006), TNR has been found to bind to multiple synaptic membrane proteins, including IgSF8 (Apostolo et al. 2020) and in a Ca2+ regulated manner to CALEB/CSPG5 (Schumacher et al. 2001) and RPTPzeta/phosphacan (Sinha et al. 2023). We thus sought to determine if the developmental trajectory of TNR incorporation is disrupted in the Mecp2 KO neocortex.

To first establish the developmental timing of incorporation of TNR in the neocortex, we isolated membrane fractions from P21, P36 and P56 cortex homogenates. We disrupted the HA backbone using the enzyme chondroitinase (ChABC) and determined the ratio of TNR in the solubilized (released, R fraction) fraction relative to the pelleted fraction (i.e., proteins still attached to the membrane, P fraction) by Western blots (Figure S8). These were then compared to the release that occurred with the combination of ChABC and EDTA to additionally disrupt the Ca2+ dependent protein–protein interactions (Figure 6A). We confirmed that most proteins were retained in the pelleted fraction by examining the relative total protein between the two fractions, and confirmed that the membrane protein Na+/K+ ATPase was retained in the pelleted fraction (Figure S6). The release of TNR with ChABC treatment from wildtype cortex shows a gradual decrease in solubility from P21 to P56, indicative of its incorporation into the ECM along with ECM maturation (Figure 6A,A'); the release of TNR at P21 is significantly greater than that at P36 and P56 (Figure 6A'). This decreasing trend in release of TNR, however, is not observed when ChABC treatment is combined with EDTA indicating that TNR increasingly associates with the cell membrane in a Ca2+ dependent mechanism during development (Figure 6C'). As an additional control, we examined HAPLN1, an ECM protein known to associate with the cell surface predominantly via its interaction with HA (Figure 6B) and not secondary Ca2+‐dependent protein–protein interactions. HAPLN1 release is comparable between treatments with ChABC and ChABC along with EDTA (Figure 6B'), which is consistent with the model that HAPLN1 is incorporated into the ECM in a predominantly HA‐dependent manner (Figure 6C').

We next compared the release of TNR and HAPLN1 between Mecp2 WT and KO cortex at P21 (Figure 7A,B), when TNR demonstrates incomplete developmental Ca2+ dependent incorporation in the WT. We found that the release of TNR with ChABC treatment from Mecp2 KO cortex was significantly lower than that of WT cortex (Figure 7A–A'), although this difference was eliminated when ChABC treatment was combined with EDTA as noted in the WT. Together, these studies suggest not only a precocious formation of PNNs but also structural maturation of PNNs in the Mecp2 KO neocortex.

Discussion

Our findings identify a non‐cell‐autonomous role for astrocytes in the precocious formation of PNNs and structural maturation of ECM during postnatal development in the Mecp2 KO cortex. PNNs form the most intricate and conspicuous extracellular macromolecular structures in the CNS. They are essential in the modulation of neuronal plasticity and brain maturation (Wang and Fawcett 2012). PNNs form precociously and the critical period of plasticity closes prematurely in RTT, likely limiting the developmental refinement of circuitry. The Mecp2 KO visual cortex displays accelerated maturation of parvalbumin (PV) interneurons (Patrizi et al. 2020), accelerated onset and closure of the critical period for ocular dominance plasticity (Krishnan et al. 2015), and precocious appearance of PNNs (Krishnan et al. 2015; Patrizi et al. 2020; Sigal et al. 2019). Similarly, PNNs form precociously and remain enhanced in CA2 of the hippocampus of Mecp2 KO mice, prematurely restricting LTP plasticity (Carstens et al. 2021). PNNs are also aberrantly increased in auditory and somatosensory cortex of female heterozygous (Mecp2+/−) mice in response to learned maternal behavior (Krishnan et al. 2017; Lau et al. 2020). Preventing PNN enhancement during this learned behavior improves the deficient pup retrieval behavior in Mecp2+/− mice, suggesting that the increased PNNs inhibit auditory plasticity that is necessary for this learned behavior (Krishnan et al. 2017).

The enzymatic disruption of PNNs can partially restore plasticity in adults and improve memory (Bruckner et al. 1998; Pizzorusso et al. 2002; Romberg et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2015), highlighting the exciting potential to modulate PNN development and structure to prevent the precocious closure of the critical period plasticity, or to reopen it, toward improvement of RTT phenotypes. PNNs are composed of the glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan (HA), chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), and secreted glycoproteins, but an understanding of their precise composition is incomplete (for review see (Lau et al. 2013)). To date, studies in Mecp2‐mutant mice have been limited to detection of PNNs by staining with Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA), a lectin considered to be a standard reagent to detect PNNs, but that does not detect all PNNs. WFA most likely binds to a chondroitin sulfate epitope dependent on the CSPG aggrecan; however, the precise mechanism of its binding to PNNs is unclear. Further, current studies have employed injections of chondroitinase ABC to digest the HA backbone of PNNs and reopen the critical period; this is a sledgehammer approach that disrupts the ECM broadly. There is little understanding of the molecular composition and structure of the aberrant PNNs formed in RTT, which is essential to develop strategies to specifically rescue their formation and elucidate mechanisms by which they disrupt plasticity.

Our data demonstrate precocious Ca2+‐dependent incorporation of TNR into the ECM by P21 in the Mecp2 KO cortex (Figures 6 and 7). TNR promotes the assembly of the ECM in the CNS, including PNNs, via clustering of CSPGs, and TNR null animals also show disrupted PNN structure (Bruckner et al. 2000; Haunso et al. 2000; Suttkus et al. 2014; Morawski et al. 2014). In the Mecp2 KO cortex, PNNs display the structural maturation of those found in a P36 or later wildtype neocortex. These data extend our understanding of the precocious PNN formation identified previously with WFA, indicating enhanced structural maturation of the PNNs in addition to increased formation. Understanding what drives both the precocious formation and maturation will be necessary to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Another key component is HAPLN1, which shows enhanced expression in the P16 Mecp2‐null cortex (Figure 5). Mice lacking Hapln1 show distinct disruptions to PNN development from Tnr‐null animals (Carulli et al. 2010; Eill et al. 2020; Haunso et al. 2000; Kwok et al. 2010; Morawski et al. 2014). HAPLN1 plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the HA backbone and cross‐linking lectican family members, and exogenous over‐expression of HAPLN1 accelerates maturation of CA1 PNNs and produces adult‐like memory phenotypes (Ramsaran et al. 2023). Recent studies employing tagged HAPLN1 demonstrate more widespread detection of ECM and PNN structures around many neurons, including excitatory neurons (Lemieux et al. 2023; Sterin et al. 2024), suggesting more extensive roles for HAPLN1 in the neural ECM and brain maturation. Modulation of HAPLN1 expression may, therefore, offer a potential therapeutic target for more focused prevention or reversal of the aberrant PNN formation in RTT. Importantly, we identified increased expression of Hapln1 by wildtype neurons in response to Mecp2 KO ACM treatment (Figure 3), and HAPLN1 is increased in Mecp2 KO ACM (Caldwell et al. 2022), indicating that modulation of HAPLN1 expression will likely require targeting of Mecp2 KO astrocytes.

Interestingly, HAPLN1 promotes pro‐inflammatory factors including IL‐6 and NF‐κB (Chen et al. 2022). Our RNA‐seq data identified up‐regulation of inflammatory cytokine production among the top Gene Ontology categories up‐regulated by wildtype neurons in response to Mecp2 KO ACM, in comparison to Mecp2 WT ACM (Figure 3). Further, a recent study indicates that IL‐6 is the primary astrocytic factor that leads to disrupted synapse development in neurons cultured with Mecp2 KO astrocytes (Albizzati et al. 2024). Our prior studies identified aberrant NF‐κB signaling in Mecp2‐mutant cortex and demonstrated improved neuronal complexity phenotypes of Mecp2‐null neurons with NF‐κB inhibition, both in vivo and in vitro (Kishi et al. 2016; Ribeiro et al. 2020). As neuroinflammation and oxidative stress disrupt PNNs (Cardis et al. 2018; Dwir et al. 2020; Phensy et al. 2020), these data suggest that reducing NF‐κB and IL‐6 signaling could also ameliorate PNN phenotypes. Further, they highlight astrocyte‐neuron crosstalk as a key target.

Secreted factors from Mecp2 KO astrocytes significantly disrupt neuronal development (Ballas et al. 2009; Caldwell et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2014), including neuronal complexity and PNN/ECM formation (Figure 4). Interestingly, we found in a co‐culture that wildtype astrocytes slowed the developmental trajectory of accumulation of PNN components on neurons, in comparison to cultures where astrocytes were largely eliminated (Figure 2). However, Mecp2 WT ACM did not significantly alter PNN/ECM formation on neurons when compared to control media, while Mecp2 KO ACM led to enhanced PNN/ECM formation on wildtype neurons by 12 DIV. These data suggest that secreted factors from Mecp2 KO astrocytes specifically enhance PNN/ECM formation. The differential impact of co‐culture with astrocytes versus secreted factors in ACM can also be observed in the recent study by Albizzati et al., where they found that enhanced secretion of IL‐6 from Mecp2 KO astrocytes only occurred with co‐culture with neurons (Albizzati et al. 2024). Thus, enhanced PNN/ECM formation on neurons is likely the result of a complex interplay between astrocytes and neurons.

It will be important to determine the signaling factors from Mecp2 KO astrocytes that lead to the altered PNN formation. Mecp2 KO ACM contains a significant increase in proteins associated with ECM and ECM remodeling (Caldwell et al. 2022; Ehinger et al. 2021), including increased HAPLN1 (Caldwell et al. 2022) and RPTPRζ (Ehinger et al. 2021). Mecp2 KO ACM also induces significant transcriptional changes in the neurons (Figure 3), including up‐regulation of Hapln1. Thus, the increased PNN development could be the result of increased presence of astrocyte‐derived PNN components combined with an up‐regulation of PNN components by the neurons. In addition to direct modulation of ECM and PNN components, the Mecp2 KO ACM might also lead to a change in the developmental timing or trajectory of the neurons, in turn leading to enhanced PNN formation. For example, we found that Mecp2 KO ACM leads to a significant increase in the expression of genes associated with GABAergic development (Figure 3). Reducing increased GAD67 expression in the developing Mecp2‐null visual cortex restored WFA+ PNN levels to that of WT (Krishnan et al. 2015), indicating the importance of GAD67 expression to the precocious PNN formation. Our data suggest that secreted factors from astrocytes lead to this up‐regulated expression of GAD65/67 that could, in turn, alter the developmental trajectory of the PNNs.

Previous studies have also identified a cell‐autonomous role for the PV interneurons themselves in the formation and/or maintenance of PNNs. Crossing Mecp2‐null mice with mice heterozygous for Gad1 (encoding GAD67) prevented the increased number of WFA+ cells in V1 at P15 (Krishnan et al. 2015). Further, a PV‐specific knockout of Mecp2 using the Pvalb‐Cre led to enhanced WFA‐positive PNNs in the adult visual cortex (Patrizi et al. 2020). It should be noted that full removal of Mecp2 from PV interneurons is not complete until P90 (Durand et al. 2012) and PNNs were analyzed at P200, so it is not clear if Mecp2 is necessary in PV interneurons for the precocious developmental formation of PNNs or plays a role in their enhancement or maintenance (Patrizi et al. 2020). Using the same PV‐Cre line, Rupert et al. (Rupert et al. 2023) demonstrated that loss of Mecp2 in PV interneurons was sufficient to recapitulate the enhanced PNN formation in the adult female Het auditory cortex in response to a learned pup retrieval task. These studies indicate that Mecp2 expression in PV interneurons cell‐autonomously contributes to PNN formation and/or maintenance in the adult brain. It will be interesting to determine if Mecp2 expression in PV interneurons is necessary to prevent precocious formation and, conversely, whether Mecp2 expression in astrocytes plays a role in the adult formation/maintenance of PNNs.

The current studies focused on male Mecp2 KO mice to gain an understanding of how MeCP2 regulates the formation of PNNs. While we did not see a significant overall increase in WFA+ cells in the more clinically‐relevant female Mecp2 Het neocortex at P16, we did see a lack of lateralization of PNN development between the hemispheres. It will be important, however, to extend these studies to determine whether the changes are cell‐autonomous in the context of the cellular mosaicism of the heterozygous brain, where approximately half the cells express the wildtype allele and half the mutant allele due to random X‐inactivation. Taken together, however, our data demonstrate that Mecp2‐null astrocytes play a key role in the precocious formation of PNNs in RTT. These results provide essential insight into the mechanisms and structure of aberrant PNNs in Mecp2‐null cortex and identify potential new avenues for targeted rescue or reversal of the precocious closing of the critical period in RTT.
