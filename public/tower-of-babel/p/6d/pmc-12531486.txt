3D spatial sampling to quantify morphologic heterogeneity in isocitrate dehydrogenase-wildtype glioblastoma
Authors: Viva Voong, Sol Beccari, Elaheh Hashemi, Birgit Kriener, Radhika Mathur, Marisa Lafontaine, Anny Shai, Janine M Lupo, Edward F Chang, Shawn L Hervey-Jumper, Mitchel S Berger, Sebastian M Waszak, Joseph F Costello, Joanna J Phillips
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in digital pathology and machine learning have the potential to revolutionize diagnostic neuropathology. Current brain tumor models are typically trained and validated using morphologic features from a single hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slide per patient. Yet, brain tumors such as diffuse glioma are known for their epigenetic, genetic, and transcriptional heterogeneity within an individual patient. The impact of this heterogeneity on model accuracy and development is unknown. To quantitatively investigate morphologic intratumoral heterogeneity in glioblastoma (GBM), we acquired 92 regionally distinct samples representing maximal tumor sampling across 10 patients with isocitrate dehydrogenase-wildtype GBM and quantified cell density, nucleus area, and nucleus circularity from whole-slide scanned images of H&E-stained slides. All 3 parameters exhibited significant morphologic variation between tumors from different patients and within a given tumor. To identify potential drivers of this variation, tumor-level and sample-level mutation profiling was performed. Mutations in tumor protein 53 both at the tumor level and the sample level had larger nuclear area and decreased nuclear circularity. Morphological features were not associated with regional location within the tumor. Accurate and robust H&E-based models to improve diagnosis and disease prognostication may require training sets that incorporate multiple spatially distinct samples per patient.Keywords: digital pathology, GBM, intratumoral heterogeneity, spatially distinct, TP53

INTRODUCTION

Advances in digital pathology and machine learning (ML) have inspired applications in pathology to advance care, specifically as diagnostic and potentially predictive tools.1 Critical to the success of these efforts, accurate and robust models are needed that reflect the heterogeneity of the disease.

The morphologic features of the tumor and the tumor microenvironment remain critical for histologic diagnosis and disease prognostication. In diffuse gliomas, nuclear atypia, including nuclear circularity and nuclear area, cellularity, and vascular structures are useful in histologic grading and for tumor stratification using ML approaches.2–4 In renal clear cell carcinomas, cancer cell morphology and the presence of nonimmune structures, including the vasculature, have been associated with outcome.5–8 In triple negative breast cancer, tumor cell nucleus area and eccentricity have been associated with patient survival.9 In early stage nonsmall cell lung cancer, nuclear shape, nuclear orientation, texture, and tumor architecture were useful to predict recurrence.10 Deep learning frameworks that utilize morphologic features extracted from whole-slide hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained images could be particularly impactful and assist with triaging cases for consultation or for advanced molecular testing.11,12 These models may also identify patients who require more aggressive therapy or for whom clinical trial enrollment may be an option. For example, in brain tumors, a deep learning framework has been developed that uses H&E images to predict DNA methylation and classify tumor types.13 Robust and accurate models based on evaluation of H&E slides could also be impactful in resource-poor settings.

Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wildtype glioblastoma (GBM) (CNS WHO grade 4) are aggressive tumors with characteristic histologic and/or genetic alterations that can include telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter mutation, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene amplification, and gain of chromosome 7 and loss of chromosome 10. Additional alterations commonly observed include EGFR mutation and/or intragenic deletion, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase p110α catalytic subunit mutation, and deletions or inactivating mutations in tumor protein 53 (TP53), neurofibromatosis type 1, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A/B.14 Despite alterations in common pathways, individual tumors are highly heterogenous with epigenetic, genetic, and transcriptomic differences across individual patients.15 Glioblastoma exhibits striking intratumoral heterogeneity.16–18 For example, amplification of platelet-derived growth factor receptor A, EGFR, and mesenchymal epithelial transition can co-occur in the same tumor, yet in independent tumor cells in a mutually exclusive fashion.19 Dynamic and reciprocal crosstalk with nonneoplastic cells in the brain tumor microenvironment further contribute to intratumoral heterogeneity.20,21

The clinical diagnosis of IDH-wildtype GBM relies on the histopathologic evaluation of multiple tumor regions and molecular profiling. In contrast, most H&E-based ML models for GBM have been built using data from a single H&E-stained slide per tumor. Given the demonstrated genetic, transcriptomic, epigenetic, and environmental heterogeneity in GBM, we evaluated the impact of intratumoral heterogeneity on 3 morphologic features that are commonly extracted by these models across a range of tumor types.2–10 Analysis of 92 regionally distinct tumor samples from 10 patients with newly diagnosed IDH-wildtype GBM demonstrated significant intertumoral and intratumoral differences. As somatic mutations at the tumor- and sample-level may help drive this heterogeneity, our data suggest robust, clinically useful artificial intelligence (AI)/ML algorithms will require consideration of intratumoral heterogeneity.

METHODS

Acquisition of 3D spatially mapped human GBM samples

For all adult patients with suspected diffuse glioma with written consent for tissue acquisition, a UCSF Brain Tumor Center Biorepository staff member was present in the operating room for tissue acquisition following strict standard operating procedures (ethics approval number 10-01318). As previously described, using a 3D spatial sampling approach and surgical neuronavigation, 103 spatially distinct samples representing maximal tumor diversity were acquired from 10 patients with newly diagnosed, treatment naïve IDH-wildtype GBM (CNS WHO grade 4).17,22 From each of 10 patients between 8 and 19 samples were acquired. The 3D spatial maps of the GBM analyzed were previously published and are publicly accessible on an interactive online platform (https://3d-gbms.shinyapps.io/search/) that enables 360° visualization of user-selected features.17 As previously described, the relative distance from tumor centroid is defined by the minimum sample distance from tumor centroid divided by the sum of the minimum sample distance from tumor centroid and the minimum sample distance from tumor periphery.17

Hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections and whole-slide images were reviewed by a neuropathologist (J.J.P.) and tumor content was estimated on a scale of 0%-100% based upon cellular and nuclear morphology and cellular architecture. Of the 103 samples obtained, 11 were excluded due to the absence of viable tumor cells. The remaining 92 samples were analyzed, including 5-19 samples per patient. For analyses focused on identifying potential drivers of morphologic heterogeneity, an additional 17 tumor samples with tumor content estimated to be less than 60% were excluded.

All studies using human biospecimens were in accordance with the ethical standards of the UCSF Human Research Protection Program Institutional Review Board and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Association and its later amendments.

Molecular profiling

As part of routine clinical care, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on a single tumor region from a single diagnostic tissue block, as described previously.17 This test was developed and its performance characteristics determined by the UCSF Clinical Cancer Genomics Laboratory, which is certified by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Act of 1988. This tumor-level profiling, currently used to molecularly profile a tumor, was used to categorize a tumor as TP53 wildtype or mutant, PTEN altered (mutant or homozygous deleted), or EGFR amplified. In addition to tumor-level profiling, we also performed sample-level profiling for TP53 mutation using data from whole exome sequencing from flash-frozen tumor samples adjacent to the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) material used for morphologic analysis. Based on these data, each sample was designated as TP53 wildtype or mutant. As reported previously, spatially mapped samples were divided such that one portion was used for whole exome sequencing and the adjacent piece was formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded for morphologic analysis.17 Summaries of data from both clinical NGS and whole exome sequencing were previously published and are publicly accessible.17

Quantification and analysis of H&E-stained whole-slide images

Whole-slide images of H&E-stained slides were obtained with a Zeiss Axioscan 7 slide scanner with 20× objective. The cell detection feature of QuPath, version 5.1, was used to quantify cell density, nucleus area, and nucleus circularity. Using QuPath software, nucleus area and nucleus perimeter were measured based upon hematoxylin staining; nuclear circularity was calculated using the formula [4π×nucleus area/(nucleus perimeter2)]. A calculated value of 0 represented a nucleus with minimal circularity and 1 represented a nucleus with maximal circularity. To optimize cell identification, the nucleus area was restricted to a minimum of 10 μm2 and a maximum of 75 μm2. An intensity threshold was set to approximately 0.2 for whole-slide images, with slight modifications to select slides. A cell expansion of 5.0 μm was used to expand the detected nucleus to approximate the full cell area. Specific colors for H&E staining were defined for each sample. Tissue regions within individual slides with extensive blood or necrosis were excluded from quantification.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. To evaluate morphologic features between molecular subgroups, nonparametric t-tests, specifically Mann–Whitney tests, were conducted. To compare morphologic features within a patient relative to the centroid, nonparametric ANOVA tests, in particular Kruskal–Wallis tests, were conducted. A linear regression analysis was performed to assess the relationship between morphological features and relative centroid distance. Hyperbolic nonlinear regression analysis was performed to assess the relationship between the normalized mean difference in morphologic feature and sample number.

RESULTS

Using a novel 3D spatial sampling approach and surgical neuronavigation,17 92 regionally distinct samples containing viable tumor cells representing maximal tumor diversity were acquired from 10 patients with newly diagnosed, treatment naïve IDH-wildtype GBM (CNS WHO grade 4). For each patient, quantitative features were extracted from whole-slide H&E digitized images from each of the 5-19 spatially mapped samples per patient (Table S1). Three features commonly extracted from H&E images across a range of tumor types2–10 were analyzed further: cell density, nucleus area, and nucleus circularity. Across the 10 patients, the mean patient-level value for each morphologic feature exhibited significant variation (Figure 1A-C). Given the potential impact of differences in tumor cell content on cellular morphology, samples with a tumor content estimated to be less than 60% were excluded for specific analyses, as noted (Table S1). Despite this exclusion, 5-17 samples were evaluated per tumor and all tumors retained significant heterogeneity with respect to cell density, nuclear area, and nuclear circularity (Figure S1).

Glioblastomas are molecularly heterogeneous.14 As this heterogeneity may contribute to morphologic heterogeneity between tumors, we investigated tumor-level molecular alterations, as determined by clinical NGS. Three alterations were identified in a sufficient number of tumors to permit stratification: PTEN alterations (mutation or homozygous deletion) (n = 6); EGFR amplification (n = 5); and TP53 mutation (n = 4). Mutations in TP53 were associated with increased nuclear area, decreased nuclear circularity, and showed a trend for increased cell density (Figure 2A-C). In contrast, no associations were observed between cell morphology and PTEN alteration or EGFR amplification (Figure S2).

Intratumoral heterogeneity

Individual tumors demonstrated significant morphologic variation across spatially distinct regions (Figure 1A-C). For example, SF12794 demonstrated differences in nuclear area across the 9 samples analyzed (Figure 3). We next investigated factors that may contribute to this morphologic heterogeneity.

ANOVA analysis with Kruskal–Wallis multiple comparisons utilizes the sample with smallest relative centroid distance as the control. ns, denotes nonsignificant, ****P < .0001. First, we evaluated the impact of regional location of a sample within the tumor on morphologic features. The relative distance to the tumor centroid was calculated as the relative distance of each sample between the MRI-defined tumor centroid (0) and periphery (1).17 Neither cell density, nuclear area, nor nuclear circularity were associated with distance relative to the tumor centroid (Figure S3A-C, Table S2). Next, we evaluated the impact of intratumoral molecular heterogeneity. At the tumor-level, somatic mutations in TP53 were associated with increased nuclear area and decreased nuclear circularity (Figure 2). As TP53 alterations can be heterogeneous within a tumor,17 we stratified each spatially distinct tumor sample based on TP53 mutation calls from whole exome sequencing.17 At the sample-level, TP53 mutations were associated with increased nuclear area and decreased nuclear circularity (Figure 4). Together these data suggest that TP53 mutations contribute to morphologic heterogeneity in IDH-wildtype GBM at both the tumor level and the sample level.

Given the degree of morphologic heterogeneity, we investigated the number of samples required to capture this variation within a given tumor. Conducting a series of in silico downsampling experiments, we found that although increased morphologic heterogeneity was detected with each sample taken, the rate of benefit to detect this heterogeneity appeared to plateau for many tumors with approximately 9-10 samples (Figure S4A-C).

DISCUSSION

The implementation of ML tools in neuropathology has the potential to improve the accuracy and rapidity of disease diagnosis and stratification. Current diagnostic neuropathology relies on the evaluation of multiple regions from a resected tumor. Yet, current deep learning training sets and algorithms typically analyze digitized histopathology slides from a single sample per patient. Diffuse gliomas, including GBM, demonstrate genomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and transcriptomic heterogeneity across patients and within an individual patient.23 How this heterogeneity impacts disease outcome or therapy response is unknown but AI/ML models that can incorporate this variability may be particularly powerful for predicting treatment responses. Across a range of tumors, morphological features extracted from digitized H&E images such as cell density, nucleus area, and nucleus circularity have been used to stratify patient tumors.2–11,24,25 Comparing these features across a cohort of 92 spatially distinct samples representing maximal tumor diversity from 10 newly diagnosed IDH-wildtype GBM, we identified significant intratumoral and intertumoral heterogeneity. While neither sample number nor regional location within the tumor could explain these differences, molecular alterations in TP53 were associated with both intertumoral and intratumoral morphologic heterogeneities.

The observed variation in cell density, nucleus area, and nucleus circularity across patient samples highlights the importance of large and diverse training sets to accurately train ML models. In addition, feature evaluation in multiple spatially distant samples from individual tumors highlighted significant intratumoral heterogeneity. These data are consistent with prior studies demonstrating GBM intratumoral heterogeneity,26 and highlight the need for appropriate tumor sampling during resection.27 While the optimal tumor sampling strategy may vary depending on the feature being analyzed, the incorporation of preoperative imaging characteristics may further refine tumor sampling strategies.28

While the number of tumors was limited, our data highlight the importance of genetic heterogeneity in driving intertumoral and intratumoral variations. Tumor protein 53 mutations were associated with increased nuclear atypia, specifically increased nuclear area and decreased nuclear circularity. These findings were true at both the tumor level and sample level. To our knowledge, the association of somatic TP53 mutational status with nuclear atypia in glioma has not previously been reported. This association is, however, consistent with the role for TP53 as a tumor suppressor, regulator for response to acute DNA damage repair, and key determinant of whole genome duplication in cancer.29

Despite the large number of tumor samples analyzed (n = 92), the number of patients (n = 10) studied was small and therefore does not represent the full range of molecular heterogeneity in GBM. Furthermore, our analysis focused on just 3 morphologic variables. However, this study quantifies morphologic heterogeneity within individual tumors and across GBM and identifies genetic heterogeneity as an important factor. These data suggest that accurate and robust H&E-based models to improve diagnosis and disease prognostication may require large training sets that incorporate multiple spatially distinct samples per patient. This process could be facilitated by the development of improved sampling strategies that optimally capture tumor heterogeneity. Overall, the impact of tumor heterogeneity on model performance is an important feature to determine for successful clinical implementation of ML approaches to neuropathology.
