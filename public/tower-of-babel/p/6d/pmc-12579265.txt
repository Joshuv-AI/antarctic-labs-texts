Effect of water application on ammonia emissions from simulated beef cattle feedyard surfaces
Authors: Myeongseong Lee, Brent W Auvermann, Carolina B Brandani, K Jack Bush, Greg B Ferguson, Kenneth D Casey, Vinícius N Gouvêa, Jason K Smith, Luis O Tedeschi, Jacek A Koziel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmmonia (NH3) emissions from beef cattle feedyards cause nutritional loss in manure and contribute to environmental pollution. Thus, the mitigation of NH3 emissions helps sustain and promote the production of environmental agriculture. Sprinkler-based water applications have primarily been used to reduce fugitive dust and heat stress in livestock. It has also been proposed as a potential NH3 mitigation strategy; however, its effectiveness remains uncertain for industrial use. This study evaluated the effect of controlled water application on NH3 emissions under simulated feedyard surfaces. Several environmental conditions (addition of urine, water and manure properties, and temperature) were subjected to different treatments, including repeated water applications or chemical amendments to improve the NH3 mitigation. Results indicated that water applications provided only a temporary mitigation effect (4–25%). NH3 emissions rebounded (1–19%) over time as the applied water evaporated, indicating that water primarily delays NH3 volatilization rather than permanently reducing total emissions. These findings suggest that while water applications provide short-term NH3 mitigation, additional strategies, such as chemical amendments or repeated applications, might be necessary for sustained emission reduction. By optimizing water application methods through further research, water application can become a valuable component of NH3 mitigation strategies for livestock operations.Keywords: Air quality, Water sprinkler, Ammonia mitigation, Environmental technology, Management practice, CAFOs, AlumSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental impact, Climate sciences, Climate change

Introduction

The majority of NH3 emissions in current inventories continue to originate from agricultural sources1,2. Confined animal feeding operations (CAFOs) have been identified as a source of NH3 emissions. Most of the NH3 produced in beef feedyards comes from fresh urine on pen surfaces3–5. As much as 80% of the total NH3 emissions from urine spots are released into the atmosphere within the first 48 h6–8. NH3 lost to the atmosphere is no longer available for managed application as nitrogen (N) fertilizer, reducing the nutrient value of feedlot manure for land application8. In addition, NH3 emissions and ultimate deposition of N can contribute to ecosystem eutrophication, nitrate contamination, soil acidity, fine particulate matter (PM) formation, impaired air quality and visibility, public health issues, and nitrous oxide (N2O) formation as ammonium (NH4+) oxidation in the microbial process of nitrification and denitrification5,9–11. Therefore, mitigation of NH3 emissions plays an important part in response to societal concerns and is necessary to achieve the goal of sustainable and environmental agriculture production.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture - Natural Resources Conservation Service (USDA-NRCS) has listed several recommended management practices for beef feedyards that have the potential to mitigate NH3 emissions12. Recommended practices include feed management, manure amendments, pen maintenance, and other strategies, with water application being one of them. Sprinkler-based water application has been used to reduce heat stress on livestock in the summer and to decrease fugitive PM emissions at selected feedyards. Since the investigation of NH3 mitigation after precipitation and water application was reported, water application is being considered as a management practice to decrease NH3 emissions8. However, its potential as an NH3 mitigation strategy remains underexplored.

Earlier research documented contradictory effects of water on NH3 emissions from cattle manure. Also, the mechanism of water application to mitigate NH3 emissions is not well understood. Previous research suggested that a dilution effect could play a role, where the simple leaching of aqueous NH4+ away from the emitting pen surface and absorption of volatilized NH313. Hutchinson et al. (1982)13 and Beauchamp et al. (1982)14 suggested that precipitation events cause a dramatic increase in the size of the aqueous reservoir available for NH3 to exist in solution, diluting the NH4+ concentration and effectively decreasing the area of the air/water interface in the manure, the boundary at which volatilization occurs. The reported NH3 mitigation effect after precipitation or water application has been approximately 9–92% to date13–22.

However, some research has reported that NH3 emissions increased by 8-160% after precipitation and water application13,14,22–25. Hutchinson et al. (1982)13 and Beauchamp et al. (1982)14 observed a substantial increase in NH3 after precipitation ceased, raising questions about whether NH4+ diluted in water volatilizes back into NH3 as the water evaporates. In addition, there is still a concern that water may cause more NH3 volatilization in the long term by increasing the microbially mediated production of aqueous NH3 within the water-filled pore space of the manure on the pen surface22. These inconsistent effects of water application on NH3 emissions suggest that various factors, such as manure moisture content, manure and water properties, temperature, and water application method, influence the mitigation outcome. To address these gaps, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of water application under controlled experimental conditions and explore strategies to enhance its NH3 mitigation potential.

The objective of this study was to (1) determine NH3 mitigation potential on both dry and wet beef manure after water application, (2) evaluate the impact of various factors (water type, manure source, moisture content of manure, and temperature) that affect NH3 suppression with water application, (3) investigate the effects of repeated water applications and aluminum sulfate (Al2(SO4)3, alum), a manure amendment with proven efficacy for NH3 mitigation as reported in previous studies26,27, on sustained NH3 mitigation, and (4) discuss the remaining research gaps and the optimization of water application as an NH3 mitigation strategy in feedyard environments. The results will provide scientific proof and potentially the mechanism that can explain the phenomenon of NH3 mitigation or generation after water application. In addition, this study will inform the potential scale-up trials at feedyard conditions to evaluate whether water application is a practical “best management practice” (BMP) to reduce gas-phase fugitive NH3 emissions from open-lot facilities.

Materials and methods

Flux chamber system

All manure samples from simulated feedyard surfaces with/without water application were evaluated for NH3 flux using an air-through flux chamber system at Texas A&M AgriLife Research, Bushland Facility (Amarillo, TX, USA). The flux chamber system (1 × 1 × 0.23 m3, L×W×H) devised by Parker et al. (2017)28 was used. The inside of the chamber was divided into four sub-chambers, and each sub-chamber was used as a control or treatment group in the experiment (Fig. 1). The bottom of each sub-chamber (0.45 × 0.45 × 0.2 m, L×W×H) was first filled with gravel and a stainless-steel plate (Lowes, Mooresville, NC, USA) was placed on top of the gravel layer to make the targeted chamber volume (0.45 × 0.45 × 0.1 m, L×W×H, Fig. 1a). Manure was added to each sub-chamber to a depth of 10 cm and manually flattened to simulate a feedyard surface (Fig. 1b).

Flux chamber system for testing the effects of water application on NH3 emissions from manure. (a) the gravel-fill adjustment of the sub-chamber volume, (b) NH3 flux sub-chamber covered by a removable lid with NH3 measurement system, and (c) the inside of the removable lid, (d) the experimental setup for testing the effect of water application on NH3 emissions from simulated feedyard surfaces.

Each sub-chamber was fitted with a removable lid to make the targeted headspace volume (20 L). Two outlets (air tube fitting, Grainger, Lake Forest, IL, USA) were made on the lid, one connected to the air pump (SKC, Eighty Four, PA, USA; extracting 18 L/min) and the other connected to the NH3 measurement system (extracting 2 L/min of headspace air to analyze). The headspace in each sub-chamber was fed with a steady airflow (20 L/min) of clean lab air, then routed to the NH3 measurement system, all facilitated by the air pump with a mass flow controller (Apex Vacuum LLC, Woodstock, GA, USA) (Fig. 1b). The targeted air-exchange rate (chamber headspace volume/time) was 1 air-exchange/min to compare the results with the previous research attempting to address the same question on a smaller experimental scale18. The space gap between the lid and the flux chamber was covered with metallic liquid glue to prevent air leakage (bright yellow in Fig. 1b). A cooling fan (8 × 8 × 2.5 cm, L×W×H, GDSTIME, China) was installed on the inside lid to mix the headspace well (Fig. 1c) and the vent (pressure vent, LI-COR Environmental, Lincoln, NE, USA) on the lid served as an air inlet. An Arduino weather sensor (DHT22, Aideepen, China) was placed inside the lid to measure the temperature and relative humidity of the headspace (Fig. 1c). A PTFE Teflon tube was connected to the outlet from the air pump and NH3 measurement system for discharging the effluent with emitted NH3 gas to the building outside (Fig. 1d). Due to the limited space of the laboratory room, equipped to control environmental conditions (e.g., temperature), within the research facility and the need to accommodate all required measurement equipment (e.g., NH₃ measurement system, pumps, mass flow controllers), only a single chamber system could be installed; therefore, a single chamber was used throughout the experiment (Fig. 1d).

Ammonia real-time measurement system

A self-designed, built, calibrated, and tested NH3 real-time and continuous measurement system equipped with electrochemical NH3 sensors of Membrapor (NH3/CR-200, Wallisellen, Switzerland) was used to measure NH3 concentrations (range: 0-200 ppmv). The detailed configuration and performance of the system were reported in Lee et al. (2023)29. The instantaneous voltage from the sensor was recorded every 30 s and converted into NH3 concentration through calibration made with standard gases and multipoint fitting. The average NH3 concentration per minute was used for data analysis. The calibration of sensors and airflow at the measurement system was R2 > 0.99. Electrochemical NH3 sensors were calibrated frequently before and after use in each treatment or control condition.

Manure

The manure that accumulated during a typical 150-d finishing period was used in the experiments. Two sources of manure were used: the first source of manure was scraped from a pen at a research feedyard in Texas (Texas A&M AgriLife Research farm, Bushland, TX) and the second manure was scraped from a pen at a commercial feedyard in Colorado (Greeley, CO). The scraped manure was sieved through a trommel screen to eliminate large manure clods that retain high moisture (Fig. 2). Only manure particles smaller than 0.6 × 1.3 cm (L×W) passed through the screen of the trommel mill and were collected as homogenized manure samples. A total of 6.5 kg of homogenized manure was loaded into each sub-flux chamber to measure NH3 concentration. The physicochemical properties of manure samples were analyzed by a ServiTech laboratory (Amarillo, TX) using standard procedures outlined in Recommended Methods of Manure Analysis (Second Edition) by Wilson et al. 202232. Water application

Three types of water were used to investigate whether there is a difference in the NH3 mitigation from water application depending on the type (and pH) of water: (1) deionized water, (2) TX groundwater (Bushland, TX), and (3) CO groundwater (Greeley, CO). The physicochemical properties of water samples were analyzed by a certified analytical laboratory (ServiTech, Amarillo, TX). Nitrate N was quantified using ion chromatography (IC), and ammoniacal N was determined using the flow injection analysis (FIA) method. The results of water properties are presented in Table 2. The use of deionized water was justified by considering the pH as one of the key parameters affecting NH3 emissions. It can be seen from Table 2 that pH in groundwater used in regional feedyards can vary, and as such, could influence the mitigation effect.

Each experiment condition involved applying 5 mm of water to one randomly chosen manure from each control vs. treatment pair, simulating a typical sprinkling for PM control. Specifically, the volume of applied water (1 L) was determined based on the surface area enclosed by the flux chamber for simulating 5 mm (uniform) water sprinkling. Each water application to the manure surface was completed within 1 min. The lid was opened only when water was applied on the 2nd d from the experiment start and remained closed immediately after water application for the duration of each experiment (4 to 12 d). The selection of 4 d was based on practical considerations regarding the frequency and desired mitigation effect duration at a commercial feedyard. Aside from the short periods of water application, there were no differences in the handling of the simulated manure pen surfaces between control and treatment.

Synthetic urine

Synthetic urine (total N concentration 10 g/L) was prepared according to Kool et al. (2006)33 and Dijkstra et al. (2013)34 and used to simulate a wet feedyard surface where cattle urine was sprayed (Fig. 4). Synthetic urine pH was 7.9 ± 0.2. The applied one-time urine dose to simulated feedyard surfaces was 200 mL. When water and urine were applied to manure together, urine was added first, followed by the immediate application of water. Regardless of whether the water application was uniform or partial, it consistently covered the entire urine-treated area (Fig. 4c) as it would in a commercial feedlot using sprinklers. To clearly distinguish each experimental condition with synthetic urine, abbreviations were used to represent the following: (i) the condition with dry manure and urine, so-called wet conditions (SYNT), (ii) the wet condition with uniform water application (SYNT + SPKL), and (iii) the wet condition with partial water application (SYNT + SPOT).

Water application on the simulated wet feedyard surface (wet condition) with synthetic urine. (a) dry manure with synthetic urine application (SYNT), (b) uniform water application on the manure surface with urine (SYNT + SPKL), and (c) partial cover water application on the manure surface with urine (SYNT + SPOT).

Manure moisture contents and air temperatures

Three manure moisture content (MC) levels (9, 19, and 35%) and three air temperatures (9, 22, and 31 °C) were established in the chamber. Manure MC was adjusted by adding deionized water to increase moisture or by allowing natural drying to decrease MC. To be specific, manure samples collected from feedyards were stored in multiple 50-gallon barrels. These barrels were initially stored in an indoor walk-in cooler maintained at approximately 3–4 °C. Among them, three barrels were stored indoors outside the cooler for approximately 6–8 months, during which moisture was allowed to evaporate naturally under ambient conditions. The temperature in the chamber headspace was controlled using a heating and air conditioning system in the research facility. Specifically, an air conditioner was installed in the laboratory to control the temperature (7–32 °C), and additional heaters (UtiliTech, Lowes, Mooresville, NC, USA) were used for experiments under high-temperature conditions (31 °C).

Multiple water applications

To investigate the hypothesis that repeated water application would improve the NH3 reduction effect, water was evenly applied to the manure surface three times at 4-d intervals.

Aluminum sulfate

Among various manure amendments, alum has demonstrated a strong NH3 mitigation effect in previous studies26,27. Thus, in this study, alum addition to water was tested to evaluate its NH3 mitigation potential. Alum (98%, Fisher Scientific, USA) was used in different doses (20, 41, 91, and 182 g/simulated feedyard surface area, corresponding to 100, 200, 450, and 900 g/m2) by mixing with 1 L of water and applied evenly to the manure surface.

Design of experiments

Several conditions and influencing factors were selected and analyzed, with all experiment designs summarized in Table 3. Manure and water collected from a commercial feedyard in Colorado were chosen as the benchmark for comparison across different conditions. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate under the specified conditions. To be specific, NH3 concentration and environmental data (temperature and relative humidity) were measured in real-time using the flux chamber system. The flux chamber system contained four sub-chambers, making it impossible to obtain triplicate control and treatment groups within a single trial. Therefore, triplicate results were achieved by conducting two separate trials. In each trial, NH3 concentrations were monitored for specific consecutive days (i.e., 4 or 12 d), followed by NH3 sensor calibration in preparation for the subsequent trial. Both trials utilized the same quantity of homogenized manure collected on the same day to ensure consistency. On the second day of each trial, sub-chambers were randomly assigned to either the control or treatment group, with water applied to the three sub-chambers designated as the treatment group. The reason for the water application on the second day was established based on the results of the preliminary performance evaluation experiment of the flux chamber system, and these results can be found in the results section.

Statistical analysis

Differences between control (CTRL and SYNT; no water application with or without synthetic urine) and treatment (SPKL, SPOT, SYNT + SPKL, and SYNT + SPOT; water application with or without synthetic urine) in NH3 flux were analyzed with JMP pro 16 (JMP Statistical Discovery, Cary, NC, USA) by using ANOVA (alpha = 0.05). As a post hoc analysis, Tukey’s HSD test was used. The parametric assumption was checked by Brown-Forsythe test (homogeneity of variance) and the Shapiro-Wilk test (normal distribution of data). R2 (the coefficient of determination) between specific factors and NH3 mitigation was analyzed with regression analysis (Microsoft Excel, USA).

Results

Preliminary trials to determine the temporal pattern of ammonia flux

The evaluation of temporal NH3 flux among flux sub-chambers is essential to determine if factors other than the treatment variables, such as the design of sub-chambers and removable lids, influence the experimental results (i.e., NH3 flux) under identical conditions. Thus, we aimed to assess if the measured NH3 flux from each sub-chamber exhibited significant differences under the same quantity of homogenized manure (6.5 kg of Colorado manure) and controlled conditions for 6 d. The temperature of each sub-chamber was very carefully controlled (an average of 22 °C), and the relative humidity was continuously reduced from 73 to 32%. The standard deviation (S.D.) difference of temperature and relative humidity between sub-chambers did not differ by less than 4% on average (Fig. 5).

The trend of NH3 flux from each sub-chamber in the preliminary test.

The temporal NH3 flux trends observed across all sub-chambers exhibited similar patterns (Fig. 5). Initially, NH3 flux increased due to the event of manure addition and chamber lid closure. This upward trend continued until a steady-state NH3 flux was established within the sub-chamber headspace. Following this peak, NH3 flux declined continuously due to the limited source of NH3 in the manure, as no additional NH3 sources were added during the experiment.

The S.D. in NH3 flux among sub-chambers varied by up to 30% (Fig. 5) during the initial phase of the experiment (before Day 1.5). However, after two days, the S.D. difference stabilized to within 5% across all sub-chambers. The observed differences in early-phase NH3 flux between sub-chambers were likely due to variations in the manure surface conditions, such as uneven surface compaction or flatness caused during manual setup. Additionally, these differences may result from inconsistent aerodynamics of the sampled air caused by differing airflow rates from the two air outlets incorporated into the flux chamber system. These factors are discussed in the discussion section. Based on these preliminary findings, the water application (treatment) was scheduled to be applied after 2 d, when the variability in NH3 flux among the sub-chambers had significantly become smaller.

To determine the background NH3 concentration entering through the inlet of the chamber system, NH3 measurements were conducted simultaneously with the preliminary trial on a desk located approximately 1 m above the floor within the laboratory room. Immediately after manure was introduced, the NH3 concentration increased to a maximum of 11 ppm; however, beginning approximately 5 min later and continuing through the end of the measurement period, the average concentration was 0.3 ppm (range: 0–3.4 ppm). Based on these observations, the background concentration was assumed to be 0 ppm for this study.

Effect of water application on ammonia flux

The study was conducted in multiple phases to evaluate the effect of one-time water application on NH3 fluxes comprehensively (Table 3). First, the effect of water application was examined under manure conditions representative of a feedyard environment, specifically dry conditions (manure only) and wet conditions (manure with synthetic urine). Second, potential factors (water type, manure sources, coverage area of manure with water, moisture contents of manure, and temperature) influencing the effectiveness of water application for NH3 mitigation were investigated. Finally, long-term changes (2 weeks) in NH3 flux following water application were assessed, along with an evaluation of additional strategies (the presence of alum addition in water and repeated water application) to enhance the effectiveness of water application as an NH3 mitigation approach.

Dry and wet conditions

Water applications significantly mitigated NH3 fluxes (p < 0.05), as evidenced by the results presented in Table 4; Fig. 6. Based on preliminary tests, water was applied on the second day of the experiment (Fig. 6). Following water application to the simulated manure surface (CO manure with CO groundwater), NH3 flux exhibited temporal fluctuations, characterized by sharp increases or decreases immediately after water addition (purple circle in Fig. 6). These fluctuations were likely due to the temporary disruption of NH3 equilibrium in the chamber headspace, which occurred when the chamber lid was opened for water application.

Under dry conditions, water application significantly reduced NH3 flux by 22% on the first day (p < 0.05), with the mitigation effect persisting for 4 d. However, from day 2 onward, NH3 flux continued to decrease numerically but not significantly (p > 0.05, Table 4). The effectiveness of the water application in mitigating NH3 fluxes diminished over time. Over the entire experimental period, water application resulted in an overall 12% mitigation in NH3 flux under dry conditions.

Similarly, under wet conditions, the mitigation effect of water application was more pronounced. NH3 flux was significantly reduced from the first (19%) to the third day (12%) after water application (p < 0.05), and a numerical reduction was observed even on the fourth day (p > 0.05, Table 4). Overall, water application led to a 16% mitigation in NH3 flux under wet conditions (p < 0.05).

Water application exhibited a greater NH3 mitigation effect under wet conditions. This effect could be explained by the relatively greater increase in NH3 emissions under the synthetic urine–applied condition (wet condition) compared to the dry condition, which amplified the observed impact of water application. In conclusion, these findings suggest that while the water application can temporarily and significantly mitigate NH3 fluxes, its effectiveness declines over time. Additionally, a subsequent increase in NH3 flux may occur after the mitigation phase, based on the observed flux trends. Although no significant increase in NH3 flux was detected under either dry or wet conditions during the four-day observation period, results from long-term experimental conditions, which are discussed later, will provide further insights into these trends.

Water type

There was no significant difference in NH3 flux mitigation depending on the type of water applied (p > 0.05). Specifically, when comparing mitigated NH3 flux after the application of different water types (Colorado groundwater, Texas groundwater, or deionized water) to the same manure (Colorado manure), no statistically significant differences were observed (p > 0.05, Table 5). As shown in Fig. 6, the trends in NH3 flux under both dry and wet conditions exhibited a similar trend across all water-type treatments. On average, NH3 flux was reduced by 13% over four days after water application, with a range of 9–16%, regardless of water type. However, under wet conditions, Texas groundwater exhibited sustained significant mitigation in NH3 flux between the control group (SYNT, no water application) and the treatment group (SYNT + SPKL, water application) through the fourth day of the experiment. This observation does not suggest that Texas groundwater inherently provides superior NH3 mitigation compared to other water types; rather, it is likely attributable to the smaller standard deviation observed between the control and treatment groups in this specific condition.

NH3 mitigation is well known to be highly sensitive to changes in pH35–38, with lower pH conditions favoring the conversion of NH3 to ammonium (NH4+). In this study, no significant difference in NH3 mitigation was observed between deionized water (pH: 7) and groundwater (pH: 8), despite their differing pH levels. This suggests that the water application did not substantially alter the pH of the water-manure mixture to a level sufficient to promote NH3 mitigation.

Manure sources

Although NH3 flux levels varied in magnitude depending on manure sources, there was no significant difference in NH3 flux mitigation (p > 0.05) when the same water type (deionized water) was applied to different manure sources (Colorado manure vs. Texas manure). Specifically, following the addition of an equal manure mass in chambers, the initial NH3 flux was approximately 450 mg/m2/h for Colorado manure but approximately 220 mg/m2/h for Texas manure (Fig. 7). This difference is primarily attributed to the higher ammonium-N concentration in Colorado manure than in Texas (0.38 vs. 0.23% as dry basis). Notably, after 2 d after the experiment’s start, NH3 flux levels in the chamber became similar between the two manure sources (approximately 130–150 mg/m2/h, Fig. 7).

Moisture contents in manure

The moisture content (MC) of manure significantly affected not only the effectiveness of NH3 mitigation (i.e., mitigation intensity) but also the efficiency (i.e., mitigation duration) after water application. Significant NH3 mitigation was observed across all MC conditions upon water application (p < 0.05, by day 1 in Table 7). The mitigation effect was highest in the order of moisture content 19%, 35%, and 9%, but there was no statistical difference (p > 0.05). Manure, with MC 19% and 35%, maintained a stable NH3 mitigation effect over four days. While there was no statistically significant difference between these two conditions (p > 0.05), MC 19% showed a numerically greater NH3 mitigation. In contrast, MC 9% initially exhibited an NH3 mitigation effect but developed a trend of increasing emissions over time. This suggests that, under low MC conditions (MC 9%), the effect of water application on NH3 flux would be weak and short-term, and may result in significant increases in NH3 flux in the long term compared to adequate moisture conditions (MC 19% and MC 35%).

The variation in NH3 mitigation trends after water application at different manure MCs can be explained by the influence of moisture on NH3 volatilization. NH3 volatilization is governed by the equilibrium between ammonium (NH4+, ion form) and NH3 (gas form)3–5,36. When moisture is present at an optimal level, a greater proportion of NH3 remains in the NH4+ form because of NH3’s high solubility in water39, thereby reducing volatilization. In the case of low MC conditions, water application initially forms a thin water layer that facilitates the conversion of NH3 gas into NH4+, temporarily suppressing NH3 volatilization. However, as water evaporates over time, the limited moisture availability prevents sufficient dissolution of NH4+, making it more susceptible to conversion into NH3 gas, thereby increasing emissions.

Excessively high moisture content can create anaerobic conditions, stimulating the activity of anaerobic microorganisms, which may enhance denitrification and NH3 gas production40. However, since the threshold of high MC is typically associated with MC of 60–70% or higher in composting36,41, the levels tested in this study (MC 19% and 35%) are not considered excessive or problematic under the current experimental conditions representative of most conditions in the dry lot feedyards in the region. In conclusion, MC 19–35% appeared to be the effective level for sustained NH3 reduction over 4 days, whereas MC 9% posed a potential risk of increasing NH3 emissions over time after transient NH3 reduction.

Air temperature

NH3 flux mitigation with water application is highly temperature-dependent (Table 8). At 9 °C, NH3 flux was significantly mitigated across all days after water application (p < 0.05), with mitigation ranging from 19% to 25% and a total mitigated flux of 4,930 mg/m2/h (23%). However, the effectiveness of NH3 mitigation declined as temperature increased. At 22 °C, NH3 flux was still significant (16% total reduction, p < 0.05) for 3 d, but mitigation was lower than the cooler condition. Under the highest experimental temperature of 31 °C, NH3 emissions were numerically mitigated for the short term after water application (9% reduction, p > 0.05) but subsequently increased over time with emissions rising by 1–9% after the mitigation phase.

In several studies, NH3 volatilization has been determined to be governed by temperature3–5,35–37,42. As the temperature increases, the pKa of the NH3-NH4+ equilibrium decreases, thereby shifting the chemical equilibrium toward the formation of gaseous NH335,36. Additionally, the solubility of NH3 in water decreases with rising temperature, leading to reactive volatilization35–37. Higher temperatures also enhance vapor pressure and diffusion of NH3 from manure, further exacerbating emissions36. A few studies14,15 reported similar NH3 emission trends with temperature, observing that NH3 volatilization increased as temperature rose following water application (or precipitation). This effect was particularly pronounced during the first two days. Additionally, elevated temperatures can accelerate microbial activity, increasing ammonification and the conversion of organic nitrogen to NH3. Vlek and Carter (1983)43 found that the rate of urea hydrolysis in soil exhibited a linear relationship with temperature over the range of 10–40 °C. In conclusion, the effectiveness of NH3 emission mitigation through water application is highly sensitive to temperature. To maximize the mitigation effect, water application is most effective at lower temperatures.

Manure surface coverage by water

The area coverage of manure surface by water significantly influenced NH3 mitigation (p < 0.05). As the proportion of the manure surface covered by water decreased, the NH3 mitigation effect weakened more rapidly (Table 9). Table 9 presents NH3 flux results obtained when water was partially applied under the same experimental conditions (CO manure and CO groundwater) as in Table 4. When water was applied evenly under dry and wet conditions, NH3 mitigation remained consistently high and lasted for four days (12–16%; SPKL-SYNT + SPKL), but the effect gradually diminished. In contrast, partial water application initially resulted in some NH3 mitigation (0–11%; SPOT-SYNT + SPOT), but NH3 emissions increased over time. These findings indicate that uniform water application (i.e., large surface coverage) is more effective in suppressing NH3 emissions and sustaining the mitigation effect over a longer duration.

The sustained NH3 mitigation observed with larger surface coverage can be attributed to the role of moisture in NH3 volatilization, as discussed earlier. When water evenly covers the manure surface, it forms a barrier that increases resistance to NH3 diffusion into the atmosphere, thereby reducing volatilization. Additionally, uniform water application enhances the dissolution of NH3 into NH4+, maintaining a more stable and effective mitigation effect. In contrast, partial water application resulted in a faster increase in NH3 flux, likely due to high NH3 emission from uncovered manure surfaces and structural alterations in the manure surface. Specifically, first, surface areas that remain dry due to incomplete water coverage continue to emit NH3 at a high rate, as there is no moisture to promote NH4+ formation and suppress volatilization. Second, localized or concentrated water application may have disrupted the structural integrity of the manure surface, leading to compaction or surface caving, which could have exposed deeper manure layers to the atmosphere, increasing the emission surface area. Additionally, we speculate that the uneven moisture distribution may have created localized anaerobic and aerobic zones, potentially accelerating microbial activity and urea hydrolysis, thereby contributing to the observed increase in NH3 emissions. Ultimately, while even water application may theoretically facilitate larger-scale NH3 production through soluble NH4+ evaporation in water and microbial activity over a broader surface area, this effect was not observed in the current experiment.

To comprehensively assess the effect of manure surface coverage by water on NH3 flux, the number of repetitions was increased (n = 12) under consistent temperature (22 °C) and moisture content (MC 19%) conditions. The results (Table 10) indicated that uniform water application showed greater and more sustained NH3 mitigation, consistent with previous findings (Table 9). Furthermore, regression analysis (Fig. 8) revealed a moderate correlation between manure surface coverage by water and NH3 mitigation, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.53. In conclusion, even (or uniform) water application led to greater and more sustained NH3 mitigation. Conversely, uneven or partial water application led to lower NH3 mitigation and may have exacerbated NH3 volatilization. Therefore, ensuring the widest possible coverage during water application is a key strategy for effective NH3 mitigation.

Trend of long-term ammonia flux after water application

Early experimental results indicated that water application significantly mitigates NH3 flux from the simulated feedlot surface (p < 0.05), with the highest reduction observed when the urine spot was completely covered by water (wet conditions). Additionally, NH3 mitigation was most pronounced immediately after water application but diminished over time. Notably, beyond the mitigation phase, NH3 emissions not only returned to previous levels but exceeded those of the control group. However, this post-increase in NH3 emission was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) for the 6-day observation and was represented only under dry conditions. Therefore, to better understand the long-term effects of water application, NH3 flux trends were monitored under wet conditions for 2 weeks.

The temporal trend of NH3 flux after water application over two weeks was presented in Fig. 9; Table 11. Initially, NH3 flux (SYNT + SPKL) was mitigated, consistent with earlier findings. However, after this initial mitigation, NH3 flux (SYNT + SPKL) gradually increased relative to the control (SYNT) and eventually stabilized at levels comparable to the control (SYNT) over time. Specifically, the water application initially reduced NH3 flux by 15%, suggesting an immediate mitigation effect (p < 0.05). Between Days 3 and 6, the mitigation effect persisted but declined progressively (from 15% to 6%). However, from Day 6 onward, NH3 flux (SYNT + SPKL) became statistically higher than in the control (SYNT), with mitigation percentages ranging from − 4% to -19% (p < 0.05 at Day 8–9). Overall, the cumulative NH3 flux over 12 days after water application increased slightly by approximately 0.3% (p > 0.05). Similar to our observations, several studies13–15 also reported that after an initial decrease in NH3 emissions after water application (or precipitation events), NH3 emissions tended to increase again over time.

The initial NH3 mitigation (days 2–6) is likely due to the suppression of volatilization by water application. Water application on the manure surface absorbed volatilized NH3 gas and expanded the aqueous reservoir in which NH3 can exist in solution13,14. This process likely leached NH3-NH4+ away from the emitting surface, diluted the NH4+ concentration, and reduced the effective air–water interfacial area for volatilization, thereby mitigating NH3 emissions13,14,22. However, as water evaporates and the manure dries over time, NH4+ gradually transforms to NH3, leading to a re-increase in NH3 flux. Additionally, elevated MC may enhance microbial activity, increasing the microbially mediated production of aqueous NH3 within the water-filled pore space of the manure.

These results indicate that, while water application provides temporary mitigation in NH3 emissions, a compensatory effect occurs as retained NH3 is released later. This suggests that NH3 dissolved in water is primarily retained in the NH4+ form rather than being converted into other chemical species, which are not easily volatile. As the drying process progresses, NH4+ is gradually converted back into NH3 and released into the atmosphere. In other words, water application delays NH3 volatilization rather than permanently reducing total emissions.

In conclusion, although short-term mitigation of NH3 emissions is evident, the rebound effect highlights that a single water application is insufficient for long-term NH3 emission control. To enhance and sustain the mitigation effect, additional strategies, such as multiple water applications or the incorporation of chemical amendments, should be considered, along with consideration of environmental variables for water application.

Effect of multiple water applications on ammonia flux

To enhance and sustain the NH3 mitigation effect of water application, the impact of multiple water applications on NH3 flux was investigated. The long-term NH3 flux trend following multiple water applications was presented in Fig. 10; Table 12. As observed in previous experiment results, NH3 flux was most significantly reduced immediately after each water application, but the effect diminished over time. Specifically, after the 1st water application, NH3 mitigation decreased from an initial 10% to a range of 9–6%. However, after the 2nd water application, the mitigation effect increased again, reaching 15% and remaining in the 15–12% range. The 3rd water application further enhanced NH3 mitigation, increasing the reduction effect to 17%, which then gradually declined to 15–12%. A notable observation was that while NH3 mitigation was relatively lower after the first water application, the mitigation effect became more pronounced following subsequent water applications. This indicated that repeated water applications sustained and enhanced NH3 flux reduction over time.

Similar to our observations, Beauchamp et al. (1982)14 also reported that NH3 emissions decreased with each successive precipitation event following the application of manure to soil. This trend is likely due to the progressive absorption of water into the manure, which shifts the NH3/NH4+ equilibrium toward NH4+, thereby reducing NH3 volatilization. Additionally, repeated water applications help maintain an optimal moisture content, further inhibiting NH3 volatilization. However, consistent with previous findings, NH3 flux did not disappear entirely after water application but tended to increase again over time. In addition, Beauchamp et al. (1982)14 observed a substantial increase in NH3 emissions as air temperature rose after precipitation events. Therefore, once additional water applications cease and the manure dries, NH3 emissions are expected to rise as previously retained NH4+ is converted back into NH3 gas.

In conclusion, repeated water applications effectively reduced NH3 flux and prolonged the mitigation effect. In scenarios where NH3 emissions must be controlled over a specific period, multiple water applications could represent a viable and effective strategy for sustained NH3 mitigation.

Effect of aluminum sulfate with water application on ammonia flux

As an additional strategy to enhance and sustain the NH3 mitigation effect of water application, the impact of water application combined with alum was evaluated. The long-term NH3 flux trends following alum-treated water application were presented in Table 13. NH3 flux was significantly mitigated after the water application containing alum, with the mitigation effect persisting throughout the experimental period. Specifically, when water with any dose of alum (20, 41, 91, and 182 g, corresponding to 100, 200, 450, and 900 g/m2) was applied to the manure surface, NH3 flux was substantially lower than in the control group (SYNT, Table 13). Additionally, the NH3 mitigation effect increased with higher alum dosages (R2 = 0.89, Fig. 11). While no statistically significant difference was observed between the 20 g and 41 g treatments (p > 0.05), greater mitigation in NH3 flux was noted at higher dosages. Notably, at a lower alum dose (20 g), the initial NH3 mitigation effect was comparable to that of simple water application (SYNT + SPKL, p > 0.05). However, over time, a significant difference was represented between them (p < 0.05), with the alum-treated groups demonstrating sustained suppression of NH3 flux for 14 d. Unlike the water-only application (SYNT + SPKL), which exhibited a rebound effect in NH3 flux after the mitigation phase, water application with alum effectively prevented this rebound.

The relationship between aluminum sulfate dose and NH3 mitigation.

The NH3 mitigation effect of alum is attributed to its chemical composition and reactions with manure. Upon dissolution in water, alum (Al2(SO4)3) releases sulfuric acid (SO42−), which has the effect of lowering the pH in the manure. A lower pH shifts the NH3 (gas) ⇄ NH4+ (ion) equilibrium toward NH4+, thereby reducing NH3 volatilization3–5,36. Additionally, released Al3+ ions may interact with water to form insoluble compounds (Al(OH)3) and release H+ to form NH4+, further suppressing NH3 emissions. Moreover, the reduction in pH can inhibit microbial activity, particularly urease, an enzyme responsible for urea hydrolysis, which functions optimally at a neutral pH range of 7.5–8.544. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the prolonged and effective mitigation of NH3 volatilization with alum treatment.

In conclusion, NH3 mitigation through water application with added alum clearly contributed to reducing NH3 flux more effectively than the simple water application and multiple water applications. However, further research is necessary to determine whether these results are held in an actual feedyard environment, where manure is continuously deposited at variable intervals (e.g., hourly or even minutely). Additionally, it is necessary to investigate how alum affects livestock in production, the effect of manure with high Al3+ on soil and crops as a fertilizer, and whether the increased sulfur content in manure due to alum, as reported in previous studies45, could lead to elevated hydrogen sulfide (H2S) emissions or increased corrosion of the feedlot infrastructure.

The effectiveness of water application (including natural precipitation) in mitigating NH3 emissions has shown inconsistency across previous studies (Table 14). While some studies have reported NH3 mitigation after water application, others have observed increased NH3 emissions. These conflicting outcomes are likely due to several interacting physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms on manure surfaces after water application.

First, NH3 gas emitted from manure surfaces can readily dissolve into the applied water layer due to NH3’s high solubility in water39. Second, water addition increases the aqueous reservoir in which NH3-NH4+ can exist in solution, reducing the effective air–water interfacial area for volatilization. This “dilution effect” can also facilitate the leaching of NH4+ deeper into the manure matrix, thereby reducing surface-level emissions13,14,46. Third, water application can enhance microbial activity by increasing moisture availability. For instance, dry manure (~ 90% dry matter) typically has minimal microbial activity and limited NH3 production36. However, elevated MC can stimulate microbial activity, accelerating the microbial conversion of organic nitrogen into aqueous NH336,47. Conversely, as manure dries and the applied water evaporates, previously absorbed or diluted NH3 can be released back into the atmosphere, and microbial activity may decline due to reduced moisture availability. While the first and second mechanisms suggest reduced NH3 emissions following water application, the third mechanism implies a potential increase.

Due to the overlapping and interactive nature of these mechanisms, it is challenging to identify the dominant process governing NH3 emissions after water application. Findings from this study, combined with previous research, provide valuable insights into these inconsistencies by elucidating the temporal dynamics of NH3 emissions following water treatment.

In this study, water application temporarily mitigated NH3 emissions from simulated feedyard manure surfaces by 4–25% (p < 0.05). However, as the applied water evaporated, the suppressed NH3 emissions subsequently increased by 1–19% (p < 0.05), offsetting the initial mitigation. As a result, cumulative NH3 emissions from water-treated surfaces were approximately 0.3–1% higher than those from untreated dry surfaces, although this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that water application delayed NH3 volatilization rather than permanently reducing total emissions. Importantly, the magnitude and duration of NH3 mitigation depend on multiple factors, including manure MC, air temperature, water application method (uniform vs. partial or multiple applications), and additional amendments such as alum. For instance, low manure MC and high air temperatures typically reduce the effectiveness of NH3 suppression. Uniform water application is more effective than partial application, and multiple applications prolong the mitigation effects. Furthermore, incorporating alum during water application can significantly enhance long-term NH3 mitigation by reducing volatilization and preventing subsequent emission rebounds.

In addition, a key observation is that previous studies reporting NH3 generation typically measured emissions several days after water application or used partial water application methods. In contrast, studies reporting NH3 mitigation generally measured emissions within the first 24 h after precipitation or involved the application of large water doses. Thus, the seemingly contradictory literature findings can be attributed to the transient nature of NH3 mitigation: initial suppression followed by a compensatory rebound as evaporation progresses.

In summary, understanding the dynamic process in which NH3 mitigation is initially achieved after water application, followed by a compensatory increase in emissions as retained NH3 is eventually released, offers a more comprehensive and consistent explanation of how water application influences NH3 emissions. This perspective mediates conflicting findings in the previous literature and highlights the temporary nature of NH3 suppression, emphasizing the need for additional mitigation strategies to achieve long-term emission reductions with water application.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations in our study should be acknowledged to better interpret our findings and identify directions for future research. The first limitation of this study is that the lack of significant findings under certain experimental conditions (e.g., manure sources and SPOT) may be attributed to the relatively small sample size (n = 3) used in our analysis, resulting in high variability and insufficient statistical power. Future studies could address this limitation by incorporating larger sample sizes or additional replicates to enhance statistical power and clarify the observed trends.

Secondly, our study is limited by the structural design and lack of validation procedures in the flux chamber system. Specifically, the sampling line (2 L/min) was spatially independent from the air-exchange line in space (18 L/min), potentially leading to inconsistency in the aerodynamics of the exchanged air and sampled air. This sampling design may have resulted in potentially compromising the representativeness of the measured NH3 concentrations. It may have contributed to the significant differences in NH3 concentration observed and sustained between sub-chambers following manure addition. Additionally, NH3 recovery tests, a step to validate the accuracy and efficiency of chamber-based flux measurements, were not conducted in this study. However, since the primary objective of this study was to compare treatment effects and temporal trends rather than to quantify absolute emission rates, we believe these limitations do not undermine the reliability of our findings regarding the NH3 mitigation trends following water application under different treatments. Nevertheless, to improve the accuracy of NH3 emission quantification within the flux chamber system, conducting recovery tests should be considered in future studies.

The third limitation is related to the experimental design, which did not allow us to comprehensively identify potential interactions among inherent factors. For instance, manure sources (Texas vs. Colorado) and moisture content (13% vs. 19%) were treated separately without adequately addressing the inherent differences in moisture content between manure sources. Utilizing a factorial design (e.g., manure source x moisture content) could help differentiate the effects of manure source itself from those of intrinsic moisture content and allow for a more detailed investigation of their interactive effects. Addressing these methodological limitations will strengthen future experimental designs and improve the robustness and applicability of findings regarding the effect of water application on NH3 emissions.

Potential of water application for ammonia mitigation

To maximize the potential for NH3 mitigation after water application, our study results indicate that a single water application is insufficient. Additional strategies should be considered to enhance NH3 mitigation, such as multiple water applications or the incorporation of chemical or physical amendments. Applying multiple water applications can be an effective short-term strategy for suppressing NH3 emissions over a specific period. However, in the long term, this approach is unlikely to reduce total NH3 emissions, as the retained NH3 is eventually released once water application ceases. Therefore, supplementing water with chemical amendments such as alum or physical amendments48 is a more viable approach for sustained NH3 mitigation. Among these amendments, the most practical and efficient method is the application of chemical amendments that dissolve in water, allowing them to be applied through existing sprinkler systems without requiring modifications. Alum has been extensively studied and have demonstrated high effectiveness in NH3 mitigation25,26,44.

The findings of our study support these results, reinforcing the potential of alum as an effective and efficient enhancer of NH3 mitigation with water application. However, as previously noted, the potential impacts of alum must be thoroughly evaluated to determine whether these positive results are applicable in an actual feedyard environment and examine its impacts on livestock in production, soil health, the risk of sulfur leaching into aquifers, manure quality as a fertilizer for crops, and the possibility of elevated H2S emissions45. Ultimately, manure is being applied as fertilizer for the purpose of resource recycling. The inclusion of aluminum (Al) should be avoided due to its potential toxicity effects on crops and soil. This risk is particularly significant under conditions of low soil pH, which may be induced during NH3 mitigation strategies with alum. Therefore, the use of calcium sulfate or iron sulfate may be considered a safer alternative amendment. Further research is necessary to refine and optimize water application strategies, ensuring both effectiveness in NH3 mitigation and safety for livestock and the environment.

Currently, water application in beef cattle feedyards is primarily used for dust suppression and to alleviate heat stress in livestock during the summer. While our study demonstrates that water application also provides a temporary mitigation in NH3 emissions, its potential as an NH3 mitigation strategy should be carefully evaluated. Because the additional benefit of NH3 mitigation from an already implemented practice (i.e., water application by sprinkler) is promising, however, our findings indicate that total NH3 emissions remain unchanged, making water application ineffective as a standalone strategy for NH3 mitigation. Furthermore, the applicability of water application to actual feedyard conditions has not yet been confirmed and additional strategies to enhance NH3 mitigation by water application were not fully proven. Therefore, ongoing research is necessary to further investigate the NH3 mitigation potential of water application at both in-vitro and field scales, with the goal of developing a more comprehensive and effective mitigation approach.

We propose the following key topics for future research to enhance the effectiveness of water application for NH3 mitigation: First, optimization of water application dose for NH3 mitigation is necessary. Identifying the minimum water dose required to achieve NH3 mitigation or determining the optimal dose for maximum mitigation will provide valuable insights for refining water application strategies. Although several studies15,21 reported a positive relationship between increased doses of water application (precipitation intensity) and enhanced NH3 mitigation, further research at a larger experimental scale is necessary to validate this finding and assess its applicability under practical conditions. Second, the influence of manure surface hardness on NH3 mitigation has not been thoroughly examined. In our study, the manure surface was manually prepared, which may not accurately reflect the compacted feedyard conditions shaped by livestock hooves. Future research should evaluate how surface compaction affects water penetration and NH3 volatilization. Third, our study evaluated NH3 mitigation following a single manure deposition event with water application, ensuring full coverage of the pen surface. This approach allowed us to assess the maximum potential mitigation effect under ideal conditions. However, in actual feedyard environments, manure is continuously deposited at varying intervals (e.g., hourly or even minutely). In addition, while the use of synthetic urine ensures consistency, it does not capture the complexity and variability of actual urine interaction, such as microbial population and pH variation. Therefore, to better reflect real conditions, future studies should investigate NH3 mitigation under repeated actual manure deposition scenarios to provide a more accurate assessment of water application effectiveness. Fourth, the effectiveness of water application should be validated under the diverse airflow conditions typically observed in commercial feedyards. As shown in Table 14, airflow (windspeed) within feedyards can vary widely. To reflect this variability in a chamber-scale study, a broad range of air exchange rates (1–6.7) was selected. Although the target air exchange rate used in this study falls within the typical range observed in previous studies, it does not fully capture the dynamic nature of airflow in real-world settings. Therefore, future studies should evaluate the effects of water application under a wider range of airflow conditions to enhance the applicability of the findings. Lastly, conducting field trials is essential to assess whether current on-farm water application practices effectively mitigate NH3 emissions. Additionally, applying the optimal water application conditions identified in laboratory and in-vitro studies to field-scale trials will help determine their feasibility and effectiveness in practical feedyard settings. In conclusion, while water application offers a temporary NH3 mitigation benefit, its long-term effectiveness remains limited and requires further optimization to ensure sustained emission reductions. Further research is needed to refine application strategies, optimize dosage, and evaluate its impact under actual feedyard conditions. By addressing these research gaps, future studies can help determine whether water application can be integrated into a comprehensive NH3 mitigation strategy that aligns with existing best management practices (BMPs).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that water application provides temporary NH3 mitigation from simulated beef cattle feedyard surfaces by 4–25% (p < 0.05). However, as the applied water evaporated, 1–19% of the previously suppressed NH3 emissions subsequently rebounded (p < 0.05). Consequently, cumulative NH3 emissions from water-treated manure surfaces were approximately 0.3–1% higher than those from untreated dry manure surfaces (p > 0.05). These findings indicated that water application primarily delays NH3 volatilization rather than permanently reducing total emissions. Thus, a single water application was insufficient for long-term NH3 emission control. Additionally, environmental factors significantly influenced the NH3 mitigation effect of water application. For example, low manure moisture content and elevated temperatures reduced the effectiveness of NH3 suppression. To enhance NH3 mitigation, uniform water coverage was more effective than partial application, and multiple applications helped sustain NH3 mitigation over time. Furthermore, adding aluminum sulfate with water application significantly improved long-term NH3 mitigation by reducing volatilization and preventing post-mitigation rebounds. Future research should optimize application frequency, dosage, and method to maximize NH3 mitigation benefits under practical feedyard conditions. Field-scale demonstration studies are necessary to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of water-based NH3 mitigation strategies in actual livestock operations. By optimizing water application methods through further research, water application can become a valuable component of NH3 emission mitigation strategies for livestock operations, enhancing its effectiveness and sustainability in actual feedyard conditions.
