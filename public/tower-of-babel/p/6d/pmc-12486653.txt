Maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnancies with rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and spondyloarthritis: a comparative study
Authors: Samar Al Emadi, Eman Satti, Priyanka Cackamvalli, Nawal Hadwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic inflammatory arthritis, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and spondyloarthritis (SpA), poses unique challenges during pregnancy due to potential maternal and fetal complications. This study aimed to compare pregnancy outcomes among women with these conditions in Qatar.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Hamad General Hospital from 2016 to 2022. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, disease features (e.g., disease duration, serological markers), treatments, and pregnancy outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records. Pregnancy outcomes included miscarriage, live birth, and intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD); delivery data such as term, weeks of gestation, and mode of delivery; and neonatal data including birth weight, low birth weight (LBW), congenital anomalies, and NICU admissions. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify associations between disease subtype, medication use, and pregnancy outcomes.ResultsA total of 189 pregnant women were included (RA = 131, SpA = 29, and PsA = 29). Pregnancy outcomes were compared across the three patient groups: SpA was associated with the highest live birth rate (89.7%), while PsA had the highest rate of NICU admissions (13.8%) and lowest preterm birth rate (6.9%). Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) occurred only in RA (31%). Multivariable analyses showed that SpA was linked to significantly higher odds of live birth and lower risks of miscarriage, peripartum complications, and low birth weight compared to RA. PsA was associated with a reduced risk of preterm birth. Advanced maternal age increases the risk of any complication taken together. Sulfasalazine use was associated with lower miscarriage risk and higher likelihood of live birth, but also increased NICU admissions, as did anti-TNF therapy. Hydroxychloroquine use was associated with a protective effect against NICU admissions and low birth weight.ConclusionPregnancy outcomes vary significantly across chronic inflammatory arthritis subtypes. Our findings underscore the need for individualized, multidisciplinary care and further prospective studies to guide clinical management.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory arthritis are a group of chronic, immune-mediated conditions that predominantly affect women during their reproductive years. These diseases can impact both maternal and fetal health, posing a significant clinical challenge in the management of pregnancy among affected women [1–4].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and spondyloarthritis (SpA) are among the most common subtypes, each with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical manifestations [5–8]. Pregnancy itself induces a range of physiological, hormonal, and immunological changes that can, in turn, influence disease activity. As such, the care of pregnant women with: Chronic inflammatory arthritis requires close coordination between rheumatology and obstetric teams. Understanding how these conditions interact with pregnancy is essential to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes [3].

Several studies have reported increased risks of adverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes among women with RA, including preeclampsia [9, 10], cesarean delivery [10–14], spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, low birth weight, and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) neonates [10, 12–16]. In addition, infants born to mothers with RA are more likely to require admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [10] and experience prolonged hospital stays [11]. These risks are often exacerbated in the context of active disease or the use of certain anti-rheumatic medications during pregnancy [17].

While the impact of RA on pregnancy outcomes has been relatively well studied, less is known about reproductive risks associated with other Chronic inflammatory arthritis such as PsA and SpA [18, 19]. Despite their clinical importance, these conditions, particularly PsA, remain underrepresented in the literature compared to RA, and their effects on maternal and fetal outcomes are not well elucidated [19–21]. Several limitations can be identified in existing studies, including the pooling of different rheumatic diseases and subtypes without performing comparative subgroup analyses, small sample sizes for risk factor evaluation, and inconsistencies across studies, all of which make it challenging for clinicians to provide individualized counseling [17, 19, 21–23]. Given the differences in pathophysiology, disease course, treatment strategies, and their potential effects on pregnancy, further research is needed to assess each condition independently and inform more tailored management approaches [7].

This study examined the sociodemographic and clinical traits of pregnant women with RA, PsA, and SpA in Qatar, as well as risk factors for maternal and fetal outcomes. It analyzed clinical and demographic characteristics, arthritis subtypes, and treatments to identify risk factors and offer treatment insights. The goal is to improve clinical care and support evidence-based decisions in rheumatology and maternal-fetal medicine.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Hamad General Hospital (HGH) in Qatar, covering the period from July 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022. This study used data extracted from the electronic medical records of a large segment of the population residing in Qatar.

Study population and setting

This study focused on pregnant women with an established diagnosis of chronic inflammatory arthritis who attended the Pregnancy and Rheumatic Disease Clinic at HGH. This clinic collaborates with high-risk obstetric and fetal medicine clinics. Eligible participants included pregnant women followed at the clinic with a confirmed diagnosis of chronic inflammatory arthritis and documented pregnancy outcomes. The present study evaluated three chronic inflammatory arthritis: rheumatoid arthritis (RA), SpA, and PsA. For RA, patients were included based on the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria [24]. Patients with SpA met the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS) criteria for the diagnosis of SPA [25]. As for PsA, the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis (CASPAR) were used to identify eligible patients [26]. Only patients who fulfilled the respective diagnostic criteria for each disease were included in the study. Exclusion criteria included cases missing pregnancy outcome data (e.g., if the mother delivered outside of Hamad Medical Corporation facilities or abroad) and multiple pregnancies.

Data collection

Data were collected through electronic medical records and a structured datasheet, without any subject interviews or additional follow-up required for this study. The data included patient age, nationality, details on inflammatory arthritis, comorbidities, medications used during pregnancy, blood workup results, and obstetric, peripartum, and neonatal outcomes. The term “peripartum complications” refers to any complications reported during the peripartum period and recorded in the medical files. Each pregnancy was recorded separately, and serial numbers were assigned to each pregnancy for patients with multiple pregnancies during the study period.

Patient sociodemographic and clinical information

Collected data included sociodemographic and clinical information such as age, nationality, disease duration, pregnancy history since diagnosis, and serological markers, e.g., rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) levels, were recorded. Medications documented included oral corticosteroids, conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs, such as hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine), biologic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and other medications (e.g., folic acid and and vitamin D).

Pregnancy outcomes

Pregnancy outcomes, including gestational diabetes, pregnancy-induced hypertension, infections, and eclampsia/preeclampsia, were documented. Delivery details (e.g., term, mode of delivery) and pregnancy losses (e.g., miscarriages, intrauterine fetal demise—IUFD) were also recorded. Miscarriage, or spontaneous abortion, refers to the natural loss of a pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, while IUFD denotes fetal demise occurring in the uterus after 20 weeks [27].

Fetal outcomes

Fetal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, low birth weight at delivery (defined as a birth weight of less than 2.5 kg), congenital anomalies, and instances of intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), as noted in ultrasound reports. Admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) was also documented.

Statistical analysis

Categorical and continuous values were expressed as percentages or as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. Descriptive statistics summarized the baseline characteristics (independent variables), including maternal age at pregnancy, nationality, diagnosis, serology, and medications. Separate descriptive analyses of outcome variables (pregnancy outcome, fetal outcome such as miscarriage, live birth, IUFD; delivery data such as term, weeks of gestation, mode of delivery; and neonatal data such as birth weight, low birth weight [LBW], congenital anomalies, and NICU admissions) were conducted for each arthritis group and then compared among the three groups. The association between medications and fetal, delivery, and neonatal outcomes was examined, along with the association between diagnosis and these same outcomes. Logistic regression analyses using a generalized linear model were performed to calculate Exp(B) values, corresponding to adjusted odds ratios (adjusted ORs), along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), to evaluate associations between pregnancy outcomes and the independent variables. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The majority of participants were Arab across the three groups: 57.3% in RA, 62.1% in SpA, and 82.8% in PsA, with median ages of 31, 29, and 34 years, respectively. Disease duration varied, with median durations of 5 years for RA, 3.5 years for SpA, and 4 years for PsA. The majority of RA patients were seropositive (74%). HLA B27 was positive in one-third of the SpA patients (37.9%), while all PsA patients were HLA B27-negative. Treatment regimens differed, with 49.6% of RA patients using hydroxychloroquine, while anti-TNF therapies were more common in SpA (48.3%) and PsA (31.0%).

The mean gestational age was 35.7, 31.8, and 29.4 weeks, respectively, for SpA, RA, and PsA. Birth weights were comparable across groups, with median values ranging from approximately 2.9 to 3.2 kg. Pregnancy outcomes such as preeclampsia were rare, occurring in 0.8% of RA and 10.3% of SpA cases while eclampsia was not reported in any case within the cohort. Gestational diabetes was more prevalent in SpA (27.6%) compared to RA (4.6%) and PsA (13.8%). Other pregnancy outcomes included a higher rate of live births in SpA (89.7%) and RA (76.3%) compared to PsA (69.0%). Preterm births were less common in PsA (6.9%) than in SpA (11.5%) and RA (16%). IUGR was noted in 31.0% of RA cases but was absent in the SpA or PsA pregnancies. Cesarean sections were performed in 38.5% of SpA, 32.1% of RA, and 27.6% of PsA live births. Peripartum complications were reported in 12.2% of RA cases, while NICU admissions were more frequent in PsA (13.8%). Finally, only one congenital anomaly was identified and reported in an RA patient (Table 2).

Pregnant women with SpA demonstrated a significantly higher likelihood of live birth compared to those with RA (adjusted OR = 4.889, P = 0.007) and a significantly lower risk of miscarriage (adjusted OR = 0.237, P = 0.012). SpA was also associated with a lower risk of peripartum complications (adjusted OR = 0.444, P = 0.027) and low birth weight (adjusted OR = 0.269, P < 0.001) compared to RA. PsA was linked to a significantly lower risk of preterm birth compared to RA (adjusted OR = 0.468, P = 0.023; for “term” as the dependent variable, adjusted OR = 2.064, P = 0.029) (Table 3).

The study also evaluated other factors, including maternal age, disease duration, and medication use, which could influence maternal and fetal outcomes. Multivariable analyses revealed that advanced maternal age at pregnancy increased the risk of overall complications, including IUGR, preterm birth, and LBW (adjusted OR = 1.037, P = 0.021), while longer disease duration was associated with a reduced risk of PROM (adjusted OR = 0.815, P = 0.011) (Table 3).

The use of sulfasalazine was associated with a decreased risk of miscarriage (adjusted OR = 0.237, P = 0.001) and an increased likelihood of live birth (adjusted OR = 3.661, P < 0.001). Sulfasalazine and anti-TNF therapy were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of NICU admissions (adjusted OR = 2.627, P = 0.004 and adjusted OR = 7.249, P < 0.001, respectively). Conversely, hydroxychloroquine use was associated with a reduced risk of NICU admissions (adjusted OR = 0.415, P < 0.001) and low birth weight (adjusted OR = 0.582, P = 0.001). Aspirin and vitamin D use were both associated with an increased likelihood of NICU admissions (P = 0.006 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to comparatively explore maternal and fetal outcomes across three different types of Chronic inflammatory arthritis, namely RA, PsA, and SpA—focusing particularly on miscarriage, cesarean section deliveries, preterm birth, low birth weight infants, and NICU admissions, among other outcomes.

Both RA and PsA were associated with high spontaneous abortion rates in our cohort, reaching 31% in PsA and 20.6% in RA, while the SpA group exhibited a statistically significant lower rate of only 10.3%. This high rate of abortion observed in the RA group aligns with findings from several previous studies [17, 28–30]. This increased prevalence may be attributed to higher disease activity, the presence of autoantibodies (e.g., anti-ACPA, RF), and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) in RA, all of which can adversely affect placental function and early pregnancy maintenance [17, 30, 31]. Unfortunately, information on disease activity was not available in our cohort. In contrast, SpA (including ankylosing spondylitis) tends to follow a more stable disease course during pregnancy, possibly due to immunological adaptations that are more favorable to pregnancy [4, 22, 31]. Regarding PsA, available data on abortion rates are scarce and relatively outdated [20, 32–34]. Overall, PsA has been associated with fewer pregnancy complications compared to RA, particularly when disease activity is well-controlled [20, 32]. A Canadian case-control study evaluating pregnancy outcomes in women with PsA reported very low rates of miscarriages and abortions, with no significant differences compared to healthy controls [20]. Similarly, other studies consistently reported a high percentage of live births among women with PsA [23, 33]. More recently, a systematic review [35] suggested that adverse pregnancy outcomes in PsA are more likely to occur in patients with moderate to severe disease activity, highlighting that disease activity, rather than the diagnosis itself, is the key determinant of pregnancy outcomes [35]. Thus, the elevated miscarriage rates observed in our PsA population could reflect uncontrolled disease activity or other unidentified factors that warrant further investigation. However, these findings demonstrate the need for additional studies specifically focused on the impact of PsA on pregnancy outcomes to inform clinical management and patient counseling guidelines.

Regarding delivery mode, nearly one-third of live births among women with chronic inflammatory arthritis were delivered via cesarean section, with rates varying by disease subtype: 38.5% in SpA, 32.1% in RA, and 27.6% in PsA. These findings are consistent with a previous study from our team, which reported high cesarean section rates in patients with peripheral SpA (48%) and axial SpA (59%) [36], primarily attributed to a high rate of elective CS, often driven by patient choice [36]. Other population-based studies have also documented an elevated frequency of cesarean deliveries in women with chronic inflammatory arthritis [10, 12, 22, 23, 28, 36–44]. Notably, a large Norwegian cohort that examined both first and subsequent births across various inflammatory diagnoses, including RA, PsA, and SPA, reported a two-fold increase in elective cesarean sections among affected women, AxSpA being associated with the highest rate (25%) [45]. Although our dataset did not distinguish between elective and emergency cesarean deliveries, previous studies have suggested that symptoms like pelvic stiffness and pain may lead healthcare providers to prefer planned cesarean sections. In some cases, obstetricians may also opt for a cesarean delivery out of caution, anticipating potential complications during labor, even in the absence of a clear medical indication [18, 23].

Preterm birth is a leading adverse pregnancy outcome and the most frequently reported adverse birth outcome associated with the three with Chronic inflammatory arthritis in the literature. Globally, it remains one of the leading causes of death in children under five years of age [46, 47]. Several neonatal and adulthood comorbidities have been associated with prematurity, including respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, and neurodevelopmental anomalies, among others [46, 48]. In our study, the prevalence of preterm deliveries was 16% in RA, 11.5% in SpA, and 6.9% in PsA, with a statistically higher risk of preterm birth observed in RA and SpA compared to PsA. These findings are consistent with numerous previous reports that have confirmed an increased risk of preterm birth among women with Chronic inflammatory arthritis even after adjustment for multiple confounding factors [10–16, 18, 22, 23, 41, 42, 44, 45, 49, 50].

Moreover, LBW prevalence was the highest among women with RA (13%) and SpA (10.3%) compared to 6.9% among those with PsA. This finding aligns with numerous studies reporting an increased LBW or SGA risk in women with inflammatory diseases, particularly RA, relative to the general population [10, 37–39, 50]. A large retrospective birth cohort study from California [50] provides further supporting evidence. Among 10,244 women diagnosed with an autoimmune rheumatic disease (ARD), including the three subtypes examined in the present study, propensity score-matched analysis revealed significantly elevated LBW ORs of 1.57 for RA and 1.96 for AxSpA [50]. Several mechanisms may explain these associations, including the role of disease activity and poorly controlled arthritis, elevated inflammatory markers (such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1 beta, and interleukin-6), and maternal immune interactions involving circulating fetal cells [51, 52]. Preterm birth, which was relatively prevalent in our cohort, especially among RA and SpA patients, represents a well-established LBW risk factor [10, 12, 44]. Finally, medication effects, particularly from corticosteroids such as prednisone (used by 18.3% of our RA patients), may also contribute to these adverse outcomes [50, 52, 53].

Beyond the heterogeneity observed across the three chronic inflammatory arthritis subtypes, several additional factors that may contribute to the differential risks associated with pregnancy and fetal outcomes were also assessed in our study. These include medication exposure, disease duration, and maternal characteristics such as age at pregnancy, parity, and comorbidities. In line with previous reports [54–56]. This study revealed that advanced maternal age at pregnancy was significantly associated with a higher risk of any adverse outcome, including IUGR, preterm birth, and LBW. Interestingly, longer disease duration conferred a protective effect, being associated with a reduced risk of premature rupture of membranes, which may reflect better disease management or adaptation over time.

Medication use also influenced pregnancy outcomes in our cohort. Prescription patterns varied by chronic inflammatory arthritis subtype: hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine were the most frequently prescribed for RA patients, sulfasalazine for PsA pregnancies (44.8%), and anti-TNF agents for SpA pregnancies (48.3%), aligning with findings from other studies [29, 57]. Interestingly, sulfasalazine use was associated with reduced miscarriage risk and increased likelihood of live birth, and hydroxychloroquine was linked to lower risks of NICU admission and LBW. These findings align with emerging evidence supporting the favorable safety profiles of both medications during pregnancy [58–61] and are corroborated by a 2025 systematic literature review informing the updated EULAR recommendations [58]. Additionally, a 10-year study evaluating medication utilization trends during pregnancy and factors influencing adverse pregnancy outcomes found no significant association between preconception use of these drugs (within three months before conception) and any adverse pregnancy outcome after multivariable analysis, further supporting their safety profile [62]. However, some findings warrant careful interpretation. Both sulfasalazine and TNF inhibitors were associated with an increased likelihood of NICU admissions in our study. This association may reflect the complexity of maternal disease profiles that require more intensive pharmacologic management.

In all cases, further research is needed to better understand the impact of these treatments on the management of pregnant women with chronic inflammatory arthritis, given the complex interactions between medication use, disease activity, comorbidities, and pregnancy outcomes [18].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a monocentric study conducted within a single specialized clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size, particularly in the PsA and SpA subgroups, also reduces the statistical power for subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, robust statistical methods and multivariate analyses were performed to mitigate potential biases. Additionally, information regarding disease activity, including inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), was not systematically available at the time of analysis, limiting the ability to assess the impact of inflammatory activity or flare-ups on pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, the short postpartum follow-up, which focused mainly on delivery and neonatal outcomes, did not allow for the systematic assessment of maternal complications such as postpartum flares. Other potential confounders, including body mass index, smoking status, parity, socioeconomic factors, dietary habits, sleep patterns, genetic predisposition, and other unreported variables, may have contributed to the observed associations in this study. Despite these limitations, the study presents notable strengths. It is one of the few studies conducted over almost seven years in a highly specialized Pregnancy and Rheumatic Disease clinic—one of the largest dedicated to the management of pregnant women with chronic inflammatory arthritis in the Arab region since 2005. Although monocentric, the clinic’s standardized approach to patient management minimizes potential biases related to clinical evaluations and data collection, ensuring consistent follow-up and assessment across patients. Furthermore, this study remains one of the few investigations to comparatively evaluate outcomes across the three principal subtypes of inflammatory arthritis, offering valuable insights into the interactions between chronic inflammatory arthritis and pregnancy outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant differences in maternal and fetal outcomes across RA, PsA, and SpA. RA was associated with higher rates of miscarriage, peripartum complications, preterm birth, and low birth weight compared to SpA. It was also associated with increased preterm births compared to PsA. Maternal age, disease duration, and medication use significantly influence outcomes. These findings highlight the need for tailored care involving rheumatologists and obstetricians to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes. Further research is essential to refine treatment protocols and guide clinical decision-making.
