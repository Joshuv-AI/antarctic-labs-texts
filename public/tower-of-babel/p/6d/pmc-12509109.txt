Intraparenchymal schwannoma in an adult presenting as a periventricular lesion of the parieto-occipital lobes: Diagnostic and surgical challenges
Authors: Christin Berjaoui, Ali Akil, Hussein Akil, Paul Hage, Joseph Mantoura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntraparenchymal schwannomas (IS) are exceedingly rare, constituting less than 1 % of all intracranial schwannomas. To date, approximately 150 cases have been reported in the literature, with only 10 involving the parieto-occipital region. Unlike vestibular schwannomas, IS lacks an apparent association with cranial nerves and often mimics high-grade gliomas or metastatic tumors, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.Research questionHow does the extreme rarity and atypical parieto-occipital location of intraparenchymal schwannomas contribute to diagnostic challenges and potential misdiagnosis?Materials and methodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, MEDLINE, and Google Scholar with the keyword “Intraparenchymal Schwannoma”. A total of 150 previously reported cases were used for the literature.ResultsWe report the case of a 56-year-old female presenting with progressive dysarthria, vertigo, ataxia, and right-sided hemiparesis. MRI revealed a large left parieto-occipital periventricular lesion with mixed solid and cystic components and significant perilesional edema. Given the progressive neurological deterioration, gross total resection of the lesion was performed. Intraoperatively, the tumor exhibited a firm, well-circumscribed morphology. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma, a finding highly unusual in this location.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges of IS and the importance of surgical resection in achieving favorable neurological outcomes. Given their radiological overlap with aggressive neoplasms, advanced neuroimaging, and careful surgical management are essential for optimizing outcomes. Increased awareness of this rare pathology can aid in early recognition, prevent misdiagnosis, and guide effective neurosurgical strategies.

Introduction

Schwannomas are typically benign WHO Grade I tumors originating from Schwann cells, most commonly arising from peripheral and cranial nerves, particularly the vestibulocochlear nerve. Rarely, do these neoplasms occur within the cerebral parenchyma. Intraparenchymal schwannomas (IS) are exceedingly rare, accounting for less than 1 % of all intracranial schwannomas (Kovalainen et al., 2021). Their pathogenesis remains unclear. It is hypothesized that ectopic Schwann cells, displaced during embryogenesis or trapped within the brain parenchyma during development, may give rise to these lesions (Vaphiades and Hackney, 2021). Due to their rarity and atypical presentation, these tumors often mimic more common intracranial pathologies such as gliomas, meningiomas, or metastases, posing diagnostic challenges (Vaphiades and Hackney, 2021).

To date, approximately 150 cases of IS have been reported in the literature, with only 10 cases involving the parieto-occipital region (Kovalainen et al., 2021). This paper presents a case of an IS involving the parieto-occipital region, emphasizing its clinical, radiological, and surgical characteristics.

Case report

Patient Presentation

A 56-year-old female with no significant medical or surgical history presented with a few weeks of dysarthria, vertigo, ataxic gait, and right hemiparesis. Symptoms progressed rapidly, leading to severe disability. The lesion demonstrated predominantly homogeneous enhancement in its larger portion, with an adjacent posterolateral component showing heterogeneous enhancement, suggestive of a possible cystic component. The tumor exerted significant mass effect on the surrounding cerebral parenchyma, accompanied by severe perilesional edema.

The patient underwent gross total resection of the mass. Postoperatively, she experienced diffuse cerebral edema, managed with Mannitol, leading to satisfactory improvement within a few days. At a 1-year follow-up, she had regained near-normal neurological function, and follow-up brain MRI showed no recurrence.

Surgical Management

A C-shaped left occipital incision was made, followed by a left occipital craniotomy. Despite preoperative administration of 250 mL of 20 % Mannitol, cerebral herniation through the craniotomy site was observed. An occipital corticectomy to a depth of 3 cm was performed, enabling visualization of the tumor.

The tumor was firm, resembling a meningioma, with a well-defined cleavage plane facilitating dissection. An additional 200 mL of 20 % Mannitol was administered intraoperatively to facilitate duroplasty, followed by cranioplasty.

Postoperative Course

Post- Operative Day Zero

The patient was intubated and admitted to the ICU for neuromonitoring, then successfully extubated a few hours later upon stabilizing and tolerating pressure support ventilation. Post-extubation, the patient remained somnolent on 4L nasal cannula with SpO2 of 100 %, and stable vitals. The patient was maintained on Mannitol 50g IV every 6 hours, and hypertonic saline with a serum sodium target of 145–150 mmol/L. In addition, Dexamethasone 4mg IV every 6 hours, Cefazolin 1g IV every 8 hours, and Valproic acid 500mg IV every 8 hours were administered.

Post-Operative Day One

The patient remained somnolent on 3L O2 via nasal cannula, with stable vitals, responding only by withdrawing to painful stimuli, with equal pupils and preserved bilateral pupillary, corneal, and gag reflexes. An urgent CT brain was performed, revealing worsening cerebral edema with a 17 mm midline shift and mild uncal herniation (Fig. 2). Post-Operative Day Three

The patient demonstrated clinical improvement but with persistent right-sided hemiparesis and a muscle strength score of 1/5 in the right upper extremity and 2/5 in the right lower extremity. Wernicke's aphasia was present, and the patient was able to initiate oral intake. Mannitol was reduced to 50g IV every 8 hours, while dexamethasone was maintained at 4mg IV every 6 hours.

Post-Operative Days Nine to Thirteen

The patient continued to improve, and mannitol was gradually tapered and discontinued by post-operative day 8. The patient was transferred to the general ward on dexamethasone 4mg every 12 hours and valproic acid 500mg every 8 hours. Clinically stable, the patient was awake, cooperative, and oriented, with persistent right hemiparesis but some improvement in speech.

The patient was discharged home on post-operative day 13 with a tapering prednisone regimen and valproic acid 500mg orally three times daily.

Follow-up on Post-Operative Month Three

The patient remained wheelchair dependent, with slight improvement in right hemiparesis with a muscle strength score of 2/5 in the right upper extremity and 3/5 in the right lower extremity. Her Wernicke's aphasia improved with better comprehension and more coherent speech. No new complaints were reported.

Follow-up on Post-Operative Month Sixteen

At the 16-month follow-up, the patient was able to ambulate independently, with an improved muscle strength score of 3/5 in the right upper extremity and 4+/5 in the right lower extremity, and comprehensive aphasia had fully resolved.

A follow-up brain MRI with Gadolinium was done (Fig. 3) which showed total resection of the tumor with no evidence of residual or recurrence of the lesion. The surgical cavity was filled with cerebrospinal fluid, and the ventricles were normal in size and shape. Histopathology

HE staining revealed spindle-shaped cells arranged in compact, elongated clusters with occasional nuclear palisading, consistent with the Antoni A pattern, as well as less cellular, loosely textured areas with indistinct processes and variable lipidization, typical of the Antoni B pattern. Discussion

Pathogenesis

The etiology of IS remains uncertain. Two main theories have been proposed: developmental and non-developmental. The developmental theory suggests these tumors arise from mesenchymal pial differentiation or migrated neural crest cells that form Schwann cell foci (Menkue et al., 2009). The non-developmental theory proposes that Schwann cells are present in perivascular nerve plexi and large subarachnoid arteries, though their presence in deep cerebral parenchyma is debated (Menkue et al., 2009).

However, Schwann cells are found in adrenergic fibers that supply cerebral arterioles, particularly in the tela choroidea, which may explain their periventricular localization (Menkue et al., 2009).

The parietal lobe appears to be less frequently affected compared to the frontal and temporal lobes, according to the already published cases. This rarity may be attributed to the relative scarcity of perivascular Schwann cells and ectodermal cell entrapment sites within the parietal region, as opposed to the frontal and temporal lobes that lie in closer proximity to cranial base structures and rich perivascular innervation. Consequently, only 10 cases of parietal lobe intraparenchymal schwannomas have been documented in the literature, underscoring the unusual nature of this location (Kovalainen et al., 2021; Menkue et al., 2009).

Clinical Characteristics

IS typically present in patients under 30, with fewer than 20 % occurring after age 50. A slight male predominance has been observed. Most cases are supratentorial, especially in the frontal lobe (Kovalainen et al., 2021). Only 10 cases involving the parieto-occipital region have been reported, making our case notable. Clinical Manifestations

Symptoms vary with tumor location and mass effect. Presentations include headache, vomiting, seizures, dysarthria, visual disturbances, ataxia, and hemiparesis. Symptoms vary with tumor location and mass effect. Presentations include headache, vomiting, seizures, dysarthria, visual disturbances, ataxia, and hemiparesis (Zhang et al., 2022). In our patient, dysarthria, vertigo, ataxia, and right hemiparesis were attributed to the lesion's parieto-occipital location and significant perilesional edema.

Diagnosing IS

Preoperative diagnosis of IS is challenging due to their rarity and atypical radiologic features. IS may resemble gliomas, meningiomas, lymphomas, or ependymomas. On CT, these tumors can appear hypo- or hyperdense, solid or cystic, often with perilesional edema. MRI may show variable intensity and heterogeneous or homogeneous consistency. Our patient's MRI showed a predominantly homogeneous mass with a cystic posterolateral component and extensive edema (Khoo and Taki, 2012). On CT, these tumors can appear hypo- or hyperdense, solid or cystic, often with perilesional edema (Khoo and Taki, 2012). MRI may show variable intensity and heterogeneous or homogeneous consistency (Kovalainen et al., 2021). Similarly, the MRI of the patient in our case demonstrated similar findings, with a predominantly homogeneous enhancement of the mass in its larger portion, and a heterogeneous enhancement of its posterolateral component suggestive of a cystic component. In addition, a huge perilesional edema was present.

Histopathology remains the most definitive diagnostic tool (Sharma et al., 2012). Intraparenchymal schwannomas are distinguished by two distinct tissue types. Antoni A areas show palisading spindle-shaped cells and Verocay bodies, while Antoni B consists of loosely arranged cells in a myxoid stroma. Immunohistochemistry further confirms diagnosis through S-100 and vimentin positivity (Sharma et al., 2012). For better confirmation, immunohistochemistry should be done as schwannomas display a significant widespread reactivity to S-100 protein and vimentin filaments (Khoo and Taki, 2012; Sharma et al., 2012). In our case, S-100 and SOX-10 expression supported the diagnosis.

Management

Gross total resection is the treatment of choice for IS due to their benign nature and favorable prognosis (Kovalainen et al., 2021). The well-circumscribed nature of these tumors usually allows for safe and complete removal. Adjuvant radiotherapy is rarely required and is generally reserved for malignant variants (Kovalainen et al., 2021).

In our case, the patient underwent total resection with significant neurological improvement and no recurrence 16 months postoperatively.

Conclusion

Intraparenchymal schwannomas, particularly those located in the parieto-occipital region, are an exceedingly rare entity. Their nonspecific clinical and radiological features make preoperative diagnosis particularly challenging. Gross total resection remains the cornerstone of treatment and is leads to favorable outcomes. This case contributes to the limited literature on the topic and highlights the potential for achieving complete neurological recovery with an extremely low risk of recurrence.
