Clinical Characteristics, Complications, and Outcomes of Heat Stroke Among Pilgrims During the Hajj Mass Gathering: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Namareq Aldardeer, Abdulrahman Harisi, Saleh S Alzahrani, Nada Alkhani, Ahmed O Alenazi, Abdulmajeed M Alshehri, Ayman Alahdal, Hanan Mohammed Almalki, Reham Alharbi, Rahaf Alqahtani, Lina Al-Dawood, Ohoud Aljuhani, Shady Kamel, Badr Alghamdi, Mohammed Alshennawi, Sahar Fahad Almushikah, Khalid Al Sulaiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeat stroke is a leading cause of mortality during the Hajj season, yet data on its clinical progression remain limited.ObjectiveThis study evaluates the clinical characteristics, complications, and outcomes of heat stroke among pilgrims.MethodsThis prospective exploratory cohort study included adult pilgrims (≥18 years) who were admitted with a confirmed diagnosis of heat stroke, defined as a core temperature ≥40°C and central nervous system dysfunction, across nine hospitals. The primary endpoint measure is the frequency of at least one heat stroke-related complication. Patients were stratified into those discharged from the treating ICU and those transferred to an advanced referral center, with transfer used as a pragmatic surrogate for greater clinical severity within the Hajj healthcare system.ResultsTwenty-three patients were included (mean age 65.1 ± 10.5 years; 56.5% male). Fifteen (65.2%) were discharged from the treating ICU, whereas eight (34.8%) were transferred to an advanced referral center. Compared with discharged patients, the transferred group showed higher ccreatinine upon admission (165.50 (146.00–185.50) vs. 130.50 (114.25–152.25); P=0.02), Peak creatinine values (275.90 (223.32–328.80) vs. 143.00 (126.00–160.50); p = 0.001), peak Urea values (17.40 (13.70–23.95) vs. 11.00 (8.70–13.00); p = 0.01); and the highest AST during admission was also higher in the transferred group (P=0.015). The lowest platelet counts during the patient’s admission were significantly reduced in the transferred group (p = 0.007). Mechanical ventilation was required more often in the transferred group (75.0% vs. 26.7%; p = 0.026), and its duration was longer (p = 0.019). Given the small sample size, these findings should be considered exploratory.ConclusionIn this small, exploratory cohort, patients requiring transfer to an advanced referral center generally had a greater burden of organ dysfunction and required more critical care interventions. These observations should not be interpreted as establishing predictors of severe heat stroke because transfer status may also reflect healthcare system factors. Larger prospective multicentre studies are required to determine whether these observed differences are reproducible and clinically meaningful.

Introduction

The annual Hajj pilgrimage is one of the largest mass gatherings in the world, drawing approximately two million Muslims to the holy city of Mecca each year.1 Mecca’s climate features high temperatures, especially during the summer months when Hajj typically occurs. Throughout the pilgrimage, pilgrims spend extended periods outdoors, walking long distances, and are frequently exposed to direct sunlight in densely crowded areas. These factors, combined with rising global temperatures, significantly increase the risk of heat-related illnesses, including heat exhaustion and heatstroke.1

Heat-related illness encompasses a spectrum ranging from heat exhaustion to life-threatening heat stroke.2 Heat stroke is characterized by severe hyperthermia, typically with a core body temperature ≥40°C, accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction such as delirium, seizures, or coma.2 There are two forms of heat stroke: classic heat stroke, which primarily affects older adults and individuals with chronic medical conditions, and exertional heat stroke, which occurs in otherwise healthy individuals during strenuous physical activity in hot environments.2,3

Risk factors for heat stroke include environmental heat exposure, advanced age, obesity, chronic medical conditions, medication use, dehydration, inadequate heat acclimatization, and genetic susceptibility.4 During Hajj, these risks are further amplified by prolonged walking, physical exertion, crowd density, behavioral factors such as inadequate hydration and delayed symptom reporting, and limited awareness or adherence to preventive measures, particularly among pilgrims traveling from cooler climates.1,4

Historically, heat-related illness has been a major challenge during Hajj in high-temperature seasons. In August 1985, approximately 2000 cases of heat stroke were reported, with over 1000 deaths within days of hospitalization.5 Epidemiological analyses have demonstrated substantially higher heat stroke incidence during “hot cycles” than cooler periods.1 During Hajj 2024, extreme temperatures exceeding 52°C were associated with a marked increase in heat-related illnesses, particularly among unregistered pilgrims, despite extensive preventive measures. More than 2700 heat-related cases were reported in a single day, underscoring the ongoing public health burden and the need for continued prevention, early recognition, and timely management of heat-related illnesses.6

During Hajj 2025, the Ministry of Health announced a 90% reduction in cases due to strict prevention measures and restrictions on unregistered pilgrims. Although all prevention measures were implemented and the ritual hospitals in Hajj are well prepared for heat stroke cases by providing timely triage, advanced supportive care capacity, expanded ICU capacity, and clear referral pathways to tertiary facilities, cases with complications are still reported. Patients with heat stroke are initially managed in dedicated ritual hospitals, while those requiring advanced organ support or specialized critical care are transferred to tertiary referral centers. Therefore, comparing patients discharged from the treating ICU with those requiring transfer provides a clinically meaningful surrogate for heatstroke severity and enables the identification of factors associated with more severe disease. Indeed, knowledge of heat stroke in the existing literature primarily comes from case reports and retrospective studies.7 Most of the prospective studies on heat stroke focus on cooling intervention and neurological prognostication. Consequently, prospective studies on clinically important data are scarce. Therefore, this prospective multicentre cohort study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, complications, and outcomes of heat stroke among pilgrims during Hajj.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This prospective exploratory cohort study was conducted during the 2025 Hajj season across multiple hospitals in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, from June 4th to 14th, 2025. The participating centers included Ajyad Emergency Hospital, Alharam Emergency Hospital, East Arafat Hospital, Jabal Al-rahma Hospital, Alharam Hospital, Mena Alwadi Hospital, Mena Emergency Hospital, Mena New Street Hospital, and Mena Bridge Hospital, for a total of nine centers. Patients were categorized as either discharged from the treating ICU or transferred to an advanced referral center. Within the Hajj healthcare system, transfer is generally undertaken when patients require specialized investigations, advanced organ support, or services unavailable at the initial treating hospital. Therefore, transfer status was used as a pragmatic surrogate for greater clinical severity, while acknowledging that organizational factors may also influence transfer decisions.

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. The study was reviewed and approved by the Global Center for Mass Gatherings Medicine and the Ministry of Health Institutional Review Board with the approval number (25–118E).

Participants and Eligibility

The study included all pilgrims aged 18 years or older who were admitted to the intensive care units of one of the participating hospitals, with a clinical diagnosis of heat stroke. We excluded all patients without a confirmed diagnosis of heat stroke.

Data Collection

Demographic variables collected included age, sex, weight, nationality, and pilgrim status (registered vs. unregistered). Clinical data comprised the presence of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, liver disease, chronic respiratory disease, history of stroke, and immunocompromised status. Information on home medications was obtained, with a focus on antihypertensive and antidiabetic agents.

Clinical management data included body temperature assessments, capturing the measurement method (rectal, oral, or underarm) and the initial temperature recorded at admission. The duration required to achieve cooling below 37.8 °C was documented, along with the specific cooling interventions used, such as ice packs, chemical ice packs, cold infusions, or fans. Details of intravenous fluid therapy were collected, including the type of fluid administered and the total volume given. Additional variables included the presence of burned skin; the requirement for mechanical ventilation and its duration; the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score in non-intubated patients; and the use of vasopressors, with specification of the agents employed.

Laboratory parameters included hematological, renal function tests, hepatic markers, musculoskeletal markers, and electrolytes, which were measured at admission and at peak or nadir values during admission. Infectious workup and antimicrobial use were also documented, including the suspected source of infection, the empirical antibiotic, antifungal, or antiviral therapy, their coverage spectrum, and duration of use.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the frequency of at least one heat stroke complication in each arm, such as acute kidney injury (AKI), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hepatic failure, rhabdomyolysis, seizures, shivering, cardiac failure, and cardiac arrest. Secondary outcomes include the requirement for mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support, length of ICU stay, and the normalization of laboratory values (hematological, renal, hepatic, and electrolyte parameters) before discharge or transfer.

Statistical Analysis

The sample comprised all eligible patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period. Given the limited sample size, all statistical analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Therefore, statistical comparisons should be interpreted with caution and should be confirmed in larger prospective studies.

Continuous variables were summarized as either the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or the median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between the transferred and discharged groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, the independent-samples Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for non-normally distributed data. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0.

Males constituted 56.5% of the population. Gender distribution was similar between groups (p = 0.645). Documented comorbidities were limited, with hypertension in 13%, diabetes mellitus in 4.3%, and no cases of ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, or liver disease. There were no statistically significant differences in comorbidities or home medication use between the groups (Table 1).

The apparent discrepancy between the low prevalence of documented comorbidities and the higher frequency of home medication use reflects differences in data completeness. During emergency Hajj admissions, pre-existing medical histories were frequently unavailable due to language barriers and patients’ critical condition. In contrast, medication histories were more reliably obtained from the medications patients carried.

Cooling methods included ice packs (56%), fans (52%), cold infusions (21%), and chemical ice packs (21%). Fan use was higher in the discharged group (p = 0.057). Intravenous fluids were administered to 95.6% of patients. Sodium chloride 0.9% was the most used fluid (82%). The mean fluid volume was higher among transferred patients (2071.43 ± 1426.78 mL) compared to discharged patients (1230.77 ± 856.72 mL), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.114). Burned skin was noted in two patients (8.6%), both from the transferred group (p = 0.043) (Table 1).

Mechanical ventilation was required in 43% of patients. This was significantly more common among transferred patients (75%) than among discharged patients (26.7%; p = 0.026). The median duration of intubation was statistically significantly longer in the transferred group, 72.00 (27.00–72.00), compared to the discharged group, 7.00 (5.50–9.75) (P=0.019) (Table 1).

Clinical Outcomes

All patients in the transferred group had at least one heat stroke-related complication. Acute kidney injury (AKI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) were significantly higher among the transferred patients (100% vs. 33.3%; p = 0.002), and (62.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.001), respectively. Additionally, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hepatic failure, and seizures were observed more frequently in transferred patients; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance in all cases. In contrast, the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis (p = 0.842) and shivering (p = 0.278) was higher in the discharged group but did not differ significantly between the two groups. The average length of stay was significantly longer in the discharged group (0.9 ± 0.4 days) than in the transferred group (0.08 ± 0.04 days); the shorter length of stay in the transferred group likely reflects the expedited transfer process necessitated by the need for advanced or specialized care. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.017) (Table 2).

Vasopressor use was more common among transferred patients, with 50% requiring vasopressor support compared to 13.3% in the discharged group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.131). Norepinephrine was the primary vasopressor administered, followed by vasopressin (Table 2).

Normalization of laboratory parameters by the time of discharge or transfer was less frequently observed in the transferred group. Complete normalization of laboratory values occurred in all patients in the discharged group (3/3, 100%), whereas none of the patients in the transferred group achieved full normalization (p = 0.175). Furthermore, recovery of specific parameters, such as platelet count, serum creatinine, and creatine kinase levels, was less common among transferred patients, suggesting slower or incomplete resolution of organ dysfunction in this group (Table 2).

Laboratory Characteristics

Antibiotic Administration

Antibiotics were administered to approximately 70% of patients included in the study. A significant difference in antibiotic prescribing patterns was observed between patients discharged from the ICU and those transferred to another hospital. Transferred patients were significantly more likely to receive antibiotics covering gram-negative (p = 0.021) and gram-positive organisms (p = 0.001), suggesting a tendency toward broader empirical coverage in this group. Nevertheless, the overall use of most broad-spectrum antibiotics was limited. Piperacillin/tazobactam emerged as the most frequently prescribed agent, administered to nearly half of the study population (47.8%). In contrast, ceftriaxone (13.0%), meropenem (13.0%), amikacin (8.6%), and ciprofloxacin (4.3%) were used in only a small proportion of patients in both groups. Additionally, antifungal agents were significantly more used in the transferred group (p = 0.056), possibly reflecting concern for severe or unconfirmed systemic infections.

When evaluating suspected sources of infection, CNS infections were exclusively suspected in the transferred group (p = 0.043). No significant differences were noted for other suspected sources such as respiratory, renal, or gastrointestinal infections.

Discussion

This study examined the challenges of hospitalized patients with heat stroke during the 2025 Hajj season, highlighting their clinical characteristics, outcomes, and management strategies.

Among transferred patients, those who exhibited significantly higher creatinine levels on admission and at peak may reflect a more severe systemic insult and a higher incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI). These findings are consistent with earlier reports linking heat stroke to direct thermal injury to renal tissue, hypoperfusion, and rhabdomyolysis-mediated nephrotoxicity. We acknowledge the seminal work by Abdelmoety et al, which reported a higher incidence of laboratory abnormalities in patients admitted with heatstroke (high glucose level (45.0%), high creatinine concentration (41.3%), hyponatremia (26.3%), and high blood urea nitrogen (BUN) concentration (26.3%).14 Similarly, higher sodium and chloride concentrations in transferred patients may indicate more profound dehydration and free water loss, supporting the importance of rapid fluid resuscitation in mitigating renal complications.

Consistent with our findings regarding patient management of heatstroke, previous reports showed that Hajj participants were managed according to heat illness guidelines recommending cooling, fluid resuscitation, and laboratory investigations to exclude organ damage, based on patient conditions.9 The ice packs and fans were the most frequently used cooling strategies reported in our study. This is consistent with the findings of Abdelmoety et al, who noted that most patients with heat illness were managed using fans, water spray, and ice packs to reduce body temperature.14 Similarly, reviews on mass gatherings emphasize that evaporative cooling methods such as misting combined with fan airflow and application of ice packs to key body areas are among the most practical and logistically feasible first-line strategies when rapid cold-water immersion is not readily available, further supporting the utilization pattern observed in our study.15

The morbidity rate and organ support in patients with heatstroke have been the subject of clinical examination. Notably, the reported respiratory support during the hajj season reached up to 29.3%.16 Compared to our study, mechanical ventilation was required in 43% of patients. This was significantly more common among transferred patients (75%) than among discharged patients (26.7%; p = 0.026).

The increased prescription of broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungal agents among transferred patients raises several significant considerations. While infection can complicate severe heat stroke, particularly in the context of multiorgan failure and invasive device use, there is also a risk of antimicrobial overuse in critically ill patients with non-infectious systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). Underscoring the need for robust diagnostic stewardship to prevent unnecessary antimicrobial exposure. This concern is echoed in a recent study, which emphasizes that in settings where rapid diagnostic clarity is lacking, excessive empiric use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals in critically ill patients may inadvertently contribute to antimicrobial resistance and adverse outcomes.17

This study has some limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limited the precision of the statistical estimates and the ability to detect modest differences between groups, increasing the possibility of both type I and type II errors. Consequently, the statistical analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Although the cohort included all eligible heat stroke cases identified across the participating centers during the study period, larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings. Moreover, no formal sample size or power calculation was performed because this exploratory study included all consecutive eligible patients presenting during the predefined study period across the participating centers. On the other hand, transfer to an advanced referral center was used as a pragmatic surrogate for greater clinical severity. Although transfers within the Hajj healthcare system generally reflect the need for specialized care unavailable at the initial treating hospital, we acknowledge that organizational and operational factors may also influence referral decisions. Therefore, some degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Also, due to high case turnover, emergency conditions, and language barriers, obtaining accurate medical histories from patients was challenging. As a result, pre-existing comorbidities may have been incompletely documented, while medication history may have been more reliably verified from the medications patients carried with them. Furthermore, data on environmental heat exposure duration, the impact of IV fluid type on organ functions, and the time to initiation of cooling measures were unavailable. Furthermore, follow-up data after transfer to advanced referral centers were unavailable. Consequently, clinically important outcomes such as in-hospital mortality, long-term organ recovery, and total hospital length of stay could not be evaluated, limiting the assessment of the full clinical course of heat stroke. From a public health perspective, these findings reinforce the importance of pre-Hajj education, early recognition of symptoms, and rapid cooling measures for high-risk groups.

Conclusion

In this exploratory prospective multicenter cohort, substantial organ dysfunction and intensive care interventions were observed among pilgrims with heat stroke, particularly in patients requiring transfer to advanced referral centers, which served as a pragmatic surrogate for greater clinical severity within the Hajj healthcare system. These findings suggest potential clinical trends rather than established predictors of severe heat stroke and should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and other study limitations. Larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these observations, better characterize factors related to severe outcomes, and inform strategies for early recognition, timely cooling, risk stratification, and healthcare preparedness during Hajj.
