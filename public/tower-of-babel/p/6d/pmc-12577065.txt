Small cell lung cancer: relationship with HLA-I alleles and gene mutation
Authors: Xuanpeng Wu, Fei Xue, Tao Jiang, Hao Wang, Ming Ni, Zhikun Jia, Yunfeng Ma, Tianju Wang, Qifei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell lung cancer is the most malignant subtype of lung cancer and has a poor prognosis. However, the mechanism is not well understood. The aim of this study was to investigate the characteristics of gene mutation in small cell lung cancer patients and the differences in HLA-I alleles between small cell lung cancer patients and healthy controls, and to explore the effects of gene mutations and specific HLA-I alleles on small-cell lung cancer from the perspective of immune regulation.MethodsDifferences in HLA-I allele frequencies between the two groups were analyzed in a case-control study. Genetic testing was performed using target gene capture and sequencing method. The association between small cell lung cancer, HLA-I alleles, and gene mutation was analyzed by statistical methods.ResultsThe small cell lung cancer group showed significantly higher HLA-A*11:02 (p < 0.001, OR = 4.693, 95%CI = 1.737–12.677), B*40:01 (p = 0.020, OR = 1.916, 95%CI = 1.101–3.333), and B*55:02 (p = 0.037, OR = 2.565, 95%CI = 1.027–6.408) and significantly lower B*44:03 (p = 0.015, OR = 0.196, 95%CI = 0.046–0.843) and C*06:02 (p = 0.005, OR = 0.369, 95%CI = 0.178–0.765) than the control group. Haplotype analysis results showed that HLA-A*11:01-B*51:01 (p = 0.044, OR = 3.433, 95%CI = 0.959–12.292), A*02:06-C*07:02 (p = 0.005, OR = 11.459, 95%CI = 1.331–98.689), A*11:01-C*07:02 (p = 0.007, OR = 3.040, 95%CI = 1.311–7.047), and B*46:01-C*01:02 (p = 0.048, OR = 2.310, 95%CI = 0.986–5.415) frequencies significantly increased and A*02:01-B*13:02 (p = 0.036, OR < 0.001, 95%CI = 0.000-0.918) and B*13:02-C*06:02 (p = 0.009, OR = 0.108, 95%CI = 0.014–0.810) frequency significantly decreased in small cell lung cancer patients. Three-locus haplotype analysis showed that the differences between small cell lung cancer patients and controls were not statistically significant.ConclusionHLA-A*11:01, B*40:01, and B*55:02 may be the immune modulators associated with susceptibility and HLA-B*44:03 and C*06:02 act as the immune modulators associated with resistance of small cell lung cancer. Haplotypes HLA-A*11:01-B*51:01, A*02:06-C*07:02, A*11:01-C*07:02, and B*46:01-C*01:02 are positively associated with small cell lung cancer. Haplotypes A*02:01-B*13:02 and B*13:02-C*06:02 are negatively associated with small cell lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03645-x.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide with the highest incidence rate. Globally, lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer mortality in men and the second highest cause of cancer death in women, after breast cancer [1]. According to Chinese national statistics, approximately 631,000 deaths were reported because of lung cancer annually [2]. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is considered as one of the most lethal malignancies with a 5-year survival rate of less than 7%, and making up approximately 13–15% of all lung cancer cases [3, 4]. SCLC can be classified into limited-stage SCLC (LS-SCLC) and extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) with the latter accounting for 60%−70% [5]. SCLC is seen as a highly aggressive and recalcitrant disease due to rapid proliferation, high vascularity, apoptotic imbalance, and high genomic instability. In addition, SCLC is also characterized by a tendency towards early extensive metastasis [4, 6]. Although the incidence of SCLC has gradually declined in recent years, the improvement in 5-year overall survival was statistically significant but clinically modest [7]. Unlike in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), there has been no major treatment advances in the past few decades beyond the standard combination of platinum-based chemotherapy and radiation until 2018 [8].

Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) exerts a critical role in tumor antigen presentation and anti-tumor immune response. HLA-I is one of the classic HLA molecules and is expressed in almost all kinds of cells. It serves as a recognition marker for CD-8 positive T cells and several studies have demonstrated the role of HLA-I-specific CD-8 positive T cell responses in anti-tumor immune response [9–11]. Downregulation of HLA-I expression was proved to be associated with acquired resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) therapy in advanced NSCLC patients [12]. Tumor cells reduce antigen presentation and achieve immune escape through a variety of mechanisms, including downregulation of HLA-Ⅰ expression in tumor cells [13]. A previous study revealed the correlation between the HLA-A02 supertype and improved survival in NSCLC patients [14]. Another study demonstrated that the HLA-B44 supertype was associated with poor outcomes in NSCLC patients [15]. All these studies have proved that HLA-I is closely associated to lung cancer. However, the relationship between HLA-I and SCLC is not well established.

Nowadays, a variety of tumor-related gene mutations have been discovered in lung cancer patients, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), etc [16, 17]. Although the mutation patterns vary in different geographical regions and patient groups [18], these genes can all be called cancer driver genes, indicating their important role in carcinogenesis. Considerable evidence has confirmed that the most significant molecular pathogenesis in SCLC is the almost universal loss of the tumor suppressor genes TP53 and RB1 [8, 19]. Researchers posit that the high genomic instability caused by the mutation of TP53 and RB1, as well as long-term exposure to tobacco carcinogens, are primary factors constituting to SCLC having one of the highest mutational burden among tumors [6]. Undoubtedly, multiple gene mutations contribute significantly to the occurrence and progression of SCLC.

As described earlier, the treatment methods for SCLC have not made significant progress over the years. For decades, platinum-etoposide chemotherapy has remained the standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC. Despite an initial sensitivity of up to 75% to standard chemotherapy, ES-SCLC patients inevitably relapse with the development of chemoresistance [5]. Recently, immunotherapies and targeted therapies have revolutionized the precise cancer treatment for NSCLC patients [20]. However, the efficacy of anti-tumor immunity in SCLC is relatively low as even the most promising combination strategy of first-line chemotherapy plus anti-PD-1/PD-L1 only achieved limited efficacy for ES-SCLC, resulting in a median survival increase of approximately 3 months [21–23]. In a phase 3 randomized clinical trial, adding toripalimab to first-line chemotherapy significantly improved survival in patients with ES-SCLC. The treatment exhibited an acceptable safety profile, supporting this combination regimen as a new treatment option for patients with ES-SCLC [24]. However, current immunotherapeutic strategies have not fundamentally reversed the treatment dilemma of this malignant disease. Deeper research is needed to explore effective methods to identify patients who may benefit from immunotherapy.

Given the role of gene mutations in driving SCLC and the potential impact of HLA-I alleles on immune surveillance, we investigated the gene mutation characteristics of SCLC and whether specific HLA-I genotypes and gene mutations affect SCLC.

Methods

Study design

This case-control study was performed after the participants were enrolled. HLA-I genotyping, HLA-I allele frequencies and haplotype frequencies detection, and gene mutation detection were conducted separately. Then, different statistical analysis was used to determine the association between small cell lung cancer, HLA-I alleles, and gene mutation.

The protocol and all modifications were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2020LSK-057).

Participants

Patients enrolled in this study were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University from June 2019 to October 2020. Eligible patients were from the Han population, aged at least 18 years and not older than 70 years, and diagnosed with SCLC by pathology. Other eligibility criteria: There was no significant other organ dysfunction or malignant tumor; patients had not received any treatment for at least 6 months before study entry.

Key exclusion criteria were other malignancies diagnosed or treated within 5 years before study entry (excluding resected basal cell carcinoma of the skin or other carcinoma in situ); received systemic anti-tumor therapy including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy before study entry; active or previous autoimmune or inflammatory disorders and history of active primary immunodeficiency.

Healthy controls were defined as asymptomatic, no physical signs, no abnormalities in laboratory and imaging tests in the same age group from the same period of checkup people. All participants provided written informed consent for participation.

HLA-I genotyping

HLA-A, -B, and -C genotyping was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) -based genotyping method at high-resolution level. As previously described, this method performed massively parallel paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform with efficient variant staging and haploid sequence assembly pipelines [25]. Genetic testing data were sequenced and analyzed by Genecast Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

HLA-I allele frequencies and haplotype frequencies

HLA-I testing was carried out as per Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using Arlequin software version 3.11 (http://cmpg.unibe.ch/software/arlequin 3).

Haplotype frequencies were calculated based on the EM algorithm using Arlequin.

Gene mutation detection

Gene mutations were identified using TruSight Oncology 500 ctDNA v2 Kit (Illumina Inc, USA). Leverage low cfDNA inputs (20 ng) from minimally invasive blood samples to achieve sensitive and accurate detection using UMI-based hybrid-capture library prep and deep sequencing. Genetic testing was performed using targeted gene capture sequencing method on the Illumina sequencing platform.

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test and Mann-Whitney test were used to test the differences of gender and age between groups. Differences in HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C allele frequencies between small cell lung cancer patients and healthy controls were compared using a chi-square test. Alleles (< 3%) and haplotypes (< 2%) with low frequencies were excluded from the analysis due to inadequate statistical power [26].

The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for each allele. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA).

Logistic regression was used to analyze the effects of gene mutations and specific HLA-I alleles on SCLC. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Forest plot was plotted using R version 4.2.1.

Results

Clinical characteristic

Of all the 107 SCLC patients enrolled in this study, 82 (76.6%) patients were male and 25 (23.4%) patients were female. The average age of the SCLC patients was 62.73 ± 9.782 years old. In the healthy control group, 161 (67.1%) cases were male and 79 (32.9%) cases were female. The average age of the healthy control group was 61.85 ± 1.847 years old. No statistical differences were observed in the age and gender distribution between SCLC patients and healthy controls (Table 1).

Gene mutation and SCLC

Considering the important role of gene mutation in the pathogenesis of lung cancer, we conducted a study of gene mutation in SCLC patients. According to our results, a total of 286 mutated genes were detected in 107 patients. We listed the top 20 genes with the highest mutation frequency (Table 4). According to our results, TP53 and RB1 had the highest mutation frequency (96.3% and 68.2%, respectively), which was consistent with the results of previous studies [27, 28].

Relationship of gene mutation and HLA-I alleles in the occurrence of SCLC

Given the impact of HLA-I alleles on immune regulation and the role of gene mutations in driving SCLC, we sought to validate the impact of both on the occurrence of SCLC. To ensure the reliability of the data, we selected genes with a mutation frequency > 3% for further analysis, and a total of 102 mutated genes were included. Logistic regression was performed on the above 102 mutated genes and 5 HLA-I alleles (statistically associated with SCLC). The results showed that the concurrent occurrence of the 102 mutated genes and the expression changes of the 5 HLA-I alleles was not observed in SCLC patients. (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Lung cancer is caused by many factors. Due to its high morbidity and mortality, it has become one of the most malignant tumors worldwide. The treatment of SCLC has long been a global challenge. In the past 30 years, there have been no significant breakthroughs in the treatment of SCLC. With the rapid development of immunotherapy, the application of ICIs has made great progress in the treatment of lung cancer. However, the efficacy in SCLC is unsatisfactory compared to NSCLC, only a small subset of patients can benefit. The relatively late genomic exploration of SCLC and the lack of abundant data have led to little knowledge about effective predictive factors, highlighting the need for better immune biomarkers to optimize the therapeutic options for SCLC. The function of HLA-I in exposing foreign antigens to the host immune system renders it a critical role in immunotherapy [29]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the anti-tumor activity of ICIs depends on HLA-I-related immune activity and resistance to immunotherapy is associated with defective antigen presentation mediated by HLA-I [30, 31]. Ricciuti et al. [32] found that there was a significant decrease in HLA-I expression in the ICI therapy cohort at the time of acquired resistance compared with the chemotherapy and the targeted therapy cohorts. Considering its importance in the human anti-tumor immune system, we analyzed the association between HLA-Ⅰ alleles and SCLC. As mentioned above, a variety of gene mutations play a crucial role in the occurrence and progression of SCLC. Therefore, we further analyzed whether gene mutations and specific HLA-I alleles are associated with the occurrence of SCLC. In this study, we conducted genetic studies on SCLC patients and observed that HLA-A*02:07, A*11:02, B*40:01, B*55:02, and C*07:02 frequencies were significantly higher in SCLC patients than in healthy controls (p < 0.05). HLA-A*01:01, A*30:01, B*13:02, B*44:03, and C*06:02 frequencies were significantly lower in SCLC patients than in healthy controls (p < 0.05). Considering that linkage disequilibrium could affect the association between HLA-I alleles and SCLC, logistic regression was used to further validate whether the association of HLA-I alleles with SCLC was intrinsically related or due to linkage disequilibrium. Based on the results, there were 5 alleles intrinsically associated with SCLC. Specifically, HLA-A*11:02, B*40:01, and B*55:02 may be the immune modulators associated with susceptibility of SCLC. These alleles may activate oncogenes and promote the occurrence of SCLC. And HLA-B*44:03 and C*06:02 act as the immune modulators associated with resistance of SCLC. In addition, haplotype analysis revealed that the frequencies of HLA-A*11:01-B*51:01, A*02:06-C*07:02, A*11:01-C*07:02, and B*46:01-C*01:02 significantly increased (p < 0.05) and that of HLA-A*02:01-B*13:02 and B*13:02-C*06:02 significantly decreased (p < 0.05) in the patient group, suggesting that these haplotypes may act as biomarkers of SCLC. It should be noted that this study’s insights into HLA alleles and haplotypes for SCLC susceptibility and resistance were incremental. The primary role of HLA-I alleles and haplotypes is to modulate immunity rather than directly alter cellular behavior like oncogenes or tumor suppressor gene. They influence immune recognition rather than promoting oncogenesis outright. The abnormal expression of HLA-I alleles might facilitate immune escape by affecting tumor antigen presentation, but these effects are context-dependent rather than deterministic. Therefore, further investigation is needed to reveal the underlying mechanisms.

Many studies have confirmed the abnormal expression of HLA-I alleles in a variety of tumors. Yu et al. [33] found that HLA-A*11:01 played a protective role in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, while A*02:07, A*33:03, B*38:02 and B*58:01 increased the risk of disease. According to our previous report, [34] HLA-A*30:01, B*13:02, and C*06:02 were higher and HLA-B*51:01 and C*14:01 were lower in lung adenocarcinoma patients than that of healthy controls. Yoshimura et al. [35] proved that the frequencies of HLA-A33, B44, B62, and B75 were lower in lung cancer patients than in the control group. Our study also found that the frequency of HLA-B*44:03 was lower in SCLC patients than in the healthy controls, which means that the HLA-B44 supertype may resist the occurrence of lung cancer. However, this conclusion is not consistent with the findings of Cummings et al. [15], and we speculate that the differences are due to different subtypes of lung cancer and ethnic differences. Loss of heterozygosity in HLA-Ⅰ (HLA-Ⅰ LOH), as a form of abnormal expression of HLA-I alleles, has long been reported, indicating that HLA-Ⅰ is a common event in many malignant tumors. Researchers observed a trend towards a negative correlation between HLA-I LOH and survival outcomes in NSCLC patients treated with the combination of first-line therapy and immunotherapy [36]. In addition, HLA-I LOH was associated with higher mutation rates of several tumor suppressor genes, including TP53 and LRP1B [37]. To the best of our knowledge, the results of the current study on HLA-Ⅰ expression in lung cancer are not completely consistent with previous studies. Therefore, further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms leading to the different findings.

The gene mutations identified in cancers are vital to the development of anti-tumor therapy. Due to a limited number of patients available for study, comprehensive genome study on oncogenic driver mutations in SCLC is currently progressing slowly compared to other kinds of cancer. Nowadays, a variety of gene mutations in lung cancer have been discovered. It is well known that lung cancer is caused by environmental variables, genetic susceptibility, and many other factors. Considering the role of HLA-Ⅰ alleles and gene mutations in lung cancer progression, we aimed to explore whether there is an association between these two factors and the occurrence of SCLC. According to our report, the simultaneous occurrence of 102 gene mutations and changes in the expression of 5 specific HLA-I alleles was not observed in SCLC. The relationship between the two has not been demonstrated in SCLC. Regarding this conclusion, we believe that there are several possible reasons. First of all, two of the five studied alleles are associated with resistance of SCLC, so they may not cause SCLC. Secondly, we selected the genes with a mutation frequency >3% to investigate the association with the occurrence of SCLC. However, rare gene mutations could interact strongly and may have important biological contributions. Excluding rare mutations may ignore potentially meaningful findings, especially in a highly heterogeneous disease like SCLC. Finally, previous studies have suggested that epigenetic modifications may affect HLA-I expression [38]. This study focused solely on gene mutation, and did not consider the effects of epigenetic modifications such as promoter methylation or histone modification on HLA expression. The potential immune escape mechanism may affect the exploration of the interaction between HLA-I allele expression and gene mutation and their association with the occurrence of SCLC.

In this study, our insights into the HLA-I alleles and haplotypes associated with SCLC, as well as whether there is an association between gene mutations, specific HLA-I alleles and the occurrence of SCLC, was incremental. Chen et al. [39] found that four upregulated genes (TOP2A, CDKN2A, BIRC5 and MSH2) were associated with platinum resistance in SCLC and normal lung samples, whereas HLA family genes were downregulated in SCLC. Immune cell infiltration analysis showed that antigen presentation was weak in SCLC, and HLA-I expression was positively correlated with M1 macrophages, memory B cells, and CD8+T cells. TOP2A was also found to be associated with poor prognosis and negatively associated with HLA-I. Similarly, our study provides guidance for investigating factors influencing SCLC from an immunomodulatory and genomic perspective, and may provide a theoretical basis for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy in SCLC. However, future studies need to integrate information from genetics, epigenetics and transcriptomics to fully explain the pathogenesis of SCLC and ultimately provide more effective methods for clinical treatment of SCLC.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, a case-control method was used. The patients and healthy controls were selected from a single hospital, which may introduce selection bias and reduce external validity. As the frequency of HLA alleles and the gene mutation burden varies by race and region, the selection of Han participants from the same hospital is not representative of the broader population, limiting the universality of the findings. Second, this study does not analyze differences between ES-SCLC and LS-SCLC. Different stages of SCLC may exhibit biological and genomic differences that could influence HLA-I allele expression and gene mutations. Treating SCLC patients as biological homogeneity masks potential stage-specific immune and genetic biomarkers. Finally, the sample size of this study was limited, so robust subgroup or multivariate analyses cannot be performed, small sample sizes may exaggerate the results, and further cohort studies are needed to validate the findings of this study.

Conclusion

SCLC remains a rare but extremely aggressive malignant tumor that is nearly universally fatal. Over the past few decades, significant progress has been made in characterizing the molecular features of SCLC. Multiple new treatment options have been found to improve the survival of patients with SCLC, highlighting the need to identify predictive biomarkers to guide the precise treatment of SCLC patients. In this study, we confirm several HLA-Ⅰ alleles may act as the immune modulators associated with susceptibility or resistance of SCLC. And we did not observe the co-occurrence of specific HLA-I allele expression changes and gene mutations in the occurrence of SCLC, and the association between the two has not been clarified. A previous study has proved the prognostic and clinical values of HLA-II in patients with SCLC [40]. We believe that the potential role of HLA-Ⅰ in prognosis will be confirmed in the future. Further research to investigate the potential interaction between genetic mutation and HLA alleles in the development and progression of SCLC is essential to understand the molecular mechanisms of drug sensitivity/resistance in this disease. This study provides insight into the factors influencing SCLC from an immunological perspective. We believe that with a deeper comprehension of the genetic level of SCLC and the continuous advancement of treatment strategies, more and more patients will obtain durable benefits.
