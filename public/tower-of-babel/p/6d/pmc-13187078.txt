TIA1-mediated stress granules inhibit the neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration after spinal cord injury through LCN2 signaling
Authors: Mengxian Jia, Ziwei Fan, Yaozhi He, Yumin Wu, Jian Zhou, Jiawei Wang, Yuhao Luo, Xiaowu Lin, Jiashu Lian, Zhoule Zhu, Zheyu Fang, Juanqing Yue, Kelun Huang, Minyu Zhu, Jian Wang, Ying Wang, Honglin Teng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal cord injury (SCI), a complex and serious neurological disease may induce severe cellular stress. In response to stress signals, cells form stress granules (SGs) as protective measures, and T-cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA1) is a vital component of SGs. However, the roles and mechanism of TIA1-mediated SGs in SCI remain unknown. In this study, we found that TIA1 was upregulated in the cytoplasm of neurons and co-localized with the cytoplasmic G3BP1+ SGs. Furthermore, Tia1 knockout in the CNS (Tia1Nestin-CKO mice) did not affect the development of the spinal cords and locomotion function, but impeded the neural regeneration, and then inhibited the functional recovery after SCI, whereas, Tia1 knockout in the astrocytes (Tia1GFAP-CKO mice) did not affect development of the spinal cords and the locomotion functional recovery after SCI. Mechanistically, we found that Tia1 deletion decreased the formation of SGs, and increased the lipocalin 2 (LCN2, a secreted protein) expression in neurons through decreasing the sequestration of their mRNA into SGs after SCI, which further induced the neuroinflammation by activating astrocytes and microglia through cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Finally, treatment with deferoxamine (DFO), an inhibitor of LCN2, inhibited the neuroinflammation and partially restored the deficits in neural degeneration and functional recovery after SCI in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice. Together, these results suggest neuronal TIA1-mediated SGs encapsulates lcn2 mRNA, halting its translation and thereby attenuating the neuroinflammation and promoted the functional recovery after SCI, and TIA1-mediated SGs serves as a novel strategy for SCI treatment by regulating neuroinflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06005-z.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) results in altered functions of the spinal cord, encompassing sensory, motor and visceral perception [1]. Globally, it impacts approximately 250, 000 to 500, 000 individuals annually, with over 2 million people enduring SCI-related disabilities, particularly among younger adults [2, 3]. Currently, there are no particularly effective treatments for SCI. Therefore, comprehending the investigation of pathophysiological changes and molecular mechanisms following SCI is crucial for developing novel treatment strategies. The pathophysiological progression of SCI comprises primary and secondary injuries. Since primary injury prevention is unattainable, current treatments primarily target secondary injury [4, 5]. The secondary injury characteristics consist of a series of reactions, including neuroinflammation, ischemia, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, apoptosis and necrosis [6–8]. Following the injury, these reactions in spinal cord tissues and cells manifest as acute stress, so it is crucial to consider the acute stress response that follows the injury to effective treatment.

In response to stress signals, cells form stress granules (SGs) as protective measures [9]. SGs is a kind of membraneless messenger ribonucleoprotein (mRNP) particle that consisting of the translation initiation factor, the 40 S ribosomal subunit, the untranslated mRNA and the RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). These components coalesce to form the core of SGs, which further aggregate with additional RBPs to create a shell without membranes [10–13]. During this process, specific pathways such as protein-protein cross-linking, protein-RNA cross-linking, and RNA-RNA cross-linking pathways may occur through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), resulting in SGs particles in the cytoplasm [14, 15]. It is essential for cell survival to form SGs during stress responses [16]. Untranslated mRNAs are sequestered into SGs, which attenuate protein synthesis and perform cell protecting functions [17, 18]. Currently, although previous studies have implicated that aberrant SGs are involved in several neurodegenerative diseases, it remains unknown that the specific role and regulatory mechanisms of SGs under SCI conditions [19, 20].

Several key proteins have been identified so far as crucial mediators of the formation of SGs, such as T-cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA1), TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), GTPase-activating protein SH3 domain-binding protein (G3BP), and ubiquitin specific peptidase 10 (USP10) [13, 21–23]. Among these, TIA1 shuttles between the cytoplasm and nucleus and forms cytoplasmic SGs under stress, which is also one of the main pathogenic proteins of central nervous system (CNS) diseases [24–26]. In stressed cells, inhibition of the development of the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (eIF2)-guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-transfer ribonucleic acid for methionine (tRNAMet) ternary complex occurs due to phosphorylation of eIF2α, which prevents GDP-GTP exchange by eIF2B. TIA1 integrates into the noncanonical preinitiation complex, which is translationally silent, potentially elucidating the molecular mechanism of TIA1-mediated SGs formation [26]. Of note, studies have shown that TIA1-mediated SGs formation could respond to environmental stresses for cell survival and modulated neuroinflammation [27]. However, it remains unknown that the roles and mechanisms of TIA1-mediated SGs in SCI.

In this study, we found that TIA1 was upregulated and translocated into cytoplasm of neurons during the early stages after SCI, contributing to the formation of G3BP1+ SGs. Tia1 CKO in the CNS impaired the formation of SGs in neurons following SCI, ultimately resulting in the inhibition of neural regeneration and the functional recovery. Mechanistically, Tia1 deletion increased the expression of neuronal lipocalin 2 (LCN2) in the injured spinal cords, exacerbating neuroinflammation by activating glial cells including astrocytes and microglia via the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Overall, this work reveals an unrecognized function of TIA1-mediated SGs in SCI and provides a promising therapeutic strategy for SCI by targeting TIA1-mediated SGs.

Materials and methods

Mice breeding and genotyping

Tia1Nestin-CKO mice and Tia1GFAP-CKO were generated through the crossing floxed Tia1 allele (Tia1f/f) (Shanghai Biomodel Organism Science & Technology Development Co., Ltd) with Nestin-Cre and GFAP-Cre transgenic mice (from The Jackson Laboratory) respectively. Tia1f/f mice were generated using a targeting vector containing the first two exons of Tia1 gene to recombineering. Specifically, the following primer sets were used to transformed ES colonies which were screened by long-template PCR: P5F (5’-AACCCTTTTTCCACTTGCTTACT-3’) and P5R (5’- GCCTTGCCCCTCATCCA-3’) to generate a 4.6-kb band for positive clones; P3F (5’- TTCAGCCAGCAACGAGTCA-3’) and P3R (5’- TGGGGAAAAGGAGGTAACATAGG-3’) to generate a 3.7-kb band for positive clones. Subsequently, 5’ and 3’ PCR confirmed the successfully targeted ES clones, and then microinjected into C57BL/6 blastocysts. Mice with a Tia1-targeted allele were crossed to Flp-recombinase carrying mice to eliminate the FRT-flanked Neo cassette and generate the Tia1 flox mice. The Flp transgene was removed by crossing the Tia1f/w mice with wild-type (WT) mice. Genomic DNAs extracted from tail tissues were genotyped using the following PCR primer set: (F: 5’-CACTGATATTGTAAGTAGTTTGC-3’, R: 5’-CTAGTGCGAAGTAGTGATCAGG-3’) that generated no band from the wild-type allele, a 725-bp band from the Flp allele. All WT, Tia1f/f, Nestin-Cre, GFAP-Cre, Tia1Nestin-CKO and Tia1GFAP-CKO mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 strain background. Genotypes were detected by PCR amplification of genomic DNAs extracted from tail tissues. The PCR primers were used as follows: Tia1 (F: 5’GAGGCATCAGAATTGTTTTAGTG3’; R: 5’GAGATTCTGCGGGGCGATAG3’), Nestin-Cre (F: 5’CAGCATTGCTGTCACTTGGTC3’; R: 5’ATTTGCCTGCATTACCGGTCG3’), GFAP-Cre (F: 5’ACTCCTTCATAAAGCCCT3’; R: 5’ATCACTCGTTGCATCGACCG3’). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Hangzhou Normal University.

SCI surgical procedures and treatment

Male mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 20–25 g were anesthetized (20 ml/kg) with via intraperitoneal injection of avertin (2, 2, 2-tribromoethanol, Sigma Millipore) dissolved in 0.9% saline solution. A laminectomy was performed at the T8-T9 level to expose the dorsal portion of the spinal cord, utilizing a surgical microscope (Nikon, SMZ745) and a mouse spinal cord adapter (#68094, RWD). The SCI model, previously described in our studies, involved inducing spinal cord compression using a vascular clip (30 g force) for 15 s [28]. Bladder voiding was conducted manually at least twice daily until urination function was restored. A laminectomy and anesthesia were administered in the sham group without SCI surgery. For DFO (138-14-7, Cayman Chemical) treatment as an inhibitor of LCN2, it was first dissolved in saline and injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 mg/kg to mice until 14 days after injury [29, 30]. For STING inhibition, H151 (HY-112693, MedChem Express) was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) firstly and injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg to mice until 3 days after injury according to the previous study [31, 32]. Subsequently, the mice were utilized for the histological, biochemical and behavioral function experiments, as described below.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral tests were performed in mice to assess the locomotion recovery below at days 0, 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28 following SCI, consistent with previously described methods [33–36]. Footprint analysis was performed following SCI to assess gait and motor coordination. Prior to experimentation, mice adapted to their new environment for at least 1 h. Subsequently, each forelimb and hindlimb was gently brushed with red (forelimbs) and blue (hindlimbs) nontoxic ink, and the mice were encouraged to traverse a paper-lined runway within a darkened enclosure. Qualitative analysis was then employed to measure stride length and width [33, 34].

The Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scoring analysis was conducted to assess the locomotor function of hindlimbs in mice after SCI. According to the scoring system (posterior ankle joint mobility, coordination, paw posture, trunk stability, and tail posture), BMS scores of mice after SCI ranged from 0 to 9 points [35, 36].

Electrophysiology

All recordings were performed at room temperature (20–22℃). Briefly, all mice were anaesthetized with 1.2% avertin and collected the complete spinal cord tissue. The torso was immersed in ice-cold, oxygenated, sucrose substituted, low calcium/high magnesium artificial cerebrospinal fluid (S-ACSF) containing: 250 mM sucrose, 25 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose, 2.5 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 6 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM CaCl2, pH 7.3 [37]. The spinal cord tissues were equilibrated in the beaker with S-ACSF for 60 min before each experiment. Recording micropipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries and had resistances in the range of 300–500 kΩ. The recording configuration was established in current-clamp mode (holding current at 0 pA), after connecting the two micropipettes to the spinal cord. A series of step currents lasting 0.2 ms was injected through the stimulation electrode to induce a spinal cord response at a 5-second interval. Each stimulus was presented five times. Signals were filtered at 2 kHz with a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Palo Alto, CA). Data were sampled at 10 kHz and analyzed by Clampfit 10 (Molecular Devices) [38].

Primary neuron culture

P0 mice were sacrificed, and their brains were carefully harvested and dissociated with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) medium. Following the removal of the cortex, the hippocampus was dissected from the brain’s surface, chopped, and then incubated with 0.125% trypsin (C0208, Beyotime) at 37 °C for 12 min. Following a wash with HBSS, cells were resuspended and cultured in neurobasal medium supplemented with B27 and GlutaMAX as described previously [39, 40]. The culture media were changed every 2–3 days.

Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation

The nuclear-cytosol extraction kit (EX2660, Solarbio) was used to separate the nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins of spinal cord tissues following the manufacturer’s instructions. The fractions were analyzed by western blot with specific antibodies.

Western blot

Briefly, cultured cells or tissues were lysed by cold RIPA Buffer and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12, 000 × g for 20 min. Subsequently, the proteins were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and then extracted with 5 × loading buffer and boiled in a water bath at 100 °C for 10 min. The proteins were separated by using 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies: mouse anti-GAPDH (1:1, 000, GB15002-100, Servicebio), mouse anti-MBP (1:1, 000, ab62631, Abcam), mouse anti-β-actin (1:1, 000, GB15001-100, Servicebio), mouse anti-tubulin (1:1, 000, GB15140-100, Servicebio), rabbit anti-G3BP1 (1:2, 000, 13057-2-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-G3BP2 (1:2,0 00, 16276-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-TIA1 (1:1, 000, 12133-2-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-Lamin B1(1:1, 000, ab16048, Abcam), rabbit anti-p-eIF2α (1:1, 000, 28740-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-eIF2α (1:1, 000, 11170-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-LCN2 (1:1, 000, 26991-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-cGAS (1:1, 000, 26416-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-p-STING (1:1, 000, #72971, CST), rabbit anti-STING (1:2, 000, 19851-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-p-TBK1 (1:1, 000, #5483, CST), rabbit anti-TBK1(1:1, 000, #3504, CST), rabbit anti-p-IRF3 (1:1, 000, #29047, CST), rabbit anti-IRF3 (1:1, 000, #4302, CST). After washing for 3 times in TBST, the membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h: goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10, 000, A9044, Sigma), goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10, 000, FDR007, FDbio). After washing in TBST for another 3 times, the protein signals were detected using electro-chemiluminescence imaging analysis system (GelViev 6000Plus, BLT). Blots were measured by Image J software.

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was performed following previously established protocols [41]. Initially, spinal cord tissues were collected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 48 h. Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose until they sank to the bottom, followed by sectioning into 20 μm thick slices using a freezing microtome (CryoStar NX50, Thermo). The sections were incubated with 0.1% cresyl violet for 3 min at room temperature and then rinsed in 95% ethanol. They were subsequently dehydrated in 100% ethanol, cleared in xylene, and then sealed with neutral resins. Images of the sections were captured under a microscope (VS200, Olympus).

Oil red O staining

Firstly, cryosections were placed in 10% PFA for 20 min, then washed in ddH2O for 10 min. Subsequently, they were rinsed with 60% isopropanol and stained in freshly prepared Oil Red O (O8010, Solarbio) working solution for 10 min. After staining, the sections were rinsed by 60% isopropanol again. Next, the sections were counterstained with alum haematoxylin to visualize nuclei, followed by a rinse with ddH2O. Finally, they were mounted in aqueous glycerine jelly. The sections were imaged under the microscope (VS200, Olympus).

Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining

LFB staining was conducted following the protocol provided in the LFB staining kit (G3242, Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd). The cryosections were incubated in LFB solution for 20 min at room temperature. Neutral red solution (G1315, Solarbio) was used for counterstaining. The sections were imaged under the microscope (VS200, Olympus).

Immunofluorescence staining

For tissue cryosections staining, the sections were placed in 4% PFA for 30 min, followed by antigen repaired for 30 min at 90 °C using sodium citrate antigen retrieval solution (C1032, Solarbio). Subsequently, the sections were blocked and permeabilized with 5% BSA plus 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies for overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, they were washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies (1:1, 000, Invitrogen). The primary antibodies used were as followed: mouse anti-NeuN (1:500, #94403, CST), mouse anti-GFAP (1:500, MAB360, Millipore), mouse anti-G3BP1 (1:1, 000, 66486-1-Ig, Proteintech), mouse anti- Iba1 (1:100, GB12105-100, Servicebio), mouse anti-dsDNA (1:50, HYB331-01, SANTA), rabbit anti-TIA1 (1:100, ab140595, Abcam), rabbit anti-GAP43 (1:500, ab16053, Abcam), rabbit anti-G3BP1 (1:1, 000, 13057-2-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-LCN2 (1:100, 26991-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-cGAS (1:100, 26416-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-p-STING (1:1, 000, #72971, CST), rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:400, ab178846, Abcam), chicken anti-GFAP (1:500, NBP1-05198, Novus), goat anti-Iba1 (1:500, ab5076, Abcam). Additionally, the nucleus was marked with DAPI (1:1, 000, #4083, CST). Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000RS, Olympus) or a fluorescence microscope (VS200, Olympus). Fluorescent images were analyzed for colocalization using ImageJ software.

For cultured cell staining, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, the cells were washed 3 times in PBS. Cells were blocked and permeabilized with 5% BSA plus 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies for overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were washed thrice with PBS, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies (1:1, 000, Invitrogen). The primary antibodies included mouse anti-NeuN (1:500, #94403, CST,) and rabbit anti-G3BP1 (1:1, 000, 13057-2-AP, Proteintech), chicken anti-MAP2 (1:10, 000, ab5392, Abcam). Meanwhile, DAPI (1:1, 000, #4083, CST) was stained for nuclear. Images were captured by a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000RS, Olympus) or a fluorescence microscope (VS200, Olympus). Fluorescent images were analyzed for colocalization using ImageJ software.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted by the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES-RN001, ES Science) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323-01, Vazyme). The expression levels of il-1β, il-6, tnf-α, inos, cxcl10, cd206, arg1, tgf-β and β-actin were quantified using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) on the Real-Time PCR detection System. Each sample was independently amplified at least three times. Relative gene expression was converted using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primer used included the following: il-1β (F: 5’AAGGGCTGCTTCCAAACCTTTGAC3’; R: 5’ATACTGCCTGCCTGAAGCTCTTGT3’), il-6 (F: 5’ATCCAGTTGCCTTCTTGGGACTGA3’; R: 5’TAAGCCTCCGACTTGTGAAGTGGT3’), tnf-α (F: 5’TCTCATGCACCACCATCAAGGACT3’; R: 5’ACCACTCTCCCTTTGCAGAACTCA3’), inos (F: 5’TCTTTGACGCTCGGAACTGTAGCA3’; R: 5’ACCTGATGTTGCCATTGTTGGTGG3’), cxcl10 (F: 5’GGAAATCGTGCGTGACATTA3’; R: 5’AGGAAGGAAGGCTGGAAGAG3’), cd206 (F: 5’TCAGCTATTGGACGCGAGGCA3’; R: 5’TCCGGGTTGCAAGTTGCCGT3’), arg1 (F: 5’TTATCGAGCGCCTTTCTCAA3’; R: 5’TGGTCTCTCAGGTCATACTCTGT3’), tgf-β (F: 5’GACATCAAAAGATAACCACTC3’; R: 5’TCTATGACAAGTTCAAGCAGA3’), β-actin (F: 5’GGCACCACACCTTCTACAATG3’; R: 5’GGGGTGTTGAAGGTCTCAAAC3’).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The RNA sequencing was performed as previous study [7, 28]. RNA sequencing was performed by the Novogene Bioinformatics Institute (Beijing, China). The mRNA was collected from Tia1f/f and Tia1Nestin-CKO mice by using Trizol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity was assessed using the Nanodrop, each sample had an A260:A280 ratio > 1.8 and an A260:A230 ratio > 2.0. Total amounts and integrity of RNA were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA) was used to purify the library fragments and 370 ~ 420 bp cDNA was selected. Then PCR amplification, the PCR product was purified by AMPure XP beads, and the library was finally obtained. After the library was constructed, it was initially quantified by Qubit2.0 Fluorometer and diluted to 1.5 ng/ul, then the insert size of the library was detected by Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. After that, qRT-PCR was used to accurately quantify the effective concentration of the library to ensure the quality. Sequencing was performed by using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and the end reading of 150 bp pairing was generated. Gene expression data for RNA sequencing analysis was available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (GSE268047).

Clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapter, reads containing N base and low-quality reads from raw data. Index of the reference genome was built by using Hisat2 (v2.0.5) and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2. Then, feature Counts (v1.5.0-p3) was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene and then the expected number of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript sequence per Millions base pairs sequenced (FPKM) of each gene was calculated. Differential expression analysis of two groups was performed by using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were chosen according to the threshold of fold change ≥ 1 and p value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis based on DEGs with p value < 0.05, which were considered significantly enriched. Xiantao tool (https://www.xiantaozi.com/?t=null) was a bioinformatics analysis web tool, and was used for DEGs and GO visualization.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

The mRNA FISH was performed as previously [42]. RNA FISH was conducted using the kit purchased from RiboBio according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, the tissue sections were fixed with 4% PFA and treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS followed by pre-hybridization. The fixed sections were then hybridized at 5 mM probe concentration overnight. Tia1 FISH probes were designed and synthesized by RiboBio. Images were captured by the SIM-Ultimate system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Spinal cord tissue specimens, each weighing 1 mg, were homogenized in 2 ml of pre-cooled PBS. All homogenization procedures were performed on ice. The resultant homogenates were subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was thereafter collected, and the concentrations of IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and TGF-β were quantified employing commercial ELISA kits in strict adherence to the manufacturer’s protocol (R-S-M0007, R-S-M0103, R-S-M0002, R-S-M10005, RUISAIQI BIOTECHNOLOGY).

Molecular docking

The X-ray crystal structure of LCN2 was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3S26). All small molecule compounds were prepared at a physiological pH of 7.4, and their three-dimensional structures were generated using Open Babel [43]. Both the receptor protein and the ligands were prepared and parameterized using AutoDock Tools (ADT3). Docking grid boxes were defined using AutoGrid, and the molecular docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0) [44, 45]. The most favorable binding pose from the docking results was selected for interaction analysis. Finally, the protein-ligand complex interaction diagram was visualized and rendered using PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least 3 times and the data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test or using ANOVA with Bonferroni post-tests with GraphPad 8.0 software. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

TIA1 was upregulated in neurons and co-localized with G3BP1+ SGs in spinal cord after SCI

To explore the role of TIA1 in SCI, we initially examined the temporal and spatial expression pattern of TIA1 in spinal cords after SCI. Based on the clip-compressive SCI mouse model, we found a significant upregulation of TIA1 expression at the lesion area, peaking at 7 days following SCI (Fig. 1A and B). Subsequently, double immunostaining of TIA1 along with cell markers (GFAP and NeuN) showed that TIA1 was upregulated and primarily localized in the cytoplasm of NeuN+ cells, while it was absent in GFAP+ cells (Fig. 1E and F, Supplementary Figure S1A-F) at 3 and 7 days after SCI. Considering that TIA1 shuttles between the cytoplasm and nucleus [26], we conducted a nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation a followed by western blot analysis and found that a significant increase in TIA1 protein levels in the cytoplasm and a decrease in the nucleus at 7 days after SCI (Fig. 1G and H). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 1E, the dense granules of TIA1 protein were accumulated in the cytoplasm of NeuN+ neurons after SCI. Given that TIA1 is a RBP and a component of SGs, we conjecture that these dense granules might represent SGs. To this end, we confirmed a significant upregulation of G3BP1 protein expression at the injury site at 7 days after injury, while G3BP2 protein levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1A, C and D), and found the colocalization of most TIA1+ and G3BP1+ puncta (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Figure S1G). Taken together, these results suggested that TIA1 was predominantly upregulated in neuronal cytoplasm at early stage of SCI, which might contribute to the formation of G3BP1+ SGs.

Tia1 deletion in the CNS inhibited the functional recovery after SCI

To explore deeper into the role of TIA1 in SCI, a conditional Tia1 deletion in the CNS mice (Tia1Nestin-CKO mice) was generated by crossing Tia1f/f mice with Nestin-Cre mice, confirmed by the genotyping experiment (Supplementary Figure S2A). As shown in Supplementary Figure S2B and C, Tia1 was signification reduced in the most brain regions of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, such as cortex, hippocampus, spinal cord and cerebellum. Surprisingly, there was no significant difference in the body size and body weight between Tia1f/f and Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Supplementary Figure S2D and E). Additionally, footprint analysis showed that Tia1 deletion in the CNS did not affect the motor function of mice (Supplementary Figure S2F-H). Furthermore, there were also no discernible differences in density of neurons or astrocytes in spinal cord between Tia1f/f and Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Supplementary Figure S2I-K). Collectively, these results suggested that Tia1 deletion in the CNS did not affect the development of the spinal cords or locomotion function.

Next, we examined whether the functional recovery was affected by Tia1 deletion in the CNS after SCI. Interestingly, footprint behavioral analysis showed the worse performance of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice in stride length at 21 and 28 days after SCI, although no significant difference in stride width among all groups (Fig. 2A-C). Furthermore, BMS scores were significantly reduced at 21 and 28 days after injury in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Fig. 2D). Electrophysiological parameters and phenotype of mice at 14 days after SCI were also evaluated to assess neurological recovery (Fig. 2E). Consistently, Tia1Nestin-CKO mice exhibited a decrease in reaction peak in injury spinal cord (Fig. 2F). To rule out the potential effects of astrocytic Tia1 in SCI, Tia1GFAP-CKO mice were generated by crossing Tia1f/f mice with GFAP-Cre mice (Supplementary Figure S3A). Interestingly, we found both the development of the spinal cords and the locomotion functional recovery were not affected in Tia1GFAP-CKO mice after SCI (Supplementary Figure S3B-F and S4I-L). Taken together, these results strongly suggested Tia1 deletion in neurons inhibited the functional recovery after SCI.

Tia1 deletion in the CNS aggravated the loss of neurons and the neurodegeneration after SCI

We further examined whether the Tia1 deletion in the CNS aggravated the loss of neurons and the neurodegeneration after SCI. Indeed, Nissl and Oil Red O staining showed the lesion area was significantly increased in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 14 days after SCI (Fig. 3A-D). Immunostaining of NeuN further showed a significant decrease of the number of neurons in the distant injury center of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice within 1500 μm (Fig. 3E and F). In addition, growth-associated protein-43 (GAP43) protein was a neuron-specific protein in vertebrates and was regarded as a marker of axon growth and regeneration [46, 47]. As expected, there was a significant decrease of the intensity of GAP43+ axon in the epicenter and adjacent to injury area in the Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Fig. 3G-I). These results suggested the loss of neurons was increased and the neural regeneration was inhibited in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI.

Myelin basic protein (MBP) was one of the biochemical components of myelin as a biomarker of myelin. The classic MBP comprise splice isoforms ranging in molecular mass from 14.0 to 21.5 kDa, and usually the 18.5 kDa and 21.5 kDa splice isoforms [48]. As shown in Figs. 3J-L and 18.5 kDa MBP protein was significantly downregulated in the Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, whereas there was no significant change in 21.5 kDa MBP level. LFB staining further supported these results, showing the increased myelin loss in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 14 days after SCI (Fig. 3M, Supplementary Figure S1H).

In parallel, the same staining in Tia1GFAP-CKO mice were performed to exclude the potential effects of astrocytic Tia1. Evidently, there was no significant differences in the density of Iba1+, GFAP+, NeuN+ cells and the lesion area between Tia1f/f and Tia1GFAP-CKO mice after SCI (Supplementary Figure S4A-H). Taken together, these results suggested that Tia1 deletion in neurons aggravated the loss of neurons and the neurodegeneration after SCI.

Tia1 deletion in neurons inhibited the formation of cytoplasmic G3BP1+ SGs after SCI

We next examined whether TIA1-mediated SG formation was involved in SCI. Firstly, as shown in Fig. 4A-C, at 3 days, the number of G3BP1+/NeuN+ cells were less in the spinal cord of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, but there were no significant differences between the two groups at 7 days after injury. Notably, in each neuron, the number of G3BP1+ SGs were significantly decreased in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 3 and 7 days after SCI (Fig. 4A-C). Under stress, protein kinase R (PKR) phosphorylates eIF2α, leading to reduce the eIF2-GTP-tRNAMet ternary complex levels, followed by TIA1-mediated SGs assembly [26]. Thus, eIF2α and p-eIF2α serve as biomarkers of this upstream regulator pathway. Interestingly, western blot results showed that the level of p-eIF2α/eIF2α was significantly upregulated in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, whereas, no significant difference in eIF2α level (Fig. 4D and E). It is conceivable that the absence of TIA1 may have led to a compensatory increase in upstream signaling. Meanwhile, G3BP1 protein was also decreased in Tia1Nestin-CKO group (Fig. 4D and E). These results suggested Tia1 deletion inhibited the formation of SGs after SCI.

Sodium arsenite, a widely used stress inducer in cell culture, was used to induce G3BP1+ SGs. Primary cultured Tia1+/+ and Tia1−/− neurons were sampled before and at 0.5, 1, 2, 6, 12 h after sodium arsenite treatment, and we found that the G3BP1+ SGs were significantly increased in cytoplasm of Tia1+/+ neurons with a peak at the 2 h following treatment, but gradually dissipated thereafter. The expression of LCN2 was upregulated in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI through decreasing the sequestration of mRNA in SGs

To explore the downstream targets of TIA1 in neurons in regulating neuroprotection and functional recovery, mRNA sequencing of injury spinal cord tissues from Tia1Nestin-CKO and Tia1f/f mice was performed after SCI. Interestingly, GO enrichment analysis showed that Tia1 deletion in CNS after SCI might affect acute inflammation related pathways (Fig. 5A). The main DEGs were shown in Fig. 5B and C, and the inflammatory response-related gene lcn2 mRNA was significantly increased in injury spinal cord of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice. Further western blot confirmed the increased expression of LCN2 protein in injury spinal cord tissues of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice 7 days after SCI (Fig. 5D). Immunostaining results further showed that the density of LCN2+/NeuN+ cells was significantly increased in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, with an increasing expression of LCN2 in each NeuN+ cell (Fig. 5E-G). Colocalization assessment revealed that LCN2 was predominantly colocalized with the NeuN+ cells, whereas its overlap with Iba1+ and GFAP+ cells was minimal and not readily detectable (Supplementary Figure S1J and K, Figure S5A-F).

Tia1 deletion in neurons aggravated the neuroinflammation after SCI through upregulation of LCN2 signaling

LCN2, a secreted protein, has been reported to regulate a series of inflammation signaling pathways in several CNS diseases by affecting neuroinflammatory cells [49–52]. Thus, we next examined whether neuroinflammation was affected by Tia1 deletion after SCI. As expected, the density of Iba1+ cells and GFAP+ cells were significantly increased in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Supplementary Figure S6A-D). In addition, qRT-PCR analysis of il-1β, tnf-α and il-6 indicated that pro-inflammatory factors were upregulated at 7 days after SCI in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, whereas anti-inflammatory factors such as cd206, arg1 and il-10 were downregulated (Supplementary Figure S6E). However, there were no significant difference in inos, cxcl10 (pro-inflammatory factors) or tgf-β (anti-inflammatory factor) between Tiaf/f and Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Supplementary Figure S6E). Taken together, these results suggested that Tia1 deletion in neurons aggravated the neuroinflammation after SCI.

We next examined whether Tia1 deletion in neurons induced the neuroinflammation after SCI through the LCN2 signaling. DFO, an inhibitor of LCN2, was applied. To this end, we first employed molecular docking, which confirmed a stable interaction between the LCN2 inhibitor DFO and the LCN2 protein. We analyzed the interactions between protein and ligand, all functional residues were identified and classified according to their interactions. There are multiple groups of residues used to form interactions between receptor protein and ligand, such as the hydrogen bond formed by ALA60 of LCN2 and ligand. With these interaction forces, the binding energy of protein-ligand complex was − 5.1 kcal/mol, which is a good performance (Supplementary Figure S7A). Then, western blot shown that DFO treatment effectively reduced LCN2 protein expression in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Fig. 6A and B). Double immunostaining further showed that, in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice treated with DFO, not only the density of NeuN+/LCN2+ cells were significantly decreased, but also the expression of LCN2 in each NeuN+ cell was decreased (Fig. 6C-E). Furthermore, the density of Iba1+ cells and GFAP+ cells were significantly decreased in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice treated with DFO (Fig. 6F-I). Moreover, the expression of pro-inflammatory factors like il-1β and tnf-α was downregulated at 7 days after SCI in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice treated with DFO, and the expression of anti-inflammatory factors such as cd206, arg1 and il-10 was upregulated. However, there were no significant difference among these groups in il-6, inos, cxcl10 and tgf-β (Fig. 6J). Consistent with these findings, ELISA further confirmed a corresponding decline in the protein levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines and an increase in select anti-inflammatory factors following DFO treatment (Supplementary Figure S7B-E). Taken together, these results suggested that Tia1 deletion in neurons aggravated the neuroinflammation after SCI through upregulation of LCN2 signaling.

We next tested whether inhibition of LCN2 signaling rescued the deficits such as impairment of the locomotion recovery and the neural regeneration in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 7A-D, the Tia1Nestin-CKO mice treated with DFO exhibited better performance in stride length and BMS scores at 28 days after SCI than that in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice treated with vehicles, but was still worse than that in Tia1f/f mice. NF200 immunofluorescence staining showed less the loss of neural axon in DFO-treated Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 14 days after SCI (Fig. 7L, Supplementary Figure S1L). Taken together, these results suggested that inhibition of LCN2 signaling by DFO partially rescued the deficits of SCI, such as impairment of the locomotion recovery and the neural regeneration in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI.

Neurons-derived LCN2 triggered the neuroinflammation by activating astrocyte and microglia through cGAS-STING pathway in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI

Previous studies have shown that LCN2 in CNS promotes proinflammatory activation of astrocyte and microglia [49, 50], and cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) serves as a danger signal that accumulates in glial and neuronal cells after CNS damage, subsequently activating the cGAS-STING pathway [53]. Thus, we next examined whether LCN2-induced neuroinflammation by activating astrocyte and microglia was mediated through cGAS-STING pathway in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI. Firstly, as shown in Fig. 8A, dsDNA was mainly observed in the nucleus after SCI, but Tia1 deletion increased the cytosolic dsDNA, however, DFO treatment reversed this process. Notably, immunofluorescence colocalization analysis specifically demonstrated that this dsDNA nuclear extrusion occurred primarily in microglia and astrocytes surrounding the lesion site in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice (Supplementary Figure S8A-H). Since classical cGAS-STING pathway was a main downstream pathway of cytosolic dsDNA, we next detected the related proteins such as cGAS, p-STING, STING, p-TBK1, TBK1, p-IRF3 and IRF3 [53, 54]. Notably, in the spinal cord of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, classical cGAS-STING pathway was significantly activated at 7 days after SCI, however, DFO treatment suppressed the activation of this pathway (Fig. 8B and C), suggesting that this pathway may be regulated by LCN2 signaling. To confirmed whether targeting cGAS-STING pathway in neuroinflammatory cells by LCN2, double immunostaining analysis was performed and showed that cGAS and p-STING were highly expressed in Iba1+ microglia and GFAP+ astrocytes, but not in NeuN+ neurons in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 7 days after SCI, however, DFO treatment significantly inhibited their expression (Fig. 8D-K). To further validate the pathway’s functional significance, we administered H-151, a specific STING inhibitor, to Tia1Nestin-CKO mice following SCI. This pharmacological inhibition of the STING pathway significantly reduced the expansion of lesion area and enhanced neuroprotective effects associated with Tia1 deficiency (Supplementary Figure S9A-G). Taken together, these results suggested that neuronal LCN2 might activated astrocytes and microglia cells through the cGAS-STING pathway, and then promoted the neuroinflammation in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI. However, the precise mechanism through which LCN2 induces cytosolic dsDNA accumulation warrants further investigation.

Discussion

In present study, we provide evidence of neuronal TIA1’s functions in the SGs after SCI and propose a working model in Fig. 9. In this model, neuronal Tia1 promotes the formation of SGs and neuroprotection after SCI. Mechanistically, neuronal TIA1 inhibits the LCN2-mediated neuroinflammation by activating astrocytes and microglia cells through cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway. Collectively, our results suggest that TIA1-mediated neuronal SGs inhibits the neuroinflammation and promotes the neuroprotection after SCI through LCN2 signaling.

Working model of neuronal TIA1 in mice after SCI. Neuronal TIA1 facilitates the formation of SGs with lcn2 mRNA-TIA1 protein complexes, thereby promoting the functional recovery and neuroprotection after SCI through inhibiting neuron-derived LCN2-mediated neuroinflammation by activation of microglia and astrocytes through cGAS-STING signaling pathway

SCI results in both primary and secondary damage to the spinal cord, leading to cell death and cellular stress. Eukaryotic cells respond to environmental stress by activating protective mechanisms to prevent apoptosis. One of the main cellular protective mechanisms is the assembly of cytoplasmic SGs [55]. Previous studies have reported that SGs has neuroprotective functions on the nervous system. One research reported that SGs were increased in rat primary cortical neurons and PC12 cells after oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) injury, and reduced the apoptosis of these cells [56]. Another research demonstrated that ototoxicity-induced SGs formation protected the sensory hair cells in mouse cochlear explants [57]. However, there were no relevant reports exist about the effect of SGs on neuroprotection after SCI. Thus, it is imperative to identify a key regulatory protein that can modulate the formation of SGs in the injured spinal cord for further investigation. Particularly, the TIA1 protein has been recognized as a crucial regulator of SGs. Traditionally, the formation of SGs is initiated by stress-induced p-eIF2α, and necessitates the self-aggregation of TIA1. As a result of p-eIF2α, the ternary complex linking the initiator eIF2α-GTP-RNAMet to the pre-initiation complex may be prevented from forming, which suppresses the productive translation initiation. In stressful situations, instead of the ternary complex, TIA1 and G3BP interacts with the complex to form SGs [55].

Consistent with these previous studies, several lines of evidence suggest that TIA1 mediated SGs is involved in SCI. Firstly, we found that TIA1 was significantly upregulated in neurons and participated in the formation of G3BP1+ SGs after SCI (Fig. 1). Secondly, after SCI, in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, the number of G3BP1+ SGs were significant decreased and the severity of injury was further aggravated, such as worse functional recovery and more neuronal loss (Figs. 2, 3 and 4A-E). Thirdly, the level of p-eIF2α/eIF2α was significant upregulated in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI (Fig. 4D and E). It was possible that in response to a lack of TIA1, the upstream signaling were compensatory increased. Finally, in primary cultured Tia1−/− neurons from Tia1Nestin-CKO mice, G3BP1+ SGs were significant decreased after arsenite stimulation (Fig. 4F and G). Interestingly, our study revealed a decrease in the number of G3BP1+/NeuN+ cells in the spinal cord of Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at 3 days, with no significant differences between the two groups observed at 7 days after injury. However, a significant reduction in the number of G3BP1+ SGs within each neuron was consistently observed in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice at all time points following SCI (Fig. 4A-C). This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that while SGs still formed in the absence of TIA1, their formation was reduced or delayed [58].

In cells, SGs serve as crucial sites for mRNA storage and triage. Consequently, through the assembly of SGs, cells transiently halt the translation of housekeeping transcripts to prevent the accumulation of misfolded proteins [55]. In particular, several apoptosis regulatory factors and untranslated mRNA are sequestered into SGs, thereby inhibiting signals that trigger stress-induced cell death. A previous study has reported that SGs sequester the signaling adaptor protein RACK1 and inhibited apoptosis induced by p38/JNK pathway [59]. Another study has reported that mTOR regulatory-associated proteins are also sequestered into SGs, safeguarding cells from apoptosis [60]. Therefore, the formation of SGs as an adaptive mechanism to provide protection by sequestering adverse conditions to regulate translation. In our study, RNA transcript sequencing data showed an upregulation of lcn2 mRNA expression in the Tia1Nestin-CKO group at 7 days after SCI (Fig. 5B and C). As a member of the superfamily of adipokines, LCN2 is a secreted glycoprotein [61]. Simultaneously, we examined the expression of LCN2 in the CSF of SCI patients. Interestingly, compared with the patient without trauma or other severe neuroinflammatory diseases, the preliminary results showed that LCN2 protein was upregulated in the CSF of SCI patient. As there was only a single case of SCI, data was not shown in this study. Furthermore, we will collect more samples for research in the future.

LCN2 has been regarded as a sensitive biomarker for inflammatory diseases, with reports suggesting its elevation in various CNS diseases where it regulates neuroinflammation [62, 63]. Previous study has confirmed that LCN2 serves as a signal from neuron, inducing proinflammatory microglia and promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines [63]. In other reports, LCN2-induced chemokines have been shown to promote migration and activation of astrocytes and microglia in CNS [49, 50, 64]. Notably, Rathore et al. established a SCI model and reported the detrimental effects of LCN2 overexpression, leading to neuron loss, proinflammatory cytokine expression and immune cell percolation [65]. Therefore, we speculate that lcn2 mRNA is sequestered into SGs to inhibit the neuroinflammatory signals after SCI in normal condition. But in Tia1 knockout mice, the decreased of TIA1-mediated SGs induced the upregulation and release of LCN2 after SCI, exacerbating neuroinflammation. Simultaneously, as an RNA binding protein, TIA1 may directly bind lcn2 mRNA to modulate its translation. Hence, it is intriguing to further test detailed role and mechanism through which neuronal TIA1 regulates the signaling pathway of LCN2 after SCI.

Astrocytes and microglia play a major role in neuroinflammation. As assessed by immunofluorescence staining, the density of Iba1+ cells and GFAP+ cells were increased in Tia1Nestin-CKO group after SCI. The mRNA of proinflammatory cytokine were significantly upregulated in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice concurrently (Supplementary Figure S6). These findings may contribute to our understanding of the link between TIA1 and neuroinflammation, potentially mediated by the LCN2 signaling. DFO, an iron chelator, also a LCN2 inhibitor, was used to reduce the expression of LCN2 after CNS injury in previous study [29]. Similarly, in this study, DFO was utilized to reduce LCN2 protein level in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI to ascertain whether the adverse effects of TIA1 deletion occurred via LCN2. A pleasant surprise was that our results suggested that inhibition of LCN2 by DFO mitigated the neuroinflammation and facilitated neural regeneration in Tia1Nestin-CKO mice after SCI (Figs. 6 and 7). Strikingly, TIA1 deletion led to increased cytosolic dsDNA and activation of the cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway in astrocytes and microglia (Fig. 7). Various stimuli such as DNA disruption, mitochondrial damage, and exosome damage result in cumulative cytoplasmic dsDNA, which is recognized by cGAS, initiating its pathway [53, 54]. The binding of dsDNA to cGAS induces the conformational variations and activates cyclic AMP-GMP (cGAMP) generation, subsequently binding to STING and recruiting TBK1 to its C-end tail. TBK1 autophosphorylation triggers IRF3 phosphorylation, leading to the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines [66–68]. Wang et al. previously demonstrated that STING exacerbated neuroinflammation and nerve injury in the progression of SCI [69]. Hence, targeting the STING-TBK1 pathway may hold therapeutic promise for SCI. Nevertheless, DFO treatment inhibited activation of cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway, suggesting that this pathway in astrocytes and microglia mainly be regulated by LCN2 signaling (Fig. 7).

Of note, our findings that neuronal Tia1 deletion exacerbates neuroinflammation and impedes recovery after SCI appear to contrast with certain recent reports. A study published in 2025 demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of stress granule assembly promoted axonal regeneration in a SCI model, while another study found that Tia1 knockout alleviated neuroinflammation in an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model [70, 71]. We postulate that these seemingly discrepant results highlight the context-dependent duality of stress granule biology. A crucial distinction may lie in the fact that the EAE model primarily emphasizes the role of pathological stress granules, whereas our acute spinal cord injury model predominantly investigates the normal physiological function of stress granules in response to acute stress. The beneficial role of SGs in sequestering lcn2 mRNA and inhibiting neuroinflammation, as identified in our study, might be particularly critical during the acute phase of compressive SCI, where global translational suppression and precise control of inflammatory mediators are vital for neuronal survival. In contrast, the reported detrimental effects of SGs on axonal regeneration might become more relevant during the subacute or regenerative phases, where sustaining local protein synthesis in growth cones is essential [70]. Furthermore, the neuroprotective outcome of Tia1 deletion in the EAE model, a primary neuroinflammatory disease, suggests that the role of TIA1 may differ fundamentally between primary inflammatory and secondary inflammatory conditions such as SCI [71]. These comparisons underscore the complex, multi-faceted functions of SGs, which are likely influenced by the cell type, the nature of the stressor, and the temporal stage of the pathological process.

Our study employed a series of time points post-SCI (3, 7, 14, and 28 days after SCI) to capture the dynamic and multi-phase nature of the pathological process. This temporal design was intentional and aligns with the known sequence of events in SCI [28, 72]. The early molecular events—such as the upregulation and cytoplasmic translocation of TIA1, the peak formation of SGs at 3–7 days after SCI, and the subsequent rise in LCN2 expression and neuroinflammation—precede the manifestation of overt histological damage and functional deficits. This is mechanistically coherent: the disruption of the protective TIA1-SGs pathway initiates a maladaptive cascade (e.g., LCN2-mediated glial activation via cGAS-STING), the consequences of which (widespread neuronal loss, demyelination, and axonal degeneration) accumulate over time, ultimately becoming clinically evident as locomotor impairments at the later stages (14–28 days after SCI). Therefore, the observed timeline, where molecular and cellular changes predate functional recovery, not only validates our proposed mechanism but also highlights the critical window for potential therapeutic intervention targeting the TIA1-SGs axis, which would likely be most effective during the acute phase following injury.

In this study, TIA1 was mainly expressed in neurons of spinal cord after SCI, and we used Tia1Nestin-CKO mice to explore the role and machine of TIA1 in neuronal SGs. However, we couldn’t entirely exclude the role of TIA1 of glial cells in SCI. Nestin-Cre mice mediate Cre recombinase in neural stem cells, thus this Cre line will knockout TIA1 in all Nestin+ cells such as neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. So, GFAP-Cre mice were used to exclude the role of glial cells, especially, astrocytes. Although GFAP is always regarded as a marker of astrocytes, indeed, it is also expressed in some neural stem cells [73–75]. Therefore, more specific neurons and astrocytes Cre mice would be used to test the detailed the role and machine of TIA1 in neuronal SGs in the future. Furthermore, while our data supported a role for LCN2 in promoting cytosolic dsDNA accumulation and subsequent cGAS-STING activation in glial cells, the precise molecular mechanisms through which LCN2 induced dsDNA release into the cytoplasm remained unclear and warranted further investigation. This represented an important area for future research that could provide deeper insights into the neuroinflammatory cascade following SCI.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study reveals that neuronal TIA1 not only contributes to the formation of SGs and functions in neuroprotection but also mediates LCN2-induced neuroinflammation by activating astrocytes and microglia through the cGAS-STING pathway. Generally, neuronal TIA1 and its downstream pathway may serve as the novel therapeutic targets for SCI treatment. Nevertheless, the prolonged presence of SGs will lead to its pathological accumulation and aggravate neural injury.
