The Perceptions of Victorian Rural Community Pharmacists on the Dispensing and Supply of Vapes for Smoking Cessation and Nicotine Dependence: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Eva BurtonClay‐McMahon, Virginia Dickson‐Swift, Richard Summers, Mwila Kabwe, Joseph Tucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the perceptions of pharmacists in regional and rural Victoria in response to legislative changes stipulating that vapes for management of smoking cessation and nicotine dependence can only be sold at participating pharmacies.SettingRegional and rural locations in Victoria, Australia.ParticipantsFifteen registered pharmacists all currently working in rural community pharmacies, recruited through professional networks.DesignA qualitative descriptive study utilising semi‐structured interviews with pharmacists was undertaken between June and August 2025. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis was undertaken and supported by NVivo 15.ResultsThe interviews highlighted that while some pharmacists were clear about the supply of therapeutic vapes as part of their role in supporting smoking cessation and harm minimisation, some reported clinical, ethical and system‐level concerns with supplying vapes. Others reported concerns for personal safety and liability and highlighted the impact that supplying vapes has on workflows within the pharmacy.ConclusionOverall, pharmacists expressed significant uncertainty and mixed views regarding the supply of therapeutic vapes in community pharmacy. Concerns centred on limited evidence, professional responsibility, regulatory clarity and workflow pressures. While some recognised potential harm‐reduction benefits, greater consultation, clearer guidelines, stronger evidence and targeted education are needed to support safe and confident implementation.

Introduction

Since early this century the emergence of electronic cigarettes and vapes have taken the Australian market by storm, with popularity of these devices continuing to grow each year [1]. In Australia, between 2019 and 2023 the daily tobacco smoking rate in people over 14 years of age dropped from 11% to 8.3% whereas the proportion of people using e‐cigarettes almost tripled [2]. In 2022–2023, 7% of the population were using e‐cigarettes or vapes daily, with use being the most common in young people: 49% of people aged 18–24 reported using vapes at some point during their life [2]. During 2023 and 2024, the Australian Government introduced many reforms and legislative changes focused on strengthening the regulation of vaping products. A key focus of this was the Therapeutic Goods and Other Legislation Amendment (Vaping Reforms) Act 2024 commenced on 1 July 2024 [3]. This act prohibits importation, commercial advertising and supply of non‐therapeutic disposable vapes [3]. From 1 October 2024, the Australian Government legislated that vapes for management of smoking cessation and nicotine dependence could only be sold at participating pharmacies. Since then, therapeutic vapes containing less than 20 mg/mL nicotine are available to people over 18 years of age as schedule 3 (S3‐over the counter, prescribed by pharmacists) and products containing greater than 20 mg/mL nicotine are schedule 4 (S4‐requiring a prescription by a medical doctor) [3].

There have been minimal studies regarding the longer‐term consequences of vapes, and as such, the overall adverse effects on health and development are largely unknown [4]. In Australia, the National Health and Medical Research Council published a position statement on vapes, which states that e‐cigarettes can be harmful [5]. The publication suggests there are no health benefits of using e‐cigarettes unless as a tool to support the cessation of cigarette smoking, but that other proven safe and effective options are preferable to help smokers to quit [5]. The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) has stated that: ‘there are currently no vapes for smoking cessation or the management of nicotine dependence included in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods, therefore no vapes have been assessed by the TGA for quality, safety, efficacy, or performance’ [3]. The Royal Australia College of General Practitioners (RACGP) and Pharmaceutical Society of Australia (PSA) guidelines stipulate that vapes are not a first‐line treatment for smoking cessation. For this purpose, the strongest evidence for efficacy is with existing registered pharmacological therapies such as bupropion and varenicline or combination nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) [6, 7]. In adhering to the new Government legislation, pharmacists have specific obligations in providing vaping products as S3 medicines; they may only supply enough product to last one month at a time and they must provide the patient with smoking cessation support services such as Quit Line to assist them [3]. There are also labelling, storage and reporting requirements that pharmacists and pharmacy owners must abide by to avoid penalties [3]. As such, the Australian Government legislation on vapes may place pharmacists in the unfortunate position of delivering a product for therapy whose efficacy has not been established, yet which carries a significant administrative burden.

The Pharmacy Guild of Australia (PGA), representing community pharmacists and owners, expressed concerns following the July 2024 vaping legislation [8]. In its publication, representatives from commercial pharmacy groups highlighted issues with the lack of clear protocols and guidelines, noting that while the shift to a prescription model was well‐intentioned, it risks undermining pharmacists' integrity and placing them under significant pressure [8]. Prior to legislative changes in 2024, the PGA and PSA stated that consultation with the pharmacy industry and pharmacists would have helped to ensure legislation was realistic and effective whilst still honouring pharmacists' professional practices standards and duty to promote better health outcomes for the community [8]. More cooperation and consultation with pharmacists prior to the announcement of vaping reforms may have prevented initial backlash and disapproval from the industry, as well as facilitated collaboration essential to addressing the vaping public health crisis [8]. Other issues around this legislation include the potential for regional and rural pharmacists to be more significantly affected by this, given the pharmacy workforce shortage is more acute regionally and rurally [9]. There are also fewer support systems for pharmacists and other health professionals outside metropolitan areas [10]. As such, vapes being dispensed in pharmacies is an issue which will likely put more strain on the already overworked pharmacy workforce in regional and rural settings. Therefore, the aim of this research was to investigate the perceptions of community pharmacists in regional and rural Victoria on legislation stipulating that pharmacies are to be the source of supply for vapes for smoking cessation.

Method

Study Design

A descriptive qualitative design was chosen to address the research aim. A qualitative descriptive approach was used to obtain a pragmatic and practice‐focused understanding of pharmacists' perspectives on therapeutic vape supply. This methodology is well suited to applied health research where the aim is to provide a rich but straightforward description of participants' experiences and views that can inform clinical practice and policy development [11, 12]. Qualitative research within pharmacy practice allows for increased understanding of perceptions and experiences of pharmacists and attempts to answer the ‘why’ questions that guide their practice [11, 13, 14]. Results from qualitative research aim to inform both future practice and guide policy making [14]. Using semi‐structured interview questions allowed for consistency between participants whilst still enabling open‐ended data to be collected hence deepening the understanding of participants' core beliefs and personal experiences [15].

Participants

Participants were all registered pharmacists currently practicing in community pharmacies in regional or rural Victoria. The Modified Monash Model (MMM) health workforce locator was used to identify rurality of the pharmacies [16]. The MMM identified postcodes of regional centres with population greater than 50 000 (MM 2), large rural towns with population between 15 000 and 50 000 (MM 3) and medium rural towns with population between 5000 and 15 000 (MM 4) in Victoria as target areas for this study [16]. Participants in these areas were recruited through professional networks and purposive sampling was used to select those that were most likely to provide relevant and valuable information [17]. Pharmacists were contacted to provide information about the study and to assess their interest in participating. A total of 18 pharmacists were approached and of these, three cited a lack of time as a reason for non‐participation; 15 pharmacists participated in this study. Most of interviews were then conducted at the time of initial contact or when the participant nominated a suitable time for the interviewer to return or attend the pharmacy to conduct the interview.

Procedure

A set of semi‐structured interview questions was developed and validated by the research team and used as a guide during interviews with participants (see Data S1). Questions included the pharmacist's opinion of current legislation, concerns around vape supply, perceived benefits of offering the service, discussion of knowledge and suggestions to increase pharmacist confidence in this area of practice. For pharmacists who did offer this service, there were further questions about the popularity and provision of the service. Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face between June and August 2025. The interviews lasted up to 20 min and were audio recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim. All audio recording and transcripts were deidentified, and each participant was assigned an ID number (see Table 1).

Data Analysis

Data was analysed using a phased thematic analysis approach as described by Braun and Clarke [18], with NVivo (version 15) used as a supporting tool. NVivo was used during the initial stages of analysis to facilitate familiarisation with the data and the generation of initial codes. Each transcript was read at least twice, allowing the researcher to become familiar with the content and to generate codes across the 15 interviews. Initial codes were discussed with the research team then exported from NVivo and collated to identify patterns and develop preliminary themes [19]. Codes and the original data were subsequently reviewed and refined by the team to ensure that themes accurately represented participants' accounts and that analytic integrity was maintained across the data [19]. Data saturation was achieved when analysis of successive interviews generated no new codes or themes and existing themes were sufficiently developed across the data set [20].

Results

Population Profile (See Table 1)

Fifteen community pharmacists were interviewed, all of whom practiced in regional or rural Victoria in MM 2 or MM 4 pharmacies. The majority of pharmacists (8/15) interviewed chose not to supply therapeutic vapes under any schedule from the TGA Standard for Uniform Scheduling of Medicines and Poisons (SUSMP). A smaller group (4/15) supplied vapes only via prescription, with dispensing occurring a few times per month or less. Three pharmacists provided therapeutic vapes as both S3 and S4 medications, reporting that this occurred at least weekly. All noted that S3 vape supplies were documented in their dispensing software in the same manner as prescription vapes, allowing for close monitoring and record keeping. The three pharmacists supplying vapes and vaping products as both S3 and S4 were the three located in MM 4 areas.

Themes

The thematic analysis revealed five key themes: [1] the place of vapes in pharmacy practice, [2] harm minimisation, [3] clinical concerns, [4] inappropriate and illegal vape use and [5] practical and workflow barriers. These are reported below with illustrative verbatim quotes (in italics) from participating pharmacists.

Pharmacists' Views on the Place of Vapes in Pharmacy Practice

The majority of participants were not in favour of legislation stipulating vapes were to be sold from community pharmacies: ‘it [legislation] was inappropriate… the pressure and responsibility should not be placed on pharmacists’ (P3). Many participants acknowledge that the scope of community pharmacists is expanding. However, vapes were not something they predicted as being a part of that emerging scope of practice: ‘I didn't imagine when I was at uni [university] I was going to become an old school tobacconist’ (P8). The lack of consultation with the pharmacy industry prior to legislative changes was a common source of frustration for participants: ‘[we were] rather irritated by the way it was approached and then announced’ (P3). One participant pointed out that legislation moving vapes into pharmacy may not be effective at managing the public health crisis of nicotine dependence as ‘the current cohort [younger generations] that are accessing vapes are not accessing pharmacy’ (P2).

Across participants a common concern was the contradiction with professional practice standards and the pharmacist's role: ‘ lack of duty of care, safety and efficacy this literally contradicts everything you think of when you say pharmacist’ (P6). One participant stated: ‘it contradicts a lot of basic medical guidelines and standards… the second you have to tell a customer that we don't know what this will do to you cross a line that I don't know I can do’ (P9). This participant also reported that current legislation compromises this and opens the door for ‘potential loss of trust in the pharmacist’ (P9). Suggestions such as up scheduling to prescription only or transitioning to a specialised service were discussed to maintain quality use of medicines and professional standards: ‘it's more a specialist area rather than all community pharmacies offering it’ (P8).

Vapes as Harm Minimisation Tools

Participants had mixed views regarding whether vape supply from pharmacy was a harm minimisation tool, similar to that of methadone for opioid replacement therapy (ORT). Some participants believed that therapeutic vapes from pharmacies were significantly safer when compared to black market vapes and cigarettes: ‘we know exactly what's in there… would I prefer someone on vapes long term than cigarettes? Absolutely’ (P12). Participants who were supplying vapes as both S3 and S4 perceived the service to be essential to their community, ‘if they don't get them [vapes] from proper channels [pharmacies] they will just get them online’ (P13). The majority of participants acknowledged offering vapes as a smoking cessation service following the principle ‘harm reduction rather than harm removal’ (P14). However, some participants disagreed with vapes being classified as a harm minimisation tool and being compared with methadone programmes: ‘we know so much about methadone and the role it plays from a therapeutic and clinical perspective and even from a chemistry perspective. But what do we know about vapes?’ (P6). Some participants had significant concerns stating, ‘instead of giving [consumers] a safe option to stop smoking we are just giving them another addiction’ (P15).

Participants that were accepting of therapeutic vapes as a harm minimisation tool stated that patient intention is a big motivator for them to implement the service: ‘for those people that are genuine I want to help them’ (P11). Another discussed their role in harm reduction services as this was seen as a crucial part of a pharmacist's duty of care: ‘I am a big fan of empowering patients to make the right decisions in their healthcare… its well within our duty of care to at least have the conversation [about vapes as a treatment option]’ (P12). However, as discussed above, some participants believed vapes had no place in pharmacy and that the product breaches a pharmacist's duty of care responsibility. All participants perceived some benefits to providing therapeutic vapes from pharmacies and expressed how these can enhance patient outcomes. The most common benefits mentioned included documentation and monitoring of supply, regular contact with a healthcare professional and ongoing support. Pharmacists also ‘ensure all the devices meet Australian regulations, labelling and warning [requirements]’ (P13). Many participants stated the biggest benefit to the provision of therapeutic vapes from pharmacies was the expertise and support pharmacists can offer to the consumer: ‘there is someone [a pharmacist] knowledgeable and informed [that] can help patients make good decisions about medications’ (P14).

Pharmacists' Perceptions of Clinical Concerns Associated With Vapes

Participants stated that there was a lack of evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of vapes as a smoking cessation tool: ‘I am a huge fan of longitudinal research, and the reality is we won't have that for some time’ (P12). Lack of TGA approval was another point of concern for participants, with some stating it as the main reason they chose to not supply these products, ‘they aren't registered [with the TGA], safety is the key issue’ (P8). Many participants stated they would recommend to patients products such as NRT patches, inhalers or gum before considering vapes: ‘existing NRT [is] less harmful than inhalation of the vapour’ (P14), ‘ existing alternatives [NRT] have been tested for safety, vapes have not’ (P9). Some participants believed that vapes may be a reasonable last‐line therapy for those who experienced treatment failure with other NRT: ‘it's an option if you have tried all other methods, therefore it's an option to explore and make available’ (P2). One participant reported being surprised that vapes were considered the last line in updated guidelines, stating, ‘it's not really a huge jump from inhalers to vapes… from an ease‐of‐use perspective they [vapes] are a lot easier to use than patches’ (P12). As healthcare professionals, participants had significant concerns about negative health outcomes for patients: ‘more of the information that comes out is showing the dangers’ (P9). Participants requested ‘more clinical trials to show the safety and efficacy’ (P13) before they felt comfortable with the products. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and other lung conditions were highlighted most frequently as long‐term health concerns by participants, some worrying that health outcomes of vaping may be as severe as cigarette smoking: ‘in terms of people who vape it might be just as bad [as tobacco smoking]’ (P10).

Participants were asked to score their knowledge and confidence on the following clinical areas of therapeutic vapes on a Likert scale from 1 to 10 (1 being no knowledge; 10 being highest knowledge): safety, efficacy, interactions with other medications and adverse effects of vapes/vaping (see Table 2).

As indicated in Table 2, pharmacists' knowledge varied across the four areas, with some outlining that they had minimal knowledge about clinical issues associated with vapes whilst others reporting being extremely confident in their knowledge. Those who reported a lack of knowledge felt that it was attributed to the lack of quality evidence and studies available to them. Many agreed that as more evidence becomes available there would be an opportunity for more education and training (online modules and workshops, which could form part of a standardised training course) to increase their knowledge and confidence.

Pharmacists' Opinions on Inappropriate and Illegal Vape Use

Many participants perceived illegal vapes use as a threat to the community ‘[vapes are] still accessible on the black market at higher doses with flavours’ (P2). Participants agreed that these products were especially attractive to ‘younger generations… and those that might not have previously smoked at all’ (P2). Six participants voiced concerns that access to vapes without a prescription may encourage the use of these products, ‘if they [vapes] are readily available you have people thinking let's try it, it's encouraging doing it’ (P14). Another source of unease for participants was the inability to monitor and track sales across multiple pharmacies: ‘people can go get vapes from different pharmacies and end up with huge quantities of liquids’ (P15). Questions were raised about what services/programs could be implemented to combat this and attempt to regulate or monitor sales. Existing prescription monitoring systems and decision‐making tools were discussed as options to control vape supply: ‘are they going to put it [vapes and vaping products] onto Safe‐Script or its own new thing’ (P9), ‘ the government can do something like ProjectSTOP… so we can monitor how much they are using and what places they get it’ (P15). The ‘ huge element of crime associated [with vapes]’ (P9) was another key concern for participants. It was noted that the ‘criminal component that would put my safety in jeopardy’ (P8) was a key barrier for some participants implementing the service in their pharmacy. Another participant voiced concerns about the ability of the government to control the associated crime: ‘factor in the criminal element, which I don't know if they [government] will ever be able to control’ (P9).

Pharmacists' Perceptions of Practical and Workflow Barriers

One‐third of participants reported uncertainty around understanding and implementing legislation: ‘the legislation is a bit muddy’ (P1), ‘ it's [legislation] tricky when its constantly changing’ (P14). Pharmacists also acknowledged fears about the consequences of misinterpreting legislation and hence making mistakes when providing services: ‘pharmacists are concerned and scared’ (P1). The liability of the pharmacist was frequently raised during interviews: ‘the increasing liability of pharmacist is something I am very concerned about’ (P8). Participant 1 identified that ‘there is a lot of room for pharmacist liability so for the safety of our profession and registration we have decided not to do it [supply vapes]’.

Of the participants who chose to not provide either S4 or S3 vapes from their pharmacy many identified added workload, Special Access Scheme (SAS) documentation and time taken for consults as a barrier to implementing the services within their workplace: ‘what employer is going to want to account for the time taken, the appointments, the staffing, it's not workable’ (P9). Some participants suggested that they would ‘need to charge a consult fee to cover the cost’ (P2) to make the service viable in the pharmacy. Of the pharmacists that supplied vapes and vaping products from their pharmacies, none reported significant changes to workload after implementing the service: ‘ it [supplying vapes] hasn't impacted the broader workflow’ (P12), one participant cited ‘the SAS forms are a pretty smooth process’ (P13). However, both participants acknowledged that they had minimal patients accessing the service (one or two patients a week), hence limiting the impacts on pharmacy‐wide workflow. Of the seven pharmacists who supplied vaping products from their pharmacies as either S3 or S4, none had decided to charge service/consult fees to these patients. These pharmacists chose to charge patients only the cost of the product plus a dispensing fee; as such vapes were priced in the same way as any non‐PBS medicine dispensed from the pharmacy. One pharmacist acknowledged the potential economic burden of implementing higher charges and stated ‘there is a fine line between valuing your time and expertise as a pharmacist… and recognising that smoking and nicotine usage is more common in lower socioeconomic communities’ (P12).

Discussion

Pharmacists in regional and rural communities play a key role in the delivery of healthcare services and advice, largely due to them being one of the most accessible health professionals [21]. This is especially important as Australian pharmacists' scope of practice continues to expand [22]. They are in a strong position to assist in the management of public health issues such as tobacco smoking and nicotine dependence [6]. Pharmacists in this study identified their role in assisting patients with smoking cessation through education, ongoing support and empowering patients in their healthcare decisions. However, for pharmacists to comfortably embed the supply of vapes into their practice, it is clear that further product and clinical education, as well as support, is required.

As vapes are unapproved goods that have not been assessed for quality, safety, efficacy or performance, pharmacists identified the difficult situation this had put them in [23]. The results of the current study highlighted that some pharmacists were concerned about where their duty of care lay regarding this issue. Some pharmacists perceived that the sale of unapproved goods breached Quality Use of Medicines principles. However, others stated that once legislation placed the supply of vapes within the scope of practice of a pharmacist, it was part of their duty of care to the public to offer it as a harm minimisation tool.

Some pharmacists were worried that people assume the risks of vaping are less than those of tobacco smoking. As most currently available evidence highlights short‐term effects of vaping such as addiction or acute inflammatory conditions [24, 25], some consumers may not consider longer term complications when obtaining a vape device. In comparison, there is substantial evidence highlighting significant consequences for the health of the smoker and those exposed to the smoking environment [26]. For pharmacists to confidently endorse vaping as a tool for smoking cessation they suggested that more money and time needs to be invested in conducting quality trials and assessments of notified products to ensure they were safe and effective for use in the community [6]. Evidence needs to show that harm reduction benefits outweigh the potential long term clinical effects caused by inhaling the e‐liquids; similar to the evidence which supports the use of methadone and buprenorphine in the case of ORT [27]. Pharmacists reported that these additional resources were essential to support patients in smoking cessation and management of nicotine dependence. Strong evidence that would allow vapes to become registered products with the TGA would increase pharmacists' acceptance of vapes as a smoking cessation tool, allowing them to confidently supply these products to their community, without fear of liability.

As community pharmacists' scope of practice continues to expand, the knowledge and skill required in these new areas of practice similarly grow. At the time these interviews were conducted, participants generally lacked confidence in their knowledge surrounding vapes and vaping products, including their safety, efficacy and clinical effects. Four areas of knowledge around vapes were assessed in this study: safety, efficacy, interactions with other medicines and adverse effects of products. Average self‐reported scores for these areas were only moderate. In conjunction, over 70% of participants expressed a desire for further education and training to enable them to safely provide the service to the community and effectively support their patients. Participants recommended online modules and workshops to assist in building knowledge in this area. Further education will allow pharmacists to feel confident in assessing patients' suitability to use vaping products, selecting an appropriate product, counselling patients on how to safely use and store the product, and providing support to gradually reduce their intake.

Future Recommendations

Guidelines and Monitoring Services

To best support pharmacists in supplying vapes and vaping products in the community, clearer guidelines on how to use vapes in therapy need to be developed. Ideally these guidelines would help pharmacists support their patients gradually reduce frequency of use and nicotine intake, providing a step‐down guide similar to that which is provided with nicotine patches. Having clear targets of when patients should start to lower their dosage can assist pharmacists and patients in tracking their progress and ensure they meet their treatment goals. Existing programmes that support patients who are aiming to quit smoking could be adapted to support vaping cessation/reduction as well. These apps/programs/support services could be designed to enable vape users to set clear goals, track current dosages and frequency of vape cartridge usage. This personalised programme could be designed with both a patient and practitioner interface, allowing doctors to record prescriptions written and pharmacists to input records of dispensing/supply of products. Such a system would record prescription, supply, usage and allow patients to log and track their own usage and progress. This would enhance sharing of information between the patient and the healthcare team, ensuring everyone is working together with the patient to achieve their goals of smoking or vaping cessation. It would enable better support and guidance from the healthcare team while addressing pharmacists' concerns, raised in this study, about the lack of monitoring of vape sales across different pharmacies, which could lead to stockpiling and misuse.

Device Adaptations

Another concern of pharmacists in this study was the inability to track usage of vapes, as they are less easy to track than the number of cigarettes smoked or the number of NRT lozenges or gum used. One option would be for all vaping products available from pharmacies to have a counter embedded in them similar to asthma metered dose inhalers. This would allow patients to keep a better record of the frequency and volume of product they use. This would also allow patients to better visualise their progress and keep on track with their goals. It would also be relatively straightforward for patients to input this data into the patient and practitioner interface monitoring system described above.

Further Education and Training

In this study, some participants suggested that prior to the provision of vapes, there should be a requirement for pharmacists to complete a standardised training course. This would allow for quality care to be delivered to patients by knowledgeable pharmacists and ensure consistency across the industry. Standardised comprehensive training for pharmacists would encourage better outcomes for patients and allow pharmacists to better support those in the community struggling with tobacco smoking or nicotine dependence. In addition, the standardised training modules would allow pharmacists and pharmacies to opt‐in to being involved with this area of practice, allowing greater empowerment in their practice choices.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study was the use of semi‐structured questions for the interviews as this provided consistency between participants and enabled the collection of open‐ended data, deepening insight into participants' underlying beliefs and individual experiences. However, this form of data collection also has recognised limitations, in that it may lead to response bias in the form of inaccurate responses because of fear of judgement or modifying information to present it in a more favourable light. The relatively short interview duration may have limited the depth of exploration of pharmacists' perspectives and experiences; however, the flexibility of semi‐structured interviews allowed for the use of follow‐up questions enhancing understanding. While the use of professional networks to recruit pharmacists may also introduce bias it allowed for a range of different perspectives to be captured. This study focused on the perspectives and experiences of rural and regional community pharmacists. This is important as there is the potential for rural and regional pharmacists to be more significantly affected by legislative changes, given that pharmacy workforce shortages are more acute in these areas. There are also fewer support systems for pharmacists and other healthcare professionals outside metropolitan zones. However, as this study was performed with a small group of pharmacists in regional and rural Victoria, generalisations may be somewhat limited. Future studies should aim to perform interviews with a greater sample size distributed across geographically and demographically diverse populations to address these limitations.

Conclusion

Whilst some pharmacists prescribed vapes and embraced the concept of harm reduction by offering safer access to these services, many reported clinical, ethical and system‐level concerns. These concerns included the lack of evidence surrounding vapes and their potential impacts on patient health and safety. Longitudinal studies to assess efficacy and safety of vapes in smoking cessation and to support vaping legislation and practice guidelines were considered important to implement, as was further pharmacist education and training to increase confidence in supplying vaping products.
