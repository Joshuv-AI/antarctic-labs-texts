An electric spring with an extended range of operation and current filtering capability for SEIG-based isolated system
Authors: Swagat Pati, Amar Bijay Nanda, Abhijeet Choudhury, Binod Kumar Sahu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work proposes an electric spring (ES) based on a back-to-back converter. The proposed electric spring is used for voltage control in a self-excited induction generator (SEIG)-based isolated system. This work focuses on extending the operating range of the ES as well as providing current filtering capabilities to the ES. For that, a back-to-back configuration-based ES is proposed in this work. This article provides a comparative study of the proposed electric spring with its widely used battery-operated counterpart. Unlike most battery-operated electric springs, the proposed one does not need a battery. The active power requirement of the electric spring is fulfilled through a back-to-back converter configuration. Using a back-to-back configuration not only improves the operating range of the electric spring but also reduces total harmonic distortion (THD) in various system currents and voltages through the active filter capabilities of its shunt side converter. The work shows that the proposed electric spring can handle 83.3% more power variation than its battery-operated counterpart. With a non-linear load, the proposed electric spring successfully filters out the current harmonics, thus keeping the current and voltage THD at 2.76% and 0.55%, respectively. In contrast, battery-operated electric springs are incapable of doing so. The performance of the proposed ES is also verified with computational delays of 1.8 and 2.5 μs, to show the effectiveness of the control structure. The proposed ES is also evaluated under dynamic load switching, both on the CL and NCL side, to show its response to suddenly changing loads. Apart from that, the real-time validation of the proposed system is carried out using an OPAL-RT 4510 platform, and the obtained results are presented in this article.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17110-1.Keywords: Battery, Supported, Back, To, Back converter, Current filtering capability, Electric spring, Extended range of operation, OPAL, RT 4510Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Energy grids and networks

Introduction

Voltage and frequency stabilization of self-excited induction generator (SEIG)-based isolated systems have been problems that many researchers have addressed. There have been quite a handful of solutions for doing so.1,2 have proposed a distribution static synchronous compensator (D-STATCOM) for stabilization of voltage. In3, a battery-supported static synchronous compensator (STATCOM) has been proposed. The use of a static synchronous series compensator (SSSC) for voltage regulation in the SEIG system is investigated in 4,5. Some researchers have found a way around by hybridizing different renewable energy sources with SEIG to regulate its voltage and frequency. In6 a squirrel cage induction generator-based wind system is integrated with SEIG to regulate the system voltage and frequency. Hybridizing the SEIG system with PV and battery storage is investigated in7. A hybrid self-excited induction generator, photovoltaic and doubly-fed induction generator (SEIG-PV-DFIG) system with battery energy storage is shown in8, where the voltage and frequency of the system are regulated using the grid side converter (GSC) of the DFIG system. Though these solutions effectively regulated the system voltage and frequency, they were expensive. Hybridizing various energy sources increases the system cost to quite an extent. Again, using a full-sized battery energy storage system (BESS) further increases the cost. For small isolated systems, the SSSC mentioned in4,5 can be a less expensive solution for voltage regulation. Being a series compensator, the SSSC requires less DC-link voltage, which reduces its cost and the rating of the BESS. On the other hand, the SSSC has to handle the whole load current.

In some recent literature, a new low-cost solution known as the electric spring (ES) has been reported for voltage regulation. For the operation of the ES, first, the system’s load is divided into two categories. The loads sensitive to voltage variation are classified as critical loads (CL), high priority loads, or voltage inflexible loads. The loads that can handle a certain amount of voltage variation are categorized as non-critical loads (NCL) or voltage-flexible loads. The ES is a bidirectional converter connected in series with the NCL to regulate its power consumption. The ES has two different modes of operation, i.e., voltage support mode and voltage suppress mode21. The ES works in voltage support mode when the system works under a power shortage. During a power deficit, the system voltage tends to reduce. The ES at this time injects a voltage such that the net voltage across the NCL reduces. This causes a reduction in the consumption of NCL power, balancing the load demand with generation and thus regulating the CL voltage at the rated value22. Similarly, the voltage suppress mode is initiated when the system has surplus power. Under these conditions, the ES injects voltage such that the net NCL voltage increases above the rated value, and the excess power is consumed by the NCL, balancing the generation and consumption23.

That way it regulates the system voltage and frequency. Being a series compensator, it injects voltage, which is a small fraction of the system voltage. The current handled by the ES is only the NCL current, which is again a fraction of the total load current. This makes the ES the most cost-effective solution for voltage regulation. The cost of the ES greatly depends upon the CL to NCL ratio. This ratio has a typical value of 1:9, as mentioned in9. As the value of the CL to NCL ratio increases, the current handled by the ES reduces, reducing its ratings and cost. Quite a few variants of ES have been reported in the literature. The first variant of ES is self-supported ES, which has a capacitor at its DC link as in10,11. This variant of ES is only capable of compensating reactive power. So, it injects voltage in quadrature to the NCL current. Due to this limitation, the DC voltage requirement of this ES variant is higher. It also requires the highest CL to NCL ratio, typically of the order 1:9. The second variant of ES is a battery-supported ES. As the name suggests, a BESS is connected to its DC link. This makes the ES capable of active as well as reactive power compensation. Depending upon the phase angle of voltage injection, this variant of ES is again divided into two sub-variants. In one sub-variant, the ES only compensates active power and injects voltage in phase with NCL current as in12. The other sub-variant injects voltage in phase with the system voltage13. This results in a minimum injected voltage requirement among all the ES alternatives. This sub-variant compensates both active and reactive power. A modified version of the battery-supported ES has been reported in14, which changes its voltage injection angle according to the NCL power factor to improve the sensitivity of the ES. The third variant of ES is the back-to-back converter-based ES as in15–18. In this variant, the shunt converter keeps the DC-link voltage constant as in15 by regulating the DC link power. In16, the shunt converter regulates the PV power injected into the system, and the ES manages the system voltage on the other side.17,18, a combined load-side power management (LSPM) and source-side power management (SSPM) strategy has been investigated. The LSPM strategy employs the ES for system voltage regulation during power shortages, and the SSPM strategy improves the NCL voltage.

However, in all the literature mentioned above, the effects of nonlinear loads are yet to be investigated. Again, limited research has been done regarding the extension of the operating range and reduction of the CL to NCL ratio of the ES when used for voltage regulation in SEIG-based systems. These research gaps have been addressed in the following article.

This work proposes an improved ES with a back-to-back configuration for voltage regulation in an isolated SEIG system. To reduce the KVA rating, size and cost of the proposed ES a higher CL to NCL ratio has been considered in this work which is 1:1.36. The ES is controlled by an orthogonal current component-based voltage control strategy which injects voltage in phase with the NCL current. So, the ES handles only active power. On the other end, the shunt side of the back-to-back converter has three different tasks to do. Firstly, it has to maintain a constant DC-link voltage. Secondly, it acts as an active filter and reduces harmonic content in the system current. The third task is to keep the power factor of the shunt converter at unity so that the shunt converter only has to deal with active power. The shunt side converter is controlled using a decoupled orthogonal voltage component-based current control strategy to fulfill all three tasks. In this article, the performance of the proposed ES is compared with its battery-supported counterpart. The extension of the range of operation is identified and presented. Again, the system is subjected to a non-linear load, and the harmonic filtering capability of the proposed ES is evaluated. The FFT analysis of the system currents and voltages is done with both proposed and battery-supported ES, and the harmonic contents are presented in the article. The whole system is dynamically modeled using Matlab/Simulink environment. The real-time validation of the system is done using an OPAL-RT 4510 setup.

This work proposes a back-to-back converter-based ES for voltage regulation of the SEIG system. The back-to-back converter-based ES is proposed in this work to improve the range of operation of the ES as well as to improve the performance of the system in presence of non-linear loads. The shunt side of the BTB converter not only filters out the current harmonics occurring due to the non-linear load but also manages the ES power to flow back into the system, that way improving its range of operation. Control structure

In this work, the voltage regulation of SEIG is done using a back-to-back converter-based ES. The back-to-back configuration improves the ES performance in two different ways. First, the back-to-back configuration helps improve the ES’s operating range. Secondly, it filters out the harmonics from the system current and improves the system power quality in the presence of non-linear loads. Two separate control structures have been designed for the two sides of the back-to-back converter described below.

Control structure for the series converter of the back-to-back setup

The series side of the back-to-back converter acts as the ES and is responsible for the voltage regulation of the SEIG system. This converter injects a voltage in phase with the NCL current, which manages the power flow of the NCL. Thus, the system voltage and, to some extent, frequency are also regulated as given in 4. The series converter is controlled using an orthogonal current component-based voltage control strategy, as shown in Fig. 2a. The figure shows that the control structure consists of two control loops. The voltage control loop controls the system voltage, and the reactive power control loop maintains the phase angle of the injected voltage in the exact phase with the NCL current. First, the system voltage and reactive power errors are calculated and fed to their respective controllers. The controller, in turn, generates the reference in-phase and quadrature voltage amplitudes ‘Vmp’ and ‘Vmq,’ respectively. The voltage amplitudes are then multiplied with their respective in-phase and quadrature unit templates (Uabc and Wabc). The improvements in the operating range of the proposed ES and the performance of the proposed system in the presence of nonlinear loads are discussed in this section. The same are also evaluated for battery-operated ES. A comparative study is done for both types of ES. The working range of the ES directly depends upon the allowable range of NCL voltage. The wider the allowable NCL voltage range, the larger the operating range of the ES, and the greater will be the power handling capacity of the ES. Again, the whole operating range of the ES can be divided into two modes i.e. suppress and support mode. In suppress mode, the system works with surplus power. So, the ES increases the voltage across the NCL, which helps manage the excess power at the NCL. Similarly, for support mode, the system works under power deficit. In this condition, the ES reduces the voltage across the NCL, reducing the power consumption at the NCL and maintaining the system voltage. In this work, for suppress mode, the maximum value of NCL voltage is limited to 253 V, which is 10% excess of the nominal value, i.e. 230 V. Similarly, in support mode, the minimum NCL voltage is limited up to 15% lower than the nominal value, i.e., up to 195 V. So, the ES gets an operating range of 25% of the nominal system voltage. The power range that can be managed by the ES directly depends upon the upper and lower limits of the NCL voltage, i.e., 253–195 V, respectively. In this section, the operating range is first determined for the battery-operated ES. Then, the operating range of the proposed ES is obtained, and a comparative study is presented. Following the comparison, the system performance is evaluated with a nonlinear load connected at the CL side for both battery-operated ES and proposed ES. A comparison of the power quality of both ESs is also provided later in this section.

Extension of operating range

Operation with battery-operated ES

First the operating range of battery-operated ES is determined for suppress and support mode of operation. As mentioned earlier, the operating voltage range of the NCL is limited within 195 and 253 V. The system loads are initially set such that both the CL and NCL operate at the nominal voltage value of 230 V. The ES voltage is zero during this time, as shown in Fig. 4b. In the next step, the CL power is reduced from 6.5 to 5.3 kW. Due to this reduction, the system has an excess generated power, which makes the ES operate in suppress mode, as can be seen from Fig. 4. This excess power is managed at the NCL by increasing the voltage across it. The ES increases the NCL voltage to 253 V, which is the maximum allowable limit of NCL voltage. In this process, the ES injects extra active power of 1.23 kW into the system, as shown in Fig. 4c. As per our convention, the power consumed is taken as positive, and supplied power is taken as negative. So, the power supplied by the ES in suppress mode has a negative sign. This power is drawn from the battery. In the next step, the CL power is increased from 5.3 to 8.3 kW. This increase in power creates a power deficit in the system. At this stage, the ES operates in the support mode. The ES injects a voltage in phase with the NCL current, which gets subtracted from the system voltage to reduce the NCL voltage, which is clearly shown in Fig. 4a, b. Reducing NCL voltage reduces the NCL power consumption and manages the system power. In this process, the ES draws power from the system, which has a positive sign, as shown in Fig. 4c, and charges the battery. It can be noticed that during suppress mode when there is already excess power in the system, the ES injects more power into the system from the battery.

Similarly, during a power deficit, the ES absorbs power from the system and stores it in the battery even though the system lacks power. In doing so, the ES narrows down its operating range and power handling ability. It can also be noticed from Fig. 4c that despite the load variations, the generator power remains constant at 17.5 kW. This shows the effective power management done by the ES. In the support mode of operation, the ES voltage remains in phase with the NCL current, whereas, in suppress mode, the ES voltage is injected in phase opposition with the NCL current. The phase change of ES voltage during suppressing and support mode can be clearly noticed in Fig. 4a. The reactive power responses of ES, CL, NCL, and generator are shown in Fig. 4d. It can be clearly noticed that the ES reactive power is effectively maintained at zero, which proves the control structure’s effectiveness in maintaining the ES’s unity power factor operation. During the whole process the system power has been efficiently managed by the ES due to which the system voltage and frequency are maintained constant. The frequency of the system remains at 50 Hz which can be seen from Fig. 4b.

Operation with proposed ES

In this sub-section, the range of operation of the proposed ES is evaluated and is compared with that of the battery-operated ES. Here the NCL voltage range is taken the same as that in the previous sub-section, i.e., 195–253 V.

Similar CL variations were done to evaluate the performance of the proposed ES. As in the previous subsection, initially, the system was operated with loads such that both CL and NCL voltages remained at 230 V. Then, the CL is reduced such that the NCL voltage increases till its allowable upper limit of 253 V. Figure 5c shows that the CL power is reduced from 6.5 to 4.05 kW. During this stage, the ES operates in suppress mode. The system has excess power managed by increasing the voltage across NCL. As seen in Fig. 5c, the ES has to supply 1.23 kW of power into the system during suppress mode. With the proposed ES, this power is supplied by the shunt side converter of the back-to-back configuration. The shunt side converter draws this power from the system, as shown in Fig. 5e. So, no extra power is used from any external source, as in the case of battery-operated ES. It can be noticed from Fig. 5c that the battery-operated ES could handle a maximum CL power reduction of 1.2 kW within the 10% excess voltage range. However, with the proposed ES, during suppress mode, the ES effectively handled a maximum of 2.45 kW CL power reduction within the prescribed voltage range. Similarly, during support mode, the battery-operated ES draws power from the system, reducing its power handling range. On the contrary, the proposed ES draws the same amount of power from the system as in the case of battery-operated ES but feeds it back into the system through the shunt side of the back-to-back converter, as can be seen from Fig. 5e.

Next, the system’s performance with the proposed ES is evaluated by subjecting the system to the same nonlinear load. The nonlinear load at the CL side introduces harmonics in the CL current. This deteriorates the CL current waveform, as shown in Fig. 8a, which has a THD of 12.51%. Applying KCL at the PCC of the shunt converter we get the following equations in (30) and (31)

The CL current makes the total load current iTotal non-sinusoidal as iTotal is equal to the sum of CL and NCL current.

The reference generator current (iaG*) on the other hand is derived from the unit in-phase and quadrature voltage templates by the control structure and is a sinusoidal signal which can be seen from Fig. 6.

When the non-sinusoidal load current iTotal is subtracted from the sinusoidal reference generator current IG* at the inner current control loop of the control structure shown in Fig. 2b, the current error eG is generated as shown in Fig. 7. This current error corresponds to the whole nonlinearity in load current which is injected due to the CL. The current errors of all the three phases are fed to their corresponding hysteresis band controllers, which generate the gate pulses for the shunt converter. The compensation current IC has the same nature as that of the current error which is evident from Fig. 7. This compensation current, when added to the total load current iTotal, eliminates all the harmonics and results in a sinusoidal generator current which is seen from Fig. 7. Due to this, the generator current has a better THD of 2.76%, which is evident from Table 3. As a result, the system voltage remains nearly sinusoidal, as seen in Fig. 8b. Compared to the battery-operated ES, the THD in system voltage and generator current with the proposed ES is much less, which results in less heating and better system efficiency. To further evaluate the performance of the control structure and its current filtering capabilities, computational delay has been introduced in the control process. The effectiveness of the proposed control structure is analyzed under two different values of computational delay, i.e., 1.8 and 2.5 µs as shown in Figs. 9 and Fig. 10 respectively. First a combined computational and sensor delay of 1.8 µs is introduced in the control process and the various current responses are obtained which are shown in Fig. 9. The detailed FFT analysis with various harmonic contents in system currents are given in Table 5. It can clearly be seen from the table that the introduction of computational delay of 1.8 µs results in an increase of 0.58% in the THD of the generator current. The THD of the generator under this condition was found to be 3.58% which lies well within the allowable range of 5%. It can be also noticed from Table 5 that introduction of delay mostly results in increase in higher order harmonics in in the generator current. With the increase in the delay from 1.8 to 2.5 µs, the harmonic contents in the generator currents also further increased, elevating the generator current THD to 3.58% as shown in Fig. 10. Increase in computational delay results in injection of even more harmonics in generator current which is evident from Table 5. Though the increase in computational delay increases the THD by a small value, but the THD of the generator current remains well within the 5% limit. This shows the effectiveness of the control logic in filtering out the current harmonics with and without the presence of computational and sensor delays.

Dynamic load switching

In this subsection, the performance of the proposed ES is evaluated with dynamic loads. The dynamic load in this work is considered to be a 1.5 hp induction motor, which is switched directly on line. The performance of the proposed ES is evaluated separately for the dynamic load connected to the CL and NCL sides.

Dynamic Load switching on CL side:

Here, initially, the system was considered to be supplying a power of 6.5 kW and 1.1 kW to the CL and NCL, respectively. At that time, both the CL and NCL were supplied with the rated voltage of 230 V at their terminals, as evident from Fig. 11b, d. Then the dynamic load is switched on, at the CL side, which results in a sudden increase in the CL current as can be seen from Fig. 11a.

Performance of proposed-ES with CL side dynamic load switching.

The CL current surges from 13.3 A to 26 A p-p, which results in a sudden increase in power demand on the CL side and tries to reduce the system voltage and frequency. But due to the voltage management done by the ES, the CL voltage and frequency are maintained at their rated values. The ES instantaneously reduces the NCL voltage from 230 to 158 V and hence the NCL power demand, as can be seen from Fig. 11b. This reduction in the NCL power demand balances the power surge on the CL side due to which a reduced effect of dynamic load switching is reflected on the generator. The generator power momentarily deviates by nearly 25%, resulting in a 1.3% maximum deviation in CL voltage and 2% deviation in the system frequency as shown in Fig. 11b, c which are within the acceptable ranges. The shunt side converter also works effectively to maintain the DC link voltage at 250 V with peak deviations of 3%.

Dynamic load switching on NCL side:

In this case, the dynamic load is connected to the NCL side. As in the previous case, the system was initially at steady state, supplying rated voltages to both CL and NCL. At that time, the ES did not inject any voltage, which can be clearly seen from Fig. 12b. As the dynamic load is switched on, the ES immediately reduces the voltage across the NCL, thus limiting the NCL power. The ES in this process injects a peak voltage of nearly 80 V, which in turn reduces the NCL voltage by 35%. So, the effect of dynamic load switching on the generator side is minimized, due to which the generator power deviates by nearly 15% as can be seen from Fig. 12b–d. This in turn results in CL voltage and frequency deviations of 0.2% and 0.6% respectively. Despite the dynamic load switching, the NCL current nearly remains constant with a maximum deviation of 7A as shown in Fig. 12a. In this process, the ES absorbs a peak power of 3.6 kW, which is successfully supplied by the shunt converter, which is clearly noticeable from Fig. 12b. The efficient power balancing done by the shunt converter assures a constant DC link voltage, where the DC link voltage is kept at 250 V with a momentary deviation of 2%, which is well below the 10% limit.

Performance of proposed-ES with NCL side dynamic load switching.

From this subsection, it can be clearly concluded that the proposed ES is well capable of maintaining a constant CL voltage and system frequency during dynamic load switching, both on the CL and NCL sides. Though the effect of the dynamic load switching on the CL side remains for a greater time as compared to that while dynamic load switching on the NCL side. This is due to the fact that while switching on the CL side, the dynamic load draws more current as it gets the full rated voltage during starting. But with the dynamic load connected to the NCL side, the load current is limited by the ES. As a result, the load switching has a lesser effect on the system when done on the NCL side.

Conclusion

This article focuses on the improvement of two major aspects of an ES. The first is to extend the operating range of the ES, and the second is to make the ES capable of reducing harmonics from the system voltage and currents, thereby reducing the harmonic losses in the system. For these purposes, the authors have proposed an ES with a back-to-back configuration. In this work, the proposed ES and its control structures have been developed, and its performance has been compared with the widely used battery-operated ES. In this work both the battery-operated and proposed ES were designed with a CL to NCL ratio as high as 1:1.36 for reduction of the overall rating and size of the ES as compared to ES proposed in previous literatures having CL to NCL ratio of 1:9. It was found that the proposed ES could successfully handle CL power variations up nearly 41.25% more than that with battery-operated ES. The extension in range of power management in case of the proposed ES was found to be 104% in the suppress mode and 88.88% in the support mode. With battery-operated ES, which is incapable of harmonic elimination, the generator current THD was 7.05%. These harmonics present in the generator current directly affect the system voltage, making its THD 4.43%. The proposed ES greatly reduces harmonics in system voltage and generator currents due to its current filtering capability. The generator current THD in the case of the proposed ES comes to 2.76% with system voltage THD of 0.55%. The performance of the proposed ES and its control structure was also evaluated taking computational delays into consideration. It was found from this work that the THD of the generator current increases to 3.34% with a computational delay of 1.8 µs and 3.58% with a delay of 2.5 µs, respectively, which confirms successful operation of the proposed ES in the presence of computational delays. The performance of the proposed ES was also evaluated with dynamic load switching and was found to be quite satisfactory. Considering losses occurring in the system, as given in Table 6, the proposed ES houses two 6-pulse bidirectional converters, resulting in switching losses twice that of the battery-operated ES, which has only one converter. Again, losses in the DC link and coupling transformer are also present in the proposed ES, though very small in magnitude, and are absent in the case of battery-operated ES. But, when it comes to battery losses and harmonic losses in the system, which generally constitute a substantial part of the system losses, the proposed ES has the advantage. Again, increasing the CL to NCL ratio reduces the converter ratings but increases the NCL voltage sensitivity towards CL. The NCL voltage could rise to dangerously high values with very low or zero CL values. So appropriate protection measures should be taken for systems where the CL has the tendency to reduce to very small values. This aspect can give rise to another regime of future research.
