Propolis nanoemulsion and mesenchymal stem cell conditioned medium promote osteoblastogenesis against lipopolysaccharide-induced osteolysis in hyperglycemic rats
Authors: Ananto Ali Alhasyimi, Alexander Patera Nugraha, Cendrawasih Andusyana Farmasyanti, Niswati Fathmah Rosyida, Hendri Susanto, Muh Ade Artasasta, Putri Cahaya Situmorang, Khairul Anuar Shariff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperglycemia is known to worsen lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory osteolysis. However, regenerative therapy with human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells conditioned medium (HUCMSCs-CM) and Tetragonula biroi propolis nanoemulsion (PNE) may suppress inflammatory osteolysis with hyperglycemia. This study investigated how HUCMSCs-CM and PNE affected the osteoblastogenesis markers in rats with hyperglycemia and inflammatory osteolysis. Twenty-eight healthy male rats (1–2 months old) were divided into seven groups: NC (negative control), LP (100 µL LPS), HG (> 230 mg/dL), LH (100 µL LPS with hyperglycemia), LHH (LPS, hyperglycemia, and 100 µL HUCMSCs-CM), LHN (LPS, hyperglycemia, and 100 µL PNE), and LHHN (LPS, hyperglycemia), and 100 µL LPS from Escherichia coli was used to induce calvarial osteolysis. PNE and HUCMSCs-CM were prepared and administered subcutaneously into the calvaria. Thereafter, hyperglycemia was induced via intraperitoneal administration of 30 mg/kg of streptozotocin for 1 week. The rats were sacrificed on day 8, and ELISA was used to measure Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin in the blood samples. Immunohistochemistry was employed to examine the osteoblastogenesis markers; RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin. In the LPS-induced inflammatory osteolysis model with hyperglycemia, administration of HUCMSCs and PNE significantly increased osteoblastogenesis markers (P ≤ 0.05). The combination treatment showed the most pronounced effect, enhancing serum Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin levels, as well as RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin expression. These findings indicate that HUCMSCs-CM and PNE synergistically promote osteoblastogenesis in hyperglycemia-aggravated inflammatory osteolysis and may represent a promising regenerative therapeutic strategy for inflammatory bone loss under compromised metabolic conditions.Keywords: Infectious disease, Medicine, Osteoblast, Regenerative dentistry, Stem cellsSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research, Stem cells

Introduction

Osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoblast-mediated bone formation are responsible for maintaining the integrity of the bone metabolic homeostasis1,2. Inflammation is crucial for the initial stages of repair following tissue injury, and its imbalance disrupts the intricate process of bone remodeling3. Osteoblast development is suppressed, bone formation is reduced, and osteoclast differentiation is enhanced by chronic inflammation, which results in increased bone resorption4. Inflammatory osteolysis diseases may develop when inflammation occurs in the vicinity of bones. The activation and recruitment of osteoclasts, which are initiated by the production of inflammatory cytokines, results in the disruption of the bone metabolic homeostasis and enhanced bone resorption. Inflammatory osteolysis is primarily caused by this process5. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a constituent of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria, is a potent inducer of bone resorption by promoting the development and activity of osteoclasts while repressing osteoblasts that are responsible for bone formation6. Systemic LPS prompts the immune system to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, which might further aggravate the disrupted bone metabolism and promote bone resorption7. Furthermore, hyperglycemia synergistically enhances LPS-induced impairment of macrophage phagocytosis via the endoplasmic reticulum stress–C/EBP homologous protein pathway in vivo and ex vivo8. In addition, LPS exposure adversely affects bone metabolic outcomes, including trabecular bone morphology and bone mineral density. Moreover, it enhances osteoclast activity, as observed from the specific bone cell markers and serum indicators9..

Propolis is a substance that is generated by stingless bees (Tetragonula biroi) and has been shown to possess a wide range of potential therapeutic bioactivities, such as antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties10,11. Furthermore, the encapsulation of propolis in a nanoemulsion can enhance its multipotent activities10. The oral administration of propolis nanoemulsion (PNE) has demonstrated enhanced anti-osteoporosis efficacy in an ovariectomized mouse model by inhibiting osteoclast activity and promoting osteoblast function, thus resulting in balanced bone remodeling12. Furthermore, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HUCMSCs) represent a viable resource in regenerative treatments because of their multipotency, self-renewal capabilities, and immunomodulatory characteristics. These attributes confer HUCMSCs with the potential for use in regenerative therapies13. HUCMSCs have been demonstrated in numerous studies to be a promising and superior therapeutic choice among all mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) types due to their beneficial attributes, such as easy preparation, cost-effectiveness, non-invasive collection methods, abundant cell yield, and minimal immunogenicity14. The principal mechanism by which HUCMSCs facilitate tissue regeneration is through the release of bioactive substances referred to as the HUCMSC conditioned medium (HUCMSCs-CM)15. Consequently, HUCMSCs-CM has become a novel and promising regenerative therapeutic alternative.

Recent evidence suggests that many of the regenerative effects of mesenchymal stem cells are mediated not only by direct engraftment, but also by paracrine mechanisms through the release of bioactive factors collectively referred to as the secretome or conditioned medium16. Conditioned medium contains a complex mixture of cytokines, growth factors, chemokines, extracellular vesicles, and other soluble mediators that can modulate inflammation, reduce oxidative stress, promote cell survival, and support tissue repair. Because it is a cell-free product, conditioned medium has emerged as an attractive alternative to stem cell transplantation, with potential advantages in manufacturing, storage, transport, and safety15. In addition, conditioned medium derived from oral mesenchymal stem cells has shown cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in experimental models, further supporting its relevance for regenerative medicine17. Therefore, HUCMSCs-CM may represent a practical and biologically active regenerative strategy for restoring bone formation under inflammatory and metabolically compromised conditions.

Bone development and remodeling are determined by the activity of osteoblasts, which arise from the condensation of MSCs. The maturation of osteochondroprogenitors and the subsequent differentiation of pre-osteoblasts into mature osteoblasts are regulated by a complex network of transcription factors. Regarding these transcription factors, Runt-related transcription factor-2 (RUNX-2) and osterix are critical in osteogenesis18. RUNX2 modulates the expression of various osteoblastic genes, including osterix, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), collagen type I (Col1a1), osteopontin, osteonectin, and osteocalcin19. Because of their critical function in bone formation, several osteoblastic genes have recently emerged as a prominent focus in research, with possible implications for the development of novel therapeutic techniques for osteolysis. Currently, there is a lack of research that emphasizes the potential effects of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE on LPS-induced osteolysis and hyperglycemic conditions. HUCMSCs-CM is known to support tissue repair and osteogenic differentiation through paracrine bioactive factors, whereas PNE may attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress while supporting bone regeneration. Therefore, their combined administration was intended to determine whether these agents could exert additive or synergistic effects on osteoblastogenesis in hyperglycemia-aggravated inflammatory osteolysis. It is hypothesized that administration of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE can enhance the serum levels and expression of osteoblastogenesis markers in hyperglycemia with inflammatory osteolysis. The objective of this study was to examine the impact of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE on the expression and serum levels of osteoblastogenesis markers under hyperglycemia conditions with osteolysis in rats (Rattus norvegicus).

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of Health Research. This study was a true experimental study with a post-test-only control group design. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines checklist. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Health Research Ethics Committee (No. 0856/HRECC.FODM/V/2025).

HUCMSCs-CM metabolite and PNE preparation

The preparation of HUCMSC commences with the collection of the plain culture media following the fourth culture of HUCMSCs. The culture media was harvested during the fourth passage of HUCMSCs and then centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 3 min to sediment and exclude debris and necrotic cells. In addition, the medium was filtered through a 0.45-µm syringe filter to purify the bioactive compounds produced by the HUCMSCs during proliferation. The HUCMSCs from the culture media were purified using dialysis to eliminate residual conditioned medium (CM) products, yielding the isolated soluble bioactive factors released by HUCMSCs during culture, which were subsequently filtered with a microfilter. A previous investigation demonstrated the formulation of PNE derived from stingless bees (T. biroi)10. Thereafter, 250 g of propolis (T. biroi) was macerated with 96% ethanol in 2.5 L vessels. The mixture was filtered, and ethanol was evaporated at 40–50 °C using a rotary evaporator. After the viscous extract had been dried in an oven, an equivalent volume of ethanol was added to the extract. As per a previous study, the 100% propolis extract was attenuated to a 1% concentration in dimethyl sulfoxide and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resulting in nanoparticles with a diameter of 151.28 to 182.2 nm10. The HUCMSCs-CM and PNE were formulated in a 1:1 ratio.

LPS-induced osteolysis animal model using Wistar rats (R. norvegicus)

Twenty-eight specific pathogen-free and healthy male Wistar rats (1–2 months old, 250–300 g body weight, Stem Cell Research Centre Animal Laboratory, Airlangga University, Indonesia) were divided into seven groups, namely NC: negative control group; LP: 100 µL LPS; HG: hyperglycemia (> 230 mg/dL); LH: 100 µL LPS with hyperglycemia; LHH: LPS, hyperglycemia, and 100 µL HUCMSCs-CM; LHN: LPS, hyperglycemia, and 100 µL PNE; and LHHN: LPS, hyperglycemia, and 100 µL HUCMSCs-CM and PNE. Sample selection was performed using the blind simple random sampling technique. An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; F. Faul, University of Kiel, Germany). Based on a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) and a statistical power of 95% (1 − β = 0.95), the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 3.25 animals per group. The number was therefore rounded up to four animals per group, resulting in a total sample size of 28 rats. Animals were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center, Universitas Airlangga, and housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50–60%. Standard pellet diet and water were provided ad libitum. Prior to the experiment, all rats underwent a one-week acclimatization period to minimize stress and maintain physiological stability.

The Wistar rat calvaria osteolysis animal model was induced with 100 µL LPS from Escherichia coli. An intraperitoneal injection of 30 mg/mL of streptozotocin (STZ) dissolved in citrate buffer was administered intraperitoneally in the region adjacent to the midline between the two nipples or in the right/left rodent umbilicus. Induction was performed once; the rat was held, and the area to be injected was sterilized with 70% alcohol. The STZ was gradually injected after the needle was inserted perpendicular to the right and left umbilical cords of the peritoneal cavity. For 7 days, 100 µL of PBS, HUCMSCs-CM, and LPS were administered once daily. The animals were euthanized via an overdose of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and cervical dislocation were performed on day 8. The calvaria of the rats were isolated for further analysis using immunohistochemistry. Further analysis was conducted using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with the sandwich technique, with blood samples collected from each group that were processed to obtain the plasma.

ELISA

The serum concentration and presence of Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin were determined using the sandwich ELISA method. The sandwich ELISA method includes the following steps: preparation, sample incubation, washing, target antibody incubation, sample washing, analysis, and interpretation. This was performed following the instructions of the rat Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin ELISA kit (BT LAB, Shanghai Korain Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

The standard curve for Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin was generated using the reference standard supplied in the ELISA kit (standard concentration: 50 ng/mL; detection range: 0.78–50 ng/mL; sensitivity: 0.16 ng/mL). Serial dilutions of the standard were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the optical density of each standard concentration was used to construct the calibration curve from which sample concentrations were derived.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemistry was employed to determine the positive expression of osteoblastogenesis markers in rats from the different groups, including RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin.

Calvarial bone tissues were carefully excised from the rats and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h at room temperature (approximately 27 °C). After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial sections of 4–5 μm thickness were then prepared from the same paraffin block of each calvarial specimen using a rotary microtome and mounted onto poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides. The slides were incubated at 60 °C for at least 1 h before staining. To allow comparison of different osteoblastogenesis-related proteins within corresponding tissue areas, immunohistochemical staining for RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin was performed on adjacent serial sections derived from the same calvarial tissue block specimen.

The slices were incubated in 3% H₂O₂ for 15 min, underwent microwave irradiation in sodium citrate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 95 °C for 15 min, and were subsequently blocked with 5% goat serum for an additional 15 min. After a 12-h incubation at 4 °C with a specific primary antibody (anti-RUNX-2, anti-osterix, anti-osteonectin, and anti-osteocalcin; Abcam, Cambridge, USA), the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. The sections were imaged using a light microscope (BX63, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), counterstained with hematoxylin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas, USA), and visualized with a diaminobenzidine kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The positive expression of RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin in the osteoblasts was assessed by two blinded investigators over five distinct fields under a microscope at magnifications of 100×, 400×, and 1,000×. Cells with brown osteoblast cytoplasm or nuclei were considered positive.

Statistical analysis

The study data were summarized and analyzed using the mean and standard deviation in the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Illinois, Chicago, USA). One-way analysis of variance and post hoc Tukey’s honest significant difference were employed. Statistical significance was determined at p ≤ 0.05. Asterisks (*) represent the level of statistical significance (*p ≤ 0.05), while “ns” indicates non-significant results (p > 0.05).

RESULTS

There is a notable difference in RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin expression among the treatment groups. PNE and HUCMSCs-CM considerably increased the expression of osteoblastogenesis-related markers (RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin) in the rats with LPS-induced osteolysis and hyperglycemia, as demonstrated by the immunohistochemistry results in vivo. The lowest expression of RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin and osteocalcin was observed in the osteoblasts in the LPS-induced osteolysis with hyperglycemic group. Conversely, the groups administered HUCMSCs-CM and those subjected to PNE exhibited heightened RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin and osteocalcin expressions, which was particularly pronounced in the combination group of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE administration. In this group, osteoblast cells demonstrated significant proliferation, as evidenced by the intense brown staining observed in the cell nucleus/cytoplasm (Figs. 1, 2 and 3 A). The quantitative analysis validated these findings, revealing significant differences among the groups (p < 0.05 to p < 0.0001), with combination group of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE administration exhibiting the highest count of RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin positive osteoblast cells, while LPS-induced osteolysis with hyperglycemic group showed the lowest expressions (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4B).

ELISA investigation showed that HUCMSCs-CM in conjunction with PNE considerably increased the Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin serum levels in the rats with LPS-associated inflammatory osteolysis calvaria and hyperglycemia (Fig. 5A-C) (p ≤ 0.05). Statistically, nearly all group comparisons exhibited significant differences (p < 0.05 to p < 0.0001), reinforcing the evidence that HUCMSCs-CM and PNE act synergistically in improving the serum levels of Coll1a1, ALP, and osteopontin and the expression of RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin.

Discussion

Up until now, this is the first in vivo investigation that examines how HUCMSCs-CM and PNE affect osteoblastogenesis-related indicators in LPS-induced osteolysis under hyperglycemic conditions. This investigation addresses a critical gap in regenerative medicine, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for complex bone pathologies that are exacerbated by systemic metabolic and inflammatory challenges. The primary mediators of inflammatory osteolysis are believed to be bacterial endotoxins, such as LPS. Moreover, LPS stimulates the formation and activity of osteoclasts by inducing the production of osteoclast cytokines, such as Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (M-CSF) and the nuclear factor kappa beta (NF-κB) ligand receptor activator nuclear kappa beta ligand (RANKL), which leads to catastrophic bone loss20.

Our findings demonstrate that the combined administration of HUCMSCs and PNE significantly enhanced the serum levels and expressions of key osteoblastic markers—Coll1a1, ALP, osteopontin, RUNX-2, osterix, osteonectin, and osteocalcin. This substantial improvement highlights the promising regenerative therapeutic potential of this combined approach for the treatment of inflammatory osteolysis, particularly in the context of hyperglycemia. The ability of HUCMSCs and PNE to counter these detrimental effects emphasizes their therapeutic relevance. The mechanism by which this combination exerts its beneficial effects likely involves a multipronged approach. The HUCMSCs likely acts by directly providing pro-regenerative signals. This includes various growth factors and cytokines and exosomes that directly stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts or enhance the activity of existing osteoblasts15,21–23. This direct stimulation leads to the upregulation of key osteogenic transcription factors, such as RUNX2 and Osterix, and subsequently the increased expression of their downstream target genes, including Coll1a1, ALP, osteopontin, osteonectin, and osteocalcin. The upregulation of these osteoblastic markers carries profound importance for the processes of bone formation and repair. Elevated levels of Coll1a1, as the predominant organic component of the bone matrix, signify enhanced collagen synthesis, which is fundamental for the structural integrity and scaffolding of new bone24. ALP serves as an early indicator of osteoblast differentiation and activity, playing an indispensable role in the initial stages of mineralization25. Furthermore, osteopontin, a non-collagenous protein, is intricately involved in bone mineralization and remodeling, and its increased levels suggest the improved organization and quality of the newly formed bone matrix26. Osteopontin is involved in the biological activities, such as proliferation, migration, and adhesion, of various bone-related cells, including bone marrow MSCs, hematopoietic stem cells, osteoclasts, and osteoblasts27. Osteoblasts synthesize osteocalcin, a significant non-collagenous protein in the bone. Typically, the formation of bone can lead to an increase in serum osteocalcin levels28. Furthermore, RUNX-2 and osterix are recognized as pivotal transcription factors that govern the osteogenic process18,19. The overexpression of RUNX-2 can enhance the maintenance of the osteoblastic phenotype in MSCs29. In addition, bone development and remodeling are intricately regulated processes that are fundamentally dependent on the precise activity of osteoblasts, which originate from MSCs30. The maturation of osteochondroprogenitors and the subsequent differentiation of pre-osteoblasts into mature osteoblasts are tightly controlled by a complex network of transcription factors31..

The regenerative potential of HUCMSCs for the treatment of bone inflammatory disease has been mentioned in several studies32–34. In an animal model with calvaria bone defects, secretomes derived from MSCs cultured as monolayers exhibited enhanced bone regeneration in comparison to CM derived from 3D spheroid cultures32. The CM of MSCs promoted osteoblast differentiation by increasing the protein expression of RUNX2, ALP, and osteopontin. Furthermore, the CM of MSCs improved bone regeneration in rats with calvaria defects by increasing the bone volume, bone volume/total volume, bone surface, and trabecular number when compared to those of the rats with untreated defects33. In addition, in a rat distraction osteogenesis model, human fetal MSCs demonstrated a high degree of osteogenic induction and low immunogenicity34..

PNEs play a crucial role in modulating the hostile microenvironment. Its well-documented anti-inflammatory properties are vital in counteracting the severe inflammatory cascade that is initiated by LPS exposure and exacerbated by hyperglycemia. Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties have been demonstrated in propolis, particularly in nanoemulsion form. Additionally, it has demonstrated anti-osteoporosis efficacy by inhibiting osteoclast activity and promoting osteoblast function35,36. Moreover, the bioactivity of propolis is improved by the nanoemulsion formulation10. PNE establishes a more favorable environment by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitigating oxidative stress, which in turn reduces the suppression of osteoblasts and diminishes osteoclast activity. In addition, the anti-osteoporosis efficacy of PNE was explicitly identified as involving the inhibition of osteoclast activity and the promotion of osteoblast function12, which directly complements the pro-osteogenic signals from HUCMSCs. This implies that PNE is actively clearing the “debris” and stabilizing the “construction site” by reducing inflammation and bone breakdown, while HUCMSCs provide the “building blocks” and “instructions” for new bone development. This dual action guarantees that the regenerative potential of HUCMSCs can be fully realized in a metabolic and inflammatory environment that would otherwise be detrimental. The success of this combined therapy indicates that treatment with HUCMSCs alone may encounter difficulty in achieving their full regenerative potential in a highly inflammatory and metabolically compromised environment. Similarly, PNE alone may not generate sufficient osteogenic signals to stimulate substantial new bone formation. Therefore, this “two-front” strategy, which simultaneously addresses environmental modulation and regeneration, is essential for the effective treatment of complex chronic diseases in which multiple pathological pathways contribute to tissue degradation.

This study directly addresses the previously unmet research gap by providing novel and crucial evidence for a combined therapeutic approach within a highly relevant and intricate pathological context. The fundamental understanding that inflammatory osteolysis in the presence of hyperglycemia represents a multifaceted pathological scenario is underscored by the efficacy of this approach. Hyperglycemia exacerbates this by impairing macrophage phagocytosis and further disrupting bone metabolism, while LPS actively promotes osteoclast activity and suppresses osteoblasts, resulting in significant bone resorption20,37. A single-target therapeutic intervention would likely be insufficient to comprehensively mitigate the injury and promote bone regeneration in this complex scenario. The comprehensive impact observed across multiple osteoblastic markers in this study indicates that this combined approach simultaneously and effectively addresses various critical aspects of bone formation and remodeling, thereby verifying the efficacy of this powerful multitarget therapeutic strategy for complex bone diseases. This method is promising for conditions such as diabetic periodontitis or other systemic inflammatory bone losses, where combination therapies may provide superior outcomes by concurrently addressing various aspects of the disease.

Although these results are encouraging, it is crucial to recognize the inherent limitations of the current study in order to inform future research endeavors. Initially, the duration of the research was relatively brief. This was sufficient to detect changes in the immediate osteoblastogenesis markers; however, longer-term studies are necessary to evaluate the structural integrity and sustainedbone regeneration. Future research should examine whether the observed increase in bone formation markers results in sustained bone regeneration and the effective prevention of osteolysis recurrence in chronic hyperglycemic conditions. Valuable insights into the treatment response kinetics over time would also be obtained by incorporating longitudinal data collection. Secondly, additional mechanistic studies are necessary to clarify the precise molecular pathways and cellular interactions through which HUCMSCs and PNE achieve their synergistic effects. Detailed investigations into osteoblast–osteoclast crosstalk, comprehensive gene expression profiling (e.g., RNA sequencing), proteomic analyses to identify key secreted factors in the HUCMSCs, and a detailed examination of the intracellular signaling cascades involved in osteoblast differentiation and bone formation could be involved in this. It will be essential to comprehend these complex mechanisms in order to optimize therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In a rat model of LPS-induced inflammatory osteolysis with hyperglycemia, this study offers compelling evidence that the key osteoblastogenesis markers are substantially improved by the combined administration of HUCMSCs-CM and PNE. The pro-regenerative signals from HUCMSCs-CM synergize with the anti-inflammatory and bone-remodeling modulatory effects of PNE to counteract the complex pathological environment, highlighting a robust multi-target therapeutic strategy. Future research should concentrate on the elucidation of the intricate mechanisms, the assessment of long-term efficacy, and the advancement toward clinical translation, with a particular emphasis on the demonstration of tangible and functional improvements in bone health.
