A simulation-based methodological analysis of caesarean-section patterns in women of reproductive age in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Md. Tanwir Akhtar, Tintu Thomas Uthup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Caesarean section (CS) rates have increased substantially in both developed and developing nations. Although CS is a life-saving procedure when medically indicated, clinical guidelines generally recommend vaginal birth for uncomplicated pregnancies. In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), reported national statistics indicate that caesarean sections account for approximately 34.6% of all births as of 2023, with considerable regional variation. However, limited individual-level data are available to empirically examine factors associated with CS in this high-income setting. The goal of this study was to reproduce publicly reported national caesarean section distributions and to illustrate statistical associations between maternal, neonatal, and regional characteristics using a simulation-based analytical framework. Publicly available aggregate statistics from the Women Health and Reproductive Care Survey 2023 were used to construct a synthetic dataset for methodological illustration. A synthetic dataset representing 28,741 women was generated for the analysis. Chi-square methods and multivariate logistic regression were used to determine the associations between maternal age, region, nationality, anaemia status, delivery assistance type, newborn weight, and delivery type (normal vs. CS). In the simulated dataset, maternal age, region, nationality, anaemia status, and newborn weight exhibited statistically detectable associations with caesarean delivery; these relationships reflect imposed population distributions rather than empirical effects. In the simulation, women aged 45–49 showed inflated odds ratios (OR = 15.64, 95% CI: 9.77–25.44) due to sparse category structure and imposed marginal distributions, illustrating a common instability in regression modelling of synthetic data. There were regional variations, with Al-Baha showing the highest CS rate (46.5%), followed by Tabuk (42.3%) and Northern Border Region (41.3%). Low birth weight (< 2.5 kg) was a strong predictor of CS (OR = 34.37, 95% CI: 30.84–38.36). Saudi nationality corresponded to lower estimated odds of CS (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.62–0.76) compared to non-Saudi residents, reflecting imposed nationality distributions rather than empirical effects. The model demonstrated high internal discrimination within the simulated dataset, which reflects internal consistency rather than real-world predictive performance. Maternal age and regional characteristics exhibited the strongest model-based associations with C-sections in Saudi Arabia. These results support creating region-specific health strategies targeted at different age groups to optimize delivery practices and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Future studies using comprehensive individual-level data are required to empirically evaluate these patterns and investigate additional factors influencing C-section trends.

Introduction

In both rich and developing nations, caesarean sections, popularly known as C-section (CS), are increasingly, however, clinical guidelines generally recommend vaginal birth for uncomplicated pregnancies.1–3 The decrease in maternal and neonatal mortality associated with medically indicated CS delivery have been cited as contributing factors to its increased utilization over the past decade.4–6 Number of demographic, clinical, and health-system factors have been associated with the rise of CS. A significant proportion of women in developing nations are delaying having children until nearing the end of their reproductive lives due to changes in demographics, social progress, and educational attainment.7 The number of women who become pregnant for the first time after turning 35 has grown as a result of societal growth combined with the accessibility of birth control and infertility treatments. In the CS, an abdominal and uterine incision is made in order to deliver the baby. When a vaginal (or traditional) birth puts the mother and the child at significant clinical risk, this treatment is recommended. Although caesarean delivery is generally considered safe when medically indicated, it is associated with higher rates of certain complications compared with vaginal birth.8 In addition to the dangers and complications that arise after birth, CS delivery is also associated with higher costs compared with vaginal birth, largely due to surgical procedures and longer hospital stays.9 The most frequent issues that mothers and new-borns experience during and after CS have already been covered.10–13.

In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), national statistics report that CS account for approximately 34.6% of all births as of 2023 according to the Women Health and Reproductive Care Statistics Bulletin.14,15 Reported regional variation is considerable, ranging from under 20% to over 35%, with Al-Baha showing the highest rates at 46.5% and Jazan among the lowest.14,15 Recent studies have reported differences in CS rates between Saudi and non-Saudi women, potentially reflecting variations in parity, access patterns, and private-sector utilization.15,16 Additionally, caesarean delivery on maternal request, without clear medical indication, has been documented in Saudi Arabia and contributes to the overall CS rate, although its prevalence remains lower than that reported in some other countries.17 Evidence further shows that elective CS rates have increased disproportionately compared to emergency CS, with common indications including previous CS, fetal distress, and malpresentation.15,16

Earlier WHO recommendations suggested a population CS rate of 10–15%,18 but current guidelines stress that rates should reflect medical need rather than strict quotas.19 Contemporary WHO guidance clarifies that the 10–15% benchmark should not be interpreted as a target and that CS should only be performed when medically justified. Efforts should be directed at giving CS to women who are in need, not toward meeting a target rate.18 More recent research examines CS trends in relation to maternal morbidity and health-system capacity rather than fixed numerical limits.28.

Recent WHO reports and international studies show that rising CS rates vary widely across regions, driven by demographic, clinical, and health-system factors.14,15,18,19

The proportion of caesarean deliveries among all births within a certain time frame and geographic location is commonly used to characterize the CS rate. Because caesarean delivery is only possible at medical facilities, the models and estimates of the CS rate that are currently in use for a certain geographic region make sense if the majority of births in that area happened at these facilities. Furthermore, because a CS is a surgical treatment that can only be performed in a medical facility, the distribution of caesarean delivery among women from various regions of KSA who had institutional births is discussed using reported aggregate statistics. This study aims to reproduce reported national caesarean section distributions and demonstrate statistical modelling approaches using simulated microdata derived from publicly available aggregate statistics.

Factors associated with caesarean section

Indeed, as per the 2030 Vision, KSA is genuinely making a consistent effort to tackle the health issues in the region.20,21 Additionally, the country aims to encourage safe and healthy maternal and child health and to promote deliveries in the presence of qualified medical professionals, within suitable hygienic conditions.22 A model has been developed to examine the correlation between the CS outcomes and selected maternal and neonatal characteristics, such as Age Group, Region, Nationality, Anaemia Status, Type of Assistance, and Newborn Weight. This classification matches the international standards for newborn weight used when assessing the risk of a C-section.18 Previous research indicates differences between Saudi and non-Saudi women in CS rates, suggesting that demographic and cultural factors may influence delivery mode. Our goal is to reproduce reported CS distributions in KSA using a simulation-based analytical framework. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to apply a multivariate statistical modelling approach on national-level data from KSA to explore how maternal demographics and regional health service characteristics are reflected in model-based associations with CS delivery.

Methodologies

Objective of the study

The goal of this study is to reproduce reported national caesarean section distributions and to examine model-based associations between maternal, neonatal, and regional characteristics using a simulation-based analytical framework. These characteristics include maternal age, region, nationality, maternal anaemia status, type of assistance during delivery, weight of newborn, and delivery-related maternal and newborn variables incorporated into the simulation model.

This objective aligns with contemporary research priorities in KSA, where rising CS rates motivate further examination of demographic and service-related patterns reported in national statistics.15,16.

Data source and study population

Publicly available aggregate statistics from the Women Health and Reproductive Care Survey 2023, which included Saudi and non-Saudi women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years), who had been married at some point and regularly residing in the KSA, were used to construct a synthetic dataset for methodological illustration. Although descriptive data included all women, only those who were ever married and reported at least one delivery were included in the main analysis of caesarean section deliveries.14,15 The survey’s birth-related questions refer specifically to the most recent birth reported by each woman.

Two phases of data collection were used for the survey: the “telephone contact” phase (CATI) and the “electronic link” phase (CAWI), during which data was gathered from a sample of fertile women (15–49 years aged) to represent all people in all 13 administrative regions of the Kingdom. A total of 51,720 women were selected as a representative sample, distributed across all administrative regions. Finally, a total of 28,741 women who reported at least one birth were included in the main analysis of caesarean section deliveries. See Reference Metadata [Metadata Report of Women Health and Reproductive Care Survey Statistics, 2023. Retrieved from: https://www.stats.gov.sa/en/1239] for further information on the data collection, validation, and compilation.

Data extraction and simulation

For extracting the raw data from the given percentage values, the distributions for age groups, nationalities, and marital status based on the specified percentages was first defined. Simulation was done which given a dataset consisting of exactly 51,720 rows by randomly sampling values for each category using these distributions. For example, age groups are sampled based on the percentages of women who have ever been married, with nationality divided into 70% Saudi females and 30% non-Saudi females. Marital status was categorized based on the breakdown of groups within the data, for example, 66.66% were married and 27.53% had never been married. By setting a seed for reproducibility, it was confirmed the dataset remains consistent and accurately replicates the specified demographic percentages. This method allows us to create a synthetic dataset that closely reflects the original demographic breakdown. The simulation process was used only to reconstruct detailed, individual-level patterns from the broader, aggregated data, and does not claim to represent actual GASTAT microdata. That distinction is important for transparency and aligns with recent recommendations for how simulated health data should be used.23,24 The steps used above for statistical simulation were reviewed by a qualified statistician to assess methodological coherence and internal consistency.

Justification and validation

To ensure the integrity of the simulation process, the Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test was utilized to compare the observed frequency distribution of variables, including “Delivery_Type” “Weight” “Assistance_Type” and “Is_Anemia_Reported?“, with the expected frequency distribution as per the given percentages. The null hypothesis (H₀) assumed that the observed data aligns with the expected distribution, while the alternative hypothesis (H₁) indicated a significant difference between the observed and expected distributions. The test results showed with high p-value (e.g., 0.9854) indicated no significant difference, suggesting that the simulated data closely reproduces the expected aggregate proportions specified in the source statistics, and the null hypothesis was not rejected. Following validation, the simulated dataset served as the basis for statistical modelling, including multivariate logistic regression to generate model-based odds ratios of CS delivery for illustrative purposes. This process ensures that the models are based on realistic and representative data, which ensures internal consistency with published aggregate distributions but does not confer empirical validity or inferential reliability. Simulation validation procedures follow current best practices for synthetic health data generation and verification.23,24

Statistical methods used

Initially, the Chi-square methods were used to evaluate the relationship between various maternal characteristic such as Age Group, Region, Nationality, Anaemia Status, Type of Assistance, and Newborn Weight—and the type of delivery (Normal vs. Caesarean Section). The Chi-square test is able to determine whether there is a significant association between these categorical variables and the CS delivery outcomes within the constructed dataset. Analytic procedures reflect current recommendations for population-level CS risk modelling in maternal health research.18,19.

Further, a multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to examine model-based associations between simulated delivery outcomes and selected maternal and neonatal characteristics —such as age group, administrative region or province, citizenship, anaemia status during pregnancy, assistance type in delivery, birth weight of newborn— a multivariate logistic regression model has been constructed. The model has been developed to examine the correlation between the CS outcomes and some related factors, such as Age Group, Region, Nationality, Anaemia Status, Type of Assistance, and Newborn Weight. Mother’s subjective estimate of age was used to divide the population into 7 subgroups: 15–19, …, 45–49. The dwelling of the population is classified into the 13 administrative regions – Riyadh, Makkah, Madinah, Qassim, Eastern Region (ER), Aseer, Tabuk, Hail, Northern-border (NB) Region, Jazan, Najran, Al-Baha, and Al-Jouf. Nationality of the participants was grouped as either Saudi or Non-Saudi. The Anaemia status of mother during pregnancy and breastfeeding were recorded as either yes, or no. The delivery assistance type is categorized as per the qualification of attending professionals such as Doctor (male or female), Nurse, or Female Attendant. Newborn weight is grouped into four categories: exactly 2.5 kg, less than 2.5 kg, greater than 2.5 kg, and cases where weight was not measured. The outcomes of the regression analysis are displayed as model-based odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

To investigate the impact of independent variables on the likelihood of both caesarean and non-caesarean birth, binary logistic regression models have been employed to analyse the associations with the CS outcomes, corresponding to each of the associated maternal deterministic factors. In these models, the occurrence of a caesarean section is represented as a dichotomous variable, with “0” for non-caesarean and “1” for caesarean deliveries.

All data visualization, data simulation, and statistical analyses were conducted using the powerful R programming language (version: 4.5.0).25 Key packages used included dplyr for data manipulation, ggplot2 for visualization, and glm from base R for logistic regression modelling. The use of open-source tools enhances transparency and reproducibility of the analytic process.

Ethical consideration

This study is a secondary analysis based on publicly available, aggregate data obtained from the Reference Metadata [Metadata Report of Women Health and Reproductive Care Survey Statistics, 2023. Retrieved from: https://www.stats.gov.sa/en/1239]. As no individual-level or identifiable data were accessed, ethical approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) was not required. The data are fully anonymized and accessible in the public domain, thus posing minimal risk to individuals. All procedures adhered to the ethical principles recommended by ICMJE and COPE for the secondary use of publicly available data.26,27.

Inferential statistics

Chi-square analysis

This study employed Chi-square method to assess whether the distribution of maternal characteristics differs significantly between females who had a normal delivery and those who had a CS. Establishing these statistically significant associations between variables within a simulated dataset can provide insights into the factors influencing a woman’s decision for a c-section or the doctor’s recommendations. These results are presented for methodological demonstration only and are not intended to inform healthcare policy or clinical practice. The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis indicate several significant predictors of caesarean section (CS) deliveries. One of the major factors influencing the type of delivery is the region. Various regions show statistically significant odds ratios (p < 0.05). For example, Riyadh province has an odds ratio (OR) of 1.01, whereas the province Najran has an OR of 0.56, both indicating a lower likelihood of CS compared to the reference region, Qaseem, which has an OR of 0.084. This suggests regional variations in CS delivery practices. Other regions, such as Aseer (OR = 0.46), the Eastern Region (OR = 0.48), Hail (OR = 0.60), and Jazan (OR = 0.40), also demonstrate significant reductions in the odds of undergoing a CS. These findings highlight that the likelihood of CS varies considerably by region.

Maternal age groups, particularly those between 20 and 49 years old, show a strong association with the likelihood of CS delivery. The odds ratios for different age categories range from 2.05 for those aged 20–24 to 15.64 for those aged 45–49, with all p-values considerably below 0.05, which indicates older age groups are significantly more likely to undergo a CS compared to younger groups. Additionally, nationality—specifically whether individuals are Saudi or non-Saudi—also appears to have a significant impact on the type of delivery, with an odds ratio of 0.68 (p < 0.05). This implies that Saudi nationals are less likely to have a CS compared to non-Saudis.

In contrast, the status of anaemia did not exhibit a significant effect (p = 0.89), and the odds ratio for anaemia (OR = 3.47e-9) is extremely low, suggesting that it has no substantial impact on the likelihood of a caesarean section (CS). Similarly, the assistance type variable produced extreme model estimates (OR = 7285.09, p < 0.05), reflecting data sparsity and quasi-separation rather than substantive effects.

In terms of newborn weight, the category of “less than 2.5 kg” had a significantly higher likelihood of requiring a caesarean section (CS), with an odds ratio of 34.37 (p < 0.05). In contrast, the categories of “more than 2.5 kg” and “not measured” showed no significant effect on the likelihood of CS, with odds ratios close to 1 ((OR = 0.63 and OR = 1.28e + 08, respectively).

In summary, the multivariate regression model indicates that regional differences and mothers’ age are the variables contributing the strongest statistical signal within the regression model. Older maternal age and specific regions—particularly Qaseem, Aseer, and Jazan—are associated with a higher likelihood of CS delivery. Other factors such as anaemia, nationality, type of assistance, and newborn weight appear to have less influence, with some variables needing further investigation due to unusually high or low odds ratios that may result from quasi-complete separation or low event frequency in certain categories. Future modelling may consider collapsing sparse levels or using penalized logistic regression.

These findings highlight the importance of regional healthcare practices and maternal age in shaping delivery choices, emphasizing the need for more targeted interventions based on regional and demographic factors.

Discussion

The present study utilizing the simulated analytical framework shows that maternal age and regional variation exhibited the strongest statistical associations with CS outcomes. This modelling behaviour is consistent with patterns reported in empirical studies, where advanced maternal age has been associated with higher CS likelihood across diverse populations.7,27,29 The imposed regional variation in the simulated data mirrors regional differences reported in empirical studies, which have been attributed to healthcare access, provider preferences, and institutional policies. Similar patterns of regional variation have also been documented in multi-region analyses, including studies conducted in Jordan,29 Turkey,30 and Pakistan,31 as well as broader WHO multicounty analyses.2,19.

The national CS rate in KSA, ranging between 25 and 32% in recent MOH reports, puts the Kingdom above the WHO’s recommended 10–15% range but similar to rates observed in many high-income countries.14,19,32 The simulated patterns were constructed to resemble trends reported in recent Saudi studies conducted in Riyadh and Eastern Province (2021–2023), which also reported increasing CS rates linked to maternal age and facility-level practice patterns.15,16,33

Low birthweight exhibited a strong statistical association with simulated CS outcomes, which shows regional evidence from Gulf countries showing increased surgical intervention among high-risk pregnancies.15,28 This association is consistent with WHO and UNICEF findings that low-birth-weight infants are more often delivered surgically in settings where hospital-based obstetric services are widely accessible.18,21 These findingsillustrate how fetal risk indicators can dominate regression outputs in hospital-based maternity datasets.

In contrast, anaemia and assistance type, while statistically significant in the chi-square analysis, showed unstable or inflated effects in regression models, suggesting data limitations rather than true causal relationships. Similar problems have been found in CS modelling studies from Jordan, Egypt, Turkey, and Pakistan, where sparse categories and quasi-complete separation led to unstable or inflated odds ratios.29–31,34,35.

Overall, the results illustrate how maternal age and regional structure can strongly influence statistical associations in simulated CS models, consistent with patterns reported in international literature.15,19,32.

Conclusion

This study provides a methodological demonstration of how simulated microdata can be used to explore statistical associations related to caesarean section delivery, identifying maternal age, regional differences, and additional factors such as anaemia status and type of assistance as significant predictors. However, the effects of anaemia status and assistance type should be interpreted with caution because the regression model revealed instability and potential sparse-cell bias for these variables. Similar issues have been reported in CS modelling studies from Jordan, Turkey, and Pakistan, where low-frequency categories distort odds ratios.29–31

The statistical analyses, including Chi-square and logistic regression, revealed that older maternal age and specific regions are more strongly associated with C-sections. Moreover, variables such as anaemia and newborn weight also impact delivery outcomes. Newborn weight demonstrated a meaningful association with delivery type, while the influence of anaemia was statistically significant only in univariate testing and did not remain robust in adjusted analyses.

These results are intended for methodological illustration only and should not be used to inform clinical guidelines or health policy. These interpretations should also be viewed in light of the simulated nature of the dataset, which limits the precision of effect estimates despite preserving population-level patterns.

Limitations of the study

Although the study provides valuable insights, it also has some limitations. The cross-sectional design based on simulated data hinders establishing causality and restricts tracking changes over time; however, it could bring forth a deeper understanding of the factors influencing delivery type. Moreover, the study’s regional analysis may not consider all factors that could affect delivery practices, such as socioeconomic status or access to healthcare. The existence of missing information like newborn weight data and reliance on self-reported information introduces potential bias that may affect the accuracy of the findings. Additionally, the sample may not adequately represent all demographics, particularly underrepresented groups, which could limit the results’ generalizability.

Furthermore, this study is based on simulated data generated from publicly available percentage distributions reported on the Reference Metadata [Report of Women Health and Reproductive Care Survey Statistics, 2023 (https://www.stats.gov.sa/en/1239). As the actual microdata or individual-level raw data were not accessible, the analysis lacks the granularity and precision that such data would provide.

The purpose of the simulation was to replicate general patterns in caesarean section (CS) likelihoods and to test analytic approaches in the absence of microdata. While this method offers useful insights, it cannot fully capture the complexity or variability of real-world data. These limitations underscore the need for future research using comprehensive microdata to validate and refine the findings presented here. This limitation corresponds with published cautions on the interpretability of synthetic health data in public health research.23,23.

Future scope of the study

Future research should explore longitudinal studies to monitor changes in maternal health and delivery outcomes over the time, which will contribute stronger insights into causality. Including socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and facility type in data collection would enhance understanding of regional disparities and delivery factors. Furthermore, a comprehensive analysis of regional healthcare practices and policies could yield empirical insights into factors associated with CS delivery patterns. In addition to that exploring pre-existing health conditions, patient choices, and cultural influences would deepen the understanding of delivery options, resulting in more personalized healthcare strategies and better outcomes for women in various regions. Future studies should also analyse actual microdata once released by GASTAT to validate simulated findings and produce more stable effect estimates.
