Association Between Diagnosis-to-Ablation Time and Postablation Outcomes in Older Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Shiyi Wang, Jiongchao Guo, Ziliang Song, Shaohui Wu, Nannan Chen, Feng Zhang, Xu Liu, Xumin Hou, Mu Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between the timing of catheter ablation and post-ablation outcomes in older patients with persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) remains uncertain.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate whether diagnosis-to-ablation time (DAT) influences post-ablation outcomes in older patients with persistent AF.MethodsFrom January 2020 to December 2022, 587 patients were stratified into 2 groups: the early ablation group (DAT ≤12 months, n = 332) and the late ablation group (DAT >12 months, n = 255) based on DAT. The study outcomes included freedom from atrial arrhythmia (AA) recurrence and adverse cardiovascular outcomes (death, cardiovascular hospitalization, ischemic stroke, and a composite outcome comprising all 3 outcomes).ResultsThe final analysis included 560 patients after 27 patients were lost to follow-up. After 24 months follow-up, 67.9% (216/318 patients) with the early ablation group were freedom from AA recurrence vs 59.1% (143/242 patients) in the late ablation group (HR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.52-0.91; P = 0.005). Moreover, the late ablation group showed higher risks of the composite outcome (HR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.23-2.34; P = 0.001) and cardiovascular hospitalization (HR: 1.57; 95% CI: 1.10-2.26; P = 0.011) compared with the early ablation group at 24-month follow-up. Ischemic stroke (HR: 1.87; 95% CI: 0.83-4.19; P = 0.126) and death (HR: 2.20; 95% CI: 0.54-8.93; P = 0.267) did not differ significantly between the 2 groups.ConclusionsIn older patients with persistent AF, a DAT ≤12 months was associated with lower AA recurrence and lower risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting that early catheter ablation may improve clinical outcomes in this population.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia worldwide. Epidemiological studies indicate that nearly 70% of patients with AF are elderly individuals (≥65 years).1 The elderly population with AF has a high risk of stroke, heart failure, and death, imposing a substantial burden on health care systems. Rhythm control is often the preferred strategy for AF management.2 Prior studies have shown catheter ablation significantly reduces AF recurrence rate and improves quality of life compared with antiarrhythmic medications.3,4 Recent studies have shown that catheter ablation elderly population have a similar efficacy and reasonably low complication rates to that in younger people for AF ablation.5, 6, 7 The 2020 European Society of Cardiology guidelines emphasized that catheter ablation for selected elderly AF patients might be a safe and effective option.8 However, the optimal timing of catheter ablation in older patients with AF remains uncertain.

Recent evidence has shown diagnosis-to-ablation time (DAT) is an independent risk factor for persistent AF.9 A study of patients with persistent AF and left ventricular systolic dysfunction demonstrated that shorter DAT (<1 year) was associated with better clinical outcomes compared with longer DAT (≥1 year).10 A recent large-scale study (n = 2,000) suggested that impact of DAT may be more crucial for persistent AF than for paroxysmal AF.11 Early ablation is associated with better post-ablation outcomes and reduces AF burden and AF progression.12 Meanwhile, age is the most important prognostic risk factor for AF. However, the relationship between DAT and post-ablation outcomes (atrial arrhythmia [AA] recurrence and adverse cardiovascular outcomes) in older patients with persistent AF is not known. Therefore, we conducted a multicenter study to evaluate the prognostic impact of DAT on post-ablation outcomes in this specific population.

Methods

Study population

From January 2020 to December 2022, we conducted a prospective, multicenter study including consecutive 704 older patients with persistent AF from Shanghai Chest Hospital and other participating clinical centers. Patients were eligible if they were 65-80 years, diagnosed as persistent AF, had symptomatic AF refractory to at least 1 antiarrhythmic medication, and consented to receive radiofrequency catheter ablation. The exclusion criteria included: 1) history of AF ablation or cardiac surgery; 2) history of severe valve disease; 3) severe structural heart disease; 4) contraindications for cardiac catheterization; and 5) life expectancy <12 months.

The DAT was defined as the interval between documented AF diagnosis and the ablation procedure. Based on DAT ≤12 months and DAT >12 months, older patients with persistent AF were stratified into 2 groups: early ablation group (DAT ≤12 months) and late ablation group (DAT >12 months). The study flowchart is presented in Figure 1. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Chest Hospital and all other participating hospitals. Ablation protocol

All patients maintained uninterrupted oral anticoagulation throughout the periprocedural period, with antiarrhythmic medications discontinued for at least 5 half-lives prior to ablation. Transesophageal echocardiography or intracardiac echocardiography was routinely performed to exclude left atrial thrombus in all cases.

The ablation procedure was performed using radiofrequency energy in all patients. Pulmonary vein isolation combined with linear ablations (left atrial roof line, cavotricuspid isthmus line, and mitral isthmus line) were performed for all patients. Detailed procedural methodology is provided in the Supplemental Appendix. The procedural endpoint was to achieve pulmonary vein isolation and bidirectional conduction block of the linear lesion. If AA persisted after ablation, direct current cardioversion was performed to convert into sinus rhythm.

Study outcomes

The study outcomes included freedom from any documented AA episode lasting more than 30 seconds, without antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs), excluding the initial 3-month blanking period and adverse cardiovascular outcomes (death, cardiovascular hospitalization, and ischemic stroke and a composite outcome comprising all 3 outcomes). Cardiovascular hospitalization was defined as hospitalization due to AF-related symptoms, or other cardiovascular diseases such as heart failure and myocardial infarction.

Follow-up

All patients received anticoagulation therapy for a minimum of 3 months post-ablation unless contraindicated. Antiarrhythmic drug therapy was administered to all patients during the initial 3-month period. Patients were followed up at 3, 6, and 12 months, and every 6 months thereafter. Electrocardiogram and 24-hour Holter recordings were performed at 3, 6, and 12 months after the initial procedure. Subsequent follow-ups included clinic visits with 24-hour Holter monitoring every 6 months. Ad hoc electrocardiograms were performed if the patients experienced arrhythmic symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and were compared by means of chi-square analysis. To compare continuous variables, Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used, depending on the distribution of the variable. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to analyze freedom from AA recurrence and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Comparisons were based on the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of all study outcomes, with results reported as HRs with 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses were performed for AA recurrence: 1) adjusting for potential confounders (age, age at AF diagnosis, gender, body mass index [BMI], CHA2DS2-VASC score, use of AADs, comorbidities, and left atrial diameter [LAD]); and 2) censoring all lost-to-follow-up patients. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc) and SPSS version 27 (IBM).

Results

Baseline characteristics

This prospective, multicenter study enrolled 704 older patients with persistent AF from Shanghai Chest Hospital and collaborating centers between January 2020 and December 2022. After excluding patients with prior AF ablation (n = 87) and contraindication to ablation (n = 30), 587 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 332 patients were categorized into the early ablation group (DAT ≤12 months) and 255 patients were assigned to the late ablation group (DAT >12 months) (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Patients were 71.0 ± 3.8 years of age and 61.4% of them were men. The early ablation group had a significantly shorter DAT compared to the late ablation group (median 6.0 months [IQR: 3.0-9.0] vs 60.0 months [IQR: 36.0-83.0]; P < 0.001). The distribution characteristics of DAT are presented in Supplemental Figure 1. The early ablation group had a significantly older age at AF diagnosis compared to the late ablation group (70.9 ± 3.8 years vs 64.0 ± 7.1 years; P < 0.001). There were no other statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the 2 groups (Table 1). Study outcomes

After 24 months follow-up, recurrent AA occurred in 102 of 318 patients (32.1%) in the early ablation group and 99 of 242 (40.9%) in the late ablation group. Risk factor–associated adverse cardiovascular outcomes

The univariable and multivariable analyses of adverse cardiovascular outcomes are presented in Supplemental Tables 2 to 5. The univariable analysis showed DAT >12 months (HR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.23-2.34; P = 0.001), age at AF diagnosis (HR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.96-0.99; P = 0.015), and use of AADs (HR: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.02-2.08; P = 0.042) were statistically associated with the composite outcome (Supplemental Table 2). DAT analyzed as a continuous variable had an HR of 1.03 per year (95% CI: 1.01-1.05 per year; P = 0.020) (for each 1-year increase in DAT, the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes increased by 3.0%). After adjustment for significant univariable predictors (DAT >12 months, age at AF diagnosis, and use of AADs), age, and gender, the multivariable analysis showed DAT >12 months (HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.07-2.32; P = 0.019) remained independent predictive value (Supplemental Table 2).

The univariable analysis showed DAT >12 months (HR: 1.57; 95% CI: 1.10-2.26; P = 0.011), and age at AF diagnosis (HR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.95-0.99; P = 0.018), were statistically associated with cardiovascular hospitalization (Supplemental Table 3). DAT analyzed as a continuous variable had an HR of 1.03 per year (95% CI: 1.01-1.06 per year; P = 0.021) (for each 1-year increase in DAT, the risk of cardiovascular hospitalization increased by 3.0%). After adjustment for significant univariable predictors (DAT >12 months and age at AF diagnosis), age, and gender, none of the variables were not statistically significant in the multivariable model (Supplemental Table 3). Neither univariable nor multivariable analyses identified statistically significant predictors of ischemic stroke (all P > 0.05) (Supplemental Table 4). None of the variables were not statistically significant in the univariable model associated with death. Given the limited number of death events, which may be insufficient for any reliable multivariable analysis, no multivariate analysis was performed (Supplemental Table 5).

Sensitivity analyses

In Model 1, after adjusted for age and gender, early ablation (DAT ≤12 months) remained associated with a reduced risk of AA recurrence during the 24-month follow-up period (Model 1, Supplemental Table 6). Further adjustments for age, gender, and LAD did not alter these findings (Model 3, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age, gender, BMI, and CHA2DS2-VASC score did not alter these findings (Model 5, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age, gender, and use of AADs did not change the results (Model 7, Supplemental Table 6). Further inclusion of baseline comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, stroke, and coronary artery disease) also did not affect the outcomes (Model 9, Supplemental Table 6). Comprehensive adjustments for age, gender, baseline use of ADDs and baseline comorbidities remained consistent with the initial findings (Model 11, Supplemental Table 6). Similarly, adjustments for age at AF diagnosis and gender did not change the results (Model 2, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age at AF diagnosis, gender, and LAD remained consistent with the initial findings (Model 4, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age at AF diagnosis, gender, BMI, and CHA2DS2-VASC score remained consistent with the initial findings (Model 6, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age at AF diagnosis, gender, and use of AADs did not change the results (Model 8, Supplemental Table 6). Inclusion of baseline comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, stroke, and coronary artery disease) also did not change the results (Model 10, Supplemental Table 6). Adjustments for age at AF diagnosis, gender, baseline use of ADDs and baseline comorbidities remained consistent with the initial findings (Model 12, Supplemental Table 6).

Finally, censoring all lost-to-follow-up patients in the Cox proportional hazards model analysis yielded unchanged results (Model 13, Supplemental Table 6).

Discussion

Taken together, this prospective, multicenter study demonstrates that a DAT ≤12 months was associated with better clinical outcomes compared to DAT >12 months in older patients with persistent AF (Central Illustration). AF recurrence is strongly associated with increased symptom burden, impaired quality of life, and higher health care utilization. Recent studies indicate that DAT ≤12 months was associated with better post-ablation outcomes.10,13 Segan et al reported that shorter DAT (<1 year) was associated with greater arrhythmia-free survival with lower AF burden and rehospitalization.10 A recent meta-analysis of 6 studies revealed a shorter DAT ≤12 months was associated with a lower relative risk of AF recurrence compared with DAT >12 months.14 A study of 1,000 patients reported a decline in ablation success with DAT longer than 12 months.13 However, a recent randomized clinical trial indicated delaying ablation by 12 months (optimized medical therapy) did not result in reduced ablation efficacy.15 However, the study also emphasizes that while ablation timing remains important, effective medical management may mitigate the clinical impact of delaying ablation by up to 12 months. Similarly, our results indicate that compared to the early ablation, late ablation is associated with an increased risk of AA recurrence and adverse cardiovascular events.

A recent study involving 7,472 patients indicated increasing DAT was associated with higher incidence for a composite outcome (death and hospitalization/emergency department visit for AF, heart failure, or ischemic stroke).16 Similarly, another study demonstrated that compared with the early AF catheter ablation group, the late AF catheter ablation group was associated with increased risks of major adverse cardiovascular events and AF recurrence.17 A Danish nationwide register study reported that early ablation was associated with a significantly lower risk of a composite endpoint of heart failure, ischemic stroke, or death.18 The study demonstrated that early ablation within 1 year after AF diagnosis was independently associated with lower AF recurrence rates in elderly patients. Consistent with these studies, our study indicate that a composite outcome of adverse cardiovascular events (included death, cardiovascular hospitalization and ischemic stroke) further reinforced the benefits of early ablation, with the shorter DAT group exhibiting lower risks of composite adverse cardiovascular events and cardiovascular hospitalization. Notably, while ischemic stroke and mortality did not reach statistical significance, a trend toward higher event rates was observed in the delayed ablation group, which may become more pronounced with longer follow-up.

The association between prolonged DAT and worse outcomes may be explained by progressive atrial remodeling, including fibrosis and left atrial enlargement-factors that promote AF perpetuation and reduce ablation success.19 Our multivariable analysis identified both DAT >12 months and larger LAD as independent predictors of AA recurrence, suggesting that structural remodeling contribute to arrhythmia persistence.

Additionally, consistent with previous studies,20,21 this study showed the importance of early rhythm control in older patients with persistent AF. Our results suggest that older patients derive substantial benefit from early ablation, with no significant increase in procedural complications. The EAST-AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention Trial) proved that an early strategy rhythm control in the elderly determines a reduction of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.22 The 2020 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend catheter ablation for selected elderly patients based on its potential benefits, and our findings provide additional evidence supporting this clinical recommendation.8

This study is relevant for clinicians managing older patients with persistent AF. First, it suggests that delaying ablation beyond 12 months may result in diminished efficacy, reinforcing the need for timely referral to electrophysiology specialists. Second, the strong association between LAD and AA recurrence highlights the importance of early imaging to assess atrial remodeling, which could help stratify ablation candidacy. Third, given the higher rates of cardiovascular hospitalization in the late ablation group, early ablation may reduce health care costs and improve patient quality of life.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, the observed association between longer DAT and poorer outcomes may be confounded, especially considering the significant difference in DAT between the early ablation group and the late ablation group. Therefore, our findings cannot establish causal relationships with the same rigor as randomized controlled trials. Second, the precise timing of AF episodes may be uncertain, as documented AF could lag behind the actual onset of symptomatic episodes. Third, in the current analysis, we only included older patients with persistent AF; the findings may not be generalized to other AF populations. Clearer subgroup analyses, particularly stratification by age, could enhance the applicability of our results. In addition, as we observed a substantial difference between our early and late ablation groups (6.0 vs 60.0 months), there may be other variables that impacted the timing of ablation that is not sufficient accounted for in our multivariable analyses. In addition, the outcome assessment was not based on implantable continuous detection equipment, which may underestimate the rate of AA recurrence.

Conclusions

In older patients with persistent AF, a DAT ≤12 months was associated with lower AA recurrence and lower risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting that early catheter ablation may improve clinical outcomes in this population.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: The results of this study provide data on the association between DAT and post-ablation outcomes in older patients with persistent AF.
