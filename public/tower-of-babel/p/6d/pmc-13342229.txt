Expressive activation enhances user satisfaction in AIGC co-creation through perceived control and meaning-making
Authors: Ya-Qin You, Shing-Sheng Guan, Yu-Liang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As generative artificial intelligence (AIGC) tools gain widespread application in creative design, growing concerns have been raised over users’ diminished sense of control and the emotional shallowness of AI-generated content. These limitations may undermine users’ sense of engagement and overall satisfaction in co-creation tasks. This study investigates how expressive activation—defined as guiding users to recall and express personal emotions and memories during a simulated creative process—shapes perceived user experience in a questionnaire-based simulated AIGC co-creation scenario. Using a survey-based scenario task, data were collected from 129 participants who had general experience using AIGC tools (68 in the expressive activation group and 61 in the non-expressive activation group). The results showed that expressive activation significantly increased user satisfaction. Furthermore, perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability served as sequential mediators, forming a chained path: expressive activation → perceived control → meaning-making → satisfaction. These findings suggest that, within a questionnaire-based simulated co-creation scenario, encouraging expressive engagement can enhance participants’ perceived psychological agency and emotional connection, thereby improving perceived satisfaction. This study identifies key psychological mechanisms relevant to human–AI co-creation and offers preliminary design insights for building AIGC systems that may foster deeper meaning and emotional resonance.

Introduction

With the rapid development of Artificial Intelligence–Generated Content (AIGC) technologies, generative AI tools have been widely applied in visual design, artistic creation, and text production. These tools not only improve creative efficiency but also reshape traditional creative paradigms and human–AI collaboration models1. However, alongside productivity gains, user experience issues in AIGC contexts have become increasingly evident. Users tend to rely more heavily on the system, experience reduced perceptions of agency, and feel limited emotional identification with generated content2,3. These concerns suggest that, in highly automated creative environments, maintaining users’ psychological engagement and sense of agency has become a critical challenge for AIGC design.

Prior studies on human–AI co-creative systems have emphasized the importance of interaction design, communication mechanisms, and user involvement in improving collaborative experience and engagement4,5. In generative AI contexts, user experience also depends on whether systems provide sufficient support for users to monitor, guide, customize, and regulate uncertain generative processes1,6. Accordingly, many existing designs focus on operational enhancements, such as adding editing steps, increasing parameter control, or providing interactive feedback to strengthen behavioral participation. However, behavioral participation alone does not necessarily improve user experience.

The black-box nature and uncertain outputs of generative AI further intensify this issue. Users often find it difficult to predict generated results and understand how they are produced, which weakens emotional connection and sense of ownership toward the content2,7. Therefore, the key to AIGC interaction design is not simply to increase operations, but to activate users’ psychological engagement in uncertain generative contexts, enabling them to integrate generated content into their own cognitive and emotional systems.

In response, this study proposes the concept of “expressive activation” and defines it as an interaction mechanism in which the system guides users to recall and express personal memories, emotions, and experiences, thereby fostering active psychological engagement during the creative process. Unlike mere operational participation, expressive activation emphasizes the externalization of subjective content. It shifts users from passive recipients of generated content to active co-creators, thereby enhancing psychological investment and sense of agency. Prior research has shown that personal emotional expression, such as nostalgic reflection, can significantly enhance individuals’ sense of meaning and emotional resilience, and can buffer uncertainty and threat by restoring meaning8. Thus, guiding users to express personal experiences and emotions may serve as a key mechanism for promoting meaning-making and psychological engagement in human–AI co-creation.

To explain how expressive activation influences user experience, this study further introduces perceived control and meaning-making as the key psychological pathways. Meaning-making theory suggests that when individuals face uncertainty, they interpret and assign meaning to external events, integrating them into their cognitive frameworks and forming self-related understanding9. Meanwhile, perceived control is a central psychological mechanism in human–computer interaction. It influences trust and satisfaction and determines whether individuals regard system outputs as self-related outcomes10. In generative AI contexts, when users perceive that they can influence the creative process or outcomes, they are more likely to develop psychological ownership and construct meaning from the generated content.

Although prior studies have examined AIGC interaction design and user experience, several gaps remain. First, most research focuses on behavioral participation and technical optimization, while psychological participation mechanisms receive less attention. Second, there is a lack of systematic theoretical models explaining how users form meaning identification and a sense of agency in uncertain generative contexts. Third, few empirical studies conceptualize expression as an interaction mechanism and examine its psychological pathways. Therefore, how interaction design can activate users’ psychological engagement and how this process shapes user experience requires systematic investigation.

Based on these issues, this study reexamines AIGC interaction mechanisms from the perspective of psychological engagement. It uses a questionnaire-based simulated co-creation scenario to investigate how expressive activation shapes participants’ perceived user experience in a generative AI context. Specifically, expressive activation is treated as the core independent variable, while perceived control and meaning-making are introduced as mediating mechanisms. A psychological pathway model is constructed in which expressive activation influences user experience through perceived control and meaning-making, explaining how users develop a sense of agency and form experiential evaluations in uncertain generative environments.

The theoretical contribution of this study lies in proposing expressive activation as a key psychological mechanism in AIGC interaction and integrating perceived control and meaning-making theories to build a mediation model that explains user experience formation. Practically, the findings provide a psychologically grounded approach to optimizing AIGC interaction design and offer actionable guidance for enhancing users’ sense of participation, identification, and satisfaction.

Accordingly, this study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1: Within a questionnaire-based simulated AIGC co-creation scenario, can expressive activation shift participants’ perceived role from passive content recipient to active meaning constructor?

RQ2: Within this simulated scenario, can expressive activation significantly improve participants’ perceived satisfaction by enhancing perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability?

Literature review

This study is structured around a variable-oriented theoretical framework. Specifically, expressive activation is treated as the core independent variable, perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability are conceptualized as mediating mechanisms, and user satisfaction is treated as the key dependent variable. Accordingly, the following review does not merely summarize general background literature, but selectively organizes prior research in relation to the psychological pathway examined in this study. The review first clarifies the theoretical basis of meaning-making and perceived meaning-making ability, then discusses the role of perceived control in human–computer interaction, and finally prepares the conceptual foundation for the hypothesis development presented in Chapter 3.

Theoretical foundations and applications of meaning making

The concept of meaning making originated in the field of psychology, aiming to explain how individuals respond to challenges or traumatic events by integrating new experiences through cognitive and emotional processing, thereby restoring psychological coherence. Park and Folkman11 defined it as a cognitive mechanism that reconciles “global meaning” and “situational meaning,” helping individuals maintain a sense of purpose and self-consistency. Neimeyer12 further emphasized that individuals can renew their sense of identity and integrate meaning through narrative reconstruction. Park9 conceptualized meaning making as a dynamic regulatory process central to psychological adjustment and well-being.

Recent studies have shown that emotional memories—particularly nostalgia—can effectively activate the meaning-making process. Compared to recalling recent positive events or imagining the future, nostalgia more strongly enhances individuals’ sense of life meaning and plays a dual role in emotional regulation and cognitive repair, especially when facing “meaning threats”8. This suggests that meaning is not only constructed through internal cognitive processing, but can also be externally activated in interactive contexts. This mechanism provides a theoretical foundation for using expressive strategies in AIGC systems to stimulate users’ perceived ability to construct meaning through emotional and experiential expression.

Meaning making has also received extensive attention in the domains of aesthetics and art reception. Leder et al.13 proposed that the aesthetic process is essentially a multi-level meaning-making activity, in which individuals cognitively integrate elements of form, symbolism, and emotional content to generate layered meanings. Tinio14 further argued that audiences often engage in reverse inference to infer the creator’s intention, thereby achieving reinterpretation of the cultural context and emotional resonance. With the increasing application of AIGC technologies in art and design, how users can actively attribute meaning to algorithmically generated content has become a core research concern. Studies suggest that when users are excluded from the meaning-making process, they are more likely to experience “emotional emptiness” and “shallow engagement”2,15. Deep meaning is more likely to emerge when individuals integrate AIGC content into their own cultural experiences and identity narratives7.

Research in cognitive science and design has further revealed the mechanisms underlying meaning construction from the perspective of processing models. Kazmierczak16 was among the first to propose that design itself is a form of “designing of thinking,” and that well-crafted interfaces can trigger users’ meaning-making activities through sensory engagement. Coulson17 subsequently argued that meaning comprehension is achieved through semantic mapping and the construction of mental spaces, emphasizing the crucial role of contextualization and situational construction. Building on this, Kintsch and Mangalath18 proposed the “construction–integration model,” which views language and image comprehension not as static decoding, but as a dynamic construction of meaning conducted in working memory through the integration of context, prior experience, and knowledge structures.

In summary, existing studies have systematically explored the ontological mechanisms of meaning making from various perspectives, including coping psychology, emotional activation, aesthetic processing, and cognitive modeling. However, most of this literature focuses on the outcome of meaning construction, rather than on whether individuals perceive themselves as constructing meaning. That is, the activation and mechanism of the subjective psychological variable known as perceived meaning-making ability remains underexplored. In the context of AIGC interactions, it is particularly unclear how specific interactive designs may trigger users’ awareness of meaning attribution and their sense of participatory engagement. Therefore, this study identifies perceived meaning-making ability as a core variable, aiming to investigate its activation mechanism in AIGC interactions.

The role of perceived control in human–computer interaction

Perceived control (PC) refers to an individual’s subjective belief in their ability to regulate processes, intervene in task execution, and influence outcomes during goal-directed behavior19. As a fundamental psychological mechanism supporting user self-regulation and system engagement, perceived control has been widely shown to influence motivation, task performance, emotional regulation, and user satisfaction20. Compared to traditional attributional views such as internal versus external locus of control, recent studies emphasize the situational sensitivity and dynamic plasticity of control, focusing on whether users experience themselves as causal agents during interaction, thereby fostering psychological empowerment.

Within the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Ajzen21 conceptualized perceived behavioral control (PBC) as comprising two core dimensions: self-efficacy (the belief in one’s capability to perform a task) and perceived controllability (the degree to which the behavior is under one’s volitional control). This dual-construct model has enhanced the conceptual clarity of perceived control and has been widely adopted in technology acceptance and user interaction research as a predictor of behavioral intention and experiential outcomes.

In AIGC (Artificial Intelligence–Generated Content) environments, perceived control faces new challenges. On the one hand, the unpredictability of generative pathways and the opacity of algorithmic decision-making make it difficult for users to define creative goals, adjust generation processes, or interpret system outputs22. On the other hand, insufficient opportunities for intervention—such as a lack of adjustable parameters or feedback loops—may diminish users’ expressive agency and participation, thereby weakening their sense of agency and trust in the system23. Prior studies have shown that reduced control may lead to increased cognitive load, emotional detachment, and decreased satisfaction, potentially triggering user anxiety and reluctance to adopt the system1. In contrast, enhancing perceived control can foster users’ psychological safety and sense of belonging within the system, supporting more positive interaction experiences and platform attachment24.

In sum, perceived control in AIGC co-creation contexts is not merely a functional perception but also a form of psychological empowerment. It influences whether users are willing to engage deeply with the system and whether they can develop a sense of ownership and expressive identification with the generated content. Although prior studies have linked perceived control to trust-related evaluations in chatbot interaction25, failure anxiety in human–computer interaction26, and users’ monitoring and regulation demands in generative AI use6, its mediating role in meaning-making pathways within AIGC co-creation remains underexplored.

Taken together, the reviewed literature suggests that user experience in AIGC co-creation cannot be adequately explained only by technical performance or behavioral participation. Instead, it is necessary to consider how psychologically oriented interaction mechanisms may shape users’ perceptions of agency, meaning attribution, and evaluative outcomes. In the present study, expressive activation is proposed as such a mechanism. Building on the above review, Chapter 3 develops a conceptual framework in which expressive activation influences satisfaction through perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability.

Conceptual framework and hypotheses

Based on the variable-oriented literature review presented in Chapter 2, this chapter develops the conceptual framework and corresponding hypotheses of the study. The hypotheses are organized in the same logical order as the reviewed variables: the direct effect of expressive activation, its relationships with the two mediators, and the resulting effects on user satisfaction.

Expressive activation and user satisfaction

In the present study, expressive activation refers to an interaction prompt that guides users in expressing emotions, memories, and personal experiences during AIGC interaction, fostering deeper cognitive and emotional engagement. This mechanism highlights user agency in meaning-making, shifting from passive content consumption to active co-creation.

Psychological studies show that memory and nostalgia are powerful triggers for meaning construction, especially when facing ambiguous or disordered stimuli. Nostalgic reflection not only evokes a stronger sense of meaning but also provides emotional regulation and psychological restoration8. In AIGC systems, prompts that encourage personal expression may enhance users’ immersion and identification with the creative process.

User satisfaction with AIGC is influenced not only by content quality and efficiency but also by expressive alignment and emotional resonance during co-creation6. When users feel their input is acknowledged and meaningfully reflected, satisfaction tends to increase5. Thus, expressive activation is expected to be a key driver of satisfaction.

Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H1: Expressive activation positively influences user satisfaction.

Expressive activation and perceived meaning-making ability

Meaning making refers to the cognitive-emotional process by which individuals interpret complex experiences to achieve psychological coherence and understanding11,12. In art engagement, it supports emotional resonance and cultural identification13,14.

In AIGC contexts, where outputs often lack emotional depth and cultural grounding, users must actively embed personal meaning to connect with the content2,7. Nostalgic memories can further trigger meaning construction and reduce disengagement from symbolically shallow content8.

This study defines perceived meaning-making ability as users’ sense of being able to interpret symbolic content, express personal emotions, and relate it to their own experiences. It is measured across three dimensions: understanding, emotional expression, and experiential connection. Expressive activation, by guiding users to mobilize emotional and experiential resources and articulate inner intentions, is expected to enhance their sense of meaning-making ability.

Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2: Expressive activation positively influences perceived meaning-making ability.

Expressive activation and perceived control

Perceived control refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to influence the generative process, direct content development, and shape outcomes during interaction19,20. In HCI and human–AI interaction research, perceived control has been linked to deeper cognitive engagement, stronger system trust, greater satisfaction, and stronger continuance intention25,27,28.

In AIGC contexts, where outcomes are often unpredictable, users face greater psychological demands to guide content creation6. Providing control options, such as parameter adjustments, can strengthen users’ sense of agency.

Expressive activation may enhance perceived control by prompting users to draw on emotions and personal experiences, shifting their role from passive recipients to intentional participants. Empowering users in this way increases their sense of authorship over the generative process. Supporting this, recent research suggests that collaborative AI systems foster greater engagement and identification than directive ones29.

Beyond its direct relevance to agency, perceived control may also create favorable conditions for subsequent meaning-making. When users feel that they can influence the generative process, they are more likely to regard the output as connected to their own intentions rather than as externally imposed content. This stronger sense of self-involvement increases the likelihood that users will actively interpret the generated content, relate it to their own memories and experiences, and construct personal meaning from it. In this sense, perceived control does not only enhance process-related confidence, but may also serve as a psychological precursor to meaning attribution in AIGC co-creation.

Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H3: Expressive activation positively influences perceived control.

Perceived meaning-making ability and user satisfaction

Meaning making is a core cognitive process underlying aesthetic experience and emotional engagement13,14. In AIGC contexts, AI-generated content often lacks emotional depth and cultural intent2. When users actively assign personal meaning to such content, they can fill emotional gaps and deepen their engagement. Heruti7 also found that encouraging meaning-making enhances users’ identification with AI-generated works and improves satisfaction.

According to Park’s9 model, meaning making occurs when individuals reconcile discrepancies between prior meaning frameworks and new experiences. This reconstruction, through reflection or expression, supports psychological adaptation.

Thus, when users perceive a stronger ability to interpret and personalize AI-generated content, their satisfaction with the co-creation process is likely to increase.

Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H4: Perceived meaning-making ability positively influences user satisfaction.

Perceived control and user satisfaction

Perceived control plays a crucial role in enhancing users’ trust and satisfaction, particularly in open and uncertain interactive environments30. In AIGC contexts, systems that generate outputs without allowing user guidance can lead to a diminished sense of authorship and psychological disengagement, reducing creative involvement and satisfaction6.

According to control–value theory, emotions are shaped by perceived task controllability and value. A lack of perceived control may trigger feelings of helplessness or detachment, undermining satisfaction31. In contrast, enabling users to direct the generative process can strengthen their agency and identification with the task32.

We therefore hypothesize:

H5: Perceived control positively influences user satisfaction.

Mediation hypotheses

Building on the preceding analyses, we propose that expressive activation is associated with both perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability, as it invites users to recall and express emotions and personal memories during the co-creation process. However, these two mediators are not treated as entirely independent. In the present study, perceived control is conceptualized as a psychological condition that may be closely related to subsequent meaning-making. When users feel able to influence the creative process and regard themselves as agents involved in shaping the output, they may be more likely to perceive the generated content as self-relevant rather than externally determined. This stronger sense of agency may, in turn, be associated with a greater tendency to connect the content with one’s own experiences, intentions, and interpretive frameworks, thereby relating to higher perceived meaning-making ability.

From this perspective, perceived control may be linked to AIGC co-creation in two ways: it is associated with users’ confidence, agency, and more positive evaluations of the interaction process, and it may also be related to deeper meaning attribution through stronger self-involvement in the generated output. Correspondingly, meaning-making can be understood as an interpretive extension of this sense of agency, through which users move from feeling involved in the process to feeling able to understand, personalize, and emotionally connect with the outcome.

As core cognitive and affective mechanisms, both perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability have been shown to be positively associated with user satisfaction and system acceptance13. Moreover, the association between control and meaning construction is also consistent with the view that agency and self-regulation are important foundations for interpretation and psychological integration33.

Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H6: Perceived meaning-making ability mediates the relationship between expressive activation and user satisfaction.

H7: Perceived control mediates the relationship between expressive activation and user satisfaction.

H8: Expressive activation indirectly influences user satisfaction through a sequential path involving perceived control and meaning-making ability.

Method

Participants and procedure

A total of 170 adult participants with prior AIGC image creation experience were recruited through Credamo, a professional online participant recruitment and data collection platform in China. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 60 years, with the majority under 40 years old and holding a bachelor’s degree or above, reflecting the current demographic trend of younger and highly educated AIGC users. Participants completed the questionnaire using their own devices, including desktop computers, laptops, tablets, and smartphones. Because the study was administered online through Credamo, device type was not experimentally restricted. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Academic Committee of the School of Art and Design, Jiangsu University of Technology. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants provided informed consent before participation.

This study was conducted as a questionnaire-based scenario experiment. The experimental manipulation was embedded in an online survey and presented through written instructions and simulated AIGC co-creation materials. Participants were randomly assigned by the platform’s internal algorithm to either the expressive activation group or the non-expressive activation group.

The procedure was organized into the following steps. First, participants completed a pre-task questionnaire assessing control variables, including AIGC cognitive self-efficacy, perceived attractiveness of AIGC output, prior AIGC usage, and demographic information. Second, after random assignment, participants read the condition-specific written instructions embedded in the questionnaire. Third, participants completed the condition-specific input task. The expressive activation group recalled and briefly described personal memories, emotions, or experiences related to the given theme, whereas the non-expressive activation group provided neutral descriptive keywords related to the same theme. Fourth, participants viewed a standardized target image as the simulated output. Finally, they completed the post-task questionnaire assessing perceived meaning-making ability, perceived control, user satisfaction, and the manipulation check item.

The questionnaire platform imposed minimum display times across different sections of the study, including the demographic section, pre-task measures, scenario exposure, and post-task items, resulting in a minimum total display time of 92 s. However, because participants needed to read the scenario materials carefully, complete the written input task, and respond to both pre- and post-task measures, the actual total participation time was generally longer and was approximately 3–5 min.

To ensure the effectiveness of the experimental manipulation, the post-task questionnaire included a manipulation check item (e.g., “In this experience, I expressed personal memories, experiences, or emotions during the image creation process”) and an attention check item to identify inattentive responses. This questionnaire-based design allowed the study to manipulate the psychological orientation of user input while keeping the visual output constant across conditions.

During data cleaning, 41 responses were excluded based on three predefined criteria. First, 26 participants failed the attention check item, suggesting that they may not have processed the questionnaire materials carefully. Second, 4 participants exhibited mechanical response patterns, such as highly repetitive answering across items, which reduced confidence in the validity of their responses34. Third, 11 participants showed manipulation check scores that were clearly inconsistent with their assigned condition (e.g., rating 1 in the expressive activation group or rating 7 in the non-expressive activation group). In the present questionnaire-based scenario experiment, these cases were interpreted not as theoretically inconvenient cases, but as cases in which the intended psychological induction may not have been successfully established. Because the main purpose of the experimental manipulation was to differentiate between expressive activation and non-expressive activation, responses indicating a complete reversal of the intended experience were treated as probable manipulation failures.

At the same time, we acknowledge that such cases may also reflect meaningful individual differences in task interpretation or engagement. For this reason, the exclusion decision should be interpreted as a procedural control strategy rather than as a judgment that these participants’ responses were inherently invalid.

After data cleaning, the final valid sample consisted of 129 participants, including 68 in the expressive activation group and 61 in the non-expressive activation group (see Table 1). Experimental materials

The experimental materials included condition-specific written prompts, response fields, and one standardized target image. These materials were embedded in the online questionnaire to simulate an AIGC image co-creation scenario.

Both versions used the same creative theme to ensure comparability across conditions. The key difference was the psychological orientation of the input: the expressive activation materials encouraged autobiographical and emotional expression, whereas the non-expressive activation materials required neutral descriptive input.

A single standardized target image was presented to all participants after the input task. This image was used as a controlled stimulus rather than as a personalized output generated from each participant’s input. The purpose was to minimize confounding effects caused by differences in image quality, visual style, or semantic richness, and to isolate the psychological effect of the input manipulation itself.

This control strategy may have reduced the perceived correspondence between individual input and final output, particularly for participants who provided highly specific personal content. To partially address this issue, both conditions were anchored to the same thematic frame. Thus, the use of one identical target image should be understood as a control strategy intended to strengthen internal validity, while its possible implications for ecological realism are acknowledged in the Discussion section (see Fig. 2).

Measurement items and psychometric properties of each construct.

In addition to the focal study variables, the experiment included a manipulation check assessing expressive engagement. This was not measured through a formal multi-item scale, but through a single-item indicator designed to capture whether participants had actually engaged in recalling and expressing personal memories, emotions, or experiences during the questionnaire-based scenario task. The item was: “In this experience, I expressed personal memories, experiences, or emotions during the image creation process.” Because this measure served as a manipulation check rather than as a substantive latent construct, it was not included in the formal psychometric evaluation reported in Table 2. Accordingly, internal consistency indices such as Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and AVE were not estimated for this item.

For perceived control, the original scale included three items. One item with a factor loading below 0.60 was removed, leaving two valid indicators. All constructs demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s α > 0.70, AVE > 0.50, and CR > 0.7035,36, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data analysis and results

Multicollinearity and common method bias

To test for multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values were examined. All VIFs were below 2.2 and tolerance values exceeded 0.4, meeting accepted thresholds (VIF < 5, Tolerance > 0.2;37). The maximum condition index was 21.252, well below the cutoff of 3038, and no substantial variance clustering was observed, indicating no serious multicollinearity issues.

Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. An exploratory factor analysis of all measurement items and demographic variables showed that the first factor accounted for 39.15% of the variance, below the 40% benchmark39, suggesting that common method bias was not a major concern.

Manipulation check and between-group differences

Prior to comparing group means, Levene’s tests for homogeneity of variances were conducted for the four main variables. Results indicated that the assumption of equal variances was violated for all variables (p < 0.05). Therefore, Welch’s t-tests were used in subsequent analyses.

The manipulation check confirmed the effectiveness of the expressive activation condition. Specifically, participants in the expressive activation group reported significantly higher scores on the single-item manipulation check assessing expressive engagement (M = 4.60, SD = 1.63) than those in the non-expressive activation group (M = 3.93, SD = 1.35), t = − 2.543, p = 0.012, with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.506). This result supports the adequacy of the manipulation in differentiating the intended expressive orientation between the two conditions.

For the primary dependent variables, participants in the expressive activation group reported significantly higher perceived meaning-making ability (M = 5.51, SD = 0.76) than those in the non-expressive activation group (M = 5.12, SD = 1.17), t = − 2.19, p = 0.031, d = 0.95.

Mediation analysis

To further explore the mechanism through which expressive activation influences user satisfaction, this study employed a bootstrapping approach (5,000 resamples, 95% confidence interval) to test the mediating effects of perceived meaning-making ability and perceived control. Gender, age, AIGC usage frequency, cognitive self-efficacy, and perceived content attractiveness were included as control variables.

The results indicated that expressive activation had a significant positive effect on user satisfaction (β = 0.481, SE = 0.111, 95% CI [0.259, 0.700]), supporting H1. Further analysis showed that both perceived meaning-making ability (β = 0.412, SE = 0.084, 95% CI [0.245, 0.575]) and perceived control (β = 0.367, SE = 0.077, 95% CI [0.214, 0.518]) significantly predicted satisfaction, supporting H4 and H5, respectively.

Regarding indirect effects, expressive activation showed a significant indirect association with user satisfaction through perceived meaning-making ability (indirect effect = 0.223, 95% CI [0.094, 0.369]), supporting H6. It also showed a significant indirect association through perceived control (indirect effect = 0.181, 95% CI [0.074, 0.319]), supporting H7. Moreover, the sequential indirect association through perceived control and then perceived meaning-making ability was also significant (indirect effect = 0.096, 95% CI [0.036, 0.176]), supporting H8.

Discussion

Analysis of main findings

This study shows that, within a questionnaire-based simulated AIGC co-creation scenario, expressive activation significantly increased participants’ perceived satisfaction by improving both perceived control and perceived meaning-making ability. Mediation analyses further revealed that these two psychological constructs not only independently mediated the relationship but also formed a sequential pathway—indicating that expressive activation influences user satisfaction through a cognitive–affective mechanism: enhancing control → enabling meaning construction → increasing satisfaction. This finding provides a novel perspective for understanding user experience in generative interactions.

Specifically, within the simulated task, the expressive activation manipulation guided participants to recall and articulate personal memories and emotions, leading to a stronger sense of psychological engagement and agency. This effect of cognitive empowerment aligns with Cerejo’s40 proposition that diminished interactional control undermines user satisfaction and trust. It also supports findings from Rezwana and Maher4, who reported that users tend to trust and be more satisfied with AI systems that respond to their individual needs. This study extends that view by highlighting the importance of user expression: when users are given space for self-expression, they are more likely to perceive the AI as a collaborative partner rather than a mere tool, which enhances both their sense of control and satisfaction.

Moreover, the results validate the emotional function of memory and nostalgia in stimulating meaning-making. While previous psychological research has shown that nostalgic recall elicits a stronger sense of meaning than thinking about recent or future events—particularly under conditions of disruption or absurdity8—this study is the first to demonstrate this mechanism within an AIGC context. Findings suggest that emotional and autobiographical expression activated by the system not only deepens users’ interpretation of AI-generated content but also fosters stronger emotional alignment and ownership. This supports theoretical accounts by Park9 and Leder et al.13, which emphasize that meaning-making is fundamental to aesthetic and emotional experience, and is consistent with Jin et al.41 research on the Reframer system, which shows that co-creative AI feedback facilitates “agentive flow” and a more immersive creative experience.

At the same time, this study acknowledges the potential risks and boundaries of empowerment-oriented interaction. Notably, expressive activation may be considered a form of advanced anthropomorphism. While prior research has highlighted the benefits of anthropomorphic design—such as increased trust, empathy, and satisfaction4,5—Łukasik and Gut42 caution that highly human-like AI behavior can blur the boundaries between human and machine, leading users to unconsciously relinquish agency and expressive autonomy. Some studies have suggested that excessive emotional responsiveness and social mimicry may shift AI from a supportive partner to a more dominant agent, thereby weakening authentic user agency. Therefore, expressive activation must strike a careful balance: it should promote personal meaning-making and psychological involvement without overstepping into user autonomy. In other words, user satisfaction depends not only on the output quality or system intelligence but on whether users feel seen, heard, and respected throughout the interaction.

Although prior research has emphasized that the quality and novelty of generated content are key determinants of user satisfaction3, the present study highlights the critical role of interaction experience and psychological engagement. Even if the generated content is of high technical quality, users may still report diminished satisfaction if they lack sufficient expressive space and a sense of control during the creative process. Recent research on affective design has further demonstrated that users’ emotional preferences are shaped not only by the content itself, but also by the system’s visual presentation and interaction modality43. These insights go beyond the traditional Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which primarily emphasizes tool functionality and outcome acceptance, and instead point toward a more nuanced, psychologically grounded framework for understanding user satisfaction.

In sum, this study is among the first to empirically validate a chain mediation mechanism—“expressive activation → perceived control → perceived meaning-making → user satisfaction.” It deepens current theoretical models by emphasizing the psychological construction process and underscores the centrality of user agency and meaning-making in human–AI co-creation. This framework provides both theoretical and practical insights for designing next-generation AIGC systems that move from automated output to human-centered co-creation.

Theoretical implications

First, this study marks a theoretical shift from “technology acceptance” to “interactive meaning construction.” While existing research extensively employs models such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) to examine users’ willingness and satisfaction with AIGC tools—primarily emphasizing external factors like perceived usefulness and ease of use44,45—these models often focus narrowly on functional attributes while overlooking users’ subjective psychological processes and deeper engagement during creation. By introducing “expressive activation” as a novel interaction variable, this study identifies a psychological pathway of perceived control → meaning-making ability → user satisfaction. This mechanism-based approach helps bridge the gap between technology acceptance and psychological meaning-making, directly addressing Lou’s2 “paradox of artificial creativity,” which highlights the lack of human emotional expression and symbolic depth in technically innovative AIGC content. Moreover, the findings align with the principles of Human-Centered AI (HCAI) design29, which advocate for AI systems that empower rather than replace the human role in the creative process.

Second, the study establishes a user-initiated mechanism for meaning construction. It is among the first to empirically validate the role of expressive activation in facilitating users’ meaning-making during co-creation. By prompting individuals to draw upon personal memories and emotional experiences, the system enhances users’ cognitive engagement and emotional identification. This mechanism extends Park’s9 theoretical framework on meaning-making and integrates insights from Pekrun’s31 Control-Value Theory, which emphasizes the pivotal role of perceived control in shaping satisfaction. Additionally, the results resonate with Self-Determination Theory33, confirming that greater perceived autonomy and control during creative interaction significantly boost intrinsic motivation and user satisfaction.

Third, this study expands the application of Self-Determination Theory within AIGC interaction contexts. The experimental findings support the “autonomy–competence–satisfaction” pathway proposed by Deci and Ryan33, demonstrating that users who actively express personal experiences and receive responsive system feedback experience stronger perceptions of control and meaning, ultimately enhancing satisfaction. This study not only broadens the applicability of SDT in the field of AI-assisted creation but also offers a concrete and testable psychological mechanism for future studies in human–AI interaction.

Practical implications

Although the present findings are based on a questionnaire-based simulated co-creation scenario rather than a live interactive system, they nevertheless offer useful preliminary implications for AIGC system design, particularly with respect to enhancing users’ perceived interaction experiences.

First, it introduces a user-empowerment–oriented interaction design strategy. Most existing AIGC tools prioritize content quality and generation efficiency while paying limited attention to users’ psychological engagement and expressive needs during the creative process3. This study demonstrates that incorporating “expressive activation” into system design—by encouraging users to draw on personal emotions, memories, and experiences—can significantly enhance perceived control and satisfaction. This finding suggests that developers should shift from tool-centric to expression-centric design models. For example, systems could incorporate personalized expression prompts, allowing users to generate content through narrative cues or emotional recall, thereby reinforcing autonomy and engagement during interaction29.

Second, the findings offer design directions to address the prevalent issue of “emotional hollowness” and lack of resonance in AIGC-generated content. Although current AIGC technologies can generate structurally complete outputs, users often report a lack of emotional connection or cultural relevance2. This study suggests that systems should actively incorporate emotion-oriented features and mechanisms to activate autobiographical memory. Specifically, tools can offer affective keyword input, empathic feedback (e.g., emotional acknowledgment), and memory-evoking prompts to help users embed personal meaning into generated content and strengthen emotional resonance.

Third, the expressive activation mechanism shows promise beyond art and design, with potential applications in education, mental health, and cultural heritage. In educational contexts, prompting learners to recall and express personal learning experiences and emotions during AI-assisted content generation can improve personalization and enhance psychological engagement with learning materials. In mental health, such mechanisms may also foster emotional awareness and meaning construction, offering supportive pathways for therapeutic intervention.

In sum, the expressive activation mechanism proposed in this study provides a practical and scalable conceptual model for empowering user interaction in AIGC systems, pointing toward a more sustainable, human-centered future for AI design.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that suggest avenues for future research. First, regarding data processing, the exclusion of participants who failed manipulation checks—while intended to ensure the integrity of the psychological induction—may have inadvertently sidelined meaningful individual differences in engagement or interpretation. This may have reduced sample variability and influenced the observed effect sizes. Future studies should enhance methodological transparency by preregistering exclusion criteria and employing multi-item manipulation checks.

Second, the experimental scope and ecological validity were constrained by the use of a cross-sectional questionnaire-based simulated co-creation scenario and a static text-image presentation. Participants evaluated a standardized simulated process rather than engaging in live, input-contingent interaction with an actual AIGC system. This design limits the ability to capture the temporal dynamics, multimodal richness (e.g., voice interaction, real-time feedback), and personalized response patterns characteristic of real-world AIGC use. Finally, the role of individual differences was not systematically examined. Subsequent research should adopt longitudinal methods and multi-level modeling to investigate how personality traits and emotional regulation abilities moderate the sustained effects of expressive activation in more dynamic, high-fidelity environments.

In addition, the present study mainly operationalized expressive activation through nostalgia and autobiographical memory. Although this approach was effective in activating perceived meaning-making in the simulated co-creation scenario, it may not be equally suitable for all AIGC tasks. In more concrete, goal-directed, or professionally constrained co-creative contexts, users may not need or wish to rely on personal memories. Future research should therefore examine alternative activation pathways, such as value clarification, goal articulation, contextual framing, cultural association, problem-based reflection, or preference-based explanation, to determine how different forms of expressive or cognitive activation support meaning-making across diverse AIGC tasks.

Conclusion

This study empirically examined the effect of expressive activation on participants’ perceived experience in a questionnaire-based simulated AIGC co-creation scenario. The results showed that expressive activation significantly increased perceived control, perceived meaning-making ability, and satisfaction within this simulated context. The proposed chain mediation model—expressive activation → perceived control → perceived meaning-making → user satisfaction—enriches human–AI collaboration frameworks by incorporating psychological mechanisms and offers theoretical support for user-centered AIGC system design.

In summary, the present findings suggest that expressive activation may represent a psychologically grounded mechanism for improving perceived user experience in generative AI contexts, although this mechanism should be further tested in actual interactive AIGC systems. Future research may further extend and validate this model across diverse contexts and user groups, and examine whether alternative activation pathways—beyond nostalgia and autobiographical memory—can support meaning-making in more concrete, goal-directed, and professionally constrained AIGC co-creation tasks. Such efforts may further support the shift toward more human-centered and meaning-driven AIGC design.
