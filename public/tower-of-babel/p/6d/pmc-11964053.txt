Aspirin‐Free Strategy for Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Patients With Oral Anticoagulation: Prespecified Subgroup Analysis From the STOPDAPT‐3 Trial
Authors: Masahiro Natsuaki, Hirotoshi Watanabe, Takeshi Morimoto, Ko Yamamoto, Yuki Obayashi, Ryusuke Nishikawa, Kenji Ando, Satoru Suwa, Tsuyoshi Isawa, Hiroyuki Takenaka, Tetsuya Ishikawa, Minoru Yamada, Tetsuzo Wakatsuki, Yoichi Nozaki, Hideki Kitahara, Ryuichi Kato, Ryoma Kawai, Yohei Kobayashi, Mitsuru Ishii, Yoshitaka Goto, Koh Ono, Takeshi Kimura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe effects of aspirin‐free strategy on bleeding and cardiovascular events in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention with oral anticoagulation (OAC) have not been fully elucidated.Methods and ResultsWe conducted the prespecified subgroup analysis based on the use of OAC, including vitamin K antagonist and direct oral anticoagulants, within 7 days before percutaneous coronary intervention in the STOPDAPT‐3 (Short and Optimal Duration of Dual Antiplatelet Therapy‐3) trial, which randomly compared prasugrel monotherapy (2984 patients) to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with prasugrel and aspirin (2982 patients) in patients with acute coronary syndrome or high bleeding risk. The coprimary end points were major bleeding events (Bleeding Academic Research Consortium types 3 or 5) and cardiovascular events (a composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, definite stent thrombosis, or ischemic stroke) at 1 month. Among 5966 study patients, there were 530 patients (8.9%) with OAC (no aspirin: N=248, and DAPT: N=282) and 5436 patients (91.1%) without OAC (no aspirin: N=2736, and DAPT: N=2700). Regardless of the use of OAC, the effects of no aspirin compared with DAPT were not significant for the bleeding end point (OAC: 4.45% and 4.27%, hazard ratio [HR], 1.04 [95% CI, 0.46–2.35]; no‐OAC: 4.47% and 4.75%, HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.73–1.20]; P for interaction=0.82), and for the cardiovascular end point (OAC: 4.84% and 3.20%, HR, 1.53 [95% CI, 0.64–3.62]; no‐OAC: 4.06% and 3.74%, HR, 1.09 [95% CI 0.83–1.42]; P for interaction =0.46).ConclusionsThe no‐aspirin strategy compared with the DAPT strategy failed to reduce major bleeding events irrespective of the use of OAC. There was a numerical excess risk of the no‐aspirin strategy relative to the DAPT strategy for cardiovascular events in patients with OAC.Keywords: aspirin‐free strategy, oral anticoagulation, percutaneous coronary interventionSubject Categories: Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Anticoagulants

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor within 1 month after percutaneous coronary intervention might be reasonable even in patients with oral anticoagulation.

Oral anticoagulation (OAC) was superior to clopidogrel plus aspirin for prevention of thrombotic events in patients with atrial fibrillation. 1 Therefore, triple therapy with OAC on top of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) has been given just after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with atrial fibrillation. However, triple therapy with DAPT plus vitamin K antagonist (VKA) as compared with dual therapy with clopidogrel plus VKA was associated with higher risk of bleeding events without reducing thrombotic events in the WOEST (What Is the Optimal Antiplatelet and Anticoagulant Therapy in Patients With Oral Anticoagulation and Stenting) trial. 2 In the meta‐analysis, VKA‐based triple therapy was associated with higher risk of bleeding, including major and intracranial hemorrhages, as compared with direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC) based dual therapy. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, the lower risk of bleeding events in DOAC‐based dual therapy compared with triple therapy was counterbalanced by a higher risk of stent thrombosis even with triple therapy within a few days or up to 2 weeks at the longest in the dual therapy group. In the 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), 1 week of triple therapy followed by DOAC plus single antiplatelet therapy was recommended as the default strategy for patients with atrial fibrillation. 8 Triple therapy for longer than 1 week and up to 1 month was recommended (Class IIa) in patients with high ischemic risk. Therefore, optimal duration of triple therapy within 1 month after PCI is still a matter of debate.

Recently, the STOPDAPT‐3 (Short and Optimal Duration of Dual Antiplatelet Therapy‐3) trial has demonstrated that an aspirin‐free strategy compared with the standard DAPT strategy failed to reduce major bleeding at 1 month after PCI, with a signal suggesting increased coronary events such as definite or probable subacute stent thrombosis and any unplanned coronary revascularization. 9 In this study, we conducted a prespecified subgroup analysis based on the use of OAC within 7 days before PCI in the STOPDAPT‐3 trial to elucidate the safety and efficacy of an aspirin‐free strategy relative to the DAPT strategy within 1 month after PCI in patients treated with OAC.

METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary files.

Study Design and Population

The STOPDAPT‐3 trial (NCT04609111) was a physician‐initiated, prospective, multicenter, open‐label, adjudicator blinded randomized clinical trial, where we compared the experimental group of 1 month prasugrel monotherapy without aspirin with the control group of 1 month DAPT with aspirin and prasugrel in patients planned for PCI using an implanted cobalt‐chromium everolimus‐eluting stent (Xience™ series, ABBOTT Vascular). The detailed study protocol and main results were reported elsewhere. 9 In brief, a total of 6002 patients with ACS or non‐ACS with high bleeding risk were enrolled between January 2021 and April 2023 from 72 centers in Japan and were randomly allocated just before PCI in a 1‐to‐1 ratio to either prasugrel monotherapy (no‐aspirin group) or to 1 month DAPT with aspirin and prasugrel (DAPT group). In the current study, we conducted the prespecified subgroup analysis of the STOPDAPT‐3 trial based on the use of OACs within 7 days before PCI. The definition of OACs was use of either VKA or DOACs at any dose.

After exclusion of 5 patients who did not receive PCI for the absence of suitable coronary lesions, 1 patient who had been enrolled in another clinical trial, and 30 patients who withdrew consent, the final analysis set consisted of 5966 patients, including 2984 patients in the no‐aspirin group and 2982 patients in the DAPT group (Figure 1). Kyoto University Certified Review Board approved the study protocol. The ethical committees in all the participating centers also approved the study protocol, and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Antithrombotic Regimen

Antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy before enrollment and intravenous heparin use after PCI were left to the discretion of the site investigators. A loading dose of prasugrel 20 mg was administered just after randomization in both groups. Aspirin 162 to 200 mg was also loaded orally in aspirin‐naïve patients in the DAPT group. In the no‐aspirin group, prasugrel 3.75 mg/day was continued up to 1 month after PCI. In the DAPT group, aspirin 81 to 100 mg/day and prasugrel 3.75 mg/day were continued up to 1 month after PCI. 10 , 11 At the 1‐month (30–59 days) visit, patients in the no‐aspirin group stopped prasugrel and received clopidogrel monotherapy up to 1 year, and patients in the DAPT group discontinued prasugrel and received aspirin monotherapy up to 1 year.

End Points

The coprimary bleeding end point was defined as the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium type 3 or 5, and the coprimary cardiovascular end point was defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, definite stent thrombosis, or ischemic stroke. 12 The major secondary end point was defined as a composite of the coprimary bleeding and cardiovascular end points representing net adverse clinical outcomes. Myocardial infarction and stent thrombosis were defined by the Academic Research Consortium criteria. 13 The definitions of other end points were described in Data S1. The independent clinical event committee adjudicated all the clinical events comprising the coprimary end points based on the source documents in a blinded fashion to the assigned treatment groups.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage, and continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD or median with interquartile range depending on the distributions. Cumulative incidences of the end points were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and the differences were assessed by the log‐rank test. The risks of OAC relative to no OAC, and the no‐aspirin group relative to the DAPT group for the end points were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs by the Cox proportional hazard model. As a sensitivity analysis, the cumulative incidences of the primary bleeding end point were also calculated by the Fine–Gray model assuming death as a competing risk that can prevent major bleeding to happen. In the present study, we analyzed the treatment‐subgroup interactions in the effects of the no‐aspirin group compared with the DAPT group in the OAC and no‐OAC subgroups for the clinical outcomes. Moreover, the analyses were stratified by the presence or absence of ACS. We also conducted sensitivity analyses in patients with and without OAC at PCI and at discharge. OAC at discharge was evaluated in patients who were alive at discharge within 30 days after the index PCI (patients with in‐hospital death within 30 days or those still hospitalized at 30 days were excluded). All reported P values were 2 sided. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analysis was performed with JMP version 15.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Study Population

Among 5966 patients, there were 530 patients (8.9%) with OAC (no‐aspirin group: N=248, and DAPT group: N=282) and 5436 patients (91.1%) without OAC (no‐aspirin group: N=2736, and DAPT group: N=2700). The proportion of patients with ACS was much lower in patients with OAC (ACS: 204 patients [38.5%], and non‐ACS: 326 patients) than in patients without OAC (ACS: 4272 patients [78.6%], and non‐ACS: 1164 patients) (Figure 1).

Baseline Characteristics

Patients with OAC were older and more often men compared with those without OAC (Table S1). Patients with OAC less often presented with ACS but more often had current heart failure compared with those without OAC. Patients with OAC more often had past histories such as PCI, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure and comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, diabetes, anemia, and left ventricular systolic dysfunction. In procedural characteristics, left main coronary artery was more often treated in patients with OAC. The intravascular imaging was more often used and staged procedure was less often performed in patients with OAC than in those without. In terms of medications, the prescription rate of beta‐blockers was higher in patients with OAC than in those without at discharge. Statins and proton pump inhibitors were less frequently prescribed in patients with OAC at discharge.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the no‐aspirin and DAPT groups, except for slightly but significantly lower level of hemoglobin in the DAPT group in patients with OAC, higher prescription rate of proton pump inhibitors in the DAPT group than in the no‐aspirin group in patients with and without OAC, and higher prescription rate of statins in the no‐aspirin group than in the DAPT group in patients without OAC (Table 1).

Antithrombotic Therapy

Within 7 days before randomization, antiplatelet agents were more frequently used and intravenous heparin was less frequently used in patients with OAC than in those without. OAC was discontinued in 12.1% of patients before PCI in the group with OAC. A loading dose of prasugrel was administered in 98.5% of patients in patients with OAC and in 99.6% of patients without OAC (P<0.001). Total heparin dose during PCI was numerically lower and activated clotting time was significantly higher in patients with OAC than in those without. After PCI, intravenous heparin was less often used in patients with OAC than in those without. At discharge, OAC was prescribed in 88.7% of patients in the group with OAC and in 5.2% of patients in the group without OAC (Table S1). At 1 month, OAC was prescribed in 90.7% of patients with OAC and in 6.0% of patients without OAC (Figure S1).

Antithrombotic therapies were well balanced between the no‐aspirin and DAPT groups, except for higher prescription rate of P2Y12 inhibitors only within 7 days before assignment in the no‐aspirin group than in the DAPT group in patients without OAC (Table 1). At 1 month, OAC was prescribed in 90.5% of patients in the no‐aspirin group and 90.9% of patients in the DAPT group with OAC and 6.1% of patients in the no‐aspirin group and 5.9% of patients in the DAPT group without OAC at 1 month. Adherence to the protocol‐specified antiplatelet therapy was 88.0% in the no‐aspirin group and 79.7% in the DAPT group with OAC and 88.1% in both groups without OAC at 1 month (Figure S2).

Clinical Outcomes

In patients with OAC, the coprimary bleeding end point occurred in 11 patients (4.45%) in the no‐aspirin group and in 12 patients (4.27%) in the DAPT group (HR, 1.04 [95% CI, 0.46–2.35]; P=0.93), whereas it occurred in 122 patients (4.47%) in the no‐aspirin group and in 128 patients (4.75%) in the DAPT group in patients without OAC (HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.73–1.20]; P=0.62) (Figure 2). Assuming death as a competing risk for major bleeding, the cumulative incidence of the primary bleeding end point was also not significantly different between the no‐aspirin and DAPT groups in patients with and without OAC (OAC: 4.44% versus 4.26%, HR 1.04 [95% CI, 0.46–2.34]; P=0.93, no‐OAC: 4.46% versus 4.74%, HR 0.94 [95% CI, 0.73–1.20]; P=0.62). There was no significant treatment‐by subgroup interaction in patients with and without OAC for the coprimary bleeding end point (P for interaction=0.82) (Figure 3).

In patients with OAC, the coprimary cardiovascular end point occurred in 12 patients (4.84%) in the no‐aspirin group and in 9 patients (3.20%) in the DAPT group (HR, 1.53 [95% CI, 0.64–3.62]; P=0.34), whereas it occurred in 111 patients (4.06%) in the no‐aspirin group and 101 patients (3.74%) in the DAPT group in patients without OAC (HR, 1.09 [95% CI, 0.83–1.42]; P=0.55) (Figure 2). There was no significant treatment‐by subgroup interaction in patients with and without OAC for the coprimary cardiovascular end point (P for interaction=0.46) (Figure 3). In patients with OAC, the incidences of myocardial infarction and any unplanned coronary revascularization were numerically, but not significantly, higher in the no‐aspirin group than in the DAPT group (3.23% versus 1.07%; HR, 3.04 [95% CI, 0.81–11.47]; P=0.10 and 1.63% versus 0.36%; HR, 4.56 [95% CI, 0.51–40.81]; P=0.17) (Table 2). The definite or probable stent thrombosis occurred in 1 patient (0.41%) in the no‐aspirin group and did not occur in the DAPT group in patients with OAC, whereas it occurred in 20 patients (0.74%) in the no‐aspirin group and in 13 patients (0.48%) in the DAPT group in those without OAC (Table 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

Among 530 patients with the use of OAC within 7 days before PCI, 64 patients had stopped OAC before PCI (Table 1). Regardless of the use of OAC at PCI, the effects of no aspirin compared with DAPT were not significant for the bleeding end point (OAC at PCI [N=466]: 4.68% and 3.19%, HR, 1.48 [95% CI, 0.58–3.74]; no OAC at PCI [N=5500]: 4.45% and 4.84%, HR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.72–1.17]; P for interaction=0.33), and for the cardiovascular end point (OAC at PCI: 5.14% and 2.78%, HR, 1.86 [95% CI, 0.72–4.81]; no OAC at PCI: 4.04% and 3.77%, HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.82–1.40]; P for interaction=0.26).

Among 5654 patients who were alive at discharge within 30 days after the index PCI, OAC was prescribed in 713 patients. Regardless of the use of OAC at discharge, the effects of no aspirin compared with DAPT were also not significant for the bleeding end point (OAC at discharge [N=713]: 3.12% and 3.89%, HR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.36–1.77]; no OAC at discharge [N=4941]: 2.90% and 3.46%, HR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.61–1.14]; P for interaction=0.94), and for the cardiovascular end point (OAC at discharge: 4.25% and 3.33%, HR, 1.28 [95% CI, 0.60–2.73]; no OAC at discharge: 1.49% and 1.55%, HR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.61–1.51]; P for interaction=0.52).

DISCUSSIONS

The main findings in the present subgroup analysis in the STOPDAPT‐3 were the following: (1) the no‐aspirin strategy compared with the DAPT strategy failed to reduce major bleeding within 1 month after PCI regardless of the use of OAC; and (2) there was a numerical excess risk of the no‐aspirin strategy relative to the DAPT strategy for cardiovascular events in patients with OAC.

Use of clopidogrel without aspirin on top of VKA was associated with a significant reduction in bleeding complications within 1 year after PCI as compared with VKA‐based triple therapy in the WOEST trial. 2 DOAC‐based dual therapy was also associated with a significant risk reduction in bleeding events as compared with VKA‐ and DOAC‐based triple therapy in several randomized trials. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In the present study, the risk reduction in severe bleeding was not confirmed in the no‐aspirin group as compared with the DAPT group in patients with OAC. The following reasons are considered for this discrepancy. The current study captured the bleeding events at acute phase such as cardiac tamponade due to cardiac rupture or coronary rupture and access‐site bleeding related to PCI and the hemodynamic support device, which had not been captured in the previous trials evaluating dual therapy as they mostly included stable patients after PCI or ACS. Removal of aspirin may not be as effective in reducing the risks for those types of bleeding regardless of the use of OAC. In this study, the rate of major bleeding did not differ between those with and without OAC, which was not consistent with a previous report. 14 The biggest reason for the absence of difference in the rate of major bleeding between those with and without OAC was that ACS had a much greater impact on major bleeding, particularly that which occurred in the acute phase, than OAC. The proportion of ACS in patients with OAC was much lower than in patients without OAC. The analyses stratified by ACS and non‐ACS demonstrated numerical excess of major bleeding in patients with OAC than in those without OAC. Moreover, the attending physicians might have concerns on bleeding complications in patients with OAC, in whom they might have modified the antithrombotic management in this study. Indeed, total heparin dose during PCI tended to be lower, and activated clotting time was significantly shorter in patients with OAC than in patients without OAC. The benefit of aspirin‐free strategy for bleeding events might have been attenuated by the lower incidence of bleeding event in patients treated with OAC. In addition, 1‐month follow‐up duration could have been too short to find the benefit of aspirin‐free strategy over DAPT strategy in bleeding events. Indeed, follow‐up durations of previous trials were longer than 6 months after PCI. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Furthermore, the risk reduction of bleeding events of DOAC‐based dual therapy relative to VKA‐based triple therapy emerged beyond 2 weeks after PCI in the ENTRUST‐PCI AF (Edoxaban Treatment Versus Vitamin K Antagonists in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Undergoing Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) trial. 7

Regarding the cardiovascular outcomes (the risk of a combined secondary end point of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, target‐vessel revascularization, and stent thrombosis), dual therapy compared with triple therapy was associated with a borderline higher risk of myocardial infarction (3.6% versus 3.0%; risk ratio [RR], 1.22 [95% CI, 0.99–1.52]; P=0.07), and a significantly higher risk of stent thrombosis (1.0% versus 0.6%; RR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.01–2.50]; P=0.04) in the meta‐analysis of DOAC‐based randomized clinical trials. 3 In the present study, the no‐aspirin group compared with the DAPT group was associated with a numerical higher risk of coprimary cardiovascular end point in patients with OAC. The risks of myocardial infarction and any unplanned coronary revascularization were numerically higher in the no‐aspirin group than in the DAPT group in patients with OAC. Indeed, the first month after PCI coincides with the highest risk period for the occurrence of thrombotic complications. 15 , 16 Considering the numerically higher rate of 1 month cardiovascular events in the no‐aspirin group than in the DAPT group with similar risk of bleeding event in patients with OAC in the current study, triple therapy up to 1 month could be a reasonable strategy, especially for those with high thrombotic risks such as ACS, which is the class IIa recommendation in the European Society of Cardiology 2023 guideline for ACS. 8 In the 2021 North American Consensus, triple therapy up to 1 month is also considered to be reasonable in patients at high thrombotic and low bleeding risks. 17 Further trials are needed to elucidate the optimal antithrombotic therapy within 1 month after PCI in patients treated with OAC.

Limitations

Some limitations to our study should be considered. First, open‐label trial design might have had an influence on the treatment after assignment. Indeed, the prevalence of proton pump inhibitors use at discharge was higher in the DAPT group than in the no‐aspirin group. Second, we used the reduced doses of prasugrel at loading/maintenance of 20/3.75 mg, which were approved only in Japan. 10 , 11 Standard doses of prasugrel would provide more protection from cardiovascular events and might alleviate the need for aspirin. However, we should await the results of ongoing trials to elucidate the effects of an aspirin‐free strategy using the global dose of prasugrel on bleeding and cardiovascular events after PCI in patients with OAC. Third, we did not have stringent recommendations on antithrombotic therapy before enrollment. Fourth, the prevalence of those treated with OAC was low and the present prespecified subgroup analysis was overtly underpowered. Fifth, this study might have selection and treatment bias. The attending physicians might have been reluctant to enroll patients with OAC and have changed their clinical practice after randomization in consideration of the bleeding complications in patients with OAC. Finally, we did not have data about prothrombin time–international normalized ratios in patients treated with VKA. We also did not have information about the duration of stay in the intensive care unit, which is one of the important risk factors for stress‐induced gastrointestinal bleeding.

CONCLUSIONS

The no‐aspirin strategy compared with the DAPT strategy failed to reduce major bleeding events irrespective of the use of OAC. There was a numerical excess risk of the no‐aspirin strategy relative to the DAPT strategy for cardiovascular events in patients with OAC.
