Cefiderocol and Moxifloxacin Have Additive and Synergistic Activity Against Pseudomonas aeruginosa Including an Isolate of the 2023 Keratitis Outbreak
Authors: Eric G. Romanowski, Brennan M. Schilling, Emily K. Young, Sonya M. Mumper, Michael E. Zegans, Robert M. Q. Shanks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe 2023 outbreak of Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis linked to contaminated artificial tears highlighted the need for new treatments targeting ocular infections caused by multi-drug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant bacteria. This study evaluated the in vitro efficacy and potential synergy of cefiderocol and moxifloxacin against P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates. The influence of quorum-sensing defects on antibiotic efficacy was also assessed because of the increase in lasR mutant keratitis isolates. Ceftazidime, which lacks the “Trojan Horse” iron-binding-mediated bacterial entry mechanism of cefiderocol, served as a comparator.MethodsTime-kill and epsilometer (E-test) assays were used to determine minimum inhibitory concentrations and drug interactions. Fractional inhibitory concentration indices were calculated to characterize drug interactions as antagonistic, indifferent, additive, or synergistic. Quorum-sensing defective strains were identified by a characteristic colony sheen phenotype associated with LasR system defects.ResultsTime-kill studies demonstrated consistent synergistic interactions between cefiderocol and moxifloxacin against seven out of seven tested P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates, including one from the 2023 outbreak. E-test–based assays showed additive or synergistic activity against 79% of isolates, although this dropped to 60% among suspected quorum-sensing defective strains. In contrast, the ceftazidime and moxifloxacin combination was less effective, particularly sheen-positive strains. The cefiderocol-moxifloxacin combination showed indifferent effects against Serratia marcescens and Staphylococcus aureus, with each antibiotic retaining individual activity.ConclusionsCefiderocol and moxifloxacin exhibited synergy against most tested P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates in vitro, including those with suspected quorum-sensing defects. These findings support further investigation of this combination for treating highly drug-resistant ocular infections.

Infectious keratitis is a leading cause of global blindness that contributes significantly to ocular morbidity. As of 2015, infectious keratitis was the cause of 36 million cases of blindness and is estimated to cause over 2 million cases of corneal ulceration annually. The burden is especially prominent in resource-limited settings, where access to appropriate medical therapy is often restricted. In the United States alone, infectious keratitis results in approximately 1 million healthcare visits each year.1

Patients typically present with symptoms such as eye pain, redness, photophobia, tearing, and blurred vision. Clinical examination often reveals epithelial defects, stromal edema, and anterior chamber inflammation.1 If misdiagnosed or inadequately treated, the condition can rapidly progress to corneal ulceration, perforation, endophthalmitis, and permanent vision loss.2 Among bacterial causes of infectious keratitis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is one of the most aggressive and vision-threatening pathogens. It is frequently associated with contact lens wear, ocular trauma, and postsurgical infection.3,4 Its virulence is attributable to its ability to adhere to damaged corneal epithelium, produce proteases and exotoxins, and form biofilms that reduce antimicrobial efficacy.3 Without prompt and effective treatment, rapid stromal necrosis and poor clinical outcomes occur. Alarmingly, there is a growing number of reports documenting treatment failures due to emerging antimicrobial resistance among keratitis isolates, leading to an increased reliance on combination or fortified antibiotic regimens.3,5,6 The 2023 keratitis outbreak linked to contaminated artificial tears was caused by an extensively drug-resistant (XDR) P. aeruginosa strain, resistant to all antibiotics typically used for keratitis. This strain, however, was susceptible to cefiderocol (FDC), a unique cephalosporin–siderophore antibiotic. FDC binds to iron and is actively transported across the outer membrane of many Gram-negative bacteria, increasing its potency against pathogens such as P. aeruginosa.7

An emerging factor in P. aeruginosa pathogenesis is the lasR gene, a key regulator of quorum sensing.8 Mutations in lasR result in a sheen-positive colony phenotype due to a build-up of 2-heptyl-4-quinolone.9 These sheen-positive isolates have altered virulence, increased antibiotic tolerance, and worse clinical outcomes in keratitis and pulmonary infections.10,11 Their ability to evade standard therapies makes them a critical target for improved antimicrobial approaches.

Combination antibiotic therapy is a well-established strategy to enhance efficacy, broaden antimicrobial coverage, and reduce the development of resistance. In ophthalmology, such combinations are often used empirically before pathogen identification.12 Due to these advantages, several research groups have focused on developing antibiotic combinations optimized for ocular infections.13–16

This study was motivated by two observations: First, the XDR strain responsible for the 2023 outbreak was susceptible to FDC, and, second, a recent report demonstrated synergy between FDC and the fluoroquinolone levofloxacin against P. aeruginosa isolates from an intensive care unit.16 Building on this, we evaluated the combination of FDC and moxifloxacin (MOX), a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone widely used for the treatment of ocular infections, against a panel of P. aeruginosa and other keratitis isolates. This included a strain from the 2023 outbreak and several sheen-positive isolates to assess the potential of this combination for treating antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa infections.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Isolates

P. aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, and Staphylococcus aureus keratitis isolates were deidentified excess clinical samples from the Charles T. Campbell Laboratory at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC). The outbreak strain, CDC1270,17 was obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Bacteria were streaked from −80°C stocks onto trypticase soy agar supplemented with sheep's blood at 5% (blood agar) and incubated at 37°C.

Antibiotics Source

Cefiderocol (Fetroja; Shionogi, Tokyo, Japan) was purchased from the inpatient pharmacy at UPMC Presbyterian Hospital. The FDC was reconstituted from a 1-gram vial according to the manufacturer’s instructions and prior studies.18,19 A 10-mg/mL FDC solution was prepared, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. For each time-kill assay, a fresh aliquot was thawed and diluted in iron-depleted, cation-adjusted, Mueller–Hinton broth (ID-CA-MHB) prepared according to methods from a previous study.20 Moxifloxacin HCl (PHR1542) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Antibiotic Susceptibility Tests

Strains used for time kill assays include the XDR CDC1270 strain that was reported by the CDC to be resistant to many antibiotics, based on Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, including amikacin (minimum inhibitory concentration [MIC] > 64 µg/mL), ceftazidime (CAZ; MIC > 128 µg/mL), ciprofloxacin (MIC > 8 µg/mL), imipenem (MIC > 16 µg/mL), and tobramycin (MIC > 16 µg/mL).21 Six other P. aeruginosa isolates used in time-kill assays were susceptible to tested aminoglycosides (gentamicin and tobramycin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin), and polymyxin B when assessed by the Charles T. Campbell Laboratory using the Kirby–Bauer method.

For time-kill assays, bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland (∼108 CFU/mL) in ID-CA-MHB.20 From these suspensions, 3-µL aliquots were added into 1.0 mL ID-CA-MHB with and without drug contained in 5-mL tubes, yielding a bacterial concentration of approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Tubes were vortexed for 30 seconds to ensure homogeneous distribution of the bacteria. The time clock for the sampling starts after the 30 seconds of vortexing.

Viable counts were performed immediately for the ID-CA-MHB control and at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours post-incubation. Tubes were incubated at 37°C between time points. Samples were serially diluted in Mueller–Hinton broth and plated on blood agar. After ∼20 hours of incubation at 32°C, colony counts were obtained, with a limit of detection of 100 CFU. Synergy was defined as at least a 2-log10 reduction in colony counts for the drug combination compared to the most active single agent and at least a 2-log10 decrease in colony counts below the initial inoculum.22 Prior to final time-kill assays, pilot assays were performed to optimize individual drug concentrations. In general, FDC was used at levels higher than MIC levels and MOX at levels lower than MIC levels. This may be due to differences between susceptibility between the different culture methods and the potential for iron-depleted media to increase the activity of FDC and fluoroquinolones.23 Table 1 provides the antibiotic concentrations used and the MIC values of the individual strains to FDC and MOX.

For MIC and E-test synergy testing, E-tests (Liofilchem, Abruzzi, Italy) were used according to the manufacturer's instructions using Mueller–Hinton II agar plates.22 Plates were incubated at 37°C for ∼24 hours, and MIC values were determined visually. Susceptibility and resistance to FDC and MOX were determined using CLSI systemic breakpoints (CLSI M100-ED35:2025 Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, 35th Edition). Susceptibility to FDC was designated as MIC ≤ 4 µg/mL. Susceptibility to MOX was designated as MIC ≤ 0.5 µg/mL.

Following MIC determination, combination antimicrobial testing between FDC and MOX was performed. To determine whether there was any interaction between the antibiotics, E-tests were placed onto bacterially inoculated plates at a 90° angle, intersecting at the previously determined MIC values.22 Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, and MICs were visually determined. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated as follows: FICI = (MICA+B/MICA) + (MICB+A/MICB). The FICIs were interpreted using the following criteria: ≤0.5, synergy; >0.5 to 1, additive; >1 to 4, indifference; and >4, antagonism.

Statistical Analysis

Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare medians, paired-Student's t-tests for paired data, and χ2 test with Yates’ correction for contingency analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results from the time-kill analysis indicated that the combination of FDC and MOX was highly superior to either individual antibiotic for a strain from the 2023 P. aeruginosa keratitis outbreak (Fig. 1) and for six arbitrarily chosen P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates (Fig. 2). E-Test Synergy Testing Confirmed Additive and Synergistic Effects

To increase throughput, E-test synergy testing was used following MIC determination (Table 2). The MIC90 values for 39 tested P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates were 0.25 µg/mL for FDC (susceptible) and 8 µg/mL for MOX (no CLSI breakpoints for MOX). Median MICs were 0.125 µg/mL and 1.0 µg/mL for FDC and MOX, respectively, when tested individually. In combination, median MICs for both drugs were reduced approximately 2.6-fold. FICI analysis showed that most isolates showed additive (>0.5 to ≤1) or synergistic (≤0.5) interactions (Table 2; Fig. 3). See Supplementary Table S1 for individual values.

Sheen-Positive P. aeruginosa Keratitis Isolates Were Less Responsive to MOX and the FDC–MOX Combination

A sheen-positive colony phenotype is indicative of a defective LasR quorum-sensing system and has been associated with worse clinical outcomes in P. aeruginosa keratitis (Fig. 3A).11 Sheen-positive strains represented around 20% of keratitis isolates in two regional sample sets.11,24 The lasR gene was previously sequenced for three of the isolates tested in this study; one had a frame-shift mutation, and the two others had insertion elements in the lasR open reading frame and were further verified as being loss-of-function alleles.24 Although FDC susceptibility was similar between sheen-positive and sheen-negative isolates, MOX MIC90 values differed markedly, with 1.5 µg/mL for sheen-negative versus 8 µg/mL for sheen-positive isolates (Table 2). The FDC–MOX combination was also more effective against sheen-negative isolates, with median FICI values of 0.501 for sheen-negative and 0.875 for sheen-positive isolates (P < 0.0001) (Table 2; Fig. 3B). All tested sheen-negative isolates had an additive or synergistic outcome, compared to only ∼40% of sheen-positive isolates (P = 0.0070) (Fig. 3C). Nearly half of the sheen-negative isolates had a synergistic response to FDC–MOX, but none of the sheen-positive isolates did (P = 0.0018) (Fig. 3D).

Comparison With CAZ–MOX Suggests a Role for FDC Siderophore Activity

CAZ–MOX was used to evaluate the contribution of the FDC siderophore-like properties. CAZ, which is structurally similar to FDC but lacks its iron-binding capability, was tested in combination with MOX using E-tests to evaluate nine arbitrarily chosen strains (four sheen-negative and five sheen-positive). The median MICs were 2 µg/mL for CAZ, 0.19 µg/mL for FDC, and 1 µg/mL for MOX. The median FICIs were 0.581 for FDC–MOX and 0.875 for CAZ–MOX for the nine strains, although the difference did not reach significance (P = 0.4485, Mann–Whitney). Stratification by sheen status revealed lower FICI values for FDC–MOX compared to CAZ–MOX in sheen-negative isolates (Fig. 4A), but no difference was observed for sheen-positive isolates (Table 3; Fig. 4B).

FDC–MOX Exhibited Limited Synergism for S. marcescens and S. aureus Keratitis Isolates

Because the FDC–MOX combination could presumably be used for empiric treatment of suspected multi-drug-resistant bacterial keratitis, non-P. aeruginosa isolates were included in the study. MIC values indicate susceptibility of all S. marcescens isolates but no S. aureus isolates to FDC (Table 2). All S. marcescens were susceptible to FDC (there are no CLSI breakpoints for S. marcescens and MOX). All S. aureus isolates had high MIC values for FDC (MIC90 = 32 µg/mL; there are no CLSI breakpoints for S. aureus to FDC), but all but one were susceptible to MOX. FICI values indicated indifferent interactions for both species (1.18 for S. marcescens, and 1.73 for S. aureus). No antagonism was observed, and, for three S. marcescens isolates, an additive effect was measured (Fig. 5; Table 2).

Discussion

The emergence of highly antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa ocular isolates, such as those implicated in the 2023 keratitis outbreak, underscores the urgent need for new antimicrobial strategies. Our findings demonstrate that the combination of FDC, a siderophore cephalosporin, and MOX, a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone commonly used for eye infections, exhibits potent in vitro synergy against P. aeruginosa isolates. MOX is favored in clinical practice due to its broad-spectrum activity, excellent corneal penetration, and favorable safety profile.25,26

Time-kill and E-test synergy assays consistently showed enhanced antibacterial activity of the FDC–MOX combination compared to either agent alone. Notably, this combination worked well against an isolate from the 2023 artificial tears–associated outbreak, despite it being resistant to multiple fluoroquinolone antibiotics, including MOX and ciprofloxacin.21 Although fluoroquinolone resistance among P. aeruginosa keratitis isolates is less than 5% at centers across the United States,27–30 some reports have documented resistance rates exceeding 50%.31 Combinations such as FDC–MOX may help counteract this trend by enhancing drug potency and extending the clinical utility of existing antibiotics.

Importantly, we observed significant phenotypic differences in drug responsiveness based on colony sheen, a surrogate marker for LasR deficiency and altered quorum sensing. Sheen-positive isolates, which have been linked to worse clinical outcomes, were significantly less responsive to MOX and the FDC–MOX combination. This contrasts with earlier findings where sheen-negative and sheen-positive isolates showed similar susceptibility to ciprofloxacin.24 This suggests a need for phenotype-guided strategies when managing infections caused by these variants.

Comparative analysis with CAZ, a structurally similar cephalosporin lacking the siderophore moiety of FDC, suggests that the FDC iron-binding mechanism may contribute to its enhanced activity with MOX, particularly in sheen-negative isolates. However, this effect was diminished in sheen-positive strains, potentially due to altered iron uptake in sheen-positive strains.32 The FDC–MOX combination showed limited benefit beyond individual drug activity against S. marcescens and S. aureus, suggesting that its utility may be most relevant for P. aeruginosa infections. Nevertheless, the absence of antagonism supports its potential role in empiric therapy prior to pathogen identification.

From a clinical standpoint, the opportunity to extend the activity of MOX by combining it with FDC has significant implications. In cases of MDR or XDR P. aeruginosa keratitis, ophthalmologists are left with limited options and often must resort to compounded/fortified antibiotics, systemic therapy, or surgical intervention.33 Cefiderocol may help address this critical therapeutic gap. However, further research is necessary to evaluate its pharmacokinetics, formulation stability, and ocular surface tolerability in vivo. Our findings show that FDC–MOX could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for culture-proven XDR infections and may have utility as an empiric treatment in high-risk clinical settings.
