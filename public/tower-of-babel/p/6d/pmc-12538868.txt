Associations of metabolic heterogeneity of obesity, cardiometabolic index with sleep trajectory among middle-aged and older adults: a longitudinal study
Authors: Yao Xiao, Xinhong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn alarming bidirectional relationship exists between prolonged sleep duration and obesity. However, obesity exhibits metabolic heterogeneity, and the cardiometabolic index (CMI) serves as a potential indicator of visceral fat distribution and function. The relationship of metabolic heterogeneity of obesity, CMI and sleep trajectory remain unclear.MethodsA total 6359 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study were included. Metabolic heterogeneity of obesity was evaluated based on four obesity and metabolic phenotypes as metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW), metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW), metabolically healthy overweight/obesity (MHOO), and metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity (MUOO). CMI was calculated using the established formula: (TG/HDL-C) × WHtR, where WHtR indicates waist-to-height ratio. Sleep duration trajectories from 2011 to 2020 were identified using group-based trajectory modeling.ResultsFour trajectories were identified in the total sleep duration: the decreasing (8.5%), the short stable (36.8%), the normal stable (49.1%) and the increasing (5.6%). In the increasing sleep group, individuals with MUOO (OR = 1.594, 95%CI: 1.163 ~ 2.161, P = 0.003) or higher CMI (OR = 1.495, 95%CI: 1.159 ~ 1.930, P = 0.002) exhibited a significantly higher risk of increasing sleep duration trajectory over time in the comparison with normal stable sleep group. Compared to normal stable sleep trajectory, MUOO in the both decreasing and short sleep group demonstrated attenuated progression toward shortened sleep duration over time.ConclusionHigh CMI level and metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity promoted the progression toward longer sleep duration. Our finding highlighted that greater attention be given to metabolism status and visceral fat for improving sleep quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24842-2.Keywords: Metabolism syndrome, Obesity, Cardiometabolic index, Sleep, Nap

Introduction

Worsening sleep quality is a widespread and significant public health problem. Evidence has indicated that the pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality was 19.0% (95% CI 15.8–22.8%; range 6.6–43.6%) in the general population [1]. Short and long sleep duration are strongly associated with a range of adverse health outcomes, including mortality and cardiovascular disease [2–4]. In addition, Sleep patterns typically undergo age-related changes. For example, sleep duration decreases, the prevalence of nighttime awakenings increases, while sleep onset and offset advance to become earlier with age [5]. Therefore, the pooled prevalence of sleep disturbances was up to 35.9% (95% CI: 30.6%–41.2%) among the Chinese older people [6]. The decreasing low-stable trajectory had increased cancer risk and cancer-specific mortality [7, 8]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify risk factors for sleep disorder to generate better prevention strategies.

Obesity and sleep disturbances have emerged as prevalent comorbidities in contemporary populations, with longitudinal evidence confirming their bidirectional pathophysiological interplay [9]. Growing evidence from both laboratory and epidemiological studies pointed to short sleep duration and poor sleep quality as risk factors for the development of obesity [10]. On the other hand, obesity contributes to the pathogenesis of numerous sleep disorders, including obstructive sleep apnea and insomnia [11]. In postmenopausal women, elevated body mass index (BMI) and abdominal obesity contribute to sleep disturbances, reducing deep sleep and impairing sleep efficiency [12]. Besides, sleep disorders and obesity demonstrate bidirectional associations, with sleep duration emerging as a key metabolic syndrome (MetS) correlate. Compared with those sleeping 7 to 8 h per night, the odds for having the MetS increased by more than 45% in both short and long sleepers [13]. In the meta-analysis included 13 studies involving 300,202 patients, short sleep and long sleep significantly increased the risk of MetS 15% and 19%, respectively [14]. These investigations, however, overlooked the metabolic heterogeneity of obesity. Epidemiological evidence has reported one-third of overweight and obese individuals were metabolically healthy [15]. Metabolic heterogeneity of obesity were classified as metabolically healthy overweight/obesity (MHOO), metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity (MUOO), metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW) and metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW) [16]. Whether the different obesity and metabolic phenotypes have different effects on sleep trajectory is still unclear.

The cardiometabolic index (CMI) reflects visceral fat tissue distribution and function, assessing the risk of obesity-related conditions. The CMI quantifies adiposity using two key components: the triglyceride (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio and the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). Evidence suggests that the CMI provides more clinically practical cutoff values for identifying obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and OSA with comorbid metabolic syndrome (MetS) compared to the lipid accumulation product [17]. Furthermore, the CMI has emerged as a novel biomarker integrating adiposity measures and blood lipid profiles, demonstrating significant discriminative capacity for both type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [18–20]. Short sleep and long sleep consistently and significantly predict the risk of the development of T2DM [2, 3, 21]. Sleep disturbances were highly prevalent in T2DM and CVD populations [22, 23]. However, little attention is paid on the association between CMI and sleep duration.

This study utilizes data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of the middle-aged and elderly Chinese population. We aim to identify sleep duration trajectories from 2011 ~ 2020, and investigate the associations of metabolic heterogeneity of obesity, CMI and sleep trajectories among Chinese middle-aged and older adults.

Method

Study population

The CHARLS is an ongoing nationally representative cohort study that began in 2011 and includes adults aged ≥ 45 years from 450 villages or resident communities within 28 provinces across China, with follow-up visits scheduled every 2 years [24]. To date, 4 follow-ups survey have been conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2020. In the present study, we retrospectively analyzed data from the CHARLS 2011 to 2020. The exclusion criteria for the present study were as follows: (1) individuals aged less than 45 years old; (2) missing data on sleep in 2011, and less than two reassessments of nighttime sleep and nap from 2011 to 2020; 4) lack of BMI and waist circumference (WC) information, and BMI<18.5; 5) lack of MetS information. As described in the Figs. 1 and 6359 adults were included in the final analysis.

Flow diagram for participants included in the study

Definitions of obesity and metabolic status

Obesity status was assessed by BMI based on China criteria [16]. Participants were categorized into two groups as normal weight (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0), overweight and obesity (BMI ≥ 24.0). Metabolic status was assessed based on four metabolic components [25]. Having 2 or more metabolic abnormalities of following four criteria were defined as metabolically unhealthy: (1) hypertension, systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mm Hg, or antihypertensive medication use. (2) hyperglycemia, fasting glucose level ≥ 100 mg dL−1 or glycated haemoglobin ≥ 6.0% or doctor’s diagnosed diabetes or antidiabetic medication use. (3) high TG: TG ≥ 150 mg dL−1 or lipid-lowering drugs use, (4) HDL-C, HDL-C < 40 mg dL−1 in men and HDL-C < 50 mg dL-1 in women or lipid-lowering drugs use. Combined with obesity and metabolic status, participants were divided into four BMI-metabolic phenotypes as MHNW, MUNW, MHOO, and MUOO to evaluate the metabolic heterogeneity of obesity. The metabolic indicators and anthropometric measures were collected in the wave 2011.

Calculation of CMI

CMI was calculated as (TG/HDL-C) × WHtR according to the published formula [18]. WHtR was calculated by WC by height. TG/HDL-C ratio was calculated by dividing TG by HDL-C. Due to lacking uniform classification criteria, we divided the CMI into quartiles, first selecting the cut-off point at the 75% level based on the distribution of CMI in the study population and defining those above 75% as “high CMI”. The metabolic indicators and anthropometric measures were collected in the baseline.

Assessment of sleep

The primary outcome measure was total sleep duration, comprising both nighttime sleep and daytime napping periods. Nighttime sleep duration was assessed by asking: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)? (This may be shorter than the number of hours you spend in bed).” Nap was assessed by asking: “During the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch?” Total sleep duration was derived by summing nighttime sleep duration and daytime nap duration.

Covariates

At baseline, the covariates selected were factors known to be associated with the risk of poor sleep quality. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex (male/female), education (illiterate, primary school or below, junior high school or above), marital status (married and others), and residence (rural, urban). Health-related factors included smoking (yes or no), drinking status (never, drink but less than once a month, and drink more than once a month), active social engagement (yes or no), depression symptoms, self-reported physician-diagnosed co-morbidities (chronic lung diseases, heart disease, stroke, stomach or other digestive disease, emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, arthritis or rheumatism) and medication for co-morbidities(chronic lung diseases, heart disease, stroke, stomach or other digestive disease, emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, arthritis or rheumatism).

The ten-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) was used to evaluate depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms were considered clinically significant at the standard cutoff score of ≥ 10 points.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Differences between metabolic heterogeneity of obesity were using the Chi-squared test or ANOVA tests.

The Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify similar developmental trajectories of total sleep duration and nighttime sleep duration in the all participants. A base model without covariates was constructed to determine the number of groups and the order of the polynomial functions of the survey wave. The best-fitting model was considered base on bayesian information criterion (BIC), relative entropy, average posterior probability (AvePP) and proportions per group. The BIC closer to 0, entropy values close to 1 and AvePP >0.7 indicate better model fit, and a minimum membership of 5% is required in each trajectory group [26]. Each participant was assigned to the corresponding trajectory group according to the maximum likelihood estimation to estimate the probability of producing variance in sleep duration.

Multivariate logistic models were used to assess the association of metabolic heterogeneity of obesity, CMI and trajectory group memberships after controlling for various confounding factors, including socio-demographic status and health-related factors. Model 1 was a crude model. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was adjusted for the variables in model 2 and additionally added residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, active social engagement, depression symptoms, co-morbidities and medication for co-morbidities. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs are reported for all regression models.

Associations between metabolic heterogeneity of obesity and sleep trajectory

Compared to normal stable sleep group, individuals with MUOO (OR = 1.594, 95%CI: 1.163 ~ 2.161, P = 0.003) or MetS alone (OR = 1.813, 95%CI: 1.417 ~ 2.319, P < 0.001) in the increasing sleep group were more likely to increase sleep duration trajectory over time (Table 3 and Table S1). In the decreasing sleep group, individuals with MUOO (OR = 0.721, 95%CI: 0.555 ~ 0.936, P = 0.014) exhibited a significantly lower risk of decreasing sleep duration trajectory over time in the comparison with normal stable sleep group. Regarding nighttime sleep trajectories, participants in the decreasing sleep group with MUOO (adjusted OR = 0.764, 95% CI: 0.613–0.951, P = 0.016) or MetS alone (adjusted OR = 0.819, 95% CI: 0.689–0.972, P = 0.023) showed significant inverse associations with short sleep duration trajectories compared to the normal stable sleep trajectory group. (Table 4 and Table S2). Furthermore, with regard to both the total sleep duration trajectory and the nighttime sleep trajectory, subjects with overweight/obesity exhibited significantly reduced odds of developing decreasing sleep duration trajectories compared to normal stable sleep trajectory (Table S3 ~ S4).

Discussion

In the present study, four total sleep trajectories and three different trajectories of nighttime sleep were identified. Subjects with MUOO or elevated CMI were more likely to be increasing sleep duration trajectory during follow up in the increasing sleep trajectory cohort. Nevertheless, within the decreasing sleep trajectory cohort, participants with MUOO demonstrated attenuated progression of sleep duration decline over time. Regarding nighttime sleep trajectories, elevated CMI in the both decreasing sleep trajectory and short stable sleep trajectory showed a significant negative association with short sleep duration patterns over time.

The recommended sleep duration for adults is 7 ~ 8 h per night. A large-scale cohort study encompassing over 320,000 adult participants revealed a significant protective association: all-cause mortality reached its nadir at precisely 7 h of daily sleep duration, with comparable risk reductions observed for cardiovascular mortality and other cause-specific mortality endpoints [4]. Furthermore, analyses from the CHARLS cohort revealed that participants with daily sleep duration < 7 h exhibited a 69% higher risk of cancer incidence compared to those maintaining 7–8 h of sleep [7]. Given these findings, the 7–8 h sleep duration category was designated as the reference group in our analyses.

In this study, individuals with both overweight/obesity and metabolic unhealthy had a higher likelihood of developing increasing sleep duration trajectories. However, overweight/obesity alone showed no significant association with sleep patterns in the increasing trajectory group. This finding suggests, as the authors concluded, that MetS itself may lead to excessive sleep duration. Previous cross-sectional study has demonstrated a U-shaped association between sleep duration and MetS [27]. Both short and long sleep durations showed significant associations with higher MetS prevalence in cross-sectional analyses [27]. The systematic review, which included 11 studies with a total of 343,669 participants, revealed that the prevalence of MetS was 13% (95% CI: 8%–18%) among short sleepers and 15% (95% CI: 9%–24%) among long sleepers [28]. Among individuals with MetS, 53% reported excessive daytime sleepiness and a minority (19%) reported long sleep (≥ 9 h) [29]. Patients with diagnosed MetS demonstrated significantly higher prevalence of sleep disorders compared to non-MetS participants [30]. Additionally, our findings revealed that MUOO had a lower likelihood of developing decreasing sleep duration trajectories. However, MetS alone demonstrated no statistically significant association with sleep pattern alterations in the decreasing sleep trajectory group. Obesity constitutes a well-established risk factor for multiple sleep disorders. In postmenopausal women, high BMI and abdominal obesity increased the risk of sleep disturbances, including decreasing deep sleep and sleep efficiency [12]. Cross-sectional studies have reported a higher BMI and prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) among adults reporting habitually excessive sleep duration (typically defined as ≥9 h) [31]. Pro-somnolence cytokines, particularly tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6 were shown to have sleep-inducing effects [32]. Accumulating evidence indicates that individuals with obesity and MetS exhibit elevated circulating levels of these somnogenic cytokines [32–34], suggesting a compensatory neuroinflammatory mechanism. It should also be noted that circadian disruption may be act as underlying mechanism for obesity and MetS [35, 36], which further affects sleep quality.

In the present study, the relationship between CMI and short/decreasing sleep duration trajectories during follow up was more profound in the nighttime sleep trajectories rather than total sleep duration trajectories. Longitudinally, participants with higher baseline CMI in the increasing sleep pattern group demonstrated greater progression toward prolonged sleep duration over follow-up. Previous study showed that CMI had more practical cutoff values for identifying obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) than lipid accumulation product [17]. The other study indicated that for every unit increase in CMI, there is a corresponding 63% increase in the likelihood of participants experiencing trouble sleeping and a 96% increase in the likelihood of developing sleep disorders [37]. Furthermore, a dose-response relationship was examined between waist circumference and the risk of sleep disorder [38]. Compared to recommended sleepers (7 ~ 9 h), very short sleepers and long sleepers had significantly greater BMIs, while long sleepers had significantly larger waist circumference [39]. Noteworthy, the individuals with metabolically unhealthy phenotypes have higher levels of CMI than the individuals with metabolic healthy phenotypes [40]. To some extent, CMI can reflect the level of metabolic abnormalities in the body. Therefore, similar results in the relationship between MUOO/elevated CMI and prolonged sleep duration over follow-up in the increasing sleep group were observed. Obesity and MetS increase the risk of several comorbidities, including depression and inflammation [41], which have been shown to be associated with long sleep duration [31]. Altogether it could be speculated that long sleep duration may partially be driven by obesity and MetS and their co-morbidities, rather than that its occurrence would precede these conditions [31].

There are several limitations in the current study. First, the exclusion of participants with missing data on nap, nighttime sleep and lost to follow-up would have selection bias and resulted in limited sample size for outcome. Second, this study used observational data, which may have biased the observed relationships by introducing confounding factors. To reduce such bias, we considered as many related factors as possible in the analysis. Third, although we had adjusted for multidimensional covariates, other residual factors or unmeasured variables might remain, such as time eaten and genetic susceptibility. These important questions should be taken into account in subsequent studies. Fourth, a key limitation is the exclusive focus on baseline obesity, MetS and CMI measures. Subsequent studies should examine how changes in obesity, MetS components, and CMI influence longitudinal sleep patterns. Finally, sleep variables were measured by subjective self-report, not by objective actigraphy measurement. Previous research has demonstrated actigraphy-assessed sleep was always shortened than self-reported sleep [42].

Conclusion

In conclusion, prospective analyses revealed that middle-aged and older Chinese adults with metabolically unhealthy obesity or elevated cardiometabolic index had significantly higher probabilities of developing increasing sleep duration trajectories during 10-year follow-up. Our findings provided the new evidence for an association of MetS and obesity with sleep disturbance, and highlighted the importance of assessing metabolic heterogeneity of obesity and CMI index in community to improve sleep quality for middle-aged and older adults in the context of China’s population aging rapidly.
