Nongovernmental Organizations in Oral Health Promotion: A Scoping Review and Thematic Analysis
Authors: Shiqi Peng, Reinhard Chun Wang Chau, Cheryl Hiu Kwan Chui, Edward Chin Man Lo, Chun Hung Chu, Chloe Meng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsThe study aimed to explore nongovernmental organizations’ (NGOs’) roles in promoting oral health for different targeted beneficiaries, and to study NGO’s evaluation methods to assess the impact and performance of their projects.MethodsThis study was a scoping review with thematic analysis. A literature search was conducted in the databases Medline, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. Publications reporting on NGO-related oral health promotions and activities were included in this review. A thematic framework was used to synthesize the literature according to NGOs’ roles, targeted beneficiaries, and evaluation measures.ResultsA total of 1363 references were identified by literature search, and 23 articles were included in this review. It was found that NGOs served various targeted beneficiaries, especially disadvantaged groups, such as children, homeless people, and older adults in developing countries and lower-income societies. NGOs functioned in various roles, ‘professional doctors’, ‘service providers’, ‘public educators’, ‘proactive volunteers’, ‘industry trainers’, ‘active advocates’, ‘field researchers’, and ‘potential funder’, to provide both direct and indirect services. Project evaluations were conducted regarding programme-oriented, beneficiary-oriented, and organization-oriented evaluations with a mixed-method design.ConclusionNGOs play an active role in promoting and improving people’s oral health and well-being by providing various services and activities. The multifaceted involvement of NGOs highlights their importance in improving oral health outcomes and addressing oral health disparities in broad community settings.Clinical relevanceThis review shows a landscape view of the various roles of NGOs in oral health promotion. It is expected to strengthen the power of NGOs to implement a wide range of activities by collaborating across sectors and engaging various stakeholders. This integrated approach will foster a holistic and impactful strategy for promoting oral health, ultimately leading to improved oral health outcomes and overall well-being.

Introduction

Nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) are widely recognized as self-governing, private, not-for-profit organizations geared to improving the quality of life for disadvantaged people in the context of development and social change.1 Notably, there is a high diversity among types of NGOs worldwide, for example, nonprofit organizations, volunteer organizations, community-based organizations, and foundations.2,3 Despite this, different NGOs share some consistent features4: relying on goodwill, mission first, and without simple and clear lines of ownership. NGOs have four instrumental and expressive functions: service delivery, social entrepreneurship, political and civic engagement, and values and faith expression. NGOs have been proven to be a significant force for public health promotion on national, regional, and international levels by empowering the community and providing direct and indirect social services to the targeted populations.5,6

In the past 20 years, NGOs have made strides in improving global oral health, especially in disadvantaged communities, with the best intentions and high motivation.7,8 According to the World Health Organization, 3.5 billion people suffer from oral diseases, leading to increasing risks to overall public health and burden of healthcare systems.9 To achieve the mission of poverty alleviation or welfare delivery, NGOs take different actions to promote global oral health. For example, Dentaid The Dental Charity, founded in 1996, has been delivering dental treatment and sending volunteers to impoverished and rural communities in more than 70 countries, including the United Kingdom (UK), Uganda, and Cambodia. Academy of Dentistry International Foundation, which has provided financial support to missions and projects to offer dental care to disadvantaged communities in Honduras, Columbia, Kenya, Jamaica, Philippines, and Belize since 2010. The FDI World Dental Federation is an international NGO representing the dental profession in oral health by developing health policies and education programmes and advocating for dentistry internationally. These examples show NGOs’ different roles and strategies in promoting oral health.

While NGOs’ practices in promoting oral health are extensive, dynamic, and diverse, there is a noticeable lack of academic records or reviews on this global and domestic topic.10,11 Understanding the comprehensive roles and strategies of NGOs is crucial for dental researchers, as it offers three key implications. First, mapping the types of services, targeted beneficiaries, and evaluation methods used by NGOs can inform the development of community-based interventions, aligning academic research with real-world practices. Second, uncovering the interconnectedness between oral health and civil society sectors can inspire interdisciplinary research, fostering innovative approaches to address complex public health challenges. Third, identifying successful NGO models and cross-sector collaborations can guide policy recommendations and the design of scalable, sustainable programmes. By consolidating existing information, this study aims to provide a foundation for future research and practical initiatives, bridging the gap between academia and grassroots oral health promotion efforts.

Methods

The present study was designed as a scoping review,12 and the findings were reported according to the guideline PRISMA-ScR (Appendix).13 A thematic analysis was employed to identify, analyse, and report patterns and themes that arise through the review process.14,15 A scoping review of the current literature base is considered the most appropriate tool to accomplish the aim by asking two research questions, (1) What are the services and roles of NGOs in oral health promotion? (2) How have these roles and services been evaluated?

Searching strategy

A literature search was conducted in Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, MEDLINE, and Embase from January 2000 to March 2023. Articles reporting on NGOs’ activities in oral health promotion were included in this review. NGOs, and oral health/dental health/oral care/dental care were selected as the searching keywords. Eligibility criteria

Peer-reviewed articles were included if they (1) explicitly reported the roles, services, or strategies of NGOs in promoting oral health among populations; and/or (2) described or evaluated the implementation and outcomes of NGOs’ oral health programmes, including both internal and external evaluations. The exclusion criteria were set as following. Articles were excluded if they (1) were not written in English; (2) were not relevant to the research questions (ie, not focused on NGOs or oral health promotion); (3) did not identify NGOs as a key role in programme delivery, design, or evaluation; and (4) were other nonpeer-reviewed documents such as working papers and unpublished reports.

Screening and data selection

References identified from the databases were uploaded to a standard production platform, Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), for further analysis. Duplicates were identified and removed. Two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the identified references independently. Full-text reading of the potentially eligible articles was carried out independently by the same two reviewers. Any doubts on the inclusion of a paper were discussed and agreed upon by both reviewers. A cross-reference check of these documents among the authors was further conducted to identify additional relevant materials.

Data extraction and analysis

The following data was extracted from the included articles: author, year, study location, study design, NGOs and project names (if any), targeted beneficiaries, specific services provided, and NGOs’ evaluation. In addition, detailed information was extracted regarding the evaluation of NGO-led oral health promotion efforts, including (1) whether any form of evaluation was conducted, (2) evaluation content, (3) evaluation framework and tools, and (4) evaluation results.

The extracted textual data were analysed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s14 six-phase approach: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the final report. An inductive, iterative approach was adopted, allowing themes to emerge from the data rather than being imposed by pre-existing frameworks. Two reviewers independently coded the data, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus.

The analysis was grounded in the explicit content reported in the primary studies. No interpretations were introduced beyond the original texts; an interpretive synthesis of study descriptions was conducted. For example, when several studies described NGOs delivering direct clinical services, we inductively grouped these under the theme of ‘service providers’. This process allowed us to develop a structured typology of NGO roles that reflected recurring patterns across the literature while remaining faithful to the data. All coding decisions and theme definitions were documented in shared files to support transparency, reflexivity, and reproducibility.

As the present study followed a scoping review methodology, no formal critical appraisal of individual sources was conducted, in accordance with the aim of mapping existing evidence rather than assessing study quality.

Results

Figure 1 shows the literature selection process. A total of 1363 items were identified, and 23 articles were finally included in this review (Table 2). Among the 23 articles, 7 were from a global perspective and focused on international NGOs and their projects; others were set in different countries and regions, including both developed and developing countries, such as the United States (US), the UK, Singapore, Malaysia, Pakistan, South Africa, and India. Two articles were published in the time period of 2000 to 2010, 15 articles were published in 2011 to 2020, and 6 articles were published in the past 3 years. A rising tendency in the research regarding NGOs’ efforts in promoting oral health was obvious. Among the included studies, 11 articles reported NGOs’ evaluation of their programmes, 4 out of 11 employed the theory-driven method. The study designs of the included studies were different, including surveys, interviews, case studies, quasi-experiments, and reviews. Theme 1: Targeted beneficiaries

NGOs provided services to diverse beneficiaries, regardless of NGO type, international NGOs, aid organizations, social service agencies, welfare organizations, religious groups, community organizations, or health centres.16 On a global scale, the targeted populations were disadvantaged groups, such as children and older adults in developing countries and lower-income societies.17, 18, 19 Besides, dental professionals, dental hygienists, students, volunteers, and potential leaders who were dedicated to improving global oral health were also the targeted beneficiaries.7,20,21 On a country/regional scale, the targeted populations were diverse. In developed countries, such as the US, the Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), as the outpatient clinics and important safety net providers in rural areas, provided oral health services to patients and professionals in underserved areas.22,23 In the UK and Canada, NGOs also offered oral health services to homeless people or undocumented migrants.8,24,25 In Singapore, persons with special care needs in disability activity centres were the targeted population.26 In developing countries such as Malaysia and India, children with special needs and the professional dental workforce were important targeted beneficiaries of NGOs’ oral health services.27,28 In many projects, the target beneficiaries could be multiple; for example, the Oral Care Program in the Netherlands covered older people, home care nurses, and dental hygienists.29,30

Theme 2: Roles of NGOs

The following classification of NGO roles was developed inductively through thematic analysis of the included studies rather than adopting a predefined framework. These categories emerged from recurring patterns in how NGOs’ activities were described across the literature.

Direct services

NGOs provided direct services to targeted beneficiaries, such as clinical dental screening and treatments (eg, tooth extractions for pain relief, placement of restorations, scaling, and treatment of cleft palate), clinical dental health care, oral health education and promotion, and integrated interventions with other social service programmes.16 Their roles were, therefore, ‘professional doctors’,17, 18, 19 ‘service providers’,20 ‘public educators’,16,21, 22, 23 and ‘proactive volunteers’.7,22,24

Professional doctors

NGOs directly recruited professional dentists to provide curative treatments and preventive care, leveraging their commitment as ‘professional doctors’. Around 40 NGOs in Hong Kong, such as Loving Smiles Special Care Dental Centre and Haven of Hope S K Yee Hang Hau Clinic, provided dental services in elderly centres and nursing homes, especially serving people in lower socioeconomic or disadvantaged groups. These NGOs set up outreach dental teams, purchased equipment and consumables, and arranged transportation of the dental equipment to the service site.19 In addition, the FQHCs, a community-based organization, provided comprehensive primary care and preventive care in the US. These centres provided on-site preventive and emergency dental services and made appropriate referrals. FQHCs were crucial in overcoming accessibility barriers nationwide for vulnerable and underserved patients by enhancing affordability and providing additional services such as transportation, translation, and case management.18

Service providers

As ‘service providers’, NGOs tended to provide integrated services including oral health care combined with other social services, to disadvantaged populations, especially in some nonprofit clinics and social service agencies. The nonprofit dental clinics in British Columbia, Canada provided oral health care to homeless and low-income people by integrating dental care services with community social services, such as mental health care, substance use programmes, shelter and housing services, social exclusion, and trauma treatment for patients with an urgent and wide range of needs.20 These nonprofit dental clinics operated cost-effectively, demonstrating that similar services could be extended more widely to un(der)served populations. Another example was Jevaia Oral Health Care, a nonprofit social service agency in Nepal that successfully established primary care dental services within the government healthcare system for approximately 50,000 individuals. Jevaia Oral Health Care adopted the structural social work theory to provide community-based primary care dental services and tackle the structural problems around the etiological aspects of oral disease.25

Public educators

NGOs empowered local communities and promoted oral health and well-being as ‘public educators’, especially in some global dental programmes and international NGOs, such as the FDI World Dental Federation.21,22 One local example in South Africa, ‘Phelophepa Health Train’, was a project to provide experiential learning opportunities for dental therapy students through the community-driven health initiatives of NGOs and the private sector in KwaZulu-Natal Province.16 NGOs also served as partner organizations, collaborating with higher education institutions to cocreate the educative tools. These tools apply the concept of ‘critical consciousness’ to codesign, implement, and evaluate a series of oral health and health workshops to strengthen social engagement and construct new health knowledge with and for young homeless people in the UK.23

Proactive volunteers

NGOs functioned as ‘proactive volunteers’ by actively recruiting and organizing volunteer activities. The American Dental Association had a well-developed international volunteer website with information on these projects and a list of sponsoring organizations.24 NGOs also proposed a framework for their volunteer initiatives, such as the Basic Package of Oral Care (BPOC), which the World Health Organization Collaborating Center in Nijmegen developed and encompassed key elements such as oral urgent treatment, affordable fluoride toothpaste, and atraumatic restorative treatment. These components provided effective, affordable, and sustainable activities to enhance oral health at the community and population level.7 However, it was worth noting that NGOs primarily focused on short-term clinical treatment without sufficient follow-up or long-term benefits.22 The drawbacks of this traditional approach to dental volunteering have been examined, along with potential changes that could lead to a more impactful and effective outcome.26

Indirect services

NGOs were also crucial in providing indirect services that benefited targeted populations by empowering dental workforces and facilitating development of dentistry. They fulfilled various roles such as ‘industrial trainers’,27,28 ‘active advocates’,7,29 ‘field researchers’,30, 31, 32 and ‘potential funders’.24,33,34 Through these diverse roles, NGOs contributed to advancing dental healthcare and the well-being of the communities they served.

Industrial trainers

As ‘industrial trainers’, many NGOs offered training programmes and workshops for dentists. These initiatives aimed to enhance dentists’ skills and knowledge, ensuring the delivery of high-quality dental care services. The Global Child Dental Fund launched the Senior Dental Leaders programme, which adopted the principles of collaborative leadership as the cornerstone in establishing a dental leadership network to combat poor child oral health worldwide.27 Oral Health America also trained administrators, medical staff, and caregivers and equipped them with the necessary oral health competencies to provide daily oral health care for residents of assisted living communities in Oregon.28 In those global volunteer programmes, NGOs served as trainers to empower the local health workers who can continue care after the departure of the foreign volunteers.26

Active advocates

NGOs functioned as ‘active advocates’, especially the professional federations and associations, raising awareness about dental health and advocating for local, regional, and national policies and programmes that promoted oral health. In Malaysia, the Malaysia Dental Association and Malaysia Private Dental Practitioner Association, as NGOs, were the key stakeholders and shared views on the drivers for change for the Malaysian dental workforce and their potential implications. Drivers for the workforce were identified across four main domains: policy-politics, trends in demography, social and economic, and technology-scientific development. In this way, the key dental workforce in Malaysia was identified and advocated higher awareness of the significance of oral health.29

Field researcher

NGOs were also engaged in research activities. Their projects served as a research field for conducting studies and gathering data to understand oral health challenges, so as to develop evidence-based solutions.8,30 In the Noor Project and SOS Village in Pakistan, researchers investigated the prevalence of dental caries and its association with oral health literacy among underprivileged children of NGOs.32 Some NGOs also conducted evidence-based evaluations to improve their oral health services. A study was conducted to educate and motivate visually impaired children to maintain good oral health using specially designed methods that were easy to understand.31

Potential funders

As ‘potential funders’, NGOs like the American Dental Association (ADA) contributed significantly by providing financial support for various dental projects. The ADA had developed an extensive international volunteer website that served as a valuable resource, offering information on these projects and a list of sponsoring organizations.24 In a specific research project, the NGO ‘De Friesland’ granted financial support to the programme ‘Sûne Mûle’ for 3 years. This funding aimed to evaluate the experiences of key stakeholders, including older people, home-care nurses, and dental hygienists, to assess the impact of an oral care programme.33,34 In Hong Kong, The Jockey Club Charity Trust stood out as one of the largest NGOs and strongest funders supporting initiatives that promote oral health and services within the city. It provided financial support for dental health-related projects, including infrastructure development, equipment procurement, and community-based initiatives.

Evaluation of NGO oral health services/programmes (n = 11, in alphabetical order of the first authors’ last name).

Majority of the evaluations were conducted by researchers, but ambiguous information was found on the role and nature of the researcher/reporter. It was unclear whether the ‘researcher’ was from the NGO, a collaborator with NGO, or worked as an independent party.

Regarding evaluation content, three main types were illustrated: project-oriented, beneficiary-oriented, and organization-oriented evaluations. Project-oriented evaluations assessed the sustainability of dental outreach programmes/initiatives in developing nations,22 and the effectiveness of a specific oral health collaborative promotion programme such as ‘Live.Learn.Laugh’21,35 and oral care programmes.33,34 Beneficiary-oriented evaluations specifically examined the utilization, unmet need, knowledge, and attitudes related to oral health care among a particular group of targeted beneficiaries,18,28 such as health centre patients in low-income communities and caregivers in long-term care facilities. Organization-oriented evaluations treated dental NGOs as independent subjects by evaluating diversity, activities, and limitations in delivering oral health services in a particular country or globally.25,36

In terms of evaluation tools, it was found that the diversity of evaluation theories and techniques, such as the Theory of Change Framework,22 Structural Social Work,25 critical consciousness,23 and a portfolio approach.35 Quality assurance with need assessment, standards for staff, and goal setting were the common measures in both developed and developing countries.36 Still, other more comprehensive tools such as monitoring and evaluation, goal setting, and a complete planning cycle were only applied by a few organizations. In specific projects, such as the oral health pedagogical workshops and oral care programmes, quasi-experiments, including the pre- and postintervention interviews and surveys, were the techniques employed to collect first-hand data and improve the programme and service design.23,31,33,34 The selection of evaluation tools varied, which more likely depended on who conducted the evaluation and what was expected to be evaluated.

As for the evaluation results, evidence indicated that NGOs’ services and programmes in promoting oral health yielded positive outcomes, such as the satisfaction of access to oral health care among patients,18 the reduced inequities in oral health care for older adults,28 the strengthened social engagement and knowledge of homeless young people,23 and the effective methods in educating and motivating visually impaired children.31 However, there was still a lack of quality assurance with limited performance evaluation due to limited funding and human resources.22 The implementation of interventions was not supported by high-quality evidence or proven effective in addressing local needs.21 Besides, the quality of internal evaluation varied considerably, even for the same project but in different countries.35,36

Discussion

The present review summarized NGOs’ various roles in promoting oral health and critically looked into NGOs’ service evaluation (Figure 2). NGOs are influential in promoting oral health globally and locally with a potential for cross-sector collaborations between oral health and civil society sectors in a structured manner.9 Apart from being a direct service (dental treatment) provider, NGOs actively play roles in providing indirect services, such as being industry trainers, advocates and funders, to promote oral health globally and locally.37 In fact, rather than using an interventional treatment approach only, which currently works as a domination, attention should be brought to promoting oral health and preventing oral diseases so as to achieve oral health equity.38,39Fig. 2NGOs’ roles, service, and evaluation in oral health promotion.Fig 2

NGOs’ roles, service, and evaluation in oral health promotion.

The nature of NGOs is different in terms of regions and types, which leads to different targeted beneficiaries and roles of NGOs. Complementary to previous research,21,35,36 we found that the locations of NGOs vary from developed to developing countries, with a wide range of services available from international dental aid and outreach programmes to community-based dental care, education, and treatment. It is important to take the nature, location, and type of NGOs into consideration when understanding their multiple roles and dynamic functions in promoting oral health. Meanwhile, different NGOs have programmes with multiple objectives. These NGOs simultaneously undertake various roles to provide direct and indirect services, such as dental outreach programmes and advocacy for the greater awareness of the public and the government towards oral health issues. Innovative tools and approaches such as mobile health (mHealth) technologies can be employed to enhance oral health education, particularly among vulnerable populations such as older adults.40 Indeed, emerging technologies, such as smartphone-based self-monitoring tools powered by artificial intelligence (AI), represent promising opportunities for future application. AI tools can significantly improve efficiency and productivity, lead to better decision-making, and ultimately, improve outcomes. NGOs may consider to make use of AI tools to enhance their engagement and impact on oral health promotion. For example, AI tools can be used to screen gingivitis and to personalize prosthodontics treatment,41,42 which significantly reduces the demand for manpower and resources. The use of AI tools by NGOs may improve the efficacy of oral health services so as to expand the reach of NGOs services in resource-limited settings.

NGOs’ oral health services are also facing the challenges of localization and generalization in different contexts. In other words, how do NGOs adapt to the local contexts? This study reviews a strategy to integrate oral health into public health and to make it more accessible and responsive. There are different types of localization, such as multiobjective public health programmes (oral health is one component), indirectly delivered oral care services via trainers, and dental treatment provided in addition to health promotion.35 Another strategy is to establish partnerships and network with local stakeholders, including the local government, industry, and communities.21 Emerging technologies, such as smartphone-based self-monitoring tools powered by AI, offer promising avenues for NGOs to deliver preventive oral health services more effectively.43 However, the sustainability of NGOs’ services and their impact on the local community have been challenged because of the conflicting expectations and perceived treatment outcomes by patients, dentists, and NGO staff, limited financial resources, logistic and communication barriers, and increasing administrative burden.8,22

Systematic and scientific evaluation should be an integral component of an oral health programme to monitor the effectiveness of NGOs’ contribution in oral health promotion. However, this review found that 12 out of 23 studies did not report any evaluation on programme/projects. Further, many of the existing evaluations were limited in scope, relying primarily on biomedical paradigms and quantitative methods rooted in clinical research.44 Despite positive outcomes based on a biomedical mode, the overall quality of evidence on evaluation remains limited due to short-term assessment timeframes, inadequate or unspecified evaluation methodologies, and limited use of robust outcome measures and theory-based frameworks.45,46 In fact, the theory of change framework is considered as a promising approach to guide programme evaluation.22 In addition, insufficient information was presented regarding role and nature of the evaluators. It is unclear the evaluations were conducted by the NGOs themselves or independent third parties. Thus, possible bias remains unknown to readers. Future efforts should be made to explore the application and contextual adaptation of such frameworks to evaluate NGO-led interventions, especially in relation to targeted populations, local communities, and programme implementation processes.

Despite the valuable insights gained from this scoping review, several limitations should be acknowledged: first, this review was limited to peer-reviewed literature, excluding grey literature such as NGO reports, policy briefs, and unpublished documents. Our intention is to focus on peer-reviewed literature to maintain methodological consistency and data quality. However, this may lead to possible omission of information on real-world NGO programmes that are active in practice but undocumented in academic journals. Future studies should consider to incorporates broader information sources, such as grey literature, organizational case studies, and programme reports, to better capture the breadth of NGO involvement in oral health promotions. Second, the reliance on published studies introduces the risk of publication bias, where successful or well-documented projects are more likely to be reported, while less successful or smaller-scale NGO activities may remain undocumented. This may skew the representation of NGO roles and impact in oral health promotion. Exploring only peer-reviewed databases may capture just the ‘tip of the iceberg’ in understanding the full extent of NGOs’ contributions to oral health. Many impactful NGO activities, especially those in remote or resource-constrained areas, may not be formally studied or published, limiting the ability to capture the true breadth of their work.

The findings of this review provide current knowledge in both research and clinical contexts, warranting future research. First, it is crucial to explore the dynamics of cross-sector collaboration among key stakeholders involved in promoting oral health, including NGOs, government offices, communities, corporate companies, and higher education institutions. Both global and local organizations are suggested to explore good practices and stakeholder collaboration. Secondly, practical barriers NGOs faced and their coping strategies should be further researched. These current barriers include structural challenges, such as top-down approaches limited to technical thinking in specific contexts. Additionally, financial constraints, honest evaluation, shortage of human resources, and organizational change pose significant challenges for all organizations. How dental NGOs use different strategies to tackle the barriers is of both theoretical and practical significance. Thirdly, special types of NGOs, such as global dental aid organizations, require further research to understand their diversity, activities, and limitations. The influence of sociocultural and contextual factors should be taken into consideration. Lastly, more investigation is warranted regarding the evidence-based evaluation of NGOs and their tendency to demonstrate accountability in their services and adapt to multiple roles. Understanding the extent to which NGOs integrate evidence-based practices and evaluating the impact of their interventions can provide valuable insights for improving their effectiveness. To support these efforts, future research should incorporate broader sources to better capture the breadth of NGO involvement in oral health promotion.

Oral health is recognized as a fundamental human right, yet stark disparities in access to oral healthcare persist, particularly among marginalized and underserved populations. Our findings showed that NGOs play a vital role in bridging these gaps, often stepping in where public health systems fall short – especially in resource-constrained areas or in developed nations where services may be unevenly distributed or deprioritized. NGOs also made contributions to equity by enabling more inclusive access to oral health services by providing direct treatment services and also indirect support. However, the impact of these actions was not well evaluated. As an interface between the community and government, NGOs aim to reduce inequity and play a unique and vital role in helping communities establish health security.47 By identifying and mapping the diverse roles of NGOs in oral health promotion, this study provides invaluable insights to help NGOs strategically leverage their resources and expertise to deliver a broader range of services more effectively. Strengthening cross-sector collaborations, including partnerships with governments, academic institutions, and private organizations, can unlock additional resources, knowledge, and funding opportunities, enabling NGOs to scale up their impact. Furthermore, understanding successful service models and evaluation methods can guide NGOs in refining their programmes, adopting evidence-based practices, and demonstrating measurable outcomes. This integrated, equity-driven approach enhances the sustainability and impact of oral health promotion efforts, contributing to a fairer and more inclusive health landscape.

Conclusion

NGOs play an active role in promoting and improving people’s oral health and well-being by providing both direct and indirect services. The multifaceted involvement of NGOs highlighted their importance in improving oral health outcomes and addressing oral health disparities in broad community settings.
