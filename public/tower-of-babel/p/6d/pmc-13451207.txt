Human norovirus GII genotypic diversity in Gwangju, South Korea, based on ORF1-ORF2 junction (RdRp-VP1) analysis from 2020 to 2024
Authors: Jeongeun Mun, Sun-Ju Cho, Eunjin Seo, Ji-eun Lee, Kwang Ho Lee, Jeong Hee Park, Yeong-Un Lee, HyeonJi Kim, Mihee Seo, Gyung-Li Gang, Jung-mi Seo, Jeongjin Park, Woojin Jun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study analyzed human norovirus genogroup II (GII) strains detected in Gwangju, South Korea, between 2020 and 2024, focusing on genetic diversity and recombination at the ORF1–ORF2 junction involving the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, ORF1) and the major capsid protein VP1 (ORF2). Using year-round surveillance–based clinical samples, the analysis revealed that recombination events near the ORF1–ORF2 junction were a major driver of genotype replacement over time. Multiple recombinant lineages, including GII.4[P16], GII.17[P31], GII.3[P25], and the emergent, rarely reported genotype GII.7[P7], were detected during the study period. Although the RdRp gene is relatively conserved, sequence variation accumulating near the ORF1–ORF2 boundary may facilitate the emergence of new recombinant RdRp–VP1 combinations. These findings indicate that gene exchange at the ORF1–ORF2 junction plays a central role in shaping human norovirus genetic diversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50533-y.Keywords: Human norovirus, RdRp, Recombination, ORF1–ORF2 junction, Genetic diversity, GwangjuSubject terms: Evolution, Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Human norovirus, the leading cause of acute gastroenteritis in humans, is a major cause of food-and waterborne infections globally1. This virus belongs to the Caliciviridae family and contains a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA genome organized into three open reading frames (ORF1–3). ORF1 encodes nonstructural proteins, including RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp); ORF2 encodes the major capsid protein (VP1) responsible for host attachment and antigenicity2. ORF3 encodes the minor structural protein VP2, which has been reported to be associated with virion assembly and capsid stability. Both ORF1 and ORF2 represent primary sources of genetic diversity, and the ORF1–ORF2 junction is a recombination hotspot3. Human noroviruses exhibit high mutation rates and frequentre combination events, leading to the emergence of numerous genotypes and periodic shifts in epidemic patterns4. Within genogroup II (GII), which accounts for the majority of human infections, GII.4 has historically been the predominant genotype responsible for global outbreaks through continual antigenic variation and recombination5.

Additional genotypes, such as GII.2, GII.3, GII.6, GII.7, and GII.17, have also been detected in community-associated infections worldwide. Notably, multiple VP1 genotypes (G-types) can be identified within the same RdRp background (P-type), a pattern closely linked to template-switching events during replication at ORF1-ORF2 junction that result in genetic recombination. Although the RdRp region is more conserved than VP1, it serves as a critical marker for tracing recombination directionality and the emergence of new RdRp-VP1 combinations, highlighting its increasing epidemiological importance6. In South Korea, continuous surveillance of human norovirus genotypes has been conducted for several years. However, most studies focus on the VP1 region, and systematic long-term analyses of RdRp (ORF1) diversity and recombination patterns remain limited7.

In this study, we aimed to characterize recent five-year trends (2020 ~ 2024) in human norovirus GII genetic diversity and recombination patterns in the Gwangju region by examining the diversity of VP1 genotypes within each RdRp background and temporal shifts in RdRp–VP1 genotype combinations. Human norovirus GII-positive specimens were collected from Gwangju between 2020 and 2024 through a national waterborne and foodborne disease surveillance program. Genotype classification and recombination analysis were conducted based on sequences spanning the ORF1–ORF2 junction (RdRp–VP1).

Seasonal and monthly trends of human norovirus detection in Gwangju, 2020–2024

Between 2020 and 2024, the monthly human norovirus detection rate exhibited a clear seasonal pattern, with markedly higher positivity rates during winter (December to February) and a sharp decline during summer (June to August). The winter peaks were most prominent in January 2023 (57%) and December 2023 (50%), with the highest detection rate over the five-year surveillance period observed in January 2024 (61%). Detection rates typically dropped to minimal levels during summer; however, an exceptional deviation occurred in 2022 when the detection rate in June surpassed that of January, indicating an unusual off-season increase (Fig. 1). This anomaly contrasts with the conventional winter-dominant trend of human norovirus circulation in temperate regions.

Yearly distribution of human norovirus genotypes detected in Gwangju, 2020–2024

Between 2020 and 2024, distinct annual shifts were observed in the distribution of human norovirus genotypes circulating in Gwangju (Fig. 2). In 2020, multiple genotypes were detected without a single dominant genotype, with several GII genotypes distributed across a broad range of proportions, including GII.4[P16] (20%), GII.3[P12] (19%), GII.4[P31] (13%), GII.17[P17] (12%), GII.2[P16] (11%), and GII.6[P7] (7%). The “others” group (GII.8, GII.13, GII.1, and untypable strains) accounted for 18%. In 2021, the distribution shifted: GII.4[P31] increased to 26%, whereas that of GII.4[P16] declined to 9%. GII.6[P7] increased sharply to 24%, whereas GII.17[P17] decreased to 2%. An additional RdRp-VP1 combination, GII.3[P25] (3%), was identified, suggesting active polymerase capsid recombination. In 2022, GII.4[P31] became the dominant genotype (64.9%), whereas GII.4[P16] (5%) and GII.6[P7] (2.9%) declined significantly. Other genotypes (GII.2[P16], GII.3[P12], GII.3[P25], GII.17[P17]) were rarely detected, reflecting the strong predominance of the P31 lineage. In 2023, the detection rates of GII.4[P31] (26.3%) and GII.4[P16] (26.6%) were nearly equal, indicating a transitional phase between the two polymerase lineages. GII.2[P16] increased to 12.2%, whereas GII.3[P12] was absent, and GII.3[P25] reappeared (2.4%). Minor genotypes (GII.21, GII.14, GII.13, untyped) increased to 25.9%. In 2024, GII.4[P16] became the predominant genotype (32.5%), surpassing GII.4[P31] (2.6%). GII.3[P12] reemerged (12.6%), and GII.17[P17], which had been nearly undetectable since 2020, reappeared. The “others” category constituted 33.7% of detected genotypes, reflecting a high proportion of untypable or rare recombinant forms. A rare genotype, GII.7[P7], was detected in Gwangju in 2024, accounting for 8.9% of detections that year. Across the study period, untypable sequences within the “others” category accounted for 5.7%, 4.7%, 14.6%, 19.3%, and 28.4% in 2020–2024, respectively.

RdRp-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3): Distinct clustering of major polymerase genotypes (P-types) was observed, including P16, P31, P7, P17, P12, and P25. Each cluster contained local Gwangju isolates and global reference strains, indicating genetic relationships across regions. Within the P16 lineage, two main clusters were identified, corresponding to the GII.2 and GII.4 VP1 backgrounds. The GII.2[P16] cluster showed minimal sequence variation, whereas the GII.4[P16] cluster included 2024 isolates forming a distinct sub-lineage, suggesting the accumulation of year-specific mutations within the same polymerase background. Diverse VP1 genotypes, such as GII.1[P16] (China, 2017), GII.3[P16] (Russia, 2012), and GII.13[P16] (Taipei, 2013), were interspersed within this lineage, suggesting the view that the P16 polymerase has recombined with multiple capsid genotypes.

The P31 lineage consisted mainly of GII.4 VP1-type strains, closely related to AB541323.1 (Osaka, 2007), JX459908.1 (NSW, 2012), and MT474032.1 (Brazil, 2013), although a few isolates had GII.17 VP1 backgrounds. Short and compact branching structures indicated that the P31 sequences were highly conserved. Two temporally distinct clusters were identified within the P7 lineage. Isolates from 2020 to 2021 were positioned near GII.6[P7] references MK301293.1 (USA, 2018) and KX268709.1 (Maryland, 2014), whereas isolates from 2023 to 2024 clustered with KX752057.1 (China, 2009, GII.6[P7]) and AB258331.1 (Osaka, 2006, GII.7[P7]).These results suggest that the GII.6 and GII.7 VP1 types alternated over time within the same P7 polymerase background. Other polymerase lineages, including P17, P12, and P25 formed distinct clusters corresponding to their VP1 partners. Overall, the RdRp phylogeny demonstrated that a single polymerase genotype can coexist with multiple VP1 types, reflecting ongoing recombination and lineage diversification.

VP1-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4): All isolates were distinctly grouped by VP1 genotype (GII.1–GII.17), and the major clusters exhibited strong bootstrap support. The largest cluster, GII.4, contained both P31 and P16 polymerase backgrounds. The Gwangju isolates formed a separate sub lineage adjacent to the KY887600.1 reference (UK, 2016, GII.4[P16]), indicating temporal diversification within the same VP1 genotype. Three time-associated sub-lineages were observed in the GII.3 VP1 cluster. Isolates from 2020 clustered near the P12 references KY905334 (Australia, 2016) and MT158854 (China, 2018). The 2021–2022 isolates formed an independent branch, whereas the 2024 isolates grouped close to MN416945 (France, 2018, GII.3[P12]).

Several isolates from 2021 to 2023 clustered with OL451532 (China, 2021, GII.3[P25]), indicating the coexistence of P12 and P25 polymerase backgrounds within the same VP1 genotype (GII.3). The GII.17 cluster included Gwangju isolates from 2020 to 2024 along with references LC037415 (Japan, 2015, GII.17[P17]), KR020503 (Guangzhou, 2014, GII.17[P17]), and PQ336944 (the Netherlands, 2024, GII.17[P17]). Early isolates (2020–2021) were positioned near Asian references from 2014 to 2016, whereas more recent isolates (2023–2024) clustered closely with PQ336944, indicating recent sequence diversification within this lineage. The GII.6 cluster comprised both GII.6[P7] and GII.7[P7] types, with isolates adjacent to MK301293.1, KX268709.1, KX752057.1, and AB258331.1. The close proximity of these clusters suggests a phylogenetic linkage between the GII.6 and GII.7 VP1 types within the same P7 polymerase background.

In summary, both phylogenetic trees demonstrated that the RdRp and VP1 regions exhibit complementary evolutionary characteristics. The RdRp-based tree revealed polymerase-level conservation and recombination potential across multiple VP1 genotypes, whereas the VP1-based tree showed higher sequence variability and temporal replacement within capsid lineages.

Recombination analysis of Human norovirus GII at the ORF1–ORF2 junction

Recombination signals near the ORF1–ORF2 junction were identified in multiple isolates using RDP5 software, with several algorithms (RDP, GENECONV, Bootscan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq) yielding consistent support (p < 0.05). These analyses were used to screen for recombination signals and to identify representative strains showing discordant RdRp–VP1 phylogenetic patterns. To illustrate representative recombination patterns, SimPlot analyses were performed for selected isolates (Fig. 5). In GJ96-2023 (GII.3[P25]) (Fig. 5a), similarity patterns differed across the ORF1–ORF2 junction: the ORF1 (RdRp) side was most similar to a P25-associated reference (GII.17[P25], OP205529.1), whereas the ORF2 (VP1) side was most similar to a GII.3 capsid reference (GII.3[P12], KY905334.1), consistent with recombination between P25-type polymerase and a GII.3 VP1. GJ15-2020 (GII.4[P16]) (Fig. 5b) showed a transition from GII.2[P16] (LC209451.1) to GII.4[P31] (MT474032.1), indicating a recombination breakpoint close to the ORF1–ORF2 boundary. GJ105-2023 (GII.17[P31]) (Fig. 5c) exhibited a similar pattern, shifting from GII.4[P31] (MT474032.1) to GII.17[P17] (KR020503.1). GJ132-2024 (GII.6[P7]) (Fig. 5d) showed a crossover between GII.6[P7] (KX752057.1) and GII.7[P8] (JX846926.1), reflecting an exchange between closely related polymerase backgrounds.

Analysis of amino acid substitutions in the RdRp(ORF1) region of human norovirus GII strains

Comparison of the amino acid sequence of the RdRp (ORF1) region revealed distinct variation patterns depending on the associated VP1 genotype (Fig. 6). Within the same partial RdRp fragment, the amino acid positions analyzed in the Gwangju isolates (approximately residues 1, 14, 15, 20, 37, 42, 45, 47, 57, and 83) correspond to nucleotide positions approximately 4870–4960 of the KY887600 genome (GII.4[P16]), which serves as a representative reference strain for the P16 polymerase lineage. All numbering refers to the codon index within the analyzed partial RdRp region, and sequence alignment was performed relative to the KY887600 reference sequence.

Amino acid variation in the partial RdRp (ORF1) region according to VP1 genotype. Distinct substitution patterns were observed between GII.4(a) and GII.2(b) groups, with multiple amino acid changes concentrated in the 2024 GII.4 samples.

In the GII.4 VP1 group (Fig. 6a), samples collected from 2020 to 2023 exhibited several amino acid substitutions compared with KY887600. Notably, the 2024 samples showed concentrated substitutions in the analyzed partial RdRp region, including K→R at codon 15 (ref: nt 4872, AAG→AGA), H→M at codon 20 (ref: nt 4887, CAT→ATG), and D→I at codon 37 (ref: nt 4938, GAT→ATC). Several additional substitutions were detected exclusively in the 2024 isolates, suggesting expanded sequence diversity within the same RdRp genotype (P16) during that year. In contrast, the GII.2 VP1 group (Fig. 6b) maintained a largely conserved amino acid composition, with only sporadic substitutions identified in a few samples and no newly emerging variants by 2024. Overall, the GII.4 VP1 group exhibited greater amino acid variability in the RdRp region than the GII.2 group, with mutation sites particularly concentrated in 2024 isolates.

Discussion

This study investigated the genetic diversity and temporal dynamics of human norovirus genotypes circulating in Gwangju, Korea, from 2020 to 2024. Using a dual-typing approach based on both the RdRp (ORF1) and VP1 (ORF2) regions, we examined temporal changes in RdRp–VP1 genotype combinations associated with recombination at the ORF1–ORF2 junction. As the RdRp region lies adjacent to this well-known recombination hotspot, variation in this region provides contextual information for interpreting genotype replacement and recombinant patterns8. Recombination at the ORF1–ORF2 junction was inferred from phylogenetic incongruence between RdRp and VP1 regions together with recombination signals detected near the junction.

Human norovirus detection displayed typical winter seasonality, consistent with the increased environmental stability and transmissibility of the virus under low temperature and humidity conditions9. During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, norovirus activity appeared to be modestly reduced, likely reflecting the implementation of stringent non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs). In South Korea, these measures included nationwide social distancing policies, restrictions on gatherings and dining facilities, temporary school and kindergarten closures with transitions to remote learning, and strengthened hygiene practices such as handwashing and environmental disinfection. These interventions likely reduced opportunities for person-to-person contact and fecal–oral transmission, thereby limiting the spread of norovirus. Reference surveillance data from 2019 indicated a detection rate of 16.3%, compared with 13.3% in 2020, suggesting a limited but noticeable decrease. However, this reduction was transient. Interestingly, an unexpected increase was observed during the summer of 2022. This variance likely reflects the relaxation of non-pharmaceutical interventions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic. Nationwide social distancing measures in South Korea were lifted in April 2022, which led to the simultaneous reactivation of enteric virus transmission. In parallel, increased detection rates of respiratory viruses such as RSV, HCoV, HMPV, influenza, and enteric astrovirus have been reported nationwide since 2022, supporting this interpretation10,11.

Throughout the study period, GII.4 VP1-based variants were predominant; however, the associated RdRp showed marked temporal shifts. In 2020, GII.4[P16] (20.0%) and GII.4[P31] (13.0%) were detected at comparable levels. In contrast, GII.4[P31] predominated during 2021–2022, followed by a transition in 2023 and the dominance of GII.4[P16] in 2024, accompanied by a marked decline in GII.4[P31]. This local trend is consistent with global observations in which GII.4[P16] has increasingly replaced GII.4[P31], possibly reflecting a replication or adaptation advantage of the P16 polymerase12–14. The GII.17 lineage, which re-emerged in 2024 after a transient decline, was previously responsible for large outbreaks across East Asia in 2014–2016, notably the GII.17 Kawasaki 2014 variant that temporarily replaced GII.415,16. Recent increases in GII.17 detections in China and the USA, where it has surpassed GII.4 since 2024, are consistent with our regional findings and indicate the global reemergence of GII.17 as a major genotype.

Within the GII.3 lineage, alternating detections of P12 and P25 polymerase types were observed, suggesting ongoing intra-lineage recombination and diversification even within the same VP1 background. In addition, a rare GII.7[P7] strain was detected in 2024, illustrating how continuous, year-round surveillance can capture uncommon RdRp–VP1 combinations that may be underrepresented in outbreak-focused studies. Similar variants have been reported from Germany (2010) and China (2024), and the concurrent circulation of GII.P7/GII.6 and GII.P7/GII.7 lineages has been described from Australia during 2012–201417. These studies showed that the GII.P7 polymerase recombined with multiple VP1 types, generating distinct evolutionary paths, with GII.P7_GII.6 being more successful18. Consistent with these observations, the Gwangju GII.7[P7] strain clustered with locally circulating GII.6[P7] lineages, suggesting that this combination likely arose through recombination among existing regional transmission lineages rather than representing an external introduction.

A small proportion of sequences could not be assigned to a specific genotype and were categorized as untypable. Although the proportion of untyped sequences appeared to increase in the later years of the study period, identical laboratory protocols and quality control criteria were applied consistently throughout the study. Such results may arise from sequence variation in primer-binding regions, primer mismatches, or incomplete amplification of the ORF1–ORF2 junction region, which can limit reliable genotype assignment.

Phylogenetic comparison of the ORF1 and ORF2 regions further demonstrated that human noroviruses evolve through frequent recombination at the ORF1–ORF2 junction, resulting in diverse polymerase–capsid combinations. In the RdRp phylogeny, multiple VP1 types were interspersed within the same RdRp cluster, whereas VP1 trees showed distinct clustering patterns reflecting capsid antigenic properties. Collectively, these findings indicate that human norovirus diversity arises not only from gradual mutations but also from accumulated recombination events and shifting RdRp–VP1 pairings19. RDP and SimPlot analyses confirmed significant crossover signals around the ORF1–ORF2 junction, reinforcing its role as a major recombination hotspot18. SimPlot analysis was therefore applied as a complementary visualization tool to support recombination signals detected by RDP-based methods and to illustrate the phylogenetic incongruence between RdRp and VP1 regions at the ORF1–ORF2 junction. Importantly, the parental reference sequences used in these analyses were selected from phylogenetic lineages that clustered closely with Gwangju-derived strains, representing genotypes that had been circulating locally during the study period. Thus, the observed recombination patterns are compatible with genetic exchange occurring among existing regional transmission lineages, rather than arising from entirely unrelated external parental strains.

Patterns of amino acid substitutions also varied depending on the VP1 type20. P16 polymerases paired with GII.4 VP1 exhibited multiple amino acid changes, whereas those paired with GII.2 VP1 were comparatively conserved. This suggests that the structural or biochemical context of the VP1 capsid can influence polymerase sequence variability, and that RdRp–VP1 combinations undergo a process of co-adaptation14,21. Although not shown in this study, the P31 polymerase remained highly conserved, maintaining a stable association with the GII.4 capsid. Previous studies have established the ORF1–ORF2 junction as a key recombination site. However, there is limited evidence that functionally characterized amino acid mutation hotspots exist within this region22. In the present study, several amino acid substitutions were identified near this junction.

Although the functional consequences of these substitutions could not be determined in the present study, their proximity to the ORF1–ORF2 boundary suggests that they may reflect adaptive adjustments following recombination—such as optimization of replication efficiency or polymerase–capsid compatibility—rather than acting as independent determinants of viral transmissibility.

Collectively, our findings indicate that recombination at the ORF1–ORF2 junction plays a key role in shaping human norovirus genotype dynamics observed in this study23. Within this framework, RdRp sequence variation—although more conserved than VP1—can provide contextual information for interpreting changes in RdRp–VP1 genotype combinations over time, rather than representing an independent focus of analysis. From a public health perspective, monitoring RdRp–VP1 combinations through dual-typing surveillance may enhance situational awareness by enabling earlier recognition of genotype shifts and emerging recombinant lineages, rather than directly predicting future outbreaks.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focused exclusively on human norovirus genogroup II (GII), which represents the predominant genogroup associated with human infections in Korea; therefore, the circulation patterns of genogroup I (GI) were not evaluated. Second, quantitative viral load data were not available for downstream analysis. Although real-time RT-PCR was performed for initial screening according to the national surveillance protocol, samples were selected for sequencing based on the presence of a detectable Ct value below the cutoff defined in the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) standard operating procedure (Ct ≤ 37.7). Subsequently, all RT-qPCR–positive samples were subjected to conventional RT-PCR, and only those yielding a visible amplicon were selected for sequencing and genotyping. As a result, variations in viral load across samples or over time could not be quantitatively assessed in this study. Third, the analyzed RdRp fragment (~ 300 bp) represented only a short portion adjacent to the ORF1–ORF2 junction, optimized for recombination detection, rather than a detailed structural or functional analysis24. Further studies using longer RdRp or full ORF1 sequences are required to elucidate the functional and evolutionary implications of these observations. Nonetheless, the analyzed region remains a practical and effective target for molecular surveillance of recombination events and early recognition of newly evolving RdRp–VP1 combinations.

Conclusions

This study analyzed human norovirus GII strains detected in Gwangju from 2020 to 2024 and revealed that diverse RdRp–VP1 combinations were continuously generated during this period through recombination. The detection of genotypes such as GII.4[P16], GII.17[P31], GII.3[P25], and GII.7[P7] demonstrates ongoing genotype turnover within the region. Although dual typing based on both VP1 and RdRp has become standard in human norovirus surveillance, our findings highlight the additional importance of focusing on the RdRp region itself. As this region lies adjacent to the ORF1–ORF2 recombination hotspot, even subtle sequence variations can signal the emergence of previously unrecognized recombinant forms. Therefore, sustained RdRp-based monitoring is essential to complement VP1 analysis, enabling earlier detection of recombinant variants and a deeper understanding of human norovirus evolutionary dynamics.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Human norovirus-positive stool specimens collected between 2020 and 2024 were obtained through the Enteric Pathogens Active Surveillance Network (Enter-Net), a national surveillance system coordinated by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). Within this system, stool specimens are routinely collected from patients presenting with diarrhea, and laboratory testing is performed to identify causative pathogens. Weekly Enter-Net surveillance reports are publicly accessible on the KDCA website (https://www.kdca.go.kr/npt).

Human norovirus-positive specimens were classified by year, and the detection rate of each genotype was calculated as the proportion of total human norovirus-positive cases per year. Representative samples were randomly selected for sequencing using an equal-proportion sampling approach.

For routine clinical diagnostics, stool specimens were collected in accordance with institutional procedures, and informed consent was obtained at the time of sample collection. For this study, only fully anonymized human norovirus surveillance and diagnostic laboratory data were analyzed; no personal identifiers were included, and no direct interaction with human subjects occurred.

Under the Infectious Disease Control and Prevention Act of the Republic of Korea, human norovirus surveillance and diagnostic data collected for public health purposes are not considered research-driven human subject data. Therefore, the secondary analysis of fully de-identified data is generally considered outside the scope of Institutional Review Board (IRB) review, and additional informed consent is not typically required. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and national regulations.

Viral RNA extraction and RT-PCR amplification

For sample preparation, approximately 1 g of stool specimen was suspended in 9 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenized by vortexing for 3 min. Viral RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and stored at − 70 °C.

The ORF1–ORF2 junction region (~ 530 bp) of the human norovirus GII genome was amplified using the SB-Plex™ Human norovirus GII Sequencing Kit (Seegene, Seoul, South Korea) with primers GII-MON431 (forward, 5′-TGG ACI AGR GGI CCY AAY CA-3′) and GII-G2SKR (reverse, 5′-CCR CCN GCA TRH CCR TTR TAC AT-3′) with Sanger sequencing25,26. This region is a standard target for human norovirus genotyping by the Korean National Surveillance System and is widely used for dual typing of polymerase and capsid genotypes27. PCR products were subjected to Sanger sequencing by a commercial sequencing service provider, and nucleotide sequences were obtained. In total, 250 human norovirus–positive stool specimens collected between 2020 and 2024 were initially selected for sequencing (50 specimens per year). Of these, 145 samples yielded high-quality sequences suitable for genotyping and phylogenetic analysis, while the remaining samples were excluded due to insufficient RT-PCR amplification or poor sequence quality, such as low signal intensity or mixed chromatogram peaks. The same sample selection and quality control criteria were applied consistently across all study years.

Sequence analysis and genotyping

The sequences were edited and aligned using MEGA v12 (Pennsylvania State University, USA)28. Phylogenetic trees were generated using the maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Genotypes were assigned using the Human norovirus Genotyping Tool (RIVM, the Netherlands; http://www.rivm.nl/mpf/humannorovirus/typingtool) and verified according to the dual-typing system based on the RdRp (P-type) and VP1 (G-type) regions27.

Recombination detection

Phylogenetic analyses of the RdRp (ORF1) and VP1 (ORF2) regions were performed separately using the maximum likelihood method with the general time-reversible model in MEGA v12. The robustness of each phylogeny was evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Circular trees were visualized using the iTOL software29. Recombination events were analyzed using RDP530 and SimPlot++ (v1.3)31, with a focus on the ORF1–ORF2 junction. Potential recombinants were considered valid only when supported by ≥ 3 independent detection methods (e.g., RDP, GENECONV, Bootscan, and MaxChi). All nucleotide sequences generated in this study were deposited in GenBank, and the corresponding accession numbers are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Amino acid analysis

Nucleotide sequences were translated into amino acids and aligned using MEGA. The representative reference sequence KY887600 (UK, 2016, GII.4[P16]) and local Gwangju isolates were aligned to identify amino acid substitutions. Difference between the Gwangju sequences and KY887600 were examined to determine notable substitutions or conserved residues. For positional interpretation, amino acid sites were defined based on the codon index within the analyzed partial genomic regions, and all substitutions were annotated relative to the KY887600 reference sequence to ensure consistent coordinate assignment.
