Withaferin A decreases glycolytic reprogramming in breast cancer
Authors: Asifa Khan, Asad ur Rehman, Shumaila Siddiqui, Jiyauddin Khan, Sheersh Massey, Prithvi Singh, Daman Saluja, Syed Akhtar Husain, Mohammad Askandar Iqbal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractReprogrammed glucose metabolism is considered as the hallmark of cancer with therapeutic implications. Phytocompounds have potential to inhibit cancer metabolism. Here, we tested the ability of Withaferin A (WA), a withanolide derived from Withania somnifera, in modulating cancer metabolism. The assessed effect of WA on aerobic glycolysis in breast cancer cell lines showed that WA decreases the glucose uptake, lactate production and ATP generation by inhibiting the expression of key glycolytic enzymes i.e., GLUT1, HK2 and PKM2. We also identified that WA induced inhibition of cancer glycolysis by targeting c-myc as validated by silencing experiments followed by metabolic readouts. Decreased glycolysis resulted in reduced cell viability, biomass and colony forming ability of breast cancer cells. To further validate our in vitro findings in breast cancer patients, we analyzed 90 metabolic pathways in ~ 2000 breast tumors and observed that glycolysis is the most deregulated pathway in breast tumors. Deregulated glycolysis also predicted poor prognosis in breast cancer patients. In addition, patient data showed correlation between c-myc expression and glycolytic deregulation in breast cancer. Taken together, our results highlight the role of WA in inhibiting breast cancer metabolism via c-myc/glycolysis axis.Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Drug discovery, Molecular medicine

Introduction

Breast cancer is the leading cause of death in women in India and across the world. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, breast cancer has now become the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women with an estimation of ~ 2.3 million new cases1. This highlights the need for the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and strategies to curb the incidence of this deadly disease2.

Metabolic dependencies of cancer cells are very different from normal cells, providing a window for therapeutic intervention3. Cancer cells are known to reprogram their metabolism to fulfil the increased biosynthetic demands for macromolecular synthesis3,4. Increased glycolysis is central to reprogrammed cancer metabolism and a key feature that provides essential cellular energy and anabolic precursors that sustain cancer cell proliferation but it also provides several other benefits such as detoxification of ROS through the production of NADPH, induction of pro-tumor immunity, suppression of anti-tumor response, chemo resistance, etc.5–8. Otto Warburg was the first to identify this property of cancer cells to produce excessive of lactate independent of the availability of oxygen-the phenomenon termed as Warburg effect9. Further, Warburg effect was discovered to occur in numerous malignancies, and is clinically exploited through18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET ) scan to locate the tumors7–10. Aberrant transcriptional regulation is one of the main cause of deregulated metabolism where myc, a multifunctional oncogene, has received attention for its essential role in the etiology of breast cancer11. Amplification/overexpression of myc in breast cancer is frequently linked to early relapse of the tumor12,13. The transcriptional regulation of metabolic genes by myc is linked with the aggressiveness of tumors13–15. Evidently, metabolic transformation is an important hallmark of cancer and represents a clinically exploitable vulnerability through synthetic or natural approaches3,16–18. Owing to the toxicity associated with conventional therapeutic regimens19–21, there is a need to search for safer therapeutic approaches wherein the use of dietary and pharmacological interventions is garnering a lot of consideration22. Plant based compounds have drawn attention due to their structural diversity, lesser side effects, easier availability and multiple targets and their potential in inhibiting cancer metabolism has also been well appreciated23–25.

Withaferin A (WA) is a steroidal lactone originally isolated from Withania somnifera (commonly known as Ashwagandha) from the family Solanaceae. Traditionally, Ashwagandha has been used for several years in treating various diseases including cancer26. Substantial amount of research on WA has shown its anti-cancer potential mediated by several mechanisms including: inhibition of Akt signaling via mTOR, Notch1 signaling, downregulation of STAT3 signaling, decrease in ROS generation and vimentin expression27–30. Clinical trials have also been conducted on W. somnifera extract. In several placebo studies carried out in clinical trials, low doses of WA and W. somnifera extracts when given to healthy people showed an overall improvement in well-being with no side effects31,32. In a phase II trial, patients receiving chemotherapy in combination with W. somnifera extract and a small amount of WA experienced similar outcomes, with the chemo-induced fatigue being reduced without any negative side effects.33. Similarly, in another phase II clinical trial study (NCT00689195), W. somnifera extract therapeutically reduced high-grade relapsed or metastatic osteosarcoma in patients34.

Clearly, WA is an appreciated compound with known anti-cancer and immunomodulatory activity, however its role in targeting metabolic reprogramming remains poorly understood albeit some evidence 29,30,35,36. This prompted us to investigate whether and how WA affects the breast cancer metabolism.

Results

Withaferin A decreases the cell viability and colony forming ability of breast cancer cells

Four breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-468, and MCF-7) were used to study the in vitro effect of WA. These cell lines represent luminal (MCF-7), Her2+ (MDA-MB-453) and basal-like (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468) subtypes. All cell lines were treated with increasing concentrations of WA for 24 h to determine cell viability. WA treatment resulted in viability inhibition in all the cell lines in a dose-dependent manner, as shown in Fig. 1a–d. At a concentration of 5 µM WA (for 24 h), the percentage inhibition of cells was 50% in MDA-MB-468, 36% in MDA-MB-453, 32% in MDA-MB-231, and 30% in MCF-7 when compared to the control in the 24 h of cell proliferation assay. We selected 5 μM WA as the optimal concentration for further experiments.

Effect of WA on the cell viability of breast cancer cell lines. (a–d) Breast cancer cells were treated with either WA at different conc. (1, 3, 5, and 10 µM) for 24 h or DMSO as vehicle control. Data was recorded and presented in mean ± SD from the three independent experiments and *P < 0.03, **P < 0.005, ***P < 0.0002, and ****P < 0.0001, ns = non-significant, calculated using ANOVA.

To further investigate the anti-tumorigenic potential of WA in breast cancer cell lines, we checked the ability of the studied cell lines to form colonies in the presence and absence of WA for 3 weeks. The level of colony formation by untreated breast cancer cells was proportional to the aggressive potential of the specific cell line. Highly aggressive MDA-MB-231 cells produce larger colonies, moderately aggressive MCF-7 and MDA-MB-468 cells produce fewer colonies and the minimally aggressive MDA-MB-453 cells produce smallest colonies as shown in (Fig. 2). In a 6-well plate, different numbers of cells were seeded and treated with WA at 5 µM concentration. When compared to control, WA treatment significantly reduced the ability of breast cancer cell lines to form colonies to an extent where the colonies totally vanished upon WA treatment (Fig. 2). All tested breast cancer cell lines showed a similar reduction in colony formation after WA treatment, indicating that WA has the potential to reduce cancer cell’s clonogenic potential required for tumor initiation and growth.

Treatment with WA reduces colonization in breast cancer cell lines. Colony formation assay performed using 5 µM WA in (a) MCF7, (b) MDA-MB-231, (c) MDA-MB-468 (d) MDA-MB-453.

WA decreases the glucose uptake, lactate release and ATP production in breast cancer cells

Aerobic glycolysis, characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate release, is widely recognized as a cancer hallmark37,38. Increased glycolysis gives cancer cells bioenergetic and anabolic advantage even in the absence of oxygen (hypoxia), which is a common feature of solid tumors37. This reprogrammed metabolism (Warburg effect) plays a crucial role in supporting the rapid proliferation and survival of cancer cells. In our study, we studied the effect of WA on the cellular glucose uptake, lactate release and ATP production in T47D, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and MCF-7 cells.

After WA treatment (5 µM), we found a substantial drop in glucose uptake and lactate release in all cell lines except MDA-MB-468, where the effect was relatively less significant (Fig. 3). Next, we examined how the inhibition of aerobic glycolysis by WA treatment affects ATP production. After WA treatment, there was a significant decrease in ATP production in all cell lines except MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 3c). This result indicates a potentially lower glycolytic capacity in MDA-MB-468 compared to the other breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-453).

WA decreases the protein expression of key glycolytic enzymes via downregulation of c-myc expression

To investigate the mechanism underlying the WA-induced decrease in glucose uptake, lactate release, and ATP production, we examined the effect of WA on the expression of key enzymes in aerobic glycolysis and its regulation. After 24 h of WA treatment, the protein expression of GLUT1, HK2, and PKM2 decreased in all four breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 4a). Densitometric analysis reveals the differences in expression of these enzymes in treated cells compared to control (Fig. 4c,d). Additionally, c-myc, one of the master regulators of glycolysis, was also observed to be downregulated upon WA treatment, indicating its crucial role in glucose metabolism (Fig. 4a). To confirm that c-Myc regulates glycolysis, transient silencing of c-Myc in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells was performed using siRNA against c-myc. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting, followed by investigation of key glycolytic protein expression after c-Myc silencing. Results showed substantial decrease in the protein expression of HK2 and GLUT1 in both c-myc silenced cell lines (Fig. 4b,c). Also, we examined the Warburg effect and ATP production in MDA-MB-231 after silencing c-myc and the result prompted us to conclude that the decrease in Warburg effect and ATP upon WA treatment is, at least in part, due to down-regulation of c-myc expression. We next investigated whether overexpression of c-myc reverses the effects of WA. As expected, over-expression of c-myc resulted in increased cellular proliferation, compared to mock transfected cells even in the presence of WA (supplementary fig. S4).We also noted a significant reduction in glucose uptake, lactate release, and ATP production, suggesting the role of c-myc in the regulation of the Warburg effect (Fig. 4e). To further validate the association of c-myc and glycolysis, we queried largest breast cancer patient dataset METABRIC. The geneset representing myc signature was obtained from MSigDB39 and subjected to Pathifier for the calculation of pathway deregulation score (PDS) in each patient's tumor. Correlation analysis between PDS of glycolysis and myc geneset signature revealed a strong positive correlation (Fig. 4f). Similar findings were observed at mRNA level, that is, mRNA expression of c-myc positively correlated with glycolytic PDS (Fig. 4f).

Glycolysis is highly deregulated and associated with poor patient outcome in breast cancer

To further validate the in vitro results, we utilized clinical and transcriptomic information of 2000 breast cancer and normal samples in the patient dataset obtained from the METABRIC dataset40. Using the transcriptomic and geneset information (RECON 1)41,42, Pathifier algorithm43 was used to compute Pathway Deregulation Scores (PDS) for each patient in order to get deregulation of metabolism at the pathway level. Pathifier calculates the deregulation score of metabolic pathways in each of the 2000 samples. Pathway deregulation score provide us the information about pathway deregulation at individual level, where each sample is assigned a particular deregulation score indicating how much a particular pathway is deviated from normal scenario in that particular sample. We can calculate how much a particular pathway deviates from normal samples in each patient. First of all we looked for the most deregulated pathways in breast cancer as compared to normal counterparts. Glycolysis ranked first among the 90 metabolic pathways analyzed as the most dysregulated pathways in breast cancer samples compared to normal samples (Fig. 5a). Also, breast cancer patients showed much higher glycolysis deregulation than normal samples (Fig. 5b). To further extend these findings at metabolite level, we used metabolomics data of 67 tumor patients and 65 normal samples from Terunuma et al.11. We found that tumor samples had a higher relative abundance of glycolytic metabolites than normal samples in metabolomics data, which supported our results. (Fig. 5c). Our next objective was to investigate the effect of glycolysis on the survival of breast cancer patients. As shown in (Fig. 5d), deregulated glycolysis was associated with poor disease-specific and relapse free survival in breast cancer. Intriguingly, patients with Glycolysishigh-MYChigh had poorer 5-year overall survival than Glycolysislow-MYClow, emphasizing on the importance of glycolysis in determining patient outcomes. Collectively, the results demonstrate the higher deregulation of glycolysis and its impact on worsening the survival of breast cancer patients.

On the basis of PDS score, glycolysis found to be the top deregulated pathway. (b) Box and whisker plot represents the PDS of glycolysis in tumor vs normal tissue. (c) Bar graph showing the relative abundance of glycolytic metabolites in breast tumors as compared to normal samples (Terunuma et al.). Discussion

Breast cancer is a deadly disease with limited treatment modalities, thus, paving way for improved therapeutic approaches in this field. The rapid growth of breast cancer is characterized by alterations in metabolism, which is complex and highly dymanic in nature. Revived interest in cancer metabolism over the 10-15 years has resulted in accumulation of wealth of knowledge that supports the therapeutic and chemo-preventive relevance of metabolism in cancer. As a result, a number of synthetic drugs targeting cancer metabolism are being tested in both preclinical and clinical settings3,16 Side effects and toxicity associated with conventional drugs made plant-based drugs received a lot of attention in the recent years. Several phytochemicals have been reported to target cancer metabolism 44. Several studies, including ours, demonstrated that phytochemicals have the potential to inhibit cancer glycolysis by targeting key enzymes23,24,45–47. The potential of Ashwagandha to target several cancer hallmarks has long been acknowledged, however, it is yet unknown whether and how WA targets glucose metabolism in cancer28–30,36,48. In this study, we show the anti-metabolic effects of WA in the context of breast cancer cell lines and reveal role of c-myc in mediating inhibitory effects on cancer glycolysis.

Considering the poor prognosis of breast cancer, particularly aggressive ones, there is a need to identify therapeutically relevant targets that may improve patient outcome. Cancer cells are highly glucose addicted as compared to normal cells and this metabolic vulnerability of cancer cells can be therapeutically utilized. Accordingly, a thorough understanding of metabolic liabilities and identification of oncogenic pathways associated with them is required for effective anti-cancer strategies. Our work demonstrates that WA is effective in inhibiting viability of all studied cell lines, highlighting the spectrum of WA as an anti-cancer drug (Fig. 1). To determine if a single cell has the ability to develop into large colonies, the colony formation was assayed. This assay is a sensitive indicator of undifferentiated cancer stem cells49. WA not only decreased cancer cell growth but also reduced the colony forming ability of the cancer cells (Fig. 2). This highlighted the anti-tumorigenic potential of WA against breast cancer cell lines.

Glycolysis is a key metabolic pathway that uses glucose to produce ATP and provide substrate that acts as precursors or intermediates of cross-talking anabolic pathway like pentose phosphate pathway (PPP)50. Consequently, it is plausible that a high glycolytic rate is essential for the survival of the vast majority of breast cancer cells. The effect of WA's inhibition of glycolysis on breast cancer cell lines disrupts their bioenergetics and biosynthetic capacity. WA decreased the glucose uptake, lactate release and ATP production in all four cell lines; however, the results for MDA-MB-468 were not statistically significant (Fig. 3). Incorporating glucose into glycolysis satisfies the biosynthetic and bioenergetic demands of rapid proliferation, leading to an increased glucose thirst in cancer cells. Key glycolytic enzymes responsible for glucose uptake and lactate production are significantly upregulated in cancer cells in order to facilitate rapid glucose uptake. Because glycolytic carbon is required for anabolic synthesis in cancer cells to produce macromolecules for daughter cancer cells, the decline in the Warburg effect inhibits the growth of cancer cells51,52.

WA showed the inhibition of glucose uptake, lactate release and ATP production through downregulation of key glycolytic enzymes (GLUT1, HK2 and PKM2) at protein level (Fig. 4a). The decrease in the protein level of key glycolytic enzymes explains the observed decrease in glucose uptake, lactate release and ATP production upon WA treatment. As reported, expression of GLUT1, HK2, and PKM2 is significantly elevated in glucose-dependent breast cancer51,53–56. PKM2 has been demonstrated to modulate glycolytic flux between energy production and biosynthesis via PPP57. Thus, inhibition of glycolysis may be a viable approach in targeting breast cancer.

Next, we checked whether the decrease in the key glycolytic enzymes is via c-myc or not. c-myc is the master transcriptional regulator of glycolysis which in turn is required to fuel anabolic processes critical for tumor growth58. It is well documented that c-myc regulates glycolysis (Warburg effect) by directly increasing glycolytic gene transcription and the protein expression58–60. In our study, we found that WA treatment decreases the protein expression of the key glycolytic enzymes in the studied breast cancer cell lines Fig. 4a. Myc directly activates all glycolytic genes by attaching to the typical E-box motif (CACGTG)61. C-myc increases the glucose transporter SLC2A1, that facilitates glucose absorption62. It is bound to hexokinase II (HK2), enolase 1 (ENO1), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) on the canonical myc-binding E-box in diverse species, according to a chromatin immunoprecipitation experiment61. Myc has been shown to activate glycolytic genes not just through transcription but also via alternative splicing. PKM2 increases the penultimate step in aerobic glycolysis, whereas PKM1 appears to enhance oxidative phosphorylation59. PKM pre-mRNA alternative splicing is mediated by three splicing factors: heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) A1, hnRNP A2, and polypyrimidine tract-binding protein59. C-myc has been demonstrated to induce transcription of these splicing factors, so increasing the expression of PKM2 over PKM1, and thereby promoting glycolysis59. By enhancing glycolysis, c-myc can provide cancer cells with several advantages, such as rapid ATP generation, biosynthetic precursor supply, NADPH production, redox balance, and tumor microenvironment remodeling63.

Silencing of c-myc demonstrates a decrease in the expression of major glycolytic enzymes, including GLUT1 and HK2, as observed in the immunoblots presented in (Fig. 4b). Additionally, a reduction in the Warburg effect, characterized by lower glucose uptake, lactate release, and ATP synthesis, is illustrated in (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the reversal of effects of WA on c-myc overexpression (Supplementary fig. S4) implies that c-myc is crucial in determining metabolic fate of cancer cells and is a critical target of WA. Our results have established previously unknown link between WA and c-myc; thereby adding a new dimension to therapeutic potential of WA.

Further, breast cancer patient data showed significant correlation between glycolysis and c-myc (Fig. 4f). This is consistent with previous reports showing that c-myc-overexpressing cell lines exhibited increased glycolytic gene expression 64. Seemingly, targeting c-myc could be an effective therapeutic strategy for breast and other myc-dependent cancers.

WA-induced inhibition of glycolysis via c-myc resulted in decreased metabolic capacities and downregulation of key glycolytic enzymes in breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 4), emphasizing the importance of c-myc/glycolysis axis in maintaining aberrant metabolism in breast cancer. Of note, the upregulation of glycolysis in breast tumor samples underline the importance of glycolysis in cancer and therefore its therapeutic significance (Fig. 5a,b). Higher glycolysis in breast cancer also predicted poor patient survival (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, metabolomics data obtained from Terunuma et al. also showed that glycolytic metabolites are accumulated at a much higher degree in breast tumors compared to normal tissue, suggesting not only the importance of glycolysis in breast cancer but also the consistency of results presented (Fig. 5c). It is important to appreciate that c-myc may not be the sole regulator of glycolysis in breast cancer cells, as c-myc silencing did not drop glucose uptake to zero (Fig. 4e). To sum up, targeting c-myc alone may not be an effective and therefore inhibiting glycolytic dependence altogether along with c-myc inhibition might be a viable strategy. Our results along with the recent published work suggests that WA has the potential to inhibit deregulated metabolism in cancer, however, more efforts are needed to improve the bioavailability and the efficacy of WA. For example, alternative formulations or structural alterations may be introduced to increase the bioavailability of WA. According to a recent study, WA is stable in gastric fluid and intestinal microbiota, but its stability decreases in liver microsomes (half-life of WA is 5.6 min)48. Thus, WA has the potential to be used as a therapeutic drug with optimal bioavailability against cancer.

Conclusion

In conclusion, WA significantly reduces the glucose metabolism via c-myc inhibition. This decreased glycolysis inhibits the ability of breast cancer cells to proliferate and form colonies as well. Additionally, WA treatment reduced the expression level of important glycolytic genes via c-Myc. Therefore, it might be argued that therapeutic targeting of glucose metabolism may improve the clinical outcome of patients. In conclusion, our study sheds light on anti-cancer role for WA from metabolic standpoint and piques interest in the potential applications of WA and its analogues in the clinically effective inhibition of metabolic addictions in cancer cells.

Materials and method

Cell culture, WA treatment, cell proliferation, cell viability

For cell culture, breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-453 and MCF-7) were procured from National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS) Pune, Maharashtra, India. All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) high glucose media (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, incubated at 5% CO2 and temperature 37 °C. Withaferin A (ChromaDex, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in DMSO; stock solution was prepared and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Cell proliferation was calculated using Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay. SRB binds stoichiometrically to cellular proteins, reflective of cell number and biomass. SRB assay was performed as previously described with minor modifications65. Thousands of cells were seeded in a 96 well plate for 24 h, then treated with WA in various concentrations (1, 3, 5, 10 µM) for 24 h before being fixed with 50% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at 4 °C for 1 h. After 1 h, the plates were thoroughly washed with water, and the cells were stained for 30 min with SRB dye prepared in 1% acetic acid (0.4% w/v). The dye was washed with 1% acetic acid. The cell bound dye was then dissolved in 10 mM tris base (pH 10.5) and the absorbance was measured.

Colony formation assay

MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-453, and MCF-7 cells were seeded (1000–2000 cells/well) in a 6 well plate, then cells were treated with WA at a concentration of 5 µM and incubated for 14 days at 37 °C. The colonies were then fixed and stained with crystal violet (0.5% w/v). After 30 min of incubation, colonies were washed with 1X PBS and plates were photographed.

Metabolic assays

For glucose uptake and lactate production media was collected from the control and WA treated samples. Spent media was centrifuged to pellet down the cell debris and deproteinization was done using 5% tricholoro-acetic acid. Later glucose and lactate levels were assayed using the Eton Bioscience (San Diego, CA, USA) colorimetric kit as per manufacturer’s protocol. For ATP, metabolites were measured using BioVision kit, as per the manufacturer's protocol.

siRNA mediated c-myc silencing

siRNA pool against c-myc was procured from addgene, USA. For control we used scrambled siRNA. siRNA was transfected by Lipofectamine® 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blotting

10% SDS gel was used for protein separation. The protein samples were prepared to conc. 2.4 μg/μl equalized with RIPA lysis buffer. 6 × loading dye was used in the sample to 1X. Equal amount of protein (i.e. 40 μg) was loaded in each well and separated on SDS PAGE. Transfer of proteins on PVDF membrane was done overnight at constant voltage further confirmed by ponceau staining. Membrane was then incubated in primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) overnight. Then incubation of HRP conjugated secondary antibodies was done (1:4000 dilution) for 1 h. Blots were developed using ECL chemiluminescence detections reagent. Alpha ease 4.0 software was used for densitometry analysis to examine the relative gene expression change after normalization with β-actin. Primary antibodies were used anti-GLUT1, anti-HK2, anti-PKM2 and anti-c-myc (Cell Signaling Technology, USA).

Data-mining

Transcriptomic and clinical data of Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) normal (n = 144) and breast cancer patients (n = 2000) were obtained from European Genome Archive (EGA) with accession numbers EGAD00010000210, EGAD00010000211, and EGAD00010000212. Metabolomics and transcriptomic data of 67 breast cancer patients and 65 normal samples were obtained from Terunuma et al.11. For survival analysis of patients, Kaplan–Meier curves were used, P values and Hazard ratio (HR) were calculated using the Mantel–Cox and log-rank test respectively.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were represented as mean ± SD. Experiments were performed three times independently. Comparison among different cell lines groups were analyzed using one-way or two way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All the statistical analyses were done using Graph Pad Prism v8 (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was considered for p-value *P < 0.05 to be statistically significant and represented in figures as *P < 0.03, **P < 0.005, and ***P < 0.0001.
