Tracking outcome changes in benign prostatic hyperplasia trials: a version history review of ClinicalTrials.gov records
Authors: Tanner Livsey, Malek Itani, Ahmed Elghzali, Rachel Hazlitt, Kellen Keefer, Ryan Sherry, Daniel Archer, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBenign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common condition with trials that frequently rely on subjective outcomes. While federal regulations require prospective outcome registration in ClinicalTrials.gov, modifications to these outcomes are permitted and often unjustified, potentially compromising transparency and interpretability. This study examined the frequency, timing, type, and transparency of prespecified outcome modifications in BPH trials.MethodsWe conducted a registry-based cross-sectional analysis of interventional BPH trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov between January 18, 2017, and December 31, 2024. Eligible trials enrolled adult males, reported posted results, contained prespecified outcomes in both original and most recent registry versions, and were linked to peer-reviewed publications. Modifications were categorized by type (e.g., additions, removals, rewordings), timing, and severity. Key variables included the Outcome Load Index (OLI) and a Severity-Weighted Change Score. Statistical analyses included Cochran’s Q tests, Kaplan–Meier time-to-event analysis, and correlation tests.ResultsOf 1383 records screened, 36 trials met inclusion criteria. All trials (100%) exhibited at least one outcome modification, none of which were justified. Modifications occurred exclusively after primary completion; half occurred before publication and half after. Minor rewording and broad-to-specific definitional shifts were most common. Changes were significantly more frequent in secondary outcomes (p < 0.01). Severity scores varied widely but were not consistently associated with funder or intervention type. OLI showed weak correlation with modification frequency (r = 0.17).ConclusionsIn this registry-based analysis of BPH clinical trials, outcome modifications were universal, occurred exclusively after primary completion, and were never justified in either the registry or corresponding publications. These findings highlight a persistent gap in transparency that could affect the interpretability of trial results. Greater oversight of registry version histories and stronger disclosure standards are needed to ensure the credibility of outcome reporting in BPH research and beyond.Trial registrationRegistered on the Open Science Framework. Introduction

Prespecified outcomes serve as the foundation for clinical trial design, analysis, and interpretation. To protect the integrity of these prespecified measures, federal policies such as the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act (FDAAA) 801 and the Final Rule (42 CFR Part 11) require investigators to register primary and secondary outcomes on ClinicalTrials.gov before participant enrollment [1, 2]. However, while these regulations promote transparency, they do not prohibit investigators from modifying outcomes throughout a trial. Such modifications (whether to definitions, timing, classification, or scope) can occur at any stage, including after enrollment, after data collection, or even after publication.

Although some outcome changes may be scientifically or clinically justified, others may reflect post hoc adjustments that complicate the interpretation of trial findings [3]. Without a systematic review of registry version histories, these modifications often go undetected, particularly when not explicitly disclosed or justified in peer-reviewed publications. This lack of transparency can obscure trial intent, create interpretive ambiguity, and introduce the potential for selective emphasis or reframing of results [3, 4].

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), a highly prevalent urologic condition among older men, provides an important context in which to examine outcome stability [5]. Clinical trials in BPH often involve patient-reported outcomes, symptom-based scales, and composite endpoints. Unlike objective, physiologically anchored measures such as mortality or specific laboratory thresholds, these outcome types can frequently be operationalized in multiple valid ways—as total scores, subscores, or dichotomized thresholds—and often lack universally agreed-upon definitions of clinically meaningful change. This interpretive flexibility may create conditions under which redefinition, reclassification, or substitution can occur over the course of a trial, whether driven by evolving clinical understanding, statistical considerations, or publication-related pressures. Moreover, variability in how BPH-related outcomes are defined and reported may have direct implications for treatment recommendations, comparative effectiveness assessments, and shared decision-making in clinical care [5, 6].

While previous studies have examined discrepancies between registered and published outcomes, few have leveraged ClinicalTrials.gov’s version history to track longitudinal changes to prespecified outcomes across registry records [4]. In this study, we conducted a registry-based audit of interventional trials for BPH with posted results to evaluate the frequency, type, timing, and transparency of outcome modifications. By comparing each trial’s original and most recent registry entries, we identified modifications such as additions, removals, reclassifications, timing shifts, and rewordings. We also assessed whether these changes were accompanied by disclosure or justification in either the registry or corresponding peer-reviewed publications.

Through this approach, we aimed to characterize how outcome definitions evolve throughout BPH trials and assess the transparency of these modifications. Understanding the dynamics of outcome reporting in BPH research is essential for strengthening the credibility of trial findings and ensuring that real-world treatment decisions are grounded in clearly defined and consistently reported evidence.

Methods

Study design and registration

We compared each trial’s earliest and most recent registry versions to identify net changes to prespecified outcomes. This approach captures the aggregate difference between a trial’s original and final registry states but does not detect modifications that were introduced and later reverted across intermediate versions. A systematic comparison of all intermediate registry versions was beyond the scope of this study. Corresponding peer-reviewed publications were reviewed to determine whether these modifications were transparently disclosed and justified. Our search was restricted to ClinicalTrials.gov for two primary reasons: (1) it is the federally mandated registration platform under FDAAA 801 and the Final Rule for US-conducted trials; and (2) its publicly accessible “History of Changes” feature provides timestamped, field-level version tracking that is not uniformly available across other major international registries, and upon which our extraction methodology directly depends.

Although observational in design, this study followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines due to its systematic approach to trial identification, screening, and data extraction. Our design builds upon prior registry audit literature but differs from traditional selective outcome reporting studies by examining the presence, type, and transparency of longitudinal changes to prespecified outcomes using full registry version histories.

This study did not meet the federal definition of human subjects research under 45 CFR §46.102(d) and (f) and was therefore not subject to additional oversight by the Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences Institutional Review Board [7].

Data source and search strategy

We searched ClinicalTrials.gov on June 28, 2025, to identify interventional trials evaluating treatments for BPH. To ensure consistency with current regulatory requirements, we limited the sample to trials with start dates between January 18, 2017 (the effective date of the Final Rule), and December 31, 2024. Only trials with posted tabular results on ClinicalTrials.gov were eligible. This criterion was applied to ensure trial completion and the availability of a finalized, verifiable registry record—not as a direct proxy for Final Rule compliance. Requiring posted results ensured that each included trial had a fully documented record from which prespecified outcomes could be compared across historical versions.

Data extraction from the ClinicalTrials.gov “History of Changes” feature was completed on July 14–15, 2025, by two independent extractors (TL, MI). For each trial, modification timing was classified by directly comparing the submission timestamp of each registry version against the trial’s recorded primary completion date.

To enhance search specificity, we constructed a Boolean search strategy using MeSH terms and relevant synonyms for BPH, rather than relying solely on ClinicalTrials.gov’s automatic term expansion feature, which is based on the Unified Medical Language System [8]. Filters were applied for study type (interventional), trial phase (phase 2, 3, 4, or “Not Applicable”), and adult male populations. This study was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF), which serves as the protocol of record and hosts the pre-registered search strategy, eligibility criteria, and extraction materials. The study was additionally submitted to PROSPERO [9, 10].

To identify additional eligible trials that may not have appeared through the registry search alone, we reviewed reference lists of included studies, searched pharmaceutical company clinical trial portals (e.g., industry-sponsored registries), and examined systematic reviews on BPH indexed in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. These supplementary strategies were designed to enhance search sensitivity for eligible trials that met registry and results-posting criteria but may have been indexed under alternative terminology. No additional online repositories, gray literature sources, or author correspondence were used. The search was updated following the initial screening phase to ensure a current and complete trial sample.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were interventional clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov that evaluated at least one treatment for BPH or lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) suggestive of BPH. These studies were required to have enrolled adult male participants (aged 18 years and older). Each trial had to include at least one prespecified outcome in both the earliest and most recent registry versions and have posted results on ClinicalTrials.gov. Matched peer-reviewed publications reporting the main trial results were also required for inclusion.

Trials were considered eligible if they assessed one or more of the following intervention categories:Pharmacologic treatments (e.g., alpha-blockers, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, PDE5 inhibitors, or novel agents);Minimally invasive surgical therapies (e.g., UroLift, Rezūm, iTIND, thermal- or laser-based procedures);Traditional surgical interventions (e.g., TURP, HoLEP, PVP, open/simple prostatectomy);Device-based or stent procedures aimed at relieving obstruction or maintaining urethral patency;Diagnostic or imaging modalities (e.g., multiparametric MRI, high-resolution ultrasound, PET) intended to evaluate prostate anatomy or treatment response;Lifestyle, behavioral, or rehabilitation strategies (e.g., pelvic floor training, behavioral therapy, structured exercise programs, or dietary modification).

Trials were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: phase 1 or feasibility studies; observational or non-interventional designs; trials without posted tabular results; or trials without at least one prespecified outcome in both registry versions. We also excluded studies that lacked clear disaggregation of BPH-specific outcomes in mixed populations (e.g., combined BPH and prostate cancer trials) or focused exclusively on prehabilitation or diagnostic device testing without therapeutic application.

It should be noted that “Study Type” and “Primary Purpose” are distinct fields on ClinicalTrials.gov. Our exclusion criterion for observational or non-interventional designs was applied to the “Study Type” field. Two included trials carry “Basic Science” or “Screening” labels under “Primary Purpose” but are classified as “Interventional” under “Study Type” and met all other eligibility criteria for inclusion.

Registry–publication matching

Matched publications were identified using the linked citations provided in each trial’s ClinicalTrials.gov record. Linked citations were identified using the most recent version of each trial’s ClinicalTrials.gov record, as the latest entry was considered most likely to contain a complete and current list of associated publications. In the absence of a linked citation, manual searches were conducted as described below.

When a linked citation was not available, we conducted manual searches in PubMed (via NCBI) and Google Scholar using combinations of the trial’s NCT number, official title, sponsor name, and principal investigator. These steps were taken to ensure comprehensive identification of corresponding peer-reviewed publications for each included trial. To be eligible, publications had to report the main results of the trial and correspond to the timing and outcomes listed in the registry record’s primary completion date. When multiple publications were available, we selected the earliest peer-reviewed article that reported primary trial results consistent with the prespecified outcome timeframe. Gray literature sources, conference abstracts, preprints, author correspondence, or other non-peer-reviewed materials were excluded to maintain consistency with reporting standards and to ensure that transparency assessments were based on finalized, peer-reviewed sources. Matching and eligibility assessments were conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (TL, MI). Discrepancies in matching decisions were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, adjudicated by a third reviewer (KK). All reviewers were masked to each other’s assessments throughout the initial screening and matching process to minimize bias.

Data extraction and training

Prior to data extraction, all reviewers participated in a structured training session that covered the study protocol, outcome classification criteria, and the use of a standardized Google Form designed for data collection. To ensure consistency in interpretation and coding, reviewers completed a calibration phase using a pilot set of 10 trials. Discrepancies during this phase were discussed in group meetings until consensus was reached on classification rules and data entry procedures. Following training, two reviewers (TL, MI) independently extracted data from ClinicalTrials.gov and the matched peer-reviewed publications. All data extraction was performed in a masked and duplicate manner to minimize individual reviewer bias. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or adjudicated by a third reviewer (KK).

Extracted variables included general trial characteristics (e.g., NCT number, sponsor, phase, enrollment size), regulatory metadata, and detailed information on outcome modifications. For each trial, reviewers compared the earliest and most recent versions of the ClinicalTrials.gov record using the publicly available “History of Changes” feature, which provides side-by-side visualization of field-level updates over time.

Outcome modifications were recorded separately for primary, secondary, and other outcomes. Modifications were classified by type (e.g., addition, removal, reclassification, timing shift, measurement change, rewording, or changes in specificity) and timing (pre-enrollment, during enrollment, post–primary completion, post-publication, or no change). The number of revisions and the total number of prespecified outcomes were recorded for each outcome category.

For each trial, reviewers also evaluated whether a rationale or disclosure of outcome modifications was present in either the ClinicalTrials.gov registry record or the matched peer-reviewed publication. Full-text articles, including methods, results, discussion, footnotes, and supplementary materials, were reviewed in detail to identify transparent reporting of any outcome changes. Disclosure status was recorded as a binary variable (“yes” or “no”), and any relevant statements were copied verbatim into the extraction form for internal verification and audit purposes.

Analytic variables

We developed two primary analytic variables to evaluate the complexity and potential interpretive impact of prespecified outcome modifications: the Outcome Load Index (OLI) and a Severity-Weighted Change Score. These variables were used to assess modification patterns across trials and to explore associations with trial-level characteristics such as sponsor type and intervention modality.

Outcome load index

The OLI served as a standardized metric of outcome density, capturing the number of prespecified outcomes relative to trial enrollment size. For each trial, OLI was calculated as:OLI = Total number of prespecified outcomes/Enrollment size

OLI = Total number of prespecified outcomes/Enrollment size

This metric was designed to reflect the reporting burden per participant and the overall complexity of a trial’s analytic space. Trials with higher OLI values may be more prone to outcome modifications due to interpretive flexibility, statistical fragility, or evolving analytic priorities over time.

Severity-weighted change score

To quantify the potential impact of outcome modifications on trial interpretability, we developed a severity-weighted scoring system. Building on guidance from SPIRIT, CONSORT-Harms, and prior meta-research, we applied greater weights (2.5–3.0) to outcome modifications with higher potential impact—such as additions, removals, or reclassifications (for example, a primary outcome being downgraded to secondary) [3, 11, 12]. Lesser weights (1.0–1.5) were applied to modifications considered less consequential, including timing shifts and changes in measurement instruments. For each trial, we coded the presence (1) or absence (0) of predefined change types. The severity score for each trial was calculated using the following formula:Severity Score = ∑ (Flagi × Weighti) where Flagi = indicator for presence of change, and Weighti = assigned weight for the change type.

This score reflects both the volume and seriousness of outcome modifications and was used in correlation analyses with the OLI, as well as in subgroup comparisons stratified by sponsor and intervention type. The full weighting scheme is provided in Table S1.

Cochran’s Q tests were used to formally test the hypothesis that the proportion of trials exhibiting each modification type did not differ across primary, secondary, and other outcome categories within the same trial. This within-trial design—comparing paired binary indicators across the three outcome category levels—controls for trial-level variability and makes Cochran’s Q the appropriate statistical test for this comparison. Where Cochran’s Q indicated significant differences (p < 0.05), pairwise McNemar’s tests were conducted as pre-specified post hoc comparisons; these are presented in Supplementary Table 2 and should be interpreted in the context of multiple comparisons.

Stratified Kaplan–Meier time-to-event curves were presented for descriptive purposes. Formal between-group statistical comparisons (e.g., log-rank tests) were not performed, as the subgroup sample sizes available for comparison were modest and the analysis was designed to characterize temporal patterns visually rather than to test distributional equivalence.

We used Kaplan–Meier time-to-event analysis to assess the time span from the originally prespecified outcome to the most recent version listed on ClinicalTrials.gov, providing a measure of how long outcome definitions remained unchanged. The time-to-event curves represent the proportion of trials that had not yet reported an outcome change over time, restricted to trials with at least one documented modification. Comparisons were stratified by sponsor type (e.g., industry vs. non-industry) and intervention type (e.g., pharmacologic, surgical, device-based, or behavioral). To assess whether trial complexity was associated with the volume or severity of modifications, we evaluated correlations between the Outcome Load Index (OLI) and both (1) the total number of outcome modifications and (2) the Severity-Weighted Change Score. These relationships were analyzed using Pearson correlation and unadjusted linear regression. Pearson correlation was selected to maintain analytical consistency between the correlation and the accompanying regression analysis. Results

Identification and inclusion of eligible studies

Through ClinicalTrials.gov’s registry search, we retrieved a total of 1383 records. Of these, 1327 records were excluded. The remaining 56 underwent full-text publication screening, during which an additional 20 records were excluded. The remaining 36 studies went through full data extraction and statistical analysis. The full details of the screening and selection process can be viewed in Supplemental Fig. S1.

General characteristics

Table 1 shows a summary of general trial characteristics. Among the 36 clinical trials reviewed, the largest proportion, nearly 40% (14/36), specified a “Not Applicable” phase, while phase 4 studies were the least common (11.1%; 4/36). The majority of trials, approximately three-quarters (26/36), reported using randomization, while the remaining were unspecified. Regarding intervention type, half (18/36) of the studies focused on pharmacologic treatments, while 41.7% (15/36) evaluated device-based therapies. Industry funding was the most common, supporting the majority of trials (61.1%; 22/36), while academic, government, and private organizations each contributed to just one trial in the dataset (2.8%; 1/36 each).

Did trials modify their prespecified outcomes?

Every trial in the cohort made at least one modification to its listed outcomes. Notably, none of these changes was documented before the studies began or even during the active data collection period. Instead, all outcome modifications were reported after the primary completion date. Half of the trials introduced changes after data collection had ended but before publication (50.0%; 18/36), while the other half modified outcomes only after the trial results had already been published (50.0%; 18/36). On average, trials underwent two outcome revisions, with some having as many as six. Despite the prevalence and potential implications of these post hoc modifications, not a single trial acknowledged them in its corresponding journal article or provided explanatory details on ClinicalTrials.gov, underscoring a broader issue of unjustified outcome reporting changes. As shown in Table 3, outcome modifications varied significantly across outcome categories (primary, secondary, and other) for several types of change. Minor rewording was the most common modification, occurring in 91.7% of trials for primary outcomes and 69.4% for secondary outcomes, with a significant difference across categories (Cochran’s Q = 50.42, p < 0.001). Broad-to-specific definitional shifts were also frequent, appearing in 80.6% of trials for primary outcomes and 66.7% for secondary outcomes, and significantly different by outcome category (Q = 43.16, p < 0.001). Changes in the timing of outcome assessment, as well as additions and removals of outcomes, also showed statistically significant variation across outcome types (p < 0.01 for each), with secondary outcomes experiencing a disproportionately higher number of additions and removals. In contrast, modifications such as measurement instrument changes and reclassification (e.g., moving outcomes to or from primary/secondary status) were less common and did not reach statistical significance. Overall, these findings suggest that secondary outcomes are more frequently and substantively modified than primary or “other” outcomes, highlighting a potential area of concern for transparency and interpretability in trial reporting. When did outcome changes occur?

To evaluate how outcome changes unfolded over time, we conducted Kaplan–Meier time-to-event analysis of outcome stability. Figure 1A presents the overall time to first outcome change across all trials with a documented modification. The probability of retaining the originally prespecified outcome steadily declined over time, with fewer than half of trials maintaining unchanged outcomes by 2000 days after registration. By approximately 5000 days, most trials had registered at least one change. Modifications occurring at such extended intervals may be expected given long follow-up periods and administrative updates. This analysis describes the timing of changes but does not evaluate their rationale or impact.

To assess whether these patterns differed by intervention type, we stratified trials by their primary intervention category (Fig. 1B). Device trials exhibited a more rapid decline in outcome stability compared to drug trials. Over 75% of device trials had registered at least one outcome change within the first 2000 days, whereas drug trials showed a more gradual curve, with some maintaining unchanged outcomes beyond 4000 days. While the underlying reasons for these differences were not examined in this analysis, these findings suggest that the timing of prespecified outcome changes may vary by intervention type.

How severe were the changes?

To assess not just how frequently outcomes were changed, but how substantively, we calculated a severity-weighted change score for each trial. As shown in Fig. 3, severity-weighted scores varied substantially across trials but did not show consistent differences by funder type. Median scores were similar among industry, academic, and mixed-funded trials, with overlapping interquartile ranges and wide distributions. Government and privately funded trials included several high-scoring outliers, but limited representation precludes strong inference. When stratified by intervention type, drug and device trials exhibited comparable median severity scores. Drug trials demonstrated greater variability, with several scoring in the upper range. Procedure-based and biologic interventions had too few data points to support meaningful comparison. What types of outcome modifications were made?

We examined how the types of outcome modifications varied by outcome category. As shown in Table 3, minor rewording and broad-to-specific definitional refinements were the most frequently observed changes. Minor rewording occurred in 91.7% (33/36) of trials with primary outcome changes and 69.4% (25/36) of those with secondary outcome changes, while broad-to-specific edits were recorded in 37 primary and 159 secondary outcomes. Both modification types varied significantly across outcome categories (Cochran’s Q = 50.42 and Q = 43.16, respectively; p < 0.001), with a higher frequency of broad-to-specific changes in secondary versus primary outcomes (McNemar’s p = 0.013).

Less common modifications included additions, removals, and reclassifications. Additions were most frequent in secondary outcomes (65), compared to 7 in primary and 3 in “other” outcomes (Q = 12.93, p = 0.002). Removals were rare but showed a similar distribution, with 14 secondary and 2 primary outcomes deleted (Q = 12.29, p = 0.002). Only four reclassifications between primary and secondary status were observed.

As shown in Table 3, outcome modifications varied significantly across outcome categories for several change types with adequate cell representation. For change types with fewer than three affected trials in any outcome category (removed, measurement instrument changed, moved to primary, moved to secondary), Cochran’s Q results are not interpretable and those rows should be read as descriptive frequencies only.

Were these changes predictable?

To explore whether trials with more densely specified outcomes were more likely to undergo outcome modifications, we examined the association between the OLI and the total number of outcome changes. As shown in Fig. 2, there was a weak and non-significant correlation between OLI and total modifications (r = 0.17, p = 0.33), indicating that trials with a higher outcome-to-participant ratio were not consistently associated with more frequent outcome changes. Most trials fell within quadrant III, reflecting low OLI and few outcome modifications. A sizable number of trials, however, appeared in quadrant I, characterized by low OLI but high modification frequency. This suggests that trials with fewer prespecified outcomes per participant were still prone to substantial outcome revisions. In contrast, relatively few trials were located in quadrant II, where both OLI and the number of changes were high, or in quadrant IV, which represents trials with high OLI but relatively few changes.

Discussion

Our analysis of 36 BPH trials revealed that all exhibited at least one prespecified outcome modification, with none transparently justified in registry records or publications. All changes occurred after data collection, split evenly between post-primary completion and post-publication periods. Secondary outcomes were significantly more likely to be added or removed than primary outcomes, suggesting greater post hoc flexibility. Outcome changes occurred regardless of funder or intervention type, highlighting a general need for stronger standards to improve transparency and preserve the interpretability of trial findings.

The timing and nature of outcome modifications identified in our study have important implications for trial interpretability and research integrity. In all cases, registry updates reflecting outcome changes were submitted after trial completion, a timepoint at which access to unblinded data is likely. While we cannot determine whether the decisions to modify outcomes were made earlier and simply documented later, the absence of timely documentation in either the registry or publication raises concerns. Delayed or undocumented modifications leave open the possibility of post hoc changes based on emerging data, increasing the risk of publication bias, and p-hacking. Our findings in BPH trials mirror those in other clinical domains. Registry-to-publication protocol divergence has been documented in other clinical domains: a cross-sectional study of 112 MS trials found that 26.6% of registered protocol elements were present in publications but absent from registry records, reflecting substantial undisclosed modification between registry and publication [15]. Prior meta-research has shown that such changes often follow null or unfavorable findings and may reflect efforts to reframe results for more favorable interpretation or publication. In our sample, 77.8% of trials included post hoc additions or removals of outcomes, which may allow selective emphasis on statistically significant findings that were not prespecified. This undermines the principle of analytic prespecification that trial registries are designed to safeguard. International guidelines, including SPIRIT and the CONSORT extension for harms, emphasize that adding, removing, or reclassifying primary outcomes after trial initiation constitutes a major protocol deviation and may compromise the validity of trial results.

While retrospective outcome modifications can be justified, the complete lack of disclosed rationale across all trials in our study is concerning. Without context, readers are left to speculate about the reasons for these changes, which undermines trust in the findings. Even seemingly administrative edits, such as rewording or narrowing outcome definitions, were frequent and unjustified, raising questions about whether these changes influenced trial interpretation. In BPH research, where outcomes often rely on subjective measures, even minor definitional shifts can affect significance and clinical meaning. The absence of justification in both registries and publications suggests that clinicians and reviewers could be relying on an unstable evidence base. The presence of unjustified changes made after data collection signals a breakdown in transparency and prespecified analysis safeguards.

The disproportionate modification of secondary outcomes observed in our study may reflect their unique position within the trial reporting hierarchy. Unlike primary outcomes, which are typically the focus of regulatory scrutiny, sample size calculations, and protocol enforcement, secondary outcomes are often regarded as exploratory and are subject to less stringent oversight. This relative flexibility may make them more susceptible to post hoc adjustments, especially when primary outcomes yield null results. Additionally, secondary outcomes are frequently defined with less precision at the time of registration, creating opportunities for later rewording, refinement, or reclassification. Logistical challenges, such as data quality issues or participant attrition, may also disproportionately affect secondary outcomes, prompting investigators to modify or remove them during analysis or manuscript preparation. Finally, pressures to frame findings in a favorable light, whether internally driven or in response to peer review, may lead to the selective highlighting or reinterpretation of secondary outcomes. Together, these factors help explain why secondary outcomes are more vulnerable to modification and underscore the importance of applying transparency standards to all prespecified outcomes, not just those designated as primary.

We introduced the OLI as a proxy measure for trial complexity, defined as the number of prespecified outcomes relative to enrollment size. We aimed to evaluate whether trials with more densely specified outcomes were more prone to instability or revision. However, the correlation between OLI and total outcome modifications was weak and not statistically significant (r = 0.17, p = 0.33), suggesting that outcome density alone does not meaningfully predict the likelihood or severity of prespecified outcome changes. This finding contrasts with the intuitive assumption that more complex trials, i.e., those with extensive outcome lists, are at greater risk of redefinition or post hoc adjustment. Several trials with low OLI values (i.e., relatively few outcomes per participant) still underwent frequent and substantive changes. These results suggest that trial complexity, at least as measured by OLI, is not a reliable indicator of outcome stability and that other factors, such as institutional practices, data management strategies, or publication pressures, may better explain patterns of outcome modification. While OLI remains a potentially useful tool for identifying outcome-dense trials, our findings emphasize that transparency lapses are not limited to high-complexity studies and call for consistent safeguards across all trials, regardless of design burden.

To capture both the frequency and interpretive impact of outcome modifications, we created a Severity-Weighted Change Score based on the type and presumed significance of each change. While trials varied in their severity scores, we found no consistent differences across funding sources or intervention types, suggesting that high-severity changes are not confined to any particular sponsor or trial category. Notably, some industry-funded and pharmacologic trials did include high outliers, but overall, the distribution of severity scores was broad and overlapping across groups. This indicates that the risk of making impactful, potentially bias-inducing changes to prespecified outcomes is widespread, rather than isolated to particular trial structures or funding arrangements. Importantly, many of the most severe modifications, such as the addition, removal, or redefinition of primary outcomes, occurred after trial completion or publication, yet none was disclosed or justified in publication. These findings reinforce the need for improved safeguards around high-impact outcome modifications, including standardized disclosure practices and editorial checks that account for registry version histories. Without such mechanisms, readers and clinicians may be unaware of substantial, unjustified changes that have the ability to alter the interpretation of trial results.

To address these concerns, we recommend that peer-reviewed publications disclose outcome version histories and provide explicit justifications for any modifications to registered outcomes. Clinical trial registries should require reflexive explanations for all outcome changes, with these documented in the version histories available on ClinicalTrials.gov. Journals and peer reviewers should routinely consult these histories to ensure analytic concordance. Regulatory bodies such as the FDA and ICMJE could strengthen reporting standards by requiring audit trails for prespecified outcomes, extending transparency from registration to registry evolution. Technically, ClinicalTrials.gov could implement visual difference tools or automated change summaries to enhance usability and interpretation. Beyond procedural reforms, the standardization of outcome selection through Core Outcome Sets (COSs) offers a more durable solution [16]. COSs have already improved consistency in urologic oncology trials, reducing outcome heterogeneity and supporting evidence synthesis. For example, MacLennan and Williamson highlight how inconsistent outcomes in prostate cancer trials have impeded meta-analyses and guideline development. Similarly, Wang et al. documented wide variation in how BPH trials report IPSS components, reinforcing the need for structured standardization [17]. A COS initiative in BPH would promote consistency in outcome reporting, discourage post hoc changes, and ensure alignment with outcomes that matter most to patients and clinicians. Rather than limiting innovation, COSs provide a common foundation that enhances reproducibility, improves comparability, and reduces interpretive ambiguity across trials.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is the first to systematically evaluate longitudinal changes to prespecified outcomes in BPH trials using full ClinicalTrials.gov version histories. By analyzing both the timing and type of modifications, and introducing quantitative metrics such as the Severity-Weighted Change Score and Outcome Load Index, we offer a more detailed assessment than prior studies using static registry snapshots. Our data were extracted in masked duplicate procedure, and all materials were publicly archived to ensure transparency and reproducibility. However, the study has limitations. Our sample was restricted to trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, which limits the generalizability of findings to non-US or dually registered trials. While several international registries record registration updates, the granularity and accessibility of version history data vary considerably across platforms. Our extraction methodology was specifically designed around ClinicalTrials.gov’s “History of Changes” feature, and extending this approach to other registries with comparable functionality represents an important direction for future research. Individual field-level modification timestamps were not extracted as a discrete study-level variable; while timing classifications were based on direct review of version-specific submission dates, future studies should consider extracting and reporting per-trial modification timestamps to further strengthen reproducibility. The Severity-Weighted Change Score is a novel construct developed for this analysis. While its design was informed by established reporting frameworks including SPIRIT and CONSORT-Harms, the weights assigned to specific modification types are not empirically validated and involve a degree of expert judgment. The transformation of complex, qualitatively distinct outcome modifications into a single continuous severity scale is inherently reductive, and differences in scores between trials should be interpreted with appropriate caution. External validation of this scoring approach across broader trial populations and disease areas is a necessary priority for future research before this metric is adopted as a standardized tool. Additionally, we did not extract whether trials were registered prior to their enrollment start date. Prospective registration is a core element of outcome prespecification integrity, and future registry-based studies should evaluate registration timing alongside outcome modification patterns to provide a more complete picture of compliance with transparency standards. We also did not assess the clinical impact of modifications or whether changes were disclosed or justified through internal channels such as IRB filings. Our extraction approach compared the earliest and most recent registry versions for each trial, capturing net outcome changes but not intermediate edits or reverted modifications. Future studies should consider extracting and comparing all available intermediate versions using the full ClinicalTrials.gov version history to more completely characterize the trajectory of outcome changes over time. Future implications may further explore a limitation of this study: this study did not evaluate whether the outcomes reported in each trial’s matched publication aligned with those in either the original or most recent registry entry. A systematic comparison of registry-to-publication outcome concordance would require a distinct extraction framework and represents an important direction for future research, particularly for the 18 trials in which registry modifications occurred after the publication date. The stratified time-to-event analysis by intervention type was descriptive in nature; formal log-rank testing between drug and device trial subgroups was not conducted. Given subgroup sizes of n = 18 and n = 15 respectively, a log-rank comparison would have limited statistical power, and formal hypothesis testing was not consistent with the descriptive intent of this analysis.

Conclusion

In this registry-based analysis of BPH clinical trials, outcome modifications were universal, occurred exclusively after primary completion, and were never justified in either the registry or corresponding publications. These findings highlight a persistent gap in transparency that could affect the interpretability of trial results in BPH research. Greater oversight of registry version histories and stronger disclosure standards are needed to ensure the credibility of outcome reporting. Whether these patterns extend to other urologic conditions or disease areas remains an important question for future registry-based research.
