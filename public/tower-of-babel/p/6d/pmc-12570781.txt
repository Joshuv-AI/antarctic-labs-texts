Can Fine‐Root Non‐Structural Carbohydrates Explain Seasonal Variations in the Respiration in Subalpine Forests?
Authors: Yuki Hashimoto, Taiga Masumoto, Takumi Ito, Koichi Takahashi, Naoki Makita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFine root respiration drives root growth and resource acquisition in cold, nutrient‐poor ecosystems, yet its association with root phenology remains unclear. Non‐structural carbohydrates (NSC), stored as soluble sugars and starch are primary metabolites that play key roles in physiological functions. Analyzing NSC pools and availability contributes to the understanding of the seasonality of root respiration. Here, we examined seasonal variations in fine‐root respiration, sugars and starch for Abies mariesii and Betula ermanii, from spring to autumn at 2000 and 2500 m in subalpine forests. Additionally, specific root length and root nitrogen concentrations were evaluated. Root respiration rates became higher, mostly following soil temperature. Sugar concentrations were the highest in spring and the lowest in summer. In autumn, sugar accumulated earlier in the fine roots of A. mariesii than in B. ermanii. Starch concentrations and the root functional traits did not show significant seasonal variations. Significant relationships between root respiration and sugars were found in spring for B. ermanii and in autumn for A. mariesii. Furthermore, root respiration and sugars were correlated positively at 2000 m and negatively at 2500 m for both species. These differences explain that B. ermanii actively consumes stored sugars for root respiration to facilitate root elongation and resource acquisition associated with spring foliation, particularly in a shorter growing season. In contrast, A. mariesii suppresses sugar consumption via root respiration because improving cold tolerance before dormancy is critical for survival at the treeline. Therefore, NSCs play different roles in the seasonality of fine roots and determine the species‐specific patterns of temperature‐independent root respiration.Keywords: elevational response, root phenology, root respiration, soluble sugar, starch

Introduction

Plant root respiration is a major component of soil respiration (Davidson et al. 2006). Of the 44 Pg year−1 Carbon (C) efflux from the soil surface (Tang et al. 2019), fine roots (< 2 mm in diameter) contribute to nearly half of it despite their small biomass (Sun et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2022). Respiration by fine roots is a fundamental physiological process for acquiring soil nutrients and water for plant growth and maintenance (Pregitzer et al. 2000; Schmiege et al. 2023). Fine root respiration shows significant temporal variation, which is affected by both biotic and abiotic factors, such as belowground C allocation (Kuzyakov and Gavrichkova 2010), soil temperature (Atkin et al. 2000; Atkinson et al. 2007), soil moisture (Ryhti et al. 2022; Du et al. 2023), and nutrient availability (Burton et al. 2012). In subalpine forests with low soil temperatures and poor resource availability, soil CO2 emissions seasonally vary, owing to the dynamics of respiration of fine roots and microorganisms (Díaz‐Pinés et al. 2010). Previous studies have shown that soil temperature is the most influential factor causing temporal variation in root respiration (Atkin et al. 2000, 2005; Davidson et al. 2006). Conceptually, respiration rates increase exponentially with soil temperature (Makita et al. 2021). However, the response of root respiration to temperature differs between seasons (Vose and Ryan 2002; Ruehr and Buchmann 2009). Franck et al. (2011) showed that the respiration rates at 20°C by fine roots of Vitis vinifera were higher in dormant seasons than in growing seasons. In contrast, Liang et al. (2010) showed that the temperature sensitivity of autotrophic respiration was the highest in early summer in a larch forest. These evidences suggest a strong link between root respiration and plant seasonality, as well as temperature‐dependent CO2 emissions, but the information on the biological factors, influencing the respiration, is still lacking.

The phenology of perennial plants is characterized by spring foliation and autumn leaf senescence. In the belowground, root phenology is characterized by a complex pulse of root production and biomass (Steinaker et al. 2010; McCormack et al. 2014). Root respiration rates increase with root growth in the early growing season because of the maintenance and structural development of elongated roots (Cui et al. 2021; Hirano et al. 2023). In addition, variability in the C balance and allocation in trees influences root respiration rates (Davidson and Holbrook 2009; Kuzyakov and Gavrichkova 2010; Savage et al. 2013). Because the optimal season for growth in subalpine forests is short, trees must grow efficiently. In this case, the phenological pattern of root respiration should be adjusted to optimize assimilated C consumption and maximize growth and resource acquisition under variable soil conditions. The stored surplus C supports physiological functions involving tree growth and phenology (Prescott et al. 2020) and might be the major source of root respiration. However, little is known about how surplus‐assimilated C is stored and utilized for respiration in fine roots at different phenological stages.

To truly understand seasonal variations in root respiration, non‐structural carbohydrates (NSC) in fine roots provide new insights. Woody plants store surplus C as NSC, which are the primary metabolites and labile C fraction produced through photosynthesis. NSC are stored in various organs over time scales ranging from minutes to decades (Richardson et al. 2015; Aubrey and Teskey 2018; Tixier et al. 2018) and are available for physiological processes and biomass construction (Hartmann and Trumbore 2016). As substrates of plant growth and metabolism, NSC dynamics are critical for evaluating C economy at both the individual roots and whole‐tree levels. NSC are mainly composed of soluble sugars and insoluble starch. In fine roots, sugars are used as immediate energy sources for growth and respiration, and/or serve as osmolytes for maintaining resource transportation (Hashimoto et al. 2023). Starch is a long‐term storage reservoir for surplus sugars. Previous studies that investigated NSC dynamics in whole trees indicated that sugar concentrations become remarkably lower in summer and higher in winter in the leaves and branches of several evergreen coniferous and deciduous broad‐leaved trees (Zhu et al. 2012; Furze et al. 2019). Starch shows the opposite seasonal variation, becoming higher in summer and lower in winter in branches, stems, and coarse roots. Martínez‐Vilalta et al. (2016), which attempted to generalize the seasonal variation in NSC, showed a complex pulse of sugars and starch storage, especially in coarse and fine roots. However, seasonal variations in NSC are more complex due to the differences in the storage among organs, plant functional types, and environments (Fajardo et al. 2012; Fermaniuk et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2024). It remains unclear how the variation in the NSC pool and its availability affects root physiological capability, represented by respiration rate.

Recent studies support the idea that NSC are actively stored in trees to adapt to environmental changes. Zhou et al. (2021) demonstrated that subalpine trees preferentially allocate the assimilated C to the roots or branches, storing it in the form of sugar and starch, rather than using them for growth and respiration, in order to improve cold tolerance and leaf maintenance. In addition, He et al. (2024) indicated that C allocation to NSC storage is prioritized over structural growth in treeline trees. Blumstein et al. (2023), which synthesized global NSC data, suggested clear temporal changes in the two functional roles of NSC: as sources of surplus C and as a buffer against stresses such as low temperature and drought by maintaining osmotic potential. In fact, the sugar proportion in the total NSC of aboveground and belowground organs was higher in response to coldness (Blumstein et al. 2023). Blumstein et al. (2023) also explained that NSC likely serves as a buffer, rather than as a surplus C source, in the tradeoff between growth and survival. Thus, subalpine trees under stressful conditions can prioritize storing NSC and controlling respiration rates of fine roots to optimize C consumption in their phenological cycle. If so, temporal changes in the roles of NSC in fine roots may cause the species‐specific patterns of root respiration that are independent of temperature. From these perspectives, we hypothesized that root respiration is closely linked to sugar storage to promote root growth or suppress C consumption during typical phenological events, such as spring foliation and autumn leaf senescence for deciduous trees, and improvement of cold tolerance before winter for evergreen trees. Whereas, it is also expected that starch, as a long‐term storage reservoir, influences fine root respiration over longer time scales.

Our objective was to clarify the seasonal variations in respiration and NSC storage of tree fine roots and their relationship from spring to autumn in subalpine forests. It has been predicted that the role of root NSC changes from a buffer for overwintering to a surplus C source for root growth in the early growing season, which promotes C consumption. In autumn, the decline in assimilated C supply from canopy and temperature decrease should allow NSC storage to serve as a buffer for improving cold tolerance. We used different elevation sites, including an alpine treeline, to understand how the NSC consumption by fine root respiration is optimized in the long‐term environmental adaptation. We targeted two different types of subalpine trees, Abies mariesii and Betula ermanii, with different leaf habits (an evergreen conifer and deciduous broadleaf, respectively) and taxonomies (gymnosperm and angiosperm, respectively). Interspecific differences in NSC storage are associated with leaf habits and tissue structure (Richardson et al. 2015; Furze et al. 2019). Furze et al. (2019) showed a larger capacity and fluctuation of sugar and starch pools in the aboveground woody organs of deciduous trees than in evergreen trees. Additionally, we examined root morphological and chemical traits such as specific root length (SRL) and root nitrogen (N) concentration, which are common indicators of the potential for root physiology and resource foraging (Bergmann et al. 2020). These root functional traits correlate with root respiration and explain root adaptability to various environments (Makita et al. 2015, 2016). Evaluation of SRL revealed a phenological shift in C investment for structural development and maintenance in root phenology (Comas and Eissenstat 2004). Root N content is related to root metabolic capacity (Makita et al. 2009; Han and Zhu 2021), and its evaluation is useful for clarifying resource allocation and physiological activity at different phenological stages (Vose and Ryan 2002; Yuan and Chen 2010). Accordingly, we tested the above hypothesis and explored the following questions: (1) Are there any interspecific differences in the seasonal variations of fine root respiration, NSC, and functional traits? (2) Do the relationships between root respiration and each NSC vary across seasons, and how are they associated with tree phenology? (3) How are long‐term environmental responses, such as elevation reflected in NSC storage?

Material and Methods

Study Site and Species Selection

This study was conducted at Mt. Norikura in central Japan (36°06′ N, 137°33′ E, 3026 m a.s.l.). The study sites were located at 2000 and 2500 m in the subalpine forest zone on the eastern slope of the mountain. Several studies have been conducted on tree ecophysiology at these sites (e.g., Takahashi and Miyajima 2008; Masumoto et al. 2025). The natural vegetation types on the eastern slope of Mt. Norikura are cool‐temperate deciduous broad‐leaved forest (1100–1600 m), subalpine coniferous forest (1600–2500 m), and alpine shrub forests of dwarf pine ( Pinus pumila , 2500–2800 m). The 2500 m site is also classified as an alpine treeline (cf. Körner 2012). The subalpine forests comprise Betula ermanii (1600–2500 m), Abies veitchii (1600–2300 m), Abies mariesii (1900–2500 m), and other tree species, such as Tsuga diversifolia (1600–2000 m; Miyajima et al. 2007). The trees at 2500 m are shorter than those found at 2000 m, owing to the lower temperatures and strong winds. At the 2500 m sites, the trees and shrubs represented by dwarf pine are mixed with several Ericaceae shrubs growing at this elevation (e.g., Vaccinium vitis‐idaea ). Mt. Norikura had a warm temperate monsoon climate. Rainfall occurred throughout the year, with two peaks in the summer due to the Asian monsoon: the early summer “Baiu” front season and the late summer typhoon season. The mean annual air temperatures from June to October in 2022 and 2023 were 14.1°C at 2000 m and 11.4°C at 2500 m. The mean annual precipitation from 2022 to 2023 was 1737 mm, as observed at the Nagawa Weather Station (36°05′ N, 137°41′E, 1068 m a.s.l.), at a distance of approximately 10 km from Mt. Norikura. The soil properties at 2000 and 2500 m were as follows: total dissolved nitrogen contents were 89.6 and 60.2 mg kg−1, dissolved organic nitrogen contents were 64.0 and 39.9 mg kg−1, pH values were 4.1 and 4.2, EC values were 9.4 and 10.9 mS m−1, C/N ratios were 20.9 and 21.7, respectively (Masumoto et al. 2025).

We selected A. mariesii, an evergreen conifer, and B. ermanii, a deciduous broad‐leaved tree, for the survey because they were the dominant tree species at both elevations (Miyajima et al. 2007; Takahashi et al. 2012). A. mariesii, which has a higher shade tolerance than any other fir species (Suzuki and Takahashi 2020) is a late successional species, whereas B. ermanii is an early successional species. The fine roots of both species are symbiotic with the ectomycorrhizal fungi.

Climate and Soil Data Collection

Seasonal variations in air and soil temperatures and soil moisture were monitored at 2000 and 2500 m. We installed two thermometers (LR5001, HIOKI) at a height of 1.0 m above the ground at each elevation to avoid direct sunlight exposure. At each site, we buried three soil thermometers (RC‐5, Elitech Technology Inc.) at a soil depth of 10 cm near the targeted trees, ensuring that no puddles had formed. Air and soil temperatures were recorded at half‐hourly intervals.

Fine Root Collection

Focusing on the phenological stages of the trees, we collected fine root samples in June, July, August, September, and October at 2000 m and in July, August, and September at 2500 m in 2022 and 2023, and from leaf development to leaf fall of B. ermanii at each elevation. We categorized June as spring, July to September as summer, and October as autumn at 2000 m. At 2500 m, July was categorized as spring, August as summer, and September as autumn. The root samples were collected monthly.

We selected 10 mature, healthy trees of each species from a 50 × 50 m plot at each elevation. The mean stem diameters at 100 cm height from the soil surface of A. mariesii were 42.2 cm at 2000 m and 16.7 cm at 2500 m, and those of B. ermanii were 18.5 cm at 2000 m and 23.8 cm at 2500 m. The tree heights of A. mariesii were 18.0 m at 2000 m and 4.0 m at 2500 m, and those of B. ermanii were 13.1 m at 2000 m and 6.0 m at 2500 m (Masumoto et al. 2025). Within 1.5 m of each target tree, following the initial removal of surface soil litter, we excavated intact fine roots of 1–4 orders at a depth of 0–10 cm. Fine roots still attached to the trees were carefully isolated from the soil to prevent damage to the roots. Living fine roots were identified based on their color and elasticity. Roots exposed to the soil were cut from their mother roots using pruning shears. A single fine‐root system was collected from each tree. During each of the assessed months, we collected 10 fine root samples from each species at the two elevations for subsequent analysis. The collected roots were immediately placed in a bath filled with tap water at 20°C and the adhering soil and organic matter were gently rinsed off. The entire procedure from cutting to washing the roots took only a few minutes, and we ensured that the measurements of root respiration commenced as soon as possible to preserve the freshness of the collected roots.

Root Respiration Measurements

We measured in situ root respiration rates using a closed chamber system following the method in Makita et al. (2009). The chamber was attached to an infrared gas analyzer (GMP343, Vaisala) coupled with a recording logger (LR5042 DC, 5 V, HIOKI). A fine root sample was immediately enclosed in the chamber after the sampling, and the concentrations of CO2 in the chamber were measured for 10 min. After the respiration measurement, the root temperatures were measured using an infrared thermometer. Considering the air disturbance in the chamber, we used the data from 5 min in the middle of the measurement time, avoiding the data from the first and last 1 min. Given the collected roots were isolated from the soil and exposed to air, the measured respiration rates differed slightly from those under natural conditions. To eliminate this difference, we standardized the respiration rates. The standardized respiration rates refer to those at the natural soil temperature during each measurement. In this regard, we used the generalized Q 10 formulas (Equations 1 and 2) relating to root respiration and temperature provided by Atkin and Tjoelker (2003), in which T soil in Equation (1) is the soil temperature in each season. Since root respiration can acclimate to soil temperature within several days (Loveys et al. 2003; Atkin et al. 2005), T soil was calculated as a 7 days moving average (±3 days around the sampling date) to provide the representative values for each season. The calculated Q 10 values obtained using Equation (1) were substituted in Equation (2), where R 1 and R 2 are the respiration rates at temperatures T 1 and T 2, respectively, with T 2 being higher than T 1. For each season, in which we compared the root temperature with T soil, the root temperatures and measured respiration rates were substituted into T 1 or T 2 and R 1 or R 2. Then, we obtained the standardized respiration rates. All the root samples were transported to the laboratory in a cooler (< 4°C) for further measurements.

Root Morphological Analyses

The root samples were washed with distilled water in the laboratory before analysis. For root morphological traits, fine roots were scanned at 16‐bit gray scale and 400 dpi using a scanner (DS‐G20000; EPSON). The total root length (m) and volume (cm3) of each root sample were analyzed using WinRHIZO Pro 2013 (Regent Instruments). The root samples were freeze‐dried for 48 h (FD‐1000; EYELA) and weighed. The specific root length (SRL; m g−1) was calculated as the dry mass divided by the total root length divided by the dry mass. Root tissue density (RTD; g cm−3) was calculated as the total root volume divided by the dry mass.

Root Chemical Analyses

After morphological analysis, the dried root samples were ground into a fine powder and analyzed for root N concentration and NSC as a percentage of dry mass (% d.m.). The N concentration was measured using an elemental analyzer (Flash EA 1112, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Soluble sugar and starch concentrations were measured using a modified version of the phenol‐sulfuric acid method (Landhäusser et al. 2018). Briefly, the 30 mg of powdered root sample was extracted with 80% (v/v) hot ethanol at 90°C for 10 min, then the supernatants were collected and used for sugar quantification. The residue was rinsed twice using the same hot ethanol procedure. After that, the residual was incubated with α‐amylase solution for 3 h and with NaOAc‐amyloglucosidase buffer for 1.5 h. The supernatants were collected and used for starch quantification. To each solution including sugar and starch we added 2% phenol and 95% sulfuric acid, then the absorbances of the samples were measured at a wavelength of 490 nm with a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sugar and starch concentrations were calculated using the standard curves of the glucose solution and expressed as the percentage of dry mass (% d.m.). The total NSC concentration was calculated as the sum of sugar and starch concentrations.

Statistical Analyses

We calculated the mean values of root respiration rates, NSC concentration, and morphological and chemical traits in each season, and those seasonal variations were evaluated. All statistical analyses were performed using the R 4.1.2 software (R Development Core Team 2021). The effects of season and elevation on root respiration, sugar and starch concentration, total NSC, SRL, and root N concentration were analyzed using linear models. In these models, season (spring, summer, and autumn) and elevation were used as fixed factors (p < 0.05). Model assumptions were evaluated using DHARMa simulations and residual diagnostic plots (Q‐Q plots and residuals vs. fitted values). Although minor deviations from the model assumptions were observed in some cases, sensitivity analyses indicated that these did not substantially affect model interpretation (Table S1 and Figure S3). The relationships between root respiration and sugar and starch concentrations were examined by a standardized major axis (SMA) regression analysis (Warton et al. 2006) using the “smatr” package of the R software, considering that both x and y variables were subject to error. The relationships between SRL and sugar and starch concentrations were also examined using SMA regression analysis.

Seasonal Variation of Root Functional Traits

We did not find any seasonal variation in SRL, which was not significant for A. mariesii (Table 1, p = 0.197) and B. ermanii (Table 1, p = 0.893). The average values of SRL of A. mariesii were 9.7 m g−1 at 2000 m and 7.9 m g1 at 2500 m, whereas those of B. ermanii were 21.8 m g−1 at 2000 m and 28.3 m g1 at 2500 m (Figure 2a,b). Elevation had a significant effect on the SRL of A. mariesii (Table 1, p < 0.001).

The average N concentration in A. mariesii was 2.6% at 2000 m and 2.4% at 2500 m, whereas that in B. ermanii was 2.7% at 2000 m and 2.6% at 2500 m (Figure 2c,d). Similar to SRL, no significant seasonal or elevational variations in root N concentrations were detected for either species (Table 1, p > 0.05).

Seasonal Variation of Root Non‐Structural Carbohydrates

We found significant seasonal variations in the concentrations of soluble sugars in A. mariesii (Table 1, p < 0.01) and B. ermanii (p < 0.001). The sugar concentration of A. mariesii varied between 3.6% and 26.1% at 2000 m, and between 12.4% and 30.4% at 2500 m (Figure 3a,b). The sugar concentration of B. ermanii varied between 3.8% and 22.8% at 2000 m, and between 3.4% and 17.2% at 2500 m (Figure 3a,b). The sugar concentration decreased from spring to summer and then increased in autumn, which seemed to have a symmetrical variation against root respiration (Figure 3a,b). The maximum values of sugars were observed in spring, and the minimum values were observed in the summer for both species (Figure 3a,b). While both species showed similar variations in sugar content from spring to summer, an earlier increase in autumn was observed in A. mariesii roots (Figure 3a,b). Elevation (p < 0.001) and the interaction between season and elevation (p < 0.05) significantly affected sugar concentrations of A. mariesii (Table 1). The average sugar concentration of A. mariesii during the season was higher at 2500 m, accounting for 20.1% and 10.3% at 2000 m, respectively. The minimum value of A. mariesii was also higher at 2500 m than at 2000 m in both years (Figure 3a,b). However, there were no significant differences in the average sugar concentration of B. ermanii (p = 0.319), accounting for 9.1% at 2000 m and 8.1% at 2500 m.

The starch concentrations in the fine roots of A. mariesii varied from 0.92% to 5.8% at 2000 m, and from 1.2% to 5.6% at 2500 m (Figure 3c,d). The starch contents of B. ermanii varied between 0.61% and 4.6% and between 1.0% and 3.7% at 2500 m (Figure 3c,d). In both species, starch concentrations tended to peak in summer, with lower levels observed in spring and autumn (Figure 3c,d). We detected a significant effect of the season on the starch content of A. mariesii (Table 1, p < 0.001). Additionally, the elevation had a significant effect on the starch content of B. ermanii (Table 1, p < 0.05).

The total NSC concentrations varied seasonally in both A. mariesii and B. ermanii (Table 1, p < 0.001), which followed a trend similar to that observed for sugars, declining from spring to summer and subsequently increasing until autumn (Figure 3e,f). Consistently, there were earlier increases in total NSC in the roots of A. mariesii (Figure 3e,f). Moreover, we detected significant effects of elevation (Table 1, p < 0.001) on A. mariesii and the interactions between the season and elevation for both species (Table 1, p < 0.05). Across the seasons, the total NSC concentration was consistently higher at 2500 m than at 2000 m for A. mariesii (Figure 3e,f).

Sugars occupied a larger proportion of the total NSC than starch did in both species (Figure 4a–d). The sugar proportion increased in spring and autumn and decreased in summer (Figure 4a–d). Over the growing season, the proportion of sugars in A. mariesii at 2500 m was consistently higher than that at 2000 m (Figure 4a,b). However, no differences in NSC compositions were found among elevations in B. ermanii roots (Figure 4c,d).

Different Relationships Between Root Respiration and NSC Among the Seasons

There were clear seasonal variations in the relationships between root respiration and root NSC. Across all seasons, respiration and sugars were negatively correlated with A. mariesii at both elevations and with B. ermanii at 2500 m (Figure 5a–c, p < 0.05). In spring, significant correlations were observed only in B. ermanii roots at both elevations (Figure 5b,d, p < 0.05). Although the relationship between respiration and sugar in B. ermanii was positive at 2000 m, it was negative at 2500 m (Figure 5b,d). In summer, the SMA regression lines tended to have negative slopes, but these were not significant regardless of species or elevation (Figure 5a–d, p > 0.05). In autumn, at both elevations, significant correlations between root respiration and sugar were found only in A. mariesii (Figure 5a,c, p < 0.05). Additionally, the relationships between respiration and sugar in A. mariesii were positive at 2000 m and negative at 2500 m (Figure 5a,c). Overall, the slopes of the regression lines tended to be negative, even though they were not significant at 2500 m (Figure 5a–d).

Different Relationships Between Fine Root NSCs and Specific Root Length Among the Seasons

In summer, we observed significant negative correlations between SRL and sugars for A. mariesii at 2000 m and for B. ermanii at 2500 m (Figure S1b,c). In A. mariesii, we observed significant negative correlations between SRL and sugars across all seasons at both elevations (Figure S1a,c), which were not observed for B. ermanii. In contrast, we found no significant relationships between SRL and starch for both species and at both elevations in any season (Figure S2a–d).

Discussion

We assessed the seasonal variation in fine root respiration in A. mariesii and B. ermanii in subalpine forests. In our study, root respiration rates were obtained in the 2.5–6.5 nmol g−1 s−1 range (Figure 1). A comparison of the respiration rates of fine roots (Abramoff and Finzi 2016; Miyatani et al. 2018) confirmed that our results were credible.

Although the root respiration of A. mariesii did not vary significantly, it peaked mostly in summer at both elevations (Figure 1a,b). Peaks of root respiration of B. ermanii at 2000 m were observed in summer following the soil temperature, whereas the peak season at 2500 m differed between the years. The root respiration rate correlated with the soil temperature over a year (Makita et al. 2021). Plant respiration responds significantly to temperature (Atkin and Tjoelker 2003). However, our results suggest that soil temperature alone does not fully explain the variations in fine root respiration in the plant phenological cycle. Seasonal patterns in fine root respiration may be due to root production (Figure 1a,b). In temperate and boreal forests, tree root growth generally peaks early in the growing season, coinciding with increases in temperature and photosynthetic activity (Steinaker et al. 2010; Abramoff and Finzi 2015). In subalpine forests, the rates of fine root elongation peaked between spring budbreak and early summer (Wang et al. 2018) and are assumed to be synchronized with root respiration. Early root production, accompanied by high respiration rates, facilitates a greater uptake of nutrients that are mineralized over winter and mobilized in response to warmer temperatures and soil thawing (McCormack et al. 2014). However, in the present study, we found that not all peaks of root respiration were observed in spring or early summer. Moreover, some peaks of the respiration associated with root production may have been overlooked, and consequently, the frequency of root respiration observed at each phenological stage may not be sufficient to draw definite conclusions. Because we did not perform direct measurements of root growth and production simultaneously in this study, our interpretations regarding the association between the seasonality of root respiration and root phenology remain speculative. Nevertheless, the observed seasonal dynamics of root respiration provide indirect evidence of a coordinated interaction between C supply and root growth. Further research, including direct measurements of root phenology, is required to verify this relationship.

We examined the seasonal variations in fine root morphology and chemistry. Root functional traits are closely related to root respiration, such as higher respiration rates and higher SRL and N concentration (Han and Zhu 2021). Because a higher SRL is advantageous for resource foraging in spite of a shorter lifespan, we predicted that SRL would increase in summer to promote tree growth, whereas it would be lower in autumn. We also predicted that the N concentration would increase in summer to maximize root growth. However, no significant seasonal variation was found in SRL or root N concentrations for either species (Figure 2a–d, Table 1). In addition to our results, Gao et al. (2024) did not find any variation in SRL among the three subtropical trees. In contrast, significant seasonal variations in root morphological and chemical traits have been observed in deciduous and evergreen trees in temperate forests (Miyatani et al. 2018; Paradiso et al. 2019; Nikolova et al. 2020). Liu et al. (2020) also demonstrated significant seasonal variations in several morphological traits, including SRL, but not in root N concentrations in a subtropical evergreen broad‐leaved forest. Thus, the seasonality of root morphological and chemical traits is complex and depends on the tree species and environment. Little variation in root traits during the short growing season of subalpine forests highlights the large contribution of NSC to the seasonal variation in root respiration. NSC may be more effective than fine root morphology and chemistry for seasonal variations in respiration rates over a short time span.

We evaluated seasonal variations in root soluble sugars, starch, and total NSC concentrations (Figure 3a–f). Because the roots are the main organs for storing NSC, storage is considered stable (Furze et al. 2019). However, this concept is based on coarse roots. In our results, a larger fluctuation in the total NSC, mainly composed of sugar, was observed than predicted. This highlights the importance of evaluating the capacity of fine roots as NSC sinks and their dynamics to accurately estimate forest C cycling.

Sugars in the fine roots were higher in spring and lower in summer (Figures 3a,b, 4a–d). Similarly, the proportion of sugars in the total NSC increased in spring (Figure 4a–d). Woody plants increase sugar concentrations in winter to maintain their osmotic potential and prevent freezing damage (Furze et al. 2019). Therefore, higher sugar concentrations were observed in spring, with remnants from the previous winter. In summer, sugar concentrations decreased. According to Wang et al. (2018), the elongation rates of fine roots were negatively correlated with their sugar concentrations. Large amounts of sugars are assumed to be consumed as a surplus C source through root growth and respiration during the early growing season. In autumn, sugar concentrations tended to increase in the fine roots. Focusing on the variation in sugars in autumn, an earlier increase was observed in A. mariesii roots than in B. ermanii roots, indicating the importance of compensation in sugar storage for evergreen trees. In other words, A. mariesii limits the consumption of sugar in fine roots early to serve as a buffer against cold stress. On the other hand, B. ermanii, as a deciduous species with a relatively low freezing risk, serves as a surplus C source for longer periods and consumes sugars until just before dormancy. At 2500 m on Mt. Norikura, B. ermanii is characterized by higher maximum rates of net assimilation than A. mariesii (Azuma et al. 2024), and a larger supply of sugars from the canopy can support prolonged root growth in B. ermanii. These results indicate that the availability and utilization of root NSC differ significantly between the two species in each season.

During the growing season, the minimum sugar concentration was higher at 2500 m than at 2000 m for A. mariesii but not for B. ermanii. We found that the trees store more sugars and starch in their roots in summer at the treeline to provide energy and osmolytes for overwintering. Woody plants at high elevations continuously accumulate more NSC than those at lower elevations for high cold tolerance (Li et al. 2018). During the growing season, the minimum sugar proportion of A. mariesii was higher at 2500 m than at 2000 m (Figure 4a–d). In this regard, Masumoto et al. (2025), who conducted the research at the same study sites, demonstrated that the turgor loss point and capacitance at full turgor in the fine roots of A. mariesii were lower at 2500 m than at 2000 m, whereas no elevational trends were observed for B. ermanii. Furthermore, the authors showed that the osmotic potential of A. mariesii roots was consistently lower than that of B. ermanii roots (Masumoto et al. 2025). These findings provide evidence that the fine roots of A. mariesii have a higher capacity for water conservation and preservation of root physiological functions in cold environments, which is associated with sugar storage. Therefore, A. mariesii constantly serves root NSC as a buffer rather than as a surplus C source at 2500 m in preparation for overwintering. Interestingly, intraspecific variation in NSC storage in fine roots is associated with root morphology. The SRL of A. mariesii was significantly affected by elevation (Table 1), and the average values were lower at 2500 m than at 2000 m. The variations in SRL explain the C investment in root structural development and maintenance (Ostonen et al. 2007). The lower SRL of A. mariesii at a higher elevation should be intended to decrease root turnover at the expense of resource acquisition. Short‐lived roots can lead to loss of stored NSC. In addition, at both elevations, significant negative correlations were found between SRL and sugars in A. mariesii across all seasons (Figure S1). Given that acquisitive roots with a higher SRL and physiological capacity consume larger amounts of sugar as an energy source for root maintenance (Hashimoto et al. 2023), it is reasonable to assume that A. mariesii alters its root morphology to efficiently use root NSC and minimize the loss under the stressful conditions encountered at the treeline.

In contrast, we observed no clear elevational trends in B. ermanii with respect to either sugars or starch (Figures 3 and 4). Similarly, we found that elevation had no significant effect on the SRL of B. ermanii (Table 1). We suspect that in B. ermanii, root NSC serves as a surplus C source rather than as a buffer against environmental stress, even at the treeline. These findings indicate that the NSC of B. ermanii facilitates the production of fine roots, even though they are short‐lived. This enables a high foraging capacity in the soil, providing an advantage in acquiring the limited resources at the treeline. Consequently, differences in root morphology during long‐term adaptation to the environments would be associated with the optimization of NSC consumption in fine roots.

Across all seasons, root respiration was negatively correlated with sugar concentrations (Figure 5a–d). This is because more substrate is required for fine roots to respire highly (Hashimoto et al. 2023). How does the relationship between fine root respiration and sugar content vary seasonally? In spring, significant relationships between respiration and sugars were observed only in B. ermanii at both elevations. In deciduous trees, spring foliation influences annual production. Although root and shoot growth are typically asynchronous (Abramoff and Finzi 2015), trees may support shoot development using surplus C for fine root respiration. Nikolova et al. (2020) demonstrated that fine root respiration of Fagus sylvatica became higher in spring and early summer, even at the same soil temperature, which was associated with root development. Thus, fine root respiration in spring plays an important role in the phenology of deciduous trees. In B. ermanii, we observed that root respiration and sugars were positively correlated at 2000 m, whereas they were negatively correlated at 2500 m. This suggests that the source of C needed for spring root behavior differs among elevations. At 2000 m, the lower limit of the subalpine zone, tree roots are capable of high respiration due to increased sugar availability in fine roots as production increases. Thus, root respiration is passively controlled by the supply of sugars. Conversely, at 2500 m as the treeline, the deciduous trees actively consume stored sugars in fine roots to maximize root growth and resource acquisition during the shorter growing season. In summer, negative but non‐significant correlations were observed for both species. When trees are biologically active, sugars are used for various C consumption processes, including the synthesis of secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds and sugar alcohols (Hartmann and Trumbore 2016), and are not limited to respiration. In autumn, significant relationships between respiration and sugars were observed only for A. mariesii. Given the importance of improving cold tolerance, evergreen trees must limit their sugar consumption at an early stage. Tjoelker et al. (1999) demonstrated that the temperature acclimation of root respiration in evergreen coniferous trees is greater than in deciduous broad‐leaved trees. In autumn, both the rates and temperature sensitivity of fine root respiration in Picea abies were lower than those in Fagus sylvatica (Nikolova et al. 2020). These indicate that regulating root respiration rates is crucial for evergreen trees in autumn. In autumn, A. mariesii roots showed a positive correlation between respiration and sugar at 2000 m, whereas they showed a negative correlation at 2500 m. At 2000 m, root respiration is gradually suppressed as productivity declines. On the other hand, at 2500 m, the evergreen trees compensate for sugar storage for cold tolerance by actively suppressing consumption through root respiration. These findings support our hypothesis that fine root respiration and sugars are closely linked during typical phenological events of both deciduous and evergreen trees.

In contrast, although the seasonal variation in fine root starch concentrations was not significant in B. ermanii (Table 1, Figure 3c,d), the concentration tended to be higher in spring and lower in autumn in both species. Starch is formed as a long‐term storage reservoir when the sugar supply exceeds the demand. Therefore, starch stored in summer is a source of energy during winter by interconversion from sugars. The trees would be able to grow in the subalpine conditions in balance with survival by retaining almost‐surplus C as sugar with a variety of functions, rather than storing it as starch. There were variations in the relationship between root respiration and starch; however, this relationship was not as clear as that with sugars (Figure 6a–d). Across all seasons, significant positive relationships were found for both species at 2500 m. At treeline, starch storage is important for root respiration in order to maintain the elongated fine roots during the growing season. Thus, the hypothesis that starch storage affects fine root respiration over a longer time scale is supported.

To conclude, we found that seasonal variation in root respiration is affected by temporal changes in the roles of NSC as a surplus C source and buffer against coldness. The subalpine trees change the relationship between respiration and sugars in fine roots to optimize C consumption in their root phenology. These findings provide a new perspective on the concept that root respiration follows soil temperature and is complemented by NSC in fine roots. We also found that the variation in root morphology among elevations might be relevant to NSC storage to optimize the root C economy in the long‐term adaptation to environments. However, there is still a wide gap in research on fine root seasonality. To deepen our understanding of the seasonality of root respiration, we need to know when fine roots are produced and how root NSC is utilized. In addition, high‐frequency observations of root respiration are necessary to understand the species‐specific characteristics of the seasonality. Further studies addressing these challenges will provide more insights into the seasonal variations in root respiration and NSC and will contribute to a better understanding of plant lifestyles and C cycling in subalpine forest ecosystems.
