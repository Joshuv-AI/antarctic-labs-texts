ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to a Cochrane review (published in April 2026) on the clinical trials of monoclonal antibodies targeting beta amyloid, this perspective outlines a number of critiques. The scientists, methodologists, subject‐matter experts, and clinicians on this perspective strongly recommend that the Cochrane editorial board consider a call for editorial response, correction of press materials, or commissioned reply to this review for four overarching reasons: (1) decision to pool antibodies that do and do not clear beta amyloid, (2) decision to include studies from a time when biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease were not available, (3) analytical and methodological limitations of the approach used, (4) conflating the distinct roles and methodological processes of systematic review and guideline methodologies, (5) contextualizing the present critique within the existing evidence‐synthesis literature, and (6) mechanistic inference beyond the scope of the presented analyses. Conclusions that may not accurately reflect treatment‐specific effects could cause significant downstream clinical and psychosocial harm to those currently undergoing treatment and their family members as well as individuals considering participation in Alzheimer's clinical trials.

PERSPECTIVE

On April 16, 2026, Cochrane published a review titled “Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease” 1 and an online news article summarizing this review. 2 The authors of this Perspective note several serious flaws in this analysis and its conclusions, noting that such reports could compromise patient care.

In their analysis, the Cochrane study authors pooled results from 20,342 patients receiving Alzheimer's disease treatments targeting amyloid beta proteins in the brain. 1 , 2 They concluded that, collectively, these interventions show minimal to no clinically meaningful benefit related to cognition and daily functioning. They also stated that this perceived reduction of amyloid plaques does not clearly translate into clinical improvement. 1 , 2

This assessment represents an oversimplified conceptualization of beta amyloid biology, introduces significant methodological risk, and prevents the formulation of clinically meaningful conclusions.

This manuscript outlines the scientific critiques of this review below: Decision to pool antibodies that do and do not clear beta amyloid: This analysis extracted data from 17 clinical trials testing monoclonal antibodies designed to target beta amyloid, including 15 studies that never progressed to regulatory approval globally. Several trials were also terminated early following futility analyses. Despite these details, the review pooled together clinical data from biologically distinct antibodies, including antibodies that meaningfully clear amyloid and have demonstrated clinical efficacy, alongside antibodies that failed to achieve substantial amyloid clearance or clinical benefit. 3 Beta amyloid exists in multiple dynamic forms within the brain, including monomers, oligomers, protofibrils, fibrils, and plaques. The antibodies included in these trials were deliberately engineered to target different amyloid species, conformations, and binding sites, resulting in substantial differences in amyloid engagement, plaque clearance, downstream biological effects, and ultimately clinical outcomes. Even the currently approved therapies, lecanemab and donanemab, do not target the same amyloid structures or epitopes and, therefore, are not biologically interchangeable.By pooling antibodies with markedly different capacities for amyloid clearance and clinical efficacy, the analysis combines successful and unsuccessful therapeutic approaches into a single class estimate. 3 By doing this, the analysis risks obscuring meaningful treatment effects and creates a form of circular reasoning in which mechanistic differences that may explain divergent outcomes are assumed away by the analytical strategy itself. The plain language summary states that the review sought “to know if anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies are an effective medicine for people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease.” However, only three antibodies evaluated in these trials progressed beyond the clinical trial setting into clinical practice, and only two remain in active clinical use today. A true evaluation of medicines used in practice, therefore, would focus primarily on therapies that demonstrated sufficient efficacy and safety to achieve regulatory approval.It is notably atypical for a Cochrane review to combine experimental agents that failed development with approved therapies to draw conclusions about the efficacy of the approved agents themselves. An analogy would be evaluating the effectiveness of statins for preventing negative cardiovascular outcomes by pooling modern high‐potency statins with earlier lipid‐lowering compounds from the 1950s to 1980s that lacked meaningful low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) reduction or clinical efficacy. Such an approach would predictably dilute treatment effects and risk misleading conclusions regarding the value of the therapies that proved effective. 4 As another example, one would not combine obsolete, ineffective antiarrhythmic drugs with modern catheter ablation and conclude that rhythm control has no value in atrial fibrillation, nor would one pool non‑targeted cytotoxic chemotherapies with biomarker‑selected human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) ‑directed therapies and then argue that HER2 targeting is ineffective in breast cancer. Indeed, estimates suggest that a substantial minority, up to ‐30%, of participants in earlier trials may not have had underlying amyloid plaque pathology at all. 7 , 8 The inclusion of trials conducted prior to the routine use of biomarker confirmation at enrollment for the trials introduces important concerns regarding population heterogeneity. Drawing conclusions about amyloid reduction as a therapeutic mechanism from heterogeneous therapeutic trial data therefore extends beyond what these analyses can directly support.

All 13 cognition studies used Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐Cog), all nine dementia severity studies used Clinical Dementia Rating scale ‐ Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), and the functional ability analyses were already stratified by scale, making standardization to SMD statistically unnecessary. This choice introduces two cascading problems. First, interpretation requires readers to back‐convert SMDs into raw clinical units, yet such conversions depend on the pooled standard deviation, which varies substantially across studies (approximately seven for bapineuzumab, nine for lecanemab, and 13 for solanezumab). Second, and more importantly, the authors apply Cohen's d thresholds (approx. 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8) to determine GRADE imprecision downgrading, rather than using thresholds derived from the null effect or minimal important differences (MIDs). Cohen's d was developed as a conventional effect size metric in psychology and does not constitute a clinically validated benchmark for meaningful treatment benefit. Because the authors already contextualized findings using minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) in the discussion, they could likewise have derived MCID‐based SMD or MD thresholds and applied them consistently throughout the analysis. Instead, the review uses Cohen's d thresholds for formal imprecision judgments while relying on MID‐based reasoning for narrative interpretation, resulting in a methodologically mixed evaluative framework.

Due to limitations of the existing evidence base, efforts are also in place to ensure that the evaluation of treatment effects, desirable and undesirable short‐ and long‐term outcomes, are being measured in real‐world settings. One such effort is the Alzheimer's Network for Treatment and Diagnostics (ALZ‐NET) which is collecting real‐world data from ongoing clinical practice to understand the implications of the two treatments approved by the FDA for patients. 14 Real‐world data studies are starting to be published, initially evaluating the feasibility, safety, and tolerability in clinical use of monoclonal antibodies from a wide geographical area. 15 , 16 Real‐world data as it continues to evolve, along with clinical trial results, should guide policy decision‐making in this space.

Of the utmost importance, the authors urge the Cochrane editorial office and the review authors to remember that behind every data point is a person. Many people living with MCI and mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease who are using these treatments are taking trips they were not sure that they would be able to take, spending time with friends and family, making plans for next month, doing things they love, and staying present in their lives and the lives of the people they care about. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Their lives and their experiences cannot be ignored, nor should they be denied access to regulatory approved options. The inferential overreach of this report could cause significant harm to those currently undergoing treatment and their family members as well as to individuals considering treatment with monoclonal antibodies or participation in Alzheimer's clinical trials.

Cochrane has a long‐standing reputation for rigorous evidence‐synthesis that has informed clinical care across medicine. For that reason, it is particularly important that conclusions drawn from Cochrane reviews remain closely aligned with the methodological scope and limitations of the underlying analyses. In our view, the conclusions of this review extend beyond what can be confidently inferred from the available evidence and risk overstating certainty in an area that remains scientifically and clinically complex. The central issue is not whether anti‐amyloid therapies should or should not be used in clinical practice but whether the current evidence base supports the strength and scope of the conclusions presented by the review authors.

Based on the above reasoning, this diverse group of leaders across the evidence ecosystem has reviewed the Cochrane materials and strongly recommends a call for editorial response, correction of the press materials, or commissioned reply from the Cochrane editorial board.

Given the potential implications for clinical decision‐making, patients currently and successfully using these treatments, future priorities, and public trust in evidence‐synthesis, we believe a more measured and methodologically restrained interpretation of the evidence is warranted, particularly when drawing conclusions regarding clinical meaningfulness, patient benefit, and future scientific direction.
