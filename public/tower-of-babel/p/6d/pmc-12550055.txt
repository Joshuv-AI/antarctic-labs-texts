Research on the design of overall stability capacity for axial compression and single sided connected eccentric compression equal leg angles
Authors: JingJing Han, Wei Zhang, LuFeng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the design methods for the overall stability capacity in equal-leg angles under axial compression and those subjected to eccentric compression with single-sided connections. It compares eight current codes from China, Europe, Australia, and the United States, emphasizing the differing design considerations for equal-leg angles. The research is based on 712 sets of axial compression tests and 226 sets of eccentric compression tests with single-sided connections on equal-leg angles, examining the discrepancies between the calculated and tested overall stability capacities as per the codes. The findings indicate significant variations in calculated capacities across the eight codes, with a general inclination towards conservatism in axial compression designs for equal-leg angles. Codes such as AS 4100, AS/NZS 4600, and the general method of EN 1993-1-1 use the axial compression-bending (N–M) interaction equation for eccentric compression, whereas GB 50,017, DL/T 5154, EN 1993-3-1, and the concise method of EN 1993-1-1 use a simplified axial compression formula for eccentric compression. These simplified methods deviate from practical engineering scenarios, especially for single-sided connected eccentric compression equal-leg angles. Following a comprehensive evaluation of the codes and test results, this study suggests a revision to the stability factor in GB 50,017 for axial compression in equal-leg angles. Additionally, it introduces a new design method for the overall stability capacity of single-sided connected eccentric compression in equal-leg angles, drawing on the N–M interaction equation from AS/NZS 4600. The paper concludes with the experimental data validation of the proposed stability factor for axial compression in equal-leg angles and the new design method for eccentric compression in equal-leg angles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-02000-3.

Introduction

Angles are extensively utilized in steel structures such as telecommunication towers, power transmission towers, and trusses, as depicted in Fig. 1. The single-sided connected angles shown in Fig. 2 are categorized as eccentric compression members and frequently serve as auxiliary materials and diagonals in tower frames, as indicated in the orange box in Fig. 1c. These members experience complex stress conditions under uneven wind loads due to the combined axial compression and bending. However, most current codes design them as axial compression members, which does not align with their actual stress state. Differing from those double symmetric sections like box or I-shaped sections, the centroid (C) and shear center (S) of angles do not coincide, as illustrated in Fig. 3, making them either singly symmetric (equal leg) or asymmetric (unequal leg). The legs of angles are prone to local buckling, and stability design is conducted based on parallel axes (y, z axes) when there are continuous lateral torsional restraints along the length of members; otherwise, it is performed along the principal axes (u, v axes). This makes the process of stability design for angles complex and cumbersome. Current codes such as GB50017-20171, DL/T 5154-20122, BS EN 1993-1-1-20223, BS EN 1993-3-1-20064, AS 4100-20205, AS/NZS 4600-20186, ASCE 10-20157, and ANSI/AISC 360-168 all encompass regulations for the overall stability capacity design of angles. Further details are provided in "Design methods and suggestion for overall stability capacity of axial compression equal-leg angles" and "Design methods and suggestion for overall stability capacity of eccentric compression equal-leg angles" sections.

In addition, existing pieces of literature have also conducted comparative studies on the overall stability capacity design methods and comparisons of codes for angles. Cho10,11 introduced a novel design approach for the overall stability of angles that supersedes the conventional line-elastic design reliant on effective length, validating its precision through experimentation and comparison with BS595012. Trahair conducted a series of studies on the biaxial bending strength13, elastic lateral buckling behavior14, biaxial bending buckling of unsupported angles15, and torsional buckling instability16,17, offering insights that contributed to the revision of BS595012.

Aydin18,19 identified that the Load and Resistance Factor Design (LRFD) considers the non-dimensional bending moment in biaxial bending of angles conservatively, proposing improved equations for overall stability in both biaxial bending and biaxial compression-bending by accounting for lateral torsional and local buckling, and presented enhanced LRFD design procedures accordingly. Concurrently, simplifying eccentric compression as axial compression for calculation, Aydin20 developed a design methodology for angles under combined axial compression and biaxial bending through 2 axial compression members, 12 eccentric compression members experiments, and ABAQUS finite element analysis, demonstrating the accuracy of methodology in compliance with EN 1993-1-1-200521 and ANSI/AISC 360-0522. Rasmussen23,24 revised the expressions for the effective width and centroid of angles in AS/NZS 4600-199626 based on 10 axial compression members with a yield strength of 396 MPa from the literature25, improving the effect of effective eccentricity on the overall stability capacity using interaction equation (i.e., N-M equation) for eccentric compression members. Behzadi27 revised the overall stability capacity design method for angles in EN 1993-1-1-200521 based on stability factor studies and bending capacity experiments28 through 45 sets of axial compression and 7 sets of eccentric compression members with the yield strength of 275 MPa. Hussain29 proposed a design method for the elastic and torsional buckling stability of angles under biaxial compression-bending, which uses the N-M equation for the eccentric compression design, and the accuracy was verified through ANSI/AISC 360-168, EN 1993-1-1-200521, and ABAQUS models. Bashar30 simulated the angle truss experiments of Elgaaly31 and analyzed the overall stability capacity of angles with different bolt connections, identifying IS 80032 as the more accurate code among others. Bezas33 performed extensive numerical and experimental research on the overall stability of angles and compared Eurocode 3 (EC3) methods in different parts for simplified axial compression, axial compression-bending capacity, and general eccentric compression design, with implications for the upcoming revision of EC3. Bai34 proposed a nonlinear variational equation model for unequal-leg angle capacity under axial compression, verified through finite element analysis. Other scholars conducted studies on locally corroded35,36 and stainless steel angles37–40, comparing the overall stability design of current codes and proposing simplified design methods, all of which treat eccentric compression angles as axial compression angles for overall stability capacity calculations.

Three primary issues in the codes and literature above require further investigations for the overall stability capacity of axial and eccentric compression angles. Firstly, the column curves of stability factor in current codes combine various cross-sections and apply to steel members with yield strength below 460 MPa, whereas modern engineering practices often employ materials with higher strength grades. Consequently, broader experimental verification and potential revision are necessary. Secondly, there is a notable inconsistency among design methods; most codes simplify eccentric compression members to axial compression for the purpose of calculating the overall stability capacity of angles, neglecting the N–M interaction equation that accounts for the combined effects of axial compression and bending. This simplification may underestimate the actual stability capacity, necessitating a verification and revision of these simplified approaches. Thirdly, there is limited research in both codes and literature regarding the commonly used N–M interaction equations for single-sided connected eccentric compression equal-leg angles. The N–M interaction equations for eccentric compression are fundamentally based on the axial compression stability design of angles. Consequently, there is a need to compare and validate the existing N–M interaction equations in codes for equal-leg angles and to develop N–M interaction equations for single-sided connected eccentric compression that are consistent with experimental data.

This study investigates the design methods for the overall stability capacity of equal-leg angles under axial compression and those subjected to eccentric compression with single-sided connections by comparing code provisions, contrasting tested values with code-calculated values, and analyzing the differences. The research assesses the variability in the overall stability design considerations for equal-leg angles under axial and eccentric compression across eight commonly used codes from China, Europe, Australia, and the United States. It compares the calculated overall stability capacities of axial compression and single-sided connected eccentric compression from these codes with 712 sets of experimental results for axial compression tests and 226 sets of experimental results for eccentric compression tests with single-sided connections on equal-leg angles, thereby evaluating the applicability of the codes. Through the comparison of existing experimental values with code-design values, this study proposes a revision suggestion for the stability factor in GB 50017-20171 for axially compressed equal-leg angles. Additionally, drawing on the N–M interaction equation for eccentric compression members, a new design method for the overall stability capacity of single-sided connected eccentric compression equal-leg angles is introduced. The paper concludes with the experimental validation of the proposed stability factor for axial compression and the new design method for eccentric compression with single-sided connections on equal-leg angles.

This study primarily focuses on the research of ordinary steel members and thick plate steel (i.e., plate thickness < 40mm) equal-leg angles with a strength grade of 560 MPa and below.

Current experimental research on the overall stability capacity of angles

Scholars have conducted experimental research on the overall stability capacity of equal-leg angles, focusing on both axial compression and single-sided connected eccentric compression. A1-Sayed41 and Adluri42 performed 8 and 26 axial compression tests, respectively, to explore the failure modes of angles. Vishnuvardhan43 conducted tests on short columns with 8 different sizes and 6 varying steel strengths to examine the impact of various cross-sectional dimensions and material strengths on overall stability capacity. Cao44 carried out 90 groups of tests on high-strength, large-size axial compression angles, covering 6 sizes and 5 slenderness ratios, with plate widths between 220 and 250mm and steel yield strengths of 420MPa, devising a calculation approach suitable for the stability factor of large-scale axial compression angle. Yu45 examined the overall stability capacities and failure modes through 45 groups of axial compression tests on Q420 high-strength angles. Popovic25 completed 6 short and 24 medium-length column axial compression tests, revealing that the test outcomes exceeded the values calculated based on AS/NZS 4600-199626, and highlighted that the additional eccentricity (L/1000) in the code applies primarily to longer columns. Liu46,47 validated the biaxial eccentric compression stability equations from ANSI/AISC 360-0522 via 4 axial compression and 24 eccentric compression tests, concluding that the design methodologies for angles are generally overly conservative. Cao48 performed 48 axial compression tests and compared the results with ASCE 10-199749, DL/T 5154-20122, and GB 50017-200350. Ban51 highlighted the absence of design rules in GB 50017-200350 for angles with yield strengths exceeding 420 MPa, conducting 48 axial compression tests on 6 sizes with slenderness ratios ranging from 30 to 120, and revised the stability factor for high-strength angles. Liu52 included 7 axial compression tests on L80 × 80 × 6 to investigate failure modes, overall stability capacities and deformation patterns for different slenderness ratios, he also compared width-to-thickness ratio limits in various codes. Gu53 conducted 9 axial compression stability capacity tests on Q345 angles with 3 sizes and 3 slenderness ratios, discussing discrepancies between test outcomes and code outcomes in Chinese and American codes. Huang54 executed 378 axial compression tests, revising the stability factor and effective length factor in ASCE 10-20157. Beyond axial compression, references [54–58] compared tests and code-calculated values for single-sided connected eccentric compression angles. However, these calculations typically simplified eccentric compression members as axial compression members, differing from actual engineering conditions.

When the ultimate bearing capacity was reached, various test members exhibited a range of failure modes, including local plate buckling, flexural buckling about the weak axis, torsional-flexural buckling, and torsional buckling. These failure modes are closely related to the slenderness ratio. Axial compression members with a low slenderness ratio predominantly failed by local buckling25,43–45,48,51,52,54. Those with a medium slenderness ratio primarily experienced flexural–torsional buckling, with some specimens also showing local buckling or purely flexural buckling25,41,42,52–54. Members with a high slenderness ratio were more likely to be characterized by flexural buckling25,41,42,46,47,52–54. For single-sided connected eccentric equal-leg angles, members with a low slenderness ratio mainly displayed local buckling54–56. Medium slenderness ratio members generally showed a transition from local buckling to flexural buckling54–57, and at the ultimate state, they exhibited bending deformation about the parallel axis, with the cross-section and upper spherical hinge of the angles accompanying slight torsion57. Eccentric equal-leg angles with a high slenderness ratio generally manifested overall flexural buckling characteristics46,47,54,56. It is observed that the instability modes of single-sided connected eccentric compression members parallel those of axial compression, characterized generally by: local buckling in low slenderness ratio members, a transition from local buckling to flexural–torsional buckling in medium slenderness ratio members, and overall flexural buckling in high slenderness ratio members.

Design methods and suggestion for overall stability capacity of axial compression equal-leg angles

This study examines eight commonly used codes from China, Europe, Australia, and the United States: GB 50017-20171, DL/T 5154-20122, BS EN 1993-1-1-20223, BS EN 1993-3-1-20064, AS 4100-20205, AS/NZS 4600-20186, ASCE 10-20157, and ANSI/AISC 360-168. This section introduces the calculation provisions for the overall stability capacity of axial compression equal-leg angles in these codes, evaluates their accuracy using 712 sets of existing axial compression test data, and proposes a new design curve for axial compression equal-leg angles. The symbols and their meanings adhere to the stipulations of the respective codes and are not elaborated upon in this paper.

Comparison of design methods in codes for overall stability capacity of axial compression equal-leg angles

Upon comparing the design clauses in the eight aforementioned codes for the overall stability capacity of axial compression angles, it is evident that the majority utilize the stability factor and the effective cross-sectional area to introduce adverse effects on stability, which depend on the slenderness ratio and the width-to-thickness ratio, respectively. The regulation of width-to-thickness ratio limits is crucial for considering the local stability. As the stability factor profoundly impacts the overall stability across any slenderness or width-to-thickness ratio, this study focuses on the stability factor, deferring the study of width-to-thickness ratio for now.

Capacities comparison

The overall stability capacities of 712 axial compression test angles were calculated using the eight codes aforementioned: GB 500171, DL/T 51542, EN 1993-1-13, EN 1993-3-14, AS 41005, AS/NZS 46006, ASCE 107, and ANSI/AISC8. The code calculations are denoted as Nuc1 through Nuc8, with test values as Nut.

Figure 5a–h illustrates a comparison of the calculated values from various codes against experimental values, where the horizontal axis denotes the experimental values, the vertical axis denotes the code-calculated values, and the gray dashed line indicates the line of equality. The data points for axial compression tests, as observed across different codes, are predominantly distributed around the line of equality. A distinct trend is noted: at lower capacities, the data points closely adhere to the line of equality, with alternating positive and negative errors and relatively concentrated error rates. At higher capacities, the error rates expand, and the data points display increased scatter, although they still maintain a relative concentration.

In practice, angle steel members inevitably exhibit concurrent imperfections such as initial curvature, initial eccentricity, and residual stresses due to manufacturing processes, hoisting, transportation, and other factors. By comprehensively considering these imperfections, actual axially compressed members in engineering applications can be conceptualized as small-eccentricity compression members with initial curvature, while also incorporating residual stresses. Consequently, determining the stability-bearing capacity of axially compressed angle steel members transforms into solving the ultimate bearing capacity problem for imperfect angle steel compression members. Meanwhile, considering the uncertainty of loads and the discreteness of material parameters in practical engineering, and ensuring the reliability index of the structure, the conservatism of the calculated values in the code is also reasonable.

Suggestion for overall stability capacity of axial compression

Most current codes use the φ-λ curve (i.e., column curve) to design the overall stability capacity of axial compression angles. Comparisons between code calculations and test values show that calculations are overly conservative and inaccurate for axial compression equal-leg angles. This comparison also highlights the accuracy advantage of GB 500171. The column curve expression in GB 500171, as presented in Eq. (2), is derived from the Perry formula, and its design philosophy is perpetuated and refined within this study.

Considering A.v.g. and C.O.V. of the ratio between code calculations and test values for the overall stability capacity, this section adopts column curve expression from GB 500171 for axial compression equal-leg angles, as indicated in Eq. (1). Utilizing the data from 712 axial compression tests for equal-leg angles, the parameters in Eq. (2) have been re-fitted and adjusted using the least square method. This process has developed a new design curve for equal-leg angles and introduced a novel approach to designing the overall stability capacity for axial compression equal-leg angles.

Given that only a few test members had steel strengths below 235MPa, only the parameters for GB-a in Eq. (2) were revised. The revised stability factor is denoted as φ-mod, as detailed in Eq. (27), with α1, α2, and α3 values of 0, 0.986, and 0.067, respectively. Components with different slenderness ratios often exhibit different failure modes when reaching the ultimate capacity61,62. The scatter plot illustrates the stability factors and failure modes of 712 sets of equal-leg angles subjected to axial compression. In this plot, LB denotes local buckling, FTB signifies flexural–torsional buckling, FB indicates flexural buckling, and Coupled refers to a compound mode comprising the aforementioned buckling types. Figure 8, which displays the distribution of buckling modes for the test members, reveals that members with lower slenderness ratios typically fail due to isolated local buckling, whereas those with higher slenderness ratios primarily fail due to bending buckling. Members with intermediate slenderness ratios are more likely to fail through a complex interaction of buckling modes, with some experiencing flexural–torsional buckling. The transition between these buckling modes is not clearly defined by slenderness ratio thresholds, indicating that the instability patterns of equilateral angle steel members are intricate. The design curve proposed in this study for equal-leg angles under axial compression is grounded in GB 50017, adopting its single-curve fitting approach for buckling modes. At present, it does not employ a more nuanced, segmented fitting method based on distinct buckling modes.

As shown in Fig. 9, most test results are higher than the GB-a curve, with some near or exceeding the Euler curve. This deviation is primarily attributed to the GB50017 design method, which calculates an overly large converted slenderness ratio through elastic stability theory, resulting in stability factors exceeding the Euler curve. The design specifications for steel structures are classified into different levels based on the degree of influence of various defects on the stable bearing capacity of axial compression members, and factors such as the form of the component section and the direction of bending instability. Based on this, the section of axial compression members is correspondingly merged and divided into different column curves. Since the column curve is determined based on the average of numerical results (i.e., 50% quantile), it aims to traverse the central region of test values for members with equivalent slenderness ratios. This also leads to significant differences in some results when using column curves to calculate axial compression test components. The revised column curve presented in this paper aligns more closely with the distribution tendency of test angles than GB-a.

Comparison of design methods in codes for overall stability capacity of eccentric compression equal-leg angles

In contrast to the capacity design of axial compression equal-leg angles, the current codes offer divergent clauses for the eccentric compression design of the overall stability capacity. The eight codes primarily employ two approaches to calculate the overall stability capacity of eccentric compression angles:

The first approach calculates the overall stability capacity directly using the N-M interaction equation for eccentric compression angles, such as the design clauses of AS 41005, AS/NZS 46006 and the general method in EN 1993-1-13. This approach is theoretically scientific and accurate. The second approach treats eccentric compression angles as axial compression angles for calculating the overall stability capacity, as seen in DL/T 51542, ASCE 107, EN 1993-3-14 and the simplified method of EN 1993-1-13.

Due to the fact that the design of bending members in steel structure specifications is mostly based on stable reduction calculations of axial compression members, it naturally inherits the shortcomings of steel structures in the design and calculation of equilateral angle steel axial compression members, that is, the design of axial compression bars integrates multiple cross-sectional forms, mostly belonging to semi empirical and semi theoretical fitting formulas. Similarly, for equilateral angle steel compression bending members, different design methods have inconsistent calculation processes, especially simplifying the calculation form of compression bending angle steel members into axial compression members, resulting in significant differences in calculation results. In addition, in practical engineering, considering the uncertainty of loads and the discreteness of material parameters, there may be some differences between the calculated values in the specifications and the experimental values. This may be the reason for the difference in results between Fig. 13 and Table 5.

As observed in Table 5, there is considerable variation in the code calculations of eccentric compression angles in different codes when compared to test values. For codes such as GB 500171, DL/T 51542, AS 41005, and AS/NZS 46006, the average ratios A.v.g.s are all less than 1, suggesting conservative calculations. Notably, AS/NZS 46006 exhibits a smaller C.O.V., signifying greater stability in its calculations. Conversely, in the general and simplified methods of EN 1993-1-13, EN 1993-3-14 and ASCE 107, the average ratios A.v.g.s range from 1.15 to 1.25. This indicates that these code calculations may be overly optimistic, potentially compromising safety, and are characterized by a higher degree of variability and less stability. The findings in Table 5 are consistent with the trends depicted in Figs. 9 and 10.

From the calculation format of the overall stability capacity, GB 500171 and DL/T 51542 simplify eccentric compression angles as axial compression angles: GB 500171 employs a reduction factor to estimate the overall stability capacity, while DL/T 51542 referring to ASCE 107 uses a slenderness ratio correction factor K to effectively increase the slenderness ratio, thus simplifying the eccentric compression to axial compression for stability calculations.

Additionally, EN 1993-3-14, ASCE 107 and the simplified method of EN 1993-1-13 introduce modified slenderness ratios to calculate the overall stability capacity of eccentric compression as axial compression. Variously, AS 41005, AS/NZS 46006 and the general method of EN 1993-1-13 rely on the N-M interaction equation to determine the overall stability capacity of eccentric compression. The method based on the N-M interaction equation is more aligned with the actual behavior, considering the specific characteristics of eccentric compression angles.

Conclusions

This study presents a comparative analysis of the design provisions for the overall stability capacity of axial and eccentric compression in equal-leg angles across eight prevalent codes. Based on 712 sets of axial compression test data, a suggestion for revising the stability factor of GB 500171 was provided for axial compression equal-leg angles. Additionally, with 226 sets of eccentric compression test data, a new N-M interaction equation was proposed for the overall stability capacity of single-sided connected eccentric compression equal-leg angles. The commonality is that all codes employ stability factors or a combination of stability factors and effective cross-sectional area factors to reduce the overall stability capacity.
