Introduction

Ever since the first diversion of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from ventricles to the peritoneal cavity in 1905 and its gradual development of better construct material of shunt system, which addressed earlier problems of frequent shunt tube occlusion and recurrent infections; VP shunt has been the standard treatment of hydrocephalus in the present day [1,2]. Laparoscopic procedure evolved nearly around the same time. In 1901, Georg Kelling first performed diagnostic laparoscopy of a dog using cystoscope [3]. It then got further popularized in the late eighties with the advent of computer chip-based television cameras [4]. Lately, with the advent of minimally invasive surgery, Laparoscopic surgery has become the standard practice in many intra-abdominal pathologies. The increase in survival of VP shunted patients and the increasing trend in laparoscopic surgery, has therefore, converged the situation where there might be a situations, where the shunted patients are in need of laparoscopic procedure at some point in their life, if they harbor other pathologies requiring laparoscopic intervention. Hence while undergoing laparoscopic procedure in ventriculo peritoneal shunted patient, there might be various intraoperative to postoperative complications among which pneumocephalus is the most frequently noted one [2,3]. The needful measures and appropriate preoperative planning is utmost to avoid dreadful complications. This case has been written in accordance with SCARE guidelines [5].

Case summary

A 18-yr-old Asian lady was undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic cholelithiasis. Patient presented with the history of multiple episodes of vomiting since 2–3 days, small in amount, non-projectile in nature, non-bilious, not blood stained, associated with dyspepsia, anorexia and mild right hypochondriac pain. There was no any feature suggestive of raised Intra cranial pressure both clinically or radiologically. Her previous medical history was unremarkable. She had undergone Ventriculo peritoneal shunt placement for hydrocephalus in early infancy (6 months) for congenital hydrocephalus. Her preoperative plain CT head do not showed any features suggestive of shunt displacement, shunt failure, pneumocephalus or any features suggestive of raised intracranial pressure or other secondary changes (Fig. 1). Along with it her preoperative plain CT abdomen and pelvis accurately pin pointed the location of peritoneal end of the VP shunt in the pelvis region (Fig. 2). Subsequently Patient was planned for laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with neurosurgery department back up. Under general anesthesia, patient kept in supine position, standard 4 port laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed. Pneumoperitoneum was created with carbon dioxide gas and intra-abdominal pressure was maintained at 10 mm of hg throughout the procedure. After introduction of the camera port peritoneal part of the VP shunt was noted along with adhesion in anterior abdominal wall. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was completed and was uneventful. Distal tip of VP shunt was found near pelvic region and free flow of clear cerebrospinal fluid was noted (Fig. 3). The intra-abdominal pressure was monitored continuously and remained less than 12 mmHg, except when instruments were introduced through the abdominal wall (Fig. 2). The surgical procedure lasted 50 min and consisted of surgical excision of a gallbladder. The patient's recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged home on the following day with postoperative plan CT head without any features of pneumocephalus and raise ICP (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Preoperative and postoperative plain CT head of the patient respectively showing position of Ventricular end of the VP shunt tip and no features of pneumocephalus in postoperative CT head.Fig. 1Fig. 2Axial and coronal section of plain CT abdomen and pelvis respectively showing track of peritoneal end of VP shunt.Fig. 2Fig. 3Intraoperative pictures showing free flow of CSF from peritoneal end of the VP shunt tip.Fig. 3

Discussion

The intraabdominal pressure (IAP) is defined as the steady-state pressure concealed within the abdominal cavity [6]. Under normal physiological conditions, the normal values in adults are up to 5 mm of Hg [7]. Whereas intracranial pressure is the pressure inside the cranium accounted to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), blood and the brain tissue. Changes in ICP are secondary to change in volume of either of those components accountable to the overall ICP. If one of the three components increases in size, the volume of the other two decrease as a compensation to maintain the constant, so as to prevent the rise in ICP. This holds true, if we consider the cranium as a rigid vault, as hypothesized by the Monro-Kellie Doctrine [8]. Cerebral Perfusion pressure (CPP) ensures the regulation of blood flow in the brain and is attained by subtracting ICP from the mean arterial pressure (MAP) [9]. The blood flow in the brain is maintained fairly constant due to cerebral autoregulation. The ICP monitoring can be done using invasive and non-invasive measures. External ventricular drainage through a catheter in the lateral ventricles and micro transducer devices attached to them monitors ICP invasively versus monitoring of ICP by optic nerve sheath diameter, CT scan, MRI, trans cranial Doppler, tympanic membrane displacement and funduscopy are some of the non-invasive ICP monitoring tool. ICP in a normal adult in supine position ranges between 7 and 15 mmHg [6]. Breschet demonstrated the connection between the intracranial venous system and the vertebral venous system in the period of 1828–1832, establishing the relationship between the ICP and the IAP [6]. Batson in 1957, after whom Batson vertebral plexus of valveless spinal epidural venous network explained the close relationship whereby, the spread of metastasis and infections into the vertebral veins and to the spine and the central nervous system bypassing the liver and the lungs, yet showed the relationship of pressure transmission between the intraabdominal and intracranial compartment [10,11]. The current evidence strongly supports that the IAP is transmitted to the central nervous system by two pathways. One pathway is retrograde flow through the venous plexus of the spinal canal and the intracranial veins. The second pathway is direct as elevations in the IAP transferred into the thoracic compartment, which in turn results in back pressure on the jugular veins and decreases the drainage of the CSF and blood, leading to an increased ICP [12].

There are no absolute contraindications for laparoscopy in patients with VP shunts, although there are always concerns about the risks of an increase in the ICP secondary to an increase in IAP during laparoscopic surgery. The abdomen is usually insufflated to the pressure of 12–15 mmHg with carbon dioxide during laparoscopic procedures. Uzzo et al. [13] suggested the decreased CSF outflow secondary to increase in IAP results in partial or complete shunt obstruction. Cobianchi et al. [14] suggested that obstruction of the antegrade flow of the cerebrospinal fluid as a result of increased IAP together with the retrograde passage of carbon dioxide through the shunt catheter result in a sudden increase in ICP during laparoscopic procedures. The hypercapnia-induced cerebral arterial dilatation and venous pressure elevation cause increased intracranial blood volume thereby increasing the ICP.A one-way valve system in most of the shunt types can withstand a pressure of 300 mmHg before retrograde flow occurs. Collude et al. [15] suggested that pressure of 12–15 mmHg which is used to insufflate the abdomen during laparoscopic surgical procedures is unlikely to produce pneumocephalus. We used a pressure of 10 mmHg in our patient with reverse Trendelenburg position to ensure there is no objective retrograde flow. Furthermore, the effluence of CSF drops through the peritoneal end is noted intraoperatively. Neale and Falk [16] studied in vitro risk of valve failure in nine different shunt systems subjected to increased back pressure in order to demonstrate any sign of valve failure. The risk of failure was found to be minimal even with IAP as high as 80 mmHg [17]. Matsumoto et al. [18] found similar finding with no reflux of co2 with pressure less than 25 mmHg. Some surgeons have even advised clamping of peritoneal end of shunt and temporary exteriorization before laparoscopic procedure [18]. The risk of retrograde passage of carbon dioxide from the abdomen to the brain is minimal with advances in biomaterials and biomedical engineering of one-way valve system in VP shunt catheters [19]. The safety of laparoscopic procedures with VP shunts has always been controversial due to potential risk of an increase in ICP, shunt malfunction, and infection. There has been admixture of reports by various authors ranging from routine monitoring only versus more comprehensive monitoring of ICP and possible complications in shunted patients undergoing laparoscopic procedure. Collure et al. [19] published a series of 4 patients with VP shunts who had laparoscopic cholecystectomy without intraoperative ICP monitoring. There was no shunt related complications noted and they concluded safety of the procedure and the procedure did not require need of invasive ICP monitoring. One the other hand, the question regarding need for routine ICP monitoring intra-operatively also prevails [20]. There however is little published data and lack of well-designed studies to suggest any one option as the standard. Jackman et al. [21] retrospectively reviewed the anesthesia records of 18 patients with VP shunt who underwent 19 consecutive laparoscopic operations, looking for signs of increased ICP. They did not document any evidence of clinically increased ICP and concluded that just routine anesthetic monitoring suffices without having to do invasive ICP monitoring. In our own case, we also did not do invasive ICP monitoring and just used intraoperative closed monitoring of the patient to see for signs of raised ICP pertaining to Cushing's reflex. Fraser et al. [22] retrospectively reviewed shunted pediatric patients who underwent laparoscopic and open abdominal operations in their institute in the period from 1998 to 2008. In the study 51 patients underwent laparoscopic surgery out of 99 patients. They reported that there was no air embolism into the shunt in the laparoscopic group. Shunt infection occurred in one patient in the laparoscopic group in comparison to 3 patients in the open group. Yoshihara et al. [23] clamped shunt catheter during pneumoperitoneum and intraabdominal pressure was kept at 8 mmHg. In their series of four patients, two with ventriculoperitoneal shunts and two with lumboperitoneal shunts, no shunt related complications were encountered. Another retrospective study reported setting insufflation pressure at 10 mmHg with routine anesthetic monitoring and no manipulation of shunt system during the laparoscopic procedure in four patients and did not observe any complication [24]. In a case report by Baskin et al. [25], they encountered radiographic evidence of shunt failure during laparoscopy feeding jejunostomy and had to do emergency shunt revision revealing isolated distal blockage; concluding the potential risk of laparoscopy in patients with VP shunt. There has been reports of laparoscopic cholecystectomy 10 days following VP shunt insertion by Schwed et al. [26] in a 73-year-old woman, with reported complication of subcutaneous emphysema and respiratory embarrassment, though patient later recovered without any postoperative shunt infection. Overall as per global literature there exist chances of intraoperative and postoperative complications of laparoscopic procedures in a patient with VP shunt in situ but with efficient skill sets and instrumental technicalities the incidence of complications can obviously be decreased.

Conclusions

Due to standard practice of VP shunt in patients with hydrocephalus and increased number of laparoscopic procedures, surgeons will encounter a situation where the requirement of such procedures coexists in a shunted patient. There always remains dilemma in such circumstances regarding how safe the procedures are for the fear of shunt malfunction and shunt related intra and postoperative complications. In this regard, the concern regarding increase in ICP secondary to increased IAP during the laparoscopic procedures are always a matter of debate. Though no strong evidence on the safety of laparoscopic procedures in shunted patients exists due to paucity of published data and patient pool, it is not an absolute contraindication especially when the surgery is performed considering special precaution and with direct or indirect measures to detect change in ICP during the procedure. Multidisciplinary team approach with involvement of Neurosurgery specialist team during the operative procedure so as to make sure any intraoperative complications do not get unnoticed should be utmost. To guide the operating surgeons there is need to construct standard protocol so as to disseminate a safer surgical practice for which there should be increase in reporting of such cases globally.
