Association of obstetrics and foetal ultrasound parameters with maternal serum Lamin A: A teaching institution-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Kumar Ranjit, Jiut Ram Keshari, Prabhakar Pankaj, Prakash Pritam, Rani Kirti, Prasad Dipali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Ultrasound imaging is commonly used during pregnancy to detect potential foetal anomalies. Lamin A (LMNA), a nuclear envelope protein, has been identified as a potential biomarker for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes. This study investigated the association of obstetrics and foetal ultrasound parameters with maternal serum LMNA during the first and second trimester of pregnancy.Methods:This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted at a teaching institution, Patna, in 2023–2025. The study included 100 singleton pregnancies at 11–24 weeks of gestation. Obstetrics data were collected at first visit of the patients in the Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Outpatient Department. Ultrasound imaging was performed using standard protocols during the first or second trimester to screen congenital anomalies in foetal. Blood sample was withdrawn to estimate the level of serum LMNA. Spearman correlation, Mann-Whitney test and receiver operating characteristics curve (ROC) were used to analyse the results.Results:A significant positive correlation was observed between LMNA and gestational age. Furthermore, maternal age, body mass index, and foetal nuchal translucency (NT) showed significant negative correlations with maternal LMNA levels. Moreover, LMNA levels were decreased in patients with increased NT (>3.5 mm; P < 0.001) as compared to the thinner NT (<3.5 mm). ROC analysis revealed a high diagnostic value of LMNA with NT for predicting anomalies. A significant positive association was also observed between abdominal circumference and maternal serum LMNA levels.Conclusion:The study demonstrated the biomarker potential of maternal serum LMNA for the diagnosis of prenatal congenital heart disease in terms of increased thickness in foetal NT.

Introduction

Ultrasonographic-based prenatal diagnosis has been developed as an important technique for the identification of possible anomalies, which enable judicious interventions and improved antenatal care and better outcomes for both mother and child.[1] Furthermore, prenatal ultrasound examination serves to determine gestational age, identify multiple gestations, assess embryonic viability, and also confirm the location of the pregnancy.[1] It has been demonstrated that congenital anomalies are a global problem that cause perinatal and infant deaths.[2] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), around 240,000 infants die every year across the globe due to congenital anomalies within their first 28 days of life.[3] Hence, conducting prenatal scans at an early stage plays a crucial role in assessing the prognosis and predicting birth outcomes.[4] In India, ultrasound imaging is extensively utilised to evaluate and monitor birth outcomes. Today, ultrasound is the key means for prenatal detection. It has been demonstrated that about half of all major structural abnormalities can be perceived in the first trimester (11–13 + 6) weeks using ultrasound, but there is wide disparity depending on the expertise and system involved.[5] Moreover, professional medical experts endorse the second-trimester anatomy scan, conducted between 18 and 22 weeks, as the benchmark for detecting foetal anomalies.[5] Furthermore, ultrasound results can be subjective and vary based on the skill of the doctors/technicians and other factors, whereas maternal serological tests are generally more specific and sensitive.[5]

Newer advances in biochemistry and molecular genetics have led to additional information with the serological markers.[6] The Lamins (60 to 80 kDa proteins) are the principal architectural framework of the nuclear cytoskeleton. They are situated on the inner surface of the nuclear membrane, where they offer a platform for the binding of proteins and chromatin, and confer mechanical stability.[7] Lamins are classified into two distinct types according to their structural attributes, protein features and expression patterns: A-type (Lamin A, Lamin C) and B-type (Lamin B1, Lamin B2).[78] Lamin A (LMNA) is essential for nuclear stability, cellular function such as maintaining stability of the cell’s structure, cell differentiation, chromosome organization, repairing of DNA damage, gene regulation, and telomere protection.[9] With the help of proteomics analysis, scientists have demonstrated that maternal LMNA has the potential for prenatal diagnosis of foetal abnormalities. Furthermore, a quantitative proteomics analysis revealed that isoform A of Prelamin-A/C (LMNA) was differentially expressed in more than 3 congenital heart defect subtypes groups, which had been verified by Multiple Reaction Monitoring Mass Spectrometry. Moreover, they also observed several differentially expressed proteins for the non-invasive diagnosis of preeclampsia.[10] It has been observed that all differentially expressed proteins belonged to belong to the cytoskeleton family. Scientists also observed that these cytoskeletal proteins were probably pregnancy-specific, and also found that serum concentration of LMNA was very low in nonpregnant women and began to rise during pregnancy. Furthermore, emerging evidence also suggests that a specific subset of cytoskeletal proteins is apparently reduced in the serum of women experiencing pregnancies complicated by Down syndrome and preeclampsia.[10] However, the precise mechanism underlying the downregulation of cytoskeletal proteins in congenital heart defects, Down syndrome and preeclampsia remains unclear.[10] It has been established that the level of LMNA is significantly reduced in foetal hearts with congenital heart defects as compared to the normal controls, suggesting that diminished LMNA in the foetal circulation may contribute to its decreased presence in maternal serum.[10] Therefore, the present study was designed to explore the association of obstetrics and foetal ultrasound parameters with maternal serum LMNA during the first and second trimester of pregnancy.

Materials and Methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, (147/IEC/IGIMS/2023) and written consent from patients was taken at the time of recruitment. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. This cross-sectional observational study was carried out between 2023 and 2025 at a teaching institution in Patna, Bihar, through a collaborative effort between the Departments of Biochemistry and Obstetrics and Gynaecology. This study presents findings from the interim analysis of an ongoing research investigation. A total of 100 singleton pregnant women, aged between 18 and 35 years and in their first or second trimester, were taken in the study. Pregnant women with the history of maternal malignancy, recent surgical procedure or organ transplantation, maternal blood transfusion, chronic maternal illnesses such as diabetes or renal disease and refusal to provide consent were excluded from the study. Ultrasound examinations of the foetal were conducted during either the first or second trimester, depending on the timing of the patient’s visit. The ultrasound examinations were performed by expert doctor (radiologist) using ultrasound machine (USS-H60; Samsung, South Korea, 100–240V, 620VA, 50/60Hz) to collect foetal ultrasound parameters such as nuchal translucency (NT), nasal bone (NB), crown-rump length (CRL), foetal heart rate (FHR), biparietal diameter (BPD), head circumference (HC), abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL). We collected the maternal weight, height, gestational age, BMI and arterial blood pressure measurements from all participants. Additionally, maternal blood was collected, and the serum was separated from all subjects. In brief, 5 mL venous blood sample was collected in separating gel vacuum tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 minutes. After that sample was centrifuged at 2500 × g at 4°C for 15 min. The serum was separated, aliquoted, and stored at –80°C until analysis. Maternal serum Lamin A (LMNA) level was estimated using a commercially available ELISA Kit (BT-LAB, Cat. No. E7656Hu), using ELISA reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany) as per the instructions of the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Office Excel (MSO H/S 2021) was used to record data of the study. Data were analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 20). The results were expressed as median and interquartile range for continuous variables. Spearman correlation coefficients were used for correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was used to assess specificity and sensitivity of LMNA in relation to maternal age, BMI, gestational age, and foetal ultrasound parameters. Additionally, Mann–Whitney test was applied to compare LMNA levels across nuchal translucency (NT) groups (<3.5 and >3.5 mm) and variations in blood pressure. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation between maternal serum LMNA and gestational age

Spearman correlation analysis was used to establish the relationship between LMNA and other parameters. Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between maternal serum LMNA levels and gestational age (ρ =0.815; 95% CI, 0.734 to 0.873; P < 0.01) [Figure 1].

Correlation between maternal serum LMNA and maternal age

We also correlated the maternal serum LMNA level with maternal age. It was observed that serum LMNA level was negatively associated with the maternal age (ρ = −0.328; 95% CI, −0.497 to − 0.135; P < 0.001) [Figure 2a]. We further executed ROC curve analysis to evaluate the diagnostic value of LMNA. The ROC curve analysis demonstrated diagnostic potential with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.902 (95% CI, 0.854 to 0.950) [Figure 2b].

Correlation between maternal serum LMNA and BMI

There was a negative association observed between maternal serum LMNA level and BMI (ρ = −0.357; 95% CI, −0.522 to −0.167; P < 0.001) [Figure 3a]. ROC curve analysis demonstrated AUC 0.863 (95% CI, 0.808 to 0.918) [Figure 3b].

Correlation between maternal serum LMNA and systolic and diastolic blood pressure

There was no significant correlation between systolic and diastolic blood pressure with maternal serum LMNA. However, there was a significant decrease in the level of maternal LMNA in the patients with blood pressure ≥140/90 (P < 0.001) [Figure 4].

Maternal serum LMNA level correlation with NT revealed that there was a significant negative correlation between LMNA expression with NT age ρ = −0.444; 95% CI, −0.593 to −0.265; P < 0.001) [Figure 5a]. Moreover, the ROC curve analysis of LMNA demonstrated an increased sensitivity (99%) and specificity (98%) to identify NT with an AUC of 0.959 (95% CI, 0.926 to 0.992) [Figure 5b].

Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a positive association between foetal abdominal circumference (AC) and maternal serum LMNA levels (ρ =0.238; 95% CI, −0.004 to 0.453; P < 0.05) [Figure 7a]. Analysis of the ROC curve revealed AUC 0.834 (95% CI, 0.762 to 0.906) [Figure 7b].

However, we did not observe a significant correlation between maternal serum LMNA levels and foetal parameters, such as NB, CRL, FHR, BPD, HC and FL.

Discussion

This study identified a correlation between maternal serum LMNA levels and several factors, including gestational age, maternal age, body mass index (BMI), and foetal ultrasound markers such as nuchal translucency (NT) and abdominal circumference (AC) during the first/second trimesters of pregnancy. Our findings are in agreement with earlier study wherein maternal serum LMNA level was significantly correlated with the gestational age.[11] It has been demonstrated that the level of LMNA started to increase at 12 weeks to second trimester during normal pregnancy. After that level of LMNA gradually fallen down at 35 week of gestation.[11] It has been proven that LMNA is a structural protein crucial for preserving the integrity and functionality of the cell nucleus. It has been also established that reduced levels of maternal serum LMNA have been linked to an elevated risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including congenital heart defects, neural tube abnormalities, and the onset of preeclampsia.[11]

Furthermore, we observed negative association of maternal serum LMNA levels with maternal age, i.e., levels of LMNA were found to be decreased with increase in the maternal age. This is the first study which shows association of maternal serum LMNA levels with maternal age. However, this variation was not reported by other research groups, potentially due to population heterogeneity such as change in genetic makeup, environmental factors, or lifestyle factors which may differentially affect LMNA expression. Additionally, an elevated risk of such complications was noted with increasing maternal age.[12] Moreover, ROC curve analysis (AUC = 0.902) of maternal age and LMNA levels in the present study highlighted their potential diagnostic utility. Furthermore, we found negative correlation between maternal LMNA levels with BMI. ROC curve analysis (AUC = 0.863) demonstrated its lower diagnostic utility as compared to the maternal age. In addition, elevated maternal BMI during pregnancy has also been shown to increase the risk of foetal malformations.[13] Thus, our study supported that levels of LMNA will be lower in the pregnant women with high BMI. This downregulation of maternal serum LMNA levels could potentially be used as a biomarker for certain adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, Chen group did not observe any correlation between maternal LMNA levels and BMI, which may be attributed to differential expression influenced by epigenetic modifications or population heterogeneity.

Likewise, we did not find any correlation between maternal serum LMNA levels and blood pressure of pregnant participants. However, there was a significant decrease in the levels of LMNA in the participants with blood pressure more than 140/90 mm of Hg. This finding is in agreement with an earlier study wherein these changes were observed in women with preeclampsia.[11] LMNA has been identified as a potential predictor of preeclampsia (PE), a major contributor to maternal and neonatal mortality. LMNA is expected to augment the diagnostic accuracy of preeclampsia when used alongside traditional metrics, although further research is warranted with a larger sample size to validate these findings.

Similarly, we observed negative correlation between maternal LMNA levels and foetal NT, i.e. decrease in the levels of maternal LMNA with increase in foetal NT. It has been demonstrated that increased NT thickness in a foetal is associated with an increased risk of congenital heart defects.[14] Thus, the level of maternal LMNA will be lowered with an increased risk of congenital heart defects in foetal which is in agreement with the earlier study.[11] Furthermore, ROC curve analysis of LMNA with NT revealed its increased sensitivity and specificity with AUC 0.959 which is close to 1. Ours is the first study to which shows association of maternal serum LMNA levels with NT thickness. This showed its high diagnostic value as compared to maternal age (AUC = 0.902). It has been well known that assessing foetal NT through an ultrasound technique is a part of the routine screening during the first-trimester test for chromosomal abnormalities, namely, Down syndrome.[1415] Moreover, it has been established that increased NT found to be linked with a wide range of chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 13 (T13, Patau syndrome), trisomy 21 (T21, Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (T18, Edward syndrome), triploidy and monosomy X (Turner’s syndrome, TS), etc.[1416] Furthermore, a significant reduction in maternal LMNA levels was observed in cases where nuchal NT thickness exceeded 3.5 mm, compared to those with NT below 3.5 mm. This finding suggests that maternal serum LMNA levels may be lower in pregnancies where the foetus is at increased risk for congenital heart defects. This finding corroborates with the earlier study.[11]

Furthermore, we found a positive association between maternal LMNA and foetal AC, which may reflect a gestational age–dependent relationship.[17] Its ROC curve analysis revealed a lower AUC (0.834) as compared to NT, which shows its lesser diagnostic utility. Further study may be needed with a larger sample size to determine the clinical significance of this finding.

In the present interim analysis, no significant correlation was observed between maternal serum LMNA levels and key foetal ultrasound parameters, including femur length (FL), head circumference (HC), foetal heart rate (FHR), and biparietal diameter (BPD). This lack of association suggests that maternal LMNA may not directly reflect foetal biometric growth or cardiac rhythm at the gestational stages assessed. Furthermore, these parameters are general indicators of foetal growth and may be less sensitive to variations in LMNA expression unless associated with underlying pathological conditions. Additionally, FL, HC, FHR, and BPD are primarily influenced by foetal developmental genes and overall foetal health, and may function independently of maternal LMNA levels.[1819] Further studies with a large sample size may help clarify whether LMNA exhibits temporal or condition-specific associations with foetal development. Moreover, socioeconomic status is also one of the most important factors responsible for the adverse pregnancy outcomes.[20] It should also be considered in further studies.

Furthermore, scientists have demonstrated that maternal serum levels of tumour necrosis factor-α, soluble Fas ligand, interleukin-6, vascular endothelial growth factor-D and transforming growth factor-α could be used for the identification of foetuses with heart failure.[21] In addition, it has also been reported that increased uric acid concentration in maternal amniotic fluid may serve as a biomarker for identifying foetal congenital heart disease during the prenatal period.[22] An important advantage of this study lies in identifying LMNA levels during routine prenatal assessments, offering potential insight into maternal and foetal health markers. By correlating maternal serum Lamin A levels with foetal growth and development indicators, the study may help identify early markers for pregnancy complications. The identification of biomarkers in maternal blood is broadly regarded as the ‘holy grail’ of prenatal diagnostics, offering a non-invasive and potentially transformative approach to early detection of foetal conditions.[10] Thus, the present study suggested the potential utility of LMNA as a biomarker for anticipating prenatal congenital heart disease.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the potential diagnostic utility of serum maternal LMNA for the diagnosis of prenatal congenital heart disease in terms of increased thickness in foetal NT and other adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as PE.

Limitations

The present study includes a relatively small sample size, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, as a single-centre investigation, its representativeness of the broader population is limited. Consequently, further multi-centre research involving a larger cohort is warranted to substantiate and extend these observations.
