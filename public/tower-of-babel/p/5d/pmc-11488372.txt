Transcarotid flow reversal for proximal control during cerebral aneurysm clip reconstruction: illustrative case
Authors: Nicole M Wenger, Matthew Hentschel, Ting I Wang, Kevin T Kim, Nicholas Caffes, Jacob Cherian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDParaclinoid aneurysms can pose an operative challenge during clip reconstruction, given the complex surrounding anatomy and the aneurysmal tendency to maintain turgor despite standard approaches to proximal control. This report demonstrates the use of intraoperative retrograde arteriovenous shunting with the transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) system to assist in the safe clip reconstruction of an irregular paraclinoid aneurysm.OBSERVATIONSA 33-year-old woman presented with perimesencephalic subarachnoid hemorrhage and was found to have an incidental 9-mm ophthalmic aneurysm. Coil embolization was not successful. During microsurgical clip reconstruction, the left common carotid artery was exposed to allow for proximal control as well as transcarotid arterial sheath placement. Flow reversal was instituted throughout the aneurysm dissection and clipping, with a visible softening of the aneurysm. Intraoperative angiography confirming successful clip reconstruction was performed utilizing the TCAR sheath. The case was complicated by the development of cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea postoperatively, requiring surgical repair. The patient has since made a complete recovery.LESSONSTranscarotid flow reversal utilizing the TCAR system has potential for use in the surgical treatment of paraclinoid aneurysms, as it may aid in softening the aneurysm for safer dissection and clip reconstruction, protect against aneurysm-associated emboli, and provide an avenue for intraoperative angiography. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24330

Paraclinoid aneurysms, originating along the internal carotid artery (ICA) from the distal dural ring to the posterior communicating artery, can be difficult to manage surgically due to their often larger size, adjacent bony and neurovascular anatomy, and morphological complexity.1, 2 Early surgical approaches to these aneurysms transitioned from common carotid artery (CCA) ligation to direct clipping around the late 20th century. Proximal control and aneurysm neck dissection often necessitate neck exposure and anterior clinoidectomy.2–5

Large and giant paraclinoid aneurysms tend to maintain turgor despite traditional techniques for proximal control. Retrograde suction decompression (RSD) techniques can make these aneurysms more amenable to clip reconstruction.6–8 In the popular description of the technique described by Batjer and Samson, the cervical ICA is exposed for temporary proximal control, an intracranial clip is placed on the ICA distal to the aneurysm, an angiocatheter is inserted into the trapped segment of the cervical ICA, and retrograde suction is applied for aneurysm deflation.7 A later variation utilizes the same approach but modifies the commonly called “Dallas technique” with endovascular access. Instead of direct surgical exposure of the cervical ICA, proximal control is gained by using a double-lumen balloon catheter placed transfemorally.9–12 Gentle aspiration in the inflated balloon catheter within the cervical ICA then allows for collapse of the aneurysm and permits dissection from the optic nerve and surrounding brain for clipping. Endovascular RSD techniques remove the need for cervical ICA exposure, theoretically reducing the risk of ICA dissection and mortality.

Building on these concepts for the control of paraclinoid ICA aneurysms, we describe the novel off-label use of the ENROUTE transcarotid neuroprotection system (Silk Road Medical; i.e., transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) system) during microsurgical clip reconstruction of a paraclinoid aneurysm. This system was originally developed for transcarotid access and has proven successful in providing embolic protection during carotid artery angioplasty and stenting procedures by allowing for reversible, retrograde blood flow across the cervical ICA via arteriovenous shunting to the femoral vein.13, 14

The TCAR system provides a convenient package in which to perform proximal control of proximal ICA aneurysms and allows for retrograde arteriovenous shunting, which could have value in permitting the softening of the aneurysm without creating a stagnant column of blood.15 By protecting against the mobilization of aneurysm-associated emboli while facilitating fundus collapse in the process, we show the potential for the TCAR system as a proximal control mechanism for the surgical treatment of a medium-sized ophthalmic ICA aneurysm. The platform also provides a ready method for performing intraoperative angiography to confirm correct clip reconstruction.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 33-year-old female with an extensive smoking history presented to the emergency department with the worst headache of her life but without neurological deficit. Computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a small amount of subarachnoid hemorrhage in the prepontine and interpeduncular cisterns (Fig. 1). CT angiography and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) did not reveal a clear source of the hemorrhage but did demonstrate a 9-mm multilobulated irregular left-sided paraclinoid aneurysm with a 3-mm neck (Fig. 2). Black-blood magnetic resonance angiography was performed and did not demonstrate mural enhancement of the paraclinoid aneurysm, corroborating its likely incidental nature. A second diagnostic DSA performed days later also failed to demonstrate a source for the subarachnoid hemorrhage. Given the patient’s young age and strong preferences, as well as the shape and size of the left ophthalmic artery aneurysm, coil embolization of the paraclinoid ICA aneurysm was attempted. Unfortunately, coiling was not successful due to the significant incorporation of the origin of the ophthalmic artery into the base of the aneurysm, which compromised flow in the ophthalmic artery during several attempts to place a coil frame.

Further options were discussed with the patient, including endovascular management with flow diversion, craniotomy for clipping the aneurysm, and observant management. Because of an unfortunate history of ongoing domestic violence, the patient declined flow diversion treatment due to the need for dual antiplatelet therapy. Observation was also deferred due to the patient’s young age and preference for definitive treatment.

Operative Course

Prior to the procedure, the patient was given methylprednisolone in anticipation of optic nerve manipulation. The patient was also given fosphenytoin for seizure prophylaxis, along with mannitol and antibiotic prophylaxis. Neuromonitoring electrodes were placed to monitor motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) throughout the case.

The patient was placed supine on an operative table with the bed turned 180˚ so that the head was opposite to the anesthesia team. The patient’s head was secured in radiolucent Mayfield pins and rotated 25˚ toward the right side with gentle extension and anterior translation. A curvilinear 3-inch incision was proposed behind the hairline. The location of the left common carotid artery (LCCA) was marked utilizing ultrasound guidance. The right groin was also prepped in anticipation of right common femoral vein (RCFV) access for the institution of flow reversal.

The RCFV was then accessed, and an 8-Fr sheath was introduced. The LCCA was then exposed through a 1-inch transverse neck incision centered on the sternocleidomastoid, two fingerbreadths above the clavicle. The LCCA was circumferentially dissected, and a vessel loop was passed twice around the artery in preparation for clamping the LCCA proximally. The LCCA was then punctured, and a 0.035-inch microwire was utilized to pass the TCAR sheath into the LCCA. The TCAR sheath was then connected to the previously placed common femoral vein (CFV) sheath utilizing a three-way stopcock (Fig. 3). A 4-cm left lateral supraorbital craniotomy was then performed through the cranial incision. A subfrontal approach was used to dissect the opticocarotid cistern. In order to visualize the entire ophthalmic aneurysm, an intradural anterior clinoidectomy was necessary, followed by release of the falciform ligament and distal dural ring. During these delicate dissections, the vessel loop around the LCCA in the neck was tightened, initiating flow reversal. This was done with a target systolic blood pressure of 140–160 mm Hg and a heart rate of greater than 70 beats per minute. SSEPs were closely monitored, and MEPs were obtained every 15 minutes without evidence of changes. With the intradural clinoidectomy completed and the aneurysm exposed, the release of the LCCA occlusion demonstrated a noticeable increase in aneurysm pulsatility. The aneurysm was then further dissected with flow reversal reinitiated by occluding the vessel loop on the LCCA.

Despite the use of flow reversal, the aneurysm remained full enough that a distal trapping clip was believed to be necessary during permanent clip application. This was done uneventfully with the placement of a temporary clip distal to the aneurysm neck on the ICA intracranially. With both flow reversal and distal clipping of the ICA, the left ICA ophthalmic origin was clip-reconstructed with an 8-mm Sugita sideward bent aneurysm clip. The temporary clip and occluding vessel loop were then removed. Flow reversal time was up to 15 minutes at a time, with a total flow reversal time of around 45 minutes.

Indocyanine green angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the aneurysm. A diagnostic angiogram was then obtained utilizing the LCCA TCAR sheath, which confirmed complete exclusion of the aneurysm but raised concerns for compromised filling of the ophthalmic artery and delayed visualization of the choroidal blush. On this basis, the clip position was readjusted, and a repeat angiogram demonstrated complete patency of the left ophthalmic artery. The TCAR sheath was then removed, and the CCA puncture site was closed with a previously placed 5-0 Prolene horizontal mattress suture. Standard dural and craniotomy closures were performed. Pressure was held over the CFV site after removal of the femoral vein sheath for 5 minutes. No neuromonitoring changes were noted at any point during the case. Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, the patient awoke with no neurological changes and was discharged home. She presented 2 weeks later with concerns for cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea. This would require nasoseptal flap repair by otolaryngology and lumbar drain placement. Formal postoperative DSA with three-dimensional reconstructions demonstrated uncomplicated reconstruction of the left ophthalmic artery origin with no residual aneurysm filling (Fig. 4). At the long-term follow-up, 12 months after surgical clipping, the patient continued to do well following surgery without any further issues. Discussion

Observations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first description of using the ENROUTE transcarotid neuroprotection system to assist in the proximal control and softening of an intracerebral aneurysm during craniotomy for aneurysm clipping. Using the TCAR system for retrograde arteriovenous shunting and proximal control allowed for significant softening of the aneurysm without creating a stagnant column of blood within the CCA and ICA. For this reason, we propose that with an active arteriovenous shunt, clamping of the CCA can be tolerated for longer periods of time. We performed anterior clinoidectomy, falciform ligament release, distal dural ring dissection, aneurysm preparation, and clip reconstruction, all under a period of flow reversal enabled through the TCAR system. Neuromonitoring interrogations throughout this period did not demonstrate concerns for ischemia.

In contrast, temporary clipping is a time-sensitive method for proximal control of the aneurysm. The maximum safe time window for temporary clipping is not exact; older studies have shown clinical safety at up to 10–20 minutes, while newer studies utilizing current neuromonitoring technology have demonstrated neuromonitoring changes at a mere 2.4–5 minutes.16–20 While the exact length of safe flow reversal time during TCAR is unknown, some studies suggest no increase in risk with increased flow reversal time;21 there were no changes in neuromonitoring during up to 15 minutes of continuous flow reversal, with approximately 45 minutes of total flow reversal in our case. There have been some notes made in the literature of intolerance to flow reversal, either transiently or after a certain period of reversal, which bears further investigation.22, 23

The TCAR system additionally provides easy access for intraoperative diagnostic angiography given the sheath already in place in the CCA. In this case, we were able to use the TCAR sheath to inject contrast and easily identify compromised filling of the ophthalmic artery prior to closure of the craniotomy. This avoided both the additional setup required for a separate access site to obtain an intraoperative angiogram and the time delay and re-opening of the craniotomy that could have occurred with a postoperative DSA.

Aneurysms fill in the direction of maximal flow from the proximal segment of the parent artery.24, 25 Thus, with the reversal of anterograde flow, the filling of the aneurysm should decrease, reducing the turgor in the aneurysm. Although flow reversal did soften the aneurysm, the aneurysm remained full enough that an additional distal clip was needed to safely clip the aneurysm. Of note, the external carotid artery (ECA) was not occluded in this case. It is possible that more softening might have been seen in the ICA with flow reversal alone had the ECA also been occluded.

If flow reversal alone is not enough to adequately soften the aneurysm, the TCAR setup simplifies performing RSD. A three-way stopcock is present at the junction of the TCAR and CFV sheaths. Although not performed in this case, this stopcock could be utilized to attach a syringe and provide active RSD if needed after a distal clip is placed on the intracranial ICA distal to the aneurysm. This blood could then be re-delivered in a sterile fashion at the end of the maneuver through the filter and back to the femoral vein, minimizing blood loss.

Although this case is promising, further experience is necessary to elucidate whether TCAR is a consistently effective method for obtaining proximal control and reducing turgor of aneurysms to perform definitive open surgical clipping safely and efficiently. Notably, the surgeon in this case is trained in both endovascular and open vascular techniques and is familiar with the TCAR procedure; when this is not the case, collaboration with colleagues to perform the TCAR procedure during microsurgical clipping will be necessary. Of note, as the ENROUTE transcarotid neuroprotection system is approved by the Food and Drug Administration for carotid artery angioplasty and stenting procedures, the use for proximal control during open surgical clipping of aneurysms constitutes an off-label use.26

Other possible applications of flow reversal include the treatment of giant ICA aneurysms and blister aneurysms. In both potential applications, the platform described in this work would facilitate standard methods to obtain proximal control of the diseased segment for dissection and treatment. For partially thrombosed aneurysms, flow reversal with arteriovenous shunting might reduce possible embolic complications of aneurysm dissection and treatment. Given the effects of flow reversal on aneurysm filling, endovascular treatment of aneurysms might also benefit. For example, flow reversal might reduce the inflow jet into large, wide-neck aneurysms and thereby facilitate navigation without utilizing more complex distal catheterization techniques such as the “around the world” method.27, 28 The TCAR system has been used by others to provide access for the endovascular treatment of intracranial aneurysms when femoral or radial access is not possible.29

Lessons

This technical description of transcarotid flow reversal for surgical clipping of a paraclinoid ICA aneurysm serves as a study of safety and possible efficacy in reducing barriers to safe open surgical treatment of paraclinoid ICA aneurysms. The TCAR system provides direct carotid access for proximal control and easy access for intraoperative diagnostic angiography; it also provides retrograde arteriovenous shunting to protect against mobilization of aneurysm-associated emboli and to facilitate aneurysm softening and safe surgical dissection. Utilizing the TCAR system during craniotomy for aneurysm clipping is a safe and promising option that combines both open and endovascular concepts to optimize the surgical treatment of paraclinoid ICA aneurysms. Not explored in this case was TCAR’s utilization for RSD or for other difficult aneurysm subtypes, and we suggest that the TCAR system deserves further exploration in the treatment of cerebral aneurysms.
