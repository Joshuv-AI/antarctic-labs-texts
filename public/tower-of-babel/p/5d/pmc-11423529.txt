(Fire)fighting the pandemic: PTSD and depression symptom profiles and longitudinal correlates
Authors: Winnie C A Ansah, Miriam J J Lommen, Lonneke I M Lenferink
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Firefighters face regular exposure to potentially traumatic events, which is associated with the development of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression. Despite the high comorbidity, there remains limited understanding of the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression in terms of symptom patterns. Due to the unique stressors added by the COVID-19 pandemic, it is relevant to also understand this comorbidity in light of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic.Objective: This study aimed to identify PTSD and depression symptom profiles in firefighters, and correlates thereof, using latent profile analysis (LPA).Method: 139 Dutch Firefighters (93% male) completed self-report surveys prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (T1) and after the outbreak COVID-19 pandemic (T2) on PTSD, depression, COVID-19 related stressors and other relevant correlates.Results: LPA demonstrated the best fit for a three-profile solution for PTSD and depression at T2 encompassing a low symptom profile (35%), a moderate symptom profile (46%), and an elevated symptom profile (19%). Profiles displayed mostly below clinical range scores for both PTSD and depression at T2. PTSD severity at T1, depression severity at T1, loneliness at T2, COVID-19-related stressors at T2 were significantly and positively related to PTSD and depression symptom profiles at T2.Conclusion: Our results shed light on the resilience of firefighters. Even in light of COVID-19 pandemic, firefighters seemed to maintain generally low levels of psychopathology. Despite firefighters’ overall resilience, our findings highlight loneliness and the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic as potential risk factors for increased severity of PTSD and depression symptoms.KEYWORDS: Firefighters, PTSD, depression, latent profile analysis, COVID-19

Introduction

Firefighters play an indispensable role in our society as first responders who bravely risk their lives to protect and save others. The inherent nature of their profession exposes them to an array of hazards and a lack of substantial control, thereby subjecting them to diverse stressors. Firefighters regularly experience traumatic stress due to exposure to potentially traumatic events (PTEs), such as death and incidents including catastrophic injuries (Beaton et al., 1998). They are also exposed to occupational stress through atypical working schedules, risk of job-related injuries, and exposure to hazardous material (Sawhney et al., 2018). Both traumatic and occupational stressors have been found to negatively impact the mental and physical wellbeing of firefighters and has been shown to put firefighters at an increased risk for developing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; Corneil et al., 1999) and depression (Carey et al., 2011).

According to the ‘building block effect’, individuals exposed to an increased number of traumatic events are more likely to develop PTSD (Schauer et al., 2003). However, considering firefighters’ relatively high trauma load, PTSD rates in this population are relatively low with a point prevalence of 9% (Martínez & Blanch, 2024), compared with a cross-national lifetime prevalence of 3.9% based on population surveys and 5.6% in those who were exposed to trauma (Koenen et al., 2017). Despite the comparatively low rates, PTSD is a detrimental and often chronic disorder that can severely negatively impact an individual, their social environment, and occupation (Galovski & Lyons, 2004), leading individuals to face significant emotional and economic challenges (Bryant et al., 2010; O’Donnell et al., 2005; Stander et al., 2014). Furthermore, the burden of PTSD is expected to be more pronounced for those who also have comorbidities (Knowles et al., 2019).

PTSD is highly comorbid with depression in the general population (Breslau et al., 1991; Creamer et al., 2001; Kessler et al., 1995) as well as in firefighters (Jitnarin et al., 2022). Individuals affected by both PTSD and depression tend to have more severe symptoms and greater impairment as compared to individuals with only PTSD (Momartin et al., 2004). Despite their high comorbidity, little is hitherto known about the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression, in terms of symptom patterns. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is one way to investigate this as it goes beyond means and prevalence rates by examining the co-occurrence of symptom profiles. LPA is a commonly used statistical technique to identify hidden subgroups within a population based on similarities in response patterns.

PTSD and depression profiles in samples exposed to potentially traumatic events

Several LPA studies have been conducted to examine the co-occurrence between PTSD and depression profiles in people exposed to PTEs. For example, a LPA study by Burton et al. (2021) used a sample of individuals with chronic PTSD to investigate profiles based on PTSD and depression. Three distinct profiles emerged with individuals in the largest profile scoring both high on PTSD and depression symptomatology, followed by a profile scoring high on PTSD and low on depression, and the smallest profile scoring low on PTSD and moderate on depression. In another LPA study investigating distinct PTSD and depression profiles in Chinese earth quake survivors, a four-profile solution was found. The largest profile included low symptoms of PTSD and depression, followed by a profile characterized by predominantly PTSD, then a profile including predominantly depression, and the smallest profile scoring high on both PTSD and depression (Cao et al., 2015).

Similarly, an LPA on the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression in female sexual assault survivors identified four different profiles at 1-.2-,3-, and 4-months post assault where profile assignment at month 1 was significantly related to profile assignment at the later timepoints. Profiles ranged from low, low to moderate, high to moderate, and severe symptom levels of both PTSD and depression (Au et al., 2013). The profiles were at none of the four time points exclusively characterized by PTSD or depression symptoms, contradicting the findings of Cao et al. (2015) and Burton et al. (2021). The presence of both disorders in each profile in Au et al. (2013) emphasizes the comorbidity and lack of distinction between the two conditions. These divergent findings in terms of profile characteristics between Au et al. (2013), Burton et al. (2021) and Cao et al. (2015) may be attributed to the differences in the nature of trauma experienced by the respective samples.

Taken together, these studies highlight the existence of distinct profiles when examining the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression. Profiles differ in terms of symptom severity and the relative prominence of PTSD and depression symptoms. While these investigations have provided valuable insights into PTSD and depression among samples exposed to PTEs, limited knowledge exists regarding PTSD and depression symptom-profiles in first responders. Considering the frequent and long-term exposure of first responders to PTEs, it is crucial to investigate the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression in this population. Therefore, the first aim of this study was to identify subgroups of firefighters that are distinguishable in terms of PTSD and depression symptom profiles.

Correlates of PTSD and depression

With the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in the beginning of 2020, risk and uncertainty of the occupation of firefighters was amplified. First responders already face increased risk for developing psychiatric disorders, this risk increases during unusual and particularly stressful events (Fullerton et al., 2004; Luft et al., 2012), such as the COVID-19 pandemic (McAlearney et al., 2022). Vujanovic et al. (2021) studied the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of first responders, including firefighters. They found that especially COVID-19-related worry was associated with PTSD and depression symptoms. Another study by Wright et al. (2021) showed that emergency personnel, including firefighters, with an immunocompromised condition had higher odds of having traumatic stress, depression, and anxiety. Additionally, COVID-19 pandemic precautions such as social distancing impacted the work and mental health of firefighters. Removal from social connections, for instance feelings of loneliness, is associated with adverse psychological outcomes such as PTSD, depression, and anxiety (Erzen & Çikrikci, 2018; Fox et al., 2021; Morina et al., 2021). Zolnikov and Furio (2021) found that social distancing prohibited firefighters to carry out their work efficiently during the COVID-19 pandemic, led to feelings of loneliness, and prevented them from getting the social support needed during these challenging times. Findings therefore suggest that social distancing might have contributed to poorer mental health in firefighters during the peak times of the COVID-19 pandemic.

In addition to COVID-19 stressors, both increased work experience (Regehr et al., 2003) and exposure to PTEs (Buchanan et al., 2001; Heinrichs et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2017; Skeffington et al., 2017) show associations with psychopathology. Additionally, there is a gender difference in the prevalence of PTSD and depression in the general population, with women being diagnosed at a higher rate in both disorders compared to men (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1987; Tolin & Foa, 2006; Weissman & Klerman, 1977). Therefore, research suggests the inclusion of work experience, exposure to PTEs, and gender, in addition to COVID-19 stressors, as relevant correlates to investigate the presence of latent profiles in firefighters’ PTSD and depression. Therefore, the second aim of this study was to examine correlates of PTSD and depression in symptom profiles.

Purpose of study and hypotheses

The aforementioned findings offer valuable understanding into the overall prevalence of PTSD and depression among firefighter populations; however, they lack insight into potentially different patterns of PTSD and depression symptomatology in firefighters and their associated risk factors. Yet, elucidating such typologies is important to gain an understanding of heterogeneity in symptom profiles, perform risk assessments, and develop tailored interventions.

Therefore, this study employs LPA to advance the existing findings by analyzing data from two time points: the first measurement taken before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and the second measurement captured during the COVID-19 pandemic. The first aim of our study was to identify subgroups of firefighters that are distinguishable in terms of PTSD and depression symptom profiles. Based on prior LPA research on PTSD and depression in samples exposed to non-occupational PTEs (Au et al., 2013; Burton et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2015; Hruska et al., 2014), we expected to identify at least three profiles, including a profile scoring low on both PTSD and depression, at least one profile with moderate levels in which either PTSD or depression might be potentially dominant, and a profile scoring high on both PTSD and depression.

Our second aim was to examine correlates of PTSD and depression symptom profiles. More specifically, we anticipated that firefighters who belong to more pervasive PTSD/depression profiles were more likely to be female (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1987; Tolin & Foa, 2006; Weissman & Klerman, 1977), have more work experience (Regehr et al., 2003), and were exposed to higher number of PTEs (Buchanan et al., 2001; Heinrichs et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2017; Skeffington et al., 2017). Furthermore, we expected that firefighters who belong to more pervasive PTSD/depression profiles to have higher pre-pandemic levels of PTSD and depression (Fullerton et al., 2004; Luft et al., 2012). In addition, we also expected that firefighters with higher levels of COVID-19-stressors, including loneliness and negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, belonged to more pervasive PTSD/depression profiles (Vujanovic et al., 2021; Zolnikov & Furio, 2021).

Method

Recruitment and procedure

This study is part of a larger project aiming to understand firefighter’s mental health. Both volunteer and professional firefighters from 11 out of the 25 safety regions in the Netherlands participated in this study. In the summer of 2017, all participating fire departments were visited by the research team to provide information about the study and to obtain informed consent. Firefighters in the involved departments were given the choice to decide on their participation in the study. Data were collected at multiple timepoints after the start in the summer of 2017. However, for this study only data from September 2019, from here on referred to as timepoint 1 (T1), and data collected between May to September 2020, from here on referred to as timepoint 2 (T2), were used. T1 is the last measure before the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in the Netherlands and T2 is the first measurement point after the outbreak of the COVID-19 in the Netherlands. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee Psychology of the University of Groningen, Netherlands.

Measures

The posttraumatic stress disorder checklist (PCL-5)

The 20-item PCL-5 was used to assess PTSD symptom severity (Blevins et al., 2015; Boeschoten et al., 2014). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely). The total symptom severity score ranges from 0 to 80. An example item is ‘In the past month, how much were you bothered by repeated, disturbing, and unwanted memories of the stressful experience?’. Psychometric properties of PCL-5 were good for English and Dutch translations (Blevins et al., 2015; Bovin et al., 2016; Van Praag et al., 2020; Wortmann et al., 2016). In previous research, a score of 33 or higher has been considered as indicative of clinically relevant levels of PTSD (Wortmann et al., 2016). Cronbach’s alpha was .94 at T1 and .96 at T2.

Beck depression inventory (BDI)

The 21-item BDI was used to assess depression symptom severity (Beck et al., 1961; van der Does, 2002). Each item is rated on 4-point Likert scales ranging from 0 to 3. Total symptom severity scores range from 0 to 63. Psychometric properties of BDI were good for English and Dutch translations (Bosscher et al., 1986; Wang & Gorenstein, 2013). An example item is ‘0 = I do not feel sad, 1 = I feel sad, 2 = I am sad all the time and I can't snap out of it, 3 = I am so sad and unhappy that I can't stand it’. A score of 0–9 indicates mild depression, 10–18 mild to moderate depression, 19–29 moderate to severe depression, and 30–63 severe depression (Beck et al., 1988). Cronbach’s alpha was .79 at T1 and .83 at T2.

PTE exposure

To assess the number and variety in exposure to PTEs witnessed firefighters, a questionnaire was created in close collaboration with firefighters. This questionnaire contained 22 PTEs that are common in the work of firefighters. Firefighters were asked to answer if they had experienced this event (yes/no). The total score ranges from 0 to 22 and is calculated by adding the number of events rated as ‘yes’. This measurement tool was specifically designed for this study and has not been used nor validated in other research contexts. Events included in this measure are, besides others, experiencing stress or panic among victims or bystanders and witnessing victims of smoke intoxication. PTEs were assessed at T1.

Negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic

At T2, the extent of the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the lives of firefighters was assessed by using a self-developed scale comprising 8 items rated on 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). The total score ranges from 8 to 56 and is calculated by taking the mean of the item scores. This measurement tool was specifically designed for this study and has not been used nor validated in other research contexts. An example item is ‘To what extent has your life been negatively impacted by events related to the coronavirus?’ Cronbach’s alpha at T2 was .66.

The De Jong Gierveld loneliness scale (DJGLS)

The 11-item DJGLS was used to assess loneliness (De Jong-Gierveld & Kamphuls, 1985; De Jong-Gierveld & Van Tilburg, 1999). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘No!’ to ‘Yes!’. Six items of this scale are negatively formulated (‘I miss having a really close friend’) and five items are positively formulated (‘There is always someone I can talk to about my day-to-day problems’). Scores of the positively formulated items are reversed scored. Every item is considered a dichotomous variable where ‘No!’, ‘No’, ‘More or less’ is considered as an indicator of loneliness on the positive items and where ‘Yes!’, ‘Yes’, ‘More or less’ is considered an indicator of loneliness on the negatively formulated items. The total score ranges from 0 to 11 and is obtained by summing the scores form both scales. Psychometric properties of PCL-5 were good (De Jong Gierveld & Van Tilburg, 2010). An example item is ‘There is always someone I can talk to about my day-to-day problems’. Loneliness was assessed at T2. Cronbach’s alpha at T2 was .8.

Demographic variables

The demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, form of employment, and work experience. Age was assessed at T1 while the other demographic variables were assessed in July 2017, as part of a larger longitudinal data collection.

Statistical analyses

Before conducting LPAs, Z scores for T2 for PCL-5 and BDI were calculated to obtain similar scales. Firstly, a one profile model was calculated which was followed by further models, each increasing in their number of profiles up till six profiles. The best-fitting model was chosen based on statistical and non-statistical criteria. For the statistical criterion, emphasis was put on relatively lower values of both the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and sample size adjusted BIC (SA-BIC). Further, relatively higher Entropy R2 values were also considered where values closer to 1 indicate increased profile separation and values > .8 are considered appropriate (Van De Schoot et al., 2017). Lastly, the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio test (BLRt) was also taken into account, whereby a significant p-value (p < .05) indicates that the model under consideration has a significantly better fit than the model with one less profile. Non-statistical criteria included comparisons of sample sizes per profile, as sample sizes of profiles should be at least 5% of the total sample size to represent a meaningful latent entity (Nylund et al., 2007). Interpretation of profiles were also taken into account, in which models were preferred that aligned with prior theoretical and empirical work.

The correlates of profile-membership were examined using the three-step approach in LatentGold. The advantage of this approach is its implementation of the bias-adjusted three step method, through which the classification error that results from assigning units to profiles with their highest probability estimate is accounted for (Vermunt, 2010). Correlates of profile-membership were estimated using the maximum-likelihood correction method. Univariate analyses were used to examine whether profiles differed in terms of PTSD prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, depression prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, loneliness, work experience, gender, and PTEs by including each correlate separately in the model. Subsequently, multivariate analyses were conducted including all correlates that were significantly related to profile-membership in the prior univariate analyses. This was done to examine which variables most strongly correlate with profile-membership when their shared variance is taken into account.

Missing data

As this study is part of a larger longitudinal data collection, only participants who filled out the PCL-5 at T2 were included in this study. A total of 139 firefighters filled out the PCL-5 at T2 while 99% of firefighters completed the BDI at T2. As this is a missingness percentage of only 1%, we chose to continue by using full maximum likelihood estimation implemented as the default method for handling missing data for LPAs in LatentGOLD (version 5.0.0) (Vermunt & Magidson, 2013).

With regards to the correlates, 83.5% of the firefighters had complete data on the correlates (PTSD at T1, depression at T1, PTEs at T1, negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic at T2, loneliness at T2, work experience at T1, and gender at T1), while 16.5% of firefighters had some missing data. The only correlate with missing data larger than 5% was PTSD at T1 (11.5%). According to the Little’s Missing Completely at Random test (p = .93), data were missing completely at random, meaning that all the observations have an equal likelihood of being missing. Missing data on the correlates in both the univariate and multivariate analysis was handled by using the LatentGOLD’s default imputation procedure (Vermunt & Magidson, n.d.).

The majority worked both professionally and voluntary as firefighters (40%), while 35.3% worked only professionally and 25% worked only voluntary.

Discussion

This study investigated symptom-profiles of PTSD and depression in firefighters during the COVID-19 pandemic. The research utilizes a longitudinal design, collecting data at two timepoints, with the first measurement before the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic and the second measurement after the COVID-19 pandemic started. The study findings indicate the identification of three symptom profiles of PTSD and depression, with PTSD and depression levels below clinical thresholds. Loneliness and COVID-19 stressors were identified as potential risk factors for PTSD and depression symptoms in firefighters. This study is the first of its kind to explore how firefighters’ PTSD and depression levels before the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, together with COVID-19 stressors, loneliness, work experience, and trauma exposure, were related to PTSD and depression symptom-profiles after the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic. Recognizing the psychological well-being of firefighters is essential given the unique stressors associated with their profession. The additional challenges introduced by the COVID-19 pandemic amplify the urgency of understanding and addressing these issues.

The first aim of this study was to identify subgroups of firefighters based on their PTSD and depression symptom assessed at T2, i.e. after the COVID-19 outbreak. An LPA revealed three distinct profiles, each characterized by varying levels of symptom severity. The low symptom profile comprised one third of the sample and exhibited consistently low levels of PTSD and depression at T2. The moderate symptom profile, which was most common (i.e. 46%), showed generally below threshold scores on both PTSD and depression at T2, though slightly higher than the low symptom profile. The elevated symptom profile, representing one fifth of the firefighters, displayed relatively high levels of PTSD and depression at T2. These results are partly in line with our hypothesis and prior research identifying at least three profiles characterized by varying levels of PTSD and depression. Yet the majority of our profiles were characterized by non-clinical levels of symptomatology. Despite the classification into varying levels of symptom severity, PTSD scores consistently remain below the clinical range across all profiles. Likewise for depression, only the elevated symptom profile meets the threshold for mild to moderate depression, while the other profiles fall below the clinical range. These findings can enhance clinical practice by providing a more nuanced understanding of PTSD and depression in firefighters, which can be incorporated in training and education for mental health professionals working with these first responders.

The second aim of this study was to examine correlates of PTSD and depression symptom profiles. Findings are partially in line with our hypotheses, revealing significant associations between PTSD and depression severity at T1 and symptom profiles. Contrary to expectations, no links were found with work experience at T1 or PTE exposure at T1. The current study thereby sheds light on the resilience and vulnerability of firefighters in the face of diverse stressors. Similar to previous research, our findings underscore the resilience of individuals frequently exposed to PTEs, marked by generally low psychopathology in light of their frequent encounters with PTEs (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018; Lenferink et al., 2022). Even in light of COVID-19 stressors, firefighters seemed to maintain generally low levels of psychopathology. This finding is in line with cross-sectional studies from other European countries showing that firefighters had lower distress and higher resilience when compared to other groups of first responders and health care workers (Pink et al., 2021). In addition, they had higher levels of psychological well-being as compared to the general population during the COVID-19 pandemic (Riccardi et al., 2023). This might be related to the fact that firefighters experienced relatively fewer job-related impacts from COVID-19 restrictions due to the systemically relevant nature of their job compared to the effects these restrictions had on the general population. For instance, while many industries faced closures and layoffs, firefighters continued to play a crucial role in emergency response and public safety. Their essential status meant that they were less affected by job-related changes and financial challenges, providing a level of stability that was not as readily available to other sectors.

However, the study also unravels significant associations between specific stressors, notably those related to the COVID-19 pandemic, and the severity of PTSD and depression symptoms among firefighters. Thereby implying that firefighters experiencing heightened levels of both loneliness and a more substantial negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic are more likely to exhibit greater severity of PTSD and depression symptoms. These findings align with existing literature on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and mental health outcomes (Gabarrell-Pascuet et al., 2023; Golińska et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022). Together, these findings imply that COVID-19 stressors seem to be a risk factor for psychopathology, even in individuals without prior psychiatric diagnoses.

While our findings underscore the group-level resilience of firefighters, there may still be instances at the individual level where psychopathology develops. A watchful waiting approach therefore becomes relevant, considering the possibility of delayed onset of PTSD (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018), which tends to be higher in first responders (Utzon-Frank et al., 2014). Research also underscores the effectiveness of watchful waiting in addressing depression (Kostic et al., 2024; Van Der Aa et al., 2015). Direct intervention does not seem necessary, aligning with evidence that routine utilization of psychological interventions cannot be recommended following traumatic events as early interventions post-PTEs is not more effective than watchful waiting (Covers et al., 2021; Roberts et al., 2009). Despite firefighters’ overall resilience, our findings highlight loneliness and the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic as potential risk factors for increased severity of PTSD and depression symptoms among firefighters. This highlights the importance of assessing or evaluating loneliness and COVID-19 impact in these first responders, and to invest in (preventive) strategies to counter feelings of loneliness and mitigate impact of COVID-19 stressors. Research shows that especially cognitive and educational interventions and the facilitation of peer communication helped in reducing social isolation and loneliness related to the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population (Williams et al., 2021). Specifically for firefighters, research shows that informal peer support is effective in helping them to cope with critical incidents and reduce the impact that PTEs have on them (Dangermond et al., 2022; Varvel et al., 2007). It is therefore advisable to actively promote and facilitate opportunities for such informal peer support.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. Firstly, the small sample size employed in this investigation may have limited the number of profiles that we were able to identify and may as well prevented us to detect differences between the profiles. Furthermore, the self-selection bias within the sample introduces another layer of limitation, potentially affecting the external validity of the study. Participants who voluntarily opted to be part of the study may possess characteristics distinct from the broader population of interest, introducing a potential source of bias. For example, distrust towards research, mental health stigma, and higher levels of anxiety have been identified as barriers to participating in research (Gouveia et al., 2022; Woodall et al., 2010). Additionally, the instrument used to assess the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic lacks formal validation and showed an alpha coefficient of 0.66, indicating low internal consistency (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). Newly created for this study due to the urgency of the COVID-19 pandemic, it has not undergone validation in other contexts. To the best of our knowledge, no other validated measure existed prior to this research. An additional potential limitation is the reliance on self-report measures, which may introduce the risk of overestimating symptom severity compared to clinical interviews (Kramer et al., 2023). While self-report measures are valuable for their efficiency, the potential for subjective bias highlights the importance of cautious interpretation of symptom severity in our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this longitudinal study investigated symptom-profiles of PTSD and depression in firefighters during the COVID-19 pandemic and reveals a resilient occupational group with generally low psychopathology, even amidst the challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic. The identification of three distinct symptom profiles underscores the heterogeneity in their mental health experiences, yet the majority of profiles displayed below clinical range scores for both PTSD and depression, emphasizing their overall resilience on a group-level. Yet, on an individual level, there may still be instances where psychopathology develops. Based on previous research, a watchful waiting approach and the promotion of informal peer support is recommended to address specific risk factors and enhance the psychological well-being of firefighters.
