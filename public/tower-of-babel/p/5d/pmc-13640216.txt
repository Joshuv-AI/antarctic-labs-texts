Background

Sural nerve injury is an uncommon but recognized complication of foot and ankle surgery, particularly following procedures involving the lateral aspect of the heel and Achilles tendon region [1]. The sural nerve is vulnerable to traction, direct injury, or postoperative fibrosis because of its superficial anatomical course.

Sural nerve entrapment may result from trauma, scar tissue formation, space-occupying lesions, or iatrogenic causes [2]. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific, including pain, dysesthesia, burning sensation, or paresthesia along the lateral aspect of the foot, which may contribute to delayed or missed diagnosis [3].

Although postoperative sural neuropathies have been described after Achilles tendon surgery and ankle arthroscopy, reports following surgery for Haglund deformity remain extremely limited in the literature. We report a case of postoperative sural nerve entrapment following open surgery for Haglund deformity, managed with surgical neurolysis and adjunctive application of oxidized regenerated cellulose (ORC).

Case presentation

A 56 year old woman with no significant medical history presented ankle pain, with numbness in the lateral part of the ankle. Her medical history was notable for open surgery for Haglund’s deformity performed 12 months earlier. After six months of conservative treatment, including physical therapy and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, symptoms persisted. Clinical evaluation revealed paraesthesia in the sensory distribution of the sural nerve along the lateral foot and ankle. Physical examination identified a trigger point at the distal portion of the previous surgical scar. Pressure over this point reproduced pain radiating along the course of the sural nerve (see Fig. 1).

Distal to this area, Tinel’s sign was positive. A diagnostic injection of 2% mepivacaine at the trigger point resulted in complete pain relief lasting approximately six hours, supporting the diagnosis of sural nerve involvement.

Ultrasonography and plain radiographs were unremarkable. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to evaluate both osseous and soft tissue structures and demonstrated reduced signal intensity of the sural nerve compared with the contralateral side, supporting the suspicion of nerve entrapment (Fig. 2).

Surgical exploration was performed under regional anaesthesia through an extension of the previous incision along the lateral aspect of the foot. Intraoperatively, injury to the small saphenous vein and entrapment of the sural nerve within fibrotic scar tissue were identified. Careful dissection and complete neurolysis were performed to release the nerve (see Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusion

Sural nerve entrapment is an uncommon and likely underdiagnosed condition, particularly when occurring after foot and ankle surgery. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may include pain, burning sensation, and sensory disturbances along the lateral aspect of the foot, which can overlap with other musculoskeletal disorders [3].

Several etiologies have been described. Traumatic mechanisms include ankle sprains, instability, and fractures [2, 4], while space-occupying lesions such as intraneural ganglia, osteochondromas, and ossified lesions have also been reported [5–8]. In addition, post-traumatic fibrosis and adhesions represent a relevant cause of entrapment [9–11]. Iatrogenic sural neuropathies have been described after Achilles tendon surgery, ankle arthroscopy, and sural nerve biopsy [12–14], whereas reports following surgery for Haglund deformity remain extremely limited.

Diagnosis of sural nerve entrapment remains challenging due to the absence of specific imaging or clinical criteria. In the present case, diagnosis was based on the combination of clinical findings, including a focal trigger point along the surgical scar, a positive Tinel’s sign, and temporary symptom relief after local anesthetic infiltration. This diagnostic approach is consistent with previously reported strategies for peripheral nerve entrapment syndromes [15].

Magnetic resonance imaging was used as a complementary tool and supported the clinical suspicion by demonstrating asymmetry of the sural nerve compared with the contralateral side. However, MRI findings in peripheral nerve entrapment may be subtle or nonspecific and should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical findings [16, 17].

The diagnosis of peripheral nerve entrapment remains primarily clinical, as imaging findings may be subtle or nonspecific. In the present case, MRI findings were interpreted in conjunction with focal symptoms, physical examination findings, and response to diagnostic local anesthetic injection. Electrodiagnostic studies and high-resolution ultrasound may provide additional information in selected cases but were not contributory or were not performed in this patient, representing a limitation of the present report.

Surgical exploration confirmed entrapment of the sural nerve within fibrotic scar tissue. Neurolysis was performed with complete release of the nerve. Given the known risk of recurrent fibrosis after neurolysis, a membrane of oxidized regenerated cellulose (ORC) was applied as an interposition barrier. ORC has been widely used in surgical practice as a hemostatic and anti-adhesion material [18, 19]. However, tissue reactions including granulomatous inflammation and foreign body response have also been described [20–22].

Its role in peripheral nerve surgery remains uncertain. Experimental studies evaluating ORC for prevention of perineural fibrosis have reported conflicting findings, with some studies failing to demonstrate a significant reduction in scar formation or improvement in nerve healing [23, 24]. Consequently, current evidence supporting ORC application in peripheral nerve procedures remains limited and largely experimental.

In the present case, ORC was used as an adjunctive barrier following neurolysis because of concern for recurrent scar formation. Although the patient experienced complete symptom resolution at 24-month follow-up, with no adverse effects, no causal relationship between ORC application and clinical outcome can be established from a single case report.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to a single case report, including lack of control group and inability to determine the specific contribution of ORC to the clinical outcome. Further studies are required to evaluate its role in preventing perineural fibrosis.

Conclusions

Postoperative sural nerve entrapment represents a rare but clinically relevant complication following lateral heel surgery. Diagnosis relies mainly on clinical evaluation supported by imaging and diagnostic injections. Surgical neurolysis may provide symptom relief in selected patients. Although ORC was used as an adjunctive anti-adhesion material in this case, its clinical benefit remains uncertain and requires further investigation.
