Association between cumulative blood pressure and the risk of cognitive impairment in Chinese older adults: a longitudinal study over 16 years
Authors: Ying Gao, Peng Zhang, Hongxi Yang, Shu Li, Haiyan Su, Qing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCumulative blood pressure (BP), which takes into account both the magnitude and duration of BP exposure, is linked to cognitive impairment. However, this association remains unclear in Chinese older adults. The critical level of cumulative BP associated with significantly increased cognitive impairment risk remains elusive. Thus, this study aims to investigate the association between cumulative BP levels and the risk of cognitive impairment in Chinese older adults.MethodsThe Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) over 16 years was divided into two consecutive sub-cohorts, namely the 2002 sub-cohort from 2002 to 2011 and the 2008 sub-cohort from 2008 to 2018. Cumulative BP exposures were calculated as the area under the curve derived from two consequence BP measurements and their corresponding time intervals. Cognitive impairment was defined according to the years of schooling and total cognitive scores of older adults during the follow-up periods. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of cognitive impairment across quartiles of cumulative BP were estimated using multivariable Cox proportional hazard models. Finally, potential dose-response relationships were examined using restricted cubic splines.ResultsA total of 2,142 and 1,920 cognitively healthy older adults participants from the two sub-cohorts were included in the analysis, respectively. Over a median follow-up of 6.2 years (IQR 6.1–6.3) and 7.0 years (IQR 6.8–7.2), 542 and 347 older adults experienced cognitive impairment in the two sub-cohorts, respectively. Higher cumulative systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and pulse pressure (PP) were significantly associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment (all Ptrend <0.01). Compared to the lowest quartile in the two sub-cohorts, the HRs (95%CIs) for cognitive impairment risk in the highest quartile were 1.85 (1.33–2.59) and 2.64 (1.76–3.97) for cumulative SBP, 2.00 (1.45–2.75) and 2.20 (1.46–3.33) for cumulative DBP, and 1.59 (1.16–2.18) and 2.10 (1.42–3.10) for cumulative PP, respectively. Linear dose-response patterns were observed for the association between the risks of cognitive impairment with all three cumulative BP levels in both two sub-cohorts. Moreover, cumulative SBP increased from 387.8 mm Hg × year to 416.4 mm Hg × year over 6 years, whilst cumulative PP increased from 138.7 mm Hg × year to 170.8 mm Hg × year—equivalent to a mean SBP increase from 131.0 mm Hg to 134.8 mm Hg and mean PP increase from 46.9 mm Hg to 55.3 mm Hg—which collectively increased the risk of cognitive impairment. In contrast, cumulative DBP was maintained at 245 mm Hg × year over 6 years, whereas the mean DBP progressively decreased from 82.9 mm Hg to 79.3 mm Hg, showed a significant association with cognitive impairment.ConclusionsElevated cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP independently increased subsequent cognitive impairment risk in Chinese older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06465-9.

Background

The aging population has led to an escalating burden of cognitive impairment and dementia, establishing it as a major cause of dependence and disability [1, 2]. In 2019, it was estimated that 57.4 million individuals were living with dementia worldwide, a figure projected to increase to 152.8 million by 2050 [3]. Numerous studies has demonstrated that the incidence of dementia is on the decline in North America and Europe, potentially attributed to rising levels of educational attainment and improvements in the prevention and management of cardiovascular diseases, as well as their risk factors [4, 5]. However, Asian studies from China and Japan suggest that the age-specific prevalence of dementia is steadily rising, likely ascribed to dramatic changes in demographic patterns, including declining fertility rates and an aging population [6, 7]. Given the anticipated persistence of these demographic trends, the number of individuals affected by dementia is expected to increase substantially.

Without established effective interventions and treatments, there is a pressing need to recognize modifiable risk factors that could delay the onset or mitigate the risk of incident dementia [8, 9]. Epidemiological evidence synthesized by the 2020 Lancet Commission underscores midlife hypertension as a predictor of increased dementia incidence in later years [10]. Meanwhile, a UK cohort study reported that civil servants aged between 45 and 61 years with sustained high systolic blood pressure (SBP) (≥ 130 mm Hg) were at an elevated risk of dementia compared with those without hypertension, even in the absence of cardiovascular disease [11]. Likewise, the Framingham Offspring cohort determined that dementia risk was higher when hypertension continued into later life (mean age 69 years) [12]. Furthermore, a pooled analysis indicated that late-life (≥ 65 years) high SBP and low diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia [13]. Notably, longitudinal blood pressure (BP) patterns (e.g., time-integrated BP reflecting exposure intensity and duration) could be more indicative of the risk burden and vascular damage that result from prolonged elevated BP levels. This sustained and cumulative exposure may promote the progression of cognitive impairment, which serves as a precursor to progressive dementia [14, 15]. Contrasting with single-baseline BP measurement, repeated BP assessments demonstrated inconsistent associations with cognitive outcomes across the deterioration spectrum (decline, impairment, and dementia) [8, 9, 16–23]. It is important to mention that the majority of these studies have been exclusively conducted on the Western population and middle-aged individuals, with only three studies conducted on the Chinese population. Specifically, the first study explored global cognitive decline [8], another study investigated cumulative SBP associated with cognitive impairment [22], and the third recruited a limited number of patients [16].

Thus, the present study sought to explore the association between cumulative SBP, DBP, and pulse pressure (PP) with subsequent cognitive impairment in a nationally representative cohort of Chinese older adults across a 16-year follow-up period.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data were drawn from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), a nationwide, ongoing longitudinal survey that investigates determinants of healthy longevity in Chinese older population. Initiated in 1998, follow-ups were conducted every 2 to 3 years in approximately half of the counties, cities, and autonomous regions that were randomly selected across 23 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in the country. As previously reported [24, 25], CLHLS employs a cohort design with explicitly defined objectives and standardized protocols.

Given the anticipated high rate of lost to follow-up (73.9% over 16 years form 2002 to 2018) in the CLHLS cohort, we implemented a dual sub-cohort design with staggered baseline, namely the 2002 sub-cohort from wave 3 (2002) to wave 6 (2011) and the 2008 sub-cohort from wave 5 (2008) to wave 8 (2018) in the present study. The period from wave 3 (2002) to wave 4 (2005) was designated as the 2002 sub-cohort, whereas the period from wave 5 (2008) to wave 6 (2011) was denoted as the 2008 sub-cohort to evaluate cumulative BP. The period from wave 4 (2005) to wave 6 (2011) and the period from wave 6 (2011) to wave 8 (2018) in the two sub-cohorts was the measurement window to assess cognitive impairment. The timelines and design of this study are illustrated in Fig. 1. Participants who met any predefined exclusion criterion were excluded from study enrollment, as detailed below: (1) below 65 years old; (2) lacking blood pressure measurements in the exposure windows; (3) being lost of follow-up or deceased during the exposure windows; (4) having self-reported dementia or being determined to have cognitive impairment according to years of schooling and cognitive scores; (5) missing cognitive test results during the measurement windows.Fig. 1Study timelines and design

Study timelines and design

Ethics approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-13074) and Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB2024-YX-132-01). All participants or their proxy respondents provided written informed consent.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure was measured by trained and certified staff using standardized procedures in each survey. The time-dependent cumulative BP, which could be adjusted to represent each BP measurement prior to the event, was estimated by the area under the curve, consistent with the approach adopted in previous studies [9, 19]. Cumulative BP exposure was calculated by multiplying the arithmetic mean of serial BP measurements by the duration of the two-visit intervals. Cognitive impairment assessment

Participants were screened by trained and certified staff using the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), which comprised 24 items covering orientation, registration, naming, attention and calculation, recall, and language function to assess cognitive function during each survey. The response “unable to answer” was treated as incorrect. The total cognitive score spanned from 0 to 30, with higher values denoted superior cognitive performance [26]. According to the years of schooling and total cognitive scores of subjects, cognitive impairment was defined based on the following criteria: (1) years of schooling were 0, and MMSE scores were below 18; (2) years of schooling were between 1 and 6 and MMSE scores were below 21; (3) years of schooling were over 6, and MMSE scores were below 25 [27]. The outcome of interest was the first occurrence of cognitive impairment during the follow-up period. Cognitive impairment was identified using established criteria at specific survey waves. For the 2002 sub-cohort, identification occurred during the 2008 (wave 5) to 2011 (wave 6) surveys, and for the 2008 sub-cohort, identification occurred during the 2014 (wave 7) to 2018 (wave 8) surveys. The time of onset was defined as the survey date when cognitive impairment was first identified. Participants who developed cognitive impairment were considered to have experienced an event. Those who did not develop cognitive impairment were censored at the date of their last available record (specifically, 31 December 2011 for the 2002 sub-cohort and 31 December 2018 for the 2008 sub-cohort).

Covariates

Participants completed standardized questionnaires assessing variables such as age (years), gender (men and women), type of residence (urban, town, and rural), marital status (married, never married, separated, divorced, and widowed), years of schooling (0 years, 1–5 years, or over 6 years), current smoking status (yes or no), past smoking status (yes or no), current alcohol consumption (yes or no), past alcohol consumption (yes or no), current exercise habits (yes or no), past exercise habits (yes or no), self-reported quality of life (good/very good, fair, or poor/very poor), self-reported health (good/very good, fair, or poor/very poor), self-reported quality of sleep (good/very good, fair, or poor/very poor), self-reported history of hypertension (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), and stroke/cardiovascular disease (CVD) (yes or no) at baseline. Weight (in kg) was also measured twice during the exposure windows in both sub-cohorts.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using median (IQR) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables in the two sub-cohorts. Cohort-specific cumulative SBP quartile boundaries were defined as follows: for cumulative SBP, the 2002 and 2008 sub-cohorts exhibited cutoff points of 360, 387, and 416 mm Hg × year versus 383, 416, and 456 mm Hg × year; for cumulative DBP, corresponding values were 229, 245, and 263 mm Hg × year versus 227, 245, and 265 mm Hg × year; for cumulative PP, corresponding values were 120, 139, and 161 mm Hg × year versus 145, 170, and 199 mm Hg × year, respectively. A z-score of standardized cumulative BP was generated by subtracting the mean cumulative BP from individual cumulative BP and subsequently dividing by the standard deviation (SD) of the cumulative BP. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of cognitive impairment across quartiles of cumulative BP were estimated using multivariable Cox proportional hazard models. The proportional hazard (PH) assumption was assessed using weighted Schoenfeld residuals, with all models satisfying PH assumptions. The analysis quantified risk estimates both absolute exposure increments (per 10-mm Hg × year of cumulative BP) and relative exposure levels (z-score standardized cumulative BP values). Adjustments were made for age, gender, type of residence, years of schooling, weight, current smoking status, past smoking status, current alcohol consumption, past alcohol consumption, current exercise habits, past exercise habits, self-reported quality of life, self-reported health, self-reported quality of sleep, self-reported history of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke/CVD, and BP at baseline. We modeled dose-response relationship using restricted cubic spline, specifying four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles to ensure curve flexibility [28]. Wald chi-square test was performed to assess the non-linearity of the model effects.

A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of major findings. Firstly, inverse probability weighting was used to address potential enrollment bias. We recalculated the individual analytic weights, defining them as the inverse of the probability of each participant’s inclusion in the analysis. The differences between excluded and included participants were assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (ASMD). Secondly, subgroup analyses were performed to explore interactions between cumulative BP and various factors, including age (less than 75 years, or 75 years and older), gender, type of residence, and self-reported history of hypertension and stroke/CVD. Thirdly, participants with a self-reported history of stroke/CVD at baseline were excluded to mitigate potential confounding effects from vascular diseases. Fourthly, to address reverse causation, participants with self-reported dementia or those who were determined to have cognitive impairment during the early follow-up period (at wave 5 and wave 7 in two sub-cohorts, respectively) were excluded. Lastly, participants overlapping in both sub-cohorts were excluded.

We performed statistical analysis in Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.3.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing). A two-sided p-value lower than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Sample size and baseline characteristics

A total of 2,142 cognitively healthy older adults from the 2002 sub-cohort and 1,920 from the 2008 sub-cohort were included in the analysis. Among these, 606 participants were included in both sub-cohorts, representing approximately 28.3% of the 2002 cohort and 31.6% of the 2008 cohort. Cumulative BP and cognitive impairment

Over a median follow-up of 6.2 years (IQR 6.1–6.3) and 7.0 years (IQR 6.8–7.2), 542 and 347 older adults experienced cognitive impairment in the two sub-cohorts, yielding an incidence rate of cognitive impairment of 42.9 (39.4–46.6) per 1000 person-years and 27.0 (24.3–30.0) per 1000 person-years, respectively. The multivariable-adjusted HRs of cumulative BP for the risk of cognitive impairment in the two sub-cohorts are presented in Table 2. As anticipated, significant positive associations were observed between higher cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP with cognitive impairment risk (all Ptrend <0.01). When contrasted against the lowest quartile within both sub-cohorts, the HRs (95%CIs) for cognitive impairment risk in the highest quartile were 1.85 (1.33–2.59) and 2.64 (1.76–3.97) for cumulative SBP, 2.00 (1.45–2.75) and 2.20 (1.46–3.33) for cumulative DBP, and 1.59 (1.16–2.18) and 2.10 (1.42–3.10) for cumulative PP, respectively. Additionally, a per 10-mm Hg × year increase in cumulative SBP and DBP was associated with a 7% (95%CI: 4–10%) and 8% (95%CI: 5–11%), and 9% (95%CI: 4–14%) and 10% (95%CI: 5–16%) higher risk of cognitive impairment in the two sub-cohorts, respectively. Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated robust findings. After inverse probability weighting adjustment to minimize selection bias, ASMDs between included and excluded participants showed significant attenuation compared to unweighted estimates. These results indicated that baseline participants characteristics did not substantively influence our primary results (Additional file 1: Table S1 and Table S5). No significant interactions were noted in the majority of subgroup analyses, except for the interactions between history of hypertension and cumulative SBP in the 2008 sub-cohort, as well as between gender and cumulative DBP in the 2002 sub-cohort (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The primary associations remained stable exclusion participants with any self-reported history of stroke/CVD (Additional file 1: Table S6). Similarly, excluding participants who self-reported dementia or cognitive impairment in their respective follow-up years (2008 and 2014) across the two sub-cohorts yielded consistent results regarding cognitive impairment risk (Additional file 1: Table S7). Moreover, the results persisted after excluding overlapped participants in both sub-cohorts (Additional file 1: Table S8).

Discussion

Based on data derived from the CLHLS, participants were divided into two older adults-based consecutive sub-cohorts from 2002 to 2018, indicating that elevated 3-year cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP predicted significantly greater cognitive impairment risk during the 6-year follow-up period. Moreover, our findings indicated that the risk of subsequent cognitive impairment was significantly elevated when cumulative BP levels exceeded the median level. Cumulative SBP increased from 387.8 mm Hg × year to 416.4 mm Hg × year over 6 years, reflecting a mean SBP increase from 131.0 mm Hg to 134.8 mm Hg, which was correlated with an elevated risk of subsequent cognitive impairment. Similarly, cumulative PP increased from 138.7 mm Hg × year to 170.8 mm Hg × year, corresponding to a mean PP increase from 46.9 mm Hg to 55.3 mm Hg, which was related to cognitive impairment. In contrast, while cumulative DBP was maintained at 245 mm Hg × year, the mean DBP decreased from 82.9 mm Hg to 79.3 mm Hg, which was correlated with a greater risk of cognitive impairment.

High cumulative BP exposure is associated with poor cognitive performance, with different cumulative BP parameters posing varying risks of cognitive impairment. Our findings were consistent with those reported in prior prospective studies [16, 22]. After accounting for confounders, individuals in the highest quartile of cumulative SBP had a risk of cognitive impairment that was 1.85–2.64 times greater than that of those in lowest quartile group. In the 6-year follow-up Asymptomatic Polyvascular Abnormalities Community study (APAC), higher cumulative SBP corresponded to a greater risk of cognitive impairment. Specifically, individuals in the highest cumulative SBP group (≥ 868 mm Hg × year, corresponding to SBP ≥ 140 over 6.2 years) exhibited a 2.25-fold higher risk compared to those in the lowest cumulative SBP group (< 744 mm Hg × year, equivalent to SBP < 120 over 6.2 years) [22]. The previous study from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) found no statistically significant correlation between cumulative DBP exposure and cognitive deterioration [8]. Our findings indicated that cognitive impairment risk in the highest quartile of cumulative DBP and PP was 2.00-2.20 times and 1.59–2.10 times higher than that in the lowest quartile group, respectively. The longer follow-up duration in the 2008 sub-cohort compared to the 2002 sub-cohort may have enabled a more pronounced effect of cumulative BP exposure on cognitive impairment. Additionally, the 2008 sub-cohort excluded a higher proportion of participants due to cognitive impairment at baseline (26.7% vs. 24.9% in 2002 sub-cohort), resulting in a relatively healthier sample with better cognitive function. The 2008 sub-cohort might be more sensitive to the detrimental effects of cumulative BP. Furthermore, the 2008 sub-cohort had higher baseline SBP and PP, but lower DBP compared to the 2002 cohort. These baseline BP differences may have further contributed to the more pronounced association with cognitive impairment observed in 2008 sub-cohort. Every SD increment in cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP was associated with 33–53%, 10–27%, and 4–28% greater risk of cognitive impairment, respectively. A previous study enrolling Kailuan employees unveiled that for each SD increase in cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP, cognitive impairment risk increased by 28%, 13%, and 28%, respectively, in agreement with our results [16]. In addition, accumulating evidence indicated that prolong exposure to elevated cumulative BP correlated with accelerate rate of cognitive deterioration [8, 9], with this association across various ethnicities [18]. Extensive researches evinced that elevated cumulative SBP and DBP from childhood or young adulthood to midlife were significantly correlated with diminished cognitive performance in midlife [19–21, 23]. Overall, our findings highlight the duration of high cumulative BP exposure may have an impact on cognitive impairment in Chinese older adults.

Existing literature have confirmed the superior discriminatory accuracy of cumulative BP over a single measurement, but the level of cumulative BP that significantly increases the risk of cognitive impairment in older Chinese remains to be elucidated. Herein, the risk of cognitive impairment significantly increased with an increase in 3 -year cumulative rose from 387.8 mm Hg × year to 416.4 mm Hg × year in the two consecutive sub-cohorts, which was equivalent to a mean SBP increase from 131.0 mm Hg to 134.8 mm Hg. Similarly, in the APAC study, participants with cumulative SBP ≥ 130–140 mm Hg × 6.2 years conferred significantly higher cognitive impairment risk, whereas individuals with cumulative SBP ≥ 120–130 mm Hg × 6.2 years showed a non-significant increase in risk compared to participants with cumulative SBP < 120 mm Hg × 6.2 years [22]. Importantly, while cumulative DBP was maintained at 245 mm Hg × year, the mean DBP decreased from 82.9 mm Hg to 79.3 mm Hg, which was correlated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment. A comprehensive meta-analysis including 209 prospective studies described that high SBP and low DBP in late life were related with an increased risk of cognitive disorders and dementia [13]. Moreover, prospective data from 7,874 Chinese older adults demonstrated that lower DBP was associated with accelerated cognitive decline during the two-year follow-up [29]. The CHARLS, spanning over 7 years of follow-up, identified two risk patterns: untreated population with PP ≥ 50 mm Hg, and antihypertensive-treated patients with PP ≥ 70 mm Hg, both exhibiting accelerated cognitive deterioration [8]. This was consistent with our dose-response analysis, where cumulative PP ≥ 170.8 mm Hg × year (corresponding to mean PP ≥ 55.3 mm Hg over 3 years) indicated a significant risk of cognitive impairment. While SBP consistently rises, DBP steadily decreases, resulting in a gradual elevation in PP as time progresses. In older population, higher SBP coupled with lower DBP may serve as indicators of increased arterial stiffness and a concomitant decrease in cerebral blood flow. Elevated PP may lead to cerebral hypoperfusion and increase the risk of cerebral microvascular diseases, which are related to neurodegenerative changes [9, 30, 31].

Our study has some implications for preventing cognitive decline in Chinese older adults. Even short-term BP management could enhance cognitive function among older adults. Our results suggest that maintaining SBP and DBP levels below 135mm Hg and 80 mm Hg, respectively, are likely to preserve cognitive health in older population. Moreover, our results support the potential cognitive benefits of maintaining PP under 55 mm Hg. From the public health perspective, our study observations imply that BP level and its duration of exposure (i.e., cumulative BP) may assist in the screening of high-risk older individuals susceptible to cognitive impairment. In addition to the management of current BP levels, healthcare professionals should prioritize enhancing the awareness and control of future BP to lower the incidence of cognitive impairment and dementia in our country.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of the present study lies on its design as one of the largest nationally representative cohort of older adults, which longitudinally examined the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive impairment over 16 years. Another notable strength lies on the repeated assessments of cognitive function conducted throughout the follow-up period. This approach facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of cognitive decline and allowed for the elucidation of the causal relationship between cumulative BP and cognitive impairment in Chinese older adults. Nevertheless, some limitations of this study merit acknowledgment. Firstly, due to a lack of clinical diagnosis of dementia, the relationship between cumulative BP and incident dementia was not examined. Secondly, cognitive assessment relied on isolated tasks, some of which may have limited sensitivity for capturing nuanced cognitive decline. Consequently, a more comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation might have yielded different associations. Moreover, a high proportion of older adults were excluded from the analysis, primarily older women residing in urban and town areas with lower educational levels and poorer health, which could have contributed to selection bias. Thirdly, despite adjusting for several potential confounding factors in the multivariate analysis, the possibility of unmeasured and residual confounding influencing the outcomes remains. This limitation precludes a definitive determination of a causal relationship [32]. Finally, antihypertensive medication use among older adults with hypertension was not collected in the 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011, or 2014 survey waves. Such data were only introduced in the 2018 survey. Therefore, our study could not analyze the impact of antihypertensive drug use on the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive impairment. In future research, we will prioritize the analysis of the impact of antihypertensive drug use on this relationship.

Conclusions

The current study demonstrated that elevated cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP independently increased subsequent cognitive impairment risk in Chinese older adults. Older adults with 3-year cumulative BP levels above 387.8 mm Hg × year (cumulative SBP), 245 mm Hg × year (cumulative DBP), and 138.7 mm Hg × year (cumulative PP)—equivalent to maintaining average short-term levels above 131.0 mm Hg (SBP), 79.3 mm Hg (DBP), and 46.9 mm Hg (PP)—exhibited a greater risk of cognitive impairment, regardless of their single BP measurement values. Overall, our findings highlight the critical importance of cumulative BP as a key indicator for identifying individuals at elevated risk of cognitive impairment in Chinese older population.
