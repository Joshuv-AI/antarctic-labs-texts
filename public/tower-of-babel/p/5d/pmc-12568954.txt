A comprehensive assessment of the applicability of semianalytical models of vortex characterization for gravitational water vortex hydropower plants
Authors: Mateo Duarte Garcia, Jose Alejandro Posada Montoya, Edwin Chica, Jhon Fredy Hincapie Montoya, Laura Velásquez, Iván David Patiño Arcila
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting and analyzing vortexes’ characteristics (shape, size, circulation, pressure, velocity, etc.) is crucial for designing gravitational water vortex hydropower plants (GWVHP’s). Despite fluid dynamic simulations become valuable for this, they demand high computational cost, and semi-analytical models of vortex characterization could be useful in early design stages. In this work, a compressive assessment of the ability of these models to predict the vortex’s free-surface profile, as well as a physically consistent behavior of some field variables, under geometrical and operating conditions of GWVHP’s, is carried out using a calibration strategy proposed here, which is applicable for both strong and weak vortexes under controlled experimental conditions. The strategy focuses on minimizing the L2 relative error norm between the free-surface profiles obtained analytically and those measured from experiments undergone in GWVHP’s. The experimental setup consists of two GWVHP’s with cylindrical, where multiple flow rates were performed for the generation of strong and weak vortices, Qinl = [1.3 L/s, 2.2 L/s, 3 L/s, 3.4 L/s] and Qinl = [0.027 L/s, 0.044 L/s, 0.055 L/s]. On the other hand, the proposed strategy enforces the fulfillment of some vortex’s dimensions and of the mass conservation law between the inlet and outlet of the basin, assuming a far-field circulation equal to such of basin inlet. In general, convergent solutions of the calibration parameters were achieved, resulting in models that exactly reproduced the total vortex height, the air core radius at discharge for strong vortexes, and the submergence depth for weak vortexes under experimental conditions; however, these models usually underestimated the vortex heights for radial coordinates corresponding to the air core zone. The calibrated models were compared in terms of the velocity components (radial, azimuthal and axial), axial vorticity, and axial and radial Rossby numbers, obtaining physically coherent results for some of them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21424-5.Keywords: Gravitational water vortex hydropower plant (GWVHP), Analytical modeling, Vortex characterization, Strong vortexes, Weak vortexesSubject terms: Fluid dynamics, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The twenty-first century has been characterized by the increase in energy demand with new requirements, such as environmental care, renewable energy, and independence from fossil fuel reserves1,2. For this reason, the Gravitational Water Vortex Hydropower Plant (GWVHP) appears as an innovative and efficient green technology3. Its low hydraulic requirements to induce the turbine motion, like low water head and volume flow rate, makes the GWVHP a viable energy option, especially for non-interconnected communities3. The easiness of construction of these hydropower plants promotes their implementation in countries with high hydraulic resources, allowing great opportunities for research and technology development4.

In general, GWVHP can be identified as a micro hydropower plant that is characterized by power outputs between 5 and 100 kW4. GWVHP uses water flows from rivers or creeks, employing the centrifugal force to produce a free surface vortex. This vortex allows the transformation of kinetic rotational energy into mechanical energy using a runner, which is connected to a generator to produce electric energy5,6. This relatively straightforward energy transformation has demonstrated its viability in countries such as Austria, Australia, Germany, India, Peru, among others, raising the interest of the scientific community in the energy sector4.

One of the most important issues involved in the design of GWVHP lies on the prediction and analysis of the vortex characteristics (shape, dimensions, vortex strength or circulation, pressure, velocity components, vorticity fields, among others)7–9. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations have arisen as a valuable tool to reduce and optimize the prototyping and trial-and-error operations; however, these simulations usually demand a high computational burden that can be inefficient in the early design stages of the GWVHP where rapid comparative evaluations between different designs may be required. Considering this aspect and earlier studies on semi-analytical models for the vortex flow field characterization, the present work is devoted to carrying out a comprehensive review about these models, evaluating its suitability for the vortex characterization under the geometric and operating conditions of GWVHP. Accordingly, the main contribution of the present manuscript lies in the assessment of the accuracy and robustness of semi-analytical models to predict the vortex’s free-surface profile, as well as a physically consistent behavior of some vortex field variables, under conditions of GWVHP’s using a calibration strategy proposed here.

Firstly, the theoretical background for analysis of GWVHP is presented in Section "Theoretical background for analysis of GWVHP", focusing on the main geometrical parameters of the GWVHP, as well as on the vortex type classification according to the streamline path, Rossby numbers, free-surface stages, vortex formation and rotational behavior. The general conservation equations and conservative quantities of vortex flow fields are also presented, along with the main semi-analytical models for the characterization of free, forced and combined vortexes, distinguishing two approaches: the layered streamlines approach, and the multi-circle spiral streamline approach.

Since the semi-analytical models presented require the calibration of free parameters, experiments involving strong and weak vortexes in GWVHPs under several inlet flow rates have been conducted. These experiments aim to evaluate the models’ ability to fit the operating conditions of such hydropower plants and to identify potential sources of error, which may guide future improvements. The experimental setup used for the parameters’ calibration is described in Section "Experimental setup and image analysis" of the manuscript. In Section "Calibration of model parameters", a strategy is proposed for the calibration of the fitting parameters, where each semi-analytical model is forced to comply with specific geometrical constraints obtained from the experiments for both weak and strong vortexes. Additionally, this strategy is mass-conservative, namely, mass conservation between the basin inlet and discharge is imposed in the parameter calibration. To guarantee numerical convergence, the guess parameters are chosen to minimize the L2 relative error norm between the analytical and experimental positions of the free-surface profile.

The results of parameters’ calibration are presented in Section "Results and analysis", where the suitability of each model to reproduce the free-surface profile is evaluated with respect to the experimental results by computing the L2 relative error norm. Results of the semi-analytical models are compared each other based on key flow variables: velocity components (radial, azimuthal, and axial), axial vorticity, and axial and radial squared Rossby numbers. Additionally, the physical consistency of each model is assessed according to the vortex theory fundamentals, and previous experimental findings.

Theoretical background for analysis of GWVHP

Geometrical description of GWVHP

In general, a Gravitational Water Vortex Hydropower Plant (GWVHP) is formed by the following components: catchment, inlet channel, deflector, basin, runner, outlet orifice, outlet channel, and discharge (Fig. 1)10,11.

Main components of the GWVHP. Created with CATIA V512.

The inlet channel and deflector have the main function of guiding and accelerating the water flow from the river towards the basin. Subsequently, the water flow enters the basin, and a vortex is generated due to the height difference and the basin geometry. Moreover, the vortex causes a centrifugal force on the runner’s surface, allowing its rotation. After this process of energy transformation, the water flows through the outlet orifice to continue towards the outlet channel and return to the river by the discharge, providing in this way oxygenation to the water due to air entrainment, without major damage to fauna and flora11.

The above-mentioned components of the GWVHP are characterized by some geometrical parameters that describe their dimensions and configuration, which are represented in Fig. 2. As can be observed, the differences between the notch width, channel width and notch length determine the notch angle, which in turn is representative for the water flow behavior since it is related to the inlet velocity magnitude and direction, water losses, and disruptive turbulence at the basin inlet2,13. Additionally, the basin geometry can be fully cylindrical, fully conical, concave, convex, or a combination of them. Several works are devoted to the comparison between the performance of GWVHP with different basin geometries. However, a common consensus has not been reached yet about the influence of the basin’s characteristics on the GWVHP performance, since this highly depends on the operating conditions, and other geometrical parameters14–19. In general, conical basins lead to higher tangential velocities, vortex symmetry, power outputs, and efficiencies for high water flow rates, whereas cylindrical basins could be preferable for small discharge rates2,20–23.

Main geometrical parameters of the GWVHP.

Figure 3 represents the main geometrical parameters of the runner, which determine the kinetic energy transformation, and vortex configuration. Several researchers have studied the influence of these parameters, as well as of the blade shape and runner position, in the GWVHP performance2,24–26. The blade height and chord line are important because the blade surface area rapidly changes with the modification of these parameters, leading to a change in the power coefficient depending on the drag and inertial effects. On the other hand, a suitable combination between the blade angles in horizontal and vertical planes (HP and VP, respectively), and the curvature radius of the blade profile are very important to guarantee an adequate energy transfer from the fluid to the turbine. The blade angle in VP is a key parameter for the extraction of the axial momentum from water, but its increment could disturb the vortex formation, thereby reducing the power coefficient. The blade angle in HP is relevant for extracting the tangential and radial components of momentum, and its increase does not usually have adverse effects on the vortex11. The introduction of the cut in the top of the blade is done with the main purpose of reducing the turbine weight, and therefore, the rotational inertia, but the removed section can generate a non-desired reduction of the effective surface area of the blades (the area in contact with the water). In ideal conditions, water should not be present in the cut zones because it can cause abnormal flow patterns that can affect energy transformation, and only the air core should exist there11. The taper angle can also be observed in Fig. 3. This angle allows the generation of a conicity at the outer edge of the blade to control the seepage of water between the turbine and the basin wall, influencing energy output and efficiency11.

Basic concepts of vortex applied to GWVHP

To understand the GWVHP operation, it is fundamental to consider some basic concepts about vortexes. The vortex definition still lacks universal acceptance and has been discussed for centuries27. However, one of the most common definitions is done by Lugts28, who describes a vortex as the rotational motion of a multitude of fluid particles around a common axis. Figure 4 shows the vortex classification considering five parameters. The first one considers the dimensionality of the vortex streamlines, according to which vortexes are classified as two-dimensional planar (concentric circular or axisymmetric) or three-dimensional planar (cylindrical or planar). The second classification depends on the stability of the air core and its influence on the flow behavior, leading to two kinds of vortexes, namely, weak and strong. In the weak vortex, the air core is not stable due to the predominance of axial forces and the possible presence of unsteady characteristics. When the height of weak vortex is close to the critical submergence (distance between the top of free surface and basin outlet), air starts to pass into the discharge. On the contrary, the strong vortex is characterized by a stable full-air core that is preserved until the outlet orifice, where both air and water are present and well-distinguished. Both kinds of vortexes can be understood in Fig. 5.

Vortex classification.

In general, the balance between in-plane forces and circulation is decisive for the behavior of weak and strong vortexes. For this reason, Klimenko29 considered the Rossby number to characterize the vorticity domain. According to Klimenko29, the interaction between and defines whether a vortex region is stable or not. In this way, when circulation and vorticity have the same sign , the local rotation of fluid particles favors the particle displacements along the streamlines and vortex is stable, leading to positive values of and . On the other hand, a negative value of the product means destabilizing effects on the flow, namely, local rotation of fluid particles opposes to vortex circulation. When circulation and vorticity have the same sign and they are large enough to lead to and , the vortex tends to be strong and stable. In such cases, the flow in the r-z plane is considerably altered by rotational forces. On the contrary, vortex is weaker as or are larger, since the rotational forces do not significantly alter the fluid flow pattern in the r-z plane in which the flow image is almost potential.

Furthermore, the vortex classification based on the free-surface state, originally proposed by Knauss30, is also reviewed here. According to this classification, there are seven types of vortexes, with the first six deemed as weak or unstable vortexes, and the last one as strong or stable vortex (Fig. 6). When the local rotation is very small, a stable surface swirl (VT1) or dimple (VT2) could be generated, but the height of the air zone is almost negligible when compared to the distance between the tip of air core and the outlet discharge. With the increase of the local rotation, an air core can be formed, obtaining the vortex types VT3 to VT5, where the air core is not yet able to reach the discharge. In the VT6 vortex type, the vortex height just reaches the critical submergence, whereas in the VT7 vortex, which was thoroughly studied by Mulligan et al.9,31,32, a strong and stable air core can pass through the intake.

Vortex type classification based on the free-surface state.

The fourth vortex classification depends on the source of vortex formation. This includes Non-Purely Stretching Sustained (NPSS) and the Purely Stretching Sustained (PSS) vortex. The NPSS vortex depends on an intake rate and a constant supplied vorticity. On the other hand, the PSS vortex can be formed by natural causes and without constant influx of vorticity. The fifth vortex classification, which is discussed in the following section, depends on whether the fluid flow field is free, forced or combined.

Modeling of free and forced vortexes

The original approach for analytical modeling of vortexes considers the motion of the fluid particles along a line vortex positioned in the r-θ plane, namely, fluid particles motion takes place in streamlines around the z-axis (Fig. 7a). The analytical models following this approach35–39 consider that tangential and radial velocities are non-dependent on the axial coordinate (z), which implies that for a given radial coordinate these velocity components remain constant from the water surface to the outlet discharge of the basin. Some of these models incorporate the axial velocity, 35,37,38, but streamlines still remain positioned in the r-θ plane, so the vortex can be conceived as a set of rotating fluid layers moving in the axial direction. Recently, the multi-circle spiral shape of the streamlines (Fig. 7b), which resembles better the experimental observations, has been considered by some analytical models by incorporating the axial-coordinate dependency of the tangential and radial velocities of the vortex flow field40. In both the layered streamlines and multi-circle spiral streamlines approach, the continuity Eq. (5) shall be complied in the water domain, as well as the momentum equation (Navier–Stokes) in the radial, tangential and axial direction, Eqs. (16) to (18)38.

Modeling of combined vortices

The Rankine model presented above has been modified by several authors35–40,47,48, because the solution for the tangential velocity, , does not guarantee the slope continuity between the free and forced vortex subdomains. The modified models can be classified according to:

Time dependency of the tangential velocity: Some models are characterized by the time independency of , where kinetic energy is uniformly provided to the fluid flow system35–40,48, whereas other models are time-dependent since the vortex has no energy supplied, causing a time-decaying circulation47. For the case of GWVHP, the first kind of models are considered, since the power is continuously supplied by controlled pumps.

Axial dependency of the tangential velocity: In some models, is independent of the axial coordinate35–39, leading to layered vortex streamlines (Fig. 7a), whereas in other ones, axial-dependency of is included by means of a free parameter, generating multi-circle spiral vortex streamlines40,48 (Fig. 7b).

Axial dependency of the tangential velocity: In some models, is independent of the axial coordinate35–39, leading to layered vortex streamlines (Fig. 7a), whereas in other ones, axial-dependency of is included by means of a free parameter, generating multi-circle spiral vortex streamlines40,48 (Fig. 7b).

Incorporation of turbulent effects: The semi-analytical models used in the estimation of the vortex behavior are based on the laminar flow equations, but some of them incorporate the turbulent effects in lumped fashion. To the best of our knowledge, a closed, semi-analytical turbulence model to describe the vortex behavior based on the fluctuation theory has not been disclosed yet. One approach to account for the turbulent effects is to increase the kinematic viscosity of the fluid with the inclusion of the eddy viscosity37,39. Other models incorporate fitting parameters in the velocity functions36,39.

From a physical point of view, the vortex field predicted by the semi-analytical models, except Rankine, can be considered as a combined vortex, where the effects of a forced and free vortex are superimposed, and their relative importance highly depends on the radial distance from the rotational axis. In these models, there is not a strictly forced-vortex subdomain with constant axial vorticity and a surrounding strictly free-vortex subdomain with constant circulation, as in the original Rankine model; but instead, there is a continuous vortex flow field whose behavior resembles such of a fully forced vortex when , and a fully free vortex when . Moreover, Rosenhead, Hite and Mih37 proposed the use of empirical approximations to improve the transition between the irrotational and rotational vortexes; also, the influence of eddy viscosity on the radial and axial velocity was considered. Experimental setup and image analysis

For the calibration of the semi-analytical models for strong vortexes, a test bench with GWVHP was built at University of Antioquia49. The bench is composed of two water tanks, a centrifugal pump, basin and inlet channel. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 8a. The pump was used to move water from the lower tank to the upper tank at a controlled volume flow rate (Qinl). To observe the vortex pattern, the basin and inlet channel were made from transparent acrylic. The flow rate Qinl was measured with a flow meter Siemens SITRANS FM MAG 5100W. The experiments were performed under various flow rates, Qinl = [1.3 L/s, 2.2 L/s, 3 L/s, 3.4 L/s]. The geometry and dimensions of the GWVHP are shown in Fig. 8b. An example of the strong vortex generation is shown in Fig. 8c.

For each experimental case, a free image processing software (WebPlotDigitalizer) was used to obtain the r-z coordinates of several points of the vortex free surface. Figure 10 shows the step-by-step extraction process. Firstly, a selection of the appropriate picture is made, which allows the extraction of the vortex pattern. Secondly, the picture is imported into WebPlotDigitalizer and calibrated following software standards. In this case, a calibration of the X-axis and Y-axis is obtained from the basin diameter (X1 and X2) and vortex height (Y1 and Y2), respectively. A correct calibration allows the software to proceed to the next step, where a selection of multiple points on the vortex free surface is made by thresholding. Finally, the points are exported and analysed for tuning, where a repositioning of the points according to the discharge radius, basin height and width allows to obtain the final coordinates of the points.

Results and analysis

Physical behavior of vortexes

As can be observed in Fig. 12, three components of velocity can be distinguished in a fluid particle (P) in the vortex fluid domain, namely, the tangential component (), radial component (), and axial component (). The expected change in the radial and axial direction of these velocity components is also shown in that figure, but not all models fit well to this behavior, as shown later. These velocities behave according to the vortex regime. As previously mentioned, most of the analytical models only consider explicitly the forced vortex (zone 1) and free vortex (zone 3) regimes, but there is also a transition regime between them (zone 2), and a near-wall regime (zone 4)51,52. As formerly mentioned, the forced vortex regime (zone 1) corresponds to a viscous core where the angular velocity (ω) is practically constant, and the angular momentum increases with the radius. This means that the tangential velocity () increases with r, and the magnitude of the inward radial velocity () must also increase with r to sustain the vortex motion by mass conservation. Moreover, the axial velocity () should decrease radially to be consistent with the spiral patterns of the streamlines converging towards the air core. This has been demonstrated in several experimental and numerical works40,53–55. In other words, as the fluid elements get closer to the vortex surface, the radial velocity () turns into an axial velocity (). In the transition regime (zone 2), there is a smooth variation of both and , and the maximum values of these velocities are reached at this zone; some semi-analytical models predict that maximum tangential and radial velocities ( and , respectively) occurs exactly at , others models, do not. Tangential shear stresses are relatively high in this zone due to the transition between forced and free vortex regimes. The axial velocity continues decreasing with r in this transition zone. In the free-vortex regime (zone 3), the angular momentum turns into constant as r increases, i.e., the variation of tangential velocity () is almost linear decreasing with r as points get far from to keep the angular momentum, implying also a decrease of the magnitude of with r. A relevant decrease of the axial velocity () is expected in this zone, where particles tend to move in the r-θ plane. As the radial coordinate (r) increases, circulation tends to the far field circulation (. The near-wall regime (zone 4) is usually small and entail the formation of a wall boundary layer in such a way that all velocity components set up to zero at the wall (no penetration, no-slip condition). This zone is not taken into account in any of semi-analytical models and can introduce discrepancies with the experimental results depending on the distance between the basin walls and the free surface.

Scheme of expected physical behavior of vortexes for GWVHP.

According to several experimental and numerical works40,53–55, vortex streamlines exhibit a spiral pattern, where and are dependent on the axial coordinate (z). This supposes some deviations of the ideal behavior described above for the vortex regimes (zones 1, 2 and 3) since can change for a fixed radial coordinate (r), violating the conservation of the angular velocity in the forced-vortex zone, and of angular momentum on the free-vortex one. These differences become more evident as fluid elements get closer to the transition zone. According to several works40,53,56,57, for a fixed value of r, can increase or decrease with z, depending on the radial position and vortex-type classification (see Fig. 6), and this change vanishes as r is larger, in other words, varies slightly with z for larger values of r. The inward radial velocity, , should follows a consistent trend, namely, if increases, the magnitude of should increase too, and vice versa. As for , variations of in the axial direction should vanish as r increases. On the other hand, for a fixed radial coordinate (r), the axial velocity should increase from the free surface towards the bottom of the basin or discharge, but this is also less noticeable as r increases40,53,56,57. The near-wall effects of the bottom basin are not considered by the semi-analytical models.

The spiral nature of the vortex streamlines has been experimentally observed using color particle tracking on the free surface40,56,58. The applied forces in a fluid element of the free surface are also represented in Fig. 1252,59,60. Two radii of curvature are identified, i.e., the vortex radius in the r-θ and r-z planes, “r” and “ξ”, respectively, which have associated differentials of centrifugal forces (dFcr and dFcξ), and of surface tension forces (dFsr and dFsξ). Surface tension effects acquire importance on the free surface when the Weber number at the discharge, as defined by is below a given threshold that has not been unified yet, with values ranging in between 120 and 720 approximately37. The centrifugal force differentials, dFcr and dFcξ, tend to generate a concave shape on the free surface of the vortex, while the surface tension differentials, dFsr and dFsξ, oriented as shown in Fig. 12, along with the pressure gradient force (dFP), a convex shape.

The change of the vortex shape in the basin relative to the inlet flow rate (Qinl), once stable condition has been achieved, can be explained from the balance between the abovementioned forces. In general, increasing Qinl leads to the increase of the tangential velocity (), which in turns could increase the centrifugal force in the radial direction, dFcr, pushing the water towards the basin walls. However, by entering more flow, it can also decrease the vortex radius in the r-θ plane (r), which would bring about a decreased dFcr. Moreover, increasing Qinl could rise the axial velocity () and the angle formed between the pressure gradient force and the horizontal axis (φ), i.e., the surface differential becomes more horizontal, which would increase dFcξ and consequently the tendency of the water to move towards the interior of the basin rather than towards the walls in the radial direction. As the fluid element gets far from the air core, particle motion tends to occur in the r-θ plane (φ and decrease), and balance is reduced to the pressure gradient force and centrifugal force.

The strong vortexes are generated with inlet flow rates of Qinl = [1.3, 2.2, 3, 3.4] L/s. Figure 13 shows the experimental results of strong vortexes.

In this case, the increase in the flow rate leads to an increase in hydrostatic pressure near the outlet, increasing the pressure gradient force, allowing the free surface motion towards the basin center and closing the air core. Calibration of weak vortexes

The weak vortexes consider inlet flow rates of 0.027 L/s, 0.044 L/s and 0.055 L/s. Figure 16 shows the pictures of the experimental results, whereas Figs. 17 and 18 show the fitting curves. As can be observed, the angle in the convex discharge zone is very pronounced, causing the formation of a weak vortex, which can be explained by the presence of low centrifugal forces, allowing the surface tension forces to close the vortex and generate a weak behavior. As can be observed in Figs. 17 and 18, all the semi-analytical models predict the presence of a forced-vortex zone close to the axisymmetric axis (value of calibrated rc is very small).

Contrarily to the strong vortexes (Figs. 14, 15), the low Reynold numbers at the discharge shown in Figs. 17 and 18 (between 637 and 1297), confirm the laminar nature of these vortexes. The Weber numbers at the discharge of these weak vortexes are in the range of 0.103 to 0.427 (Figs. 17, 18), which allows to assert the high relevance of the surface tension effects in their formation.

Following the analysis, semi-analytical models seem to fit better to the weak vortexes than to the strong ones, possibly by the significant reduction of the turbulent effects. The accuracy of free surface prediction has been demonstrated by several authors, for example, Rabe, Najafabadi, and Sarkardeh61, showed that Vatistas, Hite and Mih, and Chen models achieved good prediction with experimental data in hydropower intake analysis, with a mean deviation ranging from 4.9% to 10.1%. However, for the lower inlet flow rate, Qinl = 0.027 L/s, all semi-analytical models present some difficulties in the prediction of the shape of the convex discharge. For the greater flow rate, Qinl = 0.055 L/s, where the vortex reaches the critical submergence depth, the pronounced slope is hard to be replicated by all models. For the intermediate flow rate, Qinl = 0.044 L/s, the curve fitting looks better.

For GWVHP applications, larger values of the tangential velocity are beneficial since they permit to have a higher torque in the turbine, thereby increasing the efficiency of conversion from hydraulic to mechanical energy. Also, knowing where the highest tangential velocities are located can allow making a better turbine design to optimize the energy capture by modifying the blade parameters, such as: radius of curvature, blade angle in horizontal plane, and blade angle in vertical plane. For example, a bigger radius of curvature can be used in the zones of maximum tangential velocities, . As previously shown, in the case of strong vortexes, the location of maximum tangential velocity depends on the analytical model used and the inlet flow rate. Accordingly, for those models predicting a full free-vortex domain independently of the inlet flow rate (Burgers, Rosenhead, Hite and Mih, Vatistas), the turbine should have a large curvature radius just in the vortex surface to enhance the energy capture. However, for those models comprising a forced vortex zone no matter the flow rate (Chen in this case), this curvature radius should be large at . In general, Wang and Hite and Mih models predict the largest values of where the energy capture could be better, whereas a lower capture is expected according to the results of Chen model.

Additionally, the design of the turbine must be such that in the air core or the zones of low tangential velocities, where the induced rotational inertia can be neglected, the presence of runner material should be minimized, for instance, with the cut and taper angles. The intentional remotion of runner material in contact with air is more feasible in those cases where a forced vortex zone is present (as predicted by Chen), where the maximum tangential velocities are inside the liquid domain ; for full-free vortexes, as the ones predicted by Burgers, Rosenhead, Hite and Mih and Vatistas, this remotion could entail a decrease of the turbine performance since the maximum tangential velocities lies on the vortex free surface.

In the case of weak vortexes, it is important to calculate the submergence depth (ho) from the analytical models as an initial estimate of the runner position, as well as the vortex free surface to design the blade cut angle. Since the maximum tangential velocity lies inside the fluid flow domain for all analytical models, the percentage of runner volume submerged inside the domain is a crucial factor to capture the maximum rotational energy.

It is worth-mentioning that these observations correspond to initial design guidelines, but not to exact design criteria since the shape and fluid flow properties of the vortex are modified when the turbine is present.

Conclusions

In the present work, a comprehensive evaluation of the semi-analytical models of vortex characterization under the operating conditions of Gravitational Water Vortex Hydropower Plants (GWVHP) was performed, implementing a calibration strategy of the models’ parameters based on the minimization of the L2 relative error norm between the experimental and numerical results of the free surface profiles, and the fulfillment of some geometrical constraints and of the mass conservation law between the inlet and the outlet of the basin. For the calibration of the semi-analytical models, two experimental setups were used to reproduce strong and weak vortexes’ conditions at different inlet flow rates, and the coordinates of the free-surface profiles were obtained using image analysis. In general, convergent solutions of the non-linear systems that define the parameters’ calibration were obtained for all semi-analytical models, and the resulting free-surface profiles exactly reproduced the total vortex height, the air core radius at the discharge for strong vortexes and the submergence depth for weak vortexes under controlled experimental conditions, although for the radial coordinates corresponding to the air core zone, the vortex heights were underpredicted in most of the cases considered here. This was attributed to some inaccuracies in the calculation of the far-field circulation, the disregard of the viscous effects in the tangential velocity component and of the radial increase of the effective viscosity inward, and the non-explicit consideration of the turbulent effects. According to the L2 relative error norms, semi-analytical models fit better to the weak vortexes than to the strong ones.

For the strong vortexes, some models calibrated with the proposed strategy predicted the existence of free and forced zones in the vortex domain, whereas others only the existence of a free vortex zone. Additionally, the Reynold numbers at the discharge, , suggested that viscous and turbulent effects could have similar relative importance in the vortex domain for all flow rates entering the basin, whereas Weber numbers at the discharge, , pointed out that surface tension forces could have had some importance only for the lower flow rate (Qinl = 1.3 L/s). For the weak vortexes, the low values of indicated the laminar nature of these vortexes, while the low values of exhibited the significant influence of the surface tension in their formation.

Regarding the tangential velocity, all models showed that this variable increased from the free surface to the transition zone when a forced-vortex zone is present, and then decreased continuously towards the basin wall. The full-free vortexes had a decreasing tangential velocity from the free surface towards the basin wall. For the axial and radial velocity, inconsistencies in the Odgaard and Burgers models were demonstrated, showing behaviors that contradict the physics of the vortex flow fields. On the other hand, the z-axis dependency of the field variables predicted by the Chen model was physically coherent. For some models, and described well the conditions of weak and strong vortexes. However, Odgaard and Burgers models always led to inconsistent results of these numbers for strong and weak vortexes, Vatistas and Wang predicted incoherent values for some flow rates in strong vortexes, whereas Vatistas and Chen did it for weak vortexes.

In future works, considering the high differences between the models for the prediction of some field variables, the accuracy of these models, calibrated with the strategy proposed here, should be evaluated by measuring these variables in the vortex flow field to carry out a more reliable comparison. The proposed strategy and the implemented models have certain limitations due to physical assumptions, the exclusion of the turbine in the experimental setup, the constant flow rate, among others. These limitations modify the experimental conditions and limit direct comparisons with real-world GWVHP systems. Therefore, addressing these limitations will be considered in a future study, where more realistic conditions will be replicated.
