Chitosan and chitosan–silver nanoparticles as adjuncts to platelet-rich plasma in canine full-thickness cutaneous wound healing
Authors: Mohab Ragab, Awad Rizk, Zeinab Shouman, Alaa A. Omar, Adel Zaghloul, Marwa Abass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing full-thickness cutaneous wound repair in dogs remains challenging, particularly when infection risk and delayed regeneration are expected. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) delivers a concentrated pool of growth factors, while chitosan-based nanoparticles—especially silver-functionalized systems—may provide antimicrobial protection and support tissue remodeling. This study evaluated PRP alone or combined with chitosan nanoparticles (PRP/CS NPs) or chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles (PRP/CS/Ag NPs) in experimentally induced full-thickness skin wounds in dogs. Twenty-four healthy male mongrel dogs were randomized into four groups (n = 6): saline control, PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs. Standardized 2 × 2 cm full-thickness wounds were created surgically and followed for 21 days using clinical wound assessment, wound area and contraction measurements, histopathology, collagen deposition analysis, and immunohistochemical staining for EGF and α-SMA. All treatments significantly reduced wound size and increased contraction versus controls over time (p < 0.001). PRP/CS/Ag NPs achieved the greatest wound size reduction, particularly on days 7, 14, and 21, with a significant difference from controls at day 14 (p < 0.001). PRP promoted higher early collagen deposition (day 7), whereas PRP/CS/Ag NPs induced sustained and significantly greater collagen deposition at days 14 and 21. Immunohistochemistry showed consistently higher EGF expression in the PRP/CS/Ag NPs group across time points, while PRP/CS NPs increased α-SMA expression, consistent with enhanced myofibroblast activity. PRP accelerates early wound repair, while chitosan-based nanoparticles modulate later healing events.PRP combined with PRP/CS/Ag NPs produced the most consistent improvement in wound closure, collagen remodeling, and epithelial regeneration, supporting silver-functionalized chitosan nanoparticles as a promising adjunct to PRP for managing full-thickness cutaneous wounds in dogs.Graphical abstract Schematic overview of the experimental design and main outcomes of canine full-thickness wound healing following treatment with saline, PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs.

Introduction

Wound healing is a natural restorative response to tissue injury. It is a complex biological process that involves four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and maturation (remodeling). Successful progression through these phases is essential for proper restoration of skin integrity and function [1].

In resource-limited settings, wound management remains a significant clinical challenge due to wound-related complications (e.g., infection and delayed healing) and limited access to specialized therapeutic options. This has increased interest in cost-effective and biologically derived therapies that may support wound healing [2].

In veterinary practice, deep and extensive cutaneous wounds are common and may require urgent intervention. To allow controlled comparisons of therapeutic efficacy, standardized experimentally induced full-thickness cutaneous wounds are frequently used in canine research models. Chronic wounds often require regular debridement of damaged and necrotic tissues, local and/or systemic infection control, protection of underlying tissues, and induction of cutaneous tissue regeneration [3].

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a blood-derived product in which a high concentration of platelets is suspended in a small volume of plasma [4, 5]. In the present study, PRP was prepared as an autologous product. Platelets contribute to wound healing through hemostasis and by releasing growth factors and cytokines upon activation [6]. These mediators may enhance epithelialization, stimulate angiogenesis, promote collagen deposition, and support tissue remodeling [7]. PRP is therefore a rich source of wound-healing factors, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), among other platelet-derived angiogenic factors [8]. Increasing platelet concentration at the wound site has been shown to promote tissue repair [9].

Chitosan is a biocompatible and bio-adhesive polysaccharide derived primarily from chitin in crustacean shells and is typically soluble in dilute acidic solutions [10, 11]. Due to its hemostatic, antimicrobial, and wound-healing properties, chitosan has attracted interest for biomedical applications, particularly wound dressings and drug delivery systems [11, 12].

Because infection is a major barrier to wound healing, nanotechnology-based approaches have gained attention for managing skin wounds and infections in animals [13]. Nanoparticles (NPs) possess physicochemical properties that make them promising adjuncts to conventional therapies [14]. In open wounds, nanoparticles may improve local drug retention, enable sustained release, and modulate the wound microenvironment, potentially enhancing healing while reducing systemic exposure [15].

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been investigated due to their stability and strong antimicrobial activity, with reported lower toxicity toward eukaryotic cells at controlled doses [16]. AgNP-based products have potential applications in dermatology and wound care, including infection control, wound healing support, drug delivery, and disinfection [17, 18].

Therefore, combining PRP with chitosan nanoparticles (PRP/CS NPs) or chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles (CS/Ag NPs) may provide complementary regenerative and antimicrobial effects at the wound site. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of PRP alone or in combination with PRP/CS NPs or PRP/CS/Ag NPs in the healing of experimentally induced full-thickness cutaneous wounds in dogs. Outcomes were evaluated by macroscopic assessment, including body weight as a general health indicator, wound size, and wound contraction, in addition to histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Material and methods

Ethical approval

Animals were managed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the study protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Mansoura University (MU-ACUC approval No. VM.MS.25.12.242).

Animals

Twenty-four apparently healthy male mongrel dogs aged 10–20 months with an average body weight of 20–25 kg were used in this study. Dogs were clinically examined before inclusion and were free from visible scars, skin lesions, or systemic illness.

The experiment was conducted at the experimental animal facility of the Surgery Department, Mansoura Veterinary Teaching Hospital, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Mansoura University. All dogs were housed individually in cages under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 55% relative humidity, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle). Dogs were acclimatized for two weeks before the experiment under hygienic conditions, fed a balanced ration, and provided with clean drinking water ad libitum.

Preparation of platelet-rich plasma (PRP)

Whole blood was aseptically collected from the cephalic vein of each dog and transferred into vacuum blood collection tubes containing sodium citrate as an anticoagulant. The samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. After the first centrifugation, three distinct layers were formed. The upper plasma layer was carefully aspirated and transferred into a plain tube (without anticoagulant) and centrifuged again at 3500 rpm for 10 min. Following the second centrifugation, two fractions were obtained: the upper fraction represented platelet-poor plasma (PPP), while the lower fraction contained platelet-rich plasma (PRP) [19–21]. Platelet concentration was determined using an automated hematology analyzer to confirm platelet enrichment in the prepared PRP compared with whole blood. The PRP was used as a non-activated liquid and applied immediately after preparation to the experimental wounds. However, platelet activation is expected to occur in vivo upon contact with exposed collagen and tissue factors at the wound site, leading to platelet degranulation and subsequent release of growth factors, as previously reported [4, 8, 22, 23]. For each dog assigned to a PRP-treated group, 20 mL of autologous whole blood was collected individually to prepare PRP. The final PRP yield was sufficient to provide 1 mL for local injection around the single experimental wound created in that animal. Production of chitosan nanoparticles (PRP/CS NPs)

Chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized using the ionotropic gelation method [24] with minor modifications. Chitosan was dissolved in 1% (v/v) acetic acid to obtain a final concentration of 0.2% (w/v) at room temperature. The pH was adjusted to 5.2 using 0.1–1.0 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). An aqueous solution of tripolyphosphate (TPP) (0.2% w/v) was added dropwise to the chitosan solution (one drop per minute) using a syringe pump under vigorous magnetic stirring at room temperature, at a TPP: chitosan volume ratio of 1:4 (v/v). Stirring was continued for 1 h to allow nanoparticle formation. The resulting nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C and used for subsequent experiments.

Production of chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles (CS/Ag NPs)

Chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles were synthesized as previously described [25, 26] by chemical reduction of AgNO₃ using NaBH₄ in the presence of chitosan. Briefly, 50 mg chitosan was dissolved in 50 mL of 2% (v/v) acetic acid and sonicated for 5 min using an ultrasonic probe. The chitosan solution was then added to 50 mL of aqueous AgNO₃ (6.0 mM) and stirred vigorously at 1000 rpm at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of NaBH₄ (58 mM) was added slowly dropwise, resulting in a color change from yellow to brown and finally to brownish-yellow, indicating nanoparticle formation. Stirring was continued for 2 h to ensure completion of the reaction. The resulting stable dispersion (without additional stabilizers) was kept at room temperature until use.

Characterization of CS NPs and CS/Ag NPs

Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of chitosan nanoparticles (CS NPs) and chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles (CS/Ag NPs) were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Accu Lab UVS 280) over a wavelength range of 200–600 nm.

The morphology and size of the prepared nanoparticles were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Study design and experimental protocol

This study was designed as a controlled, randomized, prospective experimental trial. A single standardized full-thickness cutaneous wound (2 × 2 cm) was surgically induced on the dorsal trunk of each dog (n = 24), ensuring one wound per animal. Dogs were fasted for 6 h before surgery. Premedication consisted of intramuscular (IM) administration of atropine sulfate (0.04 mg/kg body weight) followed by xylazine HCl (1.1 mg/kg body weight, IM). General anesthesia was induced using intravenous ketamine HCl (10 mg/kg body weight).

On the day of surgery, the dorsal region between the scapulae extending caudally toward the pelvic region was shaved and aseptically prepared. A 2 × 2 cm full-thickness skin wound was created on the trunk of each dog by surgical excision of the skin. Following surgery, animals were returned to their individual cages and closely monitored until complete recovery from anesthesia.

Postoperative analgesia consisted of a single subcutaneous injection of meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg body weight). In the control group, wounds were flushed with 5 mL sterile normal saline (0.9% NaCl). In the treated groups, wounds were similarly flushed with saline before application of PRP alone, PRP combined with chitosan nanoparticles, or PRP combined with chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles, according to group allocation.

Clinical observations were recorded throughout the study period. Macroscopic evaluation, histopathological examination, and immunohistochemical analysis were performed to assess wound healing on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21. Wounds were photographed, and wound area measurements were recorded on days 0, 3, 7, 11, 15, and 19.

Evaluation of wound healing

Macroscopic examination

Wounds were evaluated macroscopically by a single experienced surgeon blinded to group allocation on postoperative days 0, 3, 7, 11, 15, and 19.

Body weight

Dogs were monitored for behavioral changes, and body weight (kg) was recorded on postoperative days 0, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21 as a general indicator of systemic health.

Wound size

Wound area (mm²) was measured using a ruler and documented by digital photography on postoperative days 0, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21.

Bacterial count analysis

Wound swab samples were aseptically collected from each experimental wound on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21 using sterile cotton swabs. Each swab was immediately transferred into sterile tubes containing normal saline and vortexed to release the bacteria. Serial tenfold dilutions were prepared, and aliquots were cultured on nutrient agar plates. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, bacterial colonies were counted, and the results were expressed as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL). The data were subsequently log-transformed (log CFU/mL) for statistical analysis [28].

Microscopic evaluation

On postoperative days 7, 14, and 21, skin samples were collected under general anesthesia. Samples were divided for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Histopathological evaluation

Full-thickness wound tissue samples, including adjacent normal skin, were collected from each group on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21. Specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, routinely processed, embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned at 4 µm, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and Masson’s trichome stain then examined under a light microscope [29]. The degree of inflammation and vascularization was assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring system, as shown in Tables 1 and 2. Immunohistochemical evaluation

Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm and mounted on saline-coated glass slides. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by autoclaving at 120 °C for 10 min in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 10 min at room temperature.

Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against epidermal growth factor (EGF) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (ready-to-use; BioGenex, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were then washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with the appropriate anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin [30].

Statistical analysis

Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Longitudinal outcomes (wound size and wound contraction percentage) were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model to evaluate the effects of treatment group, time, and their interaction, with individual dog included as a random effect to account for repeated measurements. The model was fitted using the lme4 package in R software [31]. Post hoc comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means (EMMs) calculated with the emmeans package [32]. Model-based results are presented as EMMs ± standard error (SE) derived from the fitted model. Semi-quantitative histological and immunohistochemical scores at each time point were compared among groups using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software [33], and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Body weight

It showed a gradual decrease over time in all experimental groups. Statistical analysis revealed a significant effect of time on body weight (p < 0.05), while no significant differences were detected among treatment groups (p > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant interaction between groups and time was observed. These findings indicate that treatment with PRP alone or combined with PRP/CS NPs and CS/Ag NPs groups did not induce adverse systemic effects, as reflected by the absence of treatment-related changes in body weight.

Macro-examination

On gross examination, the wounds in all groups showed a progressive improvement over time, with a clear visible reduction in wound area at each subsequent observation point compared to the previous one. This reduction was more pronounced in the treated groups than in the control group. Wound size

Analysis using a linear mixed-effects model demonstrated a highly significant effect of time on wound size across all experimental groups, with a progressive and continuous reduction from Day 3 to Day 21 (p < 0.001), indicating ongoing wound healing throughout the study period.

At Day 0 (baseline), no significant differences in wound size were detected among the Control, PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs groups (all p > 0.05), confirming comparable initial wound dimensions before treatment allocation.

Significant group-by-day interactions were observed at later time points. The PRP/CS/Ag NPs group exhibited the greatest reduction in wound size compared with the Control group, particularly on Days 7, 14, and 21 (e.g., Day 14: Estimate = −50.33, 95% CI: −62.66 to −38.01, p < 0.001).

Histopathological findings

Histopathological evaluation (H&E staining)

Representative hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained sections of full-thickness cutaneous wounds were examined on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21 to evaluate the progression of wound repair among the Control, PRP, Chitosan, and Silver-treated groups.

At postoperative day 7, control wounds showed an ulcerated wound gap (W) filled with granulation tissue, numerous capillaries, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration with predominance of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs), together with abundant immature fibroblasts. In contrast, PRP-treated wounds demonstrated early re-epithelialization with a multilayered, thick neo-epidermis (E) covering the wound gap and more advanced granulation-tissue maturation, with fewer capillaries and inflammatory cells. Chitosan-treated wounds still exhibited an ulcerated wound gap filled with granulation tissue and prominent inflammatory infiltrates with many immature fibroblasts. Silver-treated wounds showed a covered wound gap with hyperplastic neo-epidermis and partially mature granulation tissue, with many mature fibroblasts and relatively few inflammatory cells.

At postoperative day 14, control wounds remained largely ulcerated and were characterized by dense granulation tissue with increased inflammatory cell infiltration. PRP treatment resulted in a fully covered wound gap with thick neo-epidermis and mature connective tissue, with evident collagen deposition and mature fibroblasts; reconstruction of hair follicles (H) was observed. Chitosan-treated wounds exhibited clear re-epithelialization and more mature granulation tissue compared with the control, with reduced inflammatory cells. Silver-treated wounds showed a covered wound gap with a relatively thinner neo-epidermis and fully mature connective tissue with collagen deposition; hair follicle reconstruction was also evident.

At postoperative day 21, control wounds exhibited granulation tissue maturation with collagen deposition and fewer capillaries; however, complete epidermal regeneration remained limited. PRP-treated wounds showed well-organized, fully mature connective tissue with dense collagen deposition and complete epidermal coverage, with reconstructed hair follicles and sebaceous glands (S). Chitosan-treated wounds demonstrated organized mature connective tissue with collagen deposition and reconstructed hair follicles, indicating progression into the remodeling phase. Discussion

Wound healing is a highly coordinated biological process involving inflammation, cell proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling. In the present study, the combined macroscopic, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings provide integrated evidence that PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs exert distinct yet complementary effects on different phases of cutaneous wound repair in dogs [34, 35].

The physicochemical characterization of the prepared nanoparticles provides important insights into their biological performance. The obtained particle sizes for CS NPs and CS/Ag NPs were within the nanoscale range, which is considered suitable for biomedical applications, as smaller particles can enhance cellular uptake and interaction with the wound microenvironment [14, 15]. Moreover, the relatively uniform size distribution indicated by the polydispersity index suggests a stable formulation.

The high positive zeta potential (> +40 mV) observed for both nanoparticle types reflects good colloidal stability and may enhance interaction with negatively charged cell membranes, potentially facilitating cellular adhesion and uptake [14]. In addition, the UV–visible absorption peak observed for CS/Ag NPs confirms the successful formation of silver nanoparticles and their surface plasmon resonance properties, which are associated with their antimicrobial activity [16, 17]. These physicochemical characteristics may collectively contribute to the enhanced wound healing effects observed, particularly in the PRP/CS/Ag NPs group.

Macroscopic evaluation demonstrated a significant time-dependent reduction in wound size and a progressive increase in wound contraction in all experimental groups, confirming effective healing progression throughout the study period. Notably, wounds treated with PRP/CS/Ag NPs exhibited the greatest reduction in wound size and the highest wound contraction percentages at later time points (days 7, 14, and 21), indicating superior clinical wound closure compared with the control and other treatment groups. These findings are consistent with previous preclinical evidence that chitosan-capped silver nanoparticles can accelerate wound closure and tissue repair through combined bioactive and antimicrobial effects [25].

Quantitative assessment of collagen deposition using Masson’s trichrome staining revealed distinct time-dependent effects of the tested treatments. At day 7 post-wounding, PRP-treated wounds showed significantly higher collagen deposition compared with the other experimental groups, indicating a pronounced early stimulatory effect of PRP on extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis. This finding underscores the early-phase advantage of PRP, whereas the superiority of CS/Ag NPs becomes more evident during the later remodeling phase. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that PRP accelerates early wound healing through the release of bioactive growth factors such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF), which stimulate fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis during the proliferative phase of healing [8, 36].

In contrast, during the later phases of healing (days 14 and 21), PRP/CS/Ag NPs-treated wounds exhibited significantly greater collagen deposition compared with all other groups. This sustained increase suggests that PRP/CS/Ag NPs not only support early matrix formation but also promote prolonged ECM production and effective tissue remodeling. The enhanced collagen deposition observed in this group may be attributed to the synergistic effects of chitosan and silver nanoparticles. Chitosan provides a bioactive scaffold that facilitates fibroblast adhesion, migration, and proliferation, thereby supporting collagen deposition, while silver nanoparticles exert antimicrobial and antioxidant effects that reduce bacterial burden and oxidative stress within the wound microenvironment. Together, these properties help preserve cellular activity and create optimal conditions for sustained collagen synthesis and matrix remodeling, as previously suggested by other nanomaterial-based wound healing studies [37, 38].

Histopathological evaluation further supported the macroscopic findings. PRP-treated wounds demonstrated early re-epithelialization and rapid granulation tissue maturation, particularly evident at day 7, whereas PRP/CS/Ag NPs-treated wounds showed more advanced tissue organization and adnexal structure regeneration during the remodeling phase. The superior wound size reduction observed in the PRP/CS/Ag NPs group may therefore be explained by the combination of accelerated epithelial regeneration, enhanced collagen deposition, and improved tissue remodeling, rather than by a single dominant mechanism [23, 39, 40].

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed minimal epithelial EGF expression in the control group at day 7, which is likely due to the absence of epithelial coverage during the early post-injury period. However, the detection of EGF-positive cells within the underlying connective tissue suggests an early localized growth factor response that may initiate re-epithelialization. Importantly, PRP/CS/Ag NPs-treated wounds exhibited significantly higher EGF expression across all examined time points. Given the well-established role of EGF in promoting keratinocyte proliferation and migration, this sustained upregulation provides a mechanistic explanation for the enhanced epithelial regeneration and faster wound closure observed in the PRP/CS/Ag NPs group, consistent with previous reports on bioactive nanomaterials and growth factor modulation [39–42].

Interestingly, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression was significantly higher in the PRP/CS NPs-treated group throughout the healing period, indicating enhanced myofibroblast differentiation and vascular activity within the wound bed. As α-SMA is a key marker of contractile myofibroblasts, this finding suggests that PRP/CS NPs predominantly stimulate cellular mechanisms associated with wound contraction and neovascularization. However, overall wound closure is a multifactorial process that also depends on epithelial regeneration, collagen remodeling, and local inflammatory control. The superior wound contraction observed in the PRP/CS/Ag NPs group may therefore reflect the combined effects of sustained EGF upregulation, enhanced collagen organization, and improved microenvironmental conditions, rather than myofibroblast activity alone. These findings suggest that PRP/CS NPs primarily enhance contractile responses, whereas PRP/CS/Ag NPs promote a more balanced and advanced remodeling profile. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies on chitosan-based wound healing materials [39–41].

The observed differences among treatment groups highlight the phase-specific actions of each therapeutic approach. PRP appears to primarily accelerate early wound healing through rapid growth factor delivery and early collagen deposition, while PRP/CS NPs enhance wound contraction and angiogenesis via sustained myofibroblast and vascular activity. In comparison, PRP/CS/Ag NPs provide prolonged benefits by maintaining higher growth factor expression, supporting advanced collagen remodeling, and ensuring a favorable antimicrobial environment. These complementary mechanisms suggest that combining biological therapies with nanomaterial-based adjuncts may represent an effective strategy for targeting multiple phases of wound healing simultaneously [34, 38, 41].

Unlike conventional composite hydrogel systems that rely on pre-formed biomaterial scaffolds, the present study introduces a biologically active combination of autologous PRP with chitosan-based nanoparticles applied directly at the wound site. This approach enables dynamic interaction with the wound microenvironment and may enhance the temporal release of bioactive factors during the healing process. Therefore, this study provides a complementary strategy to existing hydrogel-based systems by integrating biological and nanomaterial-based therapies in a more adaptable and clinically relevant manner [42].

Importantly, body weight measurements did not differ significantly among the experimental groups, indicating that none of the applied treatments induced adverse systemic effects. This finding supports the overall biocompatibility and safety of PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs when applied locally to cutaneous wounds in dogs. This finding is in line with the reported overall biocompatibility of autologous PRP and chitosan-based wound biomaterials, as well as the controlled topical use of silver nanoparticle–functionalized dressings in wound care [38, 43, 44].

Despite the promising therapeutic effects observed with silver-functionalized nanoparticles, potential concerns regarding nanoparticle accumulation and long-term toxicity should be considered. Silver nanoparticles are known to exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity, particularly at higher concentrations or with prolonged exposure [38]. However, in the present study, the nanoparticles were applied locally at controlled doses, and no adverse systemic effects were observed, as reflected by stable body weight measurements. Nevertheless, further studies are required to evaluate long-term safety, biodistribution, and potential accumulation in tissues.

It is important to consider that, upon mixing with PRP, nanoparticles are exposed to a protein-rich biological environment, which may lead to the formation of a protein corona and potentially influence their physicochemical properties [45, 46]. However, the high positive zeta potential observed for both CS NPs and CS/Ag NPs (> +40 mV) indicates strong electrostatic stability and suggests a reduced tendency for aggregation.

Additionally, protein corona formation is a well-established phenomenon in biological fluids and represents a dynamic interface between nanoparticles and surrounding biomolecules [45, 46]. This process does not necessarily impair nanoparticle functionality and may, in some cases, enhance cellular interactions and biological responses [47]. Therefore, although nanoparticle stability was not directly evaluated after mixing with PRP in the present study, the observed biological outcomes suggest that the nanoparticles retained their functional integrity during application.

This study has certain limitations that should be considered. First, although the experimental design included PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs groups, separate evaluation of individual nanoparticle components (CS NPs or Ag NPs alone) was not performed. Therefore, the specific contribution of each component cannot be fully isolated. Additionally, the study utilized standardized full-thickness wounds in healthy animals, which, while allowing controlled assessment of treatment effects, may not fully replicate the complex environment of infected or chronic wounds. Such experimental models are widely used to ensure reproducibility and minimize confounding variables [34, 35]. Future studies using infected or delayed-healing models are warranted to further explore the translational potential of these findings.

The canine full-thickness cutaneous wound model was selected because it closely reflects naturally occurring clinical wounds encountered in veterinary practice, providing directly applicable evidence for canine wound management where in vitro alternatives cannot recapitulate the integrated inflammatory, angiogenic, and remodeling responses of living skin. Similar controlled canine wound models have been widely used in previous PRP and regenerative wound-healing studies in dogs [7, 27, 48, 49], supporting both the scientific and ethical justification of this approach.

Collectively, the findings of the present study demonstrate that PRP, PRP/CS NPs, and PRP/CS/Ag NPs exert distinct yet synergistic effects on canine cutaneous wound healing. While PRP accelerates early repair responses, PRP/CS/Ag NPs promote sustained tissue remodeling and epithelial regeneration, supporting their potential use as effective adjunctive therapies for improving both early and late phases of wound healing in veterinary practice.

Future studies should focus on evaluating long-term outcomes, optimizing the PRP–nanoparticle formulation, and testing the treatment in chronic or infected wound models to enhance clinical applicability.
