Immunogenicity comparison of an Escherichia coli-produced 9-valent human papillomavirus vaccine and Gardasil9 in Chinese women aged 18–26 years: three-year follow-up data from a randomised clinical trial

Research in context

Evidence before this study

A PubMed search was undertaken on June 1st, 2025, using the following search algorithm: (“HPV” OR “Human papillomavirus”) AND (“vaccine” OR “vaccination”) AND “Gardasil 9”; no time or language restrictions were applied. The results identified no human clinical trials evaluating immunogenicity between 9-valent HPV vaccine candidates and Gardasil9 beyond the base study of this extension (NCT04782895).

We also searched two clinical research databases (clinicaltrials.gov and chinadrugtrials.org.cn) to identify relevant ongoing clinical studies. To our knowledge, ten HPV vaccine candidates have been registered for head-to-head comparison studies with Gardasil9.

Added value of this study

This longitudinal immunobridging study presents comparative data on the sustained immune responses of a novel 9-valent HPV vaccine (Cecolin9) versus Gardasil9 over a three-year follow-up period. The non-inferiority of HPV type-specific antibody response elicited by Cecolin9 compared to Gardasil9, which was initially reported at month 7, was sustained up to year 3. Therefore, the established plateau-phase immunity profile of Cecolin9 suggests its long-term protective potential against targeted HPV types.

Implications of all the available evidence

The demonstrated capacity of Escherichia coli-produced Cecolin9 to maintain comparable peak and plateau immunity to yeast-derived Gardasil9 informs promising potential for Cecolin9's durable protection. Given the persistent global supply constraints of Gardasil9, this evidence indicates Cecolin9 could serve as a potential effective alternative in resource-limited regions. Furthermore, these comparative immunogenicity data might provide critical insights for regulatory agencies and public health policymakers to expedite clinical development pathways for next-generation HPV vaccines.

Introduction

Cervical cancer, the fourth most common cancer among women, resulted in an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths worldwide in 2022.1 Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types has been identified as the primary cause of cervical cancer, with HPV types 16 and 18 being specifically responsible for 77% of invasive cervical cancer (ICC) cases globally. Moreover, HPV types 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58 collectively account for 94.7% of global ICC.2 Concurrently, genital warts are commonly associated with low-risk HPV types, with HPV 6 and 11 causing approximately 90% of cases.3

Serving as a mainstay in the Global Strategy for the Elimination of Cervical Cancer, HPV vaccines have substantially reduced the national-level prevalence of HPV infections and related illnesses.4 The global HPV vaccine market currently includes six first-generation vaccines targeting either high-risk types 16/18 alone or combined with low-risk types 6/11, as well as two second-generation 9-valent vaccines (Gardasil9 [Merck Sharp & Dohme] and Cecolin9 [Xiamen Innovax]).5,6

The global inequity in HPV vaccine implementation poses a pressing public health challenge, disproportionately affecting low-resource settings.7 As of June 2025, 147 WHO member states have integrated HPV vaccination into national immunisation programs.8 However, the adoption of Gardasil9 is concentrated only in 46 regions, predominantly high-income countries.9 This disparity underscores the supply deficit of cost-effective 9-valent vaccines in resource-limited regions.

Cecolin9, a second-generation 9-valent HPV vaccine, is derived from Cecolin, a WHO-prequalified, Escherichia coli-expressed bivalent HPV vaccine.10 Cecolin has obtained market authorisation in 23 countries and been incorporated into national immunisation programs in eight countries by June 2025. The favourable safety profiles and robust immunogenicity of Cecolin9 have been successfully demonstrated in early phase clinical trials.11,12 Building on this foundation, phase 3 trial data further substantiated the clinical efficacy of Cecolin9, leading to its regulatory approval in China in May 2025.6

As an integral component of Cecolin9's clinical development program, a head-to-head immunogenicity comparison study between Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 was conducted in healthy Chinese women aged 18–26 years in 2021, during which the target age for Gardasil9 was only approved for 16–26 years in China. Our previous report has detailed that Cecolin9 could induce non-inferior type-specific immune responses across all HPV types when compared to Gardasil9 and presented an acceptable safety profile.10

The evaluation of plateau antibody levels is critical in immunobridging assessments, proposed by the 2022 WHO HPV Vaccine Position Paper.5 Additionally, Gardasil9 has demonstrated sustained high effectiveness through 10-year longitudinal surveillance, with observed antibody responses reaching a plateau after 36 months post-vaccination.13,14 In this study, we present extended follow-up data from this head-to-head immunogenicity comparison trial at year 3, thereby enhancing the relevance and implications of our initial findings.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is an extension of a randomised single-blind head-to-head immunogenicity comparison clinical trial of Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 (NCT04782895).10 The base study was conducted in 2021, involving healthy Chinese women aged 18–26 years, which basically aligns with Gardasil9's licenced target age of 16–26 years in the Chinese market at that time. Briefly, 488 healthy non-pregnant women, without prior HPV immunisation, aged 18–26 years were enrolled and randomly assigned (1:1) to receive three doses of Cecolin9 or Gardasil9 at day 0, day 45, and month 6. The visit window periods for the second and the third dose were 39–60 days and 150–210 days, respectively. For the immunogenicity assessment, blood samples were collected at day 0 and month 7. During the base study, investigators with access to the vaccines signed confidentiality agreements and were restricted from participating in other trial processes, and the other study investigators, laboratory staff, and all participants remained masked.

This extended follow-up study (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06197802), has received approval from the Independent Ethics Committee of the Jiangsu Provincial Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (JSJK2023-A017-02). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants involved in the base study were invited to donate blood samples at year 3 for neutralising antibody (nAb) level analysis. All women provided written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they received additional HPV vaccines after the base study.

Procedures

To assess post-vaccination plateau antibody levels, blood samples were collected from all participants. HPV type-specific nAb concentrations were measured by the China National Institutes for Food and Drug Control (NIFDC) utilising the same triple-colour pseudovirion-based neutralisation assay (PBNA) as the base study.15 In brief, sera from participants and reference serum were diluted and added in duplicate wells to three 384-well plates (labelled as plate A, B, and C). Subsequently, three distinct types of HPV pseudovirions, each harbouring distinct fluorescent protein-expressing genes, were added to the respective plates: plate A, HPV 6, 33, and 45; plate B, HPV 11, 31, and 58; and plate C, HPV 16, 18, and 52. 293FT cells were then added for infection. The neutralisation was quantified using a high-throughput imaging system after co-culture. The 50% inhibitory dilution (ID50) was calculated using the Reed–Muench method, and the antibody concentration of the tested serum was then determined based on the known concentration of the reference serum. Anti-HPV antibody concentrations were expressed in international units (IU)/mL for HPV types 16 and 18 using the National Institute for Biological Standards and Control international standards, in units (U)/mL for HPV types 6, 33, 45, 52, and 58 using NIFDC standards, and in Y units (YU)/mL for HPV types 11 and 31 using reference standards of Xiamen Innovax. Cutoff values for nAb concentrations were consistent with the base study, with seronegative samples assigned a value half the cutoff. The neutralisation threshold was established through dedicated experiments by NIFDC. Sera from an external-sourced cohort of HPV DNA-negative young women were analysed. The cutoff value of ID50 was calculated as the mean ID50 plus three standard deviations, and this value was converted to an antibody concentration cutoff using the reference serum's ID50 and known concentration.

Statistical analysis

The primary objective of this study was to compare the plateau antibody levels induced by Cecolin9 with those induced by Gardasil9. The primary outcome, the geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) of each HPV type-specific nAbs, was evaluated in the per-protocol set for immunopersistence (PPS-P), consisting of participants who received all the three doses within acceptable time window, had no major protocol violations, were seronegative against the relevant type at day 0, and had serology results from serum samples obtained at year 3.

In terms of the base study, within the per-protocol set for immunogenicity (PPS-I, which consisted of participants who received all the three doses within acceptable time window, had no major protocol violations, were seronegative against the relevant type at day 0, and had valid serology results from serum samples obtained at month 7), the coefficients of variation (COV) were observed to range between 0.9645–1.3481 for the Cecolin9 group and 0.9062–1.4902 for the Gardasil9 group.10 The sample size calculation was predicated on demonstrating the non-inferiority of the GMC with an equal 1:1 ratio between the two vaccine groups. Using conservative assumptions of a true GMC ratio of 0.8 (Cecolin9 vs Gardasil9) and a COV estimate of 1.4, our calculations indicated that 182 participants per arm (364 total) at year 3 would achieve 99% statistical power. This ensures the lower boundary of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the GMC ratio exceeds the 0.5 non-inferiority margin using a one-sided t-test with α = 0.025. For the extension study phase, accounting for an estimated baseline seroprevalence rate of 7%, we determined that successful follow-up of at least 392 participants would be required to maintain statistical validity.10 This accounts for potential attrition while preserving study power.

In the PPS-P, two-sided 95% CIs of GMC ratios (Cecolin9 vs Gardasil9) at year 3 were computed using a one-way ANOVA model based on the log10 transformation of the concentrations, with the vaccine group serving as the categorical variable. For a specific HPV type, non-inferiority was established if the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI for the GMC ratio was greater than 0.5. Reverse cumulative distribution curves were constructed to visualise the distribution of antibody levels at year 3. The seropositivity rates between the two vaccine groups were analysed descriptively. Within the PPS-P, a subcohort was established by selecting participants with available serological results at both month 7 and year 3. Geometric mean fold changes (GMFCs, year 3 vs month 7) were determined to evaluate longitudinal shifts in HPV antibody concentrations. A generalised estimating equation (GEE) on the log-transformed values was applied to explore potential difference in antibody decay patterns, with the age at first vaccination considered as a covariate.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software (version 9.4). All reported p values were two-sided with an α value of 0.05 unless otherwise specified. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06197802).

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study coordinated antibody detection, but had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

After excluding participants who were seropositive at baseline, the PPS-P for each HPV type included 176–189 participants in the Cecolin9 group, and 190–197 participants in the Gardasil9 group. At three years post-vaccination, the GMC ratios (Cecolin9 vs Gardasil9) of all nine HPV types ranged from 0.78 to 1.91 (Fig. 2). For seven HPV types (6/16/31/33/45/52/58), the GMC ratios exceeded 1.0, with HPV 58 exhibiting the most notable ratio at 1.91 (95% CI 1.54–2.37). In contrast, the GMC ratios for types 11 and 18 were below 1.0, with the lowest ratio for HPV 11, at 0.78 (95% CI 0.64–0.95). The GMC ratios for all nine HPV types remained relatively stable from month 7 to year 3.10 The reverse cumulative curves of nAbs for both groups continued to present largely similar trends and magnitudes across all the HPV types (Fig. 3). NAbs seropositivity rates were above 80% for all HPV vaccine types in both vaccine groups (Fig. 4). Notably, HPV 16 maintained a seropositivity rate of 100%, and the other HPV types demonstrated comparable seropositivity rates between the two vaccine groups. From seven months to three years post-vaccination, both groups exhibited a pronounced decline in antibody levels across all HPV types. GMFCs, which indicated the extent of antibody decay, ranged from 0.08 (95% CI 0.07–0.09) to 0.17 (95% CI 0.16–0.19) in Cecolin9 group and 0.07 (95% CI 0.07–0.08) to 0.16 (95%CI 0.15–0.17) in Gardasil9 group (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Figure S1). Discussion

In this extension of the direct comparative study between a novel 9-valent HPV vaccine (Cecolin9) and Gardasil9, we observed that the non-inferiority of HPV type-specific nAb levels induced by Cecolin9 compared to Gardasil9, which was initially reported at month 7, was sustained up to year 3. Both vaccines induced similar plateau levels of nAbs and sustained high seropositivity rates across all nine HPV types following a three-dose regimen in women aged 18–26 years. Noteworthy, the magnitudes of antibody decay from month 7 to year 3 within the Cecolin9 cohort were basically similar to those observed in the Gardasil9 cohort. These robust data from our study provide evidence that Cecolin9 maintains a comparable immunogenicity profile compared with Gardasil9, both at the peak and plateau levels.

In general, an initial peak in vaccine-induced antibody levels is typically noted one month after completing the full HPV vaccination course, followed by a rapid decline within three years post-vaccination.16 Thereafter, the antibody levels reach a plateau and remain relatively stable throughout the subsequent years.13,17 Gardasil9 has been demonstrated to provide sustained protective effects for up to 10 years post-vaccination in young women aged 16–26 years.14 Combining these results about effectiveness of Gardasil9 with the non-inferior immunogenicity observed in our study, it is reasonable to anticipate that such non-inferior plateau antibody responses could contribute to a comparable enduring vaccine-induced protection between the two vaccines.

Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 were developed via valency expansion based on the same technical platforms as Cecolin and Gardasil, respectively. Sustained non-inferiority in immunogenicity between Cecolin and Gardasil has been demonstrated through both peak and plateau phases following single-dose administration.18,19 An independent study led by Program for Appropriate Technology in Health (PATH) confirmed comparable antibody responses between the two vaccines, with GMC ratios (Cecolin/Gardasil) maintaining non-inferiority both at 6 months (HPV16 = 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.8; HPV18 = 1.6, 1.4–1.9) and 24 months (HPV16 = 1.1, 0.9–1.4; HPV18 = 1.4, 1.1–1.7) post single-dose vaccination.20 Consequently, immunological consistency across both vaccine generations has been systematically validated through rigorous comparative analyses.

While the minimum protective antibody threshold remains to be determined, accumulating evidence indicates sustained protection may persist at low titres. Several studies have shown that naturally acquired antibodies were associated with a reduced risk of subsequent homotypic infection.21 Notably, a single dose of HPV vaccination has been substantiated to confer durable protection. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) trial in India, representing the longest prospective follow-up of single-dose recipients with 12-year surveillance, demonstrated an effectiveness of 92.0% (95% CI 87.0–95.0) of single-dose Gardasil against persistent HPV16/18 infections.22 Immunogenicity assessments of the single-dose cohort revealed that neutralising antibody titres against HPV 6/11/16/18 were 2.05–2.64 times those observed following natural infection at 10 years post-vaccination.23 In our study, Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 cohorts maintained HPV16 antibody levels 4.9 and 4.2-fold of the natural immune levels, respectively (calculated from the antibody levels of 13 participants who were seropositive prior to vaccination; data not shown). These preliminary immunological findings collectively provide tentative evidence that Cecolin9 may support sustained protective immunity, although natural immune levels remain to be further validated and definitive conclusions require DNA-confirmed endpoint studies.

Three years following vaccination, sustained high seropositivity (>80%) for all nine HPV types maintained in both vaccine groups. The Cecolin9 cohort demonstrated slightly lower seropositivity rates and GMCs for HPV 11/18 compared to the Gardasil9 cohort. These immunological differences, however, appeared to be of limited clinical significance. Historical data from the Long Term Follow Up of the FUTURE II showed only 52.4% of Gardasil recipients remained seropositive for HPV 18 by the competitive Luminex immunoassay at 14 years post-vaccination, yet no breakthrough HPV-18-associated high-grade cervical lesions were observed.24 Furthermore, while plateau antibody levels induced by single-dose vaccination are typically significantly inferior to those generated by three-dose vaccination, evidence is accumulating to demonstrate the high efficacy of single-dose vaccination.25 Therefore, the slightly diminished seropositivity for specific HPV types in Cecolin9 recipients does not necessarily indicate a waning of vaccine-induced clinical protection.

A key strength of our research lies in the consistent use of PBNA, which serves as a reliable and objective assessment of vaccine-induced immunity, throughout both the base study and this extension. This methodological consistency enables longitudinal immunogenicity comparisons and promotes a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of antibody decay and their implications. The GMC ratios between Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 group remained stable across all nine HPV types from month 7 through year 3 post-vaccination. Through the synthesis of the results at month 7 and year 3, we noted a similar trajectory of waning immunity across both vaccine cohorts, indicating the promising immunopersistence of Cecolin9, although further observation is required to confirm these patterns.

Several limitations are included in this study, foremost being the exclusion of adolescent female participants and the lack of evaluation for one-dose or two-dose schedule. WHO has recommended a one-dose or two-dose regimen for females aged 9–20 years and two-dose for those older than 21 years. An immuno-bridging trial is currently ongoing to evaluate the immunogenicity profiles of Cecolin9 in healthy individuals aged 9–17 years compared with those aged 18–26.26 Moreover, there is a pressing need for future rigorous comparative studies between Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 under WHO-recommended age-stratified regimens.5 Another limitation is that the accuracy of estimated natural infection antibody levels is constrained by the small number of seropositive participants at baseline. This means the comparative analysis of Cecolin9's plateau-phase antibody titres relative to natural infection-induced immunity warrants further assessments. Finally, the absence of data on sexual behaviour and other potential confounding factors, such as baseline PCR positivity, hinders the assessment of whether the antibody levels were affected by HPV exposure.

In summary, this follow-up study confirmed that the comparable immune response previously observed between Cecolin9 and Gardasil9 maintained immunological non-inferiority up to three years post-vaccination. The growing body of available evidence supports the efficacy of Cecolin9, thereby indicating its potential for durable protection against the relevant HPV types.
