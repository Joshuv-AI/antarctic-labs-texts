Do we really know if they are in pain? A cross‐sectional study in hospitalised adult patients in Spain
Authors: Laura Lorenzo Allegue, Leonor Laredo Velasco, Ana María Recio Vivas, José Miguel Mansilla Domínguez, Pedro Moñino Ruiz, Luz Bueno Rey, Isabel Font‐Jiménez, Emilio Vargas Castrillón
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo describe the prevalence and characteristics of pain in adult hospitalised patients, as well as to analyse the concordance between patient‐reported and recorded pain and its impact on analgesic management.DesignA cross sectional study.MethodsThe study was performed on a sample of 611 patients, from October to December 2017. Data were obtained from patient interviews, review of medical and nursing records and review of electronic prescribing.ResultsThe prevalence of pain at the time of the interview was 36.7%. The median VAS score was 4. 90% of the patients had their pain assessed within the last 24 h; however, concordance between patient‐reported pain and recorded pain in the nursing record was slight.ConclusionPain is still often documented inadequately. Despite the wide use of analgesics, half of the patients with moderate to severe pain do not have adequate pain management. A systematic assessment and recording of pain promotes appropriate analgesic prescription.Implications for the profession and patient careThe findings of our study provide insight into the main gaps in the correct management of pain in hospitalised patients. A systematic assessment and recording of the pain suffered by the patient facilitates its control and allows a better management of the analgesic prescription by the physician. This information could help hospital managers to develop training programmes on pain assessment and on the importance of doctor–nurse collaboration to improve pain management, increasing the quality of care and reducing hospital costs.Reporting methodThe study has adhered to the relevant EQUATOR guidelines, according to The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement: guidelines for reporting observational studies.

INTRODUCTION

Pain relief was declared a “fundamental human right” in 2010 (Cousins & Lynch, 2011) and is considered a quality standard during the hospitalisation process, especially in post‐surgical patients (Meissner et al., 2018). However, despite new techniques and medical products introduced in recent decades for its treatment, there is no evidence of real progress in pain relief (Notaro et al., 2021). Moreover, pain is now recognised as a serious public health problem that leads to loss of quality of life and impairment of the personal, family and social environment of the person suffering from pain (Bai & Cheng, 2022).

BACKGROUND

Evidence has shown that suboptimal pain management incurs costs to the individual and to society (Mayer et al., 2019), comparable to those of other diseases that are considered health priorities (i.e. heart disease, cancer and diabetes) (Mayer et al., 2019). Pain interference on sleep, emotional status, has a negative impact on day‐to‐day activities and often results in incapacity for work, among other consequences (Dupoiron et al., 2022; Notaro et al., 2021). Focusing on adequate acute pain management has been identified as one of the preventive factors for the development of chronic pain in the population (Gan, 2017).

Different studies conducted in different settings show the persistence of pain among patients in surgical units (Gordon et al., 2016; Mayoral Rojals et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2021), among the paediatric population (Vejzovic et al., 2020) and among older adults (Zwakhalen et al., 2018). The presence of pain in hospitalised patients combined with inadequate pain management (Damico et al., 2018), leads to a delay in the patient's recovery and, consequently, an increase in the length of hospital stay (Memtsoudis et al., 2020).

In clinical practice, the correct assessment of pain and its recording in the patient's medical record is considered the key to achieving an effective analgesic treatment (Angeletti et al., 2018). In this regard, in the hospital environment, the concept of pain as the 5th vital constant has been encouraged, thus promoting pain assessment in a systematic and routine way (Pozza et al., 2021) through the use of validated and appropriate tools for each type of patient (Karcioglu et al., 2018).

In spite of being considered one of the key components for the correct management of pain and an indicator of good clinical practice, some authors point out that the nursing record of pain is insufficient and inadequate (Muñoz‐Alvaredo et al., 2020). This is not only a barrier to the correct approach to pain, but also makes it difficult to determine the effectiveness of pain management interventions (Plummer et al., 2017).

THE STUDY: AIMS AND OBJECTIVE

The present study aims to describe the prevalence and characteristics of pain in adult hospitalised patients, as well as to analyse the concordance between patient‐reported and recorded pain and its impact on analgesic management. The three research questions aligned to these aims were (1) What are the prevalence and characteristics of pain in adult hospitalised patients?; (2) Do nursing records reflect the reality of pain in hospitalised patients?; (3) Do pain records influence analgesic pain management?

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

A cross‐sectional study was designed. The study was carried out in hospitalised patients of a tertiary level hospital in Madrid, Spain. A systematic sampling of all patients admitted to hospital from October to December 2017 who met the selection criteria was performed until the predetermined sample size had been fulfilled. For this purpose, a list provided by the Hospital Admitting Department was used, which included all operational beds in the hospital during that period of time.

The population targeted was individuals hospitalised for at least 24 h, who were 18 years or older and who agreed to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were patients admitted to Emergency Department, Intensive Care Unit, Post‐Anaesthesia Care Unit, Psychiatry or Palliative Care Unit and those unable to be interviewed due to cognitive disorders or lack of knowledge of Spanish. The exclusion criteria applied allowed the use of the same pain assessment tool in all the analysed patients.

The hospital wards were classified as medical wards or surgical wards.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated according the wide variability of the published pain prevalence data (Gregory & McGowan, 2016) by means of GRANMO program, “Population Estimation”. A sample of 632 individuals was calculated as sufficient to estimate, with a confidence level of 95% and an accuracy of ±4 percentage units, an expected proportion of pain prevalence of around 50%. An estimated replacement rate of 5% was assumed.

Procedures

The data were collected by nurses and physicians from the Clinical Pharmacology department and the Anaesthesia department, members of the hospital's Pain Committee. The sources of data were patient interviews, review of medical and nursing records and review of electronic prescriptions (Farmatools©). The patient interview, medical history audit and review of pain medication in Farmatools© were conducted on the same day to avoid information bias. Medical and nursing records, as well as electronic prescriptions, were analysed once it had been verified that the patient met the eligibility criteria and had been interviewed. Data were recorded on a Case Record Form (CRF) designed ad hoc by the researcher team. A 3‐digit code was used to identify each CRF in order to preserve patients' anonymity. All the CRFs were validated by the researcher team prior to database entry.

The team of professionals who collected the data was previously trained by the researchers to ensure consistency in data collection. The training consisted of explaining the method of data collection and the CRF.

Interviewers were responsible for assessing patients to determine whether they met the eligibility criteria. Once a patient was found to meet the selection criteria, he/she was informed of the characteristics and aim of the study. The patient was then asked for his/her express verbal consent to participate in the study.

Variables and measures

The primary endpoint was the presence of pain at the time of the interview. If pain was present, its intensity was assessed by the interviewer by means of a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) of 10 cm. In line with other authors (Andersson et al., 2017), pain intensity of 1 to 3 cm was rated as “mild”, >3 to 7 cm as “moderate”, and >7 to 10 as “severe”.

Interviewers also asked patients about the presence of pain previous to the time of the interview, if it had been communicated and whether it had been assessed using pain rating scales. The presence of chronic pain was also investigated, defined as suffering pain during the last 3 months (Nicholas et al., 2019).

Pain assessment recording data were retrieved from the patient's medical record and the patient's nursing record. The patient nursing record is made up of a qualitative written recording and a quantitative recording (the latter corresponds to the patient observation chart). The concordance between the information recorded by the nurse in the qualitative record (“Patient reports mild/moderate/severe pain”, “seems uncomfortable”, location…) and in the patient observation chart (VAS score) was assessed, taking into account the day and shift of the records. The concordance between the pain declared by the patient at the time of the interview and the pain recorded by the nurse in the patient's medical record was also assessed, provided that there was a record for the same day and shift. Both comparisons (intra‐ and inter‐rater) were evaluated for the last 24 h before the interview in a qualitative way: “presence of pain” yes/no, and “pain intensity” mild/moderate/severe.

Information on prescribed pain medication was gathered from the medication software Farmatools©. Pain medication was categorised in four main groups: non‐opioids, weak opioids, strong opioids and adjuvant pain medication (corticoids, anti‐seizure medications and antidepressants). Timing was categorised as “as needed”, “around the clock” or as “around the clock plus rescues”. The adequacy of pharmacological pain treatment was assessed by the Pain Management Index (PMI). The Pain Management Index score is calculated by subtracting the patient's pain severity score from the prescribed analgesic score. The analgesic score is based on the World Health Organization pain ladder: no analgesic were scored as 0, non‐opioids as 1, weak opioids as 2, and strong opioids as 3. Pain severity was first categorised as: mild, moderate, and severe according to patient's response in the interview. From these categorised, the pain severity score was created: 0 for no pain, 1 for mild pain, 2 for moderate pain, and 3 for severe pain. A negative PMI indicates insufficient pain management, while 0 and positive scores indicate adequate pain treatment. Patients were also asked about the degree of relief when analgesic medication was administrated. Relief was categorised as “no relief”, “a little”, “quite a lot” or as “a lot”.

Demographic and clinical data such as age, sex, main diagnosis, hospital ward (medical or surgical), and hospital department were collected from the medical record. The variable “age” was categorised in five groups (18–40; 41–60; 61–70; 71–80; >80 year‐old) given that pain threshold increases with age and this age‐related change increases the wider the age‐gap between groups (Lautenbacher et al., 2017).

Data analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers of cases and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if they were normally distributed, or as medians with interquartile range values if not. The prevalence of pain was calculated as the number of patients in pain at the time of the interview, in relation to all patients interviewed, with no missing values for that variable. The Chi‐square test was used to detect associations between categorical data. Relationships between quantitative variables, “age” and “VAS” score, and dichotomous qualitative variables were analysed using the t‐Student test and Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test respectively. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine differences in “VAS score” variable between age groups. To examine the concordance between pain reported by the patient in the interview and pain recorded by the nurse, as well as the intra‐rater concordance, Cohen's Kappa coefficient was used. To identify predictors of chronic pain, a binary logistic regression model with chronic pain presence as the dependent variable was performed. It was adjusted by those categorical variables that had shown statistical relationship (p ≤ 0.10) in the univariate analysis. Potential predictors included were: age, sex, hospital ward (medical/surgical). Significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS software version 28.0.1.

Main results

The prevalence of pain in the sample at the time of the interview was 36.7%. According to intensity, moderate to severe pain was present in 20.4% of the sample. The median VAS score among patients in pain at the time of the interview was 4 (IR: 2–5). The prevalence of pain was slightly higher in women (40% vs. 33.5%; p = 0.094), and no differences were found by gender in the median VAS score (p = 0.97). No relationship between age groups and the presence of pain was found, neither in the median VAS score (p = 0.06), nor in the analysis by the prevalence of moderate to severe pain (p = 0.634). Surgical patients had higher pain‐prevalence rates (50%) than medical patients (31%) (p < 0.001, OR = 2.22, CI (95%) = 1.562–3.164). However, the proportion of patients with moderate to severe pain was higher among medical patients (48%) than in surgical ones (61%) (p = 0.04, OR = 1.7, CI (95%) = 1.1–3).

A total of 43.5% of the patients suffered from chronic pain. The prevalence of chronic pain was higher in women (OR = 3.5, CI (95%) = 2.5–5, p < 0.001) and in older patients (p = 0.001), according to the logistic regression model.

Regarding the way pain was assessed, only 37.2% of patients who experienced pain at some point during their hospital stay stated that their pain had been assessed by a pain rating scale. The 4‐point Verbal Rating Scale was the most commonly used (62%), followed by the Numerical Rating Scale (30%) and the Visual Analogue Scale (8%).

Strikingly, 20% of the patients who had moderate or severe pain at the time of interview had not reported it to the staff.

The percentage of patients who had their pain assessment documented in the nursing record (qualitative written record and/or patient observation chart) was 90% and only 17% of patients had their pain assessed systematically, i.e. 3 times a day. Regarding medical records, the percentage of patients who had their pain assessment documented was only 55%. Surgical patients were more likely to have their pain assessment documented in the medical record compared to non‐surgical ones (70% vs. 50%, OR = 2.3, CI (95%) = 1.5–3.5, p < 0.001).

The concordance between the information registered by the nurse in the qualitative written recording and in the patient observation chart (VAS score), was evaluated in 33% of the sample because of the lack of records. The Kappa score for intra‐rater reliability was 0.37, i.e. fair agreement. The agreement between the pain reported by the patient at the time of interview and the pain recorded by the nurse in the patient observation chart was evaluated in 44% of the sample due to the lack of records. The Kappa score for inter‐rater reliability was 0.20, i.e. slight agreement.

Most patients, 88% of the sample and 93% of the patients who were in pain, were prescribed analgesics (Table 2). The schedule of administration was regular (“around the clock” or “around the clock plus rescues”) in 81% of the patients with pain. Pain relief was reported as “quite a lot” or “a lot” by 80% of patients who had been given analgesia (Table 3). Surgical patients were more likely to have a regular schedule of analgesic administration than medical patients (OR = 3, CI (95%) = 1.8–4.7, p < 0.001). Paracetamol was the most prescribed analgesic (Table 4) mainly used in fixed schedule (64%). It is noteworthy that only 18% of weak opioids and 14% of strong opioids are prescribed on a fixed schedule.

DISCUSSION

This study shows that pain is still a frequent and relevant issue in hospitalised patients. Nevertheless, by comparing our results with previously reported prevalence data in our country as well as in Europe (Gregory & McGowan, 2016; Polanco‐García et al., 2017), there seems to be a downward trend in the number of inpatients suffering from pain. This fact could be the result of the great efforts made by various international institutions in the first decade of 21st century in order to give visibility to and solve this public health problem (González‐María et al., 2020; Health Organization Regional Office for Europe, 2016).

Ineffective pain assessment and recording

Despite screening and assessment of pain are considered the cornerstone of pain treatment (Angeletti et al., 2018), we agree with other authors that pain is still often assessed and documented inadequately (Muñoz‐Alvaredo et al., 2020; Plummer et al., 2017). Hardly 2 out of 10 patients had pain assessment documented three times a day (i.e. once for each nursing shift), physician's pain notes were absent in almost half of the patients and the agreement between the pain reported by the patient and the pain recorded by the nurse was slight. The use of standardised pain severity scales was infrequent and inadequate. The 4‐point verbal rating scale was the most commonly used, although it has been shown to be less sensitive than the VAS or NRS in detecting differences between treatments and exacerbations of pain (Karcioglu et al., 2018). This poor use of pain assessment tools is considered to be one of the main nurse‐related barriers to effective pain management (Rababa et al., 2021).

Regarding patient‐professional communication, a striking finding was that 2 out of 10 patients who had moderate or severe pain at the time of the interview, had not report it to the staff. This fact could be related to “patient‐related‐ barriers to pain management”. Some patients assume pain as an inevitable part of their illness. Others are reluctant to report pain because they do not want to bother staff (Malones et al., 2021). Therefore, to break down such barriers, nursing staff must be proactive and inquire about pain, as well as informing patients of the necessity of reporting it (Dequeker et al., 2018). Also in this respect, more frequent use of pain assessment tools could improve nurse–patient communication by helping patients to express their pain.

Record‐based pain management

The vast majority of patients in pain were prescribed analgesics, and the main schedule of administration was regular. This pharmacological pain management practice, are aligned with the evidence‐based recommendations (Joshi & Kehlet, 2019). Patient satisfaction with the effect of analgesia was high. Even so, almost 4 out of 10 patients who were prescribed analgesics, were in pain at the time of the interview. In addition, near half of the patients with moderate to severe pain did not have adequate pain management, according to PMI.

The association found between the PMI value and the recording of pain assessment is noteworthy, with a positive PMI being more likely when pain assessment is recorded. This association could explain why surgical patients had a higher percentage of positive PMI compared to medical patients. This finding confirms that analgesic prescription cannot be independent of evaluation pain scores and that a good physician‐nurse interaction is necessary. Likewise, appropriate documentation of pain assessment would facilitate communication between all health care providers in the multidisciplinary team responsible for pain management (Rababa et al., 2021).

Additionally, the wide use of “around the clock” prescriptions among surgical patients could have influenced this group of patients to have a lower prevalence of moderate to severe pain than medical ones (Manworren & Atabek, 2021). The fact that opioid analgesics are mainly prescribed “as needed” may be negatively influencing their efficacy. The professional who decides whether or not the rescue medication is administered is the nurse. However, some barriers have been described that may prevent the “as needed” prescribed analgesic from being administered. These barriers include the lack of systematic assessment of pain, the trend to underestimate patient‐reported pain and the fear of addiction to this group of drugs. (Dequeker et al., 2018; Rababa et al., 2021).

Limitations

Our study has some limitations which must be considered. Conducting a survey in a hospital setting can lead to biases related to the validity of the information provided by the patient, who is in a vulnerable situation and dependent on health professionals. Also, the lack of recorded data has limited the possibility of assessing the intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability with greater precision. Further, some of the patients admitted to the hospital are excluded from our sample, such as patients with cognitive impairment. In this group of patients, the difficulty or inability to communicate is a major barrier to pain detection. It would be necessary to design specific research to improve pain management in this particularly vulnerable patient group. Additionally, this is a single‐centre study that makes difficult the generalisability of the data when compared to the results generated in multicentre studies. Nevertheless, this is a hospital‐wide study, which gives a comprehensive picture of pain practices in an academic setting, in one of the country's leading hospitals. Finally, although the PMI was originally set up and validated to evaluate cancer pain management, we used it to evaluate pain management in our entire sample. Such use is supported by previous studies in which the PMI has been used to evaluate pain management in hospitalised patients (Plummer et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021), although it has been suggested that its results should be reviewed (Thronæs et al., 2020). Perhaps future research is necessary to develop a more optimal way to assess this aspect of analgesic treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

Pain remains a frequent and clinically relevant experience among hospitalised patients. Improving the quality of records, in terms of concordance and frequency of recording could improve analgesic management of pain in the hospital setting. The planning of a future training intervention, aimed at health care providers, could be of benefit to enhance pain recording and collaboration in pain management between nurses and physicians.
