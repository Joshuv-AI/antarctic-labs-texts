The mediating effects of parenting self-efficacy between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of preterm infants
Authors: Yarui Zhao, Jin Liu, Minmin Li, Hong Zhang, Jingjing Gong, Juan Zhang, Yun Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-discharge coping difficulty presents a significant challenge for mothers of preterm infants. The readiness for hospital discharge and parenting self-efficacy are crucial factors influencing post-discharge coping difficulty. However, the pathways through which these factors impact post-discharge coping difficulty remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the impact of readiness for hospital discharge on post-discharge coping difficulty and the mediating role of parenting self-efficacy among mothers of preterm infants. A prospective study involving 462 mothers of preterm infants from six tertiary hospitals in Shandong Province was conducted. Mothers were evaluated on the day of discharge (using the Baseline characteristics and Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale) and three weeks post-discharge (utilizing the Parenting Sense of Competence Scale-Efficacy subscale and Post-Discharge Coping Difficulty Scale). Structural equation modeling was employed to analyze the mediating effect. The results of this study revealed that readiness for hospital discharge significantly decreased post-discharge coping difficulty (β = − 0.533, P < 0.001), and parenting self-efficacy also significantly reduced post-discharge coping difficulty (β = − 0.419, P < 0.001). Furthermore, parenting self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty, accounting for 25.35% of the total effect. Mothers reported a moderate level of post-discharge coping difficulty. In assisting mothers of premature infants to alleviate post-discharge coping difficulty, nurses could implement strategies focused on enhancing readiness for hospital discharge and parenting self-efficacy.Subject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

Globally, approximately 15 million premature infants are born each year, with China accounting for 7.8% of this number, ranking second in the world1. Premature infants have underdeveloped organs and systems, often requiring admission to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) following birth. Post-hospital discharge, issues like feeding challenges, apnea, infections, and metabolic disorders can arise, potentially resulting in readmission2. The transition from medical staff to the primary caregiver, typically parents, can pose a significant challenge, especially for mothers of preterm infants who often assume the primary responsibility for their care3. These parents often face heightened levels of stress, anxiety, and depression compared to caregivers of full-term infants4. In addition to providing essential daily care, mothers of premature infants, after their infants' discharge from the hospital, must also manage their infants’ medical needs, such as administering oxygen therapy and monitoring vital signs, which can contribute to increased post-discharge difficulty5.

Post-discharge coping difficulty encompasses the physical, psychological, and environmental challenges faced by individuals, including struggles with stress management, disease recovery, childcare, household responsibilities, emotional well-being, self-assurance in caregiving, and children’s adaptability post-discharge6,7. Research indicates that mothers of premature infants often encounter significant levels of post-discharge coping difficulty, adversely affecting their overall physical and mental well-being8,9. High levels of post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of preterm infants can lead to increased risks of anxiety, depression, and even suicidal ideation10. Inadequate care for infants post-discharge may result in emerging health issues, potentially necessitating rehospitalization11. Hence, it is imperative to explore effective strategies aimed at reducing post-discharge coping difficulty for mothers of premature infants.

Readiness for hospital discharge refers to the readiness of parents of preterm infants to bring their infants home from the hospital2. A strong readiness for hospital discharge plays a critical role in ensuring a smooth transition for premature infants from hospital to home12. Conversely, feeling unprepared for discharge can hinder parents’ ability to care for their infants effectively, leading to difficulties in meeting their care needs7,13. This often results in heightened parental stress, post-discharge coping challenges, and increased rates of emergency visits and readmissions for premature infants, adding strain to healthcare resources14,15. Previous studies have highlighted a relationship between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty16,17. Specifically, mothers who felt unprepared for discharge encountered significant challenges related to stress, self-assurance, and the need for emotional support18. However, the current body of evidence remains limited and insufficient to definitively establish a link between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty in mothers of preterm infants.

Parenting self-efficacy refers to a parent’s belief in their ability to effectively organize and execute various parenting tasks, influencing their capabilities and caregiving behaviors19,20. Higher levels of parenting self-efficacy have been associated with improved outcomes after discharge for both parents and children21. Mothers lacking confidence in infant care may face unfavorable maternal experiences, struggle with embracing their role, and encounter difficulties in providing optimal care for their infants2,22,23. Moreover, low confidence in infant care has been linked to increased post-discharge feeding issues and a higher risk of readmission24,25. Enhancing readiness for hospital discharge is likely to enhance parenting self-efficacy26,27. Adequately prepared parents exhibit increased confidence in caring for their infant, facilitating a smoother transition28,29. Parenting self-efficacy could potentially act as a significant mediator in the relationship between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping challenges in mothers of premature infants. However, the current lack of empirical support for this association necessitates further investigation.

Drawing from the Transition Theory, transitional conditions can impact both proximal and distal indicators within response patterns, where proximal indicators can further affect distal indicators30. In this study, readiness for hospital discharge was identified as the transitional condition, parenting self-efficacy as the proximal response pattern, and post-discharge coping difficulty as the distal response pattern20. Inadequate parenting self-efficacy poses a significant challenge for parents of premature infants, and if unaddressed, it can exacerbate post-discharge coping difficulty2,31. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the mediating role of parenting self-efficacy between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of premature infants, aiming to offer insights for the development of targeted intervention strategies.

The hypotheses were as follows:

Hypothesis 1:

Readiness for hospital discharge is negatively associated with post-discharge coping difficulty.

Hypothesis 2:

Parenting self-efficacy mediates the relationship between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a multicenter prospective design. Mothers of premature infants were conveniently sampled from six tertiary hospitals in Shandong Province, China, between September 2022 and January 2023. Measurements were conducted on the day of discharge and at a 3-week follow-up period. Approval for this study was granted by Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (SWYX: NO.2022-366), and all participants provided informed consent. Furthermore, all procedures were executed in compliance with the pertinent guidelines and regulations of the hospital.

Sample

The study comprised of mothers of preterm infants meeting specific criteria: infants born before 37 weeks of gestational age and subsequently admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). These mothers also assumed primary caregiving responsibilities post-discharge. Exclusion criteria included mothers with a history of mental illness or cognitive impairment, as well as those concurrently enrolled in other research projects.

The sample size was determined using a structural equation modeling sample size calculator32. With a targeted statistical power level of 0.95, an anticipated effect size of 0.30, and α set at 0.05, the study involved three latent variables and ten observed variables. The recommended minimum sample size was 184; considering a potential dropout rate of 20%, the final required sample size was estimated to be 230. Ultimately, 462 mothers of preterm infants were enrolled in the study.

Measures

Baseline characteristics

The questionnaire encompassed two components: (a) essential data regarding preterm infants (gestational age, birth weight, gender, twins or multiples); (b) key details about mothers of preterm infants (age, educational level, marital status, monthly income, presence of chronic conditions, medical insurance coverage, and previous experience in premature infant care).

Readiness for hospital discharge

The Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS), developed by Weiss7 and subsequently translated and culturally adapted by Chen33, was utilized. The Chinese version features 22 items organized into four dimensions: pain status (2 items), physical-emotional status (7 items), knowledge (9 items), and expected support (4 items). Each item is rated on a scale of 0–10, with 0 signifying “not at all” and 10 indicating “completely prepared”. The total scores range from 0 to 220, with higher scores reflecting increased readiness for discharge. The Cronbach’s α value for this study was 0.94, whereas the Chinese version of the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.9133.

Parenting self-efficacy

The Parenting Sense of Competence Scale (PSOC) consists of two subscales: the Efficacy subscale and the Satisfaction subscale34. In this study, the Efficacy subscale, comprising 8 items, was employed to assess parenting self-efficacy. Responses were rated on a 6-point scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 6 (“strongly agree”), resulting in a total score between 8 and 48, where higher scores indicate enhanced parenting self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.86, while for the Chinese version of the subscales, it was 0.8035.

Post-discharge coping difficulty

The Post-Discharge Coping Difficulty Scale (PDCDS), developed by Weiss7, was utilized to evaluate the post-discharge coping challenges faced by parents of hospitalized children. The Chinese version of the scale comprises 11 items categorized into difficulty perception, support requirements, and coping effectiveness36. Using a 0–10 point scoring method, 0 reprents “none at all” and 10 reprents “a great deal”. The total score of the scale is the sum of each item score and then divided by the number of items, resulting in a total score between 0 and 10, where higher scores reflect greater difficulties. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.84, and for the original scale, it was 0.8536.

Procedure

One registered nurse was selected from each hospital to administer in the survey following consent from the head nurse of the respective hospitals. Preceding the research, the surveyors underwent standardized training and were briefed on the survey's timeline and methodology. The surveyors informed mothers of premature infants about the study’s purpose and content on the day of discharge and collected baseline characteristics and the RHDS post-consent. Three weeks post-hospital discharge of the preterm infants, the surveyors conducted follow-up via telephone, explaining the study's objective, and distributed electronic questionnaires via WeChat to collect data on the PSOC and the PDCDS. Data collection was concluded by the surveyors sending electronic questionnaires to participants, all of which were facilitated through the Questionnaire Star Platform (www.wjx.cn). Monthly multicenter meetings were held to ensure data quality, providing guidance and feedback to surveyors on questionnaire dissemination. A total of 510 mothers of preterm infants were invited to partake in the survey; however, 8 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 9 declined participation, and 3 did not provide informed consent. Ultimately, 490 mothers completed the initial data collection phase, with 15 unreachable, 5 voluntarily withdrawing, and 8 questionnaires deemed invalid. Consequently, 462 mothers were included in the data analysis, resulting in an effective questionnaire recovery rate of 90.59% (Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0. In this study, all variables exhibited a normal distribution. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were utilized to compare variable differences across baseline characteristics. Pearson correlation was employed to explore relationships among variables. The structural equation model was constructed using AMOS 23.0. To enhance reliability, the parenting self-efficacy items were parceled during model creation37. Upon factor analysis, the eight parenting self-efficacy items were aggregated into three parcels using the balanced parceling method. The structural equation model included readiness for hospital discharge as the independent variable, parenting self-efficacy as the mediator, and post-discharge coping difficulty as the dependent variable. Control variables were selected based on significant characteristics identified in univariate analysis. A model was considered to fit well if the chi-square degree ratio (χ2/df) was under 3, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was below 0.08, and the normed fit index (NFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) were all above 0.9. Mediation analysis was conducted using the Bootstrap method.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The baseline characteristics of the subjects and a univariate analysis of variables across baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the 462 mothers of premature infants, the average age was 31.62 years (SD = 4.92), with 42.2% holding a high school education or lower. The majority (97.4%) were married, and 90.7% lacked prior experience in caring for premature infants. In terms of premature infants, 256 (55.4%) were male, and 206 (44.6%) were female, with the majority (86.6%) being singletons. The gestational age of the preterm infants was 33.14 weeks (SD = 2.84), and the birth weight averaged 1999.76 g (SD = 666.90). Statistically significant differences in mothers' post-discharge coping difficulty scores were noted concerning gestational age and twins or multiples (P < 0.01). Mothers who have delivered twins or multiples experienced a higher level of post-discharge coping difficulty.

Mediation analysis

The mediating role of parenting self-efficacy was examined through a structural equation model. The initial model results were: χ2/df = 3.612, RMSEA = 0.075, CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.914, and GFI = 0.939. Based on modification indices, the residuals e1 and e2, as well as e8 and e9, were interconnected with double-headed arrows. Following model adjustments, all fit indices improved (χ2/df = 2.675, RMSEA = 0.060, TLI = 0.945, CFI = 0.959, GFI = 0.956), falling within acceptable ranges and suggesting model adequacy. Readiness for hospital discharge exhibited a direct negative correlation with post-discharge coping difficulty (β = − 0.533, P < 0.001). Furthermore, readiness for hospital discharge indirectly influenced post-discharge coping difficulty through parenting self-efficacy (β = − 0.181, P < 0.001). The total effect of readiness for hospital discharge on post-discharge coping difficulty was − 0.714 (P < 0.001). These results indicated that parenting self-efficacy partially mediated the association between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty (Fig. 2 and Table 3).

Discussion

Employing a two-wave design, this study conducted a prospective examination of the impact trajectory of readiness for hospital discharge on parenting self-efficacy and post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of preterm infants. The findings highlighted that low readiness for hospital discharge correlated with reduced parenting self-efficacy, consequently leading to heightened post-discharge coping challenges. This pioneering research delves into these connections and underlying mechanisms, offering valuable insights for enhancing post-discharge coping difficulty for mothers of preterm infants.

The readiness for hospital discharge and parenting self-efficacy scores in this study sample were comparatively lower than those documented in other nations14,38, while the post-discharge coping difficulty scores were higher14. The heightened patient-nurse ratio and significant workload in China, in contrast to some developed countries, direct a focus towards discharge planning and treatment strategies with less emphasis on pre-discharge preparedness and parental confidence and coping ability post-discharge2. These findings underscore the necessity of prioritizing interventions to enhance readiness for hospital discharge and parenting self-efficacy to alleviate post-discharge coping difficulties among mothers in the future.

The findings indicated a significant direct association between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of premature infants, aligning with prior research7,8. Parents’ perception of readiness to bring their child home from the hospital serves as a crucial determinant of potential challenges they might face upon returning home14. A perceived lack of preparedness can lead to challenging transitions and difficulties in caregiving at home. Adequately prepared parents exhibit physical and emotional readiness, possessing a solid understanding of caring for preterm infants and a certain level of psychological readiness to address potential challenges. This readiness equips them to better manage post-discharge stress and difficulties. Targeting readiness for hospital discharge may offer a promising avenue to enhance post-discharge coping difficulty. Several countries advocate different strategies to enhance maternal readiness for hospital discharge. For example, Malaysia advocates the ‘Preemie Mom: A Guide for You’ package39, while Iran promotes maternal empowerment initiatives40, Canada favors single-family rooms41, and Turkey emphasizes family-integrated care42. These effective strategies serve as noteworthy examples that could be adopted to improve outcomes.

Parenting self-efficacy demonstrated a negative correlation with post-discharge coping difficulty, consistent with prior research23. Low parenting self-efficacy can leave mothers unsure and unconfident in their child-rearing skills, impeding their ability to manage the unique needs and daily care of preterm infants effectively, thereby exacerbating the challenges they encounter. According to self-efficacy theory, mothers with diminished parenting self-efficacy may face obstacles in acquiring new knowledge and skills, struggle to cope effectively with challenges, and exhibit a higher likelihood of giving up43.

The study unveiled that readiness for hospital discharge indirectly impacts post-discharge coping difficulty through its influence on parenting self-efficacy, aligning with Transition Theory30. The results suggested that parents who are well-prepared for hospital discharge may possess increased confidence and resilience in overcoming challenges post-discharge, potentially reducing coping difficulty. Adequately prepared mothers exhibit a deeper comprehension of the unique needs and coping strategies vital for preterm infants, resulting in enhanced confidence and efficacy in caregiving. Mothers with heightened parenting self-efficacy are typically more adept at facing daunting parenting tasks with a positive and composed demeanor44. While readiness for hospital discharge is influenced by multiple factors, interventions targeting enhanced parenting self-efficacy, such as promoting fathers' active involvement and establishing crucial support networks for families, could be pivotal in ameliorating post-discharge coping difficulty when practical challenges impede the readiness improvement processes.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, while it was conducted across multiple centers, it solely focused on hospitals within one province, potentially limiting the sample's representativeness and generalizability. Secondly, the study did not include repeated measurements of the variables, emphasizing the importance of future research repeating measurements to assess variable changes over time. Thirdly, all questionnaires utilized were self-reported by participants, which could introduce subjectivity and potential bias to the findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, parenting self-efficacy acted as a mediator in the association between readiness for hospital discharge and post-discharge coping difficulty among mothers of premature infants. By improving readiness for hospital discharge and parenting self-efficacy, there is potential to alleviate post-discharge coping challenges experienced by these mothers.
