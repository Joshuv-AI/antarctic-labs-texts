Acquisition of the Competency of Generalism and Its Association With Community‐Based Clinical Clerkships at General/Family Medicine Institutions: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Yoshifumi Sugiyama, Rieko Mutai, Mariko Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan was revised in 2022, introducing a new competency, “Generalism,” to the set of basic competencies required of physicians. However, it is unclear which types of medical institutions provide the optimal educational setting for acquiring the competency of Generalism through community‐based clinical clerkships.MethodsParticipants were students at The Jikei University School of Medicine who participated in the Family Medicine Clerkship. Students rated, on a seven‐point Likert scale (0–6), the extent to which they had acquired 18 specific competencies within the competency of Generalism. We used the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test to compare total scores and scores for specific competencies by type of medical institution (general/family medicine, internal medicine, and other specialties). We used Poisson regression with robust error variance to evaluate the association between the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and the type of medical institution.ResultsIn total, 107 students participated in the Family Medicine Clerkship, of whom 69 were included in the analysis (valid response rate: 64.5%). Both the total score and scores for specific competencies related to home medical care, life processes from childhood through adulthood, and comprehensive (biopsychosocial and cultural) perspectives were higher at general/family medicine institutions. Moreover, general/family medicine institutions were significantly associated with higher total scores for the competency of Generalism (adjusted prevalence ratio 2.84, 95% confidence interval 1.20–6.71).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that community‐based clinical clerkships at general/family medicine institutions may offer valuable educational opportunities for cultivating the competency of Generalism.

Introduction

Countries worldwide are experiencing a demographic shift toward older populations, a phenomenon known as population aging [1]. Japan is at the forefront of this trend, with the highest proportion of older adults in the world: approximately 30% of its population is aged 65 years or older [2]. Population aging has given rise to diverse and complex health and social needs that cannot be fully addressed within the narrowly subdivided and highly specialized domains of traditional clinical practice. These include challenges that transcend existing medical disciplines, such as frailty, social isolation, and loneliness, as well as conditions that are intricately intertwined across multiple specialties, such as multimorbidity [3, 4, 5]. Consequently, the concept of generalism in medicine has gained increasing attention and recognition in Japan [6].

The growing emphasis on generalism has influenced undergraduate medical education in Japan [7]. The Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan, which serves as the national framework for medical education, was revised in 2022 [7]. In this revision, a new competency, “Generalism,” was introduced to the set of basic competencies required of physicians [7]. In particular, to ensure and strengthen education related to this competency, clinical clerkships mandated by universities and implemented at community‐based medical institutions are playing an increasingly important role [7].

The Clinical Clerkship in Family Medicine (Kateii‐Jisshu, hereafter referred to as the Family Medicine Clerkship) at The Jikei University School of Medicine is the oldest community‐based clinical clerkship program in Japan, and originally began as an observership [8]. The program was launched in 1986 through collaboration with the Association of Medical Practitioners (Jitchi‐Ika no tame no Kai), which later became one of the founding bodies of the Japan Primary Care Association, marking the first initiative of its kind in Japan [8, 9]. The program remains an integral component of community‐based clinical clerkships at The Jikei University School of Medicine.

Previous studies have examined how differences in training environments and program characteristics influence educational outcomes in medical education [10, 11]. For example, a review of longitudinal integrated clerkships has shown that training in community‐based or rural settings can influence learning experiences and competency development [10]. In Japan, community‐based clinical clerkships have been associated with improvements in students' perceived competencies and learning opportunities [11]. However, prior studies have not sufficiently examined how differences in medical institution types influence the acquisition of the competency of Generalism. In particular, studies that assess this relationship across multiple domains within a structured framework—such as the 18 specific competencies defined in the Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan—remain limited in the context of undergraduate community‐based clinical clerkships in Japan.

Consequently, it is unclear which types of medical institutions provide the optimal educational setting for acquiring the competency of Generalism. Clarifying this association is essential to optimize undergraduate medical education and ensure that medical students acquire the basic competencies required of physicians. This study therefore aimed (1) to describe the acquisition of specific competencies within the competency of Generalism by type of medical institution and (2) to examine the association between the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and the type of medical institution.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [12].

Setting

This study was conducted at The Jikei University School of Medicine in Tokyo, Japan. All medical students there participate in a community‐based clinical clerkship called the Family Medicine Clerkship. Students are placed at various community‐based medical institutions, including clinics and hospitals. These institutions provide primary care services and fulfill the functions expected of community‐based “kakaritsuke‐i” (family doctors) [13]. Most of these institutions are located in the Tokyo metropolitan area (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Chiba, and Saitama), but some are in regional areas across Japan. These areas were defined as those where commuting to medical institutions was not feasible within a reasonable travel time, typically requiring long‐distance travel such as bullet train, limited express train, or air travel. Students were allocated to institutions based primarily on travel time (within approximately 1 h by public transportation), except in cases where they were assigned to institutions in regional areas. This constraint was applied to ensure the feasibility of daily commuting during the clerkship period. Student preferences were not used in the allocation process. The duration of the clerkship is 1 week, usually 5 days, excluding weekends. A common syllabus is distributed to all institutions, ensuring that the objectives and educational role of the clerkship are shared and that the clerkship content is reasonably standardized. However, beyond the designated clinical departments, these institutions vary in several characteristics, including the number of physicians, the number of other staff members (including nurses), and the availability of beds and medical equipment. There is therefore some variation in clerkship content across facilities. To address this variation, a reflection session is conducted on the final day, during which students share their individual learning experiences. These reflections are also reviewed to assess whether a reasonable level of standardization has been achieved in the clerkship content.

Participants

Participants were 4th‐ and 5th‐year medical students at The Jikei University School of Medicine who participated in the Family Medicine Clerkship between September 2024 and July 2025. This period corresponds to a single cohort of students in clinical clerkships under the same curriculum and rotation schedule, during which they rotated through multiple specialties, including the Family Medicine Clerkship. Students who did not provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Data Sources and Measurement

Data on the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and on students' intended specialty were collected using a web‐based, self‐administered questionnaire. A link to the questionnaire was emailed to all eligible students on September 9, 2025, and data collection closed on September 15, 2025. The questionnaire included a Directed Question Scale (“For this item, please select ‘Somewhat disagree.’”) to detect satisficing—response behavior in which participants fail to devote sufficient attention to a survey [14]. Students who provided invalid responses to the Directed Question Scale were excluded from the analysis. Data on students' sex were obtained from the Family Medicine Clerkship roster.

Data on medical institutions and their directors were primarily collected from official institutional websites. When websites were unavailable or lacked the necessary information, supplementary data were obtained from the official websites of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and rosters published by the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, the Japanese Medical Specialty Board, the Japan Primary Care Association, and the Japanese Association for Home Care Medicine [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Variables

Acquisition of Competency of Generalism

The Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan defines 10 basic competencies required of physicians: Professionalism, Generalism, Lifelong Learning, Research, Problem‐Solving, Information Technology, Clinical Skills, Communication, Interprofessional Collaboration, and Medicine in Society [7]. Each competency has learning objectives based on the intended learning outcomes. These learning objectives are organized into four hierarchical levels: Level 1, competencies; Level 2, components; Level 3, specific competencies; and Level 4, specific actions [7].

This study focused on the competency of Generalism (Level 1). This competency contains four components (Level 2): Holistic Perspectives and Approaches, Community Perspectives and Approaches, Life Perspectives and Approaches, and Social Perspectives and Approaches. Each component is further divided into specific competencies (Level 3). Holistic Perspectives and Approaches covers six specific competencies: (1) transdisciplinary care; (2) comprehensive perspectives on biological, psychological, and social issues; (3) patient‐centered medicine; (4) evidence‐based medicine; (5) behavioral science; and (6) palliative care. Community Perspectives and Approaches covers four specific competencies: (7) basic concepts in primary care; (8) primary care in the community; (9) provision of primary care according to medical resources; and (10) primary care at home. Life Perspectives and Approaches covers six specific competencies: (11) life processes; (12) childhood in general; (13) fetal, neonatal, and infant stages; (14) school‐age, adolescence, young adulthood, and adulthood; (15) geriatric stage; and (16) end of life. Social Perspectives and Approaches covers two specific competencies: (17) health in medical, cultural, and social contexts; and (18) social sciences [7].

Students were asked the following question regarding these 18 specific competencies, each accompanied by explanatory descriptions of specific actions (Level 4): “Through the Family Medicine Clerkship, do you believe that you acquired, at least in part, specific competencies (1–18)?” Responses for each specific competency were rated on a seven‐point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (0 points) to “strongly agree” (6 points). The total score for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism was calculated as the sum of the scores for all 18 specific competencies, giving a possible range of 0 to 108 points. Higher scores indicated that students believed they had acquired, at least in part, the competency of Generalism and its underlying specific competencies to a greater extent through the Family Medicine Clerkship.

The questionnaire used in this study to assess the acquisition of the competency of Generalism was developed based on the Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan and aligned with its self‐assessment form. Each item corresponded to hierarchical learning objectives. Furthermore, the competency of Generalism is grounded in a construct that was systematically developed through a formal consensus‐based process [21]. Accordingly, the questionnaire is considered to have an acceptable degree of content validity for assessing the acquisition of the competency of Generalism.

Type of Medical Institution

We hypothesized that the board certifications held by the directors of medical institutions might influence the educational orientation of these institutions, particularly the learning environment and the educational content provided to medical students. We therefore classified the institutions into three categories based on the board certifications of the directors (clinic directors in clinics and department heads in hospitals): general/family medicine, internal medicine, and other specialties. The board certifications considered included Board‐Certified Doctor of General Medicine, Japan Primary Care Association certified primary care physician, Japan Primary Care Association certified family physician, Board Certified Specialist of the Japanese Association for Home Care Medicine, Board Certified Member of the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, Fellow of the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, board certifications in internal medicine subspecialties, and board certifications in other disciplines. When directors had multiple board certifications, classification priority was applied as follows: “general/family medicine” certifications were prioritized over “internal medicine,” and “internal medicine” certifications were prioritized over “other specialties.” Board‐Certified Doctor of General Medicine, Japan Primary Care Association certified primary care physician, and Japan Primary Care Association certified family physician were classified as “general/family medicine.” Based on the similarity of the required competencies, Board Certified Specialist of the Japanese Association for Home Care Medicine was also classified as “general/family medicine.” [22, 23, 24] Among the remaining directors, Board Certified Member of the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, Fellow of the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, and board certifications in internal medicine subspecialties were classified as “internal medicine,” and the rest as “other specialties.” This classification approach was adopted because directors did not necessarily hold a single board certification. Board‐Certified Doctor of General Medicine, Japan Primary Care Association certified primary care physician, Japan Primary Care Association certified family physician, and Board Certified Specialist of the Japanese Association for Home Care Medicine were prioritized in the classification because these certifications are relatively uncommon and are considered to better reflect the professional characteristics of physicians than certifications in internal medicine or its subspecialties [17, 18, 19, 20].

Participants' Characteristics

The participants' characteristics included sex and intended specialty. Age was not included because variation was expected to be minimal. The study population was limited to 4th‐ and 5th‐year medical students, and the vast majority (> 99%) had entered medical school immediately after high school or after one to three gap years [25]. Intended specialty was classified into the 19 basic fields defined by the Japanese Medical Specialty Board—internal medicine, pediatrics, dermatology, psychiatry, surgery, orthopedic surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, ophthalmology, otorhinolaryngology, urology, neurosurgery, radiology, anesthesiology, pathology, laboratory medicine, emergency medicine, plastic surgery, rehabilitation medicine, and general medicine [26]—as well as family medicine and others. Multiple responses were permitted.

We hypothesized that the intended specialty might influence the acquisition of the competency of Generalism. We therefore classified intended specialty into three categories: general/family medicine, internal medicine, and other specialties. When students reported multiple intended specialties, classification priority was applied as follows: “general/family medicine” was prioritized over “internal medicine,” and “internal medicine” was prioritized over “other specialties.” Students who expressed interest in general medicine or family medicine were classified as “general/family medicine.” Among the remaining students, those who expressed interest in internal medicine were classified as “internal medicine,” and the rest as “other specialties.” This classification approach was adopted because students did not necessarily have a single intended specialty. When multiple interests were reported, an interest in general medicine or family medicine—which was relatively uncommon and considered to better reflect the characteristics of participants regarding their intended specialty than an interest in internal medicine—was prioritized in the classification [27].

Characteristics of Medical Institutions and Their Directors

The characteristics of medical institutions included geographic location, type of institution (clinic or hospital), provision of outpatient care, and provision of home medical care. The characteristics of directors included sex and years since graduation.

Study Size

The study size was determined by the number of 4th‐ and 5th‐year medical students at The Jikei University School of Medicine who participated in the Family Medicine Clerkship between September 2024 and July 2025.

Statistical Methods

We conducted descriptive analyses of participants' characteristics, characteristics of medical institutions and their directors, total scores for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism, and scores for specific competencies. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages (%).

We used the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test to compare total scores for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and scores for specific competencies by type of medical institution. In addition to p‐values, we also calculated effect size (r) as |Z|/√N, where Z is the standardized test statistic from the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and N is the total number of observations across both groups.

We used Poisson regression with robust error variance to estimate prevalence ratios and to evaluate the association between the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and the type of medical institution. The total score for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism was dichotomized at the median and used as the binary dependent variable, with the type of medical institution as the explanatory variable, and intended specialty, practice setting, and directors' years since graduation as covariates. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation with the multivariate imputation by chained equations algorithm. The imputation model included the dependent variable, explanatory variable, and all covariates considered to be associated with missingness. Results from 100 imputed datasets were combined.

All statistical analyses were conducted using StataNow/SE 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant without adjustment for multiple comparisons [28]. Results from comparative analyses are presented with corresponding p‐values and effect sizes to facilitate interpretation.

Among the respondents, six declined to participate, and 11 were excluded because of invalid Directed Question Scale responses, leaving 69 students for inclusion in the analysis (valid response rate: 64.5%).

Table 2 also shows the scores for specific competencies by type of medical institution. There were several differences between general/family medicine institutions and internal medicine institutions. Within the Community Perspectives and Approaches, the scores for provision of primary care according to medical resources (3.7 [1.5] vs. 2.7 [1.6], p = 0.04, effect size = 0.33) and for primary care at home (5.0 [1.4] vs. 3.7 [2.2], p = 0.03, effect size = 0.34) were higher at general/family medicine institutions. Within the Social Perspectives and Approaches, the score for health in medical, cultural, and social contexts was higher at general/family medicine institutions (4.8 [0.9] vs. 4.0 [1.4], p = 0.04, effect size = 0.32).

Discussion

The scores for provision of primary care according to medical resources and for primary care at home, as well as those related to life processes from childhood through adulthood, were higher among students in the Family Medicine Clerkship at general/family medicine institutions. In addition, the score for health in medical, cultural, and social contexts was higher at these institutions. Moreover, the Family Medicine Clerkship at general/family medicine institutions was associated with higher total scores for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism. However, these findings may reflect differences in students' perceived competency acquisition rather than definitive evidence of actual differences in competency development.

Comparing the specific competencies acquired within the competency of Generalism yields important implications regarding general/family medicine institutions. First, the scores for specific competencies related to home medical care—such as provision of primary care according to medical resources and primary care at home—were higher at general/family medicine institutions. The former is closely associated with home medical care because of the resource limitations inherent in this setting. This is likely because, in the present study, the vast majority of general/family medicine institutions provided home medical care. In Japan, the proportion of physicians providing home medical care by specialty certification remains unclear. However, the competencies of Board‐Certified Doctors of General Medicine and Japan Primary Care Association–certified physicians explicitly include the ability to provide home medical care [23, 24]. Therefore, these physicians are considered to play an important and indispensable role in home medical care, thereby contributing to the cultivation of specific competencies related to home medical care among medical students. Second, higher scores were observed for competencies related to life processes from childhood through adulthood. Importantly, this finding reflects not only that medical students learned to provide care for a broad age range, from children to adults, but also that they acquired the perspective of situating patients' health problems within the contexts of their life cycles, life stages, and life events. This finding supports the notion that, in contrast to the fragmentation of disease‐ or age‐specific care—which represents a major challenge in aging societies [30, 31]—general/family medicine emphasizes the provision of care that is both comprehensive and continuous throughout the patient's life course [32, 33]. Third, the elevated scores for health in medical, cultural, and social contexts suggest that Board‐Certified Doctors of General Medicine and Japan Primary Care Association–certified physicians, together with the primary care they provide, are characterized by approaches that integrate biopsychosocial and cultural perspectives [23, 24]. In primary care settings, approaches informed by these perspectives are routinely required to manage problems in which biological issues such as multimorbidity, psychological conditions such as dementia and depression, and social challenges such as loneliness are intricately interwoven [34, 35]. Such settings may help medical students recognize that health is not achieved solely within the medical context but is realized through consideration of broader social and cultural contexts [36].

An analysis of regression models with and without adjustment for covariates provides a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed association between the acquisition of the competency of Generalism and the type of medical institution. The attenuation of the association after adjustment for practice setting and directors' years since graduation suggests that this association may be partly explained by these variables. However, the association remained after adjustment, indicating that the educational effect of general/family medicine institutions cannot be explained solely by these factors and may also reflect differences in educational orientation, potentially influenced by board certifications. Additionally, these variables may function not only as confounding factors but also as intermediate variables. Therefore, adjustment for these variables may have resulted in overadjustment, leading to an underestimation of the association. Nevertheless, the persistence of the association after adjustment suggests that it may have substantive meaning.

Taken together, these findings, including the persistence of the association after adjustment, highlight that community‐based clinical clerkships conducted in general/family medicine institutions provide valuable educational opportunities for cultivating the competency of Generalism. This outcome is consistent with expectations. The competency of Generalism specified in the Model Core Curriculum for Medical Education in Japan is directly aligned with the competencies required of both Board‐Certified Doctors of General Medicine and Japan Primary Care Association certified family physicians: a comprehensive and integrative approach; clinical capability in managing common health problems; patient‐centered care; collaboration‐ and coordination‐oriented management; a community‐oriented approach including community‐based integrated care; professionalism in service of the public good; and the ability to practice across diverse clinical settings [23, 24]. Through educational processes such as role modeling, these physicians have the potential to cultivate the competency of Generalism among medical students [37]. However, it may currently be difficult for community‐based clinical clerkships to be conducted solely at general/family medicine institutions, as primary care in Japan is delivered by physicians from various specialties, including internal medicine and other fields [38, 39]. Available statistics estimate that approximately 100,000 physicians—the number working in clinics—are engaged in primary care [38]. In contrast, there are only about 930 Board‐Certified Doctors of General Medicine and approximately 1200 Japan Primary Care Association certified family physicians [40, 41]. Although a certain level of specialization in primary care is expected, only a limited number of physicians have received formal training in this field. Therefore, the retraining of other physicians currently engaged in primary care delivery may be of critical importance [42].

This study has several limitations. First, the participants were limited to medical students from a single university, which may restrict the external validity of the findings. Multicenter collaborative studies are warranted to enhance generalizability. Second, due to the cross‐sectional design of this study, the analyses demonstrate associations rather than causality. The observed associations should be interpreted with caution and cannot be taken as evidence of a causal effect of the type of medical institution on the acquisition of the competency of Generalism. Third, there are some issues related to the measurement of the outcome. The acquisition of the competency of Generalism was measured using a web‐based, self‐administered questionnaire; however, its validity and reliability remain unknown. The outcomes also reflect students' perceived competency acquisition rather than objective competency development. In the absence of pre‐ and post‐measurements, it is difficult to attribute the observed differences directly to the clinical clerkship experience. More valid and reliable measures are needed to assess the actual acquisition of the competency of Generalism, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, residual confounding—unmeasured factors at both the student and medical institution levels—may have influenced the observed associations. For example, student‐level characteristics, such as prior learning experiences, baseline preparedness, and the timing and sequence of clerkships, may have influenced students' perceived competency acquisition. In addition, medical institution‐level characteristics, such as teaching culture, case mix, supervision intensity, and clerkship structure, may have varied between institutions. Moreover, the type of medical institution may function as a proxy for these unmeasured educational and environmental characteristics.

Fifth, several potential sources of bias should be considered. The valid response rate was 64.5%, and nonresponse bias may have influenced the findings. However, the potential impact of nonresponse bias could not be fully assessed, as information on differences between responders and non‐responders—particularly regarding the type of medical institution—was not available. Misclassification and within‐category heterogeneity may also have affected the results. For example, some institutions in the internal medicine category may have provided general practice/family medicine‐oriented services, whereas institutions in the other specialty category may have had a relatively broader scope of practice. These factors could have led to an underestimation of the observed associations. Because this study was conducted after all Family Medicine Clerkships had been completed, recall bias cannot be excluded. This approach was necessary to ensure voluntary participation and to maintain independence from the evaluation of the Family Medicine Clerkship; however, it may have introduced differential bias, particularly response bias, potentially eliciting more favorable responses from specific groups (e.g., general/family medicine institutions). Nevertheless, several aspects of the study design mitigate this concern: students were allocated to institutions primarily based on travel time rather than their preferences, and the type of medical institution was not explicitly disclosed to students. Overall, response patterns were considered unlikely to differ systematically across groups; therefore, the likelihood that the results were overestimated due to differential bias appears to be relatively low. Finally, as the outcomes were based on self‐assessment, they may be subject to social desirability bias and ceiling effects, potentially influencing the results. Sixth, the risk of false‐positive findings (type I error) is an important concern in studies involving multiple comparisons, [43] particularly when the number of comparisons is relatively large and the sample size is limited, as in the present study. Therefore, cautious interpretation of the findings is warranted. Seventh, in the Poisson regression with robust error variance, most categories of the explanatory variable and covariates had a reasonable number of events, but some included relatively small numbers of events, which may have limited the stability of the estimates.

In conclusion, the scores for provision of primary care according to medical resources and for primary care at home, as well as those related to life processes from childhood through adulthood, were higher among students in the community‐based clinical clerkship at general/family medicine institutions. In addition, the score for health in medical, cultural, and social contexts was higher at these institutions. Moreover, the community‐based clinical clerkship at general/family medicine institutions was associated with higher total scores for the acquisition of the competency of Generalism. Overall, these findings suggest that community‐based clinical clerkships at general/family medicine institutions may offer valuable educational opportunities for cultivating the competency of Generalism.
