Peripheral Neuropathy Induced by a Short Course of Metronidazole: A Case Report of Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Sonam Dhenga, Milan Pokhrel, Simran Rauniyar, Kundan Kumar Yadav, Sony KC, Sugam Ale Magar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetronidazole is a commonly used antibiotic with a good safety and economic profile, but it may rarely be neurotoxic, causing peripheral neuropathy and encephalopathy. Most literature suggests that prolonged drug therapy, that is, a duration of > 4 weeks and a dosage of > 42 g, is associated with neurotoxicity, though the exact dose‐dependent drug's relationship with neurotoxicity has not been discovered yet. We herein describe a case of a 41‐year‐old male diagnosed with a liver abscess and treated with a cumulative dose of 12 g of metronidazole before the onset of peripheral neuropathy. Peripheral neuropathy worsened due to the continuation of the same treatment. The possibility of metronidazole‐induced peripheral neuropathy was suspected after ruling out all the other causes. NCS suggested multiple sensorimotor demyelinating polyneuropathy in all four limbs, contrasting with most of the literature, which mentioned axonal degeneration in the case of metronidazole toxicity, but in line with some literature that has mentioned demyelination as the suspected mechanism. However, the exact mechanism of toxicity is yet to be uncovered. Improvement was noted within 5 days of stopping the drug, which further confirmed the diagnosis.Keywords: case report, metronidazole, neurotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy

Introduction

Metronidazole is a commonly used antibiotic agent in various conditions such as anaerobic bacterial infections and protozoal infections [1]. It has excellent bioavailability (> 90%) and penetration into cerebrospinal fluid and hepatic abscesses. The drug is well tolerated, with mild to moderate side effects, the most commonly reported being gastrointestinal disturbances [2, 3]. However, peripheral neuropathy is a potentially serious adverse effect associated with long‐term, moderate‐to‐high‐dose metronidazole therapy [4]. A nested population‐based case–control study done by Daneman et al. found the overall incidence of neurologic events at 100 days was 0.25% in patients receiving metronidazole [5]. Prolonged therapy regimens (> 4 weeks of treatment and/or 42 g cumulative dose) may increase the risk of developing neurological complications, in particular peripheral polyneuropathy [6]. In this case report, we describe a case of a 41‐year‐old male who developed peripheral neuropathy just after being treated with a cumulative dose of 12 g of metronidazole for liver abscess.

Case History/Examination

A 41‐year‐old male who was an occasional drinker with last intake of alcohol 3 months back, no smoking history, no reported substance use, without any comorbidities, and with no significant family history presented to the neuro medicine department with a gradually progressive symmetrical numbness up to mid forearm of the bilateral upper limb for one and a half months, which gradually progressed to involve his bilateral lower limbs. He also complained of a burning sensation in the bilateral lower limbs. He had difficulty putting on slippers, and slippers would slip occasionally. Upon asking about the past history, the patient revealed admission to the general surgery department with a diagnosis of liver abscess and being treated with metronidazole. Numbness of the bilateral upper limb started when he was given metronidazole 800 mg TDS for just 5 days. At that time, the patient complained of numbness to the doctors, and he was given methylcobalamin tablets; no investigations were pursued. The patient was asked to come for a follow‐up after 1 week for abscess drainage, which was initially not possible due to the abscess being small and multiple. He was continued on metronidazole 400 mg TDS for 4 weeks along with albendazole 400 mg BD for 4 weeks, following which the patient's numbness progressed to lower limbs and he also developed a burning sensation.

On neurological examination, the patient was well‐oriented to time, place, and person. Higher mental function and cranial nerve examination were normal. Motor examination of bilateral upper and lower limbs revealed normal bulk, tone, power (4/5), and reflex. On sensory examination, he had impaired touch and pain sensation in the bilateral upper limbs and below the knee in the bilateral lower limbs. He also had impaired vibration sense and impaired joint position sense below the knee. There were no signs of meningeal irritation and cerebellar involvement. His other system examinations were unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

With this history and examination, the patient was suspected of having peripheral neuropathy, so investigations were carried out to find its cause, and a panel including complete blood count, vitamin B12 levels, random blood sugar, and thyroid profile, serology was sent, which yielded normal results (Table 1). Nerve conduction study showed absent sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) of B/L median, ulnar, and sural sensory nerves and decreased conduction velocity of B/L tibial and left median motor nerves.

Metronidazole was discontinued as soon as it was considered the cause of peripheral neuropathy. The patient's symptoms were considerably reduced within 5 days of discontinuation of the drug. He was completely free of symptoms after 1 week, and neurological examination revealed normal results at the time of discharge. He was advised to follow up after 2 weeks, but he failed to follow up.

Patient Perspective

The patient is thankful to the neurology department for promptly diagnosing his condition. He hopes there will be better coordination between the departments in the future so that patients like him will be diagnosed and treated effectively and as early as possible.

Conclusion

Metronidazole‐induced peripheral neuropathy is a rare side effect with a controversial incidence rate. Although most of the literature has mentioned extended therapy to be the cause of peripheral neuropathy, in our patient a cumulative dose of only 12 g has led to neurotoxicity, making it more confusing whether metronidazole neurotoxicity is dose‐dependent or an idiosyncratic reaction. Moreover, in the context of the broad clinical application of this drug, its neurotoxicity may have been underestimated. We recommend that both physicians and surgeons perform a careful neurological examination and EMG study in individuals with symptoms of peripheral neuropathy during treatment with metronidazole.

Discussion

Metronidazole is a synthetic antibiotic obtained from azomycin, a nitroimidazole compound produced by Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria. It can be administered orally, intravenously, vaginally, or rectally, with oral being the most clinically used route for treating various conditions such as amoebiasis, giardiasis, trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, Helicobacter pylori , Crohn's disease, rosacea, and as a surgical prophylaxis [3]. It is normally well tolerated with mild to moderate side effects like nausea, abdominal pain, and diarrhea, but rarely, some serious neurotoxicities like optic neuropathy, peripheral neuropathy, and encephalopathy (cerebellar dysfunction, visual impairment, vestibulotoxicity, cochleotoxicity, ataxic gait, dysarthria, and seizures) have also been reported [3, 7, 8]. Although the incidence of metronidazole‐induced neurotoxicity has been estimated to be less than 1%, the exact incidence remains controversial and underestimated, as it is commonly indicated for various diseases [6, 7].

Many studies suggest the risk of peripheral neuropathy is associated with extended therapy, i.e., duration more than 4 weeks and cumulative dose greater than 42 g. A systematic review conducted by Goolsby et al. found a higher incidence of peripheral neuropathy in patients receiving > 42 g of metronidazole compared to patients getting < 42 g total (17.9% vs. 1.7%) [4]. However, it has also been reported with shorter treatments in a review of 110 cases, where the mean cumulative dose was 65.4 g with doses ranging from 5 to 2000 g, which does not support a dose‐dependent link between the drug and polyneuropathy. Additionally, individuals with concurrent kidney and liver‐related comorbid conditions, alcohol use, or those taking neurotropic medications have an increased risk of complications [3, 6, 7]. Although alcohol is also a well‐known cause of peripheral neuropathy, it is common among chronic alcohol abusers, not among occasional drinkers, as in this case [9]. This, along with relief of symptoms after metronidazole discontinuation, effectively rules out alcohol as the cause of neuropathy in our case.

The type of polyneuropathy seen with its toxicity is typically glove and stocking‐type motor and sensory neuropathy, with sensory impairment being more pronounced. Individuals present with sensory complaints like dysesthesia in the fingers and toes, and objectively, there is paresthesia or hypesthesia in those regions, which may often extend to the elbows and the knees. There is impairment of the vibration and position senses as well, but not as pronounced as the superficial senses. Motor disturbances are quite unusual compared to sensory disturbances and are typically mild when they are present [10].

The actual mechanism of metronidazole‐induced PN remains unclear. Multiple authors have identified various mechanisms associated with the neurotoxicity of metronidazole. Rao and Mason, in their study, have suggested free radical injury to the nerves as the cause of neurotoxicity [11]. In contrast, Alston suggests the formation of a thiamine analogue derived from metronidazole, causing nutritional deficiency like neuropathy [12]. Other studies propose that the union of metronidazole and its metabolites with RNA provokes the inhibition of protein synthesis and axonal degeneration of nerve fibers [3].

Nerve pathology reports of patients with metronidazole‐induced neuropathy are limited. Some studies have described severe loss of myelinated nerve fibers, mild loss of unmyelinated nerves, and axonal degeneration on electron microscopy. Nerve conduction studies have primarily indicated axonal sensorimotor neuropathy, predominantly affecting the lower limbs. However, another study showed a reduction in nerve conduction velocities and suggested the possibility of a demyelinating process [13]. NCS in our case also showed a reduction in nerve conduction velocity of the B/L tibial and left median nerve.

Electromyography (EMG) is the most effective test to determine the location, pathophysiology (axonal or demyelinating), acuity, and severity of peripheral neuropathies. Nerve conduction study (NCS) is also used by some studies to evaluate the peripheral neuropathy caused by metronidazole [14]. The diagnosis was a challenge in our case, as there was no direct clue to the condition. Only after taking the past history of treatment, we came to know about the causative agent for peripheral neuropathy.

The treatment for this condition is simply discontinuation of the drug, as metronidazole‐induced PN is considered to be reversible, although the timing for complete resolution may vary from 2 weeks to 1.5 years [6]. Our patient showed resolution of symptoms after 5 days of discontinuation of the drug.

This report highlights the rare side effect of a commonly used drug at a low dose. A demyelinating pattern in nerve conduction study is also rare in metronidazole‐induced nerve toxicity, which also makes this case rare. Additionally, it emphasizes the importance of interdepartmental collaboration for timely diagnosis and management. This case also teaches us the importance of detailed history‐taking, which was the key factor in diagnosing the patient's condition.
