“Fragmented care pathway in a layered TB care system”: a grounded theory approach to understand care pathways and delays in TB care in Odisha
Authors: Janmejaya Samal, Preetha GS, Hari Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTuberculosis is a significant public health challenge in India, accounting for one-fourth of the global burden. Complex social, economic and behavioural factors among people affected by TB, combined with health system factors, shape care pathways and contribute to delays in TB care.ObjectivesGiven this background, the study aimed to develop a constructivist grounded theory explaining how people affected by TB navigate care pathways and experience delays in two contrasting districts of Odisha.MethodsAdopting a constructivist grounded theory approach, the study was conducted in two high-burden districts of Odisha that represent demographically and geographically divergent contexts: Ganjam, a rural non-tribal district, and Mayurbhanj, a tribal-predominant district. The study participants included people affected by TB and their caregivers, as well as healthcare professionals involved in TB care. Sampling followed an initial purposive technique, with the final sample size guided by theoretical saturation. Data were collected through in-depth interviews, and analysis was iterative through constant comparison, developing co-constructed emergent categories and an explanatory framework.ResultsThe main explanatory framework, “Fragmented care pathways in a layered TB care system”, explains how people affected by TB navigated a wide range of providers and experienced delays before their final entry into the TB programme. Introduction

Tuberculosis is a formidable public health crisis, and India contributes one-fourth of the global burden. The World Health Organisation (WHO) reports an incidence of 131 and 187 cases per 100,000 population globally and in India, respectively, in 2024 [1, 2]. India’s effort to combat TB began in 1962 with the National TB Control Programme (NTCP), which has since been renamed as the National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP) as of January 1, 2020 [3]. Over the years, a key gap in the TB care cascade has been “missing millions”, with 4 in 10 cases worldwide contracting TB and missing diagnosis, treatment, and reporting under national reporting systems, as reported in the Global TB burden report-2019 [4]. The 2025 report suggests that 2.2 cases per 10 are missed [1].

The “care cascade” framework is used to understand the absolute number of people affected by TB who complete each stage of care, helping to assess the effectiveness of the health system [5–9]. The “care cascade” is a complex process that outlines how people with presumptive TB assess their health status and navigate diagnostic and treatment facilities based on their physical and financial accessibility [10]. Delays within the care cascade are categorised into three types: consultation delay refers to the time between symptom onset and consultation; diagnosis delay refers to the time between consultation and diagnosis; and treatment delay refers to the time between diagnosis and treatment initiation. The consultation delay is also called the patient delay or demand-side delay, and the diagnosis and treatment delays together are called the health system delay or supply-side delay. Together, they constitute the total delay in TB care [11]. Several studies have reported the duration of delays in TB care. A scoping review across the top 10 high-burden countries in the world reported a median total delay of 170.6 days in Myanmar, whereas the same ranged between 8 and 111 days for India and China [12]. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 124 articles estimated pooled patient delays of 28 and 10 days, health system delays of 14 and 4 days, and treatment delays of 14 and 0 days for low- and lower-middle-income countries and upper-middle-income countries, respectively [13].

Delayed diagnosis and treatment can adversely affect not only patients but also the community, as delays can perpetuate TB transmission and significantly impede ongoing efforts towards TB elimination [12, 14]. Thus, it is essential to identify presumptive TB cases as early as possible at the first point of contact and connect them with the most effective diagnostic and treatment facilities available to break the chain of transmission [3]. This is possible through improved case detection, which ensures early and accurate diagnosis. At the same time, understanding the care cascade and delays in TB care requires a contextual understanding of both the supply-side and demand-side aspects of the health system. Given this background, the study aimed to develop a constructivist grounded theory explaining how people affected by TB navigate the care pathways and experience delays in two contrasting districts of Odisha.

TB situation in Odisha and the rationale of this study

Odisha is more favourably situated in the country concerning NTEP performance metrics. It ranks fourth in overall performance indicators with a score of 93.45 out of 100; nevertheless, it is the second-best performing state in the population category over 5 million, following Himachal Pradesh. In 2023, a total of 62,387 tuberculosis cases were reported, representing 96% of the expected 65,000 notifications [2]. Despite the favourable positioning of the state, there is evidence of delays in TB care [15–17], and the underlying demand-side and supply-side factors governing the delays have not been thoroughly understood qualitatively. Additionally, there is a paucity of literature that utilises a grounded theory approach to explore the TB care cascade both in Odisha and in the larger Indian context. The existing literature poorly captures the unique socio-cultural context and the lived experiences of people affected by TB in this region. The current study uses a grounded theory approach to bridge this gap, which can generate a context-specific explanatory framework to help policy actors and program managers improve the care pathways and reduce delays in TB care.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to develop a constructivist grounded theory explaining how people affected by TB navigate the care pathways and experience delays in two contrasting districts of Odisha.

Methods

Operational definition of delays in TB care

Globally, there is no acceptable timeframe of delays in TB care [18]; however, the WHO recommends the diagnosis within 2–3 weeks of symptom initiation [19]. For this study, we have labelled delayed TB care if it was more than 28 days (4 weeks) from symptom onset to treatment initiation.

Design

The study adopted a qualitative research design using a constructivist grounded theory approach proposed by Kathy Charmaz [20]. This approach helps explore complex social phenomena with the generation of a theoretical framework based on patient experiences.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the state of Odisha across two contrasting districts that represent demographically and geographically divergent contexts, in which Ganjam is a rural non-tribal district, and Mayurbhanj is a tribal-predominant district (Figure 1). Ganjam: Ganjam is situated in the southern part of the state of Odisha and is the most populous district of the state as per the 2011 census, with a population of 3,529,031. Ganjam, although rapidly urbanizing however predominantly a rural district which serves as a healthcare hub for southern Odisha [21]. In one of the studies, the clinical epidemiological assessment suggests that MDR-TB affects the most productive population in the district and rural habitation, labour work, and the poor socio-economic status are the associated factors [22]. Mayurbhanj: A tribal-predominant and the largest district in Odisha, with a population of 2,519,738, situated in the northern part of the state. Evidence suggests that ignorance about TB symptoms, addiction to alcoholic drinks, difficulty reaching the health facilities owing to the long distances, lack of communication, and transport were reported factors that affect the health-seeking behaviour of the tribal population in Mayurbhanj [23]. Additionally, both districts have a higher incidence of TB than the national average [24].

Sampling logic

The sampling for this study was carried out in two distinct phases in alignment with the iterative nature of the constructivist grounded theory. An initial purposive sampling strategy with maximum variation was adopted to recruit a demographically and clinically diverse group of people affected by TB (Pulmonary TB cases, Extra-pulmonary TB cases, Drug sensitive TB cases, drug resistant TB cases, both genders, and paediatric and adult TB cases) who were currently undergoing treatment or had completed treatment recently (within a month). As the initial categories emerged through constant comparison, the sampling transitioned to theoretical sampling and subsequent sampling was purely conceptual to elaborate, refine and saturate the emergent categories.

Saturation horizon

For this study, the sample size was not predetermined, but rather dynamically guided by theoretical saturation. The data collection and analysis were carried out simultaneously, facilitating the emergence of categories during the early phase of data collection and further informing participant selection. Theoretical saturation was determined at a point when no new information and nuances were obtained within the established categories and the conceptual relationships between these categories [25].

Study tool

A set of in-depth interview guides was used for different types of study participants.

All the guides were developed in English, and the guides for the people affected by TB and the ASHAs were translated into Odia; the rest were kept in English. For the validation of the tools, pilot testing and expert review were followed [Supplementary File] [26].

Data collection

The primary method of data collection was face-to-face in-depth interviews using the in-depth interview guides. This was conducted to capture the lived experiences of TB patients regarding their care pathways and delays. The interviews were carried out by the first author, with all interviews conducted in the houses of the patients, caregivers, and ASHAs in Ganjam District. However, a few patient and ASHA interviews took place in healthcare settings in Mayurbhanj District. The selection of houses of the participants to conduct the interviews helped to capture their experiences with comfort and privacy. The interviews with the CHOs, STSs, and District Project Coordinators were conducted in official settings. Similarly, the interviews with the state TB officer and the WHO-HQ consultant were conducted in the state TB Cell, Odisha. Community-level and district-level development sector representatives were interviewed in official settings as well; however, the state-level development sector representative participated virtually due to his unavailability on the day of the state-level interviews. Patient interviews lasted between 30 and 40 min, ASHA interviews lasted 20–30 min, and interviews with the STSs, CHOs, DPCs, and district-level development sector representatives lasted 30–40 min. The state-level interviews lasted between 40 and 60 min for all participants. Except for the state-level interviews, all interviews were conducted in Odia, which is also the mother tongue of the first author; the state-level interviews were conducted in English. A semi-structured interview guide, aligned with the study objectives, was used to conduct the interviews. Guided by the Constructivist Grounded Theory framework, the guide was adapted to include probing questions based on categories that emerged from earlier interviews. All interviews were audio-recorded using a mobile-based device and transcribed verbatim [27]. The concept of data saturation [25] determined the number of interviews with TB patients, ASHAs, caregivers, and STSs. The first author, being a native Odia speaker, translated and transcribed the Odia interviews into English. All audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored on a password-protected computer.

Data analysis

The in-depth interviews were transcribed verbatim in English and coded. The first author was involved in transcription, and both the first and second authors were involved in coding. As per the principles of the Constructivist Grounded Theory, the data collection and analysis were simultaneous. During this iterative process, the first author wrote the memos and maintained field notes during each interview to keep track of the conceptual developments [28]. The analysis followed Charmaz’s Constructivist Grounded Theory Approach [29]. The analysis was carried out by reading the transcripts multiple times, and coding was done following a coding process proposed by Charmaz: initial coding, focused coding, which systematically constructed emergent categories, which led to theoretical integration.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity is extremely critical in this research owing to the positionality of the first author (JS) as a Team Lead for a State Technical Support Unit (STSU) that was formed to provide technical support to the State TB cell of the state, nearly two years before the time of data collection. This provided a theoretical sensitivity of the TB care system in the state. Due to this, it was important that the “insider” status of the researcher required the exercise of cautious reflexivity to make sure that the emergent categories are deeply grounded in the lived experiences of the people affected by TB and their caregivers, rather than the researcher’s previous professional assumptions. To effectively achieve this, the researcher wrote memos and constantly compared the categories to examine the influence of personal bias. The researcher tried not to keep aside and decouple his clinical and programmatic expertise completely, but rather reflexively leveraged this background as an analytical resource while consciously keeping himself open to the layers of the co-constructed narratives of the study participants [30–33].

Ethical approval

Institutional ethical committee approval was obtained from the International Institute of Health Management Research, Delhi, to conduct this study [IRBFPM007]. The researchers further followed the “Declaration of Helsinki” to maintain the confidentiality and integrity of the study participants while closely adhering to survey ethics. To maintain the confidentiality of patients’ personal information, identifiers such as names, phone numbers, and identification numbers were omitted. The data extraction sheet, which contained sensitive material, was secured with a passcode accessible exclusively to the investigators.

Results

A total of 24 people affected by TB, five caregivers, 11 ASHAs, two each Senior treatment Supervisors and Community Health Officers, two district project officers, state TB officer, one WHO-Technical consultant and three development sector representatives participated with a cumulative number of 51 study participants. The results avoided presenting the time interval of delays, given the small purposive sample size; instead, the results were presented based on the participants’ narratives, which revealed that care pathways and delays in TB care are dynamic and are socially constructed, shaped by factors related to the supply side and demand side of the health system. The demand side refers to the personal, sociocultural and economic factors related to the people affected by TB, and the supply side refers to the health service delivery, provider behaviour and the health system factors in general that affect the care pathways and delays in TB care. This concept stems from the idea that care pathways and delays in TB care are not solely the result of “individual behaviour” or the “health system behaviour” in isolation, but rather an interaction between the two and the results section reflects the same.

Circuitous entry

The category “Circuitous Entry” refers to the long-winded entry of patients to the TB care program. This is primarily related to patient-side or consultation delays. At the onset of symptoms, patients usually do not seek care within the formal TB care system for an appropriate diagnosis and treatment; instead, they undergo a lengthy process of care-seeking with informal providers, including private providers, where symptoms are normalised and treated as minor illnesses, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment. Discussion

The study focused on care pathways and delays in TB care among people affected by TB in two districts of Odisha and found that the care pathways are non-linear, long-winded, and the patients navigated through multiple layers of the health system before the final diagnosis.

This study documented patients’ lived experiences of navigating a long, winding pathway through a fragmented network of multiple providers, which mirrors patients’ experiences reported in other geographies. A study examining diagnostic and treatment pathways in a South Indian state reported that, in search of relief, patients drifted from one provider to another [34]. The caregiver of a paediatric TB case narrated how the initial diagnosis was missed and later found to be TB in a five-year-old girl [35]. Studies report being advised to move from one hospital department to another before a final recommendation for TB diagnosis [36, 37]. This study identified fragmented pathways in which multiple providers/facilities were consulted before the final diagnosis. The study found a predominance of care-seeking with faith healers in Mayurbhanj district among the tribal population, in contrast to patients in Ganjam, who primarily sought care from quacks/informal providers, private practitioners, or purchased medicines over-the-counter. Studies report that seeking care with faith healers and misconceptions about the spiritual cause of TB delay effective treatment among the tribal population [38]. Furthermore, the tribal populations frequently encounter systemic barriers to healthcare access, such as geographical remoteness, inadequate transportation alternatives, neglect from the healthcare delivery system, physician absenteeism, cultural and linguistic disparities, and belief systems that foster distrust in the healthcare system. This ultimately leads to elevated prevalence and mortality rates in these populations [39]. Over-the-counter purchases have been reported as an important determinant of delayed TB care in India. Although private pharmacies are positioned to significantly contribute to case detection and referral, inadequate management frequently leads to delayed diagnosis and TB-specific treatments [40, 41]. Earlier studies in India have indicated that private pharmacies frequently administer cough syrups, bronchodilators, antihistamines, and antibiotics over-the-counter (OTC), instead of referring patients to the suitable provider for TB diagnosis and treatment [42–44]. Self-medication and inadequate referral processes can delay the diagnosis of TB. Private pharmacies that provide incorrect medications to TB patients may further exacerbate the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. Private pharmacies have been recognised as essential stakeholders in the control of both TB and antimicrobial resistance [45]. In the fragmented TB care system, the role of private practitioners, both formal and informal providers, is very significant. Several factors cause delays in the diagnosis and treatment of TB patients owing to the behaviour of private practitioners. Studies report that they rarely recommend TB-specific microbiological tests, such as smear microscopy and culture, particularly during the initial consultation, causing delays in diagnosis [42, 46]. Rather, these providers most commonly depend on empirical treatment regimens, prescribing broad-spectrum antibiotics and fluoroquinolones, and thereafter doing chest X-rays and blood tests if the patient’s symptoms fail to ameliorate [47, 48]. As a result, when patients who remain undiagnosed or fail to show improvement in their condition often change healthcare providers, a behaviour colloquially termed “provider shopping.” This cycle is likely to persist until the patient receives an accurate diagnosis and commences effective treatment [7, 49–53]. Given this situation, private providers must adhere to standard treatment guidelines, mandatorily notify TB cases, and support the ban on Sero-diagnosis for confirmatory TB diagnosis, among other obligations of private practitioners towards patients [54, 55].

The study found several patient-related factors that delayed TB care, consistent with other studies. Patients perceived symptoms as seasonal [8], often misattributing early signs such as prolonged cough, fever, fatigue, or weight loss to seasonal problems [36]. They started self-medication and visited multiple providers, causing delays [8]. Cough is one of the most commonly reported symptoms of TB, and its absence can rule out TB [56]. Studies report that the main patient-side delay stems from misconceptions about the disease and cough. Many believe it is due to eating certain foods, such as rice, banana, and guava, or to bad weather [57], and many perceive it as a minor respiratory illness [36]. Furthermore, TB usually presents with non-specific symptoms, which in many cases are mistaken for other febrile illnesses, especially during the initial phases of the disease. Patients often continue with their daily activities, delaying care seeking; however, this poses a great risk, as those who come into contact with them may become infected with TB [58, 59]. For many, TB is believed to be caused by witchcraft, evil-related causes, or an attack of the spirit [60]. Beliefs and myths about disease causation and related health-seeking influence patients’ perceptions of susceptibility and severity of the disease, leading to delayed care seeking [60]. Addressing these through culturally sensitive health education to improve behaviour is important [61, 62], a point reinforced by the health belief model, which suggests that perceptions of susceptibility, severity, benefits of action, and barriers to action influence the individual’s health behaviour [63]. Lack of money for transport [52], out-of-pocket payments for consultations and tests [64], borrowing money, avoiding wage loss, and shifting to the public sector after economic distress caused delays in TB care [7, 52, 65–70]. An estimated 26.7% of hospitalised cases and 3.5% of patients utilising outpatient care experienced financial hardship in India due to TB [71]. Stigma has been documented as a significant predictor of TB in India [72], and it becomes more complex if the patient is a woman in India [73]. There are several typologies of stigma; however, in the context of TB, four types have been documented: anticipated, perceived, enacted and internalised [74]. Nonetheless, stigma has been a pervasive challenge in TB care. The concealment of illness, fear of rejection, and social separation by family members exacerbated psychological distress and delayed help-seeking, reflecting prevalent evidence from India and other low- and middle-income countries [72, 75]. Given this background, it is important to work on strategies to reduce stigma in communities, such as prioritising privacy-promoting techniques, reinforcing ethics education for Community Health Workers, and expanding patient rights education, thereby reducing delays in help-seeking and improving treatment outcomes [76].

The study identified several system-related, supply-side factors consistent with other studies, in which providers masked the disease due to misdiagnosis [34], non-specific tests [8], and the prescription of cough syrups [36]. Misdiagnosis of TB has been the most common factor in delayed TB care among patients. TB cases are often misdiagnosed as other diseases, such as typhoid, malaria, pneumonia, and viral infections, without TB being suspected [57]. Additionally, variable provider competencies lead to delays before final diagnosis [8, 77]. Prompt TB diagnosis is crucial to prevent further transmission and adverse clinical outcomes, and to minimise misdiagnosis of TB in the diagnostic pathway as we progress towards TB elimination [78]. As in this study, fragmented TB care has been identified as a significant factor in delayed TB care by several studies [8, 36]. Patients drift from provider to provider and navigate many touchpoints, reflecting a fragmented journey that can delay diagnosis and treatment [34, 79]. It is thus imperative that engaging one set of providers, for instance, the private providers, is not enough to meet the elimination targets; maximising the quality and coordination of basic TB services across India’s vast and fragmented healthcare system will be the key to elimination [80] Prolonged and multiple private care before referral, delayed diagnosis of EP-TB cases, recommending a TB test after symptoms worsen, and exhaustion of treatment options before the final diagnosis create a trajectory of delayed TB care [56]. This situation requires intervention on both sides of the health system, with improved patient knowledge about the disease and its related services, and, on the providers’ side, enhanced capacity to promptly suspect chest symptoms and refer for appropriate TB testing, which will reduce the delay in diagnosis [64]. We found that ASHAs act as confidants for TB patients, protecting their dignity, helping them manage TB stigma, and facilitating sputum examination and treatment completion as an integral part of the same community [81, 82]. Engaging ASHAs in TB care is paramount, as they can help reach the unreached within the same community [81, 83]. The beneficial impacts of involving Community Health Workers (CHWs) across the continuum of TB care are extensively documented in India, including improvements in treatment outcomes, increased case notifications, and reduced loss to follow-up [84–88].

Our study observed fairly divergent views on the care cascade regarding patients’ opinions and experiences, as well as healthcare providers’ perspectives on certain aspects such as stigma, delays, and seeking care from informal providers and traditional healers. We found that most ASHAs, when directly asked about the prevalence of stigma, denied it, stating that such practices were common in the past but are no longer prevalent. However, while recounting their experiences with patients, many of them inadvertently mentioned numerous encounters with stigma. Simultaneously, programme officials and intermediaries at district and state levels noted that, through the availability of healthcare facilities like portable digital X-rays and Truenat facilities, and through several rounds of active case finding, delays have been reduced. Nevertheless, on the patient side, there remains a significant awareness gap about the disease and available care options, leading many to seek treatment from multiple providers. The officials also remarked that the utilisation of healthcare services with traditional and informal providers has decreased considerably; however, in both districts, Mayurbhanj, with its traditional healers and Ganjam, with informal providers and quacks, such practices are still present, as narrated by the patients. We believe these differing views primarily stem from the contrasting orientations of the two sides: while the health system emphasises diagnostics, treatment capacities, and reporting, society perceives the issue through socio-economic and stigma lenses.

Implications of the study

Our study offers actionable insights that can improve service delivery for TB care in the regional context of Odisha. We suggest moving away from a standardised, “one-size-fits-all” strategy towards culturally tailored interventions that account for the divergent realities of rural Ganjam and tribal Mayurbhanj. TB elimination efforts in the state should address the identified demand-side factors and design health education and community engagement activities to address belief-mediated care and mitigate the stigma that drives the circuitous care-seeking behaviour of people affected by TB. At the same time, the programme must train and sensitise a wide range of care providers and effectively engage them, who act as the first point of care in the community for a majority of people affected by TB. This targeted sensitisation must focus on early symptom identification and rapid referral to avert provider-side delays and reduce the trajectory of delayed care before the final entry into the programme.

Limitations of this study

Although this is the first grounded theory study to explore care pathways and delays in TB care in an Indian setting, several limitations must be taken into account when interpreting the findings. The geographic and contextual limitations of choosing two contrasting districts restrict the generalisability of the study findings to other geographies of the state, as well as to other Indian settings with diverse health facilities, socio-cultural-economic and demographic realities. Furthermore, as the study captured lived experiences based on patient narratives, which are subject to recall bias, people affected by TB wove stories from their past about their interactions with healthcare providers and facilities, which could introduce errors about the exact sequence and duration of events. Most importantly, while our qualitative study using the grounded theory approach explained the structural mechanics and socio-cultural-economic processes behind the fragmented care, its design limits the quantification of the duration of delays and the estimation of the prevalence and financial burden on each person affected by TB in their care pathways and delays in care.

Conclusions

Our study indicates that the delays in TB care are not isolated but embedded within the trajectory of delayed care, influenced by both the supply-side and demand-side factors of the health system. On the demand side, the decisions of seeking care are mediated by awareness, stigma, belief system and financial constraints of the individual patients and their caregivers. Our study indicated that stigma played a critical role in the decisions whether to seek care or not, despite the continuous efforts of the health system to normalise TB as a treatable condition. At the same time, the financial constraints often lead the patients to take low-cost, less efficient, easily accessible care, even if these efforts are ineffective. Similarly, the supply-side factors, such as disease masking by providers and fragmented care, cause substantial delays in TB care. Lack of coordination between different levels of care and a poor referral mechanism further accentuated diagnostic delays. Our study uncovered a divergent view on delays and care pathways among the TB patients, where the program officers opined that there has been a substantial improvement in diagnostic capacity and reduction in stigma; however, on the patient side, the narrative is opposite, where there is pervasive stigma, and the diagnostic pathway is fragmented. This demands the incorporation of patient narratives into program design and implementation. Our study emphasises that TB elimination efforts should not be limited to the silos of medicine and biology but require a paradigm shift in their approach to a more integrated and patient-centric approach. Positioning the patient experiences as the primary theme of our study provides actionable insights to bridge the gap in the TB care cascade. Aligning the supply side with the demand side, addressing the fragmented care and contextualising the program interventions embedded with local realities can reduce delays and improve care pathways and TB outcomes.
