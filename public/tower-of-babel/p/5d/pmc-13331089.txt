Background

Location

A hernia is a bulge where something from inside your belly pushes through a small hole in the muscles that make up the wall of your belly (“abdominal wall”). An “inguinal hernia” is the name for a hernia in your groin, or the crease where the leg meets the body where the abdominal wall can become weak. Fat or intestine can push through the weak spot in these muscles, causing a bulge (figure 1).

Function

Hernias are very common and can happen suddenly or over a long time. Factors that may increase the risk of hernia are things like a heavy cough or weight lifting (anything that puts strain on the belly). The risk of hernia also goes up with age as this wall weakens over time.

Symptoms

You may realize you have an inguinal hernia if you see a bulge or have pain in your groin. These changes that you see and think are called symptoms and they may get worse if you strain to poop, stand for a long time, or cough. They may get better or go away when you lie down. Many hernias do not cause any other problems and are not an emergency. Hernias can be “reducible,” meaning the hernia can be pushed back into place, or “non-reducible,” when the bulge cannot be pushed back in. The intestines can become stuck in the hernia which can cause pain, an inability to poop or pass gas, nausea/vomiting, fever, and changes in skin color.

What happens in the hospital?

Diagnosis

The doctor will look at your belly and groins and may ask you to sit, stand, or cough to help them find a hernia. When a patient comes to the hospital with a hernia that is painful and not able to be “popped back in,” the doctor might try to push it back using ice, pain medication, or a muscle relaxant. If a patient is throwing up, the doctor may place a tube through the nose to drain the liquid stuck inside the stomach to stop throwing up and reduce the risk of vomit going into the lungs.

You may need a picture taken of your belly with a test such as an ultrasound or a CT scan. This helps the doctor see if your intestine is trapped inside of your hernia. You may also need a blood test to see if you have an infection. This can happen if your intestine is stuck in your hernia. If you already had a hernia surgery and think your hernia might have come back, you may need to go through these tests again.

Medical (non-surgical) options

It is safer to fix your hernia with a planned surgery at a later time rather than right away as an emergency. It may need to be fixed as an emergency if it is painful, trapping your intestine, making it hard to poop, or causing other issues. Some patients who do not need or want surgery wear a hernia belt (figure 2), which can hold the hernia in place during normal activities.

If you are being seen for a hernia that has come back after surgery, your treatment options will most likely be the same as before. Losing weight and going to physical therapy can also help you manage your hernia and avoid another surgery.

Surgical options

First-time hernia surgery

If there is intestine inside a hernia and it cannot be pushed back, or if it is causing pain or skin color changes, you will need surgery so that bacteria do not travel throughout the body, which can make you very sick. The doctor will evaluate each patient’s risk based on their other medical problems to see if surgery is appropriate, and when to do it. Open surgery to fix an inguinal hernia starts with a 5-10 centimeter cut in the groin crease so the doctor can fully see and repair the hernia. This method is often used when your doctor is worried about trapped intestine.

Your hernia might also be fixed with “laparoscopic” surgery which uses three or four small cuts (<2 centimeters) rather than one bigger cut. The surgeon puts a camera through one of these cuts and fixes your hernia from the inside. The surgeon may put a piece of mesh inside to fix the hernia and to support the weakened area of the belly wall, reducing the risk of this coming back. Recently, there has been a rise in robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery to fix hernias, which also uses a camera and other small cuts, but the surgeon controls the robot tools instead. Recovery is usually shorter when hernias are repaired with these smaller cuts.

Repeat hernia after a prior surgery

Recurrent hernias will usually be fixed by cutting a patient open if the patient previously had the hernia fixed with cameras and small incisions before. Using the camera approach might be better for a recurrent hernia if the patient had it fixed by cutting them open before.

Care after treatment

After hernia surgery, the goal is to return to normal activity as much as possible without pain. However, patients should not lift more than 5 kilograms for 1 month after having a hernia surgery to allow time to heal and prevent it from “popping out” again. Light exercise, such as taking walks, is helpful in the first few weeks after surgery. You can go back to normal exercise by 1 month after surgery.

Most patients who have preplanned hernia surgery can go home the same day as their surgery. If your hernia was fixed as an emergency, you will probably stay in the hospital for a few days. This will depend on whether your intestines were trapped in your hernia and had to be freed during surgery.

What happens next?

Discharge location

You can usually go home a few hours after a hernia surgery. Sometimes you might need therapy to help move around again, or if you are weak or have other medical problems, you might need rehab or a nursing home to get stronger before you go home. This is rare in people without serious medical problems, though.

Wound care

If you had surgery, you’ll need to keep the area clean and dry. Let warm water and soap gently run over the surgical site. Do not scrub the surgery areas, but it is important that you take a daily shower. You cannot take a bath or go into a pool for 2 weeks after surgery. If your cuts open up, smell bad, turn red, or if you get a fever, you should call your doctor as soon as possible.

Diet

A hernia does not require a special diet, but a diet high in protein and low in sugar, especially if the patient has diabetes, helps with wound healing. A diet high in fiber and fluids (like water or juices) is helpful if the patient is taking narcotic pain medications after surgery, because these can make it hard to poop. Do not smoke after a hernia surgery because it can stop your cut from healing, make you sick, and maybe even break down your repair, bringing back your hernia.

Activity

Do not lift anything more than 5 kilograms for 1 month after surgery. This helps to make sure your hernia does not pop back out. If you have a tough and physical job, you may not be able to work again for up to 6 weeks. Light exercise like walking is a good idea and helps you heal.
