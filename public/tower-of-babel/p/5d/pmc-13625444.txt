Effects of opioid-free anesthesia on postoperative anxiety in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy for breast cancer: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Cong Deng, Xianming Wen, Sisi Liu, Feng Lu, Weidong Liang, Fuzhou Hua, Junming Ye, Maolin Zhong, Shihong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioid-free anesthesia (OFA) improves somatic recovery after breast cancer surgery, but its effect on postoperative anxiety has not been evaluated. We hypothesized that OFA would reduce postoperative state anxiety in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy.MethodsIn this single-center, randomized, participant- and assessor-blinded trial, 106 patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy were assigned to OFA (n = 53) or opioid-based anesthesia (OA; n = 53). Both groups received a serratus anterior plane block. The OFA group received dexmedetomidine and esketamine intraoperatively and esketamine-based postoperative patient-controlled analgesia; the OA group received intraoperative opioids and sufentanil-based postoperative patient-controlled analgesia.The primary outcome was the State Anxiety Inventory (SAI) score 24 h after surgery. Secondary outcomes were exploratory.ResultsPreoperative SAI scores were comparable between groups. Postoperative SAI scores were significantly lower with OFA at 24 hours (median, 41 vs. 48; P < 0.001) and 72 hours (41.43 ± 4.53 vs. 45.55 ± 4.37; P < 0.001). The QoR-15 score at 24 hours was higher with OFA (median, 104 vs. 99; P < 0.001). NRS pain scores showed a modest rightward distributional shift with OFA at postoperative 24 hours (median, 3[3–4] vs. 3[3–3]; P = 0.014) but were significantly lower at postoperative 72 hours (median, 1 vs. 2; P < 0.001). The incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was lower with OFA (13.21% vs. 32.08%; P = 0.020). Time to tracheal extubation and PACU length of stay were shorter with OFA (both P < 0.001). Intraoperative hemodynamic parameters and other adverse event rates did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsThe evaluated multicomponent opioid-free perioperative regimen reduced early postoperative state-anxiety scores compared with the opioid-based regimen. Because intraoperative drugs and postoperative analgesia differed between groups, the effects of opioid avoidance cannot be disentangled from the direct effects of dexmedetomidine, esketamine, or postoperative PCIA. The clinical importance and longer-term durability of the observed anxiety difference require confirmation.Trial registrationChiCTR2500101756, 04/29/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04069-3.Keywords: Opioid-free anesthesia, Postoperative anxiety, State anxiety inventory, Modified radical mastectomy, Breast cancer, Quality of recovery

Introduction

Perioperative medicine has undergone a fundamental paradigm shift in how postoperative recovery is defined and measured. Traditionally, surgical success was evaluated using objective clinical endpoints such as complication rates, readmission rates, and hospital length of stay [1]. Although these metrics remain important, they reflect only the somatic dimension of recovery and do not capture the patient’s subjective experience [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, patient-reported outcomes have gained recognition as indispensable endpoints in perioperative research and clinical practice [2, 3]. The 15-item Quality of Recovery scale (QoR-15), a validated short-form instrument derived from the QoR-40, evaluates five domains of postoperative health status: physical comfort, emotional state, psychological support, physical independence, and pain [4]. The European Society of Anaesthesiology–European Society of Intensive Care Medicine joint task force has recommended the QoR-15 as the standardized outcome measure for quality of recovery in clinical trials [5]. In parallel, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols seek to optimize perioperative management through multimodal, evidence-based strategies, including preoperative patient education, nutritional support, precision anesthesia, refined surgical techniques, early mobilization, and effective pain management [6]. Despite these comprehensive frameworks, the psychological dimension of recovery particularly emotional well-being, remains systematically underrepresented in both ERAS implementation and perioperative outcome reporting.

Breast cancer diagnosis and treatment impose substantial psychological distress, with anxiety being one of the most prevalent manifestations [7, 8]. The perioperative trajectory of anxiety in breast cancer surgery is complex and variable [9]. In a prospective longitudinal study of 411 patients with nonmetastatic breast cancer, 44.5% exhibited abnormal anxiety preoperatively; although the proportion declined after surgery, anxiety re-emerged upon completion of adjuvant therapies, and persistent anxiety was significantly associated with pain, breast, and arm symptoms [7]. When such distress persists, it may impair quality of life, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes [7, 8]. Meta-analytic data indicate that the prevalence of anxiety among breast cancer patients is 41.9% [10], and both depression and anxiety have been independently associated with increased breast cancer mortality [11]. Moreover, moderate levels of state anxiety can persist for up to six months after breast cancer surgery [12], and high trait anxiety increases the risk of poor quality of life one year after treatment by sevenfold [13]. Despite the magnitude and clinical consequences of this problem, current perioperative intervention strategies including the selection of anesthetic technique remain largely unexplored as potential modulators of postoperative anxiety in this population.

Anesthetic protocols influence postoperative recovery not only through analgesic and anti-inflammatory mechanisms but also through the psychotropic properties of their pharmacological constituents. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist commonly incorporated into multimodal anesthesia, possesses well-characterized sedative, anxiolytic, and sympatholytic properties [14]. Recent preclinical evidence indicates that dexmedetomidine produces prolonged anxiolytic effects through suppression of hyperactivity in noradrenergic neurons of the locus coeruleus [15]. Esketamine, increasingly recognized for its rapid antidepressant effects, is another pharmacological component of opioid-free regimens with potential implications for perioperative psychological well-being [16]. Conversely, opioids may induce dysphoria, hyperalgesia, and emotional disturbance that adversely affect the postoperative psychological state [17]. Despite these pharmacologically plausible pathways, the impact of anesthetic protocol composition on postoperative psychological outcomes has received minimal systematic investigation.

Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) has emerged as a promising multimodal alternative to conventional opioid-based regimens for breast cancer surgery. More broadly, multimodal analgesic strategies combine nonopioid pharmacological agents, including α₂ adrenergic agonists and ketamine, with regional anesthetic techniques to maintain perioperative analgesia while reducing opioid exposure and its associated risks [18]. In modified radical mastectomy, pectoral nerve (PECS) block-based OFA provides superior analgesia, reflected by lower visual analog scale scores and reduced supplemental analgesic requirements, alongside decreased postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and shortened postanesthesia care unit (PACU) stay [19, 20]. A recent randomized controlled trial demonstrated that OFA utilizing esketamine combined with interpectoral plane and pectoralis-serratus plane blocks significantly enhanced postoperative recovery quality at both 24 and 48 h compared with conventional opioid-based anesthesia (OA), mitigating opioid-related adverse effects and offering a viable alternative within ERAS protocols for breast cancer surgery [21]. Additional studies have confirmed the feasibility and safety of opioid-free techniques for mastectomy [22, 23]. A critical limitation, however, pervades this evidence base: existing OFA trials have focused exclusively on somatic recovery parameters, pain, PONV, physical function, and hemodynamic stability, without incorporating postoperative anxiety as a primary or secondary outcome.

Within the multidimensional framework for perioperative recovery assessment, a significant evidence gap therefore persists. Current OFA research has addressed the physical dimensions of recovery in breast cancer surgery but has not systematically evaluated postoperative anxiety, assessed by standardized instruments as a primary endpoint. This omission is particularly consequential in modified radical mastectomy; a procedure associated with an exceptionally high psychological burden. The pharmacological rationale for OFA-mediated anxiety reduction is compelling: dexmedetomidine exerts anxiolytic effects through central α₂-adrenergic mechanisms, while elimination of intraoperative opioids removes their potential dysphoric and mood-altering actions. This mechanistic plausibility, however, lacks clinical validation. The present randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate the effect of OFA on postoperative anxiety in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy for breast cancer, with the postoperative State Anxiety Inventory (SAI) score as the primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints include analgesic efficacy, quality of recovery, and hemodynamic stability, thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of OFA across both the somatic and psychological dimensions of perioperative recovery.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This randomized, controlled, participant- and assessor-blinded, single-center clinical trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University. The protocol adhered to relevant international guideline standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (Approval No. LLSC-2025-066). The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2500101756) and filed in the Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System. All eligible patients scheduled for elective breast surgery were fully informed about the study before enrollment and provided written informed consent. This trial was reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 statement [24]. The completed CONSORT 2025 checklist is available in the Supplementary Materials.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were: women aged 18–65 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; pathologically confirmed breast cancer scheduled for elective modified radical mastectomy under general anesthesia; and no participation in other clinical trials within three months prior enrollment. Exclusion criteria were: allergy to any study drug component; preoperative opioid dependence; comorbid psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, or language/communication disorders that precluded postoperative anxiety assessment; chronic alcohol use or chronic use of psychotropic medications; and pregnancy or lactation.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to two groups (OFA and OA) using a random-number table implemented via an online randomization tool in a prospective, randomized, participant- and assessor-blinded, controlled design. A staff member not involved in study conduct or data collection generated a computer-based randomization sequence and allocated participants in a 1:1 ratio to the OFA or OA group. The allocation sequence was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. On the day of surgery, a staff member dedicated to drug preparation (with no involvement in subsequent anesthesia or data recording) opened the envelopes in order and prepared the study medications according to group assignment. All medications were placed in identical, opaque syringes or infusion bags labeled only with the randomization number and delivered to the operating room. An experienced anesthesiologist, blinded to allocation, conducted anesthesia and all intraoperative drug administration and monitoring. Participants, data collectors, and the researchers responsible for postoperative follow-up and statistical analysis were also blinded to group assignment. Unblinding was performed only after completion of all data analyses to ensure objectivity and reliability of the results.

Study procedures and interventions

Preoperatively, an anesthesiologist visited the breast diagnosis and treatment center ward to perform a preanesthesia evaluation for patients scheduled to undergo modified radical mastectomy under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation and to obtain written informed consent for anesthesia. Anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), which comprises two subscales: the State Anxiety Inventory (SAI) and the Trait Anxiety Inventory (TAI) [25]. The SAI evaluates the current emotional state and is well suited to detecting temporal changes, it was administered preoperatively and postoperatively. General demographic and clinical data were collected, the SAI questionnaire was administered and scores were recorded.

Anesthesia monitoring included pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), electrocardiogram (ECG), noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), heart rate (HR), pulse rate (PR), and respiratory rate (RR), which were recorded as baseline values. Radial artery cannulation was then performed under local anesthesia to enable continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring and measurement of mean arterial pressure (MAP). In both groups, an ultrasound-guided serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) was performed on the affected side before induction of general anesthesia. Patients were placed supine with the ipsilateral upper limb abducted to expose the axillary region. After routine skin preparation and draping, a high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (6–13 MHz) covered with a sterile sheath was used. The probe was positioned along the midaxillary line at the 4th–5th intercostal space and scanned in the sagittal plane. On ultrasound, the latissimus dorsi, serratus anterior, and ribs were identified sequentially, and the superficial and deep fascial planes of the serratus anterior were clearly distinguished. An in-plane technique was used, advancing the needle from the probe side with continuous visualization of the needle tip. After negative aspiration, 2–3 ml of 0.9% normal saline was injected for hydrodissection when the needle tip traversed the serratus anterior to its deep surface at the fascial plane overlying the ribs and intercostal muscles; an elliptic/fusiform spread between the deep surface of the serratus anterior and the ribs was observed. Subsequently, ropivacaine hydrochloride (0.375%, 20 ml) was injected slowly with intermittent aspiration to ensure accurate delivery to the target plane. Ten minutes after completing the nerve block, the block extent was assessed with a pinprick test. Using a blunt needle, the skin from the midclavicular line to the posterior axillary line between the 2nd and 7th intercostal spaces on the affected side was lightly pricked and compared with the corresponding contralateral area. Pain grading was defined as: grade 0, no hypoalgesia (same as the contralateral side); grade 1, hypoalgesia (dull sensation); grade 2, analgesia (no pain). Success was defined as block coverage of the surgical incision area with at least three consecutive intercostal dermatomes achieving a score ≥ 1. If the sensory block was graded 0 or the dermatomal coverage was inadequate for the planned procedure, the block was classified as a failure. In such cases, the study protocol was discontinued, and the patient was excluded from the per-protocol analysis.

After a successful block, bispectral index (BIS) monitoring was initiated to assess anesthetic depth. All intraoperative drugs were titrated within the predefined dose ranges to maintain BIS values between 40 and 60, ensuring standardized anesthetic depth across both groups despite the inherent need for individualized dosing. The experimental group (OFA) received a dexmedetomidine loading dose of 1 µg/kg infused intravenously over 10 min; the control group (OA) received an equal volume of normal saline over the same period. Both groups received midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), etomidate (0.2 mg/kg), and rocuronium bromide (0.6 mg/kg). Analgesic induction was differentiated: the OFA group received esketamine 0.4 mg/kg as an intravenous bolus, and the OA group received sufentanil 0.5 µg/kg as an intravenous bolus. For anesthesia maintenance, the OFA group received continuous intravenous infusions of esketamine 0.1–0.3 mg/(kg·h) plus dexmedetomidine 0.3–0.5 µg/(kg·h) with sevoflurane (1–2%) and propofol 6–8 mg/(kg·h), while the OA group received remifentanil 0.1–0.2 µg/(kg·min) with sevoflurane (1–2%) and propofol 6–8 mg/(kg·h). Intraoperatively, BIS was maintained at 40–60. Intermittent intravenous rocuronium bromide was administered to maintain neuromuscular blockade. Hemodynamic fluctuations were managed as follows: if MAP < 60 mmHg and HR < 45 beats/min, ephedrine 5–10 mg intravenous bolus; if MAP < 60 mmHg and HR ≥ 45 beats/min, norepinephrine 5–10 µg intravenous bolus; if HR < 45 beats/min, atropine 0.5 mg intravenous bolus; if MAP or HR exceeded 120% of preoperative baseline, esmolol hydrochloride 0.25–0.5 mg/kg intravenous bolus. Before surgical closure, both groups received flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg and ondansetron 4 mg intravenously.

At the end of surgery, both groups were connected to patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pumps. Experimental group (OFA): the formulation contained esketamine 1.5 mg/kg and ondansetron hydrochloride 12 mg, diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride to a total volume of 100 mL. Control group (OA): the formulation contained sufentanil 2 µg/kg and ondansetron hydrochloride 12 mg, diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride to a total volume of 100 ml. Pump parameters were identical in both groups: background infusion rate 2 ml/h, patient-controlled bolus 2 ml, and lockout time 20 min. Patients could self-administer according to pain intensity. After surgery, patients were transferred to PACU for observation. Tracheal tubes were removed after spontaneous ventilation had recovered and hemodynamic condition became stable.

Clinical outcomes and assessments

The primary outcome was the SAI score at 24 h postoperatively. The SAI comprises 20 items that assess the participant’s current anxious emotional state, including tension, uneasiness, and worry. Total scores range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating more severe current anxiety. To ensure the authenticity and accuracy of SAI scoring, all patients completed the questionnaire independently in a separate room. When assistance was needed, it was provided by a companion who was unaware of the participant’s group assignment.

Analgesic efficacy was evaluated using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) to measure pain intensity at rest (0 = no pain; 10 = worst pain), with assessments at 24 and 72 h postoperatively. Hemodynamic stability was assessed by recording intraoperative HR and MAP at prespecified time points (T0: baseline on entry to the operating room; T1: immediately after tracheal intubation; T2: at skin incision; T3: 30 min after the start of surgery; T4: at the end of surgery). Perioperative recovery quality was evaluated by the QoR-15 score at 24 h postoperatively, extubation time, and length of stay in the PACU. The incidence of adverse events was recorded within 1 day after surgery, with particular attention to opioid-related side effects such as nausea and vomiting, and documentation of other events including bradycardia, hypotension, dizziness, blurred vision, and symptoms of local anesthetic toxicity.

Sample size

This randomized controlled trial used the postoperative 24 h SAI score as the primary outcome. Based on pilot data and prior similar literature [26], the postoperative 24 h SAI scores were 44.5 in the OFA group and 47.5 in the OA group, with an expected between-group difference of 3 points and a pooled standard deviation of approximately 4.5 points. Setting α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.90, and a two-sided test, the sample size calculation indicated that at least 47 patients were required per group. Allowing 10% loss to follow-up and using 1:1 randomization ratio, 53 patients were planned per group (total n = 106) to ensure adequate power for the final analysis. The sample size was calculated for the primary outcome, the SAI score at 24 h after surgery. Secondary outcomes were not independently powered and should be interpreted as exploratory. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to the secondary outcomes; therefore, these results carry an increased risk of Type I error and should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0, and figures were prepared with GraphPad Prism 8.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± SD); between-group comparisons were performed with the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as median (M) and interquartile range (IQR), with between-group comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), and compared between groups using the Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were less than 5. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used for inter-group comparison of MAP and HR. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Secondary outcomes

Analgesic efficacy: At 24 h postoperatively, the median resting NRS score was 3 in both groups; however, the upper quartile (Q3) was higher in the OFA group than in the OA group (3 [3–4] vs. 3 [3–3]), indicating a modest rightward shift in the OFA group (P = 0.014). Postoperative recovery quality: At 24 h postoperatively, QoR-15 scores were significantly higher in the OFA group than in the OA group (104 [101–106] vs. 99 [93–102]) (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Tracheal extubation time was 18 (15.5–20) min in the OFA group and 21 (18–25) min in the OA group, significantly shorter in the OFA group than in the OA group (P < 0.001). PACU length of stay was 48.32 ± 6.59 min in the OFA group and 56.02 ± 7.43 min in the OA group, also significantly shorter in the OFA group (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that OFA significantly reduced postoperative state anxiety at 24 and 72 h, improved early recovery quality as measured by the QoR-15, and decreased the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy for breast cancer. Hemodynamic stability and safety profiles were comparable between groups. Importantly, the observed reduction in postoperative state anxiety should not be ascribed solely to intraoperative opioid avoidance. The OFA regimen is a multicomponent strategy that includes dexmedetomidine and esketamine, agents with well-established anxiolytic and psychotropic effects. Accordingly, the lower postoperative SAI scores in the OFA group more likely reflect the composite effects of the regimen, direct α₂-adrenergic anxiolysis from dexmedetomidine, NMDA receptor-mediated mood modulation from esketamine, and the absence of opioid-related dysphoria rather than opioid avoidance per se. These findings extend the evidence base for OFA beyond its established somatic benefits by providing the first prospective evaluation of a standardized psychological outcome, postoperative state anxiety as a primary endpoint in an OFA trial for breast cancer surgery. Within the multidimensional framework for perioperative recovery assessment advocated by the European Society of Anaesthesiology–European Society of Intensive Care Medicine [5], this study addresses a critical evidence gap: the omission of psychological outcomes from OFA research in a surgical population characterized by an exceptionally high anxiety burden [7, 9].

The reduction in postoperative state anxiety observed in the OFA group should be interpreted against the broader epidemiological and clinical context of anxiety in breast cancer surgery. Preoperatively, 44.5% of nonmetastatic breast cancer patients exhibit abnormal anxiety [7], and pain, breast, and arm symptoms are significantly higher in the persistently abnormal group, particularly at postoperative months 6 and 12 [7]. Depression and anxiety are highly prevalent in this population and significantly diminish quality of life by reducing functional abilities and increasing symptom burden [27]. In the present study, the median SAI score in the OFA group was 41 at 24 h postoperatively compared with 48 in the OA group, representing a clinically meaningful difference. These values are noteworthy because SAI scores above 44 are frequently used as a threshold for clinically significant state anxiety in perioperative research [28], suggesting that OFA may shift a meaningful proportion of patients from a high-anxiety to a moderate-anxiety trajectory during the early postoperative period. The observed 7-point median difference in SAI scores at 24 h should be interpreted in relation to available benchmarks for clinical significance. Although an MCID of 10 points has been proposed for the STAI State scale in lung cancer screening populations, this threshold may not be directly applicable to the perioperative breast cancer setting [29]. Notably, the OA group median of 48 exceeded the commonly used threshold of 44 for clinically significant state anxiety in perioperative research, whereas the OFA group median of 41 was below this threshold, suggesting that OFA may have shifted a meaningful proportion of patients from clinically significant to subthreshold anxiety. Nevertheless, the clinical significance of this difference should be confirmed in larger studies with prospectively defined MCID thresholds specific to perioperative breast cancer populations. To date, no prior randomized trial has evaluated the influence of anesthetic regimen composition on postoperative anxiety using a validated instrument as the primary endpoint in breast cancer surgery, limiting direct comparison of effect sizes. However, the magnitude of the between-group difference observed here is consistent with, or exceeds, the anxiolytic effects reported for pharmacological interventions such as perioperative dexmedetomidine in other surgical contexts. After adjustment for potential confounders, patients receiving dexmedetomidine were less likely to develop PTSD than those controls 1 month postoperatively in emergency trauma surgery [30]. Among patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery, a single dose of esketamine reduced the incidence of depression symptoms and anxiety symptoms at postoperative day 7 while simultaneously improving multidimensional short-term prognosis [31].

The pharmacological basis for the OFA-associated anxiolysis is multifactorial, and the individual contributions of each component cannot be disentangled within the present study design. The observed benefits of dexmedetomidine on postoperative sleep quality and anxiety may be attributed to its selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonism; by reducing sympathetic outflow and inhibiting norepinephrine release in the locus coeruleus, dexmedetomidine exerts a sedative effect that closely mimics natural sleep, and this sympatholytic action likely contributes to anxiolysis [14, 15, 32]. This anxiolytic effect of dexmedetomidine is supported by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 studies (n = 2,159), which showed significantly lower perioperative anxiety scores with dexmedetomidine versus controls and greater efficacy than benzodiazepines [33]. Concurrently, esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, exerts rapid antidepressant effects via N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonism, enhancing prefrontal glutamate release and synaptic plasticity through BDNF-mTOR pathways [16, 34]. Elimination perioperative opioids removes a further potential contributor to postoperative psychological disturbance, given that opioids may induce dysphoria and emotional instability [17]. The convergence of these pharmacological vectors, α₂-adrenergic anxiolysis, NMDA-mediated mood modulation, and absence of opioid-related dysphoria provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed reduction in state anxiety, although the present trial design cannot isolate the independent contribution of each component.

The analgesic trajectory in this study displayed a notable crossover pattern: NRS scores were modestly higher in the OFA group at 24 h postoperatively (median 3 [3–4] vs. 3 [3–3]; P = 0.014) but significantly lower at 72 h (median 1 [1–2] vs. 2 [2–3]; P < 0.001). The early marginal pain disadvantage likely reflects the potent acute analgesic efficacy of intraoperative opioids coupled with the finite duration of single-injection nerve blockade, a pattern consistent with prior OFA studies in breast surgery [35]. This temporal dissociation between pain and anxiety at 24 h is consistent with, although not definitive evidence of, a pharmacologically independent anxiolytic mechanism. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be tempered by recognition that ongoing esketamine administration through PCIA may have contributed to both the analgesic and anxiolytic profiles observed in the OFA group. In an earlier observational study of PECS block–based OFA for modified radical mastectomy, time in the recovery room, postoperative nausea, analgesic requirements, and visual analog scale scores were all significantly lower in the nonopioid group [19], although that study did not assess pain beyond the immediate postoperative period. The reversal by 72 h may be attributable to avoidance of opioid-induced hyperalgesia. A review suggests that high intraoperative doses of remifentanil are associated with small but significant increases in acute postoperative pain [36], and opioid-induced hyperalgesia occurs when opioids paradoxically heighten the very pain they are intended to relieve [37]. Esketamine’s NMDA receptor antagonism within the OFA regimen may have conferred additional anti-hyperalgesic benefit, while dexmedetomidine, a potent and highly selective α2-adrenoceptor agonist, exerts anti-hyperalgesia effects closely linked to NMDA receptors modulation [38, 39].

QoR-15 scores were significantly higher in the OFA group at 24 h postoperatively (median 104 vs. 99; P < 0.001), corroborating previous findings that OFA with esketamine and pectoral nerve blocks enhances postoperative recovery quality in breast cancer surgery [21]. Breast surgery under general anesthesia is associated with a 30%–40% incidence of PONV [19]; the 13.2% incidence in the OFA group represents a substantial reduction from this baseline, consistent with a systematic review demonstrating that OFA may decrease PONV without compromising analgesia compared with opioid-balanced anesthesia [40]. The shortened tracheal extubation time and PACU length of stay in the OFA group further support the potential of opioid-free techniques to facilitate fast-track perioperative pathways [19, 22].

A particularly informative observation is the temporal dissociation between pain and anxiety trajectories at 24 h: despite modestly higher pain scores, the OFA group exhibited significantly lower state anxiety. This pattern suggests that the anxiolytic benefit of OFA is not merely a downstream consequence of superior analgesia but reflects a pharmacological effect on psychological well-being. A bidirectional relationship exists between pain conditions and anxiety disorders, each aggravating the other [41], and higher preoperative anxiety has been linked to greater postoperative pain [42]. If the anxiety reduction in the OFA group were solely mediated by pain relief, the greatest between-group difference in anxiety would be expected to coincide with the greatest analgesic advantage, which emerged only at 72 h. Instead, the early and sustained anxiety reduction suggests that OFA exerts a direct positive influence on emotional state via dexmedetomidine-mediated sympatholysis and esketamine-mediated synaptic plasticity modulation, independent of pain trajectory. Furthermore, the lower PONV incidence and shorter PACU stay in the OFA group may have contributed to a global enhancement of subjective well-being through reduced physical distress and earlier restoration of autonomy, domains reflected by higher QoR-15 scores. This interpretation aligns with a biopsychosocial model of perioperative recovery, in which physical comfort, psychological security, and functional independence interact to shape the overall patient experience [2, 5, 43]. The convergence of reduced anxiety, improved QoR-15 scores, and favorable PACU outcomes in the OFA group supports the notion that multimodal opioid-free strategies confer synergistic benefits across the somatic and psychological dimensions of recovery.

No clear between-group differences were detected, but estimates were imprecise and the study was not powered for safety. MAP and HR did not differ significantly between groups at any of the five prespecified intraoperative time points, indicating comparable hemodynamic stability. The rates of dizziness, blurred vision, bradycardia, and hypotension were not significantly different between groups, although the numerically higher incidence of hypotension in the OA group (18.87% vs. 9.43%; P = 0.164) warrants further evaluation in larger trials [14, 22].

Limitations

This study has several limitations:

First, as a single-center trial with a sample size of 106 patients, the generalizability of the findings to other institutions and populations requires confirmation through multicenter replication.

Second, anxiety was assessed only through 72 h postoperatively; given that moderate state anxiety may persist for up to six months after breast cancer surgery [12], longer follow-up is needed to determine whether the early anxiolytic advantage of OFA is sustained beyond the immediate postoperative period.

Third, the trial relied exclusively on self-report instruments without objective biomarker validation; incorporation of plasma cortisol, inflammatory cytokines, or electroencephalographic indices in future studies would strengthen causal inference regarding the neurobiological mechanisms of OFA-mediated anxiety reduction.

Fourth, the multicomponent nature of the OFA regimen, comprising dexmedetomidine and esketamine precludes attribution of the observed anxiolytic effect to any single pharmacological agent; factorial designs will be required to disentangle these contributions.

Fifth, the postoperative analgesic regimens differed between groups: patients in the OFA group received esketamine-based PCIA, whereas those in the OA group received sufentanil based PCIA. This approach preserved the integrity of the opioid-free strategy throughout the perioperative period. However, outcomes assessed at 24 and 72 h after surgery, including pain intensity, anxiety scores, and QoR-15, may have been influenced not only by the intraoperative anesthetic technique but also by differences in postoperative analgesia. Esketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist with recognized antidepressant properties, may have exerted psychotropic effects through PCIA that contributed to the observed differences in anxiety between groups. Future studies that apply identical postoperative analgesic regimens while varying only the intraoperative anesthetic protocol would help isolate the contribution of intraoperative OFA to postoperative psychological outcomes.

Sixth, postoperative sedation was not assessed with a validated sedation scale such as the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale or the Ramsay Sedation Scale. Although dexmedetomidine was no longer administered at the time of SAI assessment at 24 h after surgery and PCIA esketamine doses were subanesthetic, residual sedation cannot be entirely excluded as a confounder of self-reported anxiety scores. Future studies should include standardized sedation assessments at the time psychological outcomes are measured to distinguish genuine anxiolysis from sedation mediated reductions in self-reported anxiety.

Seventh, although the study used participant and assessor blinding, complete blinding of the anesthesiologist is inherently difficult in OFA trials because the study agents have distinct pharmacological profiles. All medications were administered in identical opaque syringes and the anesthesiologist was nominally blinded, but intraoperative clinical observations may still have permitted partial unblinding. Even so, comparable hemodynamic profiles between groups and blinding of outcome assessors and participants mitigate the risk of performance bias affecting the primary self-reported outcome.

Eighth, although the observed SAI difference exceeded the assumed 3-point difference used for the sample size calculation, a standardized MCID for SAI in the perioperative breast cancer population has not been established. The clinical significance of the difference between groups should be confirmed in future studies with prospectively defined, population specific MCID thresholds.

Conclusions

In women undergoing modified radical mastectomy with a serratus anterior plane block, the tested opioid free perioperative regimen based on dexmedetomidine and esketamine produced lower SAI scores at 24 and 72 h than the opioid based regimen. Because the groups also received different postoperative PCIA drugs, the contributions of opioid avoidance, dexmedetomidine, esketamine, and postoperative analgesia cannot be distinguished. The clinical relevance and longer-term persistence of the observed anxiety difference require confirmation in adequately powered multicenter trials that use comparable postoperative analgesic regimens.
