Machine Learning‐Based PAT Tool for Real‐Time Residual Moisture Monitoring in Lyophilized Biopharmaceuticals Using NIR Spectroscopy
Authors: Samuele Fiorenza, Mariapia D'Urso, Ambra Massei, Nunzia Falco, Davide Fissore, Enza Torino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is a growing demand in the pharmaceutical industry to optimize analytical methodologies, reducing time and resource consumption while ensuring rapid process development and cost‐efficient manufacturing. Indeed, Food and Drug Administration (FDA) introduced the Process Analytical Technology (PAT) as an initiative enabling manufacturers to measure and control in real‐time processes based on the Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) of the product. This study explores the implementation of PAT as a strategic approach in the lyophilization process design and optimization. In this sense, Residual Moisture (RM) was selected as a CQA to evaluate lyophilized biopharmaceutical products, due to its impact on stability. Traditionally, RM quantification is obtained by Karl‐Fischer (KF) titration, a destructive technique. Recently, Near‐Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) was proposed as a non‐destructive alternative; however, its effectiveness is hindered by spectral complexity and non‐linear moisture‐spectra relationships. Here, PAT tool integrating NIRS with Machine Learning (ML) is developed to enable rapid, at‐line RM assessment, handling the complexity of non‐linear relationships between RM values and spectral features. Different ML models – Partial Least Squares (PLS), Support Vector Regression (SVR), XGBoost, and an ensemble PLS‐SVR approach – were trained on placebo samples and validated on drug products. SVR outperformed PLS at lower moisture levels, while PLS was more accurate at higher RM ranges. The ensemble model combining both approaches improved predictive accuracy across the full RM range tested. External validation using literature‐sourced datasets, including placebo and simulated drug products with different excipients, confirmed the model's robustness and adaptability. Furthermore, a minimal dataset augmentation strategy enhanced model specialization for specific formulations. The proposed NIRS‐ML ensemble model offers a scalable, non‐destructive solution for real‐time RM monitoring, supporting pharmaceutical manufacturing within the PAT framework. Its adaptability across formulations highlights its potential as a versatile tool for lyophilized biopharmaceutical manufacturing pipelines.Keywords: lyophilized biopharmaceuticals, machine learning, near‐infrared spectroscopy, process analytical technology, residual moisture monitoring

Introduction

The pharmaceutical field is investing tremendous efforts in optimizing the development of new Drug Products (DPs), a process characterized by high complexity and substantial investment. Each phase – from early research to clinical trials, regulatory approval, and large‐scale manufacturing – has a great impact on the final product quality. This process is time‐consuming and costly, spanning 15 years on average and costing $1–2 billion (Hughes et al. 2011; Sun et al. 2022).

Despite these efforts, the failure rate of drug candidates remains considerably high, with nine out of ten failing during drug discovery and phase I, II, or III clinical trials (Sun et al. 2022). A primary challenge within drug development is ensuring the drug stability, defined as the ability of a product to maintain its original properties from manufacture through its expiry date (Wong and Datla 2005). This concept is extended throughout the period of development, production, storage, and use of the drug, thus resulting in the general classification within pre‐market stability and commercial stability (Wong and Datla 2005).

Biopharmaceutical development is particularly costly and time‐expensive, especially when employing the traditional and resource‐intensive approaches required for process development, process manufacturing and stability studies (Wang and Ohtake 2019). Standard methods, indeed, do not allow real‐time process monitoring and adjustments during manufacturing, potentially resulting in batch failures. This has led to a demand for in‐line or at‐line analytical tools capable of real‐time monitoring, as emphasized by the FDA's Guidance for Industry on Process Analytical Technology (PAT) (Pisano and Fissore 2023; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Food and Drug Administration 2004, CDER, CVM, and ORA, “Guidance for industry PAT ‐ A framework for Innovative Pharmaceutical Development, Manufacturing and Quality Assurance,” ed.). The abovementioned PAT framework encourages the development and adoption of innovative tools for continuous process monitoring and control, to enhance product quality assurance and reduce manufacturing setbacks (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Food and Drug Administration 2004, CDER, CVM, and ORA, “Guidance for industry PAT ‐ A framework for Innovative Pharmaceutical Development, Manufacturing and Quality Assurance,” ed.). The applications of the PAT approach are rapidly expanding across diverse domains, from innovative immunotherapy (Thite et al. 2025) to the emerging gene therapy (Williams et al. 2023), while also transforming the modelling of traditional processes (Barhate 2024) and driving advances in industrial machine development (Kim and Han 2025) Recent studies have explored the use of advanced PAT tools, such as on‐line ultra‐performance liquid chromatography and multimode fluorescence spectroscopy, for characterizing biopharmaceutical critical quality attributes (CQAs) in real‐time, including total protein concentration and ratio of individual monoclonal antibody (mAb), demonstrating their ability to streamline analytical workflows and reduce turnaround time (Godbole et al. 2025).

Approximately 50% of the FDA‐approved drugs have been prepared by lyophilization (Pardeshi 2023; Chen and Wang 2007), including three out of the ten highest selling DPs worldwide in 2016 (Izutsu 2018). Lyophilization, commonly known as freeze‐drying, is a fundamental process in the pharmaceutical setting to enhance the stability and the shelf‐life of heat‐sensitive compounds such as vaccines, proteins, and other biologically active substances (Izutsu 2018; Tang and Pikal 2004; Vass et al. 2019). Plus, freeze‐drying process also ameliorates the handling and distribution of pharmaceutical products (Pikal 1994; Carpenter and Chang 2020). Removing water from the product at low temperatures (e.g., −20°C) and under vacuum conditions ensures that the pharmaceutical product remains stable over extended periods (O'Sullivan et al. 2022). Indeed, the success of the lyophilization process is dependent on the residual moisture (RM) content in the final product, which is one of the most impacting CQAs (Pardeshi 2023; Miller et al. 2018; Hageman 1988). Even a small quantity of the latter above the target value can negatively impact the stability, potency, and the overall quality profile of pharmaceuticals (Pardeshi 2023; Miller et al. 2018; Jameel et al. 2021). Therefore, accurate measurement of RM is pivotal in ensuring product safety and compliance with regulatory standards (EMA n.d. “ICH Q8 (R2) Pharmaceutical Development ‐ Scientific Guideline.”). While Karl‐Fischer (KF) titration emerged to be the standard analytical method for RM quantification, it is destructive, time‐ and reagents‐consuming, requires significant manual input, and cannot be implemented at‐line thus highlighting the need for non‐invasive alternatives (Bruttel and Schlink 2003).

Among those analytical strategies, Near‐Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) has been tested as a promising alternative to KF titration for the determination of RM in lyophilized pharmaceuticals (Lin and Hsu 2002; Grohganz et al. 2011; Jamrógiewicz 2012). Specifically, NIR spectroscopy (NIRS) is a widely used analytical technique in the pharmaceutical industry due to its ability to provide rapid, non‐destructive, and real‐time analysis of various CQAs, including moisture content (Jamrógiewicz 2012; Igne and Ciurczak 2021). It leverages the interaction of the NIR light (780 nm to 2500 nm) with molecules, where molecular overtones and combinations of fundamental vibrations (e.g., O‐H, N‐H, and C‐H bonds) occur, particularly those characteristics of the water molecules (Burns and Ciurczak 2007). The absorption of NIR light by these molecular bonds leads to the generation of the spectrum, that can be used to infer the moisture content of a sample. However, direct quantification of RM using NIR spectroscopy can be challenging due to the complexity of the spectra, which is often exhibiting absorption bands overlapping, and because of the non‐linear relationships between the spectral features and the moisture levels.

To address these challenges, data analysis techniques, such as machine learning (ML), have been increasingly employed to model the complex relationships between NIR spectral data and moisture content (Lakeh et al. 2020). By developing robust and accurate predictive models, ML can effectively address the inherent complexity and non‐linearity of NIR data. Machine learning includes a range of algorithms and statistical models designed to perform specific tasks by learning from data (Sarker 2021). One of its key strengths lies in the ability to automate data analysis once the model has been trained, making it highly suitable for predictive analytics, data mining, and image processing (Manesh 2019). In pharmaceutical research, ML has demonstrated significant potential in enhancing prediction accuracy, optimizing manufacturing processes, and improving Quality Control (QC). For instance, Bobba et al. proposed the use of Fourier‐Transform NIRS as a fast and non‐destructive technique for off‐line statistical QC application in freeze‐drying processes (Bobba et al. 2021). ML models have shown exceptional capabilities at analyzing high‐dimensional datasets, such as NIR spectra, and have been successfully applied to predict various pharmaceutical properties, including the RM in lyophilized products. Several studies have highlighted the promise of NIRS combined with ML for moisture prediction in lyophilized products. Particularly, Massei and co‐workers suggested the possibility of obtaining moisture predictions in freeze‐dried pharmaceuticals by using an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) based model coupled with NIR spectroscopy (Massei et al. 2023). Nevertheless, there is still the need for developing more robust strategies, especially at low RM values (below 1%) that are applicable in an industrial context, where greater precision is required to meet the strict QC standards required in pharmaceutical manufacturing. Indeed, such a low margin of error is critical to guarantee the reliability of moisture content predictions, as even slight deviations could significantly impact the stability and efficacy of lyophilized products (Miller et al. 2018; Jameel et al. 2021).

This study lies within the PAT framework, aiming to optimize analytical workflows for residual moisture prediction in lyophilized biopharmaceuticals. By leveraging machine learning models to correlate NIR spectral data with residual moisture levels measured through Karl‐Fisher titration, this approach provides a rapid, non‐destructive solution for monitoring RM and improving process control. Specifically, ML models employed are Support Vector Regression (SVR), Partial Least Squares (PLS), XGBoost, and an ensemble model consisting of a combination of SVR and PLS depending on the range of RM to be predicted. SVR and PLS were selected for the development of the ensemble method based on their performance and explainability, which is particularly valuable for pharmaceutical PAT applications where FDA is increasingly emphasizing the needs for explainable models (in compliance with ICH Q8 (EMA n.d. “ICH Q8 (R2) Pharmaceutical Development ‐ Scientific Guideline.”), ICH Q13 (ICH 2023a), and ICH Q14 (ICH 2023b)). The applicability of this approach is further demonstrated by predicting moisture levels in samples containing an Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) without including these samples in the model training phase. Additionally, the study aims to showcase the generalization of the developed models by applying them to data with different NIR wavelength ranges, obtained from a previous study (Massei et al. 2023). Finally, this work demonstrates how the model can be fine‐tuned for a specific application by enriching the training dataset with a minimal number of experimental points closer to the problem at hand – for instance, samples containing a specific molecule or a particular combination of stabilizing excipients. This study contributes to the acceleration of the biopharmaceuticals transition from research and development stages to industrial production and clinics.

NIR Spectra Acquisition and Data Elaboration

NIR spectra were collected by using a Fourier‐Transform NIR spectrometer (Antaris MX FT‐NIR, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA), equipped with an InGaAs detector and a halogen NIR source. Although the instrument supports acquisition up to 10,400 cm−1, the modality of acquisition of the spectra was in diffuse reflectance within the NIR wavelength range 10,000–4,000 cm−1, consistent with prior work (Massei et al. 2023). The algorithm of acquisition was previously implemented on the software Thermo RESULT; specifically, each spectrum resulted from the average of 96 scans to improve the Signal‐to‐Noise Ratio (SNR) of the measurements. The resolution was fixed at 8 cm−1. The spectra were collected by holding each vial upright as described in previous work by Bobba et al (Bobba et al. 2021). The values of absorbance obtained from this process were collected in a spreadsheet, whose rows reported the ID number of each sample, and the columns contained the wavelengths, to make the further data elaboration easier.

Dataset Preparation and Algorithm Development Strategy

The objective of the experimental approach was to obtain a dataset to be used in the machine learning algorithms development, to model the relations between the variations in the NIR spectra and the changes in the measured values of residual moisture.

During the algorithm development and validation, two distinct datasets were adopted: the first (referred to as ad hoc dataset) was specifically created for this study while the second was sourced from literature (Massei et al. 2023).

The ad hoc spectra collected were included in the wavelength range 10.000–4000 cm−1, but only a limited part of the data was involved in the algorithm dataset. As for the phase of Product 1‐based training and testing and Product 2 – based validation, the wavelength range 4000–4377 cm−1 was excluded from the datasets. This choice is attributable to the high amount of noise affecting the spectra, ultimately resulting in model performance penalization.

Regarding the development steps involving the dataset taken from literature (Massei et al. 2023), the wavelength range considered is 7100–4254 cm−1. In this case, the ad hoc spectra wavelength interval was restricted to the one of the external dataset (Massei et al. 2023), to ensure size compatibility and, thus, results comparability.

In order to increase the usability of the raw spectra dataset for predictive purposes, an additional step of data processing was needed. It consisted of a series of two techniques: a second‐order Savitzky‐Golay filter with a window size of 31 and a Standard Normal Variate (SNV). The first was applied to reduce data noise by preserving local features whilst the second performed numerical data scaling. Pre‐processed spectra are reported in Supporting Information.

Machine Learning Models Development

The dataset was analyzed and modeled using different regression approaches: Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression, Support Vector Regression (SVR), XGBoost, and an ensemble model combining both PLS and SVR. Each model was chosen for its specific advantages in handling high‐dimensional, complex datasets and addressing the challenges of residual moisture prediction. Detailed information on the model development strategies, hyperparameter tuning, and algorithm‐specific configurations for the Machine Learning models used are reported in Section 3 of Supporting Information.

Partial Least Squares (PLS)

PLS is a supervised statistical‐based method modelling the relationships between the independent (X) and dependent (Y) variables by projecting both into a new latent space defined by a set of score vectors. In this case, latent variables are meant to maximize the covariance between X and Y, thus providing effective dimensionality reduction while preserving the most predictive features of the dataset (R. Further details on PLS development are included in Supporting Information (Section S3.2).

Support Vector Regression (SVR)

The SVR is designed for non‐linear and complex datasets. Due to the kernel trick, they can be mapped into a multi‐dimensional space, where simpler correlations might be more easily identifiable. It is an extension of the SVM adopted to solve regression problems. The primary objective of SVM is to find a hyperplane capable of performing the separation of the space within the classes, whose direction and position are defined by the vectors. In other words, the role of these vectors is to define a decision boundary. They are points from the dataset chosen to maximize the distance within the vectors and the hyperplane, thereby improving the number of points correctly elaborated (Cervantes et al. 2020, “A comprehensive survey on support vector machine classification: Applications, challenges and trends,” ed.). Further details on SVR development are included in Supporting Information (Section S3.3).

XGBoost

XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is a scalable tree boosting algorithm that builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, where each new tree is trained to correct the residual errors left by the previous ones (Chen and Guestrin 2016). Such approach follows a gradient boosting framework in which the model is optimized by minimizing a regularized objective function incorporating both a loss term and explicit penalties on tree complexity. This regularization, combined with techniques such as column subsampling and shrinkage, makes XGBoost particularly effective at controlling overfitting while maintaining high predictive accuracy. The algorithm also handles sparse data natively and is computationally efficient thanks to its cache‐aware learning strategy. A known limitation is the relatively large number of hyperparameters to tune — including learning rate, maximum tree depth, and subsampling ratios — which can make model selection non‐trivial. Further details on XGBoost development are included in Supporting Information (Section S3.4).

The Ensemble Model

In the end, the ensemble model is an algorithm derived from combining multiple regressors to obtain a single output. The outcome is generated by multiplying the predicted values obtained from the single models involved with specific weights and ultimately summing them. Being computationally intensive, it represents an alternative solution when a single model does not meet the predictive accuracy requirements or when determining the best model among those tested is challenging since their performances are similar. Further details on the ensemble model development are included in Supporting Information (Section S3.5).

Models Implementation

The integration was carried out using Python (ver. 3.9) within the web‐based computational environment Jupyter Notebook. The PLS and SVR regression models were implemented using the scikit‐learn (ver. 1.2.2) (Pedregosa 2011) library, while XGBoost was implemented using the XGBoost Python package. The ensemble algorithm was implemented using a combination of scikit‐learn and custom ensemble method written in Python.

Prior to model development, all spectra were preprocessed using a Savitzky‐Golay filter (window_size = 31, second‐order polynomial), followed by Standard Normal Variate (SNV) normalization. The internal dataset was then randomly divided into training and test subsets using a 70‐30 split (with random state = 0), while independent external datasets, sourced both in the present study and from the literature, were reserved exclusively for final validation.

All the hyper parameters were selected performing grid search. PLS was developed using a number of 10 latent variables, selected according to the optimized conditions reported in the Supporting Information, while the hyper parameters of SVR and XGBoost were optimized coupling such approach with five‐fold cross‐validation. For SVR, the optimization included the regularization parameter (C), the epsilon‐insensitive loss parameters (ε) and the kernel function (linear, radial basis function and polynomial). For XGBoost, the number of estimators, maximum tree depth and learning rate were optimized. More information on the hyperparameters selection is contained in the Supporting Information (Section S3).

To further assess model robustness, all final models were additionally evaluated by independent five‐fold cross‐validation, and the mean and standard deviation of RMSE and R2 score were calculated.

−Residual Predictive Deviation (RPD, Equation 11) on testing and external validation, defined as the ratio of the standard deviation of the reference values to the RMSE of prediction. RPD provides a scale‐independent measure of model performance, and it is particularly employed in spectroscopic applications to assess whether a model is suitable for screening (higher than 2) or is considered unreliable for predictive purposes (lower than 1.5) (Chang et al. 2001).

Generalization Performances of the Ensemble Model

When the relationship between dependent and independent variables does not follow a unique trend, two or more models may exhibit higher accuracies when processing specific range of data. Developing an ensemble model could aid in addressing complex distribution, as it possesses the capability to unify the strength of multiple models. In order to combine them, defining one or more points of transition may be necessary. Those are independent variable setpoints that represent a rigid boundary for the algorithm during calibration. In this phase, the algorithm determines the best model to use for each prediction according to the position of the sample with respect to this settled threshold.

This strategy consents to increasing the performance of the model in the absence of a specific data trend but it also introduces an additional risk of overfitting it since the placement of these boundary values may be specific to the training dataset. A robustness test may verify whether the ensemble model can deal with unknown sets of data with sufficient accuracy. The data utilized are sourced from the research conducted by Massei et al. 2023 (Massei et al. 2023). Conclusions

The application of PAT (i.e., Process Analytical Technology) in biopharmaceuticals, particularly for real‐time monitoring and control, is a critical step toward ensuring product quality and process efficiency. Other cutting‐edge technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning showcase great opportunities to evaluate, predict, and extract desired insights either through understanding the patterns in the data or by establishing mathematical relationships between CPPs (i.e., Critical Process Parameters) and CQAs (i.e., Critical Quality Attributes) of biopharmaceutical products (Puranik et al. 2022). In this light, a major contribution of this work was the development of a PAT tool combining NIR (i.e., Near‐Infrared) spectroscopy and machine learning modelling for at‐line residual moisture evaluation in lyophilized products, which is a pivotal CQA for this product category, which directly impacts stability, efficacy, safety, and shelf‐life. Reliable and accurate RM (i.e., Residual Moisture) evaluation is essential for ensuring product quality during its manufacturing and storage. Traditional well‐established methods, such as Karl‐Fischer titration, are destructive, time‐consuming, and resource‐intensive. For these reasons, only part of the vials within a batch can be analyzed through this technique, leading to a poor process understanding. Indeed, the integration of PAT principles offers a great opportunity to design, develop, and implement advanced tools for real‐time quality monitoring, streamlining pharmaceutical processes, and improving overall efficiency. In this context, the ensemble machine learning model presented in this study has proven to be a robust and adaptable solution for RM evaluation from NIR spectra of lyophilized biopharmaceuticals. The model combines PLS (i.e., Partial Least Squares) and SVR (i.e., Support Vector Regression) methods, leveraging their respective strengths to deliver high‐predictive performance within different ranges of residual moisture with minimal compromise in terms of models explainability. This underscores the complex, non‐linear relationships between RM values and spectral features. It has showcased sufficient accuracy and low RMSE (i.e., Root Mean Squared Error) in predicting RM for both placebo and DP (i.e., Drug Product) samples, making it a promising PAT tool for both fundamental research and industrial applications. The novelty of this work lies in its ability to leverage a limited dataset of placebo samples to predict residual moisture in more complex DPs, including those with significantly higher API content. Such generalization is rarely addressed in prior NIR‐ML studies. Moreover, the ensemble model's adaptability, evidenced by performance improvements following the inclusion of a small number of target‐specific samples, further enhances its practicality by reducing development costs and timelines.

The PLS‐SVR ensemble model has emerged as the most robust and reproducible solution: it achieved competitive validation accuracy on DP samples while exhibiting the lowest cross‐validation RMSE standard deviation across folds, reflecting superior stability compared to SVR, PLS, and XGBoost individually.

Despite these promising results, additional investigations are needed to fully unlock the model's potential. While the 1.4% model‐switching threshold is empirical, it reflects underlying spectral behaviour: high‐moisture samples exhibit broader, more linear NIR signals, thus favoring PLS, while low‐moisture samples require the nonlinear flexibility of SVR. Further analyses may unravel the physicochemical basis of this transition, potentially enabling more informed threshold selection or dynamic switching strategies. In addition, previous studies including those by Massei et al (Massei et al. 2023)., have identified challenges associated with highly concentrated APIs, reducing predictive accuracy. Encouragingly, the ensemble model here developed performed well on SA3 samples, being the DP with the highest API concentration and hence the worst‐case scenario; however, further work is required to determine the maximum API concentrations the model can handle without decreasing its accuracy.

Overall, the ensemble model represents a significant step forward in the application of machine learning to RM prediction in lyophilized biologics. By enabling rapid, non‐destructive, and accurate analysis, the developed tool meets the demands of PAT frameworks, thus potentially accelerating the transition of biopharmaceutical products from research to industrial stages. Furthermore, the proposed strategy potentially aligns with regulatory expectations for PAT as outlined in ICH Q13 and ICH Q14, supporting robust and explainable data‐driven control strategies during DP lyophilization process.
