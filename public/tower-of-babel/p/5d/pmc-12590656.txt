Establishment of rabbit models of lumbar disc degeneration using three methods monitored via X-ray: a comparative study
Authors: Xuewen Shi, Yunfei Li, Jinpeng Lou, Huijuan An, Tong Wu, Yinwen Zheng, Wenji Wang, Tao Zhang, Hua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a major cause of chronic lower back pain and associated spinal disorders. Animal models play a crucial role in elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying IDD and in the development of potential treatments. Various techniques have been used to induce IDD, including annulus fibrosus puncture, nucleus pulposus aspiration (NPA), and chemical injections. However, few studies have explored the use of NPA combined with puncture needle burning (PNB) or anhydrous ethanol injection (AEI) to induce IDD. We compared the efficacy and consistency of three induction methods – NPA, NPA + PNB, and NPA + AEI – in rabbits. The extent of degeneration was assessed using MRI, X-ray, and histological analyses.MethodsTwenty-four male New Zealand white rabbits (weighing 3.5–4.0 kg) were randomly allocated to three groups (n = 8 per group). Degeneration was induced in the L2/3, L3/4, L4/5, and L5/6 intervertebral discs using one of the three methods: NPA, NPA + PNB, or NPA + AEI. The L6/7 discs served as the internal control. Four weeks post-procedure, the degree of disc degeneration was evaluated via MRI and X-ray imaging. Histological assessment was performed using hematoxylin and eosin and safranin O/fast green staining. The severity of degeneration was quantified using the Masuda histological scoring system.ResultsAll three methods successfully induced significant IDD, as confirmed by imaging and histological analyses. The NPA + PNB group had the most severe and uniform degeneration, consistently corresponding to Pfirrmann grade IV. The NPA + AEI group had a comparable severity of degeneration but with more inter-segmental variability. In contrast, the NPA-only group had milder degeneration, predominantly within Pfirrmann grades II–III. All experimental groups had significant reductions in terms of intervertebral disc height and nucleus pulposus area, with the most pronounced reductions observed in the NPA + PNB group. MRI indices and histological scores consistently indicated that the NPA + PNB method produced the most severe and reproducible degeneration. Histological staining revealed decreased cellularity, fissures in the annulus fibrosus, and collapse of the cartilage endplate in all intervention groups, with the NPA + PNB group exhibiting the most extensive degeneration.ConclusionsThese findings confirm that NPA, NPA + PNB, and NPA + AEI are effective techniques for inducing IDD in a rabbit model. Among these, the NPA + PNB method represents one of the best options for achieving a reliable, reproducible, and minimally invasive model, producing consistent and severe degeneration with a high degree of standardization. This technique presents a valuable tool for future research investigating the pathogenesis of IDD and evaluating the efficacy of potential therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Animal model, Intervertebral disc degeneration, Nucleus pulposus aspiration, Thermal injury, Anhydrous ethanol injection

Background

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is the basis of spinal degenerative diseases. It mainly involves the lumbar intervertebral discs but also the cervical intervertebral discs, leading to nontraumatic lower back pain and neck and shoulder pain, respectively [1–3]. Research on the pathogenesis of IDD and related diseases as well as the exploration and verification of new diagnostic and treatment methods almost always require experiments in animal models before research in humans can be initiated.

Animal models of IDD have been established using different animals and methods, with the aim of creating a model that is easy to manipulate, reproducible, and has a high success rate. However, primates such as rhesus monkeys [4] are expensive and their use is restricted, such that they are not an option for research teams with limited funding and incomplete experimental facilities. Animal studies of IDD thus far reported in the literature have been based mostly on sheep [5], pigs [6], canines [7], rabbits [8, 9], and mice [10, 11].

The methods currently used to establish animal models of IDD mainly include annulus fibrosus incision or puncture [12, 13], nucleus pulposus extraction [14], induction of mechanical pressure [15, 16], chemical injection [2, 17], and gene modification or knockout [18]. Methods that rely on open surgery require extensive dissection and exposure of surrounding soft tissues, resulting in severe adhesion of the soft tissues around the intervertebral discs, which complicates the subsequent injection of repair materials into degenerated discs [14]. Moreover, there is a higher risk of infection in the surgical area of the experimental animals [19]. However, minimally invasive methods guided by a C-arm X-ray machine [10, 14] are limited by the lack of an accompanying operating table, such that medical X-ray fluoroscopy must be used. Additionally, although the methods for establishing IDD animal models reported in the literature are feasible, certain approaches involve more complex surgical procedures or equipment. Coupled with variations in experimental animals and prolonged experimental periods, these factors can lead to significant discrepancies in reproducibility and success rates, making it challenging for other researchers to obtain consistent results using the same methods.

In this study, we evaluated three approaches for establishing a short-term animal model of IDD, with particular emphasis on the combined method of nucleus pulposus aspiration (NPA) and puncture needle burning (PNB). Preliminary assessments through anatomical dissection, X-ray, and MRI confirmed that the lumbar spine of New Zealand white rabbits closely resembles that of humans, supporting their suitability as an experimental model. The NPA + PNB method demonstrated high reproducibility, consistent induction of IDD, and a high level of procedural standardization. To further assess its feasibility and advantages, we performed a comparative analysis against two other models: NPA alone and NPA combined with anhydrous ethanol injection (AEI). Our findings confirmed that the NPA + PNB method is a promising alternative for researchers seeking a reliable and standardized IDD animal model.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and main equipment

Twenty-four healthy, male New Zealand white rabbits (weight: 3.5–4.0 kg, 12 months old) were randomly divided into the three groups (8 rabbits each). Each rabbit was examined via lumbar spine anteroposterior X-ray 1 week before and 4 weeks after modeling (Siemens, Germany; Kv: 53.40, mAs: 5). The rabbits also underwent T2-weighted MRI (3.0 T MRI scanner; General Electric, USA), performed using a standard knee joint orthogonal coil, with the middle scanning line passing through the mid-sagittal position of all lumbar vertebrae and intervertebral discs. The scanning parameters were as follows: TR, 3500; TE, 82.2; FoV, 200 × 200; slices, 18; slice thickness, 2.0. The height of the intervertebral disc, the area of the nucleus pulposus, the signal intensity (average grayscale value), and the MRI index of the nucleus pulposus were determined before and after modeling surgery, as described below. X-ray and MRI were performed by three imaging technicians. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University, Ethics Approval Number: LDYYLL-2025–822.

Two additional New Zealand white rabbits were euthanized using a fatal dose of sodium pentobarbital administered via the marginal ear vein, inducing rapid unconsciousness followed by cessation of cardiac and respiratory activity. The complete lumbar segments were dissected to confirm the location of the puncture point. Several puncture training sessions were conducted on both sides of the target intervertebral disc to confirm the position and angle of the puncture needle (Fig. 1). A photo taken after dissection of the nucleus pulposus was used for confirmation during puncture modeling.

Surgical procedure

Prior to surgery, the operating room was sterilized for 1 h with a UV ozone disinfection lamp; the surgical instruments were sterilized as well. Experimental rabbits were fasted (but allowed water) for 8 h before surgery. Each rabbit received an intramuscular injection of gentamicin sulfate (0.1 mL/kg) to prevent postoperative infection.

Anesthesia and positioning: The rabbits were placed under general anesthesia by injection of pentobarbital sodium (3%, 30 mg/kg) into the ear vein. Oxygen (flow rate: 1 L/min) was administered through a face mask during the entire process. Hair was removed from the puncture area on the rabbit’s back using an electric hair clipper. Next, the rabbit was placed in a prone position on a homemade wooden board.

Identification of the intervertebral disc and skin puncture points: The fixed rabbit was moved to the examination bed of the X-ray machine. Iliac crest planes were identified on both sides as the positioning markers to mark the L6/7 intervertebral disc. A Kirschner wire was used to aid positioning, which was confirmed and adjusted under X-ray monitoring (Fig. 2). A positioning line and skin puncture point were drawn on the back skin.

Assisted positioning of the intervertebral discs for modeling, achieved using A Kirschner wires and B percutaneous puncture, confirmed and adjusted under X-ray monitoring. C A positioning line and puncture point drawn on the back skin

The skin in the puncture area of the rabbit’s waist and back was disinfected three times with 75% medical alcohol and the surgical site was draped with sterile towels. L5/6, L4/5, L3/4, and L2/3 were sequentially punctured and modeled, with the entire process maintained as sterile as possible.

NPA group: The tip of the facet process on one side of the vertebral body was tapped to assist in locating the insertion point of the intervertebral disc (~ 1.5 cm below the tip of the facet process); the angle between the puncture needle and the plane of the back skin was ~ 20° (Fig. 3). An 18G percutaneous puncture needle was slowly inserted into the center of the intervertebral disc under X-ray guidance. Then the needle core was removed and the needle was connected to a 10-mL syringe. The puncture needle was slowly removed with the needle tube rotated during suctioning. The extracted nucleus pulposus tissue (approximately 35µL) was compared to the previously dissected one. This method was used to sequentially model the L5/6, L4/5, L3/4, and L2/3 intervertebral discs of 8 rabbits.

C The puncture needle is slowly inserted into the center of the intervertebral disc under X-ray guidance. D The nucleus pulposus is aspirated using a 10 mL syringe (red box in the upper right corner shows the aspirated nucleus pulposus). E A needle core is heated until it is red hot and quickly inserted into the sleeve. F An appropriate amount of anhydrous ethanol is injected into the intervertebral disc using a 10-mL syringe

NPA + PNB group: The nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral disc was aspirated as described above, after which the puncture needle was inserted into the same intervertebral disc. The tip of the needle core was heated using an alcohol lamp until it became red hot (approximately 900°C) and then was quickly inserted into the sheath of the puncture needle for 5 s; this process was repeated three times (Fig. 3E), after which the needle was removed. This method was used to sequentially model the L5/6, L4/5, L3/4, and L2/3 intervertebral discs of 8 rabbits.

NPA + AEI group: The nucleus pulposus was extracted from the intervertebral disc as described above, after which the puncture needle was reinserted into the same intervertebral disc and an appropriate amount of anhydrous ethanol (approximately 25µL) was injected through the sheath using a 10 mL syringe (Fig. 3F). This method was used to sequentially model the L5/6, L4/5, L3/4, and L2/3 intervertebral discs of 8 rabbits.

Following the modeling procedures, rabbits received intramuscular injections of gentamicin sulfate (0.1 mL/kg) and analgesic treatment with meloxicam (0.2 mg/kg) once daily for three consecutive days. Each animal was housed individually in a separate cage, and recovery was monitored daily over a 4-week period.

Confirmation of the IDD model

Four weeks after modeling surgery, the surviving rabbits in the three groups were subjected to lumbar MRI and X-ray examination and then euthanized. The intervertebral discs were removed for histological examination.

Imaging assessment indicators for IDD

The contour of the nucleus pulposus was extracted from sagittal T2W images of the lumbar spine using LASSO. The area and signal intensity (average grayscale value) of the nucleus pulposus were automatically displayed and used to calculate the MRI index of the nucleus pulposus (nucleus pulposus area × signal intensity; Fig. 4). The degree of IDD was classified according to Pfirrmann [20]. The height of the intervertebral disc was measured from a lateral X-ray using the formula Hdisc = (Hant + Hpos) /2, where Hant and Hpos are the height of the anterior and posterior edges of the intervertebral disc, respectively.

Histology

Four weeks after the modeling surgery, the rabbits were euthanized by administering a fatal dose of sodium pentobarbital via the marginal ear vein, resulting in rapid unconsciousness followed by cessation of cardiac and respiratory function. The intervertebral discs were then dissected, cleaned, and placed into sterile centrifuge tubes. After 72 h of fixation with universal tissue fixative (4% PFA), the discs were decalcified with EDTA, cut into left and right halves along the sagittal plane (ensuring retention of the nucleus pulposus), and embedded in paraffin. Continuous 5 µm sections were cut on a microtome and stained with H&E or safranin O/fast green. Col II (1:100, BS-10589R, BIOSS) and Aggrecan (1:100, MA3-16,888, Thermo Fisher) were used as immunohistochemical markers.

Two pathologists who were blinded to the experimental conditions independently assessed the degree of disc degeneration using the Masuda histological scoring system [21], a widely accepted and validated method. This system assigns scores ranging from 4 to 12, with scores ≤ 4 indicating normal disc morphology and higher scores corresponding to increasing severity of degeneration. The evaluation encompasses pathological changes in the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilage endplate, including assessments of cell density, matrix staining, fibrosis, and the structural integrity of the nucleus pulposus.

Two chief spinal surgeons examined the imaging and histological test results to confirm the successful establishment of an animal model of IDD.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0.1) and Python (SciPy library), and graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and/or percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distributions. Paired sample t-tests were applied to compare pre- and post-modeling values within each group. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the MRI index of the nucleus pulposus, intervertebral disc height, and Masuda histological scores at the L2/3, L3/4, L4/5, and L5/6 levels among the three experimental groups. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Seven rabbits survived in the NPA group, with one death attributed to an anesthesia-related complication. All 8 rabbits in the NPA + PNB group survived. Six of the rabbits in the NPA + AEI group survived, with two deaths due to anhydrous ethanol leakage.

Imaging evaluation

Comparison of the MR images of the intervertebral discs in the three groups of rabbits before and after modeling revealed a slightly smaller nucleus pulposus area, evidenced by a slight reduction in the area of high signal intensity, and milder disc degeneration in the NPA group (Fig. 5), consistent with the volume of the extracted nucleus pulposus. In the NPA + PNB group, the discs exhibited significant degeneration and the nucleus pulposus area was of low signal intensity. The degree of IDD in the NPA + AEI group was inconsistent, due to differences in the volume of the extracted nucleus pulposus and/or the volume of anhydrous ethanol injected. In contrast, in all three groups, MRI of the control L6/7 intervertebral disc before and after modeling showed almost no difference, as the signal intensity of the nucleus pulposus remained high, and that of the annulus fibrosus and cartilage endplate remained low, with clear boundaries.

Four weeks after modeling, the area of the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral disc had decreased and the signal intensity (average grayscale value) of the intervertebral disc was significantly reduced compared to before modeling. The MRI index of the nucleus pulposus and the height of the intervertebral disc (Hdisc) were also significantly lower than before modeling (P < 0.05) (Tables 1, 2, 3). These changes indicated the induction of IDD. The differences in the L6/7 controls before and after modeling were not significant (P > 0.05).

Changes in the NPA + AEI group before and 4 weeks after modeling (n = 6 rabbits)

Analysis of the MRI index of the nucleus pulposus revealed statistically significant differences among the three groups at all intervertebral disc levels (P < 0.01). At the L3/4, L4/5, and L5/6 levels, MRI index values in both the NPA + PNB and NPA + AEI groups were significantly lower than those in the NPA group (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05), indicating more disc degeneration. At the L2/3 level, significant differences were observed between the NPA + PNB group and both the NPA group (P = 0.0037) and the NPA + AEI group (P = 0.014), whereas no significant difference was observed between the NPA and NPA + AEI groups (P = 0.413). Additionally, no significant differences in MRI index values were detected between the NPA + PNB and NPA + AEI groups at any disc level (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that both combined modeling methods (NPA + PNB and NPA + AEI) induce more severe disc degeneration than NPA alone, with the NPA + PNB method producing more pronounced and consistent degeneration.

Analysis of intervertebral disc height revealed significant differences among the groups at most disc levels: L2/3 (P = 0.036), L3/4 (P = 0.00291), L4/5 (P = 0.0298), and L5/6 (P = 0.0063). At the L3/4 level, disc height in the NPA + PNB group was significantly lower than in the NPA group (P = 0.0093) and the NPA + AEI group (P = 0.0240). At the L5/6 level, disc height in the NPA + PNB group was also significantly lower than in the NPA + AEI group (P = 0.0020). At the L4/5 level, the difference between the NPA and NPA + PNB groups approached significance (P = 0.0518). No significant differences in disc height were observed among groups at the L2/3 level. Overall, these findings suggest that both combined methods – particularly NPA + PNB – induce more pronounced and consistent IDD and loss of disc height compared with NPA alone.

According to the Pfirrmann grading system, the intervertebral discs in all three groups were classified as grade I before modeling; 4 weeks after modeling, MRI showed varying degrees of degeneration in the L2/3, L3/4, L4/5, and L5/6 intervertebral discs in each group. Almost no change was seen in the L6/7 intervertebral control discs before vs. after modeling (all grade I). In the NPA group, approximately four-fifths of discs were classified as Pfirrmann grades II–III. In the NPA + PNB group, more than three-fifths of the discs were classified as grade IV, representing the highest proportion within a single grade. Thus, both groups demonstrated relatively consistent degeneration patterns, but the NPA + PNB group exhibited more severe and consistently advanced degeneration. Significant IDD was also seen in the NPA + AEI group but the degree of degeneration was inconsistent (Fig. 6).

Morphological and histological evaluation

The area of nucleus pulposus tissue was significantly smaller after modeling. In the NPA group, the boundary between the nucleus pulposus and the annulus fibrosus was relatively clear, and the texture of the latter indicated its flexibility (Fig. 7). In the NPA + PNB group, the nucleus pulposus area was slightly yellowed (caused by needle core burn) but the contour in the fibrous ring was still clear and the annulus fibrosus retained its flexibility. In the NPA + AEI group, the boundary between the nucleus pulposus and the annulus fibrosus was not clear, and the annulus fibrosus showed obvious signs of melting, with a texture resembling wax blocks. In the controls, the nucleus pulposus was gelatinous, crystal clear, and clearly bounded by the annulus fibrosus and cartilaginous endplate.

Intervertebral disc morphology after modeling A Nucleus pulposus aspiration group. B Nucleus pulposus aspiration + puncture needle burn group. C Nucleus pulposus aspiration + anhydrous ethanol injection group. D Normal intervertebral disc

H&E staining

In all three groups, contour changes in the nucleus pulposus area indicated narrowing, accompanied by the appearance of cracks or cavities and significant decreases in the number of nucleus pulposus cells and extracellular matrix. Cracks in the fibrous ring indicated structural disorder. Cartilage endplate collapse towards the nucleus pulposus and a decrease in the height of the intervertebral disc were also observed. In the normal intervertebral disc, the contour of the nucleus pulposus was clear (approximately circular), with concentrated cells and abundant extracellular matrix. The fibrous ring exhibited distinct layers and a clear boundary with the nucleus pulposus. The morphology and height of the cartilage endplate were normal (Fig. 8).

Histological staining of the intervertebral discs 4 weeks after modeling (× 3.0). Slice thickness: 5 μm; scale bar: 500 μm

Safranin O/fast green staining

A reduced staining intensity and a smaller red-stained area were present in the nucleus pulposus area and cartilage endplate of the three groups compared to the control. In contrast, the intensity and area of green staining of the fibrous ring increased. These changes, indicating significant decrease in the aggrecan content of the nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate and an increase in collagen fibers, were more pronounced in the NPA + PNB and the NPA + AEI groups. The nucleus pulposus, inner layer of annulus fibrosus, and cartilaginous endplate of normal intervertebral discs showed obvious red staining, and the outer layer of annulus fibrosus obvious green staining (Fig. 8).

Immunohistochemistry of COL-II and aggrecan

In normal intervertebral discs, significant brownish yellow staining in the nucleus pulposus, cartilaginous endplate, and inner layer of the annulus fibrosus indicated COL-II and aggrecan positivity. In all three modeling groups, however, lighter staining reflected a decrease in the content of COL-II and aggrecan (Fig. 8).

Masuda histological scores

The Masuda histological scores for IDD differed significantly among the three groups (Kruskal–Wallis test: H = 54.50, P < 0.0001). Subsequent pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction revealed that the NPA + PNB group had significantly higher degeneration scores than both the NPA group (P < 0.0001) and the NPA + AEI group (P < 0.0001). The NPA + AEI group also had significantly higher scores than the NPA group (P = 0.0041). These findings indicate that the NPA + PNB method induced the most severe and consistent histological degeneration, followed by the NPA + AEI method, while the NPA method resulted in the mildest changes (Fig. 9).

Masuda histological scores for intervertebral discs in the NPA, NPA + AEI, and NPA + PNB groups. Significant differences were observed in all pairwise comparisons: NPA vs. NPA + AEI (P ≤ 0.01, **), NPA vs. NPA + PNB (P < 0.0001, ****), and NPA + AEI vs. NPA + PNB (P < 0.0001, ****). Severity and consistency of intervertebral disc degeneration followed the pattern: NPA + PNB > NPA + AEI > NPA

Thus, both the imaging and the histology results in all three experimental groups suggested IDD after modeling. The consistency and standardization of IDD were highest in the NPA + PNB group.

Discussion

Before the commencement of this study, we observed through dissection, X-ray, and MRI scans that the lumbar spine of New Zealand white rabbits is surrounded by several layers of thick muscle tissue, rich in nerves and blood vessels. The structure of the vertebrae and intervertebral discs closely resembles that of humans, which was one of the main reasons for selecting New Zealand white rabbits as experimental animals. However, there are significant differences in the physiological curvature of the lumbar spine compared to humans, and potential variations in the cellular composition and density of the intervertebral discs may also exist.

New Zealand white rabbits have six lumbar intervertebral discs, four of which were used in this study to model IDD. A similar grouping method was used in previous studies [14, 21], which is more consistent with the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) and aligns with the humanitarian requirements of scientific experimentation. The rabbits were administered general anesthesia to ensure that they were free of pain and remained in the desired position throughout the modeling process. In a previous study, IDD models were obtained with the animals under local anesthesia to reduce the mortality rate caused by anesthesia accidents, and good results were reported [14]. However, while the procedure was minimally invasive and conducted with X-ray imaging assistance, puncturing four intervertebral discs in each rabbit took a long time; this guided our decision to use general anesthesia.

The lumbar intervertebral disc of New Zealand white rabbits is relatively small and located deep in the body, such that modeling surgery under direct vision requires dissecting and separating a large amount of soft tissue, resulting in significant trauma and bleeding. The risk of infection at the modeling site and the death of the animal are accordingly high. We therefore recommend using minimally invasive methods and X-ray monitoring for observing, counting, and locating the intervertebral discs and monitoring the depth and angle of puncture needle insertion. While these steps greatly reduce the amount of trauma to the animals, they prolong the experiment, require higher technical skills, and inevitably expose the operator to X-rays. Accordingly, modeling and treatment studies have also been conducted under ultrasound guidance and satisfactory results have been reported [22, 23], However, the utility of this approach in animal models of IDD remains to be confirmed.

Annulus fibrosus puncture is one of the most commonly used methods for modeling IDD, with improvements made as needed [24, 25]. Comparisons of the use of puncture needles of different diameters during modeling have shown that IDD is more successfully induced using larger-diameter needles [26, 27]. In this study, we used a medium (18G) percutaneous puncture needle. Nucleus aspiration can also induce IDD [14], with the degree of degeneration closely related to the volume of the extracted nucleus pulposus. Anhydrous ethanol can cause intervertebral disc ischemia, insufficient nutrient supply, and thus degeneration [28], as also demonstrated in this study. However, anhydrous ethanol is prone to leakage, resulting in high rates of paralysis and mortality in experimental animals. Moreover, the degree of IDD varies, which can cause significant errors in the results of the experiments. Using a heated needle core to thermally damage the intervertebral disc after NPA resulted in a consistent degree of IDD and thus a satisfactory animal model of IDD.

MRI is undoubtedly the best choice for the imaging evaluation of IDD, particularly the nucleus pulposus, based on its signal intensity. Micro CT has also been used [29]. Anteroposterior and lateral X-rays of the lumbar spine are mandatory although CT and X-ray provide a more intuitive display of the structural changes in the lumbar spine and in intervertebral disc height; the use of contrast agents provides more detailed images of the intervertebral disc [30]. The optimal imaging method depends on the species of experimental animal and the anatomical site. For example, if an intervertebral disc from the tail of a rat is used to model IDD, the changes in disc height will be difficult to measure and evaluate because the disc size is very small.

H&E staining clearly displayed the structure and cellular morphology of the rabbit intervertebral discs. Simultaneous observation of the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilage endplate requires sagittal or coronal sectioning of the intervertebral disc [31] whereas the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus can only be visualized using axial (cross-sectional) sectioning [14]. Safranin O/fast green staining reveals the distribution and changes in proteoglycans and collagen fibers in both the intervertebral discs and the articular cartilage [32, 33]. The detection of COL-II and aggrecan, the main extracellular matrix components of the intervertebral disc, using immunohistochemical techniques can aid the evaluation of IDD [24, 34].

The Masuda histological scoring system [21] is a widely used and well-validated method for assessing the severity of IDD in animal models. It evaluates four key histological components – the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, the boundary between them, and the cartilage endplate – providing a standardized, quantitative framework for assessment. Each component is scored from 1 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 4 (normal) to 12 (severe degeneration). This simple, objective, and highly reproducible system enables reliable comparisons of degeneration severity across different modeling techniques and facilitates the validation of novel IDD models. Due to its strong applicability and consistency, it is widely regarded as a reference standard in preclinical studies. In recent years, modified versions of the Masuda scoring system have also been reported and applied in various experimental settings [35, 36].

Study limitations

This study involved several limitations. The first relates to the methods used to induce IDD rely on physical and chemical injury, which cannot fully replicate the natural progression of disc degeneration. Second, the percutaneous puncture needle lacked a depth-limiting device, necessitating a high level of technical proficiency and caution during the procedure to avoid overpenetration. Third, although the needle core was externally heated to approximately 900°C, its visible redness dissipated rapidly upon insertion into the cannula, and an optimal method for accurately measuring the intrabody core temperature remains undetermined. controlling the volume of anhydrous ethanol injected into the disc is technically challenging. Finally, a 4-week observation period is relatively short and may not fully capture the chronic nature of intervertebral disc degeneration. However, this duration is relatively favorable for graduate students and research teams with limited program duration and research funding. These limitations will be further investigated and addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, all three methods – NPA, NPA + PNB, and NPA + AEI – effectively induced IDD in the rabbit model. Among them, the NPA + PNB method represents one of the best options for achieving a reliable, reproducible, and minimally invasive model, producing consistent and severe degeneration with a high degree of standardization. This approach has significant potential for future research related to the pathophysiological mechanisms of IDD and the evaluation of novel therapeutic interventions.
