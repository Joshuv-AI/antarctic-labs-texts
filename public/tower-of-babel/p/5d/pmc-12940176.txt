Engineered nano-pesticides: a multifaceted strategy for sustainable crop protection and enhanced food security in the era of climate change
Authors: Krishan K Verma, Xiu-Peng Song, Qiang Liang, Lin Xu, Kamlesh K Mishra, Gan-Lin Chen, Hai-Rong Huang, Yang-Rui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo ensure global food security and sustainable agricultural productivity in the coming years, modern technology is essential. It can boost food production, increase resilience to agroclimatic disruptions, and maintain healthy agroecosystems. Pesticide use is widely considered unsustainable due to inefficient application, high energy and water consumption, and potential harm to agroecosystems. Engineered nanoparticles (ENPs) present a promising alternative to traditional pesticides by enabling targeted delivery and controlled release of active ingredients (AIs), thereby enhancing pesticidal efficacy and efficiency. This review examines how nanopesticides, which enhance crop defense and directly target pathogens much like optimal plant nutrition, can be used to manage agricultural pests and improve yields. Nanopesticides offer several benefits, including improved crop yield and quality, enhanced foliar adhesion, and targeted delivery of active ingredients (AIs) to reduce damage from biotic and abiotic stressors. This article finds that while nanopesticides are more effective and sustainable than traditional pesticides, they may also pose greater ecological risks. Therefore, further research is needed to fully understand these potential detrimental impacts. By boosting crop yields, these benefits promote sustainable agriculture and enhance global food security.Keywords: Engineered nanoparticles, pesticides, disease tolerance, enhanced nutrient uptake, slow-release, crop yield

Introduction

The application of synthetic pesticides has become a cornerstone of modern agricultural systems, deemed essential for safeguarding crop yields. However, the efficacy of this approach is increasingly compromised by a dual challenge, i.e., the continual influx of novel agricultural pests and pathogens, coupled with the rapid evolution of pest resistance to the chemicals themselves.1,2 This dynamic creates a dependency cycle in which increased pesticide use accelerates resistance, necessitating even higher application rates or more potent compounds. Agricultural production in many developing countries remains heavily reliant on conventional pesticides, as climatic conditions often favor the proliferation of diverse pests and diseases.3‐5 Chemical compounds are toxic to humans and wildlife and are challenging to control during natural atmospheric conditions.6,7

Engineered nanoparticles (ENPs) hold significant promise for applications across diverse sectors, including agriculture, energy, defense, and environmental remediation. Within agriculture, their development into nanopesticides represents a particularly active area of research. These novel plant protection products are designed to enhance pesticidal efficacy and specificity, offering potential solutions to challenges, such as environmental runoff and pest resistance.8‐10 Nanopesticides “involve either very small particles of a pesticide active ingredient (ai) or other small engineered structures with significant pesticidal characteristics.”2,11,12

Nanopesticides represent an emerging technological approach that, compared with conventional pesticides, could offer a range of advantages, such as enhanced efficacy, durability, and reduced active ingredient requirements. Different formulations have been proposed, including emulsions (nanoemulsions), nanocapsules (with polymers), and inorganic ENPs, such as metals, metal oxides, and nanoclays.13,14 At various stages in the product development cycle, these products may be applied to enhance the efficacy of existing pesticide-active ingredients or improve their environmental safety profiles.15,16

The fact that the fate and behavior of ENPs in the environment can differ significantly from those of conventional materials has raised concerns about their impacts on atmospheric conditions. Nanopesticide formulations may enhance the solubility of agrochemicals in water, improve their bioavailability, and protect them against environmental degradation, thereby revolutionizing the management of pathogens, weeds, and insects in crop plants.17,18 Nanopesticides from runoff of agricultural and industrial wastewater during precipitation, driven by soil-permeating leaching, reach the water supply, affecting its quality, increasing human exposure time and frequency, and raising concerns for ecosystems.19,20

Different applications, including inorganic and organic polymeric materials with diverse structural morphologies, have been utilized to develop conventional antimicrobial nano-enabled materials.21,22 Polymer-based nanoformulations in the shapes of nanospheres, nanocapsules, nanogels, and nanofibers showed diverse degrees of biodegradability. In nanospheres, the active substances are evenly dispersed throughout the polymeric matrix, whereas in nanocapsules, they reside at the center and are surrounded by the polymer matrix. Nanogels are matrices of cross-linked biopolymers that fill the active component with pores.10,23 Nanogels containing active substances, such as copper, essential oils, or pheromones, have been proposed to meet organic farming regulations.24

Furthermore, pesticides can be nanoencapsulated; their active component can be released under regulated conditions over an extended period by modifying the outer shell with nanoscale materials. Using water or oil nanoemulsions can also increase the solubility and effectiveness of antimicrobials. The overarching objective of nanoencapsulation design is to generate cutting-edge materials that efficiently regulate the bioavailability of the active ingredient to the target pathogen while minimizing adverse effects on non-target species.18,25

Some elements have demonstrated antimicrobial activity, and they are essential plant nutrients. This has led to the use of nutrient-element-based materials to simultaneously suppress plant pathogenic diseases, enhance crop productivity, and improve nutrient use efficiency (NUE).26,27 More research is being conducted to determine how effectively element-based ENPs inhibit plant diseases in in vivo, greenhouse, and in vitro conditions. However, various elements, including Mn, Ti, and Ce, as well as biopolymers such as chitosan and β-D-glycan NPs, have been discussed.28‐30 The current article addresses recent developments in various ENPs assessed for their ability to protect against plant diseases. It emphasizes the knowledge gaps that must be addressed to ensure the sustainable application of nanopesticides to advance agroecosystems and achieve global food security and safety, thereby supporting socioeconomic development in the near future.

Silver-based nanopesticides

Different field demonstrations have shown that silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) protect against various diseases. The toxicity mechanism of Ag NPs is still not fully understood, but it appears to be triggered primarily by the release of ionic Ag+. Ag ions are well recognized as highly hazardous; a well-established mechanism involving plasma membrane proteins containing cysteine disrupts the pathogen's membrane. After three to seven days, the biosynthesized Ag NPs were shown to exhibit in vitro antifungal efficacy against Alternaria solani, reducing the spore count (100%) and biomass (73%).31‐33 Compared to control plants, Bipolaris sorokiniana exposed to Ag NPs (2–10 ppm) in in vitro conditions showed reduced fungal conidial development.34 It was demonstrated that DNA-directed Ag NPs were grown on graphene oxide. These Ag@dsDNA@GO composites effectively downregulate Xanthomonas perforans cell viability in culture and on plants. At 16 ppm of Ag@dsDNA@GO, the composites exhibit better antibacterial activity in culture, with significant advantages in stability, enhanced antibacterial activity, and greater adsorption capacity.35 The researchers hypothesized that bacterial cell functional groups, including thiol, carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino, phosphate, and imidazole, interact with Ag NPs and the Ag+ released from them, leading to inactivation and, ultimately, total damage. Ag enclosed in the nanochitosan carrier demonstrated a minimum inhibitory concentration that was lower than that of solitary Ag NPs, indicating the enhanced antibacterial activity of nanoencapsulated Ag on gram-negative bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa).23,36

The ability of the combination of graphene oxide/Ag NPs to combat Xanthomonas oryzae pv. Oryzae, the bacterium that triggers bacterial blight disease (Tables 1 and 2). The nanocomposite, formed with graphene oxide powder, poly(N-vinyl-2-pyrrolidone), and AgNO3, was four times more efficient than Ag NP alone.37 However, investigations have shown that Ag NPs can be biotransformed into Ag2S, AgCl, Ag0, or Ag-cysteine in aqueous and soil applications, among other substances, which may reduce toxicity and mitigate adverse environmental effects.38,39

Copper-based nanopesticides

Organic Cu-compounds account for 56% of the weight of pesticides based on Cu, followed by CuSO4 (34%), Cu2O (6%), and CuO (4%).45 The use of nanoscale Cu, such as Cu(OH)2 and CuO, as microbicides has increased with the adoption of Cu-based products.46 Studies have shown that Cu-based NPs exhibit antibacterial activity in vitro. Cu NPs downregulate the frequency of plant disease development, including Alternaria alternata, Curvularia lunata, and Phoma destructive, the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method.47 According to Bramhanwade et al.48 field application of Cu NPs reduced the growth of Fusarium sp. (64%) after 9 d and Botrytis cinerea (13%–18%) after 72 h. Cu NPs also downregulated the radial dispersion of plant pathogens, such as Fusarium sp., F. culmorum (19 mm), F. oxysporum (20 mm), and F. equiseti (25 mm).49 CuO NPs can protect the growth of P. ultimum and P. aphanidermatum, two pathogenic Pythium isolates found in T. aestivum plants. To improve the release and effectiveness of Cu, citrate was integrated into the growth media. The CuO NPs (250 and 500 ppm) in the plates indicated the release of Cu2+ ions from the NPs. CuO NPs inhibited the growth of P. ultimum and P. aphanidermatum (10% and 35% inhibition, respectively) compared to the control, demonstrating species-dependent variations in the bioresponse. Moreover, it was shown that the CuO NPs reduced the mycelia-bound ferric reductase activity.50

The gradual release of Cu2+ by CuO-NPs suggests that their effectiveness is longer-lasting than that of CuSO4. CuO NMs were more effective in protecting S. lycopersicum plants against Phytophthora infestans than the negative control and reference products (Kocide 2000 and Kocide Opti).51 CuO NPs (500–1000 ppm) significantly increased crop productivity compared to control and other NPs, such as MnO, SiO, TiO, and ZnO. Additionally, they suppressed Fusarium wilt disease in watermelons caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum.52 The effect of bulk equivalents of various NPs levels was found to be less beneficial. However, the experiment employed a higher concentration level of Cu-based NPs. In the field experiment, biosynthesized Cu NPs (2.5 ppm) were used to reduce (80%) Poria hypolateritia-caused red root-rot disease in tea plants.53

Under greenhouse conditions, an advanced Cu composite comprising core-shell Cu, multivalent Cu, and fixed ammonium Cu effectively suppressed tomato bacterial spot disease caused by Xanthomonas spp.23,54 When treated at 20% of the Cu as a commercial product (Cu-mancozeb) in field demonstrations, the Cu nanocomposites dramatically reduced the incidence of disease caused by Cu-tolerant X. perforans, with no significantly higher crop productivity was achieved.54 It is an important finding because bacterial spot disease has evolved a resistance to different traditional Cu-based bactericides. When used in limited amounts, the antibacterial properties of Cu-based NPs can serve as a viable alternative to conventional pesticides in environmentally friendly farming practices (Tables 1 and 2). Furthermore, commercially available Cu insecticides are currently over-applied in agricultural fields, leading to plant diseases becoming resistant to Cu. Most investigations showed that Cu-based NPs were more effective against infections and improved productivity, frequently at relatively low concentrations.

Zinc-based nanopesticides

ZnO NPs synthesized using enviro-friendly approaches have demonstrated antimicrobial activity in in vitro conditions in response to a variety of bacterial and fungal pathogens, including Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus nidulans, Aspergillus niger, B. cinerea, Penicillium expansum, Fusarium graminearum, Trichoderma harzianum, and Rhizopus stolonifer.55,56 The microbial cell wall appeared to be severely damaged by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated at the surface of the ZnO nanoparticles, leading to organism inactivation. For long-duration exposure to ZnO NPs application in the sand (63% inhibition) and mung bean broth agar (75% inhibition), F. graminearum growth was suppressed.55 It was reported that ZnO NPs (25 ppm) plate assess to suppress the growth of A. flavus and A. niger, with maximum inhibitory zones of 19 and 22 mm for each fungus.57 According to He et al.,58 ZnO NPs (3–12 mM) dramatically reduced the development of B. cinerea (up to 80%) and P. expansum (up to 91%). Additionally, field experiments have shown that ZnO NPs suppressed Pythium isolates (50–500 ppm) compared to control plants and downregulated the radial spread of F. oxysporum.49,59 ZnO NPs are a broad-spectrum antibiotic that inhibits bacteria, fungi, and oomycete pathogens. For NPs containing Ag, Cu, and Zn, Zn-based NPs appear to be less harmful to plants and may be a more practical approach, as they can be applied as nanopesticides with fewer negative impacts on the atmosphere (Tables 1 and 2).

Zinc is considered a plant disease management tool due to its antibacterial properties and its importance in the human diet. Currently, limited studies have shown its major function in disease management in vivo. Citrus canker lesion disease caused by Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri was inhibited in grapefruit in field and greenhouse conditions by applying two different ZnO NPs formulations, namely, the plate-like Zinkicide SG4 and the particulate-like Zinkicide SG6.60 A light-activated TiO2/Zn NPs composite was developed by Paret et al.61 and its ability to inhibit disease in open-field trials was demonstrated. Compared to the control, the formulation (500–800 pm) reduced the incidence of Xanthomonas sp. caused bacterial leaf spot disease in rose plants (62%–71%). Using a higher concentration of the nanoformulation proved beneficial for controlling leaf spot disease. It is necessary to assess potential effects on organisms that are not the intended targets and to compare them with those of commercial pesticides. ZnO NPs (1 ppm) were applied in the greenhouse to reduce tomato wilt disease.60 Information gaps exist in investigating the potential of Zn NPs as insecticides. Given that different plant genotypes respond differently to Zn, further research is needed to determine the optimal conditions for the widespread application of Zn NPs in sustainable agriculture. It is important to understand how zinc moves up the food chain through trophic transfer to prevent zinc toxicity in common dietary items and human foods, thereby improving socioeconomic value.

Chitosan and other ENPs as pesticides

Chitosan NPs (CNPs) may serve as an effective antibacterial agent against harmful bacteria, fungi, and viruses.30,62 Alternatively, it can be combined with other ENPs to generate nanocomposites that will gradually improve their microbicide properties and deliver nutrients and active compounds to plants. CNPs were combined with an anionic protein solution extracted from Penicillium oxalicum culture.63 Pyricularia grisea, A. solani, and F. oxysporum were used to investigate the antifungal efficacy of the biologically produced CNPs. CNPs enhanced in vivo seed germination, the seed vigor index, and chickpea biomass while also dramatically reducing in vitro the growth of pathogenic fungi.63

According to Xing et al.,64 CNPs significantly reduced the mycelial growth of Ceratocystis fimbriata in vitro, altering hyphal morphology and causing irreversible membrane damage. It was also observed whether any intracellular compounds, particularly potassium ions, leaked out due to the NPs' induction of membrane permeability. Seventy percent of the spores were executed, indicating that the CNPs caused necrotic cell death within 3 h of exposure based on the considerable drop in viable cells.65,66 It has been demonstrated that CNPs and their composite with silver (CAgNCs) damage F. oxysporum membrane structure. The pathogen exhibited morphological and ultrastructural changes due to the NPs, and the pathogenic radial expansion decreased with CAgNCs compared to single CNP use.67 Similar results on V. dahliae were reported for the oleoyl–chitosan nanocomposite.68 When applied to Aspergillus parasiticus spores, a bionanocomposite including CNPs and a chitosan/pepper tree (Schinus molle) essential oil demonstrated antifungal activity.68 According to Luque-Alcaraz et al.,69 the nanocomposite (12.5–200 ppm) reduced the viability of A. parasiticus cells by up to 50%. The nanocomposite depends on each component's strength separately, while it is believed that the CNPs' function played a major role. Overall, more field research results evaluating these NPs antimicrobial activity indicate a mode of action based on ROS production, making it abundantly evident that these substances can function as direct fungicides or bactericides.

Fusarium head blight, caused by F. graminearum, was effectively controlled by pretreating wheat with CNPs (1000 ppm) prior to pathogen inoculation.70 The efficacy against the pathogen was lower than that of the conventional “Tilt” fungicide with propiconazole as the active ingredient. Comparing an untreated infested control to a detached leaf assay showed that CNPs substantially inhibited rice leaf blast disease caused (50%) by Pyricularia grisea.71 More research is needed to fully understand the mechanism of action of chitosan in the rice-pathogen system. Compared to the untreated infested controls, CNPs minimize the onset of blast disease symptoms in infested finger millet from 15–25 d and decrease the disease (64%) on the 50th day of application. The authors observed increased ROS generation and peroxidase activity, which were plant responses to CNPs and contributed to disease control.72 When CNPs (250 mg kg−1 soil) were applied to millet seeds, the incidence of downy mildew disease caused by Sclerospora graminicola decreased significantly (∼82%) compared to the control73 (Tables 1 and 2).

Siddaiah et al.,73 observed a correlation between disease suppression and the upregulation of genes encoding ammonia lyase, catalase, peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase, phenylalanine, and superoxide dismutase in treated plants. Cu–chitosan nanocomposite was found to improve the defense mechanisms of Fusarium verticillioides,74 C. lunata,75 and finger millet against P. grisea.76 It has been demonstrated that crop yields increased significantly when the nanocomposite was applied. In finger millet, foliar application of the nanocomposite in combination with seed treatment proved more effective than foliar application alone in controlling P. grisea. Seed treatment was especially recommended to enhance the plant's defense enzymatic capabilities and protect it against pathogen invasion. Particle size, surface charge, treatment frequency and duration, solubility, biodegradability, and permeability in agroecosystems affect particle antibacterial activity. Changes in the physicochemical characteristics of CNPs can enhance their antimicrobial activity (Tables 1 and 2).

In various disease systems, alternative ENPs, including carbon-based ENPs, non-nutrient metallic ENPs other than Ag, and composite ENPs, have also been investigated. Following 72-h inoculation, B. cinerea development was 20% lower with fullerene amendment (50 ppm).49 In maize, Si NPs decreased the incidence of disease caused by F. oxysporum and A. niger.77 There is still limited knowledge of the mechanisms of action of these ENPs.51 Mesoporous alumina NPs and the commercial fungicide tolclofos-methyl effectively inhibited F. oxysporum caused root rot in greenhouse trials, resulting in a 20-fold increase in tomato plant growth compared to control plants.78

Fe2O3NPs, TiO2NPs, or carbon-based NPs (MWCNTs and C60) suppressed Turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) infection in Nicotiana benthamiana plants.79 The virus was injected into newly formed tobacco leaves pretreated with ENPs (50 or 200 ppm) for three weeks. Compared to the control plants, the ENPs treatment dramatically downregulates TuMV growth on the leaf surface after 5 d of TuMV inoculation. When compared to infected plants without ENPs, the fresh biomass of tobacco plants infected with metallic (50 ppm) and carbon-based (50 and 200 ppm) ENPs increased (55%).79 Metallic NPs accumulated in the chloroplasts of the plants, although the plants' cellular integrity was undamaged. The significantly lower levels of TuMV coat proteins and higher (40%) phytohormone levels suggest that ENPs may have a mechanism of action in disease suppression. Applying β-D-glycan NPs (0.1%, w/v) externally to turmeric plants infected with rhizome rot disease improved the defense enzymatic activities, including β-D-glucanase, polyphenol oxidase, peroxidase, and protease inhibitors.79,80 Compared with those in untreated plants, these enzymes showed increased activity in the incidence of the condition (Tables 1 and 2). A limited number of experiments have employed ENPs at moderate concentrations and compared their effects with those of conventional pesticides.12,54,74,81

Fate of the nanopesticides

The physicochemical properties, dosage, and application frequency of NPs determine their effect on crop productivity, which can occur through the disruption of biochemical or photochemical photosynthetic pathways.10,14 The shape and size of the NPs correlate with their mechanical impact on plants, thereby confining them to specific sites on organ surfaces or within plant tissues. Despite this localized confinement, the effects are systemic, inhibiting plant growth and development throughout the entire vegetative stage.16 Nanopesticides impair plant photosynthesis by entering the cytoplasm and binding directly to the photosynthetic apparatus of chloroplasts. This interaction disrupts specific photosynthetic reactions, and research has demonstrated that metallic NPs can cause significant functional and structural loss, harming leaf gas exchange.2,97,98 A comprehensive understanding of how NPs interact with photosynthetic processes is critical, as it reveals their mechanisms of action, including the inhibition of electron transport, photoinduction of oxidative stress, and subsequent activation of the plant's antioxidant defense system.26 For instance, studies on tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill.) have shown that 3 nm carbon nanotubes (CNTs) alter germination rates by facilitating water uptake through the seed coat.99 Conversely, carbon-based NPs inhibit growth via cytotoxicity mechanisms primarily involving mechanical damage, such as perforation of organelles and cellulose membranes.19,100 The demonstrated capacity of titanium-based NPs and clay-based colloidal suspensions to compromise hydraulic conductivity in Zea mays L., inhibiting transpiration and development,96,101 necessitates a thorough assessment of NPs' functions and predicted behavior within pesticide nanoformulations102 (Figures 1 and 2).

Mechanisms, benefits, and impacts of applying engineered nano-pesticides in agriculture. These applications extend beyond direct pathogen suppression to include plant immunomodulation, microbiome reshaping, and broader outcomes, such as increased yield, reduced environmental footprint, improved resource efficiency, and reduced chemical dependency, contributing to sustainable agricultural systems and socio-ecological resilience.

The concentration of the active ingredient is a key determinant of the environmental fate of NPs in soil. This relationship is quantified through soil release kinetics, which measure the persistence of a nanoformulation within the soil pore water. Distinguishing between bioavailable and soil-bound pesticide fractions requires robust analytical methods to assess persistence accurately.97,103,104 The phytotoxicity or beneficial effects of NMs on plants are governed by the material's concentration and intrinsic properties.16,105 These effects are further modulated by the dynamic transformation of NMs in the environment, where interactions with surrounding media and the adsorption of molecules alter their surface profile and reactivity. Biotransformation, driven by interactions with atmospheric biofilms, biological organisms, and plants, is a key process governing the fate, behavior, and persistence of NMs.19,20 Multiple transport and fate models, including the FOCUS models, such as TOXSWA, MACRO, PEARL, and PRZM, are available to simulate the behavior of organic chemicals across environmental compartments and support decision-making.106‐108

Synergistic effects of nanopesticides on crop productivity and nutritional food value

A recent study demonstrated the effectiveness of metal-based nanopesticides, indicating that their targeted application can lead to substantial improvements in agricultural productivity. This includes enhancements in crop biomass, yield, and nutritional quality.109,110 The utilization of metal-based nanopesticides, specifically silver (Ag), titanium (Ti), copper (Cu), and zinc (Zn) nanoparticles, has been shown to significantly increase the contents of sugars, fatty acids, chlorophyll, carotenoids, and essential elements in a variety of plant species.111‐113 The observed improvements can be attributed to several critical physiological alterations,114 including the optimization of chlorophyll structure and function, which enhances light absorption and electron transfer efficiency. Furthermore, there are improvements in CO₂ assimilation and water uptake, efficient removal of excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibition of pathogenic activity, and induction of a more robust plant immune response due to beneficial modulation of metabolic pathways115 (Figures 1 and 2).

A key priority is to explore the intricate relationships among nanopesticides, soil properties, the rhizosphere microbiome, and plant nutrients. Gaining insights into these interactions is essential for optimizing the efficacy of nanopesticides. The introduction of metal-based formulations can disrupt the recycling and availability of macronutrients by altering the structure and ecological networks of the microbiome. Additionally, nanopesticides can modulate cell signaling pathways and the composition and functionality of the rhizosphere microbiome, potentially enhancing the bioavailability of micronutrients. These improvements can increase plant productivity and nutritional quality, ultimately supporting soil health and biodiversity within agroecosystems.7,116 However, recent studies concerning the long-term impacts of silver nanopesticides (at 100 mg kg−1) on the maize rhizosphere microbiome have shown concerning effects, including reduced microbial populations, disruption of the nitrogen cycle, and lower crop yields.117 Thus, further research is essential to evaluate how nanopesticides at realistic concentrations influence crop production, soil health, and the rhizosphere microbiome under long-term field conditions.

Field-based assessment of nanopesticide effectiveness

Field studies, although they represent a relatively small proportion of comparisons with target organisms, provide critical data for scaling up nanopesticide technologies. The preponderance of these field trials has focused on metal-based nanopesticides, particularly those formulated with various copper compounds, including Cu, CuO, Cu₃(PO₄)₂, and Cu(OH)₂. In contrast, investigations of nanopesticides using nanocarrier encapsulation are limited, with only eight examples reported to date. The effectiveness of these treatments is primarily measured by their ability to mitigate disease, typically quantified by reductions in disease incidence or the area under the disease progress curve (AUDPC). Analysis of field data indicates that nanopesticides offer an average efficacy advantage of approximately 19% over conventional pesticides. Notably, comparative field studies conducted over a complete crop cycle demonstrated that a foliar spray of 150 nm Cu₃(PO₄)₂ nanosheets achieved a 26% reduction in disease severity and a remarkable 46% increase in fruit yield compared to traditional CuSO₄ salt applications. This enhanced performance is largely attributed to the activation of plant defense and antioxidant systems initiated through optimized crop nutrition to combat Fusarium wilt disease.118

Similarly, foliar application of 30 nm CuO nanopesticides at the seedling stage resulted in a 69% reduction in the AUDPC for both Fusarium oxysporum and Verticillium dahliae, accompanied by a 64% increase in yield for tomato and eggplant crops. Elmer and White84. These observed benefits can be partly attributed to the dual functionality of copper-based nanopesticides, which act as nanofertilizers (NFs) and enhance plant immunity by providing essential micronutrients. Elmer and White.84 Advancing the development of sustainable nanopesticides hinges on precisely manipulating engineered nanomaterial (ENM) characteristics—such as size, morphology, and composition—enabling a single foliar application at the seedling stage to confer protection against soil-borne pathogens, ultimately maximizing crop yields and health.

Foliar immersion with a 500 ppm suspension of the chitosan-coated mesoporous silica nanopesticide resulted in a 27% reduction in AUDPC and a remarkable 70% increase in watermelon fruit yield.119 The mechanistic basis for the action of chitosan and silicon involves the stimulation of plant defense and antioxidant systems. The nanocarriers facilitate the controlled, sustained release of active ingredients in response to stress conditions induced by weeds and insects. When applied at a reduced dosage—one-third lower than conventional pesticides120,121—these nanopesticides demonstrated enhanced efficacy and prolonged activity against target organisms. This approach supports sustainable, cost-effective pest management by minimizing material use and application frequency,119 thereby reducing environmental damage associated with excessive agrochemical use. According to Kah et al.,​​​​​​122 the crop protection industry considers a minimum threshold of a 20% gain in efficacy, productivity, or cost savings for a nanoformulation to be competitive. However, the authors stress the need for more rigorous performance trials conducted across diverse geographical regions with varying soil and water systems, as well as different climatic conditions over multiple growing seasons. To advance the field, promising nanopesticides identified in small-scale trials should transition to large-scale demonstrations, where some may prove more efficient than their non-nanoscale counterparts. These should be evaluated under a variety of climatic scenarios.123

Climate-induced degradation and functional longevity of nanopesticides

The productivity of global agriculture is increasingly undermined by climatic constraints, particularly the rising frequency of extreme weather events.109,124 These abiotic factors have the potential to reduce global crop yields by more than 50%.125 Heat and drought, in particular, have been identified as significant threats in the 2020 IPCC report. Predictive models suggest that a warmer climate could decrease yields of critical cereals such as soybean, maize, rice, and wheat by 2.9%–10.6%, even before accounting for secondary effects such as increased pest and pathogen pressures.126 As a result, the evolution of agricultural practices is crucial for building systemic resilience, with responsive nanopesticides emerging as a promising technology to enhance crop adaptation to these mounting stresses. The nanofungicide was developed using a thermosensitive polydopamine–graphene oxide nanocarrier to encapsulate hymexazol.127 This targeted release mechanism is essential for effectively controlling pathogens during their peak activity periods and reducing competition for resources, thereby potentially enhancing crop growth.127 The release kinetics of 2,4-D from the MSN@2,4-D nanoherbicide were accelerated and more extensive in 0.1 M NaCl solution than in pure water. This phenomenon is attributed to electrostatic interactions, in which chloride ions displace 2,4-D anions from the positively charged trimethylammonium groups on mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs). Additionally, the release profile was influenced by temperature and pH, resulting in high efficacy against target organisms while maintaining selective phototoxicity toward wheat.128 Beyond facilitating plant growth through improved photosynthesis, MSNs exhibit a parallel in metal-based nanopesticides, which enhance plant immunity and stress tolerance.129

The protective effect is mediated by the regulation of defense-related compounds, including secondary metabolites, ROS, and phytohormones, which depend on their physicochemical properties, application methods, and dosages.115,130,131 Research shows that metal-based nanopesticides (e.g., Ag, CuO, TiO₂, and ZnO), even at low concentrations, can stimulate plant growth and confer resistance to various abiotic and biotic stresses, including fungal pathogens like F. oxysporum, viruses,118,132 as well as environmental challenges such as drought,133 cold,134 salinity,135 and heavy metal toxicity.136 These findings highlight the potential of these nanomaterials in stress mitigation and improving agricultural productivity. Nanopesticides based on metallic compounds demonstrate enhanced effectiveness in combating both biotic and abiotic stressors. An innovative approach involves engineering advanced nanopesticides that integrate metal-based nanomaterials as active ingredients within encapsulating nanocarriers. By utilizing a controlled-release mechanism, these systems offer significant potential to protect crops from simultaneous stresses, including heat, drought, and pathogens, thereby effectively enhancing agricultural output in a changing climate (Figures 1 and 2). A crucial next step is to conduct field trials assessing the responsive release of active ingredients under climatically relevant conditions.

Dynamics of nanopesticides in the plant‒soil continuum

A comprehensive understanding of the interactions between nanopesticides and plant species—including their transport and transformation dynamics within plant–soil systems—is essential for their effective integration into integrated pest management (IPM) strategies. Upon application via foliar spray or root uptake, nanopesticides can enter plant tissues through aerial or subterranean pathways. Research on tomatoes, for example, indicates that the cuticle and epidermis facilitate the rapid uptake of polymer-based nanobactericides (14–32 nm), thereby facilitating their movement into the mesophyll and phloem.137 Interestingly, despite their larger size (256–345 nm), a polymer@atrazine nanoherbicide can penetrate plant tissues via hydathodes (a few to several micrometers in size), effectively bypassing typical size-related biological barriers.138 Once inside, these nanopesticides translocate via apoplastic and symplastic pathways.139 This evidence suggests that even larger nanopesticides (~80–200 nm) can be internalized into cells, challenging the prevailing assumption that only smaller nanoparticles can pass through cellular structures.140,141 The internalization of nanopesticides is facilitated by their interactions with cell walls, which can occur via endocytosis and pore formation. Furthermore, pre-existing damage to plant tissues caused by stress or disease can create additional entry points. Once inside the cytoplasm, nanopesticides can interact with key cellular components, including organelles, proteins, and DNA, thereby influencing the plant's physiological, biochemical, and metabolic processes (Figure 1).

Risks and toxicological implications of nanopesticide application

While nanopesticides exhibit significant pesticidal efficacy, they also pose a risk of toxicity to non-target organisms. The nature and extent of the observed adverse effects depend on various factors, including the physicochemical properties of the nanopesticide, the specific non-target species involved, and exposure parameters such as route, concentration, and duration. The evidence suggests that commercial formulations such as Zerebra Agro (Ag-based) and Kocide 3000 (Cu-based) can induce a range of detrimental effects at the physiological, metabolic, and genetic levels in plants, microcrustaceans, and the soil microbiota. These effects include structural tissue alterations, chlorophyll depletion, disruptions to the antioxidant defense system, metabolic reprogramming, and genetic over-regulation.142 The application of nanopesticides raises concerns about soil microbial activity; however, long-term field studies indicate that these negative impacts are mitigated when nanopesticides are applied at agriculturally relevant doses, likely because of the soil's self-recovery capacity.17,143

To address the potential risks associated with nanopesticides, it is essential to expand research to include other formulations with high market potential under varied exposure conditions. Crucially, the toxicity of nanopesticides is not limited to terrestrial environments. For instance, studies on a Cu-based nanofungicide in marine settings have shown that its bioactive form can persist even after treated lumber is submerged in seawater.144 Furthermore, exposure of benthic communities to the same Cu-based nanopesticide in controlled laboratory conditions resulted in statistically significant adverse effects.145 Thus, despite the advantages of nanopesticides, their translocation into non-target environments poses a risk of negative ecological consequences, highlighting a critical knowledge gap that must be addressed. The application of nanocarriers alone, without active ingredients, has been shown to promote the growth of beneficial nitrogen-cycling bacteria.146 This finding suggests that encapsulated nanopesticides could provide secondary benefits in the field, as their components are likely to separate in the soil over time. Additionally, unlike their metal-based counterparts, encapsulated nanopesticides tend to be less disruptive to the soil microbiota.147 This enhanced safety profile is likely due to their larger size and associated biological response, which renders them more compatible with the vital soil biota and rhizosphere microbiome responsible for nutrient cycling and overall plant health.148

Multiomics analysis for assessing the toxicity of nanopesticides

The application of multiomic techniques has established a robust framework for elucidating the mechanistic basis of nanopesticide toxicity.149 Operating within the adverse outcome pathway paradigm,150 this integrated approach—spanning genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—characterizes stress responses and modes of action in biota from the molecular to the organismal level. Research in this area often utilizes Arabidopsis thaliana as a model system to investigate the toxicity resulting from exposure to metal-based nanopesticides, such as silver (Ag)151 and copper oxide (CuO).152 The incorporation of Venn networks, heatmaps, and gene correlation network analyses enables the identification of proteins and genes involved in metabolic alterations, stress responses, and detoxification mechanisms (e.g., DNA repair, protein synthesis, and reactive oxygen species production) induced by nanopesticide exposure. For instance, phytohormone-mediated defense responses regulated by ethylene, abscisic acid, and salicylic acid are encoded by genes such as SUB1, DRO1, and HKT1.124 These candidate molecules have the potential to serve as molecular biomarkers for assessing nanopesticide ecotoxicity across a broad range of complex organisms, from crop species to wildlife and humans.

Cellular analyses reveal that mitochondria and chloroplasts are the primary targets of metal-based nanopesticides in fungi, plants, and cultured human tissues. These organelles play crucial roles in energy metabolism and the modulation of reactive species for detoxification. The impairment of mitochondria and chloroplasts represents a conserved response to nanopesticide exposure, observed across a range of eukaryotic models, from simpler organisms to higher organisms and human cells.153,154 Therefore, integrating omics data provides valuable insights into how nanopesticides exert toxicity on complex organisms within various environmental ecosystems.

Regulatory framework and public acceptance

The growing prevalence and anticipated market introduction of various nanopesticides necessitate the establishment of a robust framework for the pre-screening, classification, and grouping of these substances based on shared hazard profiles. Such a framework is essential for effective risk characterization. Its development relies on identifying commonalities in physicochemical properties, toxicokinetic behavior, and ecotoxicological impacts, as illustrated by the tiered exposure–hazard–risk approach proposed by Kookana et al.97 Comprehensive evaluations of the exposure‒hazard‒risk continuum for nanopesticides, considering their life-cycle stages and primary environmental factors, are crucial.155 The resulting data will create a vital evidence base for regulatory authorities to formulate robust risk assessment and management protocols. The market viability of nano-agrifood products ultimately depends on societal perceptions and consumer acceptance. Safety concerns associated with food produced using nanopesticides involve a diverse coalition of stakeholders, including government agencies, policymakers, and the agricultural industry. Addressing these issues requires a collaborative, multi-stakeholder framework where strategic risk communication is crucial. Public sentiment tends to favor naturally derived solutions, such as biogenic active ingredients (e.g., essential oils) and nanocarriers (e.g., chitosan), which are perceived as safer alternatives and generally encounter less regulatory scrutiny due to their GRAS classification. Similarly, biosynthesized metallic nanoparticles (e.g., Ag and Cu) are preferred for integrated pest management and are well accepted by the public.156

Conclusion

Nanopesticides represent a smart technological innovation that enhances efficacy while protecting the atmosphere and human health. To ensure efficacy, a complete understanding of the mechanisms by which ENPs suppress disease, as well as an awareness of their consequences and fate in agroecosystems, is essential. Owing to difficulties in producing food and the lack of economic advantage in agriculture, only sustainable methods can be widely implemented. These data emphasize the importance of materials with enhanced functionality, sensitivity, and tunability to achieve higher efficiency. However, the application of ENPs offers several advantages over traditional agrichemicals, foremost among them reduced material requirements for food production. Improved solubility, increased adhesion and absorption to plant leaves, controlled and responsive release of active ingredients, targeted delivery, enhanced bioavailability and biodegradability, and improved stability of active ingredients in the environment are just a few of the formulation-specific properties that may contribute to these outcomes. Global food security and safety can be achieved and sustained through nano-enabled agrichemicals, provided their physicochemical properties are adjusted and optimized sustainably. This will contribute significantly to global socio-economic growth.

Future research directions

Nanopesticides have demonstrated positive effects on soil health and sustainability. More research is needed to find out how these products affect soil ecosystems and people's safety. This highlights the need for an in-depth understanding of the potential field effects of various nanopesticides to maximize water-use efficiency (WUE) and prevent cropland deterioration. Future studies must address several gaps to further develop our understanding of soil water dynamics in relation to the application of nanoformulated pesticides. Owing to differences in soil characteristics and environmental factors, the results vary between field and laboratory conditions. The factors and processes affecting the environmental behavior and effects of nanopesticides can differ from those of “conventional” pesticides, and therefore, new or refined risk assessment approaches are required. To fully understand the actions and impacts of nanopesticides, more extensive field research is needed across various management systems, soil types, and pedoclimatic conditions. Furthermore, careful preparation is required before applying nanopesticides on a broad scale to minimize disturbance to soil physical characteristics and ecosystems, thereby promoting a cleaner, greener environment.
