Osimertinib-induced acute decompensated heart failure: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Hou-Chen Lee, Meng-Heng Hsieh, Horng-Chyuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which is one of the most common cancer types, has caused lots of deaths to patients worldwide. Aside from the surgical treatment or chemotherapy, the third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), Osimertinib, is used as a treatment for patients with non-small cell lung cancer. However, the cardiotoxicity related to Osimertinib, which is rarely reported, is still a controversial issue to be discussed.Case presentationA 70-year-old Taiwanese female diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma had been with Osimertinib treatment for two years. During admission for a routine lung cancer staging workup, she developed acute-onset dyspnea with pulmonary edema. She did not present with any infection-related symptoms. The echocardiography revealed global hypokinesis, while a thallium scan showed no evidence of myocardial infarction. Her symptoms resolved gradually following the withdraw of Osimertinib.ConclusionWe report a case of acute decompensated heart failure in a patient with lung cancer who had been receiving Osimertinib therapy for two years. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the longest the interval reported between initiation of Osimertinib and the onset of cardiotoxicity. The diagnosis of Osimertinib-related heart failure was made by exclusion of the other etiologies, and the patient’s clinical symptoms recovered after cessation of Osimertinib for one week. Although the cause of Osimertinib-related cardiotoxicity is still unclear, regular cardiac examination of the patients should be considered.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Osimertinib, Heart failure, Cardiotoxicity, Case report

Introduction

The third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), Osimertinib, is used as part of first-line treatment in patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and is associated with significant improvements in outcomes [1].

Although a previous study did not suggest a causal relationship between Osimertinib and cardiac failure-related adverse events [2], an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that Osimertinib could result in cardiotoxicity [17], and further investigation of the adverse effects is required [3, 4]. We herein present a case of acute decompensated heart failure in a patient receiving Osimertinib treatment and review related cases that have been published.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old Taiwanese woman without cardiac risk factors was diagnosed with adenocarcinoma of the lung in 2017. Her initial presentation was abnormal image study at healthy checkup without any symptom such as cough or body weight loss. After complete workup, thoracoscopic wedge resection of right upper lobe with lymph nodes dissection was done. The surgical staging was T2N0M0 according to AJCC 7th edition. Given the presence of EGFR L858R mutation, oral Tarceva (Erlotinib 150 mg once daily) was given as adjuvant therapy for three years.

She was found to have recurrent adenocarcinoma of the lung with an EGFR mutation (L858R mutation in exon 21) in 2020, and subsequently initiated on Osimertinib 80 mg/day. Two years after Osimertinib initiation, she presented with acute-onset dyspnea. Her blood pressure decreased to 68/53 mmHg. She was hypoxic at 89% despite of 6 L/minute of oxygen supplementation and was started on bilevel positive airway pressure therapy. The chest X-ray showed bilateral pulmonary edema (Fig. 1). The B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) was elevated above 4700 pg/ml, the echocardiography revealed an ejection fraction of 31%, with global hypokinesis and left atrial dilation, and a diagnosis of acute decompensated heart failure was made. The electrocardiogram revealed T wave inversion in leads V2 and V3, and the troponin-I level was 0.219 ng/ml. (lower than 0.3 ng/ml). Norepinephrine was infused, furosemide was started, and Osimertinib treatment was then stopped.

The chest radiograph shows findings consistent with pulmonary edema

Myocardial perfusion imaging via a thallium-201 scan revealed no evidence of myocardial infarction or ischemia (Fig. 2). Moreover, she had taken no medications associated with cardiotoxicity. There was no other identifiable cause that lead to her heart failure apart from Osimertinib treatment. The patient’s symptoms were recovered within one week after cessation of Osimertinib. Single oral Osimertinib was continued until 2023 though local recurrence was suspected by image in 2022 October. Multiple metastasis and worsening respiratory symptoms developed later. Single Pemetrexed was given in 2023 June. The patient passed away on September 15, 2023.

Discussion

A literature review was conducted using the PubMed database to identify previously reported cases of Osimertinib-associated cardiotoxicity up to July 2025. The following keywords were used: “lung adenocarcinoma”, “Osimertinib”, “cardiotoxicity”, “heart failure” and “cardiomyopathy.” Articles were screened based on titles and abstracts, and relevant full-text articles were reviewed. Cases with reported cardiac adverse events related to Osimertinib were included for analysis.

Following the phase III FLAURA study [1], Osimertinib has been used as part of the first line agent for EGFR mutant NSCLC with improved outcomes. However, a study of retrospective review revealed that the incidence of cardiotoxicity caused by Osimertinib was greater than that caused by other TKI [5]. Here, we present a case of acute decompensated heart failure related to Osimertinib administration and review the cases of Osimertinib related cardiotoxicity which have been published (Table 1). Currently, there are no gold standard diagnostic criteria for Osimertinib-associated cardiotoxicity, and a diagnosis of exclusion is needed.

Although previous reports have described structural cardiac changes, including left atrial or left ventricular dilation, in patients receiving Osimertinib [3, 8, 9, 12, 13], other specific findings are needed to help clinicians evaluate these patients. The laboratory data of BNP or N-Terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-ProBNP) may be helpful; however, the cardiotoxicity related to Osimertinib not only causes heart failure but also other cardiac dysfunctions such as QT prolongation or arrhythmia are also reported [13, 15]. A study from Japan have performed myocardial biopsy, and the specific pathologic features are still under investigation [4]. The procedure used to obtain biopsy samples may be invasive, and the risks as well as complications should be considered. Our patient had no clinical symptoms of virus infection and no past history of autoimmune diseases or endocrine disorders. The thallium scan revealed that there was no evidence of ischemic heart disease. After the exclusion of identifiable causes, we concluded that the heart failure was caused by Osimertinib.

A previous study [5] indicated that Osimertinib was associated with an increased risk of cardiotoxicity compared with the control arm (other EGFR-TKIs). A total of 315 cardiac adverse events were noted, 150 (6.1%) due to Osimertinib, 8 (5%) due to Osimertinib combined with other TKIs, and 157 (2.7%) due to other TKIs. Our patient received Erlotinib as first-line treatment and Osimertinib as subsequent second-line therapy. Whether cardiac-related adverse events are greater in first-line or second-line Osimertinib monotherapy needs further investigation.

Cardiotoxicity related to anticancer agents can be classified into two types: type 1 involves irreversible and dose-dependent injury to the heart such as anthracyclines, and type 2 involves reversible and dose-independent injury, which is related to the interruption of the HER2 signaling pathway, such as Trastuzumab [6]. Animal models have shown stronger inhibition of HER2 with Osimertinib than with other TKIs [7], and the mechanism of this effect requires further study. The duration of onset of cardiac toxicity after taking Osimertinib is variable, ranging from weeks to years. A study indicated that there is a dynamic echocardiographic change following Osimertinib treatment [18]. In our case, it takes two years to have the event. To our knowledge, it is longer than other published cases. While the risk factors associated with the Osimertinib-related cardiac toxicity are still unknown, among the reported cases summarized in Table 1, 20 of 24 patients (83.3%) were female, suggesting a possible female predominance in Osimertinib-associated cardiotoxicity.

After the withdrawal of Osimertinib for a period of time, several strategies have been developed to restart target therapy for these patients. These include rechallenge the patients with a full dose or half dose of Osimertinib every day [4, 9, 12], as well as switching to the first-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors [4, 10]. To our knowledge, there is no standard guidance for clinicians to manage this situation, and the prognosis or quality of life associated with different strategies is still lacking. Currently, the assessment of decompensated heart failure relies largely on clinical judgement. In this case, the patient’s symptoms including dyspnea was improved during outpatient follow-up, indicating clinical stabilization and recovery of cardiac function. Based on this improvement, a cautious rechallenge with Osimertinib was undertaken. Our patient was treated with a full dose of Osimertinib every other day for two months. Given the patient’s clinical condition and quality of life, cisplatin-based chemotherapy was not considered appropriate. After discussing with the patient and her family, full-dose daily Osimertinib was reinstated. With respect to strategies to restart TKI or not, it is also important to share decision-making with patients. Furthermore, regular examinations to monitor heart function could be arranged. On the other hand, clinicians should also pay attention to this rare but serious complication when patients receive Osimertinib therapy.

Conclusion

Although the mechanisms and risk factors for Osimertinib-induced cardiotoxicity remain to be studied, as demonstrated by this patient, we conclude that the cardiotoxicity caused by Osimertinib might be reversible by dose reduction and that the time of onset is variable; therefore, regular cardiac examinations, such as electrocardiogram or echocardiography should be considered for patients receiving Osimertinib treatment.
