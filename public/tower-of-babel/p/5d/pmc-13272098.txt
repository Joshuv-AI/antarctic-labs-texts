Perception, Knowledge and Attitudes About Organ Donation in Nursing Students at the University of Málaga
Authors: Jorge Vallejo‐Báez, Antonio Zamudio‐Sánchez, Adolfo Romero‐Arana, Ana Rebollo‐Rubio, Adolfo Romero‐Ruiz

Introduction

Organ transplants are currently the most effective treatment for end‐stage organ failure and are sometimes the only option. They improve the life expectancy of patients who depend on treatments that seriously compromise their quality of life, such as dialysis (Antoun et al. 2023). The World Health Organization (WHO), however, defines organ and tissue donation as the process by which a living or deceased person donates their organs or tissues for transplantation purposes. Thsis decision must always be voluntary and altruistic, with the sole objective of treating diseases and improving survival rates or quality of life (Principios rectores de la OMS sobre trasplante de células, tejidos y órganos humanos 2010). In this context, Spain is a world leader in organizing donations from deceased individuals per million inhabitants, a position it has held for 30 years. However, there is room for improvement in tissue donation (Registro Mundial de Trasplantes 2024; Donación de Tejidos 2024).

In Spain, donation and transplantation are regulated by Law 30/1979 and Royal Decree (RD) 1723/2012. These regulations, which have been in force for more than 40 years, cover donations from both living and deceased donors. They stipulate that donations must be made without financial conditions and solely for the purpose of transplantation (Ley Española de Trasplantes 2024; Real Decreto 1723/2012 2012).

Spanish law presumes that all Spanish citizens consent to organ donation upon their death unless they have expressly stated otherwise. However, reality differs from what is legally established because, in addition to ascertaining the deceased's wishes regarding organ donation or the absence of an opinion against it, an agreement is always sought with the family through a procedure called a ‘family interview for donation’ to obtain up‐to‐date information (Familia y Donación de Órganos 2007). Notably, the family may decide not to donate, even if the deceased has not expressed any opposition. In such cases, organ removal is usually not performed, as it could cause public alarm and have a negative effect on organ donation in general (Frutos and Blanca 1995).

In Spain, the loss of donors as a result of refusals expressed during interviews with the families of potential donors was 18% in 2023 (Actividad de donación y trasplante en 2023 2024). While this figure may seem low, it equates to the loss of over 500 donors, and nearly 1500 transplants are not performed per year (de la Rosa et al. 2012). This situation significantly impacts the health of many people awaiting transplants, as it can seriously compromise their chances of survival. In this context, training healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and doctors, to optimise information and social awareness of organ and tissue donation is considered very important (Matesanz 2008; Ríos 2018; ONT 2024; Frutos 1999).

Studies have been conducted both within and outside our borders to ascertain the opinions, perceptions, and attitudes toward donation held by different sectors of society, including healthcare professionals. Given that the first link in the chain of donations is to achieve an adequate level of knowledge and attitudes in these individuals (Matesanz 2008), it seems appropriate to extend these questions to undergraduate students, who will soon be responsible for these tasks as healthcare professionals. Therefore, it is important to understand whether their opinions on organ donation are based on accurate information, positive news, or ‘fake news’—that is, false or misleading news that spreads quickly on social media—and whether they have adequate knowledge of the process (Zanatta et al. 1992; Mento et al. 2023).

Various studies have shown that there is a statistically significant relationship between knowledge and attitudes toward organ and tissue donation among healthcare personnel, including students. These results may be due to undergraduate training. For example, medical students may have a better grasp of the concept of brain death and attitudes toward donation than nursing students (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredád, et al. 2019; Krucińska et al. 2020; McGlade and Pierscionek 2013). However, to gain a better understanding of this issue, further research is needed to investigate the nuances that have not yet been reported. In some cases, questionnaires have been used, particularly among medical students. The main finding was that students demanded specific training in organ donation and transplantation (Krucińska et al. 2020; McGlade and Pierscionek 2013), an issue also raised by nursing students (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredád, et al. 2019). Qualitative approaches have also been employed, such as in Lei's study (Jones‐Riffle and Stoeckle 1998), which used in‐depth interviews.

In light of the above, this study aimed to explore the postseminar level of knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes toward organ donation among second‐year nursing students at the University of Málaga, Faculty of Health Sciences, using focus groups as a qualitative research tool.

Materials and Methods

Design

This study used an exploratory qualitative design with a phenomenological eidetic approach. This approach was considered appropriate because the aim was not to quantify students' knowledge or attitudes but to explore how nursing students perceived, interpreted, and gave meaning to organ donation after the seminar. The eidetic orientation sought to identify the essential meanings underlying participants' discourse across the focus groups. To support this approach, bracketing was used as a reflexive tool throughout data collection and analysis. Before and during the analytic process, the researchers explicitly reflected on and set aside, as far as possible, their own prior assumptions, professional experiences, and expectations regarding organ donation and nursing education. This reflexive bracketing helped preserve the participants' perspectives as the central source of interpretation. The focus groups were subsequently analysed using inductive thematic analysis, which allowed recurrent meanings and shared experiential patterns to be identified from the data.

Scope of Study

Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Nursing and Podiatry, University of Málaga.

Study Subjects and Sample

Students from the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Málaga who were enrolled in the second year of the Nursing Degree during the 2022–2023 academic year constituted the eligible population. Convenience sampling was used because the study was embedded in scheduled Adult Nursing I seminar groups.

Sample

A total of 152 students were enrolled in Adult Nursing I and were therefore eligible to be invited. An invitation to participate was sent through the Moodle platform. Of these, 18 declined to participate, and 14 did not attend class on the day of data collection, resulting in a final sample of 120 students. Thus, 152 refers to the eligible course cohort, whereas 120 refers to the students who provided written consent and completed the focus group procedure. All participating students received a specific seminar on organ donation before the focus groups.

The students' ages ranged from 19 to 36. By gender, the distribution was 24 men and 96 women. No one reported having had previous personal experience with organ donation, and only 12 reported having had such experience with family members or friends.

Instrument or Technique

Focus groups were selected as the technique to approach this research. This technique was chosen due to the special configuration of the students' groups when they attended the seminars, which allowed the researchers to select according to number, sex and age distribution. The focus groups were held in a seminar room of the Faculty of Health Sciences. Each group lasted between 60 and 90 min.

Data Collection and Procedure

From October to November 2022, 12 focus groups were organized with between eight and 12 students, depending on attendance in each seminar group. The focus groups were conducted after a specific seminar on organ donation; consequently, the study should be interpreted as an exploration of students' posttraining reflections rather than as a baseline assessment of knowledge. A male faculty member involved in teaching acted as moderator, and a male external researcher attended the sessions as an observer and recorded the groups. To reduce potential power dynamics and social desirability, participation was voluntary, students were reminded that their decision would have no academic consequences, and the moderator emphasized that there were no correct or incorrect answers.

The three questions that guided the groups were ‘What is your perception of organ donation?’; ‘Do you consider yourselves to be well informed on this topic?’; and ‘If you or your close relatives needed to donate, what would you do?’. Finally, gender inequalities in living donation were discussed. Before the sessions began, the students were asked to provide their written consent to participate in the study. Eighteen declined to participate, and fourteen did not attend; the rest completed the entire procedure. The participants were informed that the sessions would be recorded for subsequent discourse analysis.

All focus groups were conducted in Spanish. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Spanish and analysed in the original language. The illustrative quotations selected for the manuscript were translated into English by a bilingual member of the research team and then reviewed by a second bilingual researcher to preserve semantic equivalence. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, prioritising conceptual meaning over literal wording.

The coded viewpoints were then collected to write detailed and exhaustive descriptions and identify whether there were similar views. Repetitive viewpoints were deleted to sublime the theme and were considered information saturation.

With respect to doubtful content, return visits were paid to verify whether the record was consistent with the interviewee's views. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data analysis, other members of the project team reviewed the extracts to confirm that the content could truthfully reflect the thoughts and attitudes of the interviewees.

Data Analysis

After the focus groups were conducted, the data were transcribed verbatim and analysed using an inductive thematic approach (Data S2). Coding was not based on a predefined theoretical framework; instead, initial codes were generated from repeated readings of the transcripts. Three researchers first coded the transcripts independently and then compared coding decisions in joint meetings. Differences were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and a shared preliminary coding framework was then applied to the remaining material. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into candidate themes, which were reviewed against the original transcripts and refined until the final categories were agreed upon. This coding process followed the principles of Taylor and Bogdan (Taylor and Bogdan 1992). Data saturation was considered to have been reached when no new codes emerged in successive focus groups, following Urquhart's definition as ‘the point in coding when you find that no new codes occur in the data’.

Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis method was employed through the following phases: familiarization with the transcripts, generation of initial codes, organization of codes into preliminary themes, review of candidate themes, definition and naming of themes, and preparation of the final report (Braun and Clarke 2006). Guba and Lincoln's four pillars of reliability were also considered: credibility (the authenticity of the findings), transferability (the possibility of applying the results to similar contexts), dependability (the stability of the findings, regardless of time and context), and confirmability (an objective study) (Ahmed 2024).

Data were registered using Microsoft Excel, with separate sheets for each researcher involved in coding and a final consensus sheet documenting the agreed codes, categories, and analytic decisions.

Rigour and Trustworthiness

The rigour of the qualitative process was addressed according to Guba and Lincoln's criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Ahmed 2024). Credibility was strengthened through investigator triangulation, repeated reading of the transcripts, consensus meetings among the three coders, and verification of doubtful content against the original recordings. The use of verbatim quotations in the Results section was intended to preserve the participants' own meanings and to allow readers to assess the link between the data and the interpretations.

Transferability was supported by describing the setting, participants, sampling procedure, and focus group context in sufficient detail for readers to judge the applicability of the findings to similar nursing education settings. Dependability was reinforced by keeping an audit trail that included the interview guide, recordings, transcripts, coding sheets, successive versions of the coding framework, and records of consensus decisions. Confirmability was promoted by maintaining the connection between codes, quotations, categories, and the final interpretation while recognizing the researchers' role in the production and interpretation of qualitative data.

Reflexivity was particularly relevant because the moderator was a faculty member involved in teaching. To minimize the influence of this relationship, the voluntary nature of participation was emphasized, students were assured that their responses would not affect academic evaluation, and an external researcher observed the sessions. Nevertheless, this potential influence was retained as a limitation of the study.

A subsequent analysis was carried out on the most frequently repeated phrases, which were considered the most relevant. The aim was to categorize them into the following sections: lack of knowledge about the donation process; improving training, generosity and solidarity; legacy; and the influence of gender factors.

Lack of Knowledge

It should be noted that there is a widespread perception of ignorance about the donation process [I have a relative who has had a transplant, but I had no idea about the subject…]. The lack of accessible and understandable information on how and why to donate represents a significant barrier for students when making decisions about organ donation [all future nurses will be among the professionals who encourage people to become donors…]. The need for clear and accessible information campaigns is considered a priority, for which various communication channels should be used to reach different segments of the population [we have many ways of reaching society, discussing it with family, friends and through social media, etc.]. This theme did not simply reflect the absence of information; rather, students described uncertainty about the practical steps involved in donation, the role of families, and the professional responsibility they expected to assume as future nurses. The discourse therefore connected personal insecurity with an anticipated clinical role.

Improving Training

The participants emphasized the importance of improving training for healthcare professionals, especially with respect to handling complex situations such as brain death and nonheart‐beating donation [it is very important that once we finish our degree, we are able to answer questions on this fascinating topic…]. Participants framed training as a prerequisite for safe and confident communication with patients and families, particularly in emotionally demanding scenarios. In this sense, the request for education was not limited to theoretical knowledge but also included communication skills, legal aspects, and clinical decision‐making around donation pathways.

Furthermore, healthcare professionals, especially nurses, play a key role in motivating and supporting families throughout the donation process. It is also recommended that the subject of donation be an integral part of university and continuing education for healthcare professionals [it is very important to inform healthcare professionals like us because, at least in my case, I did not know anything about this subject, I had no information on it…].

On a social level, the group studied perceives the need for more active and accessible dissemination of information about organ donation [the world should be aware of the importance of organ donation…]. The participants suggested that information should be accessible not only through traditional means such as leaflets but also through family conversations and the use of social media.

Generosity and Solidarity

The data obtained highlight that generosity and solidarity are key motivating factors for people to consider organ donation. The participants emphasized the value of organ donation as an altruistic act that saves lives [we must be generous because we no longer need them (the organs when we die) …] and suggested reinforcing this message in awareness campaigns. In addition, the idea of reciprocity, i.e., the possibility of needing a transplant in the future or the close experience of a family member who needs one, was also identified as an important motivational factor [helping other people in the same way that I may also need help…]. These accounts show that favourable attitudes were anchored in moral values and imagined reciprocity, but they also depended on whether students felt sufficiently informed to transform a general positive disposition into an informed decision.

Legacy

The concept of donation as a form of legacy and continuing to live through donated organs was another motivation for some participants [it is important to reflect on this, above all because it is a way for deceased people to continue living…]. Encouraging family dialogue about donation wishes and highlighting this important issue in campaigns can encourage decision‐making in this area, as reflected in [it is important to discuss your wishes beforehand…].

Gender Factors

Finally, the influence of gender factors on the willingness to donate during one's lifetime was identified. Women, in particular, are perceived as more predisposed to donate, influenced by biological and social aspects [after all, a mother has a special feeling…]. The study sample also considered that it is necessary to further explore the reasons behind this gender difference and to promote equitable reflection on donation decision‐making without reinforcing stereotypes [family pressure because women are always caring…]. This finding was interpreted cautiously: the focus groups revealed perceived gendered expectations around care and family responsibility, but the study was not designed to establish gender differences in donation behaviour.

Discussion

Analysis of the data obtained seems to indicate a lack of training in organ donation, centered, according to participants' comments, on a lack of knowledge about the donation process, as well as the need to improve training for both students and professionals, as reported in other studies (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredá, et al. 2019; Ríos et al. 2017; García‐Galicia et al. 2023; Chabalewski et al. 2002; Hakeem et al. 2021). Recent evidence supports this interpretation. Alwahaibi et al. reported low knowledge among Omani university students and identified lack of knowledge as a major obstacle to favourable donation attitudes (Alwahaibi et al. 2023). Similarly, Hamdan et al. showed that a structured educational intervention improved allied health students' knowledge and attitudes toward organ donation, reinforcing the educational relevance of our findings (Hamdan et al. 2026). Hanifa et al. also found generally positive attitudes among healthcare students in Syria while highlighting the role of information sources and broader contextual trust in the healthcare system (Hanifa et al. 2026).

Because all participants had received a seminar before the focus groups, the findings should be interpreted as post‐training reflections. This prior exposure may have sensitized students to the importance of donation and may partly explain the favourable tone of their attitudes. However, it also allowed students to articulate more specific gaps in knowledge, particularly regarding legal procedures, brain death, family communication, and professional preparedness.

For many years, the important role that nurses and nursing students can play in organ donation has been highlighted (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredá, et al. 2019). Therefore, exploring this aspect is considered a relevant strategy for increasing knowledge on the subject and delving deeper into the factors that may influence the opinions, attitudes, and concerns of nurses and nursing students. Following this line of argument, in a very similar scenario, medical students were fully aware of the importance of including a specific course on organ donation, which is the same opinion held by our students. This point could be considered striking, given that both the geographical and cultural contexts are very different (Lei et al. 2019).

In line with previous studies, our results show that participants clearly demand specific training in organ donation. However, studies using scales and questionnaires (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredá, et al. 2019; Ríos et al. 2017; García‐Galicia et al. 2023; Chabalewski et al. 2002; Hakeem et al. 2021; Lei et al. 2019; Espinoza‐Espinoza et al. 2024) predominate over qualitative studies (Hakeem et al. 2021; Lei et al. 2019). It is important for students to report feelings of insecurity about their future role in the organ donation process. Indeed, Hakeem et al. demonstrated interest in including this subject in the doctors' curriculum, given their knowledge deficit but positive attitudes toward donation, as we previously comment in the paragraph immediately above (Lei et al. 2019).

In this sense, Lei et al. reported that future doctors urgently needed knowledge about donation procedures and regulations, indicating a widespread demand for a formal course on the subject, although their source was a group of medicine students (Lei et al. 2019). Our data, which were collected from nursing students, reveal a lack of structured information in this degree program's curriculum: most students admitted to being unaware of details such as how to register as a donor, the definition of brain death, and other relevant legal protocols, and this coincides with other studies in our country, only 18% of Spanish.

Nursing students believed that their knowledge about organ donation and transplantation is adequate and good (Martínez‐Alarcón, Ríos, Santainés‐Borredá, et al. 2019; Espinoza‐Espinoza et al. 2024). In other countries, there seems to be a similar distribution.

However, this knowledge gap is not unique to our study; for example, Espinoza‐Espinoza et al. reported that over half of the university students interviewed were unaware of the current donation law, despite an overwhelmingly positive attitude toward donation (more than 95% were in favour) (Espinoza‐Espinoza et al. 2024).

Notably, while most participants in this study considered participation in the process to be a nursing responsibility, they also felt ill prepared to take on this task in the near future. These findings are consistent with those of Cerrato (Cerrato et al. 2017), who reported a relationship between knowledge and attitudes toward organ donation, including knowledge of brain death, which led to greater support for organ donation. This relationship reinforces the need to include organ donation and transplantation training in nursing curricula. Furthermore, the need for better strategies to convey information about the donation and transplantation process was emphasized, which aligns with another study in which students expressed a preference for online resources and social media to obtain information about organ donation (Gürkan and Kahraman 2018). This is consistent with Alwahaibi et al., who found that online sources and social networks were the main sources of information for 84.13% of students, suggesting that formal curricula may compete with noninstitutional information channels (Alwahaibi et al. 2023).

Regarding motivations for donation, ethical considerations and cross‐cultural differences were presented [When you die, it is a legacy you can leave behind]. The group recognized that altruism and the desire to save lives were central motivations. In line with previous findings, most of those studies stated that organ donation seemed to be an act of solidarity and even a future professional duty. This finding coincides with the willingness to donate observed in other studies, in which community values and social responsibility often emerge as the main reasons for donating (Hakeem et al. 2021; Lei et al. 2019; Espinoza‐Espinoza et al. 2024). The participants also expressed personal motivations, such as having experienced transplants within their family or knowing donors personally. These life stories reinforced their individual commitment. This more intimate, testimonial dimension was less explored in other studies, probably because of their curriculum‐oriented approach. In any case, the most notable finding is that, in both contexts, future healthcare professionals feel prepared to advocate donation if they receive the appropriate training.

A favourable attitude toward donation was detected in both healthcare contexts, although a lack of training was evident, suggesting that specific topics should be incorporated into the curriculum. This gap is perhaps more striking in Spain, where a system of ‘presumed consent’ facilitates donation, highlighting the importance of strengthening the educational component of healthcare training. The categories identified, however, are grouped around two specific constructs, training and values, as they do not have a material entity that is directly observable (Figure 1; Lei et al. 2019; Cerrato et al. 2017).

In short, our findings align with those of other studies (Hakeem et al. 2021; Lei et al. 2019; Cerrato et al. 2017) in emphasizing the importance of strengthening education on organ donation. Students support the idea of specific activities to stimulate interest and improve donation rates—a recommendation that aligns with the needs expressed in our focus groups. Overall, these studies emphasize the need to incorporate practical and regulatory content on donation into the nursing curriculum to promote informed attitudes and altruistic motivations in future generations of healthcare professionals. In this sense, it is considered a valuable strategy to improve teacher training to help students learn, as previously reported (Gürkan and Kahraman 2018; Sancho‐Álvarez et al. 2016).

Notably, there are similarities across qualitative and quantitative studies, despite differences in cultural and healthcare contexts. This convergence suggests that limited knowledge, the perceived need for formal training, and the presence of favourable but sometimes insufficiently informed attitudes are recurrent issues among healthcare students. Therefore, the information obtained suggests that formal, continuous, cross‐disciplinary training in organ donation, complemented by active methodologies such as simulations, workshops, and testimonials, could improve knowledge and consolidate professional attitudes and skills aligned with equity, solidarity, and the humanization of care. In the Spanish presumed‐consent context, such training may be relevant for strengthening future professional preparedness; however, any direct effect on family refusal rates or transplant outcomes should be tested in future intervention studies rather than inferred from the present exploratory findings (Alwahaibi et al. 2023; Hamdan et al. 2026; Hanifa et al. 2026).

Therefore, a specific training course could be useful for increasing awareness about this topic, communicating adequate information, and providing familiarity with organ and tissue donation. Nurses will need to have not only a solid background but also an increasing supply of new information that addresses the advances (Chabalewski et al. 2002).

These results should be considered when planning future training programs. This study is consistent with our data, which also emphasizes that teaching should offer experiences close to the clinical environment, not be solely theoretical.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study is its qualitative focus on nursing students' perceptions, allowing the analysis to move beyond questionnaire scores and to identify how students construct meanings around knowledge, professional responsibility, altruism, legacy, and gender. The use of 12 focus groups, three coders, consensus‐based thematic analysis, and a trustworthiness framework strengthens the credibility and transparency of the findings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a single university and used convenience sampling, which limits transferability to other educational and cultural contexts. Second, the sample was predominantly female, reflecting the gender distribution of the course but potentially shaping the discourse, especially regarding gendered expectations of care and living donation. Third, all participants received a seminar before the focus groups; consequently, the results represent postseminar reflections and cannot be interpreted as baseline knowledge or as evidence of the seminar's effectiveness. Fourth, because the moderator was a faculty member, social desirability and power dynamics may have influenced students' responses, despite efforts to emphasize voluntariness and confidentiality. Fifth, the focus groups were conducted in Spanish and translated for publication in English, so some nuances may have been affected despite bilingual review. Finally, the study identifies perceived educational needs and potential implications for professional training but does not measure subsequent behaviour, family refusal rates, or transplant‐system outcomes.

Conclusions

This exploratory qualitative study suggests that second‐year nursing students perceive important gaps in their knowledge of organ donation, even when they express broadly favourable attitudes toward donation. Their discourse indicates that education should address not only general awareness but also the practical, legal, ethical, and communicative dimensions of the donation process.

Specific training programs on organ donation and transplantation may therefore be useful in nursing education and, potentially, in other health science degrees. Such programs should include active methodologies, discussion of real clinical scenarios, communication with families, and critical engagement with misinformation and social media sources.

These findings suggest that organ donation education could strengthen nursing students' preparedness for future clinical nursing practice, particularly in relation to patient and family communication, health education, and professional involvement in donation pathways. However, effects on family refusal rates or transplant‐system outcomes should be examined in subsequent studies rather than inferred directly from this exploratory qualitative work.

The broader impact of these educational strategies should be considered a potential implication rather than a direct outcome of the present study. Future research should evaluate whether structured training improves knowledge, attitudes, communication confidence, and, ultimately, clinically relevant outcomes within donation and transplantation systems.
