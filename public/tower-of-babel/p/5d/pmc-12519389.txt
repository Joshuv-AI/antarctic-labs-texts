Steatotic liver disease and cancer: from pathogenesis to therapeutic targets
Authors: Zhihong Yang, Zhenjie Liu, Wanqing Liu, Xiaocheng Charlie Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs environmental exposomes are changing with time, so are liver diseases around the globe. Due to an increasing prevalence of overnutrition and sedentary lifestyle in the global population, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and steatohepatitis have been increasing steadily in the past decades. Alcohol consumption is another common risk factor for liver diseases such as alcohol-associated liver disease or hepatitis. For both alcohol-associated and metabolic dysfunction-associated liver diseases, hepatocyte injuries are among the early events during the development of steatotic liver disease (SLD). Hepatic inflammation and fibrosis ensue with recurring hepatic damages owing to immune responses from multiple immune cell types, particularly neutrophils, Kupffer cells and monocyte-derived macrophages in response to damage-associated and pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and extracellular matrix production predominantly from hepatic stellate cells. Both environmental and genetic factors contribute to the SLD pathogenesis. Common environmental risk factors include obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, unhealthy diets, sedentary lifestyle and excessive alcohol consumption. Numerous genome-wide association studies have identified multiple genetic variants associated with key traits of SLD, including patatin-like phospholipase domain containing 3 rs738409, transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 rs58542926, membrane bound O-acyltransferase domain containing 7 rs641738 and hydroxysteroid 17 beta-dehydrogenase 13 rs72613567. Cirrhosis and liver cancer are common late-stage developments of various chronic liver diseases; however, there are pathological differences according to disease aetiologies. Therefore, preventive and therapeutic intervention strategies should align with the underlying causal and modifiable factors. Currently, multiple therapeutics have been developed or approved for treatment of SLD, including thyroid hormone receptor β agonists, glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists and fibroblast growth factor 21 analogues. Introduction

Chronic liver diseases (CLDs) are very common in the general population, accounting for over two million deaths in the world annually.1 Cirrhosis and liver cancer are usually terminal outcomes of CLDs. There are various aetiologies of CLDs, including viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV)), alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) or hepatitis (AH), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) or steatohepatitis (MASH). This review primarily covers the most common CLDs—MASLD/MASH, ALD/AH and liver cancer.

The prevalence of MASLD has increased remarkably in recent decades, estimated to be approximately 32–38% globally.2 The MASLD prevalence varies by region, sex and race in the world. The regional prevalence of MASLD was 44% in Latin America, 37% in the Middle East, 34% in South Asia, 33% in South-East Asia, 31% in North America, 30% in East Asia and 25% in Western Europe, according to a meta-analysis of population-based studies from 1990 to 2019.3 African populations have a prevalence of MASLD at 28.2% in another meta-analysis.4 Globally, the prevalence of MASLD is higher in males (39.7%) than in females (25.6%).2 MASH is a progressive and more severe form of MASLD, which manifests as elevated hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. Approximately 20–30% of patients with MASLD progress to MASH.5 It is estimated that 4–7% of the global population lives with MASH.6 Approximately 0.5–2.6% of patients with MASH-associated cirrhosis may develop liver cancer.5

It has been estimated that approximately 43% of the global population consumes some alcoholic beverage.1 Depending on the amount and frequency of alcohol consumption, drinkers can either maintain relative health or develop acute AH or chronic ALD. It is estimated that ALD accounts for 5.1% of all diseases and injuries in the world.7 In a cross-sectional analysis of 40 189 patients in the UK Biobank, among patients with steatotic liver disease (SLD), 89% had MASLD, 7.9% had metabolic dysfunction and ALD (MetALD), 1% had ALD and 2.2% had at-risk MASH.8 Approximately 90–95% heavy alcohol drinkers develop SLD.9 Approximately 10–35% of patients with SLD and heavy alcohol consumption may progress to alcohol-associated steatohepatitis (ASH). It is estimated that 10–20% of patients with ASH progress to fibrosis or even cirrhosis.7 Approximately 3–10% of patients with alcohol-associated liver cirrhosis (ALC) progress to liver cancer.9

According to the estimates by the Global Cancer Observatory, there were 866 136 new cases and 758 725 deaths of liver cancer in 2022, respectively.10 The majority of liver cancers in adults are hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for approximately 85%.11 The aetiology of liver cancer has shifted in the recent decades due to changes in major risk factors from traditionally viral infections to metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver diseases. Except for Central American countries where liver cancer rates are high, many other populations have twofold to fivefold higher rates of HCC in men than in women.10 Historically, HCC incidence and mortality rates have been much higher in East Asia and South Asia than in other regions in the world. In 2022, the highest age-standardised HCC rates in the globe occurred in Mongolia with 116 new cases in males and 81 in females per 100 000 persons.10

MASLD and MASH pathogenesis

MASLD is defined by presence of hepatic steatosis (>5% liver weight as fat or >5% hepatocytes infiltrated with steatosis by imaging or biopsy) plus at least one of the following five criteria in adults: (1) Body mass index ≥25 kg/m2 (23 for Asians) or waist circumference >94 cm for males and 80 cm for females or ethnicity adjusted equivalent; (2) Fasting blood glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L or 2-hour postload glucose levels ≥7.8 mmol/L or glycated haemoglobin ≥5.7% or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) or treatment for T2DM; (3) Blood pressure ≥130/85 mm Hg or specific antihypertensive drug treatment; (4) Plasma triglycerides (TG) ≥1.7 mmol/L or lipid lowering treatment; (5) Plasma high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol ≤1.0 mmol/L for males and ≤1.3 mmol/L for females or lipid lowering treatment (figure 1).12 There are multiple risk factors for MASLD, including genetic variants, obesity, T2DM, overnutrition, unhealthy diets and sedentary lifestyle (figure 2A).

Genetic variants play a significant role in the aetiology of MASLD. Thus far, numerous genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple loci showing association with MASLD (figure 2B). Among those genes, the patatin-like phospholipase domain containing 3 (PNPLA3) 148I>M (rs738409 C>G) variant has been shown as the single largest genetic risk factor broadly associated with MASLD in various genetic studies. A major molecular mechanism underlying this genetic variant involves functional alterations of PNPLA3 in lipid droplet metabolism in both hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). The PNPLA3 148M variant has been shown to have a loss of its lipid hydrolase function.13 Further, this PNPLA3 variant has been shown to be resistant to ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation,14 15 for example, by an E3 ubiquitin ligase bifunctional apoptosis regulator,16 17 and competitive binding to abhydrolase domain containing 5 (ABHD5, or known as CGI-58) with adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL, or PNPLA2) on lipid droplet. This leads to a decrease in lipolysis.18 In addition, the PNPLA3 148M variant has also been implicated in retention of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in hepatic TG and impairment of PUFA flux to phospholipids for very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)-TG secretion.19 20 One study has shown that the PNPLA3 148M variant increases accumulation of retinols in lipid droplets of HSCs.21 Thus far, the PNPLA3 148M variant is consistently associated with MASLD, MASH, liver cirrhosis, and liver cancer.

Other genetic variants associated with MASLD and MASH include a missense polymorphism 167E>K in the transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) gene, a 446P>L missense variant in the glucokinase regulator (GCKR), a 3′-flanking variant rs641738 C>T nearby the membrane bound O-acyltransferase domain containing 7 (MBOAT7) gene and two splicing variants (rs72613567 T>TA and rs62305723 G>A) in the hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13) gene (figure 2B).

TM6SF2 encodes a transmembrane protein that shows an essential role in lipidation and secretion of VLDL particles from hepatocytes. The 167E>K variant of TM6SF2 seems to impair this function and leads to a decrease in secretion of large TG-rich VLDL particles from the liver.22 GCKR is a negative regulator of glucokinase (GCK), a rate-limiting enzyme in hepatic glycolysis. GCKR binds to GCK and sequestrates it during the fasting state. In the postprandial state, GCKR releases GCK to promote glucose uptake, glycolysis and de novo lipogenesis. The 446P>L alteration leads to a partial function loss of GCKR for regulating GCK, leading to increased glucose metabolism and TG accumulation in the liver.23 MBOAT7 is an acyltransferase that modulates the acyl-chain composition of phosphatidylinositols by catalysing the transfer of arachidonyl-CoA to lysophosphatidylinositol. While it is unclear at present the causal mechanism of the genetic variant rs641738 C>T in the regulation of MBOAT7 function, hepatic deficiency of MBOAT7 in mice demonstrated impaired lysosomal lipid homeostasis and increased risk for MASLD and MASH.24 HSD17B13 encodes a lipid droplet protein that has been implicated in lipid and retinol metabolism.25 The rs72613567 T > TA variant gives rise to an insertion of A at the splicing site near exon 6 of the human HSD17B13 gene, resulting in a truncated, non-functional protein.26 This variant has been shown to have a protective effect against liver steatosis, MASH as well as fibrosis and inflammation in multiple genetic studies.26

Obesity and diabetes are among the major risk factors for MASLD. It is estimated that approximately 75% people with obesity may have MASLD.27 A meta-analysis of 156 studies has shown that the prevalence of MASLD in T2DM is 65%.28 In another meta-analysis, it has been found that obesity is associated with a 20% increase in risk of severe liver disease outcomes and T2DM increases the risk by 225%.29 A common denominator of obesity and T2DM is insulin resistance. In white adipose tissue, insulin resistance impairs lipid storage but enhances lipolysis. Free fatty acids (FFAs) and glycerol released from white adipose tissue are circulated in the body and they can be re-esterified into triglycerides in the liver. The pituitary gland is a critical endocrine organ that regulates metabolic and energy homeostasis. Obesity can cause blunted unfolded protein response in the pituitary gland, subsequently impacting the thyroid hormone (TH)–TH receptor (THR)—X-box binding protein 1 axis in the liver and causing MASLD.30 In addition to interorgan crosstalks, intraorgan communications in the liver have also been shown to influence diet-induced MASLD. REV-ERB nuclear receptors in hepatocytes have a significant impact on circadian rhythm in hepatic macrophages through both SREBF chaperone/sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP)-dependent (lipid signals) and SREBP-independent (hepatokines such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)) mechanisms.31

Dysregulation of lipid droplet homeostasis plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of MASLD. Lipid droplets are formed and eventually bud off the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane. Several MASLD-associated genetic factors, including PNPLA3, cell death inducing DFFA-like effector B and HSD17B13, are lipid droplet-associated proteins. The role of autophagy in hepatic lipid droplet breakdown has been increasingly appreciated. As a key regulator of autophagy, autophagy-related 14 (ATG14) has been shown to regulate lipid droplet metabolism by at least two different mechanisms: lipophagy and lipase-mediated lipolysis. ATG14 can recruit an autophagy protein complex to lipid droplet for initiation of lipophagy. Alternatively, ATG14 can also directly enhance the activity of ATGL through interaction with ABHD5.32

Bile acids (BAs) play an important role in absorption of dietary lipids and fat-soluble vitamins in small intestine. Excess or overflow of BAs in the liver can cause tissue damage and trigger inflammation and fibrosis. This is particularly relevant to human CLDs such as MASLD and MASH, as human BA pool is more hydrophobic or damaging to cells.33 BAs are synthesised in hepatocytes from cholesterol through both classical and alternative pathways, resulting in primary BAs in the liver. Primary BAs are essential in forming micelles to facilitate intestinal absorption of lipid-soluble nutrients. After completion of lipid absorption, primary BAs undergo enterohepatic circulation to return to the liver, while those that are not recirculated are converted to secondary BAs by gut microbiota.34 BAs regulate various metabolic functions through modulation of nuclear and membrane receptors.35 Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a key nuclear receptor involved in maintaining BA homeostasis; it suppresses hepatic BA synthesis and promotes their enterohepatic circulation. FXR also plays a significant role in regulating lipid metabolism, reducing inflammation and fibrosis and has potential implications as a liver tumour suppressor. Disruption of FXR function in animal models has been linked to liver diseases commonly observed in humans, such as cholestasis, MASLD and HCC.

Transition from MASLD to MASH often involves hepatic injury, most likely initiating from hepatocytes. As a result, both innate and adaptive immune cells are involved in the MASH pathogenesis (figure 3). Damaged hepatocytes release various signalling molecules including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Excessive lipid deposition in hepatocytes can cause oxidative stress and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further activate proinflammatory responses in hepatocytes. Increased ROS production can also activate hepatic immune cells to further increase inflammatory responses. Mitochondrial DNAs from damaged or dead hepatocytes can activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase—stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor pathway in macrophages to promote hepatic inflammation.36 Hepatocyte-released extracellular vesicles (EVs) due to steatosis-associated ER stress can activate macrophages to increase hepatic inflammation under MASH conditions. In addition to hepatocytes, cholangiocytes have also been implicated in the regulation of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. NF-κB-inducing kinase in cholangiocytes has been shown to promote ductular reaction and activation of proinflammatory macrophages and fibrogenic HSCs.37 Of note, inflammatory macrophages have been shown to play a significant role in the early stage of liver fibrosis, especially monocyte-derived macrophages.38 Interestingly, macrophages also metabolise PUFAs to specialised proresolving mediators such as resolvins, lipoxins, maresins and protectins. Resolvin D1 has been recently reported to improve MASH-associated hepatic inflammation by suppressing the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1)–C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 10 pathway in macrophages, to reduce hepatic ER stress-mediated apoptosis and to promote fibrosis regression by increasing expression of matrix metallopeptidase 12.39 MASH-associated macrophages have been implicated in the promotion of MASH development. A recent study has identified membrane-spanning 4-domains A7 protein as a mediator of lipid droplet-induced inflammasome activation in hepatic macrophages.40 This work reinforces the involvement of lipid droplets in the MASH pathogenesis.41 Future studies are needed to dissect macrophage subtypes regarding their roles in the MASH pathogenesis.

Cell–cell crosstalks in the liver in the development of MASLD/MASH. Both hepatic resident cells and infiltrated immune cells are involved in the development of MASLD and MASH. It is believed that hepatocyte injuries are among the early events of MASLD pathogenesis. With the involvement of immune cells, hepatic inflammation occurs to various extents in different patients. When the inflammation is not resolved, proinflammatory factors further trigger additional reactions from other cells in the liver, including hepatic stellate cells, LSECs, cholangiocytes and immune cells. At this stage, MASLD has progressed to MASH with hallmark features of hepatic steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis. Neutrophils are often first responders of immune response to hepatic inflammation during transition from MASLD to MASH (figure 3). At the same time, neutrophil-released proteases, oxidants, cytokines, chemokines and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are also involved in tissue damage and inflammation. Neutrophil to lymphocyte ratios are correlated with several hallmark features of MASH including hepatocyte ballooning, lobular inflammation and fibrosis in patients with MASH.42 It is worth noting that there are differences between human and mouse neutrophils; for example, mice have relatively threefold fewer neutrophils than humans, and mouse hepatocytes less effectively attract neutrophils than human counterparts in response to inflammation.43 Neutrophils are involved in MASH development through multiple mechanisms including release of myeloperoxidase, neutrophil elastase, lipocalin 2, S100A8/9 Ca2+-binding proteins and NETs.44 Although neutrophils have been mostly studied for their roles in tissue inflammation, the role of neutrophils in proresolution and restoration by modulating conversion of proinflammatory monocytes to proresolving macrophages, inhibition of inflammatory cytokine production and release of proresolving ligands such as annexin A1 has also been documented.45 Neutrophil-enriched microRNA-223 (miR-223) also plays a significant role in resolution of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis partly through regulation of CXCL10, transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ, also named WWTR1), GLI family zinc finger 2 and PDGF receptor alpha/beta (PDGFRA/B).46 Additionally, neutrophils also crosstalk with hepatocytes through miR-233-enriched EVs to ameliorate MASH in mice.47

HSCs play a critical role in liver fibrosis (figure 3). Recent studies suggest that multiple modes of regulation control the HSC quiescence/activation status. By using single-nucleus RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses of human liver samples (normal, MASLD and MASH), transcription factor JUNB (JUNB, also called AP1 for activator protein 1), RUNX family transcription factor 1/2 and forkhead box A1/2 have been identified as HSC lineage-specific, cluster-specific and signal-specific transcription factors, respectively.48 Parathyroid hormone receptor 1 is elevated in activated HSCs and promotes HSC activation and fibrogenesis by activation of cAMP response element-binding protein-like 2.49 Extracellular matrix protein 1 has also been shown to protect against liver fibrosis and biliary ductular reaction through interaction with connective tissue growth factor and inhibition of the transforming growth factor beta signalling.50 A recent study using human pluripotent stem cell-derived liver cell coculture has demonstrated that human PNPLA3 rs738409 C>G (I148M) promotes the IL6-STAT3 signalling pathway in macrophages, hepatocytes and HSCs and subsequently MASLD phenotype in vitro.51 Hepatokines also play a positive or negative role in the regulation of HSC activation. For example, osteopontin (officially SPP1 for secreted phosphoprotein 1) is upregulated by E4 promoter-binding protein 4 and Yes1-associated transcriptional regulator (YAP) in hepatocytes and induces HSC activation and promotes the MASLD to MASH transition.52 YAP and TAZ/WWTR1 are also directly involved in HSC activation. As an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, sirtuin 6 can protect against HSC activation by deacetylation and suppression of YAP and TAZ.53 To better model liver diseases in vitro, hepatocyte organoids have been developed in the field. It has been recently demonstrated that optimised concentrations of triiodothyronine and dexamethasone and removal of commonly used growth factors promote hepatocyte maturation in the form of organoids. This in vitro model has been successfully tested for modelling MASLD and ALD.54

ALD and AH pathogenesis

After it is consumed, alcohol is absorbed in the stomach and small intestine and then transported to the liver through portal vein for metabolism. Through alcohol dehydrogenase 1, cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily E member 1 and catalase, alcohol is first metabolised to acetaldehyde and then acetaldehyde is converted to acetate through aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) (figure 4). This process mainly takes place in the liver, which has long been believed to be the principal organ for alcohol detoxification.55 A recent study has demonstrated that the gut also plays a significant role in detoxification of ethanol-derived acetaldehyde by the intestinal ALDH2. Approximately 30% acetaldehyde is excreted into bile and later delivered to the small intestine.56

Even though alcohol seems to be the direct inducer for ALD, not all heavy drinkers develop AH or ALC. According to the current guidelines for diagnosis of ALD and AH, patients with ALD tend to have moderate drinking behaviour and manifest hepatic steatosis, aspartate transaminase>alanine transaminase, serum bilirubin <3 mg/dL whereas patients with AH tend to have excessive alcohol consumption (≥4 drinks/day or ≥8 drinks/week for women and ≥5 drinks/day or ≥15 drinks/week for men) for ≥6 months and to manifest jaundice and serum bilirubin >3 mg/dL (figure 5).

There are multiple risk factors for ALC and AH, including genetic variants, sex, alcohol consumption amount and frequency and body and dietary conditions (figure 5). Regarding the role of genetic factors, multiple large-scale GWAS have been conducted for ALC and AH.57 58 Interestingly, genetic association patterns largely resemble those of MASLD and MASH. PNPLA3 148I>M and TM6SF2 167E>K are the most significant genetic variants that have consistently been shown to have a strong association with an elevated risk of the whole spectrum of ALD.59 Other variants like GCKR, MBOAT7 and HSD17B13 have also been associated with ALD in the same direction as that with MASLD/MASH. Recently, Fas-associated factor family member 2 (FAF2) rs374702773 variant (intronic) has been associated with reduced risk of ALC among heavy drinkers.60 Interestingly, FAF2 protein is also localised to lipid droplet and interacts with ATGL in hepatocytes.61 Silencing the FAF2 gene reduces alcohol-induced hepatic steatosis through modulation of lipolysis and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), which is involved in the regulation of low-density lipoprotein receptor degradation.62

Although alcohol has a broad effect on multiple organs, the liver carries a greater burden of alcohol toxicity (figure 4).56 Moreover, the adverse effect of alcohol on gut and adipose tissue can further exacerbate the liver problem through endotoxins from gut and FFAs from adipose tissue, respectively.63 64 Alcohol intake is known to cause gut microbiota dysbiosis. As a result, intestinal microorganism-produced vitamin B6 is reduced in ALD mice and human patients. Vitamin B6 insufficiency negatively impacts hepatic amino acid metabolism and glutathione biosynthesis.65 Alcohol also exacerbates age-related hepatic inflammation and fibrosis by activating NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)-mediated inflammasome. Inhibition of NLRP3 with a small-molecule inhibitor MCC950 significantly improves hepatic inflammation and fibrosis partly through reduction of hepatocyte-derived DAMPs such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1).66

Neutrophil infiltration is a hallmark of AH as a response to alcohol-induced liver damage.67 Interleukin (IL)-8, also known as CXCL8, a key chemokine in the recruitment and activation of neutrophils, is strongly correlated with the AH severity.68 Targeting IL-8 or its receptors C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CXCR1) and CXCR2 can be potentially useful for treatment of AH. Recent reports on potentially beneficial roles of neutrophils suggest the ‘double-edged sword’ nature of neutrophils as they can help resolve inflammation by impairing macrophage polarisation and clearing cellular debris through phagocytosis, as well as promoting liver regeneration through production of hepatocyte growth factors. Nevertheless, a unique population called low-density neutrophils (LDNs) has been found in individuals with AH and alcohol-fed mice but not in healthy controls, exhibiting a functionally exhausted phenotype, in contrast to high-density neutrophils that function normally. Those dysfunctional LDNs are also difficult to clear by macrophages and remain in the circulation and liver.69 Additional studies on these alcohol-induced phenotypic changes of neutrophils are warranted.

Alcohol is also a leading cause of cirrhosis in the world.70 In the USA, ∼50% patients with cirrhosis are due to chronic alcohol drinking.9 A report has shown that the mortality rates of ALC have markedly increased from 3.3 to 10.6 per 100 000 between 1999 and 2019 in the USA.71 Alcohol has been shown to induce HSC activation through multiple mechanisms, including alteration of epigenetic factors such as mixed lineage leukaemia protein 1 (MLL1) and lysine demethylase 5A/B (KDM5A/B).72 There are two types of cirrhosis —compensated at the early stage of disease and decompensated as the advanced form of cirrhosis. The manifestations of the transition from a compensated state to a decompensated state include ascites, hepatic encephalopathy and variceal bleeding. Globally, alcohol consumption is estimated to account for 23% of the decompensated cirrhosis cases in 2017.

Cholestasis and biliary dysfunction also occur in AH; however, the pathophysiology remains poorly understood. By using a mouse model with a combination of chronic ethanol diet feeding and a single injection of α-naphthylisothiocyanate, several features of AH are recapitulated in the mouse model, including elevated liver injury, cholestasis, necrosis, neutrophil infiltration and accumulation of NETs. Depletion of neutrophils or enzymatic removal of NETs can improve AH in the experimental mice.73 Additional studies are needed to investigate the cross-talks among hepatic resident cells and infiltrated immune cells during the AH pathogenesis.

Liver cancer pathogenesis

There are multiple risk factors for liver cancer (figure 6). Currently, viral hepatitis is the major cause of HCC in most populations, followed by excessive alcohol use and MASH. In a prospective study of a Swedish cohort, compared with the general population, the odds ratios (ORs) of HCC are 34.4, 2.4 and 40.7 for patients with chronic viral hepatitis, alcohol use disorder (AUD) and cirrhosis alone, respectively; the ORs of HCC are 27.3, 118.5, 22.4 and 171.4 for patients with chronic viral hepatitis and AUD, chronic viral hepatitis and cirrhosis, AUD and cirrhosis and all three conditions, respectively.74 There are significant pathological differences between MASH-related HCC and other HCCs, including genetic factors, signalling pathways and tumour microenvironment. For example, activin A receptor type 2A (ACVR2A) has a higher mutation rate in MASH HCC than other types of HCCs (10% vs 3%).11 Other studies have also demonstrated that MASH HCC may also have a high frequency of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) gene promoter and catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1) mutations.75 To model MASH-related HCC in an animal study, mice were fed a Western diet for 5 months followed by hydrodynamic injections of plasmids of myr-Akt1, N-Ras(V12) and Sleeping Beauty transposase. By comparison to non-MASH-related HCC, MASH HCC tumours have elevated gene signatures in translation, RNA processing, cell cycle and senescence. The tumour immune microenvironment is also different. MASH-HCC has fewer macrophages in the non-tumorous adjacent tissue, fewer natural killer (NK) cells at the tumour margin and fewer monocytes, macrophages and NK cells inside the tumour.76 On the other hand, ALD HCC shows higher mutation rates in tumour protein 53 (TP53), AT-rich interaction domain 1A (ARID1A) and chromatin remodelling genes but low CTNNB1 activation mutations.77 A recent study has shown that transgenic mice expressing the human PNPLA3 148M variant have twofold higher HCC incidence rates compared with control wildtype mice on an alcohol-related diet.78

Sex-determining region Y box 9 (SOX9) has been suggested as a liver progenitor cell (LPC) marker. A report has shown that early injuries of LPCs contribute to the liver cancer development. When phosphatase and tensin homologue deleted on chromosome 10 (Pten), a tumour suppressor gene, is specifically knocked out in Sox9+ cells in mice, more than 50% of knockout mice develop liver tumours with features of both HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma after 1 year. Pten deficiency-induced liver cancer is further accelerated and exacerbated by high-fat diet or hepatotoxins such as 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine.79 Hepatic steatosis has also been shown to promote liver metastasis through hepatocyte-derived EVs that carry YAP signalling-related microRNAs. Those microRNAs inhibit large tumour suppressor kinase 2 (LATS2) and subsequently activate YAP in tumour cells.80 BAs have been shown to modulate HCC tumour immunity. Primary BAs induce oxidative stress in CD8+ T cells, whereas secondary BA lithocholic acid induces ER stress in CD8+ T cells; however, ursodeoxycholic acid protects CD8+ T cells from ER stress and improves liver cancer immunotherapy.81

Current treatments and new therapeutic targets

To date, liver transplantation is the only medical intervention for acute liver failure and end-stage CLDs including severe AH, decompensated cirrhosis and HCC (figure 7). Hepatic ischaemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury during surgery often complicates transplant outcomes. Recent work has suggested that cross-talks among hepatic immune cells play a critical role in liver repair and regeneration.82 In a hepatic I/R mouse model, eosinophils are recruited to the liver and stay there for up to 5 days. Eosinophil depletion and adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived eosinophils strongly support a key role of eosinophils in the hepatic I/R recovery. Mechanistically, eosinophil-secreted IL-4 but not IL-13 acts on hepatic macrophages; subsequently, macrophage-derived heparin-binding epidermal growth factor-like growth factor (HB-EGF) promotes liver regeneration.82 These findings suggest that IL4 and HB-EGF have potential for the treatment of hepatic I/R injury. Regarding liver regeneration, Snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (SNAI1) and SNAI2 have also been shown to promote liver regeneration by activation of cyclin A2 and D1 gene expression.83 Using a concanavalin A-induced acute liver injury mouse model, it has been reported that deletion of Siglec-H, a receptor specifically expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells, reduces liver injury and inflammation through promotion of immune tolerance and IL21+ CD4+ T cells.84

The association of low thyroid function with MASLD has been well documented.85 The thyroid produces the prohormone T4 that is intracellularly converted into the bioactive hormone T3. There are two receptors for T3: THR α and β (THR-α and THR-β). THR-β is the predominant T3 receptor in the liver. THR-β agonists have been tested for both MASLD and MASH. In March 2024, resmetirom, a THR-β selective agonist, was conditionally approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treatment of MASH. In a 52-week phase 3 trial, at least one stage improvement of liver fibrosis with no worsening MASLD activity score was achieved in 24.2% and 25.9% of patients in the resmetirom groups at doses of 80 and 100 mg, respectively, compared with 14.2% of the placebo group.86

Recent clinical trials using glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor (GLP1R) agonists or in combination with glucagon receptor (GCGR) agonists or gastric inhibitory polypeptide receptor (GIPR) agonists have shown very encouraging results for the treatment of MASH (figure 7). In a 52-week phase 2 trial using tirzepatide, a dual agonist of GLP1R and GIPR, the tirzepatide groups have shown remarkable dose-dependent improvement on MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis in 44%, 56% and 62% patients at 5, 10 and 15 mg doses, respectively, compared with 10% in the placebo group.87 In another 48-week phase 2 trial using survodutide, a dual agonist of GLP1R and GCGR, 47%, 62% and 43% patients who have taken survodutide at 2.4, 4.8 and 6.0 mg, respectively, have improved MASH without worsening of fibrosis, compared with 14% of those in the placebo group.88 In a 48-week phase 2 trial using retatrutide, a triple agonist of GLP1R, GIPR and GCGR, the retatrutide groups have lost 22.8% and 24.2% weight at 8 and 12 mg doses, respectively. At 24 weeks, liver fat has been reduced by 42.9%, 57.0%, 81.4% and 82.4% at doses of 1, 4, 8 and 12 mg, respectively, compared with an increase of 0.3% in the placebo group.89

In a meta-analysis of 27 clinical trials for MASLD treatment, different therapeutics perform differently in terms of MASLD features. For the liver fat reduction, GLP1R-based polyagonists are most effective, followed by fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) analogues, GLP1R agonists and THR-β agonists. For the improvement of liver stiffness, FGF21 analogues are most effective, followed by THR-β agonists and GLP1R agonists. For the fibrosis improvement, GLP1R-based polyagonists are most effective, followed by FGF21 analogues, THR-β agonists, pan-peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists and GLP1R agonists. For the resolution of MASH, GLP1R-based polyagonists are most effective, followed by THR-β agonists, GLP1R agonists, FGF21 analogues and pan-PPAR agonists.90 In another meta-analysis, surface under the cumulative ranking curve was used to rank 29 randomised controlled clinical trials for MASH. The top 10 treatments for fibrosis improvement at least one stage without worsening MASH endpoint are: pegozafermin (an FGF21 analogue), cilofexor (an FXR agonist)+firsocostat (an ACC inhibitor), cilofexor+selonsertib (an ASK1 inhibitor), PXL065 (an R-pioglitazone), firsocostat+selonsertib, denifanstat (an FASN inhibitor), survodutide, obeticholic acid (a BA analogue and an FXR agonist), tirzepatide and efruxifermin (an FGF21 analogue). The top treatments for MASH resolution without worsening fibrosis endpoint are: pegozafermin, survodutide, tirzepatide, efruxifermin, liraglutide, vitamin E+ pioglitazone, cilofexor+firsocostat, resmetirom, pioglitazone and vitamin E.91

Mitochondrial homeostasis plays a critical role in maintaining normal liver function.92 There are multiple ways of mitochondrial quality control by degrading mitochondrial proteins or clearing damaged mitochondria altogether. Autophagy is a major means of mitochondrial clearance via lysosome. Lysosomes are the terminal recycling organelles in eukaryotic cells, which are responsible for degradation of damaged organelles and macromolecules including carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Neurotropin has been used as a drug for pain relief in Japan and China. Interestingly, administration of neurotropin to diet-induced MASLD mice significantly improves hepatic steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis through enhancement of mitochondrial dynamics and function.93

Targeting BA homeostasis for MASLD and MASH has been pursued for a long time.94 Multiple FXR agonists have been tested for treating MASLD and MASH in clinical trials.95 Cytochrome P450 family 8 subfamily B member 1 (CYP8B1), a key enzyme in BA biosynthesis, has been shown as a potential drug target for obesity and MASLD. Interestingly, the clinical benefits of vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) seem to require downregulation of CYP8B1.96 In response to BAs, the ileum of small intestine secretes FGF19 (FGF15 in mouse) that inhibits BA biosynthesis in the liver through gene regulation. Deletion of the Fgf15 gene in mouse intestine has been shown to enhance the VSG benefits, suggesting that additional mechanisms are in play.97 A recent phase 2b clinical trial of aldafermin, an FGF19 analogue, in patients with MASH and compensated cirrhosis, 21% and 23% patients treated with either 1 mg or 3 mg had at least one stage of fibrosis improvement compared with 15% in the placebo group.98

Impaired glycine metabolism has been implicated in cardiometabolic diseases including MASLD and MASH. Administration of Gly-Gly-Leu tripeptide in MASH mouse and non-human primate models has shown a significant improvement of hepatic steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis by promoting fatty acid oxidation, glutathione synthesis and microbial BA metabolism.99

As the PNPLA3 148M genetic variant is quite common in the general population, targeting this genetic variant can be useful to improve the PNPLA3-148M-associated CLD. A recent phase 1 trial using a liver-targeted antisense oligonucleotide against PNPLA3 messenger RNA in overweight or mildly obese healthy volunteers or patients with hepatic steatosis. Hepatic steatosis was decreased by 7.6% and 12.2% in participants on 25 mg and 50 mg doses at week 12, respectively.100

For the treatment of ALD and AH, alcohol abstinence is the most effective approach. But this is not easy for patients with AUD. Motivational interviewing and related treatments are considered as cornerstones of AUD treatments. Several medications have been approved for the treatment of AUD like naltrexone, nalmefene, acamprosate and disulfiram.101 GLP1R agonists have been reported to have some potential benefits for AUD and ALD.102 103 Corticosteroids have been used for treatment of severe AH. A recent meta-analysis has revealed that the median time from patient admission to steroid initiation is about 7 days and the most common steroid dosing regimen is 40 mg prednisolone daily for 28 days. The most frequent adverse events include infections, gastrointestinal bleeds and renal impairment.104 In a meta-analysis of a total of 23 studies including six randomised trials and 17 non-randomised studies of interventions, baclofen, a muscle relaxer, has been shown as the only statistically significant intervention in terms of the primary outcomes for patients with AUD and cirrhosis.105 For patients who have severe AH and do not respond to therapeutic treatments, liver transplantation is the only treatment option for a potential long-term survival.

HCC treatment has improved remarkably over the last two decades. Surgical therapies with surgical resection and liver transplantation are considered as curative and have multiple years of overall survival in some patients.106 Local ablation with radiofrequency or microwave radiation is recommended for patients with HCC detected at an early stage but not suitable for surgery, while transarterial radioembolisation (TARE) or stereotactic body radiotherapy is an alternative approach. Adjuvant therapy with atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1/programmed cell death ligand 1 monoclonal antibody) and bevacizumab (anti-VEGF/vascular endothelial growth factor monoclonal antibody) for patients with high-risk recurrence after HCC resection has been shown to significantly improve recurrence-free survival. For intermediate-stage HCC, transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) or TARE may yield a median overall survival of 26–30 months.

A phase 3 study using durvalumab (PD-L1 monoclonal antibody) with or without bevacizumab with TACE for unresectable HCC (EMERALD-1) has reported median progression-free survival of 15 months for the durvalumab plus bevacizumab group compared with 8.2 months for the placebo group (table 1).107 Another similar phase 3 study using TACE combined with lenvatinib (a multikinase inhibitor) plus pembrolizumab (PD-1/programmed cell death protein 1 monoclonal antibody) for unresectable and non-metastatic HCC (LEAP-012) has reported median progression-free survival of 14.6 months compared with 10 months for the placebo group (table 1).108 There are multiple systemic therapies for HCC at the current time (table 1).109,118 Atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, tremelimumab (CTLA4/cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 monoclonal antibody) plus durvalumab, lenvatinib and sorafenib (a multikinase inhibitor) are first-line options (figure 7, table 1). It has been suggested that CLD aetiologies also impact efficacy of HCC systemic treatment; however, the results from multiple phase 3 studies on unresectable or advanced HCC are inconsistent (table 1).109,118 Although most immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown better efficacy as monotherapy or in combination with another ICI or a multikinase inhibitor than sorafenib in patients with HBV-related HCC, the efficacy of the same treatments in patients with non-viral HCC or HCV-related HCC is quite variable among those trials (table 1).109110 114,116 118 Even among patients with non-viral HCC, the underlying CLD aetiology may be different such as MASLD or ALD. The results of a randomised phase 3 study - cabozantinib plus atezolizumab versus sorafenib for advanced HCC (COSMIC-312) have shown that the efficacy of cabozantinib plus atezolizumab is inferior to sorafenib in patients with MASLD or ALD (hazard ratio (HR): 1.72 (0.83–3.55) and 1.26 (0.71–2.22), respectively).116 It has been suggested that hepatic accumulation of exhausted, aberrantly activated CD8+ PD1+ T cells in MASH-associated HCC leads to tissue damage and immune surveillance impairment.119

There are multiple second-line options as well such as regorafenib (a multikinase inhibitor), cabozantinib (a multikinase inhibitor), ramucirumab (VEGFR2/VEGF receptor 2 monoclonal antibody), pembrolizumab and nivolumab (PD-1 monoclonal antibody) plus ipilimumab (CTLA4 monoclonal antibody).106 However, there is no effective treatment for advanced HCC yet so far. Some studies have shown that even combination treatments may not improve overall survival for advanced HCC. For example, in the COSMIC-312 study for advanced HCC, median overall survival for the combination treatment group with cabozantinib plus atezolizumab was 16.5 months compared with 15.5 months for the sorafenib group (table 1).116 Apparently, new effective therapeutics are badly needed for patients with late-stage HCC.

Conclusions

As a significant portion of the global population has developed MASLD or ALD, there are outstanding challenges for both patients and clinicians. Patients should be aware of the consequences of the CLD progression and take necessary actions on body weight control and lifestyle optimisation on a regular basis. Clinicians should work with patients to come up with an intervention strategy and follow-up if needed. A better understanding of the disease pathogenesis should facilitate prevention and therapeutic development. Multiple therapeutics have shown promising efficacy for MASLD or MASH. Nevertheless, the long-term efficacy and adverse events should be investigated. Looking forward, CLDs will remain a major health issue in many societies until cures can be achieved.
