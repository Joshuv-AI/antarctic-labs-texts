Aortic dissection in the ED: a medico-legal perspective on diagnostic delays and failures
Authors: Jacqueline H Fortier, Mehrnaz Mostafapour, Robert Ohle, Kate Barbosa, Richard Liu, Ming Guo, Gary E Garber, Heather E Murray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAortic dissection is a life-threatening emergency and delayed or missed diagnoses can result in significant morbidity and mortality for patients.MethodsWe conducted a quantitative and qualitative analysis of medico-legal cases from a national repository involving thoracic aortic dissection seen in the emergency department during a 10-year period. We thematically analyzed peer expert criticism of the care in these cases, including provider, team, and systems factors related to diagnostic issues. We also applied two clinical decision tools to explore whether use of these tools could have affected the diagnostic process.ResultsOf 3,531 medico-legal cases in the emergency department during our study period, just 43 were related to aortic dissection. Patients were primarily male (68.2%), presented for care at large urban centers (65.1%), and were triaged as urgent or emergent (72.1%). Thirty-six patients died of their aortic dissection. A thematic analysis identified atypical presentations, diagnostic anchoring, misinterpretation or misuse of tests, communication breakdowns, and resource limitations as common in these cases.ConclusionsMissed diagnoses of aortic dissection in the ED often result from a combination of cognitive, communication, and system-level factors. Understanding these contributors can inform evidence-based, systems-level supports and strategies to enhance diagnostic accuracy and reduce patient harm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s43678-025-00999-y.Keywords: Aortic dissection, Diagnostic error, Diagnostic delay, Acute aortic syndrome

Introduction

Aortic dissection is a time-sensitive and life-threatening medical emergency, identified as one of the top five conditions vulnerable to misdiagnosis in the emergency department (ED) in a recent systematic review [1, 2]. In Ontario, the estimated annual incidence of acute aortic syndromes is 0.61 per 100,000 people, with an estimated misdiagnosis rate of 12.5% [3]. Diagnostic delay is particularly consequential for patients with acute aortic dissection, as the highest mortality risk is in the initial hours after presentation [4]. Deterioration and death in these patients can occur rapidly, even in patients who appear initially stable [4].

Despite advances in imaging technology, the misdiagnosis rate in Ontario remained stable over a 15-year study period, suggesting opportunities for system-level interventions to improve diagnostic accuracy [3]. One potential system-level intervention is to apply a clinical decision tool in an effort to standardize risk stratification and investigation to reduce diagnostic delays and improve detection of aortic dissection [2, 5]. Clinical decision tools aim to improve diagnostic accuracy while minimizing unnecessary investigations. A Canadian practice guideline published in 2020 contains a structured approach to risk stratification, and a similar tool, the AORTAs pretest probability tool, was endorsed by a recent clinical guideline for the diagnosis and management of aortic disease [2, 5, 6].

This study analyzes Canadian medico-legal cases of acute aortic dissection in the emergency department to identify underlying factors that contribute to delayed or missed diagnosis.

Methods

Setting

This is a descriptive study of medico-legal cases supported by the Canadian Medical Protective Association (CMPA), a not-for-profit mutual defense organization that provides discretionary medico-legal advice and support to over 110,000 physician members across Canada. Physicians contact CMPA requesting support with complaints made to provincial and territorial Colleges, or if they are named in a civil legal matter.

Ethical approval for this study was provided by the Canadian review services of the Advarra Institutional Review Board.

Data sources

Experienced registered nurse analysts routinely code medico-legal cases in a previously described process [7]. The analysts generate a summary and apply standardized medical coding frameworks, including ICD-10-CA, Canadian Classification of Health Interventions, and in-house frameworks for classifying patient harms (see Appendix 1) and contributing factors [8–10]. Medical coding is subject to regular quality assurance review.

We extracted civil legal or medical regulatory cases closed between 01 January 2014 and 31 December 2023 that were coded as aortic dissection or for ruptured or unruptured thoracic or thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (ICD-10-CA codes I71.0–2, I71.5-I71.9) with an associated ED care setting. Abdominal aortic aneurysms were excluded.

The cases were independently reviewed by a registered nurse and an emergency medicine specialist to confirm the final diagnosis of an acute aortic dissection. Dual screening was performed to ensure consistency, and disagreements were resolved through consultation with a cardiac surgeon.

Data extraction

We reviewed the clinical summary, decisions, and peer expert opinions for each case to collect patient and provider characteristics, hospital details, and clinical information (Appendix 2).

We calculated the AORTAs and the Canadian clinical decision tool risk scores for each patient. Depending on the patient’s score, we identified whether the tools would have recommended specific diagnostic testing and whether that test was done. If the data needed for the calculations were not explicitly recorded, they were assumed to be absent. If the provider did not order a test that would have been recommended by the clinical decision tool, it was considered a case in which management could have been changed with application of the tool.

Quantitative analysis

We used descriptive statistics for quantitative analysis. For variables of interest, we calculated frequencies and proportions and used univariate statistics. The categorical variables were described as proportions and continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. We used SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA), and a custom data analysis tool in Microsoft Access (Microsoft Corporation).

Qualitative analysis

We studied prior literature to identify potential issues, such as errors in diagnostic reasoning, communication, and systems factors that could contribute to the missed diagnosis of aortic dissection, and used an internal framework designed to identify factors contributing to patient safety incidents [8]. The thematic analysis was conducted iteratively, following standard principles of thematic analysis [2, 11–13]. While the framework and extracted factors provided a structured starting point for coding, the framework was adapted throughout the thematic analysis process to incorporate emergent themes as new patterns were identified. Additional themes were inductively developed in response to novel factors identified during the review process.

Coding was independently performed by an emergency medicine specialist (HM) and a researcher (JF), and discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions. Case examples were selected and summarized with limited representative details to preserve the privacy of patients and physicians.

Results

In the 10-year period covered by our study, CMPA closed 3531 medico-legal cases where care was provided in EDs, and 61 were related to aortic dissection. Excluded cases included abdominal aortic aneurysms (n = 13), duplicate cases (n = 1), complaints unrelated to care provided in the ED (n = 2), and unclear diagnoses (n = 2). This left 43 cases for analysis.

The average age of patients in our cases was 52.5 years (SD 14.9 years). Fourteen patients (32.6%) were women. A majority (n = 28, 65.1%) presented for care at EDs in large urban areas (population 100,000 or greater). About a quarter of cases (n = 12, 27.9%) involved a transfer between hospitals. Most patients (n = 31, 72.1%) were categorized as emergent or urgent at triage (Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale [14] level II or III) (Table 1).

Twenty-nine cases were Stanford Type A dissections (67.4%), nine were Type B dissections (20.9%), and 5 had insufficient information to classify (11.6%). Thirty patients (69.8%) were only diagnosed with aortic dissection after their death; treatments of the fifteen patients who were diagnosed are shown in Table 2. In total, 36 patients (83.7%) died of complications related to aortic dissection. Deaths occurred out of hospital following discharge from the ED (17 cases, 39.5%), in the ED (12 patients, 27.9%), and in other hospital units (7 cases, 16.3%).

Peer experts were critical of the care provided by the physicians in 29 cases (65.9%). In 21 cases, the outcome was decided against the physician (48.8%).

We did not identify any cases in which the physician documented the use of a clinical decision tool, and most cases occurred before these tools were available. If a clinical decision tool had been available, the AORTAs tool would have changed care in almost 80% of cases, and the Canadian guideline would have changed care in almost 35% of cases (Table 2).

There were several factors that were commonly identified as contributing to diagnostic delay or failure (Table 3). Common provider factors included clinical decision-making (e.g., inadequate physical examination or failure to perform a test) or were related to situational awareness (e.g., failure to see information in the chart or premature discharge). Contributing team factors included documentation issues and communication breakdowns. Identified systems factors were primarily related to resource availability. Qualitative analysis

Our thematic analysis identified several factors contributing to missed diagnoses of aortic dissection.

As expected in a medicolegal dataset, atypical presentations were common, including young patient age or the absence of common symptoms of aortic dissection. In one example, at the time of the patient’s examination by the ED physician, they reported their shoulder pain gone, with stable vitals and a normal exam. The patient was discharged and died at home the next morning.

Misinterpretation of diagnostic tests was common—for example, a chest x-ray showed a widened mediastinum but was interpreted as normal. There were also instances where physicians had a flawed understanding of the roles of diagnostic tests in excluding an aortic dissection diagnosis; there were several cases where physicians “ruled out” aortic dissection using either a point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS) or chest x-ray.

Anchoring on an alternate diagnosis appeared frequently. In one example, a patient presented with complaints of epigastric pain after a large meal; the working diagnosis in the ED was severe gastritis. Other examples include patients presenting with pain following exertion or exercise and being diagnosed with musculoskeletal pain. Sometimes patients were assessed for acute coronary syndromes; when preliminary results were normal, they were discharged for outpatient follow-up.

Resource limitation was another theme. We identified cases where the timing of the patient’s presentation (e.g., nights, weekends) affected the availability of diagnostic tests. Advanced imaging was often inaccessible in rural or community hospitals. ED crowding and lack of inpatient bed availability were identified in some cases. In one case, the ED physician correctly diagnosed aortic dissection but was unable to find a hospital to accept the transfer for urgent cardiac surgery.

Finally, team communication issues were observed among the files with adverse outcomes. For example, in one case, a nurse assessed a patient and upgraded their acuity status but did not inform the ED physician, resulting in a several-hour delay to assessment.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

Acute aortic dissection can be challenging to diagnose and the cases we describe highlight the serious harms that can occur when there are diagnostic failures. The factors contributing to the misdiagnosis of aortic dissection are complex and multifaceted, encompassing individual-, system-, and patient-related factors, often in combination.

Comparison to previous studies

Conceptual models of diagnostic reasoning have identified 3 iterative stages: information gathering, information integration and interpretation, and the formation of working and differential diagnoses [15]. Our qualitative analysis identified challenges that are linked to each of these domains. We saw evidence of information gathering challenges due to breakdowns in team communication, typically where physicians were not made aware of concerning symptoms or clinical deterioration. Possible interventions to improve these processes include the use of intake questionnaires to be completed by patients or their advocates, standardized structures for information sharing, and face-to-face communication and handovers [16]. We identified many cases where information integration or interpretation was a challenge, including clinicians misunderstanding the role of tests (such as over-reliance on negative CXR or POCUS in a patient with high-risk features) or misinterpreting test results. Use of a structured clinical decision tool in these scenarios may have improved diagnostic accuracy. While many of our cases predate the development of clinical decision tools for aortic dissection, the recent development of several tools to support the diagnosis of aortic dissection may support the diagnostic process [2, 5, 17]. A recent observational study evaluated several clinical decision tools in a prospective and retrospective manner, and suggested that these tools may be outperformed by clinicians’ subjective assessments of the likelihood of aortic dissection, indicating that the role of these tools is to provide structure to the diagnostic process, not to replace the physician’s diagnostic reasoning [18].

Our study also highlights the critical importance of recognizing atypical presentations of aortic dissection when forming working or differential diagnoses. We identified multiple cases where clinicians anchored prematurely on alternative diagnoses despite high-risk indicators (e.g., severe pain or hypotension), resulting in adverse outcomes. Atypical features such as younger patient age, transient symptoms, or seemingly benign causes of pain (e.g., exertional or postprandial pain) frequently led clinicians astray. Our findings highlight the need for emergency physicians to consider the possibility of aortic dissection, even when classic symptoms are absent. Training in cognitive debiasing has not been shown to be effective in reducing diagnostic error [19]. However, expanding retrievable, experiential knowledge that can be applied in the diagnostic process has been suggested as a training tool in Emergency Medicine [19]. Highlighting atypical aortic dissection presentations and diagnostic pitfalls from our case series provides clinicians with practical insights, which is one way to gain that retrievable, experiential knowledge and potentially improve diagnostic accuracy and patient safety.

Strengths and limitations

CMPA provides medico-legal advice and support to over 111,000 physicians across Canada, providing a nationally representative sample of medico-legal cases. Our data’s limitations are also important to consider. Medico-legal cases are a biased subset of adverse outcomes, and the generalizability of findings to broader clinical populations may be limited. Cases with favorable outcomes (i.e., those without diagnostic error) are unlikely to be included, which inflates the perception of diagnostic failures. While our clinical coding procedures are robust and subject to routine quality assurance processes, the coding is subjective and may be prone to bias or error. As well, physicians reach out to CMPA for support at their own discretion, so not all aortic dissection cases with a medico-legal complaint will be captured. Our data are also subject to the typical limitations of secondary uses of data, including a limited scope of variables, temporal limitations, the risk of missing or incomplete data, and a lack of standardization. Medico-legal case data cannot be shared in a data repository due to ethical and privacy concerns about identifying patients and physicians.

Clinical implications

Improving the diagnosis of aortic dissection requires quality-improvement efforts at the individual, team, and systems level. Our study identified potential pitfalls at all stages of the diagnostic process, and the literature suggests that strategies such as expanding retrievable, experiential knowledge may improve diagnostic reasoning. Clear, timely communication at all phases of care is essential, as delays in information sharing, poor handovers, and weak physician–patient communication can contribute to missed diagnoses. Emergency physicians should remain alert to the possibility of aortic dissection even when classic symptoms are absent. Our case narratives also suggest that resource limitations within healthcare settings, such as long ED wait times and restricted access to advanced imaging modalities, can impede accurate diagnosis. Such systemic issues can slow down the diagnostic process and impede communication among healthcare providers, further increasing the risk of misdiagnosis [20, 21].

Research implications

Future research opportunities should focus on the implementation of structured clinical decision support tools for aortic dissection in the assessment of patients with acute onset of chest pain, back pain, or malperfusion symptoms. Uncertainty exists regarding the impact of these tools on testing use, diagnostic accuracy, and ED throughput, and on which tool performs optimally in the clinical environment [18]. Case narratives suggest that resource limitations within healthcare settings, such as long ED wait times and restricted access to advanced imaging modalities, can impede accurate diagnosis. Such systemic issues can slow down the diagnostic process and lead to miscommunication among healthcare providers, further increasing the risk of misdiagnosis.

Conclusions

Aortic dissection is a medical emergency, and our analysis of medico-legal cases confirms the severity of the condition and the significant challenges related to timely diagnosis. Emergency medicine physicians face several barriers to the timely diagnosis of aortic dissection, including the rarity of the condition and its variable presentation. Efforts to improve the diagnosis of aortic dissection should continue to focus on the implementation of structured approaches to diagnostic reasoning and the development of evidence-based, systems-level supports that will improve the diagnostic process and outcomes for patients.
