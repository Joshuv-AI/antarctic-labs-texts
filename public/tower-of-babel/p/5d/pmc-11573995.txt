A Retrospective, Longitudinal Registry Study on the Long-Term Durability of Ivacaftor Treatment in People with Cystic Fibrosis
Authors: Christian Merlo, Teja Thorat, Lisa J. McGarry, Christina V. Scirica, Maral DerSarkissian, Catherine Nguyen, Yuqian M. Gu, Aruna Muthukumar, Joe Healy, Jaime L. Rubin, M. Alan Brookhart
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIvacaftor (IVA) has been shown to change the trajectory of cystic fibrosis (CF) disease progression by slowing the rate of lung function decline in clinical studies. Long-term real-world data help to confirm the durability of this response.MethodsThis non-interventional, longitudinal study used data from the US CF Foundation Patient Registry to describe the annualized rate of change in lung function in people with CF receiving IVA. The IVA-treated cohort included people with CF aged ≥ 6 years who had ≥ 1 CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR)–gating mutation and initiated IVA between 31 January 2012 and 31 December 2018. An age-matched comparator cohort included people with CF heterozygous for the F508del-CFTR mutation and a minimal function mutation (R117H excluded) and had not received CFTR modulator therapy. Baseline characteristics were balanced using standardized mortality ratio (SMR) weights computed from estimated propensity scores. The annualized rate of change in percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s (ppFEV1) was estimated over 5 years and used to calculate the relative annualized rate of change in lung function in the IVA-treated versus comparator cohorts.ResultsIn the 5-year follow-up period, 548 people were in the IVA-treated and 541 in the comparator cohorts after SMR weighting. The annualized rate of change in ppFEV1 over 5 years was −1.23 (95% CI −1.45, −1.03) and −2.03 (−2.16, −1.90) percentage points in the IVA-treated and comparator cohorts, respectively. There was a 39% reduction (95% CI: 28, 50) in the rate of lung function decline in the IVA-treated versus comparator cohort over 5 years. Findings were generally consistent with those of shorter follow-up periods.ConclusionIVA showed a durable clinical benefit by slowing the rate of lung function decline over 5 years. Results support a sustained and consistent impact of IVA on lung function trajectory in people with CF.Word count: 300 (limit: 300 words).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41030-024-00269-9.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Ivacaftor (IVA) has been shown to slow the rate of lung function decline in people with cystic fibrosis (CF); however, additional long-term data from real-world studies are needed to understand the durability of this effect.We conducted a retrospective, non-interventional, longitudinal study using data from the US CF Foundation Patient Registry to assess the relative rate of change in lung function over 5 years in people with CF aged ≥ 6 years with ≥ 1 CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR)–gating mutation who received IVA versus an age-matched comparator cohort that had not received CFTR modulator therapy. What was learned from the study? The annualized rate of change in percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s (ppFEV1) over 5 years of follow-up was −1.23 (95% CI −1.45, −1.03) percentage points in the IVA-treated cohort and −2.03 (−2.16, −1.90) percentage points in the comparator cohort.Over the 5-year period, there was a 39% (95% CI 28, 50) reduction in the rate of decline in ppFEV1 in the IVA-treated cohort versus the comparator cohort; this reduction was generally consistent over 3, 4, and 5 years of follow-up and across subgroups stratified by age (< 18 and ≥ 18 years).These results support the sustained and consistent impact of IVA treatment on preserving lung function by reducing the rate of decline in ppFEV1 in people with CF in a real-world setting.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive, life-limiting disease caused by a defective CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein, which affects multiple organs and leads to progressive loss of lung function [1]. Greater declines in lung function are associated with more severe underlying lung disease and are predictive of premature mortality [2, 3]. Prior to the availability of CFTR modulator (CFTRm) therapies that target CFTR dysfunction, lung function (measured by percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s [ppFEV1]) typically declined by 1–3 percentage points annually in people with CF [4]. The greatest decline in lung function was observed during adolescence.

Ivacaftor (IVA), the first available CFTRm, was initially approved in the United States in 2012 for people with CF aged ≥ 6 years who have ≥ 1 G551D-CFTR mutation [5]. This indication was expanded in 2014 to include people with CF aged ≥ 6 years who have any of the nine CFTR gating mutations [6]. Over time, the US label has expanded to include children with CF aged ≥ 1 month who have ≥ 1 CFTR mutation that is responsive to IVA based on clinical and/or in vitro assay data [7]. Clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of IVA in people with CF, with improvements seen in ppFEV1, pulmonary exacerbations (PEx), nutritional parameters, and CF Questionnaire–Revised respiratory domain score [8–11].

The impact of IVA on the rate of lung function decline has been previously assessed in two studies. In a study by Kirwan et al. (2019) that evaluated change in lung function pre- and post-IVA treatment using data from the CF Registry of Ireland (CFRI), IVA either improved or maintained lung function in people aged < 18 years, but not in people aged ≥ 18 years, over a 3-year follow-up period [12]. In a separate study, Sawicki et al. (2015) assessed the impact of IVA on the rate of lung function decline in people receiving IVA as part of a phase 3 clinical trial compared with a matched cohort of people with CF from the US CF Foundation Patient Registry (CFFPR) who were not receiving any CFTRm. Over 3 years of follow-up in this study, the rate of lung function decline was reduced by nearly half in people who received IVA compared with untreated controls [13]. Additional studies are required to understand how these findings may translate to people with CF receiving IVA in the real-world setting and whether the observed reductions in lung function decline are maintained over a longer period in both pediatric and adult populations.

In a previous related study, we reported the long-term impact of IVA (up to 7.9 years of follow-up) using the US CFFPR database [14]. In that study, we found lower rates of mortality, lung transplant, and PEx, along with improvements in lung function and body mass index (BMI), in people with CF receiving IVA compared with a matched untreated comparator cohort. Here, we report the results of an analysis using real-world data from the US CFFPR to estimate the rate of change in lung function over 5 years in people with CF who received IVA compared with an age-matched cohort that did not receive any CFTRm treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a non-interventional, longitudinal study using data from the CFFPR between 2010 and 2019 (Fig. 1) to describe the annualized rate of change in lung function in people with CF receiving IVA over 5 years [15]. The US CFFPR protocol review committee provided feedback and approved the protocol with revisions prior to study start. The Advarra Institutional Review Board, which operates in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments, granted an exemption for this research project based on the scope of the research in July 2020. All the patients in the CF registry provide informed consent to include their data in the registry.

Individuals from the CFFPR who were eligible for this study included people with CF with ≥ 1 CFTR gating mutation (Table S1) who initiated IVA treatment between 31 January 2012 and 31 December 2018 and were aged ≥ 6 years on the index date. These individuals formed the IVA-treated cohort. The comparator cohort comprised people with CF from the CFFPR who were heterozygous for the F508del-CFTR mutation and a CFTR minimal function mutation (F/MF) (Table S2) who were not eligible to receive IVA and were aged ≥ 6 years on the equivalent index date. For the IVA-treated cohort, the index date was defined as the encounter date (clinic visit) prior to the encounter when IVA was first received. The equivalent index date for individuals in the comparator cohort was defined as the encounter date closest to the index date (within ±6 months) of an age-matched person in the IVA-treated cohort. Individuals across both cohorts had data available for ≥ 24 months prior to the index date (i.e., the baseline period) and did not have any of the following: R117H-CFTR mutation or other CFTR residual function mutations, including splice-site mutations (to reduce heterogeneity arising from differences in disease severity and progression); lung transplant prior to the index date; evidence of use of any CFTRm prior to the index date; or evidence of pregnancy in the calendar year prior to or the same year as the index date (based on annual data).

Data were collected at CF Foundation-accredited care centers during annual patient reviews and encounters using online data capture forms. People were followed up from the index date for up to 5 years and censored upon treatment with a CFTRm other than IVA, IVA discontinuation (in the IVA-treated cohort), death, pregnancy, end of data availability, or lung transplant.

This study calculated annualized rates of change in lung function across four follow-up periods, each starting 1 month after the index date (2–24 months, 2–36 months, 2–48 months, and 2–60 months), herein referred to as 2, 3, 4, and 5 years. To be included in these analyses, individuals were required to have ≥ 3 ppFEV1 measurements spanning ≥ 6 months between month 2 and the end of each of the four follow-up periods. In both cohorts, with each longer follow-up period, a greater number of people were expected to meet this inclusion criterion. However, all ppFEV1 measurements during the first month following the index date were excluded to avoid changes in ppFEV1 related to the initial acute increase observed with IVA treatment.

Study Measures and Analysis

No formal sample size calculation was conducted. The maximum potential study population size was the number of people with available data in the CFFPR and those who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Demographic and clinical characteristics were reported at baseline and included age, race and ethnicity, sex, ppFEV1 (calculated using Global Lung Function Initiative equations [16]), number of PEx, prevalence of CF-related complications and respiratory microorganisms, medication use, healthcare resource utilization, type of health insurance, BMI, BMI z-score, and socioeconomic status. To ensure comparability between the two cohorts, people were matched on age at index date. For each of the four follow-up periods, baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of people with CF in the two cohorts were also balanced using standardized mortality ratio (SMR) weighting based on propensity scores. This approach reweighted the comparator cohort to be similar to the IVA-treated cohort at baseline; SMR weighting produced an estimate of the average treatment effect in the IVA-treated cohort (the average difference that would be found if everyone in the IVA-treated cohort received IVA vs. no treatment). Baseline covariates included in the propensity score model were selected based on clinical significance and included sex, race and ethnicity, type of health insurance, employment status, median household income by ZIP code (categorical), education level, ppFEV1 (categorical), change in ppFEV1 (categorical), number of PEx, average BMI and BMI z-scores (categorical), prevalence of CF-related complications, prevalence of respiratory microorganisms, number of hospitalizations and outpatient visits, and medication use. To eliminate extreme propensity scores, weight trimming at the 1st and 99th percentiles was performed to reduce the contribution of individuals with large weights, as they were unlikely to be representative of the overall cohort [17]. Covariates with standardized mean differences of < 10% between the SMR-weighted cohorts were determined to be effectively balanced at baseline [18].

To assess the durability of the impact of IVA treatment on lung function, the outcome of the study was the annualized rate of change in lung function as measured by ppFEV1, which was compared between the IVA-treated cohort and the comparator cohort over the four follow-up periods. The annualized rate of change in lung function was estimated using an SMR-weighted mixed model with an unstructured covariance structure. This included random effects for the intercept and slope of each individual with CF and fixed effects for IVA treatment, time, and the IVA treatment × time interaction term.

Annualized Rate of Decline in ppFEV1

In the IVA-treated cohort, the annualized rate of change in ppFEV1 was −1.23 (95% CI −1.45, −1.03) percentage points over 5 years; this was generally consistent with the rate of change in ppFEV1 seen over the shorter follow-up periods of 2, 3, and 4 years (Table 2). In the comparator cohort, the annualized rate of change in ppFEV1 was −2.03 (95% CI −2.16, −1.90) percentage points over 5 years. The annualized rate of lung function decline was numerically reduced in the IVA-treated cohort versus the comparator cohort by 28% (95% CI −13, 55) over 2 years; it was significantly reduced by 33% (13, 50) over 3 years, 36% (21, 49) over 4 years, and 39% (28, 50) over 5 years (Fig. 2 and Table 2). Discussion

In this analysis of real-world data available in the CFFPR between 2010 and 2019, IVA was found to reduce the annualized rate of lung function decline and the relative annualized rate of decline (39%; 95% CI 28, 50) versus no treatment over 5 years of follow-up, with no evidence of a waning effect over time. In addition, a generally consistent effect of IVA on the rate of lung function decline was seen across both pediatric and adult subgroups, with a numerically greater relative reduction observed in the adult population compared with the pediatric population.

This study adds to the growing literature discussing the impact of IVA on clinical outcomes in real-world settings. This is the first study to evaluate the rate of change in lung function in people with CF treated with IVA and the relative reduction in the rate of decline versus a CFTRm-untreated comparator cohort over 5 years of follow-up.

Lung function decline was previously assessed in people with CF receiving IVA as part of a phase 3 clinical trial and was compared with that in a matched untreated control population from the CFFPR. In this pivotal study by Sawicki et al. (2015), the estimated annualized rate of ppFEV1 decline was found to be 47% lower in the IVA-treated population (−0.91 percentage points per year) than in the control population (−1.72 percentage points per year; P = 0.03) over 3 years [13]. Although this relative reduction is greater than that observed over 5 years in our present study, it is important to note that the assessed populations were different. In the study by Sawicki et al. (2015), the control population was homozygous for the F508del-CFTR mutation (F/F) and matched based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the clinical trial [13]. As a result, the mean age was ≈ 22 years and mean ppFEV1 was ≈ 66–67 at baseline across the IVA-treated and control populations. In contrast, in our study, the comparator cohort had an F/MF genotype, and individuals across both cohorts were younger (mean baseline age, ≈19 years) and had higher baseline ppFEV1 (≈81–82). Despite these differences, the impact of IVA in reducing the rate of lung function decline in Sawicki et al. (2015) over 3 years is generally consistent with that observed in our real-world study over 5 years.

In the present study, a consistent, long-term, durable benefit of IVA treatment on the rate of lung function decline was seen across both pediatric and adult subgroups. However, a numerically higher relative reduction versus the comparator cohort was observed in the IVA-treated adult population compared with the IVA-treated pediatric population (47% vs. 34%). The subgroup of adults in this study experienced a relative reduction in the rate of lung function decline similar to that reported in the Sawicki et al. (2015) study; this is likely due to the parallels between the two populations in terms of older age and disease severity. Previous research has suggested that the rate of lung function decline in CF may be steeper in adolescent individuals; this may explain the numerically greater lung function decline seen in the pediatric population in the present study [4].

Reductions in the rate of decline in ppFEV1 after IVA initiation have also been reported in people with the G551D-CFTR mutation in the CFRI over 3 years [12]. While the study by Kirwan et al. (2019) did not compare the rate of decline observed with IVA to that in matched, untreated controls, it showed positive improvement in the annualized rate of lung function decline in the post-IVA period versus the pre-IVA period in people aged < 12 and 12 ≤ 18 years at IVA initiation. However, this positive effect was not observed in people aged ≥ 18 years at IVA initiation. Interpretations of pre- and post-IVA comparisons in the CFRI study are challenging due to the older study population with high disease severity, small sample size, large CIs, and lack of a comparator arm to help contextualize the results [12].

Overall, the results from our study corroborate results observed in previous studies, strengthening the evidence that treatment with IVA may change the trajectory of CF. Additionally, the current study expands on prior studies by showing that the reduction in lung function decline observed with IVA is durable over a longer follow-up period of 5 years in the real-world setting and providing evidence that the effect of IVA treatment does not wane over time.

Because most people with CF in the United States who had received treatment with IVA were eligible to switch to the triple-combination treatment regimen of elexacaftor (ELX)/tezacaftor (TEZ)/IVA when it became available in late 2019 [19], high attrition precludes the ability to follow the IVA-treated cohort to observe the durability of treatment effect beyond 5 years. However, ELX/TEZ/IVA’s demonstrated positive impact on the rate of lung function decline in real-world data strongly suggests that the benefits of CFTRm on lung function will continue [20].

This study has several limitations. Exact matching on age and SMR weighting was used to minimize differences in disease severity and phenotype between cohorts. However, residual confounding from covariates that were not measured cannot be ruled out. Additionally, although the comparator cohort was chosen to match disease trajectory in the IVA-treated cohort, exact genotype matching was not possible. This creates a risk of confounding due to genetic heterogeneity. Additionally, results may not be generalizable to all people with a CFTR-gating mutation in the CFFPR, as people with a CFTR-gating mutation who did not receive IVA during the study period and were therefore not included in this study may be different from those included in the IVA-treated cohort.

Informative censoring, which could have occurred if prognostic factors were independent predictors of censoring risk, could also have biased this analysis. However, SMR weighting ensured that baseline characteristics were similar in both cohorts. A limited number of people had ≥ 3 ppFEV1 measurements spanning ≥ 6 months from the end of month 1 to the end of 2 years of follow-up. Thus, the study was not powered to demonstrate a significant relative reduction in the rate of lung function change through year 2 between the IVA-treated and comparator cohorts; however, the magnitude and direction of change are similar to those in other years of follow-up.

A large number of people in the IVA-treated and comparator cohorts were anticipated to initiate treatment with the combination of ELX/TEZ/IVA following its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of CF in October 2019. As such, we restricted our analysis to data collected through the end of 2019 to reduce the impact on cohort attrition. Other limitations inherent to observational research apply, such as a lack of standardized assessments and regular clinic visits.

Conclusion

A long-term, durable benefit of IVA treatment on the rate of lung function decline was observed in a real-world setting, with no evidence of this benefit decreasing over 5 years of follow-up. The reduction in the annualized rate of lung function decline observed with IVA during the initial years was maintained over the longer follow-up period of 5 years. Consistent benefit in the reduction of the rate of lung function decline was also seen in both pediatric and adult subgroups. Overall, these results expand on those from prior studies and show that IVA has a meaningful and sustained impact on reducing the rate of lung function decline in people with CF in a real-world setting.
