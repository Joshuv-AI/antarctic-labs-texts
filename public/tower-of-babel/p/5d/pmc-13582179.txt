Automated surgical phase recognition and analysis in single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy using artificial intelligence
Authors: Kezhong Tang, Chuan Shen, Hai Hu, Yizhao Zhou, Gaige Chen, Yongzhou Li, Bo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) is technically more challenging and has a steeper learning curve compared to conventional multi-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy. A deep learning-based system for surgical phase recognition and prediction in SILC can facilitate surgical quality assessment, enhance training efficiency, and support safer procedural execution. In this study, we aimed to develop a multicenter surgical phase recognition and prediction system for SILC.MethodsThis study included 148 SILC videos collected from two medical centers. Videos were labeled by surgeons, and a deep learning model was developed based on 122 videos. The performance of the model was tested in an additional 26 videos by comparing it with the annotated ground truth of the surgeon. Deep learning models were trained to identify SILC phases. The performance of models was measured. For surgical phase classification, the following metrics were used: accuracy, precision, recall, and Jaccard Index. For surgical phase prediction, a variant of mean absolute error (MAE) was adopted.ResultsThe analyzed SILC videos had a mean operative duration of 14.36 min, with considerable variability across cases. The Trans-SVNet model achieved strong performance in surgical phase classification, with overall accuracy, precision, and recall of 0.933, 0.939, and 0.939, respectively. In terms of phase transition prediction, the model yielded an overall inMAE of 37 s, eMAE of 34 s, and pMAE of 50 s. Notably, increasing the number of training cases led to significant improvements in model performance.ConclusionsWe successfully developed the Trans-SVNet model, which enabled automatic classification and temporal prediction of surgical phases in full-length SILC videos. With continued refinements, artificial intelligence could be utilized in huge data surgery analysis to achieve clinically relevant future applications.Keywords: Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Surgical phase recognition, Artificial intelligence

Surgical Process Modeling (SPM) offers numerous advantages, primarily due to its capacity to delineate distinct surgical phases. Moreover, the potential of SPM is set to grow further with the advancement of deep learning-based image recognition technologies [1]. Deep learning-based surgical phase recognition has been deployed across a range of applications, for instance, predicting the remaining operative time from intraoperative field images, supporting real-time surgical decision-making, enabling indexed retrieval of surgical videos in databases, and evaluating surgical skill through video analysis [2–4]. A critical requirement for any deep learning model deployed in the operating room is its ability to generalize effectively to novel or previously unseen intraoperative images. Recent years have witnessed remarkable breakthroughs in AI-driven systems that support real-time surgical decision-making, signaling a transformative shift in perioperative care [5]. As these technologies mature, the clinical demand for accurate, robust surgical phase recognition is anticipated to surge in the near future.

Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) has been proposed as an advanced minimally invasive approach for benign gallbladder disease, offering potential benefits such as reduced postoperative pain, better cosmetic results, and shorter hospital stays [6]. Compared with traditional three- or four-port cholecystectomy, SILC also follows a structured and standardized procedural workflow that can be recognized and learned by AI. However, SILC differs slightly in its surgical steps from conventional approaches. For example, it does not involve the step of inserting trocars into the abdominal wall. Notably, thanks to the larger single incision, many gallbladders can be extracted directly through the port without requiring a retrieval bag. Previous studies have all focused on phase recognition in conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Due to differences in surgical technique and workflow, SILC exhibits distinct procedural sequences and visual scenes compared to traditional approaches. Consequently, findings from prior studies cannot be directly transferred or applied to phase recognition in SILC.

Our goal was to develop a deep learning model for automatic surgical phase recognition in SILC videos and to assess its accuracy. We hypothesized that AI could accurately identify surgical phases, facilitating automated review, annotation, analysis, and quality evaluation of SILC procedures.

Materials and methods

Patients and dataset

SILC videos were obtained from the Second Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine (SAHZU) and the East Hospital Affiliated to Tongji University (EH). A total of 148 videos performed by Tang and Hu in 2025 were included in the study, including 20 non-insufflation SILC videos. All duplicate videos were removed. Eligibility criteria included laparoscopic recordings where a cholecystectomy was performed by single-incision port, including the entire surgical procedure. Three/four-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy was excluded. To train the model with variable surgical planes, videos with adhesion and inflammation were also included in the study. Each video was sampled at 1-s intervals, resulting in a total of 127,496 images. From 148 cases, 26 were randomly selected as the test set, and the remaining cases were used as the training set. The ratio of training to test image samples was approximately 5:1. The number of image samples per surgical phase in the training and test sets is detailed in Table 1. Given the anonymity of the videos, demographic and other personal identifying information was unavailable. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

Video annotating

All videos were anonymized by removing patient names and hospital identification numbers, and audio tracks were deleted. The videos were then converted to MPEG-4 format using CapCut Professional Version 8.5.0, with a resolution of 720 × 560 pixels and a frame rate of 25 frames per second (FPS). Two surgeons independently annotated the videos. Following annotation, one frame was extracted per second from each video. After reaching consensus, the surgeons divided the single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) procedure into six distinct phases: (1) Other; (2) Preparation; (3) Dissection of Calot’s triangle; (4) Clipping and cutting; (5) Gallbladder dissection; and (6) Gallbladder retrieval, cleaning, and coagulation. The start and end points of each phase were defined based on specific surgical actions or instrument movements (see Table 2).

Data preprocessing

This study uniformly resized the original images to 512 × 512 pixels, as opposed to the original protocol’s 256 × 256 pixels, to enhance the performance of the visual encoder. During training of the visual encoder, online data augmentation was applied to the resized images, including: Random cropping: randomly extracting a 224 × 224 region from a 250 × 250 cropped frame; Random horizontal flipping: mirroring the frame with a certain probability; Color jittering: randomly adjusting brightness, contrast, and saturation to improve robustness under varying lighting conditions.

For surgical phase classification, ground-truth phase labels were encoded as one-hot vectors for training. For surgical phase prediction, training targets were automatically generated from the phase classification labels. Specifically, for each phase, a value in the range [0, h] was assigned, where h denotes the maximum prediction horizon (in minutes). A value of 0 indicates that the phase is currently occurring, while h signifies that the phase will not occur within the next h minutes. In this study, h was set to 3 min for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Model training and testing

The experiments in this study were conducted in an environment based on PyTorch 1.12.1, Python 3.8.10, CUDA 11.3, and cuDNN 8.3.2, using an NVIDIA Tesla V100 32 GB GPU for training. The core training strategy of Trans-SVNet follows a stage-wise approach (Fig. 1): first, the visual encoder (ResNet50) is trained independently. The visual features extracted by this encoder are then used to train the temporal encoder (TCN). Once both the visual and temporal encoders are fully trained, their parameters are frozen, and their outputs are concatenated (or otherwise combined) to serve as input for training the feature fusion network (Transformer).

Trans-SVNet model architecture for model building

This study employed different evaluation metrics tailored to each task. For surgical phase classification, the following metrics were used: accuracy, precision, recall, Jaccard Index. For surgical phase prediction, a variant of mean absolute error (MAE) was adopted, specifically: inMAE: MAE computed over time segments where the ground-truth prediction labels fall within the range [0,1]; eMAE: MAE calculated over segments where the ground-truth labels are in [0,0.1], focusing on the final countdown phase; pMAE: MAE evaluated over time segments where the model’s predicted outputs lie within [0,1].

Results

Statistics used to analyze the SILC videos

The analyzed SILC videos had a mean surgical duration of 14.36 min (standard deviation [SD]: ± 6.55 min; n = 148), with substantial inter-case variability, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Among the six defined phases, P3 (Clipping and cutting) was the longest, lasting on average 3.47 ± 2.42 min, while P0 (“Other”) was the shortest, averaging 0.51 ± 0.69 min. Only videos containing at least one complete occurrence of all six phases (P0–P5) were included in the analysis, and the full duration of these videos was utilized. Discussion

In this study, the classification results using the Trans-SVNet model showed the overall accuracy, precision, and recall were 0.933, 0.939, and 0.939, respectively, which were higher than those reported in other similar studies. Conventional approaches to surgical workflow analysis typically adopt a two-stage pipeline: first, spatial features are extracted using convolutional neural networks (CNNs), followed by modeling temporal dependencies with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) [7–9]. This sequential processing often dilutes or discards critical spatial information in the final predictive representation. Moreover, although TCNs expand their receptive field through dilated convolutions, the dilation operation itself may lead to the loss of certain temporal cues. Trans-SVNet overcomes these issues by introducing the self-attention mechanism of Transformers. The core architecture of the method consists of three key components: spatiotemporal vector extraction module, hybrid feature vector aggregation module and joint optimization strategy. This integrated design allows Trans-SVNet to effectively capture both fine-grained spatial semantics and long-horizon temporal dynamics, offering a robust solution for real-time surgical workflow understanding.

The full-length LC videos we used in this study had a mean surgical duration of 14.36 min (standard deviation [SD]: ± 6.55 min; n = 148). Notably, the average surgical time in the videos contained in Cholec80 was 38.4 (SD: ± 17.0; n = 80) minutes [10]. The average surgical time of full-length LC videos reported by Shinozuka et al. used for surgical phase recognition was 94.0 min (SD: ± 41.6; n = 115) [8]. Compared with other similar studies, the SILC videos included in this work exhibit significantly shorter operative times. In single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC), there is no need for trocar insertion, resulting in a notably shorter duration for phase P0 (“Other”) compared to three-port or four-port cholecystectomy procedures. Moreover, during phase P5 (gallbladder retrieval, cleaning, and coagulation), the gallbladder can be directly extracted through the single-incision port, further reducing procedural time. Additionally, patients eligible for SILC typically present with milder gallbladder inflammation [11]. The cases included in this study exhibited less inflammation, fewer adhesions, and clearer anatomical delineation of Calot’s triangle compared to those reported in other studies. These factors likely contributed to the reduced operative duration observed in our dataset. Of course, surgeon skill level is also a critical factor influencing surgical time. Notably, all procedures in this study were performed exclusively by surgeons Tang and Hu. Future work should incorporate cases from multiple centers and additional surgeons to enable more comprehensive analysis and generalizable conclusions.

As evident from Tables 3 and 4, phases P2 and P3 are the most challenging to recognize across both classification and prediction tasks, and this difficulty is consistently reflected in the prediction results as well. Notably, however, these two phases actually contain the largest number of training and testing samples among all phases. This clearly indicates that their relatively poor recognition and prediction performance is not attributable to insufficient data or inadequate model fitting, but rather to their intrinsic complexity, making them fundamentally more difficult to distinguish than other phases. During surgical phase recognition, P2 (Calot’s triangle dissection) and P3 (Clipping and cutting) exhibit substantial temporal and visual overlap, rendering them hard to differentiate. While the start of P2 (incising the gallbladder neck) is typically clear and easily identifiable in the videos, the endpoint of P2 (achieved critical view of safety) is often ambiguous and may not be explicitly observable. In many cases, P2 is deemed to end only when P3 begins. The transition is defined by the emergence of P3 rather than a distinct termination of P2. In other words, in numerous SILC procedures, surgeons begin dissecting or clipping the cystic duct or cystic artery before achieving the critical view of safety, a key milestone traditionally associated with the start of P3. To further improve the model’s performance, there are three fundamental directions for future work: 1. Continuously collect more diverse surgical data, such as cases covering a wider range of patient ages and multicenter procedures, to enable the model to learn from a broader variety of scenarios and enhance its robustness on unseen test data; 2. explore and adopt more advanced model architectures through preliminary research to boost the model’s recognition capability; 3. merge the recognition of phases P2 and P3, which may simplify the classification task and improve overall phase recognition accuracy.

Our model holds significant potential for video-based assessment of surgical performance. Surgical videos have long been utilized to evaluate technical competence and correlate intraoperative skill with patient outcomes [12–14]. Established assessment frameworks, such as the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) and Observational Clinical Human Reliability Analysis (OCHRA), underscore the value of laparoscopic video review in objectively measuring surgical proficiency [15, 16]. Full-length operative videos offer the advantage of capturing the entire procedure, including not only technical execution and strategic decision-making but also certain nontechnical skills (e.g., situational awareness and team communication) [17]. However, reviewing complete videos is highly time intensive. To address this, prior assessment protocols often rely on edited clips that highlight only the critical phases of the operation, allowing reviewers to focus on key performance indicators without unnecessary overhead [18]. Our model can automate this curation process by accurately identifying and extracting the essential segments of SILC, thereby streamlining performance evaluation. Beyond assessment, the underlying AI system integrates multiple advanced modules, including anatomical structure detection and segmentation, Critical View of Safety (CVS) verification, and real-time safe-zone guidance, to support intraoperative decision-making and enhance procedural safety. Additionally, the model enables comparative analysis of surgical phase execution between novice and expert surgeons. Such insights can inform training curricula, help identify skill gaps, and ultimately contribute to the optimization and standardization of the SILC workflow.

This work also had a few limitations. First, this study only included surgical videos from two surgeons at two hospitals, which limits the model’s generalization capability. In the future, it is essential to continuously collect more diverse surgical data, such as cases covering a broader range of patient ages and multicenter procedures, to enable the model to learn from a wider variety of scenarios and enhance its robustness during testing. Second, in clinical practice, the boundary between surgical phases P2 and P3 is often ambiguous, which has led to reduced discrimination accuracy for these two phases in our model. To address this, we may consider merging the recognition of phases P2 and P3, which could simplify the classification task and improve the overall accuracy of phase recognition.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed the Trans-SVNet model, which enables automatic classification and temporal prediction of surgical phases in full-length SILC videos. By leveraging Hybrid Vector Aggregation Transformer technology, our approach holds promise for real-time intraoperative phase recognition, potentially enhancing surgical workflow efficiency. To advance this system toward regulatory approval as a medical device, we will conduct rigorous clinical performance evaluations and identify key challenges that must be addressed to ensure its safe and effective integration into routine clinical practice.
