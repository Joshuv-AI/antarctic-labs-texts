Myofascial pain and dysfunction as predictors of tinnitus in adults: a case-control study
Authors: Ana Cristina Mafla, María José Velasco-Burbano, Laura Sofía Peña-Argotty, Carlos Andrés Taramuel-López, Wendi Nayeli Rivadeneira-Arellano, Falk Schwendicke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the association of symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction with the presence of tinnitus in adults.MethodsA case-control study involving 80 cases presenting tinnitus and 160 controls without this condition was designed. Sociodemographic variables as well as symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction measured using the Brux-Screen, psychopathological symptoms obtained from the Symptom Checklist Revised (SCL-90-R) and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), stressful life events score quantified through the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS) and presence of tinnitus supported by items of the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI) were collected. A directed acyclic graphs (DAG) analysis was performed to identify potential relationships, and multivariable logistic regression used to assess associations.ResultsExposure to high levels of noise (OR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.14–4.60, P = 0.020), plugged ear canals (OR = 2.99; 95% CI: 1.28–7.03, P = 0.012), tense face-and-neck muscles without clenching (OR = 2.34; 95% CI: 1.24–4.43, P = 0.008) and symptoms of depression (OR = 2.17; 95% CI: 1.41–3.33, P < 0.001) significantly increased the risk of suffering from tinnitus.ConclusionFace-and-neck muscles and mental health assessments may be warranted in individuals with tinnitus. Further studies are necessary to clarify causal mechanisms and evaluate potential management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13005-026-00635-9.Keywords: Case-control study, Tinnitus, Depression, Stressful life events, Cerumen, Hearing loss

Introduction

Tinnitus is a condition characterized by the perception of sound (ring, roaring, pulsations buzzing, or other noises) that is not coming from an external source. A systematic review and meta-analysis found a prevalence of any tinnitus in adults of 14.4% (95% CI, 12.6%-16.5%) [1]. The prevalence of tinnitus in children and teens with normal hearing was reported at 4.7 to 46%, while it was 23.5–62.2% in children and teens with hearing loss [2]. Tinnitus has been classified as pulsatile or non-pulsatile, and reported risk factors include hearing loss, ototoxic medication, head injury, depression [3] and ageing [4]. While rarely associated with a serious medical condition, tinnitus may be debilitating, influencing the ability to sleep or concentrate [5] as well as overall quality of life. There is no remedy for tinnitus, but there are ways to reduce symptoms such as the use of sound therapy devices (including hearing aids), medications, cognitive behavior therapy [6, 7] and multimodal psychosomatic therapy approaches aim to address individually constellated medical, audiological and psychological influences [8]. A short-acoustic test has been shown to be potentially effective to guide therapies for tinnitus control [9].

Tinnitus can arise when inner ear damage alters the signals transmitted by nerves to the brain’s sound-processing centers. Although the noise may feel like it is coming from the ear, it is produced in the brain, specifically in the auditory cortex. Research suggests that irregular interactions between this auditory region and other brain networks may contribute to tinnitus. The auditory cortex is connected to areas responsible for attention and emotions, and studies have found that individuals with tinnitus often show changes in these non-auditory regions of the brain [5].

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are other highly prevalent conditions that affect about 10–30% of the adult population. They include myofascial pain and dysfunction related to bruxism (clenching and grinding teeth while awake or sleeping). Among patients with these TMDs, more than half also display tinnitus, while conversely, in patients with tinnitus, nearly everybody suffers from these TMDs [10]. Although the association between tinnitus and these TMDs has been frequently reported, this association may have a great variability. However, the presence of painful TMJ without sleep bruxism was significantly associated with tinnitus [11] reflecting more muscles tension by themselves. A compressive effect on the retromandibular vein and external jugular vein which may be associated with pulsatile tinnitus, a synchronous heartbeat in the ear or ears, for example. Moreover, no significant associations were found between tinnitus intensity and age, freeway space, sleep bruxism, presence of clicking, or headache [12].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the association of symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction with the presence of tinnitus in adults. We hypothesized that face-and-neck muscles tension (without clenching or grinding teeth) was associated with tinnitus.

Materials and methods

Study, sample and setting

Our reporting follows the STROBE guidelines [13].

Sample size was set for an expected OR = 2.4, and α = 0.05 (two-sided, Z = 1.96), power = 0.80 (Z = 0.84), a proportion exposed among controls (p₀ = 0.20) and a case-control ratio = 1:2 (k = 2). 80 cases and 160 controls (n = 240) were matched according to gender and age groups. Data collection was performed between July 2024 and March 2025 within an academic population (individuals who are engaged in teaching, research, administrative tasks and those who provide support services and operational functions) at Universidad Cooperativa de Colombia, from Pasto, Colombia. To reduce selection bias, researchers recruited the target participants (cases of tinnitus) using a word-of-mouth communication technique, therefore we asked about this condition in rooms and places of work of this academic population.

Cases and controls

Cases

Tinnitus was defined as the experience of sound when there is no actual external source of that sound. It is often described as a ringing in the ears, buzzing, hissing, or other noises such as hearing the own pulse hammering in the ear temporarily, cyclical or continuously affecting one or both ears [5], and to support case identification, we inquired about suffering from any type of difficulties caused by tinnitus using items from the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory [14]. Study participants were ≥ 18 years old and currently perceived symptoms of tinnitus. Participants who did not fill out the whole questionnaire for different reasons such as not having enough time, privacy, the topic not being relevant for them, concerns about questionnaire length, physical (acute pain or fractures that impeded to write or concentrate in selecting responses) and mental disturbances (fatigue, or emotional distress requiring pharmacological support) were excluded.

Controls

We included individuals ≥ 18 years old that did not report a perception of tinnitus and that were matched by group of age and gender, sampled from the same academic population. Individuals whose questionnaire answers were incomplete (same reasons as for cases) were excluded.

Measures

The survey was conducted by four trained dental students who interviewed the participants based on a questionnaire that contained different variables, as follows:

Sociodemographic information

Age was measured as a continuous variable; gender (classified as male, female and non-binary) according to World Health Organization [15]; place of birth (catalogued as Pasto (capital) and other); residency (coded as Pasto (capital) and other); living zone (classified as rural and urban); socioeconomic status (catalogued as low, middle and high but dichotomized as low and middle/high according to the public service superintendence [16]); marital status (catalogued as singles or married); children (coded as have or not to have children); type of health insurance (classified as none, public (subsidized), private (contributory) and special); education (coded as none and primary studies, secondary studies, and university studies) and job/occupation (unemployed, looking for a job, employee, independent and students).

Tinnitus

Data about place where tinnitus is heard (coded as right ear, left ear, and right and left ears), presence of other primary and secondary causes of this condition [17] associated with tinnitus “coded as yes and not”, such as exposed to a high level of noise, hearing loss, current or previous ear infection, ear canal plugged up with earwax, head injuries, and neck injuries. Additionally, we inquired about whether participants had medical conditions or were taking some medicines, also “coded as yes and not”. The participants were asked to detail what type of medical condition and medications they had at the moment.

Symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction

The Brux-Screen questionnaire developed by Lobbezzoo et al. [18] was utilized to determine the presence of symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction (bruxism and jaw symptoms) such as clenching and grinding of teeth during sleep or while being awake, holding the teeth together while being awake, tense muscles without clenching or bringing teeth together, as well as, experiencing symptoms in temple, face, jaw or jaw joint pain upon awaking such as pain, tension, and stiffness.

Psychopathological symptoms and stressful life events

Our questionnaire also addressed the presence of psychopathological symptoms such as anxiety, depression, stress and stressful life events. The Symptom Checklist Revised (SCL-90-R) [19] translated into Spanish [20], and validated previously in a Colombian sample [21, 22] was used to determine symptoms of anxiety and depression. Symptoms of stress in the last month were measured using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [23–25], which has been validated in a Colombian population before [26] and permission to use was granted by the author [27]. Moreover, stressful life events were measured with the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS) which consists of the measurement of 43 life events, ranging from major events like death of a partner to more everyday events like changing jobs. A score of ≤ 150 suggests low stress, 151–299 suggests moderate stress, and ≥ 300 suggests high stress, which is associated with an 80% chance of developing a stress-related disorder [28]. This scale has been translated and validated into Spanish [29] and validated in a Colombian population [30]. The SCL-90-R, PSS-10 and SRRS were described as continuous variables. Higher scores of these instruments indicate higher symptoms of anxiety, depression, stress and increased scores of stressful life events.

Pre-test evaluation

A pre-test questionnaire evaluation was performed on 12 participants sampled from the sampling frame but not participating in the main study. After this process, some adjustments were made to the pre-test questionnaire as the participants did not understand some questions. Three additional items derived from the question “How often do you firmly hold, tighten or tense muscles while awake without clenching or bringing teeth together?” were included in the Bruxism section. More detailed information about the places of the affected muscles were explained, and figures showing face and neck muscles involved such as temporalis, masseter, or sternocleidomas added. For jaw symptoms, we picturized the places mentioned in the question “How often you experience these symptoms in your temple, face, jaw or jaw joint”. Additionally, the item “I don’t know” in the Bruxism section was removed as it altered the Likert scale proposed in the instrument.

Control of bias

The study used validated instruments to minimize information bias. Moreover, to assess symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction the questionnaire included figures of the face and neck muscles’ location.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis included frequencies and percentages, and age, psychopathological symptoms and stressful life events were measured using means and standard deviations. A directed acyclic graphs (DAG) analysis was performed to identify covariates that best line up with clinical diagnosis, literature and to estimate a better fit model (https://www.dagitty.net/) (Fig. 1). Kolmogorov-Smirnov (n > 50) normal distribution test was calculated, and Student’s t test or U Mann Whitney test were used for group comparison. A multivariable logistic regression model used to explore associations, with Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using statistical software SPSS for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, USA).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 240 respondents participated, among them 80 cases and 160 controls. The mean age of the respondents was 32.88 ± 16.12 years. The majority were women (62.1%), born outside Pasto (capital) (56.7%), residing in the capital (71.3%), living in the urban area (85.8%). 72.5% belonged to a low socioeconomic status (SES), most were singles (75.4%), without children (60.0%), with public health insurance (73.3%), with university education (53.8%) and with different occupations. There were statistical differences in socioeconomic status (P = 0.032) and type of health insurance (P = 0.024) between cases-controls groups (Table 1).

Features of tinnitus cases

According to the place where the case group heard tinnitus, 23 (28.7%) heard it in the right ear, 26 (32.5%) in the left ear, and 31 (38.8%) in both ears. 42 (52.5%) had additionally pulsatile tinnitus, among them, 16 (38.1%) heard it in the right ear, 12 (28.6%) in the left ear, and 14 (33.3%) in both, right and left ears. 28 (35.0%) had been exposed to a high level of noise, 13 (16.3%) had hearing loss, 5 (6.3%) had current or previous ear infection, 19 (23.8%) had their ears canal plugged up with earwax, 4 (5.0%) had had head injuries, and 2 (2.5%) neck injuries. 20 (25%) had a disease such as hypertension, gastritis, irritable bowel syndrome, migraine, among others; and 16 (20.0%) was taking medicines such as Losartan or Captopril (angiotensin II receptors), ibuprofen, vitamins, among others. There were no statistical differences by gender, except for having been exposed to a high level of noise being higher in males (17, 54.8%) than females (11, 22.4%) (P = 0.003).

Discussion

Tinnitus, characterized by the perception of sound in the absence of an external auditory stimulus, remains a complex and often debilitating condition affecting a significant percentage of the population. The present findings contribute to the growing body of literature by highlighting both the variability of tinnitus experiences and the multifactorial nature of its etiology. We observed associations between exposure to high levels of noise, face-and-neck muscles tension in patients who do not report to clench, impacted cerumen, symptoms of depression, and the presence of tinnitus.

Acute exposure to loud sound levels could induce cochlear synaptic degeneration without causing permanent hearing loss, and may contribute to tinnitus perception in individuals with normal hearing sensitivity [31, 32]. Noise exposure has been associated with continuous tinnitus in young adults [33]. A short-acoustic test has been shown to potentially predict the effectiveness of therapies for tinnitus control [9].

Myofascial pain and dysfunction related to bruxism have been found to decrease cortical activation pattern, indicating decreased blood flow and decreased neuronal activation. Consequently, bruxism, as an oral motor disorder, concerns not only dentistry, but neurology and psychiatry [34]. Although specific symptoms of bruxism could be associated with the presence of tinnitus, we did not find clenching or grinding associated, but face-and-neck muscles tension. Such tension may produce a compressive effect on the retromandibular and external jugular vein producing a synchronous heartbeat in the ear or ears. Severe pulsatile tinnitus has been associated with a bilateral dilated mastoid emissary vein [35].

The presence of impacted cerumen was also associated with tinnitus in our study. Cerumen production is a normal and protective process for the ear canal. However, cerumen should be removed when it causes symptoms such as hearing loss, itching, pain, or tinnitus, or if it prevents assessment of the external auditory canal, the tympanic membrane, or audiovestibular system [36]. Interestingly, cerumen is typically expelled from the ear canal spontaneously via a self-cleaning mechanism that is assisted by jaw movement [37]. If there is face-and-neck muscles tension, this mechanism may be less effective.

Additionally, consistent with previous research, our results suggest that psychopathological symptoms such as depression may exacerbate tinnitus severity, underscoring the need for a multidisciplinary approach to treatment. In a retrospective cohort study including over 140 million patients, researchers observed that pulsatile tinnitus patients had significantly higher rates of pre-existing generalized anxiety disorder and depression [38]. A European multi-country study concluded that becoming widowed (a life stressful event) increased the risk of severe tinnitus, too [39].

In summary, there were differences between cases and controls according to SES. Notably, tinnitus was moderately more frequent in individuals with middle/high compared with low SES. This is in line with data from ecological analyses showing that anxiety and depressive disorders are more frequent in high- versus low-income countries, variables measured in this study [40].

The strengths of this study lie in its design and analytic pipeline. Employing a case-control study allowed us to efficiently sample individuals and power our analyses while assessing multiple risk factors for our target outcome, tinnitus. Using DAG analysis also allowed us to capture the complexity of the presence of tinnitus.

The findings of our study contribute to the body of knowledge by confirming that variables such as tensed facial-and-neck muscles without clenching or bringing teeth together or those related to emotional disturbances, could have an important impact on tinnitus apparition. Moreover, these results extend prior research by revealing that depression probably derived from stressful life events could be a relevant cause of tinnitus. Furthermore, our study provides empirical evidence supporting the multivariate nature of tinnitus apparition addressing gaps identified in earlier works. These findings also could guide work policies, clinical practice, or education by providing evidence-based recommendations not only in dental but in medical fields.

Future research should focus on expanding the sample size and including more diverse populations to enhance the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, longitudinal studies would allow us to assess internal and external factors associated with tinnitus and to establish temporal relationships. Further investigation into emotional disturbances could provide deeper insights of the mechanisms underlying the observed outcomes. Integrating other methodologies, such as qualitative approaches, may also enrich future studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, overall case-control studies often rely on relatively small samples. This may limit statistical power, increasing the risk of type II errors and sometimes small numbers in subgroups may be difficult to conduct stratified analyses. Second, findings may not be generalizable, as both cases and controls are often recruited from specific settings. Moreover, selection of controls may reduce representativeness and external validity. Third, case-control studies cannot establish temporal relationships and causality. Hence, we cannot determine whether the exposure truly caused the disease or if the disease caused the exposure, e.g. depression or muscles tension. Fourth, reliance on self-reported data – as in our study – may introduce inaccuracies due to memory lapses, misunderstanding of questions, or social desirability bias. Misreporting may differ between cases and controls, leading to differential misclassification, especially for those cases with mild and very mild tinnitus. Participants who had the disease might be more prone to remember or emphasize certain exposures like stress or muscles tension, while controls might not recall those same exposures as accurately.

Conclusion

There is a complex network of risk factors related to tinnitus. However, symptoms of myofascial pain and dysfunction, among other physical factors (exposure to high levels of noise or ear canal plugged up) and psychopathological symptoms (depression), significantly increased the presence of tinnitus in adult population. We observed a statistically significant association between face-and-neck muscles being tense without clenching and presence of tinnitus (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.24–4.43, P = 0.008) evaluated using a generalized linear model. These findings are potentially useful for broadening diagnostics and addressing modifiable risk factors which may enhance tinnitus relief. Further investigation into emotional disturbances could provide deeper insights of the mechanisms underlying the observed outcomes.
