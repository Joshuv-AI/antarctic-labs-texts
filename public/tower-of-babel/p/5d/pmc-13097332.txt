Phase‐based analysis of ICD‐10 psychiatric diagnoses in outpatients and inpatients after the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake
Authors: Yumeno Furukawa, Reiko Hashimoto, Hiroaki Kihara, Tatsuya Nagasawa, Yusuke Nitta, Takashi Uehara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study examined temporal changes in patterns of clinically treated psychiatric conditions during the first year after the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake. Although post‐disaster psychiatric symptoms often peak within the first year, prior studies have frequently lacked standardized phase definitions. We applied disaster‐response phases defined in the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare manual to clarify phase‐specific diagnostic trends and inform allocation of psychiatric resources.MethodsAmong 869 new outpatients and 133 new inpatients recorded at Kanazawa Medical University Hospital, the tertiary medical center closest to the epicenter, between January and December 2024, we analyzed 80 first‐time outpatients and 29 newly admitted inpatients reporting earthquake‐related damage. Medical records were reviewed for demographics, ICD‐10 primary diagnoses, disaster‐related stressors, and timing of presentation categorized by disaster‐response phases.ResultsPsychiatric presentations were concentrated within the first month after the disaster, with secondary increases around 7 months and a modest rise at 12 months among outpatients. Most cases occurred during Stage 4 (mid‐term recovery; Months 2–12). Among outpatients, neurotic, stress‐related, and somatoform disorders (F4; 37.5%) and mood disorders (F3; 27.5%) were most frequent, whereas among inpatients, mood disorders (F3; 44.8%) and schizophrenia spectrum disorders (F2; 24.1%) predominated. Mood disorders were prevalent from the early phase, while F4 disorders increased later. Relocation and housing‐related problems were common stressors.ConclusionClinically treated psychiatric conditions showed phase‐dependent patterns, underscoring the need for phase‐specific interventions, including early management of mood disorders, mid‐term support for displacement‐related stress, and long‐term monitoring for symptom re‐emergence.

INTRODUCTION

Japan is one of the most earthquake‐prone countries in the world. Since around 2018, intermittent seismic activity has been observed in the Noto Peninsula of Ishikawa Prefecture, with a marked increase beginning in December 2020. On January 1, 2024, a magnitude 7.6 earthquake struck the Noto Peninsula, causing substantial crustal deformation and triggering a tsunami that inflicted extensive damage on the region. 1 , 2 This event was the strongest earthquake recorded in the area since official records began in 1885. Although smaller than the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake (GEJE) (M9.0), it exceeded in magnitude the 1995 Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake (M7.3) and the 2016 Kumamoto Earthquake (M6.5). As of July 30, 2025, the disaster had resulted in 592 confirmed deaths (including disaster‐related fatalities), two missing persons, and approximately 1395 injuries, with all evacuation centers closed by March 2025. 3

Natural disasters such as earthquakes profoundly affect the onset and recurrence of mental disorders. 4 , 5 , 6 Previous studies have reported increased rates of posttraumatic stress disorder, anxiety disorders, and major depressive disorder in the aftermath of earthquakes. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Although many affected individuals demonstrate psychological resilience, 16 , 17 others develop delayed or chronic psychiatric conditions, including post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), over time. 10 , 18 , 19 Together, these findings underscore the importance of sustained mental health assessment and intervention beyond the acute phase of disasters. 11 , 20 , 21

Longitudinal studies following the GEJE have demonstrated that disaster‐related psychological distress and posttraumatic stress reactions follow long‐term and dynamic trajectories. Community‐based cohort studies and large‐scale syntheses of psychosocial support activities have shown that mental health consequences persist well beyond the acute phase, emphasizing the need for phase‐appropriate and sustained mental health interventions. 22 , 23

Building on these long‐term findings, the current study addresses a distinct gap by focusing on clinically treated psychiatric morbidity during the first post‐disaster year following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake. Whereas prior GEJE studies primarily examined community‐based cohorts and long‐term psychosocial outcomes, our study systematically evaluates time‐dependent changes in the Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10) psychiatric diagnoses using clearly defined disaster‐response phases in a tertiary medical setting, thereby clarifying the timing and nature of acute and subacute mental health needs after a major earthquake.

Several studies have further examined the relationship between time since a disaster and mental health outcomes. Psychiatric symptoms often peak within the first post‐disaster year, 10 with some evidence of gradual declines in depressive symptoms over time. 15 , 24 However, despite important longitudinal evidence derived from the GEJE, many studies have focused on single time points or later post‐disaster periods, 25 with limited data capturing changes from the immediate aftermath. Consequently, research focusing on the first post‐disaster year is particularly critical, as psychiatric symptoms often reach their highest prevalence during this period.

One challenge in such research is the absence of standardized definitions for post‐disaster timeframes. The model by Meyers and Zunin describes six conceptual phases—Predisaster, Impact, Heroic, Honeymoon, Disillusionment, and Reconstruction—but lacks precise temporal boundaries. 26 Kim's model, widely used in Japan, outlines four phases—Heroic, Honeymoon, Disillusionment, and Reconstruction—but the duration of each phase varies across studies, limiting its utility for longitudinal analysis. 20 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

In response to this limitation, the present study examined the temporal progression of psychiatric diagnoses following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake using the clearly defined disaster phases specified in the Manual for Municipal Mental Health, Medical Care, and Welfare Activities in Disasters issued by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. 27 These standardized timeframes allow for structured and consistent temporal analysis.

The objective of this study was to investigate time‐dependent changes in ICD‐10 psychiatric diagnostic categories among patients who sought mental health care after the earthquake. The findings aim to (1) enhance understanding of the psychological impact of large‐scale disasters such as the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake and (2) inform evidence‐based strategies for the appropriate allocation of psychiatric resources to healthcare institutions serving disaster‐affected populations.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were recruited from patients who made their first visit to the Department of Neuropsychiatry at Kanazawa Medical University Hospital (KMUH) between January 1 and December 31, 2024, as well as from patients newly admitted during the same period.

KMUH is located in Uchinada Town, at the entrance to the Noto Peninsula, and is the university hospital closest to the epicenter of the earthquake. Its Department of Psychiatry serves as a major provider of mental healthcare for the Noto region. In 2024, the year in which the earthquake occurred, patients from the Noto region accounted for 67.8% of all outpatient visits and 69.1% of all inpatient admissions at KMUH. Within the Department of Psychiatry, patients from the Noto region comprised 61.6% of outpatient visits and 57.7% of inpatient admissions. These figures indicate that KMUH allocates a substantial proportion of its medical resources to the provision of care for patients from the Noto region.

Within Ishikawa Prefecture, KMUH and Kanazawa University Hospital (KUH) are the only tertiary centers providing psychiatric services. While KUH primarily serves as a central academic and referral hospital for the entire prefecture, KMUH—owing to its geographic proximity to the Noto Peninsula—plays a key role in delivering both inpatient and outpatient psychiatric care to the affected region. Leveraging this position, KMUH has also been actively involved in community‐based mental health services, particularly those targeting older adults and dementia care. 31 In addition, the Ishikawa Prefectural Hospital of Mental Health is the only designated disaster base psychiatric hospital in the prefecture and serves as a central institution for specialized and long‐term psychiatric care.

Following the earthquake, Noto General Hospital—the sole provider of psychiatric emergency services in the Noto region—experienced disaster‐related functional disruptions, including prolonged water outages. Furthermore, several other public hospitals in the Noto region were affected by infrastructure damage, resulting in a substantial reduction in regional emergency and inpatient psychiatric capacity. 32 Under these circumstances, Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Teams (DPATs) were coordinated at the prefectural level, and multiple core institutions—including KMUH, KUH, and the Ishikawa Prefectural Hospital of Mental Health—independently dispatched DPATs to support psychiatric medical care and mental health activities in the affected areas. These institutions also collaborated in establishing systems for accepting patients requiring involuntary psychiatric hospitalization. Municipalities with seismic intensity ≥ 6 were identified using data from the Japan Meteorological Agency, and the distribution of psychiatric facilities in the Noto region was visualized (Figure 1).

In the surrounding areas, the earthquake caused building collapses, water shortages, power outages, and soil liquefaction. 33 KMUH sustained only minor damage and was able to continue regular outpatient services while providing disaster‐related medical care in the immediate aftermath. 34 Following the New Year public holidays in Japan (January 1–3), outpatient services resumed on January 4. During the study period at the Department of Neuropsychiatry, KMUH recorded 869 new outpatients (464 men and 405 women) and 133 inpatients (46 men and 87 women).

The analysis included first‐time outpatients who reported earthquake‐related damage, identified based on the date of their first outpatient visit, as well as newly admitted inpatients with earthquake‐related damage documented in their pre‐admission medical history, whose disaster phase was determined based on the date of hospital admission. Patients seen solely for consultation‐liaison psychiatry in non‐psychiatric departments were excluded, as the direct impact of the earthquake on their mental health could not be clearly determined.

The final analytic sample comprised 80 first‐time outpatients and 29 newly admitted inpatients (Figure 2).

Disaster phase–based classification

The disaster‐response stages were defined as follows: Stage 0 (preparedness), the period prior to the earthquake (on or before December 31, 2023); Stage 1 (activation), January 1–4, 2024; Stage 2 (operations), January 5–21; Stage 3 (transition), January 22–31; Stage 4 (mid‐term), February 1 to December 31, 2024; and Stage 5 (long‐term), from January 1, 2025 onward.

Stage 1 corresponds to the initial post‐disaster period from January 1 to January 4, 2024. Because January 1–3 coincided with the New Year public holidays, outpatient services were limited, and no eligible patients presented during Stage 1.

Data collection

This retrospective study extracted the following information from electronic medical records: sex, age, history of psychiatric treatment, primary diagnosis (classified according to the ICD‐10), and the month of the first outpatient visit for first‐time outpatients or the month of admission for newly admitted inpatients. Data on earthquake‐related damage were obtained from the outpatient first‐visit record and the inpatient admission record.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki (1995, as revised in Seoul 2008) and carried out with the consent of the university medical research ethics review committees at the authors' universities (No. C162). Participants provided informed consent using an opt‐out procedure. Informed consent documents were posted in the outpatient waiting area and inpatient wards. The documents informed participants about the purpose and importance of the study, the research methods, the voluntary nature of participation, and the anonymity and confidentiality of their responses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and age group frequency distributions) were calculated for age. Independent samples t‐tests were used to examine differences in mean age between outpatients and inpatients, and chi‐squared tests were applied to assess sex differences and psychiatric treatment history. Primary diagnoses were classified according to ICD‐10 codes, and frequency distributions were calculated. For outpatients, the month of first visit was analyzed; for inpatients, the month of admission was examined. Frequency distributions of earthquake‐related damage were calculated for both groups.

Because the duration of disaster‐response phases differed substantially, a Poisson regression model with a log link function was employed to compare patient incidence rates across 13 temporal categories, including Stage 2, Stage 3, and the monthly intervals within Stage 4. Log‐transformed exposure time (days or months, as appropriate) was included as an offset variable to adjust for differences in the length of each period. The same modeling approach was applied in a secondary analysis restricted to Stage 4 to compare monthly incidence rates.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM spss Statistics, version 22 and 29, Advanced Analytics, Inc, Tokyo, Japan.

RESULTS

Overall comparison between outpatients and inpatients

Among 80 outpatients, 41 (51.3%) were men and 39 (48.8%) were women, with a mean age of 54.0 years (SD = 22.8). Among 29 inpatients, 11 (37.9%) were men and 18 (62.1%) were women, with a mean age of 55.7 years (SD = 20.4). A chi‐square test showed no significant difference in sex distribution between outpatients and inpatients (χ 2(1) = 0.51, p = 0.22). Similarly, an independent samples t‐test indicated no significant difference in mean age between the two groups (t(107) = −0.36, p = 0.72). A history of psychiatric treatment was reported by 42 outpatients (52.5%) and 27 inpatients (93.1%) (Table 2).

Outpatients

No significant sex difference was observed among outpatients (χ 2(1) = 0.05, p = 0.82). The most common age group was the 70s (20.0%), followed by the 50s (16.3%) and 20s (15.0%). The proportion of patients with versus without prior psychiatric treatment did not significantly deviate from an equal distribution (χ 2(1) = 0.20, p = 0.66).

The most frequent primary diagnoses were neurotic, stress‐related, and somatoform disorders (ICD‐10 F4; 37.5%), followed by mood (affective) disorders (F3; 27.5%) (Table 2).

Inpatients

No significant sex difference was found among inpatients (χ 2(1) = 1.69, p = 0.19). The most common age group was the 60s (27.6%), followed by the 20s, 50s, and 80s (13.8% each). A significantly greater proportion had a history of psychiatric treatment than expected (93.1%; χ 2(1) = 21.55, p < 0.001).

The most frequent diagnoses were mood disorders (F3; 44.8%), schizophrenia, schizotypal, and delusional disorders (F2; 24.1%), and F4 disorders (13.8%) (Table 2).

A history of psychiatric treatment was frequent in Stage 2 (81.8%) and Stage 3 (80.0%) but declined markedly in Stage 4 (45.3%).

The most frequently reported earthquake‐related impact among outpatients was “relocation (n = 36),” followed by “housing damage (n = 26),” and “residence in evacuation shelters (n = 19).” Certain impacts, including “bereavement of a close person” and “relocation to temporary housing,” were reported exclusively in Stage 4 (Figure 5).

The “other” category includes cases that could not be assigned to predefined categories, such as “contracted gastroenteritis at an evacuation center,” “reduced outings after the earthquake,” and “harshly criticized by evacuees staying at a relative's home.”

To clarify whether the high number of cases observed during Stage 4 was attributable to its extended duration, a Poisson regression analysis was conducted. The omnibus likelihood ratio test demonstrated a significant effect of temporal category on outpatient counts (χ 2(12) = 30.53, p = 0.002), which was supported by the Type III Wald test (χ 2(12) = 27.11, p = 0.007). Using Stage 3 as the reference category, incidence rates were significantly lower at 9–11 months post‐disaster. Although outpatient counts at 7 and 8 months were numerically higher than those in adjacent months, these differences did not reach statistical significance. At 12 months post‐disaster, outpatient incidence did not differ significantly from the reference level, indicating that the reduction observed during Months 9–11 was not sustained.

To further examine temporal variation within Stage 4, a secondary Poisson regression analysis incorporating an offset for the number of days in each month was performed. The omnibus likelihood ratio test showed no statistically significant overall effect of month (χ 2(10) = 17.26, p = 0.069), consistent with the Type III Wald test (χ 2(10) = 13.77, p = 0.184). No individual month differed significantly from the reference category.

Temporal distribution of inpatient admissions across disaster phases

Of the 29 inpatients, 6 were in Stage 2, 1 in Stage 3, and 22 in Stage 4 (Table 4). The distribution differed significantly across stages (χ 2(2) = 24.897, p < 0.001), with more patients than expected in Stage 4 (+12.3) and fewer in Stage 3 (−8.7). A history of psychiatric treatment was uniformly present in Stage 2 (100%) and Stage 3 (100%), and remained high in Stage 4 (90.9%). Diagnostic patterns varied by stage: in Stage 2, most patients were diagnosed with F3 disorders (66.7%); in Stage 3, all patients were diagnosed with F2 disorders (100%); and in Stage 4, F3 disorders predominated (40.9%), followed by F2 (27.3%) and F4 disorders (18.2%) (Table 4). To evaluate whether the number of inpatient admissions observed during the disaster‐response period was influenced by differences in phase duration, a Poisson regression analysis was conducted, incorporating exposure time as an offset variable. The analysis included 13 temporal categories: Stage 2, Stage 3, and individual monthly intervals within Stage 4.

The omnibus likelihood ratio test showed no significant overall effect of temporal category on inpatient admission rates (χ 2(12) = 15.90, p = 0.196), consistent with the Type III Wald test (χ 2(12) = 17.16, p = 0.14). Using the 12‐month post‐disaster period as the reference category, no individual period demonstrated a statistically significant difference in admission rates. Although admission rates were numerically higher during Stage 2 and the early months of Stage 4 (Months 2–5) and showed a transient decline during Months 9–11, these variations did not reach statistical significance. At 12 months post‐disaster, admission rates were comparable to those observed earlier in Stage 4.

To further assess monthly variation within Stage 4, a secondary Poisson regression analysis incorporating an offset for exposure time was performed. No significant overall effect of month was detected (χ 2(10) = 6.59, p = 0.763), and no individual month differed significantly from the reference category, indicating that inpatient admission rates remained relatively stable throughout Stage 4.

DISCUSSION

This study leveraged a unique circumstance in which the university hospital closest to the epicenter of the Noto Peninsula Earthquake was able to promptly resume medical services and begin treating patients immediately after the disaster. This allowed for the examination of temporal changes in ICD‐10 diagnostic classifications from the immediate aftermath through subsequent recovery phases, using the clearly defined framework provided by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

Previous studies have identified female gender as a factor associated with increased vulnerability to the psychological effects of earthquakes. 10 , 13 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 However, in the present study, no significant gender differences were observed among either outpatients or inpatients.

With regard to age, the predominance of older adults in both outpatient and inpatient populations is partly consistent with the markedly aged population structure of the most severely affected areas of the Noto Peninsula. 42 Notably, a substantial proportion of younger adults was also observed, suggesting that the mental health impact of the earthquake extended beyond older populations and across a broad age range. This finding underscores the need for disaster mental health interventions that address age‐specific vulnerabilities across the lifespan. Although previous studies have suggested that older adults may be particularly vulnerable to the psychosocial consequences of disasters, 6 , 10 , 13 , 35 , 38 the present findings indicate that post‐disaster psychiatric morbidity cannot be attributed solely to age‐related vulnerability. Importantly, the Noto Peninsula is characterized by a high aging rate, which may have influenced the observed diagnostic patterns, particularly the prevalence of mood disorders, dementia‐related conditions, and hospitalizations among older adults.

Consistent with earlier studies, 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 the most common outpatient diagnoses were F4 (stress‐related disorders, including PTSD and anxiety disorders) and F3 (mood disorders), whereas inpatients most frequently had F3, followed by F2 (schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders) and F4. Notably, outpatients consistently exhibited high rates of F3 diagnoses (>25%) across all phases, while F4 diagnoses increased over time and became predominant in Stage 4. The emergence of F0 (organic mental disorders, including dementia) after Stage 3 further underscores the diverse and evolving needs of affected populations. Among inpatients, F3 remained predominant, but the later increase in F2 and F4, as well as the appearance of F0 in Stage 4—particularly among older adults—suggests that both chronic psychiatric conditions and age‐related vulnerabilities were exacerbated as recovery progressed.

The relatively high proportion of F2 diagnoses among inpatients aligns with prior findings that individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders are at heightened risk of admission in the aftermath of natural disasters. 43 This pattern may reflect the disruption of essential support systems, including family caregiving and continuity of care, as reported in earlier research. 44 Moreover, the overrepresentation of patients with a prior psychiatric history supports the view that pre‐existing vulnerabilities significantly increase the risk of hospitalization following disasters. 10 , 39 Taken together, these findings indicate that outpatient and inpatient populations exhibited distinct temporal trajectories of psychiatric morbidity shaped by disaster‐related stressors, aging, and pre‐existing conditions, underscoring the need for interventions tailored to both the disaster phase and population subgroup.

Disaster‐related stressors were broadly similar across patient groups but differed in prevalence. Outpatients most frequently reported relocation, followed by housing damage, shelter residence, and increased household size. In contrast, inpatients most commonly reported shelter residence, followed by relocation and housing damage. Such stressors—including loss of home, community disruption, and prolonged displacement—are well‐recognized contributors to depressive and anxiety disorders. 45 Among older adults, housing damage and relocation have also been associated with cognitive decline and delirium, 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 underscoring the importance of age‐sensitive interventions. Certain stressors, such as relocation to temporary housing, were observed exclusively in Stage 4. Additionally, among inpatients, “other” stressors were most frequently reported, and the diversity of stressors increased over time, highlighting the need for individualized support.

Although our primary analyses were based on the stages defined in the manual, the first 4 weeks after a disaster are widely recognized as a critical period for relief operations. For example, the United Nations Disaster Assessment and Coordination (UNDAC) designates the first 3–4 weeks as the initial response phase, 43 and the Japanese Guideline for Local Mental Health Care Activities after a Disaster similarly defines the first month as the initial response period. 50 Previous studies have shown that during this time, the number of patients seeking medical care increases, with high rates of acute stress disorder, 51 PTSD, 45 depression, and generalized anxiety disorder. 52 Consistent with these findings, in our study, the largest number of outpatient visits occurred within the first month (Stages 2 and 3; 20.0%), and the highest proportion of inpatients was also admitted during this period (25.0%). Both outpatients and inpatients in the present study included a substantial proportion of individuals with a prior psychiatric history, supporting earlier reports that people with pre‐existing psychiatric vulnerabilities are particularly affected by disasters. 10 Large‐scale outpatient studies conducted after the GEJE demonstrated a transient increase in new psychiatric outpatients during the disaster year, followed by a return to predisaster levels in subsequent years, with stress‐related disorders peaking early and declining thereafter. 53 These findings indicate that, at a population level, clinically treated psychiatric morbidity tends to peak in the early post‐disaster phase and subsequently normalize.

Consistent with this broader epidemiological pattern, the present hospital‐based study following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake revealed a more fine‐grained temporal trajectory. Psychiatric consultations were concentrated in the acute post‐disaster phase and declined thereafter; however, early consultations were predominantly accounted for by individuals with pre‐existing psychiatric disorders, whereas consultations among individuals without prior psychiatric history increased over time. This temporal shift is concordant with recent DPAT‐based observations after the same disaster, which similarly reported an early predominance of patients already receiving psychiatric treatment and a gradual increase in dementia‐related problems (F0) and direct stress reactions (F4) over time. 54

Taken together, these findings across outpatient clinic data, tertiary hospital data, and DPAT activity underscore a key implication for disaster mental health preparedness: individuals with pre‐existing psychiatric disorders constitute the primary group requiring immediate support, whereas disaster‐related psychiatric needs among previously untreated individuals tend to emerge progressively over time.

Against this broader epidemiological background, outpatient visits in the present study exhibited greater temporal fluctuation across disaster‐response phases, whereas inpatient admissions remained relatively stable after adjustment for phase duration. In addition, a substantially higher proportion of inpatients had a prior history of psychiatric treatment compared with outpatients. This contrast suggests that outpatient service utilization may be more sensitive to temporal changes in post‐disaster conditions and healthcare‐seeking behavior, including delayed help‐seeking and situational distress emerging over time. By contrast, inpatient admissions may represent a more clinically stable demand, driven predominantly by pre‐existing psychiatric vulnerability and illness severity, and thus may be less responsive to short‐term temporal variation across disaster phases. Taken together, these findings imply that post‐disaster outpatient and inpatient psychiatric services may serve partially distinct populations: outpatient services capturing fluctuating psychosocial needs over time, and inpatient services addressing more persistent and severe conditions among individuals with established psychiatric histories. However, given the marked difference in baseline psychiatric treatment history between the two groups, as well as the exploratory nature of the analyses and the limited sample size, these interpretations should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.

Because Stage 4 covered an extended period, month‐by‐month analyses were conducted to further characterize temporal trends in psychiatric service utilization. Outpatient visits showed modest peaks around 1, 7, and 12 months after the disaster, suggesting that psychiatric help‐seeking may fluctuate in response to evolving psychosocial stressors across different recovery stages, including the acute aftermath, mid‐term recovery challenges, and anniversary‐related reactions. These temporal variations were more evident in outpatient visits than in inpatient admissions, indicating greater sensitivity of outpatient service utilization to changing post‐disaster conditions over time. Mid‐term increases around 7 months may reflect prolonged displacement or uncertainty regarding housing and recovery, whereas the increase at 12 months is plausibly related to anniversary reactions 55 , 56 and heightened media attention. 57 , 58 , 59

In contrast, among inpatients during Stage 4, schizophrenia spectrum disorders (F2) persisted into the mid‐term recovery phase, whereas mood (F3) and stress‐related disorders (F4) showed irregular temporal patterns. Although previous studies have reported an increased risk of psychiatric hospitalization among patients with schizophrenia primarily in the acute aftermath of natural disasters, the present findings indicate that this risk may extend beyond the acute phase. This underscores the importance of early initiation and continuous psychiatric support throughout the mid‐term recovery period.

Our findings emphasize the importance of phase‐specific, tailored interventions in post‐disaster mental health care. Interventions should be strategically timed to align with the temporal course of recovery and the specific vulnerabilities of survivors. In the acute phase (within 1 month), timely detection and treatment of mood disorders—as well as acute stress and anxiety—are critical. In the mid‐term phase (around 7 months), attention should shift to psychosocial stressors such as prolonged displacement, housing instability, and community disruption. The disaster anniversary (12 months) represents another period of heightened risk, warranting proactive monitoring and preventive care. Interventions should therefore be tailored not only to clinical needs—including early intervention for mood and stress‐related disorders, relapse prevention for those with pre‐existing psychiatric illness, sustained support for schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and age‐sensitive care for both younger and older survivors—but also to disaster‐specific stressors such as relocation, housing damage, and shelter residence.

Over time, stress‐related disorders (F4), psychotic disorders (F2), and cognitive impairments (F0) emerged or worsened, indicating that post‐disaster psychiatric morbidity evolves beyond the acute phase and may persist over extended periods. These findings underscore the importance of sustained monitoring and flexible, phase‐appropriate interventions that can respond to increasingly diverse clinical needs.

In disaster‐affected regions where reconstruction has been delayed, 60 prolonged psychosocial stressors—such as displacement, uncertainty, and erosion of social support—may contribute to the persistence of psychiatric morbidity well beyond the first post‐disaster year. 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 Accordingly, long‐term surveillance systems and sustainable mental health care frameworks are essential to mitigate these enduring challenges and to support recovery across the lifespan.

Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to confirm these findings and to further refine phase‐specific mental health interventions following large‐scale disasters.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single‐center study included only first‐time outpatients and newly admitted inpatients who reported earthquake‐related impacts at their initial visit, which may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability to other regions or cultural contexts. Second, baseline psychiatric vulnerability differed substantially between outpatients and inpatients, with prior psychiatric treatment far more common among inpatients; this difference may partly explain variations in temporal patterns and warrants cautious interpretation. Third, although ICD‐10 classifications enabled international comparability, diagnoses were based on routine clinical assessments and may be subject to inter‐rater variability, and the disaster‐phase framework derived from a Japanese national guideline may not be fully applicable to other settings. Despite these limitations, this study provides clinically relevant insights into phase‐specific changes in psychiatric diagnostic patterns following a major earthquake.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that psychiatric diagnostic patterns following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake varied across clearly defined disaster‐response phases. Mood disorders were predominant during the acute stage, whereas stress‐related disorders increased over time, accompanied by the later emergence of psychotic and organic disorders. These findings underscore the importance of phase‐specific, flexible, and sustained mental health interventions that respond to both evolving clinical presentations and disaster‐related stressors, thereby supporting effective long‐term psychiatric care in disaster‐affected populations.
