BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, affecting 6.9 million individuals in the United States alone. 1 Late‐onset AD (LOAD) occurring after age 65 has a strong genetic component with heritability estimates of 58% to 79%. 2 High‐throughput genomic techniques, such as genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have identified > 75 genetic loci associated with LOAD. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 However, these findings were obtained primarily from studies of European ancestry (EA) populations, thus limiting our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying AD in other populations such as those of African ancestry (AA) that have a comparatively higher proportion of LOAD. 7 , 8 , 9 Further, it is estimated that only ≈ 40% of LOAD genetic variance has been accounted for, indicating that much of the genetic architecture remains to be identified. 10 The heritability knowledge gap in LOAD may be explained in part by genetic interactions with specific environmental factors (GxE) amplifying the effects of otherwise low‐frequency susceptibility variants. 2 , 11 , 12 Further investigation of GxE interactions is imperative to understand the underlying mechanisms and develop personalized therapeutic strategies for LOAD.

The Lancet Commission's most recent report indicates that approximately half of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by modification of 14 environmental factors, one of which is cigarette smoking. 13 Cigarette smoking in mid‐ and late life increases dementia risk, with smoking cessation decreasing the risk to be comparable to never smoking. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Smoking‐related oxidative stress may be a mechanism for cardiometabolic disease and/or accelerate cognitive aging, which in turn increases dementia risk. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 The relationship between smoking and dementia risk is complex, however, with nicotine having both neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects, and recent research indicating that nicotine may protect against cognitive decline through involvement of neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. 22 , 23

Previous gene–smoking interaction studies of LOAD were focused on candidate genes. There is evidence that smoking increases risk only in non‐carriers of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4, the most common LOAD genetic risk factor, although the underlying mechanism associated with this finding is not well understood. 17 , 24 , 25 Polymorphisms in CHRNA7, a gene that codes for an α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor that colocalizes with amyloid beta (Aβ) in neuritic plaques, reduced the detrimental effect of smoking on LOAD risk. 26 The well‐established AD‐associated TREM2 R47H missense variant has a significantly larger magnitude of association with LOAD among current smokers compared to never smokers. 27

In the current study, we investigated genome‐wide, cross‐ancestry (EA, AA) single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐by‐smoking interaction effects on LOAD and conducted smoking‐stratified GWASs of LOAD in datasets from the Alzheimer's Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) and the Framingham Heart Study (FHS), totaling > 25,000 participants. Participants were classified as smokers and non‐smokers based on self‐reported history of ever smoking cigarettes, and additional analyses tested for interaction with cumulative exposure to smoking by using pack‐year data available among self‐reported smokers. Top‐ranked findings were further investigated with bulk and single‐cell gene expression data from the Genotype‐Tissue Expression portal (GTEx), the Religious Orders Study and the Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP), FHS, Boston University Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (BU ADRC), and Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB).

METHODS

GWAS participants

We obtained GWAS and smoking history data for participants in 34 ADGC GWAS datasets. Six of the eleven AA datasets contained < 100 participants each and were combined into a single dataset, referred to as merged6_AA. Further details on the merging of these datasets are provided in the Supplementary Methods in supporting information. Adjudication of AD in each ADGC cohort has been described previously. 28 Further details on all ADGC datasets are provided in Supplementary Methods. We also analyzed data from the Original and Offspring cohorts from the FHS, a longitudinal cohort study established in 1948. 29 A complete description of the dementia review process, including AD adjudication, has been detailed previously. 30 , 31 Participants in these cohorts aged ≥ 65 years or older at time of censoring (age‐at‐onset for AD cases or age‐at‐last‐examination/age‐of‐death for controls) with available genetic and smoking history data were included in this study.

Smoking

Self‐reported history of ever smoking cigarettes was treated as a dichotomous exposure. Study‐specific details about how smoking was assessed and ever smoking was dichotomized are included in the Supplementary Methods. Treated as a continuous variable, pack‐years was calculated as the product of the average packs per day and the total years smoked. We did not consider current smoking because there were few current smokers, and the sample was likely underpowered to identify significant genetic associations.

Genotype quality control, imputation, and principal component generation

Genome‐wide SNP data were obtained and processed as previously described. 28 In brief, SNP imputation was performed using the Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine r2 (TOPMed‐r2) reference panel and aligned to Genome Research Consortium human build 38. 32 For each cohort, SNPs were excluded if they had a call rate < 98% for the Illumina array or 95% for the Affymetrix array panels, departed from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium among controls for variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 (p < 10−6), or had informative significant missingness by case‐control status (p < 10−6). Samples were excluded if the genotyping call rate was < 95%, X chromosome heterozygosity indicated inconsistency between predicted and reported sex, or population structure analysis indicated inconsistency between reported ancestry and clustered ancestry from the 1000 Genomes (1 kG) Phase 3 reference panel. SNPs were included if the imputation quality (R 2) was > 0.4 and had a MAF > 1% or imputation quality score > 0.8 and a MAF < 1%. Principal components (PCs) of population structure were generated for individuals within each cohort using “smartpca” in EIGENSOFT on a subset of ≈ 20,000 linkage disequilibrium (LD)–pruned SNPs. 33 , 34 To determine the number of PCs to include in association tests, we followed ADGC guidelines, plotting PCs from low to high (1 to 10) on scatter plots and observing when the distribution of PCs was tight around 0. The first four PCs appeared dispersed and were included as covariates in association testing.

GWAS statistical analysis

We evaluated the modifying effect of smoking on the association of SNPs with AD risk using SNP‐by‐smoking interaction tests and models stratified by smoking status. For the interaction tests, smoking was defined in two ways: a dichotomous variable for ever smoking and a continuous variable for pack years. Only participants with a history of smoking were considered in the pack‐year interaction analyses. Association test models were evaluated separately in each dataset using logistic regression, including covariates for age (age‐at‐onset for AD cases and age‐at‐last‐examination or age‐at‐death for controls), sex, and the first four PCs. A genetic relationship matrix was included in the model to account for family relatedness in the FHS and Washington Height‐Inwood Community Aging Project (WHICAP) datasets. Because the SNP‐by‐smoking models did not converge when including a genetic relationship matrix in analyses of the family‐based Multi‐Institutional Research in Alzheimer's Genetic Epidemiology study (MIRAGE) dataset, MIRAGE data analyses were limited to an unrelated set of individuals. Analysis models for the merged6_AA dataset included a single covariate for the original dataset. Models including interaction terms were evaluated using MAGEE, an R package for genome‐wide gene–environment interaction analyses based on linear mixed models. 35 SNPs with MAF < 0.5% were excluded from analyses involving pack years to ensure model convergence given the interaction term between SNP and the continuous variable of pack years. Effect sizes for pack‐year terms represent the effect of a 10 year increase in pack years. GWAS analyses conducted in subgroups of participants with and without smoking history were performed using SAIGE. 36 Results for datasets within ancestry groups were combined by meta‐analysis using the inverse‐variance weighting option in METAL. 37 After within‐ancestry meta‐analysis, results for SNPs were excluded if the SNP was present in less than half of the samples or had an overall MAF < 1%. Results for SNPs present in both EA and AA groups were combined by meta‐analysis using an inverse‐variance weighting option in METAL.

Bulk brain gene expression data acquisition, processing, and analysis

Gene expression data derived from bulk RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) performed in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) tissue from ROSMAP, FHS, BU ADRC, and MSBB brain donors were analyzed to assess differential expression of genes located in regions of the top‐ranked GWAS SNPs between pathologically confirmed AD cases and controls and between smokers and non‐smokers. Data for ROSMAP and MSBB participants were obtained from the CommonMind Consortium panel (http://www.synapse.org). Detailed preprocessing procedures and quality control of the RNA‐seq data and normalization of the gene expression data have been described elsewhere. 38 , 39 , 40 Analyses were performed using DEseq2, which applies a median of ratios normalization method, and models were adjusted for age at death, sex, and removing unwanted variations (RUVs) to control for batch effects, library preparation, and other nuisance effects. 41 Expression quantitative locus (eQTL) mapping was performed for genes within a 500 kb window using linear regression with normalized expression counts from DEseq2 as an outcome and imputed SNP dosage as a continuous variable predictor, adjusting for age at death, sex, and RUVs. Results from these analyses performed within each of the four studies were combined with METAL as described above.

Cell type–specific eQTL mapping and colocalization

We mapped eQTLs for our top‐ranked GWAS result from smoking‐stratified analyses to 23 cell subtypes with brain single‐nucleus RNA‐seq (snRNA‐seq) data in the ROSMAP cohorts. 42 Pseudo‐bulk counts for each gene were calculated by summing read counts across cells annotated as the same cell subtype for each individual. Counts were normalized based on read depth and rank‐based inverse normal transform with R library (RNOmni). We applied probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) to infer the latent factors underlying inter‐individual variation and performed eQTL mapping in each cell type for genes within a 3Mb window using linear regression with transformed expression counts as an outcome and imputed SNP dosage as a continuous variable predictor, adjusting for sex, age, and top 25 PEER factors. 43 Colocalization analysis was performed using coloc to quantify the posterior probability (coloc PP4) that gene expression and AD status share the same causal genetic variants. 44 We calculated coloc PP4 with eQTL signals either from bulk brain RNA‐seq or snRNA‐seq within 100 kb from the top‐ranked variant.

Results are presented below, including sample characteristics (section 3.1), GWAS results stratified by smoking status (section 3.2), and GWAS results that include a SNP‐by‐smoking interaction term among the full sample (section 3.3). In sections 3.2 and 3.3, we present analyses combined across ancestry groups and then stratified by ancestry group. Focused on the top hit from section 3.2, section 3.4 includes post hoc gene expression analyses using bulk and single‐cell RNA‐seq data to conduct differential expression and colocalization analyses.

Association findings stratified by smoking status

We conducted GWAS analyses stratified by smoking status in the total sample and in individual ancestry groups. Many variants in the APOE region, including the ɛ4 SNP rs429358, were significantly associated with AD risk without evidence of genomic inflation in smokers and non‐smokers and in both ancestry groups (Figures S1–S6 in supporting information). For rs429358, there were similar magnitudes of effect in both smokers (odds ratio [OR] = 3.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: [2.84, 3.34], p = 3.1 × 10−162) and non‐smokers (OR = 3.31, 95% CI: [3.07, 3.57], p = 2.4 × 10−211), suggesting that smoking does not modify the ɛ4–AD association (Tables 2 and S3 and S4 in supporting information). Genome‐wide significant (GWS) evidence of association with AD risk was also observed with APAF1 intronic SNP rs12368451 among smokers only (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: [1.12, 1.27], p = 3.0 × 10−8), with other SNPs in LD with rs12368451 showing moderate evidence of association (p < 1 × 10−4). However, this region did not show significant associations among non‐smokers (Figure 1). Importantly, there is evidence supporting the association of rs12368451 in both EA (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: [1.09, 1.25], p = 6.5 × 10−6) and AA (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: [1.20, 1.75], p = 1.1 × 10−4) smokers (Table S4, Figure S7 in supporting information), noting that the effect size is substantially larger in the much smaller AA sample. Effects were consistent across cohorts within ancestry groups (Figure S8 in supporting information), but there was evidence of heterogeneity of effects across EA and AA groups (p = 0.03). Suggestive evidence of association (p < 1 × 10−6) among smokers was identified with variants in the CR1/CR1L region, a well‐established AD locus, including sentinel variants (both intronic) rs6656401 in CR1 (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: [1.16, 1.37], p = 6.3 × 10−8) and rs11118328 in CR1L (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: [1.15, 1.36], p = 1.4 × 10−7; Table 2). Associations with these variants in non‐smokers were somewhat attenuated (rs6656401: OR = 1.14, 95% CI: [1.06, 1.24], p = 7.8 × 10−4; rs11118328: OR = 1.12, 95% CI: [1.04, 1.21], p = 2.8 × 10−3).

SNP‐by‐smoking interaction association findings

We identified near GWS associations for a SNP‐by‐smoking interaction in the total sample with SNPs at one locus, including rs1870927, an intron of LIN9 (ORGxE = 1.27, 95% CI: [1.16, 1.38], p = 1.4 × 10−7), and rs10915961 located 15 kb away in the promoter region of MIXL1 (ORGxE = 1.27, 95% CI: [1.16, 1.39], p = 1.4 × 10−7; Table 2). Several other variants spanning LIN9 and MIXL1 that are in high LD with rs1870927 showed similar association size (Table S10 in supporting information). Further examination of this interaction showed the rs1870927 minor allele increased AD risk among smokers but was protective among non‐smokers, though neither within smoking stratum result reached genome‐wide suggestive significance. SNP‐by‐smoking history interaction effects for rs1870927 were consistent across cohorts (Figure S9 in supporting information). A GWS SNP‐by‐smoking interaction was also observed with SLC22A23 SNP rs9392488 (ORGxE = 0.45, 95% CI: [0.35, 0.58], p = 1.5 × 10−9) among AAs (Table S11 in supporting information). To better understand the interaction, we conducted a smoking‐stratified analysis that showed the minor allele was protective among smokers (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: [0.57, 0.85], p = 4.4 × 10−4) but increased AD risk among non‐smokers (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: [1.27, 1.91], p = 2.0 × 10−5). Suggestive evidence for interaction of smoking with SNPs at other loci also showed modestly significant opposite association directions in stratified GWAS among smokers and non‐smokers (Table 2). There were no GWS SNP‐by‐smoking interactions in the EA group (Table S12 in supporting information). Although there was no evidence of genomic inflation in the cross‐ancestry (λ = 0.981) or AA (λ = 1.015) smoking interaction GWAS, there was modest inflation in the GWAS for EAs (λ = 1.087; Figures S10–S12 in supporting information).

GWAS analyses testing associations with a SNP‐by‐pack‐years interaction included far fewer individuals and yielded far fewer significant findings (Figures S13–S15, Tables S13–S15 in supporting information). Suggestive evidence for interaction was identified with DGKI SNP rs11771895 (ORGxE = 0.92, 95% CI: [0.89, 0.95], p = 3.3 × 10−7). This association was significant only in the EA group (ORGxE = 0.92, 95% CI: [0.89, 0.95], p = 8.8 × 10−7), but the association size was similar in the AA group (ORGxE = 0.92, 95% CI: [0.78, 1.09], p = 0.3398). Effect sizes for pack‐years terms represent the effect of a 10‐year increase in pack years.

Gene expression analyses

Gene expression data for 54 tissues (including 13 brain regions) from GTEx (https://gtexportal.org/home/) were used to initially examine the expression profile across available tissues of five genes (ANKS1B, APAF1, IKBIP, SLC25A3, and TMPO) that are within 500 kb of rs12368451. ANKS1B expression was highest in the 13 brain regions compared to all other tissues (Figure S16 in supporting information). APAF1, SLC25A3, and TMPO are expressed in cerebellar hemisphere, cerebellum, and frontal cortex, but expression is higher in other tissues and less brain specific than ANKS1B. IKBIP showed very little expression in brain tissue. The rs12368451 minor allele (G) was significantly associated with lower expression of ANKS1B in the hippocampus (p = 1.6 × 10−6) and nominally associated with lower expression of ANKS1B in frontal cortex (Table S16, Figure S17 in supporting information). Associations of rs12368451 with expression of neighboring genes in the brain are provided in Table S16.

Differential expression was evaluated for five genes located within 500 kb of rs12368451 using data generated from DLPFC from 1150 ROSMAP, FHS, BU ADRC, and MSBB brain donors. We found that ANKS1B (Log2FC = −0.028, 95% CI: [−0.054, −0.003], p = 0.0304) and SLC25A3 (Log2FC = −0.036, 95% CI: [−0.053, −0.020], p = 1.3 × 10−5) were significantly downregulated in pathologically confirmed AD cases compared to control brains, and TMPO was significantly upregulated (Log2FC = 0.034, 95% CI: [0.005, 0.063], p = 0.0204; Table 3). None of these genes was differentially expressed between smokers and non‐smokers (Table S17 in supporting information). eQTL analysis revealed that the rs12368451 G allele, observed to be associated with increased AD risk among smokers, was associated with significantly lower ANKS1B expression only in non‐smokers (β = −58.21, 95% CI: [−100.60, −15.82], p = 0.0071; Table 3). The rs12368451 AD risk allele was also associated with lower expression of IKBIP in non‐smokers (β = −3.13, 95% CI: [−5.58, −0.67], p = 0.0127).

Identification of a significant association of rs12368451 with AD among smokers (but not non‐smokers; Table 2) and a significant association of rs12368451 and ANKS1B expression among non‐smokers (but not smokers; Table 3) are seemingly disparate.

To investigate further, we tested the hypothesis that the association of rs12368451 with gene expression among smokers and non‐smokers is cell type specific by examining a wider genomic window (3Mb window surrounding rs12368451) using brain snRNA‐seq data from the ROSMAP cohort. Among smokers, rather than non‐smokers, we identified cell type–specific eQTL signals for rs12368451 (nominal p < 0.01) with ANKS1B in oligodendrocytes and TMPO in a subtype of excitatory neurons (Figure 2). We performed colocalization (coloc PP4) to quantify the posterior probability that gene expression and AD status share the same causal genetic variants. Among smokers, but not non‐smokers, rs12368451 showed the most significant posterior probability for being the causal variant via ANKS1B (PP = 0.15) and TMPO (PP = 0.24) expression in the corresponding cell types (Figure 2). Taken together, these results suggested a complex cell type–specific interaction among smoking, genetic variation at this locus, gene expression, and AD pathogenesis (Figure S18 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

In a large LOAD multi‐ancestry genome‐wide interaction and stratified study with smoking, we identified two genome‐wide cross‐ancestry associations near APAF1/ANKS1B and LIN9/MIXL1 and an ancestry‐specific genome‐wide significant locus near SLC22A23 (AA). The lead SNP in the APAF1/ANKS1B locus, rs12368451, was significantly associated with AD risk only in smokers. Additional in silico investigation of the region implicated rs12368451 as an eQTL with activity that differed across potential AD candidate genes (ANKS1B, TMPO) by smoking status and brain cell types (oligodendrocytes, excitatory neurons). The lead SNPs in the LIN9/MIXL1 region and SLC22A23 regions showed significant interaction effects but modest, non‐significant associations in each smoking stratum.

Among the loci identified in cross‐ancestry analyses, both APAF1 and ANKS1B are potential targets for AD. ANKS1B is a tyrosine kinase signal transduction gene that has been estimated to be the second most highly expressed gene in the brain. 45 ANKS1B has also been shown to have decreased expression in the medial prefrontal cortex during aging. 46 This is consistent with findings from GTEx showing that ANKS1B expression was highest in brain tissues compared to other tissues. Our findings showed that compared to controls, ANKS1B was downregulated in brains of those with pathologically confirmed AD. ANKS1B encodes AIDA‐1 protein (amyloid beta precursor protein intracellular domain associated‐1) that regulates Aβ plaque build‐up by binding intracellular Aβ. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 AIDA‐1 is highly expressed at neuronal synapses, regulates synaptic plasticity, and binds to N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptors (NMDARs). 51 ANKS1B’s role in Aβ aggregation and synaptic function, both mechanisms in AD pathogenesis, implicate ANKS1B as a potential AD therapeutic target.

Although APAF1 showed less brain‐specific expression than ANKS1B, past research suggests it might still be a potential candidate. In brain tissue, neurons contain low levels of APAF1, which plays a key role in apoptosis during brain development. 52 With respect to neurodegenerative diseases, cell death in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) appears to be largely APAF1 mediated. 53 Similarly, high levels of APAF1 have been detected in animal models of Huntington's disease, with studies suggesting that inhibition or inactivation of this gene may decrease neuronal apoptosis and pathological protein aggregation. 53 , 54 Drugs used in the treatment of Parkinson's disease have been shown to exert neuroprotective effects through the downregulation of APAF1. 55 , 56 In contrast, APAF1 levels in the brains of individuals with AD have been found to be comparable to those of normal controls. 57 Taken together, APAF1 can inhibit the activation of caspases and apoptosis of neuronal cells, making it a potential drug target, but further research is needed. 58

Previous research suggests APAF1 and ANKS1B may have a connection to smoking. APAF1 may have gene–smoking interaction effects in preeclampsia and non–small cell lung cancer. 59 , 60 Variants in ANKS1B are associated with increased risk of substance use disorders, potentially by the addictive drugs suppressing ANKS1B gene expression, with overexpression of the gene increasing resistance for addictive behavior. 61 , 62 , 63 With respect to smoking, a locus in ANKS1B has shown genome‐wide significant association with smoking initiation, with some evidence that ANKS1B may play a role in smoking‐related molecular alteration in cancer. 64 , 65 SNPs in the ANKS1B/APAF1 locus have additionally been associated with body mass index and body fat percentage 66 , 67 suggesting that the pathway through which the observed gene–smoking interaction acts on AD may be cardiometabolic.

Interestingly, although we found a significant association of rs12368451 with ANKS1B expression in bulk RNA‐seq data from DLPFC, this association was significant only among non‐smokers. In further investigation with snRNA‐seq data, we found through colocalization analysis that among smokers, but not non‐smokers, rs12368451 showed the most significant posterior probability of being the causal variant via ANKS1B expression in oligodendrocytes and TMPO expression in a subtype of excitatory neurons. These results suggest there may be a complex relationship between genetic variation and smoking that varies across cell types in the brain. It may also be that there is a SNP–expression relationship that is modified by smoking history, but that expression of these genes is not the causal mechanism for AD.

There is less evidence in the literature linking LIN9/MIXL1, the other locus identified in cross‐ancestry analyses, with neurodegenerative conditions. MIXL1 is responsible for the formation of mesodermal and endodermal germ layers during embryonic development, with one study implicating it as a possible risk candidate gene linked to LOAD. 68 , 69 LIN9 is a subunit of the DREAM complex, which regulates gene expression in the cell cycle, but it has not been linked to brain aging or neurodegeneration. With respect to smoking, LIN9 has shown sub‐genome‐wide association (p = 6 × 10−6) with nicotine dependence, whereas there is limited evidence linking MIXL1 with nicotine. 70

Within‐ancestry analyses revealed genome‐wide significance in the SLC22A23 gene only in AA studies. SLC22A23 is an orphan gene in the SLC22 family of membrane transporter genes. While there is limited evidence linking this gene to smoking and/or AD risk, it is expressed in brain tissue at high levels, and one study showed that Slc22a23 knockout rats have reduced hippocampal volume. 71 , 72

Strengths of this study include that it is the largest GWAS of gene–smoking interaction associations on LOAD, is multi‐ancestry, and implicates three promising loci. Nonetheless, there are limitations. Most of the sample was EA (87.5%), though we did identify an AA‐specific locus. Statistical power is inherently low for tests of GxE interaction, and smoking data were not available on all ADGC datasets, further limiting power. 73 Further, we did not have a replication cohort. Additionally, there is some evidence that current smoking status, as opposed to ever smoking, may be a larger risk factor for AD. 74 Although we had data on current smoking status, the small number of current smokers meant we were very underpowered to identify associations. Last, while informative, the gene expression analyses do not provide a definitive smoking‐dependent mechanism linking the rs12368451 locus to AD, and further research is needed.

In conclusion, we identified a genome‐wide cross‐ancestry association near APAF1/ANKS1B among smokers, but not non‐smokers. RNA‐seq analysis suggested there may be a complex relationship between genetic variation and smoking that differs across brain cell types. ANKS1B encodes AIDA‐1, which is highly expressed in synapses and regulates Aβ plaque accumulation. We also identified a genome‐wide AA‐specific significant SNP–smoking interaction near SLC22A23, an AA‐specific genome‐wide significant locus near SLC22A23 in smokers only. Overall, findings highlight the strength of using cross‐ancestry datasets and considering both genetic and environmental factors together toward a personalized medicine approach to AD.
