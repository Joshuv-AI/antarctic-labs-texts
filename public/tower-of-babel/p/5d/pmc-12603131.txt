Process optimization and quality assessment of redistilled Ethiopian traditional spirit (Areke): ethanol yield and zinc contamination control
Authors: Estifanos Kassahun, Abdiwak Tamene, Yobsen Tadesse, Bethlehem Semagn, Addisu Sewunet, Shiferaw Ayalneh, Solomon Tibebu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research presents a comprehensive optimization of the ethanol re-distillation process focusing on maximizing ethanol purity while minimizing heavy metal contaminants, particularly zinc. Using a rigorous experimental design, key parameters, holding time, reflux ratio, and temperature, were systematically varied to evaluate their effects on ethanol concentration and zinc levels. The optimized conditions achieved an ethanol content as high as 88.01%, with zinc concentrations consistently maintained below 3.63 mg/kg, ensuring both product quality and safety. Industrial-scale trials further confirmed the reproducibility of these results, with the Heart fraction yielding the highest ethanol volume and purity, consistently surpassing 90% alcohol content across batches. Detailed physicochemical analysis demonstrated significantly lower volatile acids, esters, and heavy metals in the Head and Heart fractions compared to the Bottom and Tail fractions, which contained elevated impurities. These findings underscore the effectiveness of the optimized process parameters in enhancing ethanol yield and purity, providing a scalable and industrially viable method for producing high-quality ethanol with controlled contamination levels.

Introduction

Traditional alcoholic beverages are widely produced and consumed across the globe, often reflecting the unique cultural and regional identities of their origins1–4. In Ethiopia, the most commonly consumed traditional alcoholic drinks include Areke (a distilled spirit), Tella (a cereal-based fermented drink similar to beer), and Tej (a honey wine)5–8. These beverages are deeply embedded in cultural practices and are typically consumed during social gatherings and festivities.

The production of these traditional drinks generally involves artisanal fermentation and basic distillation or brewing techniques2,9–11. Among them, Areke is particularly notable for its widespread use, especially in rural and semi-urban areas8,12–14. It is typically produced using locally fabricated equipment, including a spherical boiling pot, a cloth-insulated bamboo condenser, and firewood as a heat source3,8,13,15–17. While these methods are cost-effective and deeply rooted in cultural tradition, they often lack scientific oversight and quality control mechanisms.

In urban contexts, Areke remains popular among lower-income populations due to its affordability compared to industrially manufactured alcohol8,18,19. However, this low cost is often associated with minimal process regulation and substandard production hygiene, increasing the risk of contamination. Previous assessments of Ethiopian agro-processing systems have highlighted inefficiencies, lack of quality monitoring, and limited adoption of environmentally sound practices, particularly in traditional food and beverage sectors20–22.

A major public health concern tied to Areke consumption is the limited awareness among producers and consumers about its chemical composition and potential toxicity4,8,23,24. Ethanol concentration is typically unregulated, and the product may contain harmful compounds such as heavy metals (e.g., lead, zinc, arsenic) and volatile organic compounds25–29. When present at unsafe levels, these substances pose significant health risks. While previous work has explored the improvement of traditional Ethiopian foods such as injera through nutritional and sensory analysis30–34, the chemical safety of unregulated alcoholic beverages has received little scientific attention.

Recent studies on optimizing bioethanol production from agro-industrial byproducts have shown the importance of controlled fermentation and distillation for improving yield and purity35–40. Motivated by these advances, the present study investigates the feasibility of enhancing traditionally produced Areke through controlled re-distillation. This includes analyzing its chemical composition and evaluating the removal of potentially toxic elements such as zinc, with the goal of improving product quality at both laboratory and industrial scales.

Despite the widespread consumption of Areke, limited data exist on its chemical safety and production variability. Traditional methods do not meet national or international standards and lack structured frameworks for quality assurance. Furthermore, there has been little effort to develop Areke into a higher-grade product that could serve industrial or pharmaceutical purposes. This study addresses these gaps by (i) evaluating the chemical composition and safety of locally produced Areke, and (ii) optimizing re-distillation conditions to enhance ethanol yield while minimizing residual zinc contamination, thereby supporting both public health and industrial alcohol production goals. The novelty of this work lies in its integrated approach, combining ethanol optimization with heavy metal (zinc) reduction using response surface methodology, validated at both laboratory and industrial scales. Such a dual-focus optimization for a traditional spirit is rare, and the results contribute to the fields of food engineering, public health, and sustainable process development by offering a scalable framework for improving the quality and safety of artisanal alcoholic beverages.

The methodology employed in this study involved the application of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) using a Central Composite Design (CCD), which systematically varied key distillation parameters, reflux ratio, holding time, and temperature, to identify the optimal conditions for maximizing ethanol yield while minimizing zinc contamination. This approach enabled a detailed exploration of the complex relationships between these parameters and their effects on product quality, providing insights that are directly applicable to scaling up the process for industrial applications. The findings offer a practical guide for enhancing the safety and quality of Areke production, paving the way for both public health improvements and industrial scalability.

Materials and methods

Sampling and description of the study areas

A purposive (judgmental) sampling method was employed to select representative Areke production areas across different regions of Ethiopia. Three major towns known for their high levels of traditional Areke production were selected: Dembecha, Debre Berhan, and Arsi Negele. These locations were chosen based on field observations and local reports indicating significantly higher Areke output compared to other towns in the country41–43. A total of 600 L of Areke samples were collected, with 200 L obtained from each site. The samples included different types of Areke commonly produced and consumed in each area. Care was taken to ensure variability in producers and methods to capture representative chemical profiles. A brief description of the selected sampling areas is provided in Table 1.

Re-distillation process by GUNT CE 600 rectification unit

The re-distillation of Areke at laboratory scale was conducted using the GUNT CE 600 rectification unit, a bench-scale distillation system designed for educational and experimental purposes. This unit was used to simulate industrial distillation under controlled conditions. Prior to each batch experiment, the system was configured to operate at ambient pressure, and the manual stop valves were adjusted according to the manufacturer’s specifications to achieve the desired flow conditions. The setup allows for visual monitoring and control of the re-distillation process, with color-coded flow paths to indicate the movement of vapors and liquids within the column. The GUNT CE 600 unit is a bench-scale distillation system used for the experimental re-distillation of Areke. The column consists of 8 stages, with a diameter of 7 cm and approximately 15 cm spacing between each stage. The internal structure of the column is covered, and while the exact configuration is not visually confirmed, it is designed to enhance vapor-liquid contact for efficient distillation. Stainless steel components are used throughout the system to ensure durability and improve performance. The experimental setup, including the process flow diagram and the physical appearance of the GUNT CE 600 unit, is presented in Fig. 1. Initially, a composite Areke sample was prepared by mixing equal volumes (1:1:1 ratio) of samples collected from Dembecha, Debre Berhan, and Arsi Negele. The mixed sample was thoroughly homogenized in a clean canister and then transferred to the evaporator (boiler) of the GUNT CE 600 unit, ensuring the total volume added was 8 L. Once the sample was loaded, the measurement and data acquisition system were activated. The reflux ratio was set according to three experimental conditions, 55%, 70%, or 85%, based on results from preliminary optimization trials. The condenser water supply was opened and maintained at a constant flow rate of 100 L/h to ensure efficient condensation. The electric heater was then turned on, and the initial power input was set to 100%.

Based on pilot studies, the target condenser temperature was fixed at 73 °C. The system was allowed to run until the first distillate appeared in the phase separation tank, at which point reflux began and equilibrium was established in the distillation column. After the system reached steady-state operation, three different holding times were tested, 2.0, 3.5, and 5.0 h, to examine their effect on the final product quality. At the end of each experiment, the system was cooled before collection. Both the distillate (top product) and the bottom residue were collected in clean, labeled glass containers for further analysis. The shutdown procedure involved turning off the boiler to stop heating. Five minutes later, the reflux ratio was adjusted to 100%, and the condenser water supply was turned off to complete the run.

Experimental design for the top product optimization

The application of statistical methods is advantageous for understanding the interactions among process variables with a minimal number of experimental runs, ultimately enabling the identification of optimal operating conditions. In this study, a standard Response Surface Methodology (RSM) approach using a Central Composite Design (CCD) was employed to optimize the re-distillation process of Areke samples. This design is particularly effective in evaluating both linear and quadratic effects of variables, as well as their interactions.

The CCD applied in this work comprised three types of experimental runs: 2ⁿ factorial runs, 2n axial (or star) runs, and six center point runs, where n represents the number of independent variables considered. In this case, three process variables were selected based on preliminary trials and process relevance. These included the reflux ratio (%), the holding or waiting time (hours), and the top temperature (°C) within the distillation column. These variables were known to influence the composition and purity of the distillate (top product) and were therefore chosen for detailed investigation.

According to the structure of the central composite design, a total of 20 experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects and interactions of the three variables on the quality of the distilled Areke. Each experiment was performed using a unique combination of the variables based on the design matrix generated by the CCD model. Re-distillation process by MÜLLER gmbh unit

The second re-distillation stage of the Areke samples was conducted using a commercial-scale distillation unit manufactured by MÜLLER GmbH (model D 77704, Germany). This distillery is equipped with state-of-the-art rectification plates and is designed for high-efficiency distillation, particularly suitable for preserving and enhancing aromatic compounds in distilled products. Parametric characterization of the distillate was performed in accordance with various standard protocols commonly used for evaluating liquor quality. The key technical specifications of the distillation unit include a filling capacity of 380 L, a water bath volume of 220 L, an operating pressure of 0.9 bar, a maximum operating temperature of 120 °C, and a total power input of 60 kW. Steady-state operation was typically reached within 15 min from the start of the process. For each batch run, 76 L of traditionally produced Areke were fed into the distillery. Characterization of ethanol, zinc, lead, and arsenic

The chemical characterization of the Areke samples was carried out using standardized analytical methods to ensure accuracy and comparability with national and international benchmarks. The ethanol content was determined according to the Ethiopian Standard ES 804:2012 and reported as a percentage by volume. The concentrations of heavy metals, specifically zinc, lead, and arsenic, were analyzed using the AOAC 2015/01 method, employing Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS). The results for these metals were expressed in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg).

Although lead and arsenic were analyzed due to their known toxicity, their concentrations remained low and showed limited variation in our samples. As discussed in Sect. 3.1, zinc was selected as the primary heavy metal target for optimization, given its relatively higher concentration and greater impact on product safety within the studied context.

To contextualize the thresholds for ethanol purity and zinc contamination, international regulatory standards were referenced to ensure alignment with safety benchmarks. For zinc, the World Health Organization (WHO) drinking water quality guidelines recommend a maximum permissible concentration of 3 mg/L in water44. The European Union (EU) regulation allows a maximum of 5 mg/L for zinc in food and beverages45. Regarding ethanol, the FDA establishes standards for industrial alcohol, requiring a minimum ethanol content of 95% in alcoholic beverages46, while the EU mandates a minimum purity of 96% ethanol for alcoholic beverages47.

Result and discussion

Analysis of raw Areke heavy metals

The concentrations of zinc, lead, and arsenic were measured across all raw Areke samples to assess potential public health risks arising from heavy metal contamination. Among these, zinc emerged as the most variable and quantitatively dominant contaminant, making it the focal point of subsequent process optimization in this study. The average zinc concentration was 0.6313 mg/kg, with a standard deviation of 0.6028 mg/kg, indicating substantial variability among samples (coefficient of variation ≈ 95%). Zinc levels ranged from 0.07 mg/kg in DG, a sample flavored with Gibto (Lupinus albus L.), to a maximum of 1.62 mg/kg in DBK, a Kosso-flavored variant (Hagenia abyssinica). This significant difference suggests that specific botanical additives and raw material combinations may influence zinc content, either through leaching during distillation or inherent contamination from source ingredients.

In contrast, lead concentrations were relatively lower and more consistent, averaging 0.213 mg/kg (SD = 0.105 mg/kg). The highest lead content was recorded in MD (0.366 mg/kg), while the lowest was in DBK (0.053 mg/kg). Arsenic levels were uniformly low across all samples, with an average of 0.0069 mg/kg, a minimum of 0.001 mg/kg in DB, and a maximum of 0.038 mg/kg in DBM. Given their relatively lower concentrations and narrower variability, lead and arsenic are noted but not prioritized in this study’s quality improvement focus.

The observed heterogeneity in zinc concentrations underscores the need for targeted mitigation strategies during re-distillation. This study therefore places a particular emphasis on zinc as the critical heavy metal of concern, both in its health risk potential and in the optimization of processing parameters for its reduction. Process output from experimental design

Table 5 presents the ethanol content and zinc concentrations obtained from 20 experimental runs, designed using a central composite design to investigate the effects of holding time, reflux ratio, and temperature on product quality. The results exhibit substantial variation across treatments, offering key insights into the interplay between distillation parameters and target responses. Ethanol content ranged from 80.01% (Run 13) to a maximum of 88.01% (Run 19), indicating that parameter tuning has a notable impact on alcohol yield. Runs with moderate-to-high reflux ratios (70–85%) and mid-range holding times (3.5–5 h) tended to produce higher ethanol levels, suggesting improved separation and reduced losses due to tailing or early cutoffs. The highest ethanol yield (Run 19) was achieved at 85% reflux and 76 °C, reinforcing the importance of adequate condensation and controlled boiling for effective ethanol recovery.

Zinc concentrations demonstrated even greater variability, ranging from a low of 0.11 mg/kg (Run 20) to a high of 6.94 mg/kg (Run 10). Notably, the highest zinc level occurred at 5 h holding time, 55% reflux, and 79 °C, conditions which may have allowed prolonged contact or incomplete volatilization, facilitating zinc carryover into the distillate. In contrast, zinc levels below 0.5 mg/kg were consistently observed in runs with higher reflux ratios and lower temperatures, particularly when holding time was either minimized (2 h) or moderately extended (3.5 h), for example, Run 3 (0.42 mg/kg) and Run 7 (0.49 mg/kg). Interestingly, some parameter combinations led to high ethanol and low zinc simultaneously, such as Run 7 (84.59% ethanol, 0.49 mg/kg zinc) and Run 20 (85.53% ethanol, 0.11 mg/kg zinc). These runs offer a strong indication of optimal operational zones where product quality and safety can be jointly maximized.

Experimental design results of zinc

The zinc concentration data obtained from the re-distilled Areke samples were analyzed using Design-Expert Software (version 8.0.0). This software was employed to identify non-significant factors, isolate critical process variables, and conduct least squares regression analysis of variance (ANOVA). It further facilitated the development of a predictive model equation, assessed interactions among the independent variables, and generated three-dimensional surface plots. These plots were constructed by varying two factors at a time while keeping the third factor constant, allowing for comprehensive visualization of the response surface behavior.

Model adequacy and validation

The influence of process variables and their interactions on zinc concentration was evaluated statistically to determine the most appropriate model fit. The adequacy of the developed model was assessed using standard statistical metrics including the coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², and predicted R² values. These coefficients provided insight into the model’s ability to explain and predict variability in the data.

In addition to R-squared values, other critical indicators were examined to validate model quality. The coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as the standard deviation expressed as a percentage of the mean, was used to assess the precision of the model. The predicted residual error sum of squares (PRESS) was analyzed to evaluate how well the model fits each design point. Furthermore, the adequacy precision ratio, which reflects the signal-to-noise ratio attributable to random error, was calculated. A ratio greater than 4 is generally considered acceptable. In this study, the adequacy precision was found to be 8.204, indicating a strong signal and confirming the model’s robustness for navigating and exploring the experimental design space. Interaction analysis of process parameters on ethanol yield

The analysis of interaction effects among process parameters revealed that none of the interaction terms, holding time and reflux ratio (AB), holding time and temperature (AC), or reflux ratio and temperature (BC), significantly influenced ethanol yield, as indicated by their high p-values (p > 0.05). This suggests that the influence of each parameter on ethanol production operates largely in an independent rather than synergistic manner within the studied experimental range. Given the statistical insignificance of these interactions, no 3D response surface plots are presented for ethanol yield, as they would not meaningfully enhance the interpretation of the results. Notably, only the main effect of reflux ratio (p = 0.0218) and the quadratic term of temperature (C², p = 0.0001) were statistically significant, highlighting the dominant role of reflux ratio and the nonlinear sensitivity of ethanol yield to temperature. Consequently, process optimization for maximizing ethanol content should primarily focus on fine-tuning these individual variables rather than their combinations, underscoring the importance of precise temperature control and appropriate reflux management in re-distillation processes.

Optimization of process variables

The optimization phase of the re-distillation process focused on identifying the most favorable combination of process variables, namely, holding time, reflux ratio, and top temperature, to maximize ethanol yield while maintaining operational feasibility. The optimization was conducted using the desirability function approach embedded in Design-Expert Software, targeting ethanol content as the primary response variable while constraining the input factors within feasible process ranges.

As presented in Table 13, multiple solutions were obtained with varying degrees of desirability scores ranging from 0.935 to 1.000. These results illustrate the complex interplay between the process parameters and their influence on ethanol concentration. In all optimization cases, the holding time was maintained at or near 2.00 h, underscoring its critical role as a time-efficient variable that does not require extensive manipulation to yield favorable results. This fixed holding time likely ensures that the ethanol-rich fractions are effectively captured without promoting thermal degradation or excessive energy consumption.

The reflux ratio was a more influential parameter. In solutions with a target reflux ratio of 55.00%, typical of traditional or energy-conservative configurations, the ethanol content reached up to 87.01%, with a desirability of 0.935. Although this configuration is effective, enhanced ethanol purity was achieved when the reflux ratio was allowed to increase to values around 82.53%–84.99%, resulting in a maximum ethanol concentration of 88.16% and a perfect desirability score of 1.000. This finding suggests that while a lower reflux ratio is energetically favorable, a higher reflux ratio significantly enhances the separation efficiency and purity of ethanol, albeit at a higher operational cost.

Temperature also played a synergistic role in optimizing ethanol content. The optimal temperature range consistently converged around 75.2 °C to 76.2 °C, suggesting that this is the sweet spot for maximum ethanol recovery without compromising volatility balance. Deviations beyond this range could lead to undesirable separation of other volatile compounds, potentially impacting the purity or safety of the final product.

The optimization runs presented in Table 13 also reflect different strategic priorities. For example, Optimization Case 2 prioritized minimum holding time and allowed flexible reflux ratio values, achieving the highest ethanol concentration (88.16%) with maximum desirability. In contrast, Optimization Case 5 retained a fixed reflux ratio of 55.00% while still reaching 87.01% ethanol content, making it suitable for systems with energy or equipment limitations.

Overall, the optimization results confirm that a moderate holding time (around 2 h), slightly elevated temperatures (≈ 75.5 °C), and adjustable reflux ratios (depending on the desired purity and system constraints) together determine the success of the re-distillation process. The high desirability scores across the board demonstrate the robustness of the developed model and its applicability for both laboratory-scale optimization and potential scale-up scenarios.

Industry scale trial

To validate the process at a practical level and assess its performance under industrial conditions, a trial distillation was carried out on three independent batches. The distillation output was divided into three fractions: Head, Heart, and Tail, and the volume of each fraction was recorded in liters. The purpose of this evaluation was to determine the consistency, distribution, and efficiency of ethanol recovery at scale. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the Heart fraction consistently accounted for the largest volume across all three batches, confirming its role as the primary and most valuable output of the re-distillation process. In Batch 1, the Heart volume reached 21.63 L, while the Head and Tail volumes were 8.8 L and 5.4 L, respectively. This composition indicates that the process was tuned to maximize Heart yield while minimizing undesirable fractions.

In Batch 2, the Heart volume increased significantly to 28.3 L, suggesting either enhanced operational efficiency or improved feedstock quality. Meanwhile, the Head and Tail volumes were 4.7 L and 6.9 L, respectively. This redistribution hints at a possible tightening of cut points or better separation efficiency favoring the central ethanol-rich fraction. Batch 3 exhibited a similar Heart volume (28.0 L), but with a further reduction in Head volume to 4.0 L and an increase in Tail volume to 8.5 L. This pattern may reflect adjustments in the reflux ratio or distillation rate to recover more ethanol from the tailing end without compromising product quality. Collectively, the volume data in Fig. 8 highlight the reproducibility of Heart maximization across industrial runs, despite minor fluctuations in Head and Tail volumes. These fluctuations are likely attributable to batch-specific variability in feed composition or subtle operational differences during the distillation sequence.

In addition to absolute volumes, yield percentages were calculated to provide a normalized view of the process efficiency across the three product streams. As shown in Fig. 9, Batch 1 yielded 11.6% Head, 28.5% Heart, and 7.1% Tail, setting a baseline for comparative evaluation. In Batch 2, the Heart yield increased markedly to 37.2%, whereas the Head dropped to 6.2%, and the Tail rose slightly to 9.1%. This shift suggests a more optimized process configuration, concentrating ethanol recovery within the Heart fraction while maintaining acceptable by-product levels. Batch 3 followed a similar pattern with the highest Tail yield (11.2%), a slight decrease in Heart yield (36.8%), and the lowest Head yield (5.3%) among the three batches. The inverse relationship between Head and Tail yields, combined with a stable Heart yield, may point to controlled extension of the distillation cut toward the tail to improve total ethanol recovery.

Overall, the yield analysis across the three batches underscores the industrial process’s robustness and adaptability. The consistent dominance of the Heart fraction, both in volume and percentage, confirms the efficacy of the distillation protocol in selectively capturing ethanol-rich fractions. Minor variations in Head and Tail outputs reflect operational tuning or batch-specific inputs, offering valuable insights for further process refinement. These results are crucial for informing scale-up strategies, resource planning, and establishing quality control benchmarks in future production.

Table 14 presents an extensive chemical characterization of distillation fractions (Head, Heart, Bottom, Tail) from three production batches alongside a mixed sample. The evaluated parameters include volatile acid, alcohol content, total acidity, ester concentration, heavy metal contamination (zinc, lead, arsenic), as well as total suspended solids (TSS) and total dissolved solids (TDS).

The Head and Heart fractions consistently demonstrate superior chemical quality, marked by high alcohol concentrations ranging from 84.9% to 92.83% in the Head and 85.12% to 90.6% in the Heart fractions. These elevated alcohol levels reflect their higher purity and suitability for further industrial applications (Table 14). Volatile acid and total acidity values for these fractions remain minimal (0.002–0.004 and 0.03–0.04 respectively), indicating reduced acidic impurities and improved chemical stability. Moderate ester concentrations, particularly in the Heart fraction (6.05–12.24), further confirm their refined status, as excessive esters can negatively affect product quality.

In stark contrast, the Bottom and Tail fractions exhibit substantially lower alcohol content (as low as 1.24% in the Bottom) with considerably elevated volatile acid and total acidity levels, peaking at 1.04 and 0.3 respectively in the Bottom fraction. This trend underscores their relatively poor chemical quality, likely attributable to the accumulation of heavier and more acidic compounds towards the end of the distillation run. The ester concentrations in these fractions are markedly higher, up to 567.74 in the Bottom and 577.5 in the Tail, signaling the presence of ester-related by-products that can impart off-flavors and reduce overall product quality.

Heavy metal analysis reveals minimal contamination in the Head and Heart fractions, with zinc, lead, and arsenic levels mostly below detectable limits (< 0.05, < 0.003, < 0.002 respectively). Conversely, the Bottom and Tail fractions show elevated levels of these metals, with zinc up to 2.75 mg/L, lead reaching 1.53 mg/L, and measurable arsenic in the Bottom fraction. This suggests potential health and safety concerns requiring careful management or disposal (Table 14).

Total suspended and dissolved solids are also significantly higher in the Bottom and Tail fractions, with TSS values reaching 332 mg/L and TDS 321 mg/L in the Bottom fraction, further reflecting their impurity and lower suitability for direct use or consumption. The mixed sample generally exhibits intermediate values, confirming that the fractionation process effectively isolates higher-quality ethanol-rich fractions while segregating the lower-quality residue fractions (Table 14). This comprehensive analysis highlights the efficacy of the distillation separation in enriching desirable compounds in the Head and Heart fractions while concentrating impurities and contaminants in the Bottom and Tail fractions. Future process optimization efforts should aim to reduce the impurities and heavy metal content in these lower-quality fractions to minimize environmental impact and improve overall process sustainability.

Additionally, future studies could incorporate simulation-based optimization as a complementary approach. Simulation models, particularly those based on process modeling and computational fluid dynamics (CFD), could be used to rigorously predict the effects of distillation parameters on ethanol yield and contaminant reduction. By first performing simulation-based optimization, it would be possible to narrow down the best operational conditions before proceeding with experimental validation, potentially saving time and resources. Further validation of the simulated results with experimental data could ensure the reliability and applicability of the optimized conditions in real-world settings.

Lastly, while this study focused on optimizing ethanol yield and zinc reduction, it is important to note that holding time significantly impacts energy consumption, water usage, and CO2 emissions. Longer holding times generally lead to increased energy usage and water consumption, which could affect both the economic and environmental sustainability of the process. Future studies should integrate energy consumption analyses, water usage metrics, and CO2 emissions to fully assess the impact of different operating conditions. This would help identify the most sustainable operational parameters, ensuring that optimization not only focuses on product yield and quality but also minimizes the ecological footprint and operational costs.

Conclusion

This study optimized key re-distillation process parameters, including holding time, reflux ratio, and temperature, to enhance ethanol yield and reduce heavy metal content in traditionally produced Areke. Under the selected conditions, ethanol content reached up to 88.01%, and zinc contamination was minimized to below 3.63 mg/kg. These outcomes indicate the potential for improving the quality and safety of Areke; however, further investigation is required to validate long-term performance and reproducibility under varying feedstock conditions. The industrial trial results demonstrated promising consistency, particularly in Heart fraction recovery, while variability in the Head and Tail fractions underscores the need for additional refinement and real-world validation.

Future work should include a techno-economic evaluation, full-scale implementation trials, and investigation into alternative strategies to manage or repurpose contaminated fractions. Additionally, integrating simulation-based optimization and sustainability assessments (e.g., energy consumption, CO2 emissions, water usage) will be essential for optimizing both the economic and environmental impact of the process. Overall, the findings support the viability of re-distillation as an effective method for improving the chemical quality of Ethiopian traditional spirits, though further development is needed before widespread application.
