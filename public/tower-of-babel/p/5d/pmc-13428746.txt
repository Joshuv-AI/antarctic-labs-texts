Metatranscriptomics reveals urbanization-driven divergence in rodent viromes and zoonotic risks in Chinese megacities
Authors: Zhou-fu Xiang, Hui Wang, Fan Yang, Si-jun Chen, Ting-sheng Huang, Si-qi Wang, Zi-hao Jiang, Yi-yi Hu, Miao Xiang, Ke-xin Wang, Yu-zhu Wang, Ya-lan Huang, Yi-rong Li, Mang Shi, Wei Hou, Liang-jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metagenomic sequencing has advanced our understanding of wildlife-associated viruses and enabled identification of potential zoonotic pathogens. However, most studies remain geographically limited, with few systematic comparisons of virome compositions across urbanization gradients. To address this gap, we conducted large-scale sampling in two densely populated Chinese megacities—Wuhan and Shenzhen—with distinct climates. We collected 1,072 rodents from four species that frequently interact with humans, enabling comparative analysis of urban rectal virome dynamics and zoonotic risks in rodents. We identified 35 vertebrate-associated viruses. Among them, 9 were potentially novel species, including Norovirus and Orthopicobirnavirus species, and 3 had zoonotic potential, namely Orthohantavirus seoulense and human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV_OC43). We also discovered two viruses previously unreported in rodents, Erinaceus hedgehog Seoul orthohantavirus and Canine astrovirus, which revealed cross-order transmission risk. Additionally, 22 high-risk viruses were identified, with Wuhan and Shenzhen showing distinct prevalence patterns. Our analysis shows that inter-city rectal virome divergence is structured by meteorological variables, independently explaining 5.0% of the variation in viral community composition. Our findings highlight the importance of spatial distance in shaping the distribution and transmission of rodent-borne viruses. These insights are essential for proactive surveillance and mitigation of emerging zoonotic threats in high-density urban environments.

Introduction

Wild rodents serve as natural reservoirs for zoonotic viruses, posing persistent threats to public health1–3. Comprising over 40% of mammalian species4,5, commensal rodents particularly exhibit high habitat overlap with human settlements6, facilitating transmission of high-consequence pathogens. Previous studies have identified high-risk rodent-borne pathogens: Hantavirus (about 12% prevalence in Rattus norvegicus7,8) causing hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS); Mammarenavirus lassaense, responsible for fatal viral hemorrhagic disease9,10; and coronaviruses such as human coronavirus OC43 and human coronavirus HKU1, which are believed to have rodent origins11. Additionally, the detection of Orthopoxvirus monkeypox12, Mammarenavirus choriomeningitidis13, and Mammarenavirus chapareense14 in rodents further highlight rodents’ epidemiological importance. While targeted pathogen screening has identified these threats, primer-dependent approaches inherently underestimate viral diversity15,16, leaving critical gaps in spillover risk assessment.

High-throughput sequencing has transformed virome research, with metatranscriptomics enabling unbiased detection both known and novel viruses17,18 while elucidating evolutionary traits such as host tropism. For instance, multi-organ sequencing of 1688 small mammals in Yunnan, China, identified 102 novel viruses and 24 high-risk zoonotic viruses, revealing distinct organ-specific viral tropisms19. Another large-scale study of 4336 wild small mammals (including rodents, bats, and shrews) detected 669 viruses (534 novel), with rodents showing particularly high frequencies of potentially cross-species transmissible viruses (e.g., members of Arenaviridae and Arteriviridae)20. However, existing research predominantly targets viral detection in organs such as lungs and kidneys, overlooking the intestinal tract and its contents. The intestine serves as a critical site for viral replication21,22 and poses heightened spillover risks due to direct environmental exposure of excreted intestinal contents (e.g., through contaminated food or water)23,24. Intestinal viromes exhibit exceptional diversity25, and fecal viruses26 demonstrate prolonged environmental persistence – critical factors for understanding long-term transmission dynamics. Comprehensive intestinal virome analysis therefore represents a strategic paradigm for assessing zoonotic threats to humans and livestock.

Although metatranscriptomics provides a powerful tool for virome characterization, comprehensive cross-regional viral surveillance remains challenging, resulting in fragmented and incomplete understanding of viral diversity19. Previous studies have attempted to correlate virome composition with ecological factors—such as habitat type20, climatic conditions19, and host traits27—yet these efforts were often confined to single regions or specific ecological contexts. Systematic comparison of urban rodent viromes, especially in densely populated areas where human-rodent interactions increase spillover risks28, are particularly lacking. Urbanization-driven land-use changes and environmental modifications have reshaped human-wildlife interfaces, potentially amplifying zoonotic transmission28,29. Thus, a comparative analysis of urban rodent-borne viromes across cities, incorporating ecological variables, is essential to uncover transmission mechanisms and identify key risk factors.

Wuhan and Shenzhen represent two high population-density megacities in China with distinct eco-geographical characteristics30. Although both cities experience a subtropical monsoon climate, Wuhan has distinct seasons, whereas Shenzhen is marked by abundant rainfall and high humidity31. Both are endemic regions for Orthohantavirus hantanense32,33 yet exhibit divergent viromic profiles: surveillance data have detected Paramyxovirus34 in Wuhan versus Mammarenavirus and hepatitis E virus (HEV)32 in Shenzhen. In this study, we systematically sampled urban hotspots with pronounced rodent-human interface, employing metatranscriptomic sequencing of rodent rectal contents to comprehensively characterize rectal viromes and identify zoonotic threats.

Given the substantial ecological and climatic differences between these cities, we hypothesized that: (i) rodent rectal virome composition would differ significantly between the two locations due to their contrasting environmental conditions; and (ii) specific environmental factors, particularly mean annual temperature and precipitation patterns, contribute to shaping viral community structure. Specifically, our study aimed to: (i) characterize the diversity and composition of vertebrate-associated viruses in urban rodents; (ii) compare virome structures between the two cities using multivariate modeling; and (iii) explore potential ecological drivers associated with the observed variation.

In summary, our comparative analysis revealed significant inter-city viral composition differences, which we correlated with meteorological variables (mean annual temperature, precipitation patterns) through multivariate modeling. Our observations lay a data foundation for further exploring environmental drivers of urban rodent-borne viral variation.

Results

Sample information

From 2021 to 2024, we conducted large-scale, year-long continuous sampling in the Wuhan area, capturing a total of 726 wild rodent samples. Similarly, from 2022 to 2024, we collected 346 wild rodent samples in the Shenzhen area (Fig. 1A). Host species identification revealed that rodents in Wuhan comprised four species: Rattus norvegicus (n = 530), Rattus tanezumi (n = 177), Mus musculus (n = 14), and Rattus nitidus (n = 5). In contrast, the Shenzhen area only comprised two species, with Rattus norvegicus (n = 343) and Rattus tanezumi (n = 3) (Fig. 1B). Samples from the same region, year, and host species were pooled into a single library, with each library containing no more than 24 samples. If fewer than 24 samples were available, we directly mixed them into one library without cross-region mixing. A total of 68 libraries were generated, including 43 from Wuhan and 25 from Shenzhen (Fig. 1C, Supplementary data 2). Metatranscriptomic sequencing of these libraries collectively generated 995.6 Gb of data, averaging 14.6 Gb per library. The 68 libraries produced a total of 998.62 million clean reads, with an average of 1.49 million clean reads per sample.

Characterization of rodent rectal virome

Despite our discovery of many viruses, we focused on vertebrate-associated viruses—those phylogenetically related to known virus families or genera infecting vertebrates. Among the 68 libraries analyzed, 56 libraries contained vertebrate-associated viruses. No vertebrate-associated viruses were detected in any libraries derived from Rattus nitidus, so data of this species was not presented in Fig. 2. We identified 129 vertebrate-associated viral contigs sequences representing 35 distinct viruses from 13 families (Supplementary data 3), including 33 RNA viruses from 11 families (Astroviridae, Arenaviridae, Coronaviridae, Caliciviridae, Picornaviridae, Picobirnaviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Hepeviridae, Hantaviridae, Flaviviridae, Sedoreoviridae), and 2 DNA viruses from 2 families (Adenoviridae, Parvoviridae) (Fig. 2A and all phylogenetic trees of 13 families were provided in Supplementary Figs. 1–13). Viral diversity varied significantly across families. Among the identified viral families, Picobirnaviridae had the largest diversity, containing 9 viruses, followed by Astroviridae, Coronaviridae, Hantaviridae, Picornaviridae and Sedoreoviridae containing 3 viruses respectively, while other viral families were rarely detected (Fig. 2B). Notably, only Coronaviridae and Astroviridae were found in all three host species, underscoring their broad prevalence in wild rodents. Sedoreoviridae, Picornaviridae, Picobirnaviridae and Caliciviridae were detected in both Rattus norvegicus and Rattus tanezumi. In contrast, Adenoviridae and Arenaviridae were only detected in Rattus tanezumi, while Parvoviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Hepeviridae, Hantaviridae and Flaviviridae were solely found in Rattus norvegicus, demonstrating the host specificity of these viruses (Fig. 2C).

The most frequently detected viral families included Astroviridae, Coronaviridae, Picornaviridae and Sedoreoviridae, which were identified in at least 10 libraries each, while other families appeared only sporadically. Among libraries containing vertebrate-associated viruses, 75% (42/56) harbored 2–5 viral species (Fig. 2D). Both library counts and viral contig numbers followed similar distribution patterns, with the highest quantities observed in WH_G1, SZ_G1, and WH_G2 regions (Fig. 2E).

Evolutionary history and cross-species transmission of vertebrate-associated viruses

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that most identified viruses showed close evolutionary relationships with known viral sequences. However, we also detected several divergent viral sequences (<90% amino acid sequence identity in conserved regions) likely representing novel viruses. Of note, human coronavirus OC43 and Orthohantavirus seoulense, known to be able to infect humans, have been detected. Additionally, we report the first detection of Erinaceus hedgehog Seoul orthohantavirus and Canine astrovirus in rodent hosts.

Our study revealed 9 potentially novel viruses of 2 families Caliciviridae and Picobirnaviridae in rodent populations, including: (i) two cross-species Norovirus variants detected in R. tanezumi (Wuhan, 785 bp) and R. norvegicus (Shenzhen, 3762 bp) that clustered phylogenetically with 98.8% sequence similarity despite geographic and host divergence; and (ii) seven Orthopicobirnavirus sequences—three from Wuhan R. norvegicus, two from Wuhan R. tanezumi, and two from Shenzhen R. norvegicus—exhibiting high similarity to Porcine picobirnavirus but distributed across distinct hosts and regions, suggesting a potential for broad host and geographic distribution.

Our surveillance revealed remarkably conserved viral lineages between Wuhan and Shenzhen. Rodent astrovirus-like virus, Lucheng Rn rat coronavirus-like virus and Cardiovirus B-like virus 2 exhibited frequent co-circulation in both cities. Members of Sedoreoviridae demonstrated pronounced cross-regional transmission patterns: Rotavirus B showed 98.9% sequence similarity between Wuhan R. tanezumi (915 bp) and Shenzhen R. norvegicus (2698 bp), while Rotavirus G3 was detected in both cities’ R. norvegicus and Wuhan R. tanezumi with >98% inter-regional similarity. Strikingly, novel Norovirus variants—though were detected in different hosts (785 bp from R. tanezumi vs 3762 bp from R. norvegicus)—shared 98.8% sequence homology, indicating genetic relatedness consistent with possible cross-species and cross-regional transmission.

Among the 35 identified viruses, three were related to known human pathogens, and one—a novel detection in rodents—demonstrated cross-order transmission capability. (Supplementary Fig. 14). To investigate the evolutionary relationships of the identified viruses with other known viral sequences, intra-species phylogenetic trees were constructed based on the RdRp domain. The HCoV_OC43 sequence identified in this study clustered with other HCoV_OC43 reference sequences, showing 100% amino acid identity in the RdRp region. Orthohantavirus seoulense (SEOV) was identified in Rattus norvegicus in Shenzhen across multiple years. Sequences detected in 2022 were similar to Seoul orthohantavirus isolate SZ68 (NCBI ACCESSION: OM049767, 99.7% amino acid identity), and both sequences obtained in 2024 had shown 99.8% amino acid identity to known reference sequences. Erinaceus hedgehog seoul orthohantavirus was detected in three samples from Shenzhen in 2022, showing high similarity to reference sequence Erinaceus hedgehog seoul orthohantavirus (NCBI ACCESSION: OP899813, 98.8%-99.7% aa identity). As a virus originating from other species and detected for the first time in rodents, this finding indicated that the virus is capable of cross-species transmission and that such transmission is not a sporadic event. Similarly, a Canine astrovirus identified for the first time in this study clustered with other canine viruses in the phylogenetic tree and was highly similar (99.7% aa identity) to Canine astrovirus strain B194/China/GZ/2019 (NCBI ACCESSION: MN882009), confirming its cross-species transmission potential. The coverage and depth of these key viruses were provided in Supplementary data 4.

Characteristics of viruses with high emergence potential

To assess cross-species transmission risks, we classified the identified viruses based on their potential to cross species barriers between humans and animals or among different animal species. We identified a total of 22 high-risk viruses exhibiting frequent host jumps, including 7 zoonotic viruses (previously found in humans), 6 cross-order viruses (not found in humans but detected across multiple animal orders), and 9 potentially high-risk novel viruses (new viruses from at least one genus within three mammalian orders) (Fig. 4A). We detected the largest number of risk viruses in R. norvegicus (n = 17). Among these, Rotavirus B, Rotavirus G3, Cardiovirus B and Porcine picobirnavirus were found in both R. norvegicus and R. tanezumi. The two cities exhibited distinct viral prevalence patterns, with WH_G1 showing the highest concentration of risk-associated viruses (n = 13) while all three Shenzhen sampling sites contained at least three risk viruses each (Fig. 4B). Wuhan displayed a clustered distribution characterized by disproportionately high viral diversity in G1 region, whereas Shenzhen demonstrated more geographically widespread viral circulation—particularly for Cardiovirus B and Jeilongvirus beilongi which were detected across all three regions. Notably, four viruses (Rotavirus B, Rotavirus G3, Cardiovirus B, and Porcine picobirnavirus) were found to be co-circulating in both cities, suggesting transmission networks across these urban centers.

Our study revealed shared viruses among three rodents, with R. norvegicus and R. tanezumi exhibiting the most extensive overlap (6 shared viruses). While Mus musculus carried fewer viruses (n = 2), it shared one pathogen (Rodent astrovirus-like virus) with the other two hosts (Fig. 4C). Among the 6 shared viruses of R. norvegicus and R. tanezumi, most were rodent-associated variants (e.g., Lucheng Rn rat coronavirus-like virus, Rodent astrovirus-like virus and Murine kobuvirus 1-like virus), alongside the broadly distributed Rotavirus B, Rotavirus G3-like virus and Cardiovirus B-like virus.

Meteorological variables influence viral composition

Through systematic characterization of rodents’ rectal viromes, we identified substantial viral composition differences between Wuhan and Shenzhen. While only five viruses were shared between cities (Fig. 5A), rodents of Wuhan and Shenzhen harbored 17 and 13 viral species respectively, demonstrating geographical partitioning. This compositional divergence was reflected in the abundance-ordered heatmap (Fig. 5B), where the 5 shared taxa occupied the top row, followed by taxa abundant in Shenzhen (left) and Wuhan (right). MPC1, which represents a cold-dry versus warm-wet meteorological variables gradient (positive loading on atmospheric pressure (AP) and zonal wind velocities (Ulat), negative loadings on mean temperature (Tmean), precipitation (P), temperature (T), net radiation and downward solar radiation), explained 82.8% of the total meteorological variance (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. 15). Given that AP and Ulat exhibited positive loadings on MPC1, these two variables likely exert a predominant influence on the differentiation of viral communities between Wuhan and Shenzhen. During our sampling period, Wuhan exhibited significantly higher MPC1 values than Shenzhen (t-test, p < 0.001; Fig. 5D), indicating that the local meteorological conditions in Wuhan were characterized by relatively higher atmospheric pressure, and lower temperatures, precipitation and solar radiation compared to Shenzhen. This pronounced climatic contrast underpins the distinct viral communities observed in each location (Fig. 5A, B).

Constrained redundancy analysis (RDA) using MPC1–MPC3 as predictors revealed a clear directional structuring of viral communities along the primary meteorological variables gradient, with samples from Wuhan and Shenzhen separating distinctly along RDA1 (Fig. 5E). Wuhan samples clustered in high-MPC1 regions, corresponding to cooler and drier conditions, whereas Shenzhen samples occupied low-MPC1 regions, reflecting warmer and more humid environments. Non-overlapping 95% confidence ellipses confirmed this spatial differentiation. The RDA model was statistically significant (permutational ANOVA, p = 0.001, 999 permutations), supporting that meteorological variables impose a significant directional constraint on viral community composition.

Variance partitioning analysis (VPA) revealed that meteorological variables (MPC1–MPC8) independently explained 5.0% of viral community variation, while time and habitat contributed 3.0% independently and shared an additional 5.0% with meteorological variables (Fig. 5F). Host species (rodent identity) showed no independent explanatory power (0.0%). When pooling size (Num) was included as a covariate, it similarly explained 0.0% of variation (p = 0.89) (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17), confirming that unequal sample pooling did not confound community patterns. The large residual fraction (93.0%) indicates that unmeasured variables dominate viral community assembly. Notably, the forward-selected MPC subset (MPC2, MPC3, MPC5) yielded consistent results, with meteorological variables retaining its dominant independent role (5.0% independent, 5.0% shared with time and habitat; Supplementary Fig. 18), reinforcing that meteorological variables are important measurable drivers.

Discussion

Our systematic rectal virome investigation across two high population density megacities (Wuhan and Shenzhen) characterized 1072 rodent samples using metatranscriptomics, revealing distinct viral transmission risks, urban epidemic patterns, and host-sharing dynamics. Through multi-year, multi-regional surveillance, we identified significant inter-city rectal virome divergence driven by meteorological variables, a pattern that remained unchanged after controlling for unequal sample pooling sizes as a covariate, with ecological and temporal drivers partially explaining these variations. These findings advance our mechanistic understanding of zoonotic transmission in urban ecosystems and provide actionable insights for precision public health interventions in megacities.

Our study revealed a limited diversity of wild rodent species and associated viral communities. Using cage-trapping methods in densely populated urban areas, we conducted continuous four-year surveillance in Wuhan and three-year surveillance in Shenzhen. Rattus norvegicus emerged as the dominant species in both regions, reaching an extremely high proportion of 99% (343/346) in Shenzhen, which is consistent with the previously identified dominant species in China35. However, in the Hainan region of China, Rattus norvegicus was not the dominant species in wild rodent sampling36, demonstrating that the composition of host species varies across different regions. In this study, a total of 13 viral families were identified in the rectal fecal samples of wild rodents. Among them, two DNA viral families each contained only one viral sequence, while the RNA viral families Astroviridae, Picornaviridae, and Coronaviridae were detected in multiple samples, which is consistent with the results reported before19,20. Other RNA viral families were mostly detected as few viral sequences in individual libraries and did not dominate the rectal virome. Additionally, the common viral families in the rectal fecal samples differ from those in other organs. In other studies that included multiple organs, the primary viral families in the lung were Arenaviridae and Hantaviridae, while the liver, spleen, and kidney all contained Flaviviridae, Arenaviridae and Hantaviridae. Moreover, the spleen and kidney also included Paramyxoviridae19,20.

Our study provided critical insights into zoonotic transmission risks by analyzing host-virus associations in urban rodents. We identified 22 potentially high-risk viruses, including four RNA viruses demonstrating cross-species transmission between two rodent hosts, while most viruses exhibited host specificity - a pattern consistent with the established paradigm that phylogenetic distance limits viral spillover19,37–40. Notably, the dominant species Rattus norvegicus carried the highest diversity of risk-associated viruses, including known human pathogens and newly discovered variants. Of particular concern, both R. norvegicus and R. tanezumi harbored Rotavirus B isolates (89.7–91.2% amino acid identity to human variants) with spillover potential, despite phylogenetic divergence. We also identified HCoV_OC43, a virus typically prevalent during winter to spring (December to April)41,42. The sample in our study were collected in April, which is consistent with its known epidemiological seasonality. These findings underscore that while dominant host species may maintain greater viral diversity, less abundant but human-proximal rodents still pose substantial zoonotic risks through their carriage of both known and emerging pathogens.

Our comparative analysis revealed distinct virome compositions between the two urban rodent populations. Several viral families exhibited city-specific distributions: Arenaviridae and Adenoviridae were only identified in the Wuhan area, while Flaviviridae, Hantaviridae, Paramyxoviridae, and Parvoviridae were only detected in the Shenzhen area. The spatial distribution of risk-associated viruses differed markedly between cities. In Wuhan, viral risks were geographically concentrated, with the WH_G1 region showing clear aggregation while other areas harbored fewer viral types. In contrast, all three sampled regions in Shenzhen contained at least three risk-associated viruses, suggesting more widespread viral prevalence across the urban landscape. We identified five viral species shared between both cities, spanning three viral families: Astroviridae, Picornaviridae and Coronaviridae, demonstrating the broad host range and widespread geographical distribution of these viral families - consistent with previous findings20. Notably, while Hantaviridae were undetected in Wuhan, we identified six distinct Hantaviridae viruses in Shenzhen—a finding consistent with prior regional surveillance studies43.

Our study evaluated how meteorological variables shape inter-city variations in rodent rectal viromes, while elucidating urbanization’s multifaceted impacts on viral transmission dynamics. Urbanization drives three key shifts: (i) reduced wild rodent diversity44, (ii) altered host morphological traits (e.g., body size)45, and (iii) modified population densities through anthropogenic food subsidies (primarily food waste) and habitat fragmentation46. Furthermore, climate warming prolongs the activity periods of arthropod vectors (ticks and fleas), effectively extending the transmission window for rodent-borne pathogens47. Our analyses revealed that viral communities in Wuhan and Shenzhen are distinctly structured, with only a few taxa shared between the two cities. Climatic conditions, particularly the cold-dry versus warm-wet gradient captured by MPC1, significantly influenced this differentiation, with higher atmospheric pressure and zonal wind velocities in Wuhan corresponding to cooler and drier conditions, and lower MPC1 values in Shenzhen reflecting warmer, more humid conditions. Redundancy analysis (RDA) confirmed a strong directional effect of meteorological variables on viral composition, while variance partitioning analysis (VPA) indicated that meteorological variables independently explained a measurable portion of community variation, with temporal and habitat factors contributing additionally. Host species identity had no independent effect, and the majority of variation remains unexplained, suggesting that unmeasured host attributes or ecological factors may also shape urban rodent rectal viromes.

These observed statistical associations are consistent with several ecological mechanisms proposed in the literature, though direct causality remains to be established. The dominance of temperature and precipitation signals in Wuhan aligns with retrospective studies in China reporting seasonal associations between meteorological variables and viral respiratory infections48. This correspondence suggests that similar climatic drivers regulating host activity and population dynamics could potentially create seasonal windows for viral transmission and persistence in the environment.

Regarding wind dynamics, higher zonal wind velocities in Wuhan samples offers an intriguing, albeit indirect, clue. While our data do not provide direct evidence of aerosol transmission, this pattern resonates with hypotheses from urban pandemic studies linking airborne particulate matter to viral dispersal49. It is plausible that atmospheric circulation in dense urban environments could facilitate the transport of aerosolized particles or dust, potentially influencing local exposure to virus-containing materials. Moreover, the overlap along the zonal wind axis implies that wind regimes may not only modify local microclimates—affecting humidity and viral stability as discussed in pandemic spread analyses50—but might also serve as proxies for broader regional climatic systems. These systems could theoretically synchronize rodent movement and habitat use across both cities, contributing to the observed virome patterns. However, it is crucial to note that these associations do not confirm direct causal relationships. Instead, the identified meteorological variables likely represent interconnected environmental processes shaping viral community patterns in urban rodent populations. Our findings highlight potential links between meteorological forces and urban viral ecology, while suggesting that additional environmental factors related to urbanization may also contribute to these dynamics. Future studies integrating both climatic and urbanization-related variables may therefore help to further disentangle the complex mechanisms underlying host-virus-environment interactions.

For large residual faction in the study, in addition to meteorological variables, time and habitat factors, existing research indicates that annual mean water vapor pressure, vegetation density, and land use types can all significantly influence the viral richness of rodents19,51. Moreover, population ecological characteristics (density, age structure, sex ratio) and food web structure (prey diversity) may also affect virus transmission dynamics by regulating host-environment interactions52,53. However, due to the unavailability of detailed host attribute and ecological factors data in our study, the potential contribution of host traits could not be assessed. Additionally, while our analyses indicate that variation in pooling size did not significantly influence virome community structure, we acknowledge that the lack of strictly standardized pooling protocols represents a limitation. The potential impact of pooling on detection sensitivity for rare variants or low-abundance viruses remains uncertain and warrants caution in interpreting absolute richness estimates. Addressing these gaps through standardized sampling designs, individual-level viral quantification, and integration of host demographic data will be essential for refining predictive models of zoonotic disease emergence and strengthening precision public health interventions in urban ecosystems54, in line with the One Health strategy advocated by the World Health Organization (WHO).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified 35 viruses in wild rodents, 9 of which were previously undocumented. Our research focused on the comparison between two megacities, Wuhan and Shenzhen, and found significant divergence in the rodent rectal viromes of the two cities. This divergence implies distinct zoonotic risks and urban epidemic patterns in these areas. The ecological analysis serves as a means to understand the observed divergence, suggesting that urbanization and related environmental changes play a crucial role in shaping the viral communities in these cities. These findings significantly enhance our understanding of the rich diversity of vertebrate-associated viruses carried by commensal rodents in densely populated urban areas. They form an essential basis for the proactive prevention and control of emerging zoonotic pathogens. We strongly recommend the establishment of a comprehensive long-term surveillance strategy. Special attention should be paid to high-risk viruses and host species with cross-species transmission potential. By doing so, we can proactively mitigate spillover threats in rapidly expanding urban centers and safeguard global public health.

Methods

Study design and sample collection

This study aims to characterize the rectal virome of wild rodents inhabiting high human-activity areas across two geographically and climatically distinct megacities in China: Wuhan (central China) and Shenzhen (southeastern coast). We systematically compared urban rodent rectal virome compositions and investigated ecological drivers underlying observed differences. From 2021 to 2024, we collected samples using cage trapping methods across three high-density urban areas in each city in April each year and subsequent experiments carried out in May (Wuhan: 2021–2024; Shenzhen: 2022–2024). The selected cities (>1000 km apart) represent contrasting ecological zones while sharing high human-rodent interaction potential.

A total of 1072 wild rodents representing four species were captured, including Rattus norvegicus (n = 873), Rattus tanezumi (n = 180), Mus musculus (n = 14), and Rattus nitidus (n = 5). All animal specimens were initially identified to species by experienced field biologists according to morphological traits upon capture. Species identification was further confirmed using the cytochrome b (CytB) gene. Following, the animals were humanely euthanized, and entire specimens were stored in dry ice before being immediately transferred to −80 °C laboratory freezers for storage. During subsequent dissection, rectal fecal samples were collected from rodents under ice-cold sterile conditions, and all samples were immediately snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C until nucleic acid extraction.

RNA extraction and host identification

Total RNA was extracted from rectal fecal samples using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol, and subsequently used for host identification, sample pooling and library construction. Host species identification was confirmed by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) amplification of the CytB gene from total RNA, followed by Sanger sequencing. The obtained CytB gene sequences were compared and analyzed against known reference databases (NCBI GenBank) to confirm the identification of the host animals.

Sample grouping, library construction, and metatranscriptomic sequencing

We pooled RNA samples (300 ng each) from conspecific rodents sharing geographic origin and collection year (≤24 animals per library) for representative virome profiling (Supplementary data 2 for library metadata). No rarefaction or subsampling was performed during the experimental process or subsequent bioinformatic analyses to equalize sequencing depth across different libraries. After removing host rRNA using the Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat) (Vazyme, China), the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, China) was used for library preparation. Each RNA library was then sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500 platform (Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) with 150 bp paired-end reads.

Identification of the vertebrate-associated viruses

For clarity, we provided detaild data analysis workflow in Supplemental Methods. For each library, adaptors and low quality sequences were first removed using fastp55 (version 0.23.4, --disable_adapter_trimming --cut_front_window_size 5, --cut_tail_window_size 5, --cut_right_window_size 5 --cut_front_mean_quality 20, --cut_tail_mean_quality 20, --cut_right_mean_quality 20 --length_required 50 --n_base_limit 0) and then duplicate, low complexity and remaining rRNA sequences using SOAPnuke56 (version 2.1.9, -q 0.2 -n 0.02 -4 50), PRINSEQ++57 (version 1.2, -lc_entropy=0.5, -lc_dust=0.5) and SortMeRNA58 (version 4.3.6, --paired_in –fastx), respectively. Clean reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT59 (version 1.2.9) with default parameters. For viral identification, contigs (>600 bp) were first annotated against the NCBI non-redundant protein database (NR database, as of May 6, 2024) using DIAMOND (version 2.1.9) blastx60 (e-value threshold of 1e−5). Viral contigs were then validated against the NCBI non-redundant nucleotide database (NT database, as of May 6, 2024) using blastn (version 2.15.0)61, applying a threshold of e-value threshold of 1e−5 and removing contigs that best matched non-viral sequences in the NT database. All identified viral sequences were then aligned with the Virus-Host Database, retaining only vertebrate-associated viral sequences for further analysis. CD-HIT62 (v4.8.1, -c 0.95) was used to cluster the identified viral sequences (threshold of 95% similarity), the viral sequence with the longest length was selected to represent the viral cluster.

To achieve more precise species identification of vertebrate-associated viral sequences, contigs containing conserved domains were retained: RdRp domain (for RNA viruses), DNA polymerase domain (for Adenoviridae), and NS1 (for Parvoviridae). Each viral sequence was assigned to a specific viral species based on amino acid sequence similarity with reference sequences. The classification followed the species criteria set by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) for both known and new viruses. For viruses without explicit classification standards by ICTV, a new viral species was identified if its amino acid sequence similarity with known viral species was below 90% (Supplementary data 5). Phylogenetic analysis was then used to verify these identifications.

Quantification of viral abundance

To determine the abundance of viral species within the libraries, the clean sequences post-quality control using SortMeRNA were aligned against the SILVA database to remove rRNA-related sequences. The remaining sequences are then mapped to the viral species sequences identified using Bowtie263 (v2.5.1). The abundance of viruses was normalized using “Reads Per Million non-rRNA sequences” (RPM) per library. Viral contigs with an RPM value less than 1 were not presented in Fig. 2A. Based on the normalized abundance profiles, the host-virus association network was constructed using the igraph package in R and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.2).

Evolutionary analysis

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of vertebrate-associated viral sequences, we first classified these sequences into their respective viral families based on blastx results. We used ORFfinder (v0.4.3) (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/TOOLS/ORFfinder/) to identify ORF regions and used DIAMOND blastp to align these sequences against a locally constructed reference database, identifying contigs with conserved domains. Subsequently, MAFFT64 (version 7.526, --genafpair) with the E-INS-i algorithm was employed to align the amino acid sequences of viral replication-related domains (RdRp for RNA viruses, DNA polymerase for Adenoviridae, NS1 for Parvoviridae) from these contigs against reference sequences from GenBank. After alignment, ambiguous regions were removed using TrimAL65 (version 1.4.rev22). Phylogenetic trees for each viral family were estimated using IQ-Tree66 (version 2.3.4) based on the maximum likelihood method, and all phylogenetic trees were visualized using MEGA 11 (v11.0.13).

Definition and identification of high-risk viruses

The metadata results table of all viral species identified was downloaded from the NCBI Virus database. Only the records from GenBank were considered further. We used Taxonkit (version 0.16.0)67 to obtain the host order range for each viral species based on the NCBI Taxonomy database (on the basis of data available in January 2025). We classified potential high-risk viruses into three categories: (i) Zoonotic virus: viruses that have been found at least once in humans; (ii) Cross-order virus: viruses that have not been reported to infect humans but has been found in two or more animal orders; (iii) Novel potential risk virus: viruses with more than 80% amino acid similarity to known viruses, where the genus in question has been found in more than three mammalian orders. (that is, as there are necessarily no previous reports for the novel viruses identified in this study).

Meteorological data collection and analysis

To investigate the impact of meteorological variables on the diversity of vertebrate-associated viruses carried by wild rodents, we collected meteorological data from six areas in Wuhan and Shenzhen from the public database ERA5 (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/). The data spans from 2021 to 2024 and includes eight meteorological variables: atmospheric pressure (AP), mean temperature (Tmean), precipitation (P), dew point temperature (T), meridional (Vlon) and zonal wind velocities (Ulat), net solar radiation (net), and downward solar radiation (down) (Supplementary data 6). These variables were selected based on their theoretical potential to affect virus ecology. Literature suggests that temperature and precipitation influence host population dynamics, behavior, and seasonal activity patterns48. Moisture-related variables, such as dew point temperature, reflect atmospheric humidity conditions that may influence viral stability and persistence in environmental substrates. Solar radiation can affect viral inactivation rates in exposed environments50, while atmospheric circulation variables, including zonal and meridional wind speed, may influence the dispersal of aerosolized particles or contaminated dust in urban ecosystems49. Together, these variables were used to evaluate potential climatic drivers shaping rectal virome composition in rodent populations.

To account for co-linearity between all these 8 variables, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) and retained the first three principal meteorological components (MPC1–MPC3), which collectively explained 97.8% of the total variance (individual contributions: 82.8%, 9.9%, and 5.1%). These orthogonal components were used to represent climatic variation in all downstream statistical analyses (Supplementary data 7, Supplementary Fig. 15).

We then conducted Variation Partitioning Analysis (VPA; vegan68 R package) to partition viral community variation among three predictor sets: (1) meteorological variables (represented by the first eight principal components, MPC1–MPC8), (2) host species, and (3) temporal and habitat factors (Year, Type), with significance assessed via permutational ANOVA (999 permutations) on constrained Redundancy Analysis (RDA) models using Hellinger-transformed viral abundances. To account for potential confounding effects of unequal sample pooling sizes across sites, we included pooling size (Num in Supplementary data 7) as a covariate in all multivariate analyses. Specifically, Num was incorporated into the “Host & Pooling Size” group in the variance partitioning analysis (VPA) and retained as a covariate during forward selection of environmental variables. To specifically evaluate meteorology-driven structuring, we performed constrained RDA using MPC1–MPC3 as predictors, tested model significance and adjusted explained variance by permutation, and visualized directional effects of MPC1 and MPC2 on community composition through ordination biplots.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using vegan R package for multivariate analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Variation Partitioning Analysis (VPA), constrained Redundancy Analysis (RDA), and permutational ANOVA (999 permutations). Detailed descriptions of the statistical methods can be found in the Methods sections and Supplemental Methods 3. The significance level was set at p = 0.001, denoted by p < 0.001*** in Fig. 5.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the internationally accepted principles and guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Wuhan University and approved by the ethics committee of Wuhan University.
