Integrated transient chromophores for efficient photo-induced radiofluorination
Authors: Zhengbo Zhu, Chia-Yu Huang, Richard W Russell, Peyton Kinon, Xinrui Ma, Yifan Mao, Xuedan Wu, Zhanhong Wu, David A Nicewicz, Zibo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPhotoredox catalysis has emerged as a powerful tool in organic synthesis, enabling substrate activation via visible light in the presence of an external photocatalyst. However, reliance on bimolecular interactions between the photoexcited catalyst and substrate limits efficiency, a critical drawback for applications such as radiolabeling. To address this challenge, we report a molecular design strategy that bypasses this constraint by integrating a transient chromophore directly into the substrate. This approach eliminates diffusion-dependent activation, achieving highly efficient transformations through a self-correcting mechanism: reactive intermediates either engage nucleophilic addition or revert to the ground state via decay, minimizing side reactions. The transient chromophore additionally acts as a charged leaving group, enabling facile purification of the neutral labeled product. We demonstrate this strategy in radiofluorination reactions, achieving superior yields and simplicity compared with conventional photoredox methods. Its utility is highlighted by the synthesis of positron emission tomography (PET) tracers for oncology, neurology, and cardiology.

INTRODUCTION

Photoredox catalysis has transformed synthetic chemistry by harnessing visible light energy to drive challenging transformations, enabling the sustainable construction of pharmaceuticals and functional materials under exceptionally mild conditions.1–5 Yet, despite its transformative potential, the field remains constrained by the reliance on intermolecular energy or electron transfer between transiently excited photocatalysts and substrates via bimolecular collision, generating high-energetic intermediates (Figure 1A). The short half-lives of the excited photocatalysts necessitate sufficient substrate concentration to ensure productive collisions.6 This stochastic bimolecular regime inherently limits efficiency, scalability, and precision, critical shortcomings in applications like radiolabeling, where rapid, high-yielding reactions are essential under high-dilution conditions.

Here, we disrupt this longstanding paradigm with a unified molecular architecture that eliminates the need for an external photocatalyst. By covalently embedding a transient chromophore directly into the substrate through a conjugated tether, we enable intramolecular energy/electron transfer as a self-contained photoredox system (Figure 1B). This design reimagines substrate activation—photoexcitation triggers a directional charge shift along the conjugated backbone, generating reactive intermediates with atomic precision while bypassing the inefficiencies of diffusion-dependent bimolecular collisions. Crucially, the system’s integrated architecture introduces a self-correcting mechanism: unproductive high-energetic intermediates decay back to the precursor through the same conjugated pathway, dynamically suppressing side reactions.

Over the past decade, our research has unlocked the unique potential of 9-aryl acridinium dyes as potent photoredox catalysts, enabling previously challenging transformations of arenes,7 alkenes,8 and alkynes.9 A key breakthrough was recognizing the dual role of the 9-aryl group, providing steric protection to the acridinium core and serving as a tunable electron reservoir, critically modulating the catalyst’s photophysical and redox properties. Upon photoexcitation, the acridinium scaffold forms a short-lived locally excited singlet state (LES), which undergoes intramolecular charge transfer (CT) to populate a CT singlet state (CTS) with the 9-aryl group as an electron donor.7 This CTS exhibits remarkable reactivity. While it decays back to ground state in the absence of quenchers, it engages in productive electron transfer when encountering electron-rich arene substrates, generating arene cation radicals primed for downstream functionalization. To enhance efficiency, we considered tethering the acridinium core directly to arene substrates, enabling intramolecular CT that bypasses diffusion limitations and minimizes decomposition pathways. Comparative studies of attachment sites revealed the N-aryl position outperforms 9-aryl linkages (see Figure S2) while maintaining synthetic accessibility.10 The aniline intermediates were readily obtained from phenol precursors via simple SNAr with 4-fluoronitrobenzene, followed by a reduction step. These intermediates were condensed with a xanthylium salt to yield the desired integrated transient chromophore (ITC) substrate in overall high yields (Figure 1C). Crucially, introducing saturated carbon linkers between the acridinium core and the N-aryl ring substantially lowered the yield compared with the conjugated congeners, strongly suggesting that CT occurs more efficiently through the conjugated system upon photoexcitation (Figure 1D). To evaluate the efficiency of our new design, Stern-Volmer quenching analysis was performed. High levels of static quenching were observed with the ITC substrate, leading to almost no detectable emission, which likely suggested highly efficient intramolecular CT (Figure S12). Moreover, examining the 18/19F-CRASNAr reaction rate for the catalytic system and the ITC system, the ITC substrates displayed markedly faster kinetics than the catalytic version (Figures S8 and S9). In addition, this activation model also allows for functionalization of recalcitrant substrates, such as electron-neutral arenes, as also demonstrated by the synthesis of synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) positron emission tomography (PET) ligand SynVesT-2 (11),11,12 which failed under the corresponding catalytic conditions (Figures 1E and S4). The inherent charge of the nucleofuge further simplifies purification via ion-exchange cartridges, offering practical advantages for radiochemistry.

The most compelling aspect of our design lies in its potential to streamline the discovery of novel 18F-labeled PET tracers.13 PET imaging has become a cornerstone of precision medicine,14,15 providing unprecedented capabilities for disease diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and therapeutic personalization through real-time, non-invasive visualization of biological processes.16,17 These remarkable advances rely heavily on radiolabeled molecules, which target specific biological pathways to pinpoint disease locations or assess therapeutic outcomes. However, the synthesis of such PET probes remains challenging due to complex radiolabeling procedures and demanding purification requirements that often limit their widespread clinical application. Among PET isotopes, fluorine-18 (18F) holds a distinctive role due to its practical half-life of 109.8 min and widespread availability, making it a cornerstone of PET radiopharmaceutical development,18–25 where over half of FDA-approved PET tracers incorporate 18F. While transformations with the stable isotope 19F are well-established, adapting these methods for 18F poses unique challenges: the short half-life and low radioisotope concentrations require rapid reactions at low reactant levels, rendering many traditional organic methods unsuitable for PET tracer synthesis.20,26,27 Current Ar-18F bond formation relies heavily on traditional nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) mechanisms, which successfully produce clinical-grade tracers but are limited to specific substrates with highly activated nucleofuges, narrowing their applicability.21,23 Transition metal catalysis employing palladium or copper has expanded the scope of Ar-18F bond-forming reactions, yet it introduces practical drawbacks such as high precursor and catalyst loadings, ligand dependency, air sensitivity (especially with Pd(0) catalysts), and complex setups for clinical environments.28–38 Preparing transition metal adducts, such as palladium-precursor complexes, can streamline the process, but this still requires stoichiometric metals, risking trace metal contamination for patients.39,40 These constraints highlight the pressing need for innovative, efficient synthetic methods that align with the demands of PET tracer production in clinical settings. In this vein, Ritter’s group introduced a concerted nucleophilic aromatic substitution strategy via the intermediacy of uronium fluorides.27 While this strategy is exceptionally efficient for electron-deficient arenes, its application to electron-rich arenes remains limited. This limitation was largely addressed by introducing stoichiometric amount of ruthenium complex, albeit with a large excess of reagents.41

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To this end, our investigation began with conditions optimized for the deoxyradiofluorination strategy (Figure S1),42 which afforded a modest 3.3% radiochemical yield (RCY) of [18F]8 but revealed a key advantage that nearly all of the precursor was effectively removed with a strong cation exchange (SCX) cartridge. By systematically reducing the amount of TBAHCO3 (tetrabutylammonium bicarbonate), dramatic improvements in reaction efficiency were achieved, reaching 57.2% RCY. Given the necessity of a salt to release [18F]fluoride from the quaternary methyl ammonium (QMA) anion-exchange cartridge, we explored the use of tetrabutylammonium perchlorate (TBAClO4) as a non-nucleophilic additive, which has been applied to elute 18F.43 Unlike TBAHCO3, increasing the amount of TBAClO4 correlated with higher RCY of [18F]8. However, using TBAClO4 exclusively led to fluoride loss, likely due to insufficient base to maintain [18F]fluoride in its anionic form. To resolve this, a minimal amount of TBAHCO3 combined with TBAClO4 was employed for elution, ensuring optimal fluoride retention. The overall high efficiency of this transient chromophore model also allows for a significant reduction in precursor loading to 0.5 mg, achieving a 26.3% RCY, enhancing its potential for future applications (Figure S6).

To probe the generality of the transient chromophore model, we tested electron-rich and neutral arenes attached to the acridinium core (Figure 2). Both para-methoxy and para-alkyl substituted arenes were successfully engaged in this transformation ([18F]12-[18F]15). Meta- and ortho-methyl and methoxy arenes were also tolerated ([18F]16-[18F]18). In prior work from our laboratories, competing byproduct 4-chlorofluorobenzene was formed with electron-neutral arenes, particularly meta-substituted arenes. However, this transient chromophore design eliminated the possibility of this byproduct formation, leading to a highly selective transformation. It is worth noting that for ortho-substituted arenes, poor to moderate RCYs were obtained with chromophore A, possibly due to the steric hindrance from the 2,7-di-tert-butyl group. While changing the position of the di-tert-butyl group to 3,6 positions (Acr+ B), a significant improvement in reaction outcomes was observed (Figure S5). With Acr+ B, 2-methoxy-substituted arenes with various functional groups at 4 and/or 6 positions were all accommodated, providing the desired products in moderate to good RCYs ([18F]19-[18F]26).

Heterocycles such as benzothiazole and pyridine were also well tolerated ([18F]27 and [18F]28). The importance of 18F-labeled prosthetic groups in the construction of PET agents motivated us to develop an efficient transformation to access them. In previous reports, high loading of precursors (50 μmol scale) and multiple high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purification steps largely limited their applications in PET agent development, especially in (pre)clinical studies.44 However, this new design allows for significantly lower precursor loading (5 mg, 3–8 μmol) and the use of simple cartridge filtration to remove the precursors, thereby minimizing the need for extensive HPLC purification steps. To test the feasibility of this approach, azide ([18F]29), aryl and alkyl alkynes ([18F]30, [18F]31), and NHS ester ([18F]32) were selected as examples, affording moderate to good RCYs. An HPLC trace indicated that the quality of filtrates was sufficiently pure for immediate use in the next step, which suggests the potential for clinical-scale applications within a short synthesis time.

The scope with electron-rich and neutral arenes inspired us to further extend our platform to clinically relevant PET tracer synthesis (Figure 3). An excellent RCY was obtained for protected fluorouracil using an optimized biphenyl linker (Acr+ C) ([18F]33), which was subsequently deprotected under acidic conditions to [18F]fluorouracil ([18F]33–1) with a single HPLC purification. Similar to glucose metabolism, the metabolism of amino acids plays a key role in cancer progression. Methods toward synthesizing PET isotope-labeled amino acids are crucial in this area.45,46 Benzoyl-protected leucine was selected as a model to test the feasibility of directly labeling acyl-protected amino acids, a key feature in peptides. Good RCYs were obtained from both methyl and tert-butyl esters ([18F]34 and [18F]35), and following acid-mediated deprotection, they afforded the labeled acyl-protected leucine ([18F]35–1) in good yield. While reported methods, including C-H radiofluorination,47 deoxyradiofluorination,42,43 and direct 19F/18F exchange strategies43,48 for phenylalanine and tyrosine labeling suffered from purification complexity and reduced molar activity, the transient chromophore approach delivered 18F-tyrosines in useful RCYs with a simplified workflow ([18F]36-[18F]38). [18F]Flurpiridaz,49 an FDA-approved PET radiopharmaceutical for myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), has attracted the interest of radiochemists for developing analogs to extend its application. Exploring the Flurpiridaz analog precursors under the standard conditions, the desired [18F]Flurpiridaz analogs ([18F]39-[18F]42) were obtained in modest but useful yields with good molar activity (47.7 GBq/μmol for [18F]42). Compared with [18F]Flurpiridaz49 and analogs,50–52 switching the 18F from the terminal alkyl chain to the arene provides additional opportunities to modify the terminal position of the alkyl side chain to alter physical properties of the myocardial drugs. In addition, this strategy enabled direct labeling of complex drugs like prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-targeting Glu-NH-CO-NH-Lys53 ([18F]43 and [18F]44). Further exploring the cartridge-purified reaction mixture of [18F]43 and [18F]44 to acid hydrolysis condition, the desired products ([18F]43–1 and [18F]44–1) could be obtained in applicable overall yields with a single HPLC purification process.

We have demonstrated the effectiveness of our labeling methods through numerous substrate examples. To underscore the real-world impact of this newly developed technology, we highlight its applications in three critical fields of molecular imaging: oncology, neurology, and cardiology. In neurology, our method excels by successfully labeling optically pure SynVesT-2 with 18F in 27.0% RCY, a significant improvement over previously reported methods using organotin (6.8% RCY) or iodonium (<1% RCY) precursors.11 A key advantage of our method is the elimination of tin precursors, which are toxic and raise concerns about residual contamination, offering a safer and more efficient synthesis of this important PET tracer.

PET imaging study

A critical objective of our transient chromophore methodology is enabling high molar activity PET tracer production for (pre)clinical translation to cancer diagnostics. While our previous 19F/18F exchange method demonstrated prominent tumor uptake of 18F-labeled O-methylated tyrosine with MCF-7 models,48 the requirement of multiple HPLC purifications limited its large-scale production. By contrast, the same agent generated by our new method demonstrated increased tumor uptake and lower background at later time points, highlighting the importance of obtaining high molar activity and underscoring the potential of this design for clinical-scale synthesis (Figure S18). Another compelling example is [18F]44–1, which delivers high tumor uptake and a cleaner background even at early time points. Unlike most FDA-approved PSMA PET agents, which exhibit elevated kidney uptake,54 [18F]44–1 stands out. Its backbone also suggests potential for radiotherapeutic applications, broadening its utility in oncology (Figure 4A). Turning to cardiology, MPI has been widely used to evaluate heart function and diagnose coronary artery disease (CAD).55,56 Our methods provide us easy access to Flurpiridaz analogs ([18F]39-[18F]42). When studied in murine models, [18F]41 displayed rapid uptake and clearance properties, which may allow repeated patient imaging within the same day. Moreover, analog [18F]42 demonstrated sustained heart muscle uptake up to 3 h post injection (Figure 4B). It is worth noting that both [18F]41 and [18F]42 showed high heart uptake (~60% ID/g), comparable to the reported Flurpiridaz and its analogs, but many previously reported agents suffered from defluorination at later time points (Figures 4B and S21).51 The tunability of these analogs underscores the versatility and promise of our approach in cardiac imaging.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our ITC strategy overcomes diffusion-limited activation barriers posed by intermolecular photoredox catalyst systems. By combining the photocatalyst, substrate, and leaving group into a single programmable unit, this platform surpasses incremental advances. It establishes a novel class of photoredox systems that activate challenging substrates, minimize side reactions, and simplify purification under mild conditions. Demonstrated through the synthesis of PET tracers across oncology, neurology, and cardiology, this technology provides a robust, rapid, and scalable pathway to high-purity compounds. It stands poised to redefine the frontiers of light-driven chemistry in molecular imaging.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

Originating from the principles of photosynthesis, photoredox catalysis has emerged as a powerful synthetic tool capable of forming challenging chemical bonds under mild conditions. However, its dependence on bimolecular interactions between an excited photocatalyst and a substrate imposes kinetic limitations that are especially problematic in radiochemistry, where rapid and efficient transformations are essential. A promising solution is the covalent tethering of the substrate and photocatalyst, which converts substrate activation from a collision-dependent bimolecular event into a unimolecular process, which markedly enhances reaction efficiency. Notably, charged photocatalysts such as acridinium derivatives offer an added advantage: their charged property can function as traceless, easily removable leaving groups, greatly simplifying product purification. These features offer an exceptional chance for radioisotope incorporation, including 18F, and enable efficient labeling even at low substrate loadings. Importantly, the improved activation mode also enables the use of lower substrate loadings while maintaining high efficiency, facilitating the radiolabeling of valuable and structurally complex drug molecules.
