Investigating seasonal metabolic variations in bipolar disorder: a targeted metabolomics study
Authors: Xue Li, Xinzi Liu, Ziyan Li, Sitong Feng, Sisi Zheng, Hongxiao Jia, Dongqing Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar disorder (BD) is a severe mental illness characterized by seasonal episodes, yet its metabolic basis remains unclear.MethodsThis study utilized targeted metabolomics to investigate seasonal metabolic changes. Serum samples were collected from 9 BD patients And 9 matched healthy controls (HCs) at six time points: 15 days before, on, And 15 days after both the spring and autumn equinoxes. All samples were analyzed using the same platform for consistency.ResultsIn the autumn phase, repeated-measures Analysis identified 15 metabolites in HCs And 38 in BD with time effects, And 111 showed group-by-time interactions; none remained significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. In contrast, Analysis across the spring And autumn revealed significant time effects in 277 metabolites in HCs And 263 in BD, with 16 triacylglycerols showing significant interactions (FDR-p < 0.05). Group differences peaked around the autumn equinox, with opposite trends observed. Amino acids such as glutamine, lysine, histidine, and asymmetric dimethylarginine continuously increased from spring to autumn in both groups. These seasonal variations in BD metabolites from spring to autumn were mainly linked to the biosynthetic pathways of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan.ConclusionThese findings indicate a seasonal modulation of serum metabolite profiles in individuals with BD, which may underlie or reflect mood-related changes. However, causal relationships and mechanistic pathways require further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07418-7.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Seasonal episodes, Triacylglycerols metabolism, Amino acid biosynthesis

Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe and disabling psychiatric disorder characterized by alternating episodes of mania and depression [1]. The global prevalence of BD varies between 1 And 2% [2], leading to a significant economic burden on both individuals and society [3]. Evidence suggests that seasonal variations are a notable feature of BD [4]. Specifically, individuals with BD tend to experience more frequent and severe manic episodes during the spring and summer months [5], while depression is generally more common in the autumn and winter [6, 7]. Despite these findings, the specific mechanisms underlying the relationship between seasonal patterns and BD remain unclear.

Metabolomics is an effective method for exploring the metabolic changes that occur during seasonal transitions [8]. Metabolites can provide important insights into how environmental factors influence organisms that are sensitive to such changes [9]. Previous studies indicated that the metabolism of energy, galactose, tryptophan and amino acids, such as glycine, serine, and threonine is significantly altered in patients with BD, which may contribute to the mood fluctuations associated with the disorder [10]. In our previous study, we identified 27 metabolites in BD altered over time around the spring equinox. The essential pathways encompass arginine biosynthesis, D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism [11]. However, there is a significant gap in understanding metabolic changes during other seasons, particularly autumn, which is associated with a higher frequency of depressive episodes [6]. In contrast to spring, autumn is marked by shorter daylight, cooler temperatures, and shifts in biological rhythms [12, 13], all of which may influence key metabolic pathways, particularly energy production, the synthesis of amino acids and neurotransmitter pathways [14]. Therefore, to address this gap, we collected blood samples during autumn, precisely 15th day before, on, And 15th day after the autumnal equinox [15] to observe the metabolic changes of BD and healthy controls (HCs). The data would be integrated with spring data to analyze the seasonal patterns and metabolites in both HCs and BD. These comparisons will provide valuable insight into seasonal biomarkers and metabolic pathways that differentiate manic and depressive episodes. This research aims to elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying the seasonal rhythms of BD.

Methods

Participants

A cohort of 18 participants, comprising 9 individuals diagnosed with BD And 9 HCs, were recruited between the spring And autumn of 2021. All participants were of Han Chinese descent And residing in Beijing. Their age range spanned from 19 to 49 years. Venous blood samples were collected at six longitudinal time points, corresponding to 15 days before, on the day of, And 15 days after the spring equinox (timepoints 1–3), And similarly, 15 days before, on the day of, And 15 days after the autumn equinox (timepoints 4–6). The same cohort of participants completed all six sampling sessions as part of a within-subject repeated-measures design. The Institutional Ethics Committee of Beijing Anding Hospital granted ethical approval for this study (202025FS-2) and conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The inclusion criteria for BD encompass the following: (1) Meeting the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5); (2) Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) [16] score equal to or less than 7; (3) Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [17] score equal to or less than 6; (4) Maintenance of a consistent dosage of psychotropic medication for a minimum duration of 8 weeks, accompanied by a period of clinical stability exceeding 4 weeks.

The exclusion criteria for BD are as follows: (1) The presence of clinically significant suicidal or homicidal thoughts or behaviors; (2) The existence of neurodegenerative, metabolic, or endocrine diseases; (3) A previous history of electroconvulsive therapy or hormone therapy within the preceding 6 months; (4) The occurrence of stressful life events in the last 6 months; (5) Being pregnant or lactating.

HCs were matched with BD for age, gender, weight, and height. They had no history of significant illnesses or allergies. The psychological condition of HCs was assessed via the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Inventory (MINI).

Sample collection and serum Preparation

Participants were instructed to abstain from all foods or beverage consumption at least 12 h before sample collection. The intake of tea, coffee, And alcohol was explicitly prohibited during this fasting period. Blood samples were collected between 8:00 am And 10:00 am. Peripheral blood samples were obtained from all participants using BD Vacutainer SST II Advance Tubes. Samples were processed within 4 h, with serum and plasma separated, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at − 80 °C. Frozen serum samples, packed in dry ice, were transported to Beijing Protein Innovation Co., Ltd. in Be for metabolomics testing and analysis.

Metabolic technique

Targeted metabolomic profiling of serum samples was performed using the Biocrates® MxP® Quant 500 Kit (Biocrates Life Sciences AG, Innsbruck, Austria), which enables the quantification of 630 metabolites across multiple compound classes. The analysis was carried out at the Beijing Protein Innovation Co., Ltd. in Be. All samples were processed under identical conditions at the same laboratory, using the same targeted metabolomics platform and protocol, ensuring data comparability across time points. The manufacturer assigns nomenclature to metabolites according to an annotation system based on the LIPID MAPS classification. Metabolite species are expressed in the format “xx: x,” where “xx” indicates the total number of carbon atoms and “x” indicates the total number of double bonds.

Sample information, including group assignment (e.g., sex, disease status) and covariates (e.g., age, collection date, processing time), was first recorded in the metabolic laboratory information management (Met-LIMS) system. Each 10 µL serum sample was transferred into a 96-well plate And dried under a nitrogen stream for 40 min. Derivatization was then carried out using 5% phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC), a reagent that reacts with amino groups to enhance detection sensitivity. The plates were incubated in the dark for 1 h, followed by An additional 2-hour nitrogen drying step. Subsequently, 300 µL of extraction solvent was added to each well, And the plate was shaken at 44G for 30 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 78G for 10 min to collect the supernatant extract. For liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) Analysis, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 100 µL of water, And 5 µL of the diluted solution was injected. For flow injection analysis–mass spectrometry (FIA-MS), 10 µL of the extract was diluted with 240 µL of running solvent, And 20 µL was used for injection.

MS analysis was performed using a QTRAP® 6500 system (SCIEX), a triple quadrupole–linear ion trap hybrid mass spectrometer, coupled to an ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters) for chromatographic separation. The system used a Biocrates® MxP® Quant 500 UHPLC column (Part No. 22005), with water containing 0.2% formic acid as mobile phase A And acetonitrile containing 0.2% formic acid as mobile phase B. The column temperature was maintained at 50 °C. Two chromatographic methods were applied: LC1 for detection in positive ion mode and LC2 for negative ion mode. These methods differ in their gradient elution profiles, optimized for different metabolite classes.

Raw mass spectrometry data were imported into the Met-LIMS software. For LC-MS/MS data, calibration curves were generated using designated internal standards, And the concentrations of individual metabolites were calculated accordingly. In contrast, FIA-MS data were quantified based on a single-point calibration approach. Metabolites without detected values in some samples were considered missing values, typically due to concentrations falling below the lower limit of detection or exceeding the upper limit. Although it is common practice to exclude metabolites with more than 50% missing values across samples, this threshold was not applied in the present project. All target metabolites were retained for downstream analysis, regardless of missingness.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Analysis were performed using the SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp.) And the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 platform (http://www.MetaboAnalyst.ca/) [18]. Since all serum samples were Analyzed under identical experimental conditions using the same metabolomics platform, reagents, And protocols, no batch effect correction was required to integrating data across the spring and autumn sampling sessions. Concentration values log transformed and mean centering normalized for all metabolites. A two-stage statistical analysis was performed. Stage 1 focused on the autumn phase (timepoints 4–6), as the spring-phase data had been previously analyzed and published [11]. Stage 2 included both spring and autumn phases (timepoints 1–6). In each stage, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was first applied separately within the BD and HC groups to assess time effects, followed by a mixed-design ANOVA with group (BD vs. HC) as the between-subject factor and time as the within-subject factor to evaluate group, time, and interaction effects. To control for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. As is common in exploratory studies with limited sample sizes, few metabolites remained statistically significant after FDR correction. Therefore, both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted p-values were examined, and adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant And selected as candidate metabolites. Pathway enrichment Analysis was performed on significantly time-dependent metabolites using the Pathway Analysis module in MetaboAnalyst 5.0.

Metabolites analysis in autumn

A repeated-measures ANOVA was performed separately within the BD and HC groups to assess time-dependent changes in metabolite levels during the autumn sampling period (timepoints 4–6). A total of 15 metabolites in the HC group showed time effects (Table S1), And 38 metabolites displayed time-related variation in the BD group (Table S2). However, none of these remained statistically significant after controlling for multiple comparisons using the FDR correction.

To evaluate whether temporal trajectories differed between groups, a mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA was applied, with group (BD vs. HC) as a between-subject factor And time as a within-subject factor. A total of 111 metabolites showed group-by-time interaction effects (Table S3). Additionally, 9 metabolites exhibited time effects (Table S4), And 46 showed group effects (Table S5). Nonetheless, none of these remained statistically significant after the FDR correction.

Analysis of metabolites from spring to autumn

After analyzing the autumn-phase data (timepoints 4–6) independently, we expanded the investigation to include spring-phase data (timepoints 1–3) to explore longitudinal metabolite trajectories across six timepoints.

A one-way repeated-measures AnOVA was conducted separately within each group to evaluate time-dependent changes. In the HC group, 277 metabolites exhibited significant temporal variation after FDR correction (Table S6). In the BD group, 263 metabolites showed significant time effects after FDR adjustment (Table S7). These results indicate significant seasonal variations in the metabolite profiles for both groups.

Among them, 240 metabolites exhibited the same time effect in both groups. In addition, 37 metabolites exhibited significant time effects only in HCs, including cholesteryl esters (CE) 15:0, 2 ceramides (Cer), 3 phosphatidylcholines (PC), 23 triacylglycerols (TG), And 6 amino acids. Conversely, 23 metabolites showed time effects uniquely in BD, including CE 17:0, Cer d18:1/23:0, PC 32:2, lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) 14:0, sphingomyelins (SM) 20:2, 2 diacylglycerols (DG), 14 TG, And 2 amino acids.

Mixed-design repeated-measures AnOVA identified 65 metabolites exhibiting significant group-by-time interaction effects (p < 0.05), of which 16 remained significant after FDR correction. All 16 metabolites belonged to the TG (Table 2). To visualize the differential temporal trajectories, we analyzed the pattern of metabolite fluctuations between groups. In HCs, two distinct trends were observed. The first involved a gradual increase from T1 (15 days before the spring equinox) to T4 (15 days before the autumn equinox), followed by a transient decrease at T5 and a secondary rise at T6. The second trend displayed an initial decline from T1 to T2 (spring equinox), then a steady increase toward T4, a decrease at T5, and a modest rebound at T6. In contrast, the BD group exhibited a more uniform profile, with concentrations decreasing at T1, increasing through T4, peaking at T5 (autumn equinox), and subsequently declining at T6. While spring-phase trajectories were relatively similar across groups, divergence emerged during the autumn phase, with BD and HC participants showing opposing concentration shifts around the autumn equinox, forming an inverse (mirror-like) temporal pattern (Fig. 1). In addition, 323 metabolites demonstrated significant time effects across all participants (Table S8), with 322 remaining significant following FDR correction. Group main effects were identified for 39 metabolites (p < 0.05, uncorrected), although none retained significance after multiple testing adjustment (Table S9).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of spring and autumn on metabolite alterations of BD and HCs, using the target metabolomics technique. In autumn, temporal changes in specific metabolites were detected across both groups, including Cer, DG, TG, Hexosylceramides and amino acids, with BD exhibiting more pronounced metabolic alterations. From spring to autumn, significant shifts in metabolite levels occurred in both groups, accompanied by clear group-by-time interaction effects. The levels of TG metabolites were higher in BD compared to HCs, and the two groups exhibited contrasting trends in autumn. Amino acid metabolites displayed a similar pattern of decline and recovery across both groups, with four amino acids-Gln, Lys, His, and ADMA-exhibiting a continuous increase over time. In BD, these seasonal metabolic changes were closely linked to pathways involving neurotransmitter precursors such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan. Our study suggests that seasonal variations in spring and autumn significantly affect lipid metabolism and amino acid levels in patients with BD, highlighting the seasonal influence on metabolic regulation.

During autumn, the expression of TG metabolites in the HCs group exhibited a pattern resembling a checkmark shape, with a decrease followed by a subsequent increase. Blood samples were collected at three distinct time points (15 days before, on, and after the autumn equinox), which is consistent with the rate of solar radiation variation. This timing suggests that human biological rhythms may align with natural environmental cycles [19]. In contrast, BD patients displayed a notably different pattern at the autumn equinox. This suggests that BD may experience different metabolic shifts during the autumn, potentially contributing to increased vulnerability to depressive symptoms during this season [20, 21].

TG metabolite analysis also revealed a cyclical pattern in HCs, with lower concentrations in spring and higher in autumn. Seasonal variations in light duration and intensity modulate the body’s biological clock, which in turn affect lipid metabolism and brown adipose tissue function in response to reduced light exposure and lower temperatures [22, 23]. Sixteen TG metabolites exhibited significant group-by-time interaction effects, highlighting divergent seasonal regulation between BD and HC groups. While TG levels were similar in spring, but responses differed markedly in autumn, particularly around the autumn equinox (September 22), a timepoint characterized by equal daylight and darkness. Such findings may indicate an impaired ability among BD patients to synchronize lipid metabolism with seasonal changes. It is noted that patients with BD exhibited consistently higher levels of TG concentrations. All BD patients were maintained on stable mood stabilizer or antipsychotic regimens, which are known to influence TG levels [24]. However, the divergent TG responses observed at the autumn equinox suggest that lipid dysregulation in BD stems from both medication effects and intrinsic deficits in physiological adaptation to season changes.

The combined spring-autumn Analysis identified 236 metabolites with significant time effects shared between both groups, indicating the presence of shared seasonal regulatory mechanisms. However, An additional 37 metabolites in HCs And 23 in BD patients showed time-dependent changes that were not observed in the other group. Among the BD-specific metabolites, SM 20:2, DG 14:0_18:2, And DG 16:0_18:1 are notable members of lipid subclasses implicated in membrane structure, energy metabolism, and intracellular signaling, which may reflect dysregulated seasonal rhythmic control of lipid metabolism of BD [25, 26]. Aside from these, most of the remaining metabolites belonged to common lipid subclasses, such as TG, DG, CE, Cer, and PC, but differed in their chain length and degree of unsaturation. These findings imply that even within structurally related lipid classes, subtle compositional differences may contribute to divergent patterns of seasonal regulation across groups, warranting further investigation into their pathophysiological relevance [27].

Amino acid levels are typically elevated in autumn compared to spring, indicating that season variations and light intensity may influence amino acid synthesis and degradation [28–30]. While many amino acids exhibited a decline followed by a rebound, four amino acids, including ADMA, Gln, Lys, and His showed a consistent upward trend across all time points in both groups. Among them, ADMA, Gln, and Lys showed significant time effects in both groups, while His exhibited time dependence only in the HCs group. In addition, eight amino acids (e.g., Alanine, Betaine, Kynurenine, and His) showed time effects exclusively in HCs, while only serine and trans-4-Hydroxyproline (t4-OH-Pro) were BD-specific (Fig. 2). These amino acids are involved in several neurophysiological processes. His is a precursor of histamine [31], plays a key role in mood regulation, cognition, attention, and the sleep-wake cycle [32, 33], and is likely implicated in the pathophysiology of major depressive disorder [34, 35]. Glutamate, produced by neurons and glial cells, influences neurotransmitter synthesis and release [36], with disturbances in its metabolism observed in BD [37]. Lithium has been shown to increase glutamate levels, potentially aiding in the management of BD [38, 39]. ADMA, a product of arginine methylation, is elevated in BD and affects cognitive function through acetylcholine, influencing cerebral blood flow and frontal lobe metabolism [40–42]. Lys is an essential amino acid crucial for metabolism and immune function [43], and its relationship with BD warrants further exploration.

The pathway enrichment analysis revealed that individuals with BD showed specific enrichment in the biosynthesis and metabolism of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan. These amino acids are essential precursors for neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, which play crucial roles in regulating mood. Disruption in amino acid transport may impair the synthesis and signaling of these neurotransmitter systems and contribute to mood disorders [44, 45], potentially through mechanisms involving the kynurenine pathway [46]. Previous studies have indicated that levels of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan tend to rise during manic episodes and decrease during depressive states in BD patients [37, 47–49]. In this study, participants with BD were clinically stable and maintained on long-term treatment with mood stabilizers such as lithium or valproate. Animal studies suggest that these medications can influence the metabolism of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan [50]. The medication-induced effect on amino acid metabolism may help explain the metabolic disparities observed in BD that warrants further investigation.

This study was an exploratory study with several limitations. First, we utilized metabolomics techniques, which had a relatively limited range of detection methods. A more comprehensive understanding of seasonal onset in BD could benefit from integrating proteomics and genomics for a more holistic analysis. Second, the study focused on two distinct time points: spring and autumn. This finding partially indicates the seasonal occurrence of BD; however, the limited data availability from summer and winter undermines the adequacy of the research time points for all four seasons. Future research should investigate the complex interaction between amino acid metabolism and BD across the seasons. Moreover, it is essential to investigate the broader relationship between environmental factors, specifically climate and light, and the seasonal occurrences of BD, as well as amino acid metabolism.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study investigated the temporal variations of metabolites during spring and autumn in BD and HCs. The findings indicate that seasonal variations may affect TG metabolism and amino acid levels in patients with BD, which may offer new perspectives for exploring the impact of seasonal variations on the mood swings of BD.
