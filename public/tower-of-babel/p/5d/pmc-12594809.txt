Application of salicylic acid and silicon can enhance drought stress tolerance in Scrophularia striata L.
Authors: Fariba Shohani, Arash Fazeli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Water deficit is a major environmental stress significantly affecting plant growth and productivity. This research examined the molecular and biochemical responses of Scrophularia striata, a traditional Iranian medicinal plant, to drought stress. It also evaluated whether the separate and combined application of salicylic acid (SA) and silicon (Si) could alleviate these negative effects. The present study was conducted in a greenhouse with a four-factor factorial completely randomized design (FCRD) experimental design with three replications. The treatments included combinations of drought stress level 50% of field capacity (FC50%), SA at two levels (0 and 100 mg L− 1), as well as Si at two levels (0 and 1 g L− 1). The evaluated biochemical parameters included β-carotene, α-tocopherol, and beta-amyrin (βA) content, as well as changes in the expression of genes involved in the terpenoid pathway. The mean comparison revealed that drought stress lowered the levels of β-carotene, α-tocopherol, and βA. This suggests that the adaptive metabolites changed in response to adverse environmental conditions. It reduced the expression of biosynthesis genes such as geranyl diphosphate synthas (GPPS), isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase (IPPI), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGR), farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase (FPS), and beta-amyrin synthase (βAS). The use of SA heightened the expression of the isopentyl diphosphate isomerase gene. When salicylic acid (SA) and Si were employed together, they elevated the levels of β-carotene, α-tocopherol, and βA under FC50% drought stress conditions. The findings provide important insights into the transcriptional and metabolic reprograming of S.striata under drought stress. These results have significant implications for the development of drought-resilient of medicinal plants increasingly confronting climate changes stresses.

Introduction

There are roughly 220 genera and 3000 species in the Scrophulariaceae herbaceous family1. It may be found in many places, including the deciduous woods of North America, Asia, and central Europe2. Scrophularia is one of the most significant genera in this family, with over 300 species2. Meanwhile, S. striata is a blooming plant that grows all year around3. S. striata has been shown in several studies to have qualities that fight cancer, germs, fungi, and depression4. So far, phytochemical investigations have indicated that this plant contains cinnamic acid and flavonoids such quercetin, isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside, and napitrinand5. Drought is an abiotic factor which may hurt the growth and quality of both medicinal and aromatic plants. It does this by lowering photosynthesis, which in turn slows down plant growth and development6 .Terpenoids, which include monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, diterpenes, and polyterpenes, are highly important secondary metabolites made by plants. These chemicals are some of the signaling molecules in plants, and they frequently initiate stress responses7. So far, two separate biosynthesis routes have been found for terpenoids that have been found in higher plants (Fig. 1). One of these enzymes, HMGR, mediates the conversion of HMG-CoA to mevalonate in cells and controls various processes in the mevalonate metabolic pathway. Another enzyme, isopentyl diphosphate, is also a significant factor which significantly affects the direction of intermediates in the chloroplast MEP pathway towards dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP). In addition, the essential gene geranyl diphosphate synthase is involved in monoterpenoid biosynthesis and produces monoterpene precursors by combining two 5-carbon molecules8. The FPS gene is also another significant enzyme in the terpene metabolism pathway and plays a role in the production of farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP)9. These enzymes collectively coordinate complex processes enabling plants to adapt to challenging environmental conditions and produce valuable secondary metabolites10.

Beta-amyrin synthase (βAS) is the primary enzyme responsible for the production of βA (Fig. 2). It also serves as the precursor for oleanolic acid and ursolic10 acid, both being secondary metabolites found in plants supporting their response to drought stress11. Some compounds such as SA and Si have been studied to boost the production of secondary metabolites12. Chemically, SA is a 2-hydroxybenzoic acid13, (Fig. 3) with a phenolic segment14. It is often present in plants whose modest amount does not affect the plant itself15. It serves a crucial function in plant development, such as seed germination, root growth, flowering induction, and ion absorption in plants16. SA also helps plants deal with biotic and abiotic stressors such heat, heavy metal toxicity, and pathogen invasion13. Many studies have revealed that SA helps plants defend themselves against abiotic stressors17. SA is responsible for turning on genes that are part of the phenylpropanoid pathways18. Another study found that use of SA made the phenyl ammonia lyase (PAL) gene more active in Salvia officinalis and crocus sativus plants that were under stress19. Under drought stress, SA controlled how the OsDREB2A, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK10, and OsMYB2 genes were expressed15.

Although Si is not an essential element, it is an important element since it plays a useful role in many metabolic and physiological processes in plants. The amount of Si that builds up in plants varies considerably from one species to another20. It is vital for protecting plants and enhance productivity, especially under difficult situations. Si strengthens the cell wall and changes the manner of gene expression when there is drought21. The use of Si promoted the expression of genes including TaCHS, TaPAL, TaDFR, TaF3H, TaCI, and TaANS, which are all involved in making flavonoids22. It has also been stated that Si treatment, through enhancing the expression of genes that code for antioxidant enzymes such as TaSOD and TaCAT, helped plants under drought stress not to build up too much hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)22.

According to research, use of Si led the plant to create stress-responsive genes more via controlling transcription factors including OsNAC5 and OsDREB2A23. Drought, which is caused by a complex interaction of physical and environmental factors, has profound and widespread effects on both crop productivity and availability of essential food resources24. When exposed to drought stress, S.striata, as with many other crops, undergo complex adaptations significantly affecting the production of their secondary metabolites. These metabolites include a wide range of phenolic compounds and flavonoids. These metabolites, with their diverse properties and applications, not only have medicinal properties but also contribute to the plant’s adaptation to abiotic stresses. Approximately 30% of Iran’s lands consist of arid and semi-arid regions. Cultivation of medical herbs is common in these challenging environments due to low water demand, high economic value, and compatibility with the climatic conditions8. This study explores the effect of drought stress on the expression of selected genes and metabolic shifts in Scrophularia striata, with a focus on the potential of SA and Si to mitigate the adverse effects of water deficit. The following hypotheses are proposed: both SA and Si are expected to alleviate drought stress by modulating molecular and metabolic responses; meanwhile, their combined application may produce more pronounced beneficial effects than when applied individually.

Results

Effect of drought stress on β-carotene and α-tocopherol content

The analysis of variance indicated that drought stress and ecotype had a major influence on the amount of β-carotene in the shoot (P < 0.01). The triple interaction effects on the amount of β-carotene in the shoot were also significant (P < 0.01). However, the quadruple interaction effects of ecotype× stress×SA×Si were not significant (Table 1). The mean comparison revealed that in the Ilam ecotype, control × Si treatment had the lowest β-carotene in the shoot, while in the Abdanan ecotype, FC50% SA×Si treatment had the largest content. Drought stress and ecotype also had a big effect on the amount of α-Tocopherol in the shoot (P < 0.01). The triple interaction effects of ecotype×SA× Si and stress× SA×Si had also a significant effect on the amount of alpha-tocopherol at the 0.01 level of probability. However, the quadruple interaction effects of ecotype× stress × SA× Si did not show any meaningful impact. The mean comparison demonstrated that the control treatment Abdanan ecotype and the control treatment Abdanan ecotype× Si had the lowest amount of beta-carotene in the shoots, though these effects were not significant. The Ilam ecotype × FC50% SA× Si treatment showed the highest beta-carotene content (Figs. 4, 8).

Effects of drought stress on the βA content in the aerial parts

One biological replicate of beta-amyrin content was analyzed using HPLC (Fig. 5). The examination of βA content revealed that the control treatment Abdanan ecotype had the lowest βA, whereas the Ilam ecotype × FC50% Si × SA had the largest content. It was also discovered that use of SA and Si at the same time in the Ilam ecotype of the stress treatment elevated the βA content by 264.4% compared to the Abdanan ecotype of the control treatment (Fig. 6).

Expression profiles of IPPI, HMGR, GPPS, FPS and βAS genes in S. striata in aerial organs

The analysis of variance indicated that drought stress and ecotype had a huge influence on the expression of IPPI, HMGR, GPPS, FPS, and βAS genes at the P < 0.01 level. The four-way interaction effects had also a heavy impact on the expression of the IPPI, HMGR, GPPS, FPS, and βAS genes (P < 0.01). According to the mean comparison, the βAS gene was far less active in the shoots of S. striata when they were under drought stress. However, it was far more active when they were not stressed and in the Ilam ecotype of the control ×SA×Si treatment (Figs. 7A and 8). The findings indicated that when SA was applied to the shoots of the Abdanan ecotype in non-stressed settings, GPPS expression grew considerably compared to when it was applied in stressed conditions. However, the FC50% × Si treatment generated a sharp drop in the transcript levels of this gene in the shoots of the Ilam ecotype when they were under stress (Figs. 7B and 8). Under drought stress, the amounts of FPS transcripts did not vary much compared to the control plants. In the Abdanan ecotype, on the other hand, the FC50% × Si × SA treatment made the FPS gene in the shoots more active when they were under drought stress (Figs. 7C and 8). Drought stress also made the IPPI gene less active, though this alteration was not considerable. In the Ilam ecotype, on the other hand, the FC50% × SA treatment made the IPPI gene far more active while it was under stress (Figs. 7D and 8). The HMGR gene expression pattern demonstrated that the FC50% × Si treatment dramatically lowered the expression of the HMGR gene in the shoots of the flowering plant in the Ilam ecotype. However, the FC50% treatment, which employed SA and Si at the same time in the Abdanan ecotype, induced a major increment in the expression of the HMGR gene when the plants were under stress. (Figures 7E and 8).

Correlation between gene expression levels and secondary metabolites

Table 2 reports the results of the correlation between the respective metabolites, providing valuable insight into the interrelationship between genes and the level of metabolites. The results indicate that the GPPS gene has a positive and significant correlation with the βAS gene at a probability level of P ≤ 0.05, as well as with the FPS and HMGR genes at a probability level of P ≤ 0.01. Similarly, the FPS gene has also a positive and significant correlation with the βAS gene at a probability level of P ≤ 0.05, along with the GPPS and HMGR genes at a probability level of P ≤ 0.01. Additionally, the HMGR gene has a positive and significant correlation with both the GPPS and FPS genes at a probability level of P ≤ 0.01. Interestingly, the HMGR gene has the highest positive correlation with the GPPS and FPS genes. This could be due to the key role of HMGR in mevalonate biosynthesis, which may have a significant influence on most genes. As reported, the Pearson correlation was employed to test the relationship between gene expression and metabolite levels. However, the results revealed no significant correlation between the two variables. This suggests that, statistically, there is no relationship between gene expression and secondary metabolite content. Note that this may not fully and accurately reflect the results, as the genes being studied may be regulated at the post-transcriptional level. This could potentially indicate a post-transcriptional regulatory role in the lack of correlation between transcription levels and enzymatic activity of the genes under study.

Discussion

Effects of drought stress on β-carotene and alpha-tocopherol content

In this study, the application of salicylic acid and silicon elevated the levels of beta-carotene and alpha-tocopherol in the Abdanan and Ilam treatments, respectively, under drought stress. As a possible explanation, the plant’s environment, influenced by climate change, can impact the production of secondary metabolites in the plant. In line with with the current study, A study found that the environment affects the type and quantity of chemical reactions by influencing the production of metabolites as well as factors involved in the production process, such as enzymes25. β-carotene may assist elimination of free radicals and singlet oxygen. Several studies have indicated that plants that are stressed in their environment may lessen the negative effects of stress by augmenting the levels of β-carotene26. Researchers believe that α-tocopherol is an essential antioxidant polyunsaturated fatty acid protecting the plant against photooxidation by stopping and removing ROS as well as alkyl peroxy radicals which are produced during photosynthesis27. SA and Si stimulate the synthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites in plants. These metabolites play a key role in scavenging free radicals in plants under drought stress28.

According to other reports, the application of SA and Si, by boosting the activity of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants and reducing oxidative parameters such as H2O2 and malondialdehyde (MDA) and increased the relative water content and root-to-shoot ratio of S. striata under drought stress conditions12,28.

Isoprenoid synthesis pathway in S. striata under drought stress

Secondary metabolites, such alkaloids, terpenoids, phenols, and flavonoids, are produced when plants are stressed out by their surroundings. These chemicals are necessary for survival of the plant29. Through examining transcriptional data from medicinal plants, several functional genes that are involved in the production of secondary metabolites have been predicted and tested. The MVA route in the cytosol or plastid was utilized to find the primary pathways for terpenoid biosynthesis in plants in this work30. So far, the important genes associated with the synthesis and regulation of physiologically active chemicals in blooming plants while being under drought stress have not been identified accurately. In this work, the βA content in the FC50%×SA×Si treatment ecotype of Abdanan was higher than in the control treatment. In the SA×Si control treatment ecotype, the activity of the βAS gene was greater than in the drought stress treatment. The βAS gene is the precursor to βA synthesis.

One reason for the reduction in expression of this gene could be the regulation of the βA synthesis pathway, which may redirect resources to other important processes8,31For instance, since βA is the first step in the synthesis of oleanolic and ursolic acids, these acids are likely to help plants under drought stress by conferring antioxidant properties32. According to a study, the βAS gene in Glycyrrhiza glabra was less expressed when the plant was under drought stress33, which is in accordance with what the present research found. Drought stress caused the HMGR gene transcription to diminish in this research. HMGR is an important enzyme in the MVA pathway helping convert acetyl coenzyme A and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A into MVA34. This result agrees with another research, stating the expression of the HMGR gene in Glycyrrhiza glabra was lower when the plant was under drought stress35. Also, the expression of this gene was far higher in the Abdanan ecotype of the ×SA×Si treatment when there was drought stress than in the control treatment. The use of SA and Si probably increases the expression of HMGR and the accumulation of secondary metabolites, including beta-carotene, under drought stress conditions by regulating the expression of genes involved in the isoprenoid pathway36,37 .

In summary, carotenoids contribute to a multifaceted role in plant drought stress, influencing antioxidant activity, root development, and root exudate metabolomes. Their contribution to plant resilience under drought conditions makes them a significant focus of research in understanding and mitigating the impacts of drought on plants38. In accordance with the current study,39 and36 have shown that the expression of the HMGR gene grows in Ziziphus jujuba and Glycyrrhiza glabra plants when subjected to drought stress. This finding is in line with the results of the present study. In this study, the use of SA boosted the expression of the IPPI gene in the Ilam ecotype. This may be due to demand for the production of monoterpene chemicals. For example, IPPI plays a key role in regulating the biosynthesis of terpenoids, including beta-carotene, through reversible conversion of IPP and DMADP, two general C5 units derived from the plastidic MEP and cytoplasmic MVA pathways40. It is known that the biosynthesis of specialized plant metabolites, such as isoprenoids, is transcriptionally regulated. SA may promote beta-carotene accumulation by regulating genes involved in the isoprenoid pathway23. Concordant with the current study, they reported that this type of regeneration may be a sign of how plants are recovering, as they need to overcompensate to bring their metabolism back into balance41. This might be due to issues such as short-term stress alleviation, activation of cellular repair systems, or the plant’s tendency towards boosting its defensive system by encouraging creation of monoterpenes42.

In this study, the GPPS gene, responsible for producing the GPP building block for terpenes, demonstrated a significant decline in expression under drought stress conditions when the Ilam ecotype was treated with SA. This may indicate that terpenoid production plays a key role in the response of plants to drought stress43. Reference44 reported that GPPS gene expression in Salvia officinalis was reduced under drought stress, suggesting that lowered GPPS expression is likely a common response to drought and is in line with the results of this study. Researchers have found that plants make more GPP-derived chemicals when they are under drought stress. The FPS gene, a critical enzyme in the mevalonate pathway responsible for the production of FPP, can be regarded as a key gene in the metabolism of terpenes in plantsy45. In this research, the expression of the FPS gene diminished when the plants were under drought stress compared to the control group. Gene expression depends on several factors, such as the plant species and the amount of stress it experiences. Further, the duration of stress and even the severity of stress have significant effects on the rate and pattern of changes in gene expression46. Lower gene expression is likely to affect the production of primary and secondary metabolites8,47However, the application of SA and Si enhanced the expression of the FPS gene in the Ilam ecotype under stress treatment. FPP is a key step in the isoprenoid pathway and is employed to make phytosterols48. Sterols are essential parts of membrane lipid bilayers and are involved in many plant functions, such as regulating growth and development as well as helping plants deal with stress. One of the most important jobs of sterols in plant cells is to keep the membranes stable. It is reasonable to think that sterols may play a major role in how plants respond to stress as they are important for sensing abiotic environmental factors in plant cell membranes. The expression of biosynthetic genes under drought stress often indicates reduced patterns. In accordance with this study findings,49 suggested that the difference between HDR mRNA and protein levels in different organs may be due to post-transcriptional regulation and owing to the fact that HDR expression is more dependent on the growth stage. Another point that may not fully and correctly represent the results is the fact that the studied genes are regulated at the post-transcriptional level. Thus, the over- and under-expression of these genes cannot be definitively attributed to the variability in metabolite levels. In a study on three basil cultivars, researchers found a weak correlation between the transcription level and the enzymatic activities of the TPS, FDS, and GDS genes, which could reflect the role of post-transcriptional regulation.

Conclusions

Environmental stressors, particularly drought, present significant challenges to plant survival, growth, and productivity. As immobile organisms, plants need to develop molecular and biochemical strategies to adapt to these adverse conditions. In this study, salicylic acid (SA) and silicon (Si) were applied separately and simultaneously on the two S. striata ecotypes under control and drought stress conditions. Our findings clearly demonstrated that drought stress significantly impaired gene expression and secondary metabolite contents in S. striata. The application of SA and Si improved adaptation under drought stress conditions in both the ecotypes through enhancing metabolite content and expression of some genes’ accumulation. Interestingly, the simultaneous application of SA and Si had a greater incremental effect on the metabolite content and expression of some genes under drought stress in both ecotypes. The results of this research revealed that SA and Si play a key role in mitigating the negative effects of drought stress in S. striata. The mechanism of action of SA and Si in stress relief can help biotechnology researchers in breeding drought-resistant medicinal plants. In this study, certain technologies were not available. Nevertheless, future research on mitigating drought stress should incorporate molecular and genomic perspectives, with a particular emphasis on utilizing advanced omics technologies such as metabolomics, proteomics, genomics, transcriptomics, and phenomics. These approaches can offer valuable insights into the responses of S. strita to stressful conditions.

Materials and methods

Seed preparation

Seeds of the S. striata plant were collected from two distinct regions in Ilam, Iran. The first region, characterized by a temperate mountainous climate, is located at an altitude of 1427 meters with an average temperature of 18.1°C. Its coordinates are 23°21’30” N latitude and 46°51’19” E longitude. The second collection site, Abdanan in southern Ilam, has a hot as well as arid climate and is situated at an altitude of 880 meters above sea level with an average temperature of 24.1°C. Its coordinates are 47°25’ N latitude and 32°59’ E longitude. Both regions are located in the Zagros Mountains in western Iran, with the seeds collected in January 2020. The botanical characteristics of the studied samples were confirmed based on Flora Iranica50. Seeds of the two ecotypes have been deposited in the Gene Bank of Ilam University under the accession number IUGB02076, and are available for use by all researchers. Prior to germination, the seeds underwent a thorough cleansing process involving a one-hour immersion in water, followed by a brief 30-second exposure to a 10% sodium hypochlorite solution. Next, the seeds were subjected to multiple rinses with distilled water. To overcome dormancy, they were treated with 400 mg L⁻¹ of gibberellic acid for 48 h at 24 °C, facilitating the subsequent germination process12.

Plant growth condition

After 48 h, germinated seeds were placed in Petri dishes containing moist filter paper in a Germinator model (KBWF 240, Germany) at 25 °C with the illuminance of 4000 lx, 16 hday length and a relative humidity of 90%., the germinated seeds were relocated to Petri dishes containing moist filter paper within the same germinator. The germinated seedlings were transferred into pots measuring 12 cm in height and 18 cm in diameter. The pots were filled with a soil mixture of soil, animal manure, and clay in a ratio of 2: 1: 1. The mixture had a measured electrical conductivity of 1.3 EC (µmol/cm), a relative humidity of 28%, and a pH of 7.4. These pots were placed in a greenhouse with controlled conditions, including a 16/8-hour light/dark cycle (220 µmol of photons m⁻² s⁻¹), and day/night temperatures of 25/30°C. The relative humidity levels were maintained within 50–70%51.

Treatments and experimental design

To examine the plant response to drought stress under Si and SA treatment, an experiment was undertaken in a factorial design based on a completely randomized design with three replications in the greenhouse of the Faculty of Agriculture, Ilam University, along the years 2020–2021. After establishing the S. striata plants and 127 days under uniform environmental conditions, when the seedlings reached the four true leaf stage, three pots were randomly assigned for each treatment. Based on previous studies indicating that SA and Si could play a role in improving drought stress tolerance, optimal concentrations for SA (0 and 100 mg L⁻¹) and Si at two levels (0 and 1 g L− 1) were carefully chosen49,52. They were applied as a foliar spray in the early morning every four days, ensuring that the aerial parts of the plants would be fully covered. This process was repeated eight times over four weeks. Forty-eight hours after applying SA and Si treatments, drought stress was applied at control levels and 50% of field capacity for 50 days. Field capacity was measured using the weighing method. Initially, the soil was saturated in the pots. Following drainage, the pot soil sample was covered with aluminum foil and placed at laboratory temperature for 24 h. Next, 2 g of wet soil weight (FSW) was immediately weighed (wet weight), then transferred to an oven (Schutzart, GERMANY Co) for 24 h at 105 °C to obtain the dry soil weight (DSW). The soil moisture percentage at field capacity was calculated using the following formula (1)53. The field capacity was equivalent to 28%; based on the weight of the pots (2.312 kg), the water consumption required for 100% and 50% field capacity was determined to be 526 and 263 mL, respectively. Based on the initial weight of the pots, the drought stress levels were calculated to be 3.326 and 3.063 g, respectively. Daily irrigation was performed, and the amount of water lost through evaporation and transpiration was compensated. Finally, 177 days after planting, sampling was conducted between 11:00 AM and 2:00 PM. The plants were harvested above the soil surface after 198 days, between 8:00 AM and 11:00 AM.

Beta-amyrin (βA) extraction

The plant’s aerial parts were dried at room temperature without light and in the shade. They were then ground into a fine powder and stored at room temperature (28 ± 2 °C). In this case, 500 mg of the powdered material was put into a 100 mL conical flask with a cover, after which 20 mL of n-propanol was added. Next, the solution was put on a conical shaker which shook it at 80 revolutions per minute. A filter paper (Whatman No. 41) was applied to filter the contents of each flask and then one biological replicate was used for the assay. Before being re-analyzed, the sample solutions were filtered through filter paper again. The mobile phase utilized in this investigation was acetonitrile and water in a 95:5 ratio. Thereafter, the mobile phase was put in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min to remove gas. A Unicam Crystal-200 device with a binary gradient pump was employed for chromatographic separation. HPLC (Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, USA) was utilized to find out the beta-amyrin content. The Zorbax RP C8 column (5 μm × 150 mm × 4.6 mm) was the stationary phase, while acetonitrile plus distilled water constituted the mobile phase in a volume ratio of 95:5, which was employed for analysis. The system operated at a flow rate of 0.7 ml/min for 12.4 min. A 10 µl sample was injected into the chromatographic system and analyzed at a wavelength of 210 nm in units of (µg/g)54.

α-Tocopherol assay

A volume of hexane solvent (1 mL/g) was added to the dry material that had been ground up. After being mixed together, it was put into polypropylene tubes and shaken for 1 h at room temperature. The mixture was spun at 10,000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was placed inside fresh tubes, mixed with more solvent, and then centrifuged again. The supernatant was kept at 4 °C. Finally, 0.1 mL of the hexane extract was blended with 1 mL of reagent solution (0.25 mmol phosphomolybdenum) and put on a shaker at 37 °C for 90 min. Ultimately, a spectrophotometer (Shimaduz 1900i, Japan) was utilized to quantify absorbance at a wavelength of 695 nm in units of (µg/g)55.

Measurement of β-carotene

For the extraction, 1 g of the dry material was mixed with 5 ml of cold acetone and held at 4 ± 1 °C for 15 min, while being stirred intermittently. Thereafter, the solution was centrifuged at 1370 g for 10 min. The supernatant was place inside another test tube, and was then extracted again with 5 ml of acetone and centrifuged again, just like previously. Whatman filter paper (42) was employed to mix the two supernatants. A spectrophotometer (Shimaduz 1900i, Japan) measured the extract’s absorbance at a wavelength of 449 nm in units of (µg/g)56.

RNA and cDNA Preparation

RNA extraction was performed based on the instructions57 with some modifcations35 (Fig. 9). In order to ascertain the quality of RNA, 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis was used. Gel photography was performed by Gel Doc™ XR + device from BIORAD model and saved using Image Lab™ software. Next, the amount of RNA was measured using a Thermo Fisher Scientific NanoDrop device (USA) by finding the 260/280 and 230/260 ratios. Synthesis of cDNA was performed using a kit (Sambio South Korea).

Isolation of nucleotide sequences of IPPI, HMGR, GPPS, FPS, and βAS genes

Degenerate primers are highly useful and work well for finding and isolating unknown gene sequences in molecular biology investigations. Degenerate primers are used when no genomic sequence of the plant under study is available. To create degenerate primers for the IPPI, HMGR, GPPS, FPS, and βAS genes which are involved in the βA biosynthesis pathway in S.striata, gene sequences were used from plants such as Osmanthus fragrans (accession code KY992862.1), Olea europaea (accession code XM023036651.1), Sesamum indicum (accession code XM011098260.2), and Cimum basilicum (accession code JQ809437.1). The gene sequences of these plants were acquired from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) first. Using the Bioedite Sequence Alignment editor software, the sequences were initially lined up whereby the sections that were the same were found. Next, the site http://biotools.nubic.northwestern.edu/OligoCalc.html was used to inspect a pair of particular primers from the conserved areas with the most nucleotide similarity across the samples under study. To obtain the reverse complementary sequence, the reverse primer was also made from the site (http://www.bioinformatics.org/sms/rev_comp.html) (Fig. 10). Depicts a diagram of the conserved areas that were created for the βAS gene. Figure 10 displays the main features and specifications of the primers for the βAS gene, such as temperature, length, G + C content, and primer dimer. This information may be found online at http://biotools.nubic.northwestern.edu/OligoCalc.html. Finally, the primers were made with the help of Sambio Korea and Bio Magic Company, which were made using a high purity salt-free method called lyophilization (Tables 3 and 4).

It has been stated that in the βAS biosynthesis pathway in plants, some plant species do not have the sequences of these genes. Instead, they took a number of important genes from different families whose sequences were available in scientific databases and made degenerate primers. The AUPAC system (http://www.bioinformatics.org/sms/rev_comp.html) is employed to name defective nucleotides. In this study, PCR was done on each gene in a final reaction volume of 20 µl. This included 10 µl of PCR buffer (10X) and 1 µl of each of the forward and reverse primers: 1 µl of cDNA that has not been diluted and 7 µl of double-distilled water. A Bio-Rad machine (USA) was utilized to conduct PCR. The program started with 300 s of initial denaturation at 94 °C, followed by 35 cycles. Each cycle included 30 s of denaturation at 94 °C, 45 s of annealing of each primer at the recommended temperature for primer binding, 2 min of extension and amplification at 72 °C, and finally 5 min of final extension at 72 °C. Note that a no-template control (NTC) or negative control is employed in PCR studies. We utilized an electrophoresis apparatus (BIO RAD model, USA) and a 1.8% agarose gel to find the product resulting from PCR. In this approach, 3 µl of loading dye was added to 5 µl of the PCR product. The combination was then put in a well in a 1.8% agarose gel with TBE buffer. The research found that the optimal voltage and duration for separating PCR samples were 90 V and 50 min, respectively. After undertaking the methods above to stain the gel, it was placed in an ethidium bromide solution for 30 min. Finally, after rinsing the gel with distilled water for 2 min, a GelDock device (BIO RAD type, made by a US business) was employed to capture a picture of the amplified bands using UV light (Fig. 11). After cleaning the PCR product, it was submitted to Microsciences in Switzerland to be sequenced with the help of Topagen. This was performed to check the correctness and performance of the primers. The sequences acquired for the IPPI gene (accession code PV055686) and the FPS gene (accession code PV055979) were registered at the NCBI sites https://submit.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/subs/?search=SUB15055268 and https://submit.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/subs/?search=SUB15055236.

Gene expression analysis by real-time PCR

A a Micq equipment (Bio Molecular System, Australia) was used to perform Real-Time PCR reactions. Three biological replicates and three technical duplicates were employed, with a final volume of 10 µl, according to the kit and the manufacturer’s instructions (Amplicon Denmark). Preliminary studies were utilized to find the best amounts of cDNA and primers to use when comparing the expression of the βAS, IPPI, GPPS, FPS, and HMGR genes against that of the internal control. Gene expression levels were normalized separately for each ecotype, with expression data normalized for every ecotype using the FC100% control treatment of the same ecotype as a calibrator. This approach would ensure the relative gene expression results accurately reflect the ecological conditions of each ecotype. The best temperature and number of cycles were also found for each gene using a range of cycles and temperatures. Given the large number of target genes, the primer efficiency for each gene was standardized separately through individual experiments alongside the curve. Instead, the efficiency values reported by the device software, which were calculated from control and experimental samples, were used. In addition, the efficiencies of each primer were calculated based on data of treatment and control samples. A total of 40 cycles were chosen as the optimal amplification settings since the PCR product did not achieve a steady state (Fig. 11; Table 4). They show the reaction parts and temperature cycles that are part of the Real-Time PCR procedure. The beta actin gene is a reference gene which not only normalizes the data but also helps fix experimental differences that cannot be avoided. We measured the increase in fluorescence emission that occurred when the dye was bound to Syber Green to perform relative quantification. Following the reactions, each gene’s melting curve was observed, whereby the peaks that were found validated the products’ specificity and lack of primer dimers. The Ct values (threshold cycle) obtained for each gene would be used in the calculations. The threshold cycle (Ct) for a sample is defined by the visible fluorescent signal above the threshold, which is called Ct. For each sample, three biological and three technical replicates were utilized with the method35. We found ΔΔct by looking at the rise in gene based on Relations (2) and (3). SAS version 9.4 was employed to inspect the data along with R version 4.3.2 and GraphPad version 9 to create graphs. To check for genomic contamination in the genomic process, a negative control (NTC) was utilized. In this case, the master mix sample had the primer for the real-time reaction, but the template DNA was not there. The main point of NTC is observe whether a signal forms or not when the target nucleic acid is not present. This control can find contamination as well as any interactions between probes and primers that might spoil the findings.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was confirmed via the Shapiro-Wilk method normality test in SPSS statistical software (SPSS, version 29(. This test is employed to assess the goodness-of-fit of a normal distribution. By analyzing the test’s statistic, one can determine if the data follows a normal distribution or not. The experiment was conducted as four-factor factorial experiment in a completely randomized design (FCRD) with three replications. Data processing was performed using SAS version 9.4 software. In the study, a four-way analysis of variance was performed, taking into account the ecotype factors of SA and Si stress. When the effects were significant (P ≤ 0.05), differences between means were evaluated by applying the least significant difference (LSD) test (P ≤ 0.05). Graphs were drawn using Graph Pad software, version 9.
