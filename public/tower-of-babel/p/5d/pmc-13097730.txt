Co-design and validation of a tool for monitoring and improving the quality of general practice learning environments
Authors: Samia Toukhsati, Belinda O’Sullivan, Tina George, Leila Greenfield, Simon Morgan, Rebecca Kippen, Carla Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous quality improvement of general practice learning environments is critical for training the next generation of general practitioners (GPs). An evidence-based framework (the General Practice Clinical Learning Environment Framework or GPCLE Framework) describes six elements which are important for best practice learning in general practice. However, there is no validated tool to guide the implementation of quality learning. This research aimed to draw from the GPCLE Framework to develop and validate a tool with specific criteria and evidence-based standards, one that is useful and practical for benchmarking and developing higher quality general practice learning environments for general practice registrars.MethodsThis national study applied co-design methods (June 2023-June 2024) engaging various stakeholders, including practice managers, GPs and general practice supervisors (trainers), registrars (trainees), medical educators and training directors, across three stages. In phase 1, the draft tool was developed using a broad literature review, Delphi-style workshop and expert review. In phase 2, a survey involved field testing concepts across the general practice training sector to inform refinements to the criteria and standards. In phase 3, the tool was pilot tested in two general practices, comparing an online self-assessment process with an objective on-site evaluation by the research team, coupled with interview feedback to inform relevance, practicality, and feasibility.ResultsThe final GPCLE tool comprised demographic questions and eleven criteria with associated evidence-based standards, self-assessed as ‘yes/no/unsure/not applicable’ based on the available evidence. These provide a performance rating on a three-point continuum, from satisfactory to commendable and then exemplary. The tool aligned with the original GPCLE Framework. When piloted, it took around 20 min to complete and practices found it relevant, practical, and feasible.ConclusionThe GPCLE tool could be useful for practices to undertake in-practice reviews of the quality of the learning environment for general practice registrars. This could stimulate more specific planning and improvements for practices to strive towards achieving higher performing standards, above and beyond meeting minimum requirements as a training practice. Further, it could also be applied to planning for accreditation, or identifying practices delivering best practice training which can provide exemplars to the sector.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14232-8.Keywords: General practice, Medical education, Quality improvement, Vocational

Introduction

High quality clinical learning environments are the cornerstone of excellence for training the next generation of general practitioners (GPs) [1]. In Australia, GPs complete a formal fellowship of a minimum of two years in general practice settings. However, the nature and quality of their learning environments may be highly variable. The quality of general practice training has major implications for attracting registrars (trainees) who have a positive experience and go on to work as GPs [2]. Validated tools to support quality improvement of general practice learning environments are needed; particularly in countries like Australia where there is a significant projected GP workforce shortage [3].

In 2019, General Practice Supervision Australia (GPSA) sought to address this quality issue by developing a Framework of six elements which should be attended to for best practice Clinical Learning Environments in General Practice (GPCLE) (Fig. 1) [1]. The elements are: (1) the practice values learning; (2) best practice clinical care; (3) a positive learning environment; (4) an effective general practice/training provider relationship; (5) effective communication processes; and (6) appropriate resources and facilities. However, this framework required further development and validation, in order to make it applicable to informing quality improvement in general practice learning settings.

Elements and objectives of the GPCLE Framework

Positive vocational training experiences are associated with better preparedness to practice independently [4] and train others [5]. Many general practices operate as small businesses with a high administrative burden, yet they desire to attract and train new doctors to replenish their workforce and evolve with the contemporary thinking of emerging cohorts [6]. While attracting registrars relies on a practice’s reputation for teaching and learning, there is currently no way to validate higher performance in this area.

In Australia, most general practice registrars are hosted in private training practices, working under the supervision of at least one accredited supervisor (a Fellowed GP who has completed their vocational training and supervision requirements) for approximately two years [7]. The two general practice training colleges in Australia, the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) and the Australian College of Rural and Remote Medicine (ACRRM) specify requirements for accredited general practice vocational training [8, 9] and set the minimum standards for accreditation of general practice training posts. These standards are well suited for integration into a quality framework, such as the GPCLE, aligned with both College's goals to foster a continuous cycle of improvement. The accreditation process is conducted every three years. More regular quality improvement data (at least annually) might benefit practices and registrars if there were tools available to support it.

In Australian general practice training, there are blunt systems such as satisfaction surveys for general practice registrars to provide feedback about the quality of their training posts [10]. Other research describes the features of high quality supervision and depicts feedback systems for improving general practice supervision in rural general practice training [11–13]. However, there is no national or international tools describing the criteria and evidence-based standards for guiding quality of the overall learning environment (not just supervision) for all types of practice settings.

With this background in mind, this research aimed to draw from the GPCLE Framework to develop and validate a tool with criteria and evidence-based standards which is practical and useful for reviewing, planning and developing higher quality general practice clinical learning environments for general practice registrars.

Methods

Co-design methods were applied using a three phase approach based on the social and behavioural change (SBC) flow chart process, comprising the following phases: (1) Define (2) Design & Test (3) Apply [14]. The SBC flow chart draws heavily from the human centred design approach and was used because the focus of this research was around behavioural change in the context of a diversity of general practices and learners. Its key strength is that it actively and meaningfully engages target groups and key stakeholders from the beginning to end of the design process, ensuring genuine co-creation.

In alignment with co-design methods, we engaged a diverse research team of 22 people and a wide range of stakeholders involved in general practice training across Australia, including GPs/general practice supervisors, general practice registrars/new fellows and medical students, international medical graduates, practice managers, regional directors of training at both general practice colleges, general practice training providers, training researchers and medical educators. This range of participants aimed to generate insights from a range of contexts including different types of practices, learners and people with different system responsibilities so the tool would be grounded in practical realities. The governance structure comprised a core Research Team (Working Group), Steering Group (SG), and Expert Advisory Group (EAG), all of whom contributed to the development and refinement of the tool as part of co-design methods, with most members represented in multiple groups. The entire project was conducted under ethical approval with informed consent (see ethics approval and consent to participate section under Declarations). All data collection instruments used in this study were developed for this study.

Phase one - the “define phase”

Data were collected through a broad literature review, pre-workshop activity, and Delphi-style workshop to explore existing knowledge and define the scaffold of a draft GPCLE tool with questions for this process (and all phases of the project), reflected in Table 1. Beginning in June 2023, the research team conducted a broad literature review and mapped the six GPCLE Framework elements (considering the objectives for each element) (Fig. 1) to general practice college accreditation standards to identify any relevant quality indicators for the GPCLE tool.

In August 2023, the research team, SG, and EAG were invited via email (contact details known at project commencement) to complete a qualitative reflection on SurveyMonkey © pre-workshop to garner perspectives about how the GPCLE Framework elements could be measured objectively (such as behaviours or actions that demonstrate quality). Each member was assigned one GPCLE element and asked to reflect on evidence of the element objectives being met, prioritising relevant and practical examples based on experience.

The results of the reflective activity were thematically analysed by the research team using deductive coding (informed by the GPCLE Framework elements) and inductive coding based on emerging concepts in the data, in NVivo 14 © [15]. Objective indicators from phase 1 activities were then collated by the research team, discussed with the SG and checked for concurrence with the GPCLE Framework. This helped to build a scaffold for the GPCLE tool and inform the phase 1 Delphi-style workshop questions.

In September 2023, a face-to-face Delphi-style workshop was held, facilitated by the research team and attended by the EAG (direct invitation to known members) with the aim of developing the GPCLE tool. Among other questions (Table 1), participants were asked to explore objective indicators of quality, prompted by ideas which had emerged from the reflective activity.

After generating objective indicators representing each GPCLE element, the EAG participated in a gallery walk (moving around a room) to review the items and identify exemplars which were collated as draft material on posters at different stations. Following this, facilitated by a member of the core research team, mixed-stakeholder small groups spent two hours organising the quality indicators into themes and arranging them on a performance continuum from lowest to highest, to inform the range of GPCLE items and evidence-based standards.

The emerging GPCLE tool, and any insights about the items, indicators, and standards were then reviewed and refined via a facilitated whole-of-group, consensus-building discussion for two hours. Additional resources were also identified which were not published at the time [16], providing material for further reference when considering the content of the tool. Following the workshop, the research team then conducted two further iterative rounds of EAG review to refine the organisation of the material and prepare a version for the design and test phase (version 1 of the GPCLE tool or V1).

Phase two - the “design and test phase”

In October 2023, the first version of the GPCLE tool (V1), which included items and indicators for rating the quality of the learning environment, on a draft performance continuum from satisfactory to commendable to exemplary, was circulated via an email to the SG and EAG with a link to an online SurveyMonkey © questionnaire. The scale was designed from scratch to represent wording which could readily identify levels of performance for the audience. The survey sought feedback to improve clarity and further develop the performance criteria and evidence-based standards. The findings were summarised using frequency counts and proportions and the research team applied results to inform an updated version two (V2). In December 2023, V2 was circulated for a second round of review by the SG, EAG and six representatives of key stakeholder groups in general practice training including the Australian College of Rural and Remote Medicine, the Australian Medical Association, the Australian Indigenous Doctors Association, Rural Doctors Association of Australia, Indigenous General Practice Registrars Network, and the Remote Vocational Training Scheme.

Round two of the iterative cycle was undertaken via email inviting feedback on an electronic version of the GPCLE tool V2 and the results were descriptively analysed and used by the research team to inform the GPCLE tool version 3 (V3) and adapt it to an online version. An online sector-wide survey was then undertaken in February 2024, whereby the online V3 GPCLE tool was circulated to all those involved in general practice training (including supervisors, practice managers/owners, medical educators, registrars and medical students) through GPSA, General Practice Registrars Australia (GPRA), General Practice Student Network, and the RACGP Directors of Training. Willing participants were asked to provide feedback by early March 2024. This feedback confirmed the V3 tool was ready to pilot.

Phase three - the “apply” phase

In April to June 2024, the V3 tool was field tested as an early pilot of its validity in real-world general practice training practice settings. Participants invited to the phase two online sector-wide survey were able to complete an expression of interest to participate in phase three, noting the intention of the research team was to select field testing sites that had a level of practice diversity. Eligibility included being a current training practice, having a practice liaison person who could invite all staff within the practice, and being willing for all general practice staff to spend time self-completing the GPCLE tool V3 online prior to participating in a daylong site visit by two members of the research team (an academic research expert and a general practice medical educator).

The number of pilot practices was limited by time and budget constraints. The practice liaison was provided emailed materials for the self-assessment and facilitated the completion of pre-visit GPCLE tool V3 by practice staff and registrars (hard copy use). Then the research team visited, blind to the pre-visit assessment, and independently used the GPCLE tool V3 to review the quality of the learning environment and conducted face-to-face interviews with staff/learners to explore the relevance, practicality, and feasibility of the tool. Practice self-evaluation data were then compiled and compared to the review of the research team to assess the validity of the V3 GPCLE tool. The interview schedule was developed by the research team to learn about the practical application of the GPCLE tool. Data from two pilot practices were triangulated with objective assessment data to inform revisions of the final GPCLE tool in June 2024, such as the refinement or addition of any measures.

Participants involved in the study received honorariums commensurate with usual salary to compensate personal / practice time.

Phase one: Pre-workshop reflection and workshop insights

Overall, 16 of 22 phase one participants responded to the pre-workshop reflective activity (73% response rate; 12 women). Qualitative data were analysed using NVivo 14 ©. A combination of deductive and inductive thematic coding revealed a range of indicators and evidence to assess quality, situated within each GPCLE element, providing a scaffold for the tool and shaping the workshop questions.

The Delphi-style workshop produced a large volume of objective, quality indicators for each of the six GPCLE elements (> 250 indicators in total), and whole-of-group discussion identified (i) key indicators and (ii) determined their position along a performance continuum ranging from satisfactory, to commendable to exemplary. Following refinement of the first stage of the tool, informed by workshop data, V1 of the GPCLE tool consisted of 23 items aligned with the six elements of the GPCLE Framework. Each item addressed a specific theme (e.g., teaching time), with a description and associated evidence for each of the three levels of quality (e.g., satisfactory level description: ‘Teaching time is allocated in accordance with College requirements, scheduled and protected’; evidence: in-practice teaching roster).

Phase two: Expert review and wider sector-wide review

The first round of expert review was sent to 20 SG and EAG members, of whom nine provided responses (45%) via an online survey about the GPCLE tool V1. All considered the tool valuable (relevant/compatible) with general practice training and 71% considered it feasible to implement in general practice. Informed by expert feedback, the tool was revised to improve clarity, reduce duplication and maximise brevity, producing the GPCLE tool V2, which comprised 21 items.

A second round of expert review was then initiated; 11 of 20 SG and EAG members (55%) and 2 of 6 key informants (33%) responded by submitting feedback on an electronic version of the GPCLE tool V2. All respondents indicated that the items and indicators were relevant, practical, and feasible to implement in general practice. All considered the tool could be used annually or more often by practice managers, GPs, practice owners, learners and training entities. Feedback was compiled and reviewed by the research team through structured discussions during weekly meetings over six weeks. The researchers aimed to remove or combine similar items and ensure that the core concepts were represented). This informed the reduction of items from 21 to 12 producing the GPCLE tool V3.

A sector-wide review of the GPCLE tool V3 was then initiated, via an anonymous online survey comprising seven demographic items, 12 GPCLE items, and eight implementation items. A total of 229 participants began the survey, of whom 188 (82%) answered at least one GPCLE item. Respondents achieved broad representation of all Australian states and territories and practice roles (including GP/rural generalist (RG) supervisors, GP/RG registrars or new fellows, medical educators, practice managers, and others).

Data analysis identified that the GPCLE items and evidence-based criteria could be effectively organised on a continuum of satisfactory to commendable to exemplary. Suggestions were made for future iterations of the GPCLE tool to include an evaluation of the availability of evidence, not just whether evidence showed the achievement of standards. Further suggestions were made for the data collection to include the respondents’ role in the general practice given different roles have access to different information.

Between 66 and 76% of the sample agreed or strongly agreed that the GPCLE tool is relevant for general practice, practical to use and feasible for self-assessment, with 9 to 10% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing, and 15–24 indicating ambivalence. Over 70% also agreed that the GPCLE tool could be used by anyone in general practice training, prior to or at the commencement of training. Finally, over 90% agreed that it should be used at 6-month (20%), 12-month (44%) or two-year (23%) intervals.

Phase three – pilot test

Four general practice training practices in diverse locations that had submitted an expression of interest in Phase two were invited to participate in the pilot study of the GPCLE tool V3. Two were unavailable to participate within the field-testing timeframe. One metropolitan based practice and one practice in a large rural town (> 15,000 population) participated in field testing, with 15 total participants across the two practices (comprising practice owners, supervisors, practice managers, registrars, and practice nurses). Materials for testing the GPCLE tool were provided to practices in advance.

Participants found the tool was useful for general practice training. Overall, 90% of participants identified that the GPCLE tool is relevant, practical and feasible for self-assessment. The tool took practice staff around 20 min to complete. All (100%) agreed that the GPCLE tool could be used by anyone in general practice training. Most agreed that it should be used at 12 month (67%) or two-year intervals (27%).

Final version

Qualitative interview data provided rich evidence about the context of use, and how the tool aligns with the needs of the practice team and learners, and areas where it could be further refined. Data from the sector-wide review and the field-testing pilot study were triangulated to inform the final version of the tool in June 2024 (GPCLE tool V4). The GPCLE tool comprised seven demographic questions, short instructions, and yes/no/unsure/not applicable answers to the availability of evidence for each of 11 criteria (consolidated based on ongoing refinement by the research team), which aligned with the original GPCLE Framework and evidence-based standards depicting satisfactory to exemplary levels of quality. The choice of simple yes and no type responses was intentional to reduce burden on users and improve actionability compared with a Likert scale.

The final version of the tool and supportive evidence are outlined in the additional file.

Discussion

The GPCLE tool was co-designed with multiple stages of feedback and reflection built into its development, application, and refinement. It is the first tool of its kind to comprehensively measure general practice clinical learning environment quality, including clear criteria and evidence-based performance standards. Feedback from the general practice training sector indicates that the GPCLE tool could be relevant, practical, feasible, and completed in around 20 min. By being developed by experienced sector-wide stakeholders, the nature of evidence for each standard was shown to be practical for all practice staff to measure and monitor the quality of the general practice clinical learning environment.

The tool could be relatively simple, and with more development could be made available in paper or online formats, for use by practices or wider agencies wanting to explore quality improvement issues. This provides the opportunity to use a standardised GPCLE tool to showcase exemplary standards for the clinical learning environment, whilst understanding any gaps in best practice quality, and working to address these. Its use by practices could be facilitated by the general practice colleges and peak bodies like GPSA to improve engagement around quality improvement and provide more evidence to inform where they can action quality improvement tools, resources and training. This could be especially important for rural practices where having the opportunity to showcase high quality learning environments could help to attract more GP learners and build the workforce.

The GPCLE tool could also be used within quality improvement processes, planning for accreditation and for regular performance monitoring. It provides a more standardised structure for considering actions to produce evidence of different quality standards. Our findings suggest that the GPCLE tool could be useful at approximately 12 monthly intervals for in-practice reviews. Or the interval of use could be contracted for practices which need help or are perceived to have issues with their learning environments and expanded for those with an excellent record of quality training.

This annual cycle could complement and enhance the current training practice accreditation cycle which occurs every three years and focus it above meeting standards, to embed a mindset of continuous quality improvement. More regular quality reviews are likely to identify issues earlier, allowing for responsive action around the quality of training delivery in practices. Wider research suggests quality improvement tools embedded into processes of continuous learning across a sector can be effective mechanisms for improving health systems when coupled with resources and capacity building [17]. The GPCLE tool matches the need to firstly decide how quality is to be defined when considering general practice as a learning system [18].

Further, the GPCLE tool could be helpful for new training practices to prepare when they are accepting general practice registrars for the first time. It could help them to prepare and onboard registrars appropriately and engage in quality improvement cycles as they become used to being training practices. The tool could equally identify high performing ‘exemplary’ practices in certain areas which could be buddied with those who seek to lift their standards around the quality of the training environment. This could work in a networked model across training regions to facilitate wider engagement around quality improvement across training practices. The aim would be to create more-standardised high quality general practice training environments to attract and sustain the next generation of learners. It also ensures practice training settings are accountable and transparent about their training quality based on evidence, rather than the current system which relies on ad hoc information.

The co-design process for the GPCLE tool was mostly focused on general practice registrars. The generalisability of the tool to other learner cohorts is not yet known. Future research is needed to support the tool’s ongoing development and implementation for other learners in general practice such as medical students, prevocational doctors, and other disciplines like practice nurses. The project methods aligned with modern validity frameworks by testing the content validity through the co-design steps including the Delphi-style workshop, response validity through different respondents and types of practices and consequential validity, through exploring the applicability of the findings for benchmarking practices. However, an overarching focus was on the face validity of this tool for a sector which is extremely dispersed and often time poor and under-resourced.

It should also be noted that the field-testing phase for piloting the tool was limited to two practices inclusive of 15 participants. This provides limited information to justify relevance, practicality and feasibility at a level which is generalisable to all practices, learners and roles. Wider demonstration of the application of the tool to different practice settings is needed to further validate the tool and inform further refinements. While the criteria and standards of the tool are simple to use, further research is needed to develop and validate a scoring system that produces a total, albeit weighted, score. The absence of a total score was intentional within this research to avoid false precision and further research is recommended in this area. This would facilitate usability and provide a means by which practices could showcase an overall quality guarantee not limited to one criterion.

Conclusion

Developing the GPCLE tool through co-design involved broad iterative engagement and development cycles to achieve a relevant, practical and feasible tool for self-assessing the quality of general practice learning environments for GP registrars. With 11 items, three performance standards and evidence-based criteria, the tool is estimated to take only 20 min to complete, yet it has the potential to provide rich information for reflection and setting an agenda for ongoing quality improvement of the general practice learning environment. The GPCLE tool could be more widely used for practices to showcase their quality for learning and for the general practice sector to engage and support quality improvement of accredited training posts for general practice registrars.
