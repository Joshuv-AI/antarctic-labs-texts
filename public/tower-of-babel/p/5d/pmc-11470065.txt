PVC-based mixed-matrix membranes based on IL@AC/NH2-MIL-101 nanocomposites for improved CO2 separation performance
Authors: Narmin Noorani, Abbas Mehrdad, Parastoo Shamszadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mixed matrix membranes (MMMs), an important class of organic-inorganic nanocomposite membranes, were developed to overcome some of the limitations of purely polymeric membranes. In this study to improve the separation performance of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) membranes, mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) were prepared from incorporating choline prolinate based ionic liquid (IL) in a the coke/metal-organic framework (MOF) (NH2-MIL-101(Cr)) as a filler in polyvinyl chloride (PVC), which can be viewed as a potential solution to the trade-off problem with polymeric membranes because of the combination of the processing versatility of polymers and the high gas separation capability. Coke/MOF/PVC and IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMMs with different filler loadings of 5, 10, and 15 wt% were prepared using solution casting method and characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) analyses, and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area test. The porous structure of MMMs nanocomposites causes to which coke/MOF composite effectively accelerate gas diffusion in the PVC matrix. The permeability date was measured at 288.15, 298.15, 308.15 and 318.15 K and pressure up to 4 bar for CO2 and N2. According to the outcome, the addition of the IL([Cho][Pro]) filler, the permeability of the AC/MOF/PVC MMMs is increased compared to the pure PVC membrane. Introduction

The current industrial progress and rapid economic growth have led to a rapid increase in the demand for energy by humans. Fossil fuels are currently the primary source of energy needed by humans, increasing CO2 emissions1. The detection an effective and energy-saving gas separation technology is an efficient method to regulator CO2 emissions2. Gas separation using polymeric membrane has attracted interest due to its energy-saving, in the recent years. However, the main challenging in separation performance by polymeric membrane is the trade-off gas selectivity and permeability3,4. This limitation causes that separation performances of the polymeric membrane’s layout under the Robeson upper bound limit. Mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) are composite membranes made by combining an inorganic or inorganic-organic hybrid material in the form of micro- or nanoparticles (the discrete or dispersed phase; additive or filler) and a polymer matrix, have been attended as a potential material to overcome the polymeric membranes problem5. The surface morphology of filler/polymer is significant challenge that should be considered enduring the design and construction of MMM. MOFs are offered as favorable candidates for developing new MMMs for gas separation6,7.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of porous materials with exceptional adsorption/separation properties. MOFs has gained considerable attention for several applications due to their tunable structure, high specific surface area (SSA), and high pore volume8,9. The creation of additional porosity of MOFs during their synthesis results in the incorporation of specific kinds and concentrations of appropriate substrates. This is advantageous results in more adsorptive sites and improves the adsorption capacity of gas. Activated carbon (AC) is an inert carbon matrix with a relatively large specific surface area that functions as an economical and environmentally beneficial adsorbent10.

AC is extensively used for gas adsorption due to its adjustable pore structure, high surface area, mechanical and chemical stability, and cost-effectiveness11. Zhang et al.12 have investigated the CO2 capture in CMK-3/MIL-101(Cr) composite, which is made by incorporating mesoporous carbon into MOFs. The outcomes showed that the gas adsorption capability is improved by the CMK-3/MIL-101(Cr) composite. Kayal et al.13 have studied incorporations of activated carbon in MIL-101(Cr) MOF composite to storage CH4 and CO2. The results indicate that the synthesized composite adsorbed more CO2 and CH4 compared with the raw MOF. However, it has been shown that the large void space in the MOF matrix does not entirely contribute to the adsorption/separation processes. Incorporating additional porous structures, particularly carbonaceous ones, into the main MOF matrix to create a composite material is one method that can be used to make up for the weak interactions between the guest molecules and the interior pores. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), activated carbon (AC) and graphene oxide (GO) are the main carbon-based materials which have been incorporated in MOF structures14–17.

Ionic liquids are attracted as gas adsorbent because of their no pollution characteristics and selective adsorption capacity18. Nevertheless, low diffusion coefficients, high viscosity, and high cost are the current disadvantages that limits application of ILs in membrane-based gas separation. One of the methods to circumvent these problems is to confine ILs into MOF matrices19,20. Ban et al.21 investigated the CO2/O2 permeability of [Bmim][Tf2N]/ZIF8 composite in polysulfone (PSF). The obtained results indicate that the MMM permeability for CO2 and O2 was incremented by 48% and 39%, respectively. Ma et al.22 have studied mixed matrix membranes made of PIM-1 polymer and TSIL/NH2-MIL101(Cr) composites. When compared to the pure PIM-1 membrane, the CO2/N2 selectivity increased by 119% and the CO2 permeability increased by 23%. Chen et al.23 prepared PIM-based MMMs containing MOF801/IL nanocomposites. They reported that IL/MOF801 composites notably improve the PIM-1 membranes performance. Although the PVC membrane has excellent physical properties, its permeability is disappointing. To improve its permeability, in this research PVC composite including ionic liquid and active carbon was prepared. Cholinium amino acid based on proline ([Cho][Pro]) as filler were impregnated to AC/NH2-MIL-101(Cr) composite using vacuum method. Then MMMs were prepared by addition IL@AC/NH2-MIL-101(Cr) to PVC matrix. MMMs were characterized by FTIR spectroscopy, BET test, SEM combined with EDX, and TGA analysis. Also, the effects of some operating parameters such as temperature and pressure onto the MMMs permeability for CO2 and N2 were studied.

Experimental

Materials

Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) with K-value 69–71, chromic nitrate hydrate (Cr(NO3)3 9H2O), purity > 99% wt%), 2-amino terephthalic acid (NH2-H2BDC, purity > 99% wt%), Choline chloride (purity > 99.0 wt%), L-proline (Pro) (purity > 99% wt%), potassium hydroxide (KOH) (> 95.0% purity) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) (> 37.0% purity), were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich products. N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) (> 99% purity), tetrahydrofurane (THF) (> 99% purity), and Ethanol (> 99% purity) were supplied by Merck. The PC was purchased from Morvarid Petrochemical Company (Assaluyeh, Iran). CO2 gas (> 99% purity) was used in gas absorption tests.

Synthesis

Activation of pyrolytic coke

To activate the pyrolytic coke (PC), at first grinding up the pyrolytic coke (PC) into fine particles and then filtering them through a 270 nm sieve. Next, they mixed the PC with potassium hydroxide (KOH) with a weight ratio of 1:1 was mixed together. This mixture was then heated in a nitrogen atmosphere for 2 h at temperature of 1123 K24. This process “activates” the PC, giving it more surface area and making it more adsorbent. After the heating, pyrolytic coke washed thoroughly with hydrochloric acid and water to remove any leftover KOH or impurities. Finally, they dried the activated PC (APC) in a warm oven for 12 h to prepare it for further use. In order to get rid of any last bits of KOH and possible impurities, the product was washed three times using distilled water and 1 M HCl. After that, the activated pyrolytic coke (APC) was dried for 12 h at 353 K in an air-circulated oven.

Synthesis of AC/MIL-101-NH2 (Cr) composite

A hydrothermal technique was used to synthesis AC/MIL-101-NH2 (Cr)25. The process involved mixing 4 g of chromium nitrate hydrate, 1.8 g of 2-aminoterephthalic acid, 50 mg activated coke, 1 ml hydrochloric acid, and 50 ml of deionized water by a magnetic stirrer. This mixture was then heated in a sealed container for 12 h at 473 K. The resulting slurry was separated and washed thoroughly to remove any unreacted starting materials by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 20 min. Finally, the AC/MIL-101-NH2 (Cr) composite was dried under vacuum at 373 K for 12 h.

Synthesis of IL ([Cho][Pro])

To synthesis of the IL(Cho][Pro]) ionic liquid, at first, the anion exchange of [Cho][Cl] was performed by anion exchange resin to prepared of the aqueous solution of [Cho][OH]. After that, the 1.2 equimolar Histidine, Tyrosine, and Proline aqueous solutions were added to the [Cho][OH] aqueous solution and stirred for 72 h at room temperature26,27. Then a rotary evaporator was used to water remove at 352 K for 10 h and to eliminate the excess amino acid, the product was washed several times with precipitation in absolute ethanol. At last, the product was vacuumed to remove ethanol for 5 h at 338 K. The water value of IL was evaluated using Karl–Fischer titrator (720-KSS Metrohm) which was 0.005 in mass fraction. 1H NMR spectra of IL is directed in the supplementary (Figs. S1). The purity of IL was ≥ 0.98 according to integrated hydrogen peaks.

Impregnation of IL in MOF

Confinement of IL on MOF was done by the ILs solution with a ratio of 1:1 wt/vol using vacuum impregnation method. Then the obtained slurry was dried by an oven for 24 h at temperature of 378 K.

Membrane preparation

To prepare of PVC-based MMMs, first the AC/MOF (wMOF = 5, 10, 15%) or IL@AC/MOF (wMOFIL = 15%) was dispersed in THF under sonication and stirred conditions. Then PVC was dissolved in the THF. After that, PVC solution was added to the AC/MOF or IL@AC/MOF solution and stirred. Then the obtained solution was casted on a flat glass plate and slowly evaporated at room temperature for 48 h. Finally, the prepared MMMs was dried at 333 K for 24 h in an oven. Density and mass of MMMs were measured to determine their thickness. The density of MMMs was determined by the flotation method in combination of acetone and chloroform. After adjusting the composition to keep the MMMs suspended throughout the procedure, the density of the solution was determined with a density analyzer (DSA-5000, Anton Paar).

Characterization analysis

The surface properties and morphology of AC/MOF, AC/MOF/PVC and IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMM were investigated using FESEM images EDX spectra. The ATR-FTIR spectroscopy analysis was used to notify available chemical bonds and functional groups. The ATR-FTIR spectra were recorded with (Bruker, Tensor 27) spectrometer. The TGA curve has been recorded by a TGA instrument, (TGA-DAT, perkinelmer pyris diamond) with rate of 10 0C/min under N2 purging condition. Using a gas adsorption analyzer (BEL BELSORP MINI II, Japan), nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K. Before the measurements, the samples were heated to 120 ºC for 10 h while being degassed under vacuum at a rate of 10 ºC per minute. Nitrogen adsorption at 77 K was used to measure the samples’ BET surface area.

Gas separation performance

In order to increase the permeability and separation performance of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) membranes, IL@AC/MOF filler was added into the PVC matrix with different mass percent of AC/MOF. The gas separation performance of IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMMs was evaluated with pure N2 and CO2. CO2/N2 gas separation performance is based on solution–diffusion mechanism which is dominant mechanism of the gaseous penetrant transport via dense polymeric membranes. The permeation of gases CO2 and N2 onto PVC, AC/MOF/PVC MMMs with different mass percent of (wMOF = 5, 10, 15%), and IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMMs with mass percent of (wMOF,IL=15%) were measured at temperatures 288.15, 298.15, 308.15 and 318.15 K and pressures 2–4 bar. Before measured of CO2 and N2 gas permeability, the membrane was remained under vacuum for 4 h to achieve a stable state. Before changing the feed gas, the membrane must be degassed for some time to sure of the full desorption of the previous permeate gas. The permeability, solubility, and diffusion coefficients of PVC-based MMMs at different temperatures and pressures for CO2 and N2 were listed in Table S1. According to the Table S1, the permeability of CO2 is substantially higher than permeability of N2, that is owing to the little dynamic diameters of CO2. The kinetic diameters of N2 and CO2 are 3.64 Å and 3.3 Å, respectively43. The size of the gas molecules influences the gas diffusion rate in the membrane, and permeability is inversely proportional with gas molecule size. The permeability, solubility, and diffusion coefficients of pure CO2 and N2 gases and CO2/N2 selectivity of the membranes was illustrated in Fig. 6 at 288.15, 298.15, 308.15 and 318.15 K and pressure up to 4 bar. The Fig. 6 shows CO2 permeability of IL@AC/MOF/PVC-15% MMMs improves from 97.54 to 2795.25 Barrer when compared to pure PVC membrane, and the CO2/N2 selectivity was also raised by 15.3% at 298.15 K and 2 bar. The addition of IL@AC/MOF composite to PVC leads to an improvement in the gas permeation performance that arises from the higher solubility of CO2 in the composite due to the IL existence. Meanwhile, the high-porosity AC/MOF, which serves as the IL carrier, can expand the area in which IL and CO2 come into contact, thereby enhances the CO2 permeability of the IL@AC/MOF composite. The increase in N2 permeability is significantly less than that of CO2 due to the poor affinity between N2 and IL@AC/MOF and the relatively high dynamic diameter of N2. The rate of gas diffusion in the membrane increases with the decrease penetrant size and the gas molecules size is in reverse related to permeability, while the solubility increases by increasing in gas condensability. Moreover, CO2 and N2 permeability of PVC membrane increased by increasing of AC/MOF and IL@AC/MOF Fillers loading. The solubility mechanism is main mechanisms in the gas permeation through membranes. As a result, CO2 permeability increases while CO2/N2 selectivity reduces. Moreover, reduced selectivity are related to poor compatibility between PVC and filler, which causes non-selective defects5. The solubility coefficient (S) and diffusion coefficient (D) of the MMMs were obtained through the method of time-lag44. According to Fig. 6, the filler has a porous structure that provides transport pathways for gas molecules, MMMs have a higher gas diffusion coefficient than pure PVC membrane. Also, the solubility coefficient of CO2 into MMMs is also more than that of pure PVC membrane because of the high affinity of the IL@AC/MOF filler for CO2. In compared to N2, IL rises the solubility of CO2 due to higher quadrupole of CO2 that leads to more solubility in the polymer45,46.

Effect of feed pressure

The permeability of CO2 and N2 gases in the MMMs at constant temperature and different pressures to examine the impact of feed pressure on separation parameters was shown in Fig. 7 at 318.15 K. The obtained data show with increasing feed pressure reduces the penetration of carbon dioxide and nitrogen34,47 that the membrane compaction and gas solubility are the two main causes of this phenomenon. Reduced membrane permeability results from the compaction processes’ reducing of the membrane’s free volume.

A low degree of penetrant or “microvoid environment” is accessible for sorption and permeation in glassy polymers like PVC. Saturation of the microvoids at high penetrant concentrations reduces the available permeation pathways caused to lowers permeability48.

Conclusions

In this work, to improve the permeability and separation performance of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) membranes, AC/MOF and IL@AC/MOF fillers were added into the PVC matrix. The mixed matrix membranes were characterized using FTIR spectroscopy, TGA, BET, SEM, and EDX. The porous structure of MMMs nanocomposites causes AC/MOF composite to effectively accelerate gas diffusion in the PVC matrix IL was impregnated in AC/MOF and IL@AC/MOF was embedded in PVC matrix. Comparing the IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMMs (wMOF, IL = 0.15) with the PVC membrane reveals that the incorporation of the IL@AC/MOF filler increased permeability by about 17.6 times and the CO2/N2 selectivity increases 2.94 times at 298.15 K and 4 bar. These results demonstrated that incorporation of IL@AC/MOF as a filler into PVC to manufacture IL@AC/MOF/PVC MMMs resulted in a significantly increased CO2/N2 selectivity. Moreover, MMMs indicated the highest gas separation efficiency and noticeably exceeded the 2008 Robson’s Upper Bound. Also, the values of activation energy shows that the permeation has a high-temperature dependence.
