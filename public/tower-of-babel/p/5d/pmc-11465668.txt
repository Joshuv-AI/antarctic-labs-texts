Frequency of polymorphisms in the IKZF1 and CDKN2A/2B genes and descriptive analysis in pediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a multicenter hospital-based prevalence study in Rio De Janeiro
Authors: Ariadne da Rocha Figueiredo, Thayana da Conceição Barbosa, Mariana Emerenciano, Marcelo Gerardin Poirot Land
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common childhood cancer, and B-cell ALL (B-ALL) is the most common subtype. The understanding of ALL has advanced significantly in recent years due to genomic sequencing, which has made it possible to identify genetic variants and detect the association between “single nucleotide polymorphisms” (SNP) and certain diseases.MethodsWe evaluated 126 patients diagnosed with B-ALL in hospitals in Rio de Janeiro. We described the frequency of polymorphisms in the IKZF1, CDKN2A/2B genes, the contribution of these genetic variants in pediatric ALL, and compared them with the general population of Rio de Janeiro.ResultsWe demonstrated that the SNPs rs3731217, rs4132601, and rs11978267 were more frequent in patients with B-ALL.ConclusionsThese findings contribute to a more complete understanding of B-ALL. They can guide future studies, bringing new perspectives on personalized therapies with reduced side effects and optimization efficacy of B-ALL treatment in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13005-y.

Background

Acute leukemia is a disease of primary clonal origin of the bone marrow (BM) in which there is blockage of differentiation, abnormal proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis of hematopoietic precursor cells, causing their accumulation. It promotes the replacement of normal medullary cells by these precursor cells, called blasts. In acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the blast is the lymphoblast. It is defined according to the expression of antigens in the cytoplasm, which may be B precursor hematopoietic cells (B-ALL) or T lineage hematopoietic cells (T-ALL) [1]. ALL is the most common childhood cancer, accounting for 25% of all childhood cancers [2]. In Brazil, in 2021, the incidence in the age group from 0 to 19 years was 1519 registered cases, according to DataSUS. The incidence peak occurs between 2 and 5 years, and genetic alteration can often be detected at birth [3]. B-ALL comprises 80–85% of childhood ALL. The B precursor cell has specific markers such as CD9, CD19, CD22, and CD79a, and it is distinct from the mature B precursor cell as it does not present immunoglobulin on the surface. Most B-ALL shows CD10 (CALLA) expression, called common-ALL [4].

In recent years, the understanding of ALL has advanced significantly due to genomic sequencing. Acquired genomic variants, also known as somatic changes, result from genetic damage to an individual’s cell throughout life. This genetic change is generally found predominantly in tumor cells. Germline variants are present in every cell in the body and are inherited genetic variants. Although somatic changes have already been studied and associated with clinical and biological aspects of ALL, inherited genetic variations have also been shown to be important determinants of susceptibility to ALL, the quality of chemotherapy response, and toxicity to therapy [5].

The IKZF1 gene, whose acronym stands for IKAROS Family Zinc Finger 1, encodes the transcriptional factor Ikaros, which plays a vital role in hematopoiesis, mainly in the maturation and differentiation of lymphoid progenitors, in addition to being a tumor suppressor gene in B-ALL. This gene’s function loss can occur for several reasons, such as copy number alteration, deletion, or polymorphism [6]. Germline variants of IKZF1, such as rs11978267 and rs4132601, are associated with the risk of developing ALL, especially the B-ALL subtype. CDKN2A/2B are tumor suppressor genes and are frequently deleted in hematological malignancies. CDKN2A germline variants, such as rs3731249 and rs3731217, and the CDKN2B germline variant rs662463, are associated with the risk of developing ALL.

The objective of this study is to describe the frequency of polymorphisms in the IKZF1 and CDKN2A/2B genes in patients with ALL from the main leukemia treatment centers in Rio de Janeiro compared to a putative healthy population of our Brazilian region and to describe the association between the presence or absence of these polymorphisms with sociodemographic features, laboratory data, and other predictive variables.

Methods

Subjects

This study includes samples from 126 patients, 18 years old or younger, diagnosed with B-ALL and ascertained from 2017 to 2021. Patients have been recruited from different cancer treatment centers in Brazil, including Prontobaby group, Instituto de Puericultura e Pediatria Martagão Gesteira (IPPMG-UFRJ), Hospital Federal da Lagoa, Instituto Nacional de Câncer (INCA) and Hospital de Icaraí. Patients who had undergone any previous therapeutic procedure, who were in relapse, or who had genetic syndromes were excluded. The diagnosis and classification were performed through several methods, including morphologic, multiparametric flow cytometry, and molecular and cytogenetics analyses, according to standard criteria recommended by the World Health Organization. Primary and additional recurrent alterations in B-ALL were previously characterized using Reverse Transcriptase Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR), Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH), and Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA), according to conditions previously described [7, 8]. Patients negative for the primary recurrent alterations evaluated (ETV6::RUNX1, TCF3::PBX1, BCR::ABL1, KMT2A-r, and High hyperdiploid) were classified as B-other. Additionally, we characterized the Ph-like subgroup based on CRLF2 alterations, according to a previously described protocol [9]. We collected diagnostic, molecular, laboratory, and follow-up data from medical records.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Patients have no risks or discomforts because this is a retrospective observational study. This project is under resolution 466 of December 12, 2012. The INCA Research Ethics Committee approved this study (CAEE #87793418.0.0000.5274). We obtained informed consent from all subjects or their parents in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Genotyping of IKZF1 and CDKN2A/2B SNPs

Genomic DNA was obtained from BM or peripheral blood (SP) mononuclear cells using the DNA MiniBlood kit (Qiagen) according to a standard protocol. DNA was quantified using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Genotyping was performed using the following TaqMan allelic discrimination assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific): C__26019772_10 (rs4132601), C____199413_30 (rs11978267), C____207701_10 (rs3731217), C__25611114_10 (rs3731249) and C___2290621_10 (rs662463). Reactions were performed using the ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To ensure the quality of genotyping, samples were analyzed in duplicates.

The genotypes were identified by viewing the allelic discrimination graphs, where the wild-type, heterozygous mutant, or homozygous mutant patterns were identified by comparison with the reference controls for each allele using the Genotyping software made available online by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Each specific SNP has a type of genotype characterized as wild type or mutant, as shown in Table 1.

Statistical analyses

For numerical variables, the mean (standard deviation) and median (interquartile interval and range) values ​​were calculated. To calculate statistical significance in variables with normal distribution, the T student test was used. For variables that do not meet the requirements for normal distribution the Mann-Whitney test for two groups and Kuskal-Wallis or median test for 3 or more comparison groups.

For categorical variables, the percentage in each group was calculated and the risks were estimated. Fisher’s exact or chi-square test was used. The relative risk (RR) associated with SNPs occurrence across subgroups of patients with deletions or not at the same gene affected was determined by calculating RRs with 95% confidence interval (CI) using Wald test in Desctools package. SPSS version 20 and R were used for all analyses.

Estimation of the genotype frequency of the population of Rio de Janeiro

To estimate the genotype frequency (GF) of the Rio de Janeiro population (hypothetical healthy population), we used the calculation recommended by Gopalan et al. [10], based on the GF of the 5 SNPs studied in each of the source populations of the genetic mix of the state of Rio de Janeiro (European, Amerindian, and African) weighted by the percentage weight in the genetic mix. In Rio de Janeiro, the contribution percentages of each population are 63.5% for the European source population, 25.4% for the African source population, 11.1% for the Amerindian source population. Therefore, we obtain the following formula:

GF of SNP cat(phs) = 63.5% x GF of SNP cat(eu) + 25.4% x GF of SNP cat(af) + 11.1% x GF of SNP cat(am).

cat = SS, SM, MM.

eu = european; af = african; am = ameridean.

The genotypic frequency data of source populations was obtained in the global SNP database (1000 Genomes Project phase 3) accessed in https://www.ensembl.org/info/genome/variation/species/sets.html.

Results

The distribution of clinical and molecular characteristics of the 119 B-ALL patients included is shown in Supplementary Table S1. Most patients belonged to the age group of 1 to 10 years (77.3%) and were male (54.6%). Additionally, most patients had a leukocyte count at diagnosis of less than < 50 × 109/L (81.9%) and no involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) (91.5%). The most frequently found cytogenetic and molecular alterations at diagnosis were the ETV6::RUNX1fusion (22.0%) and B-other (39.0%). The treatment protocol chosen by the majority of hospitals was BFM 2009 v1.3 (76.5%), with most patients presenting negative minimal residual disease (MRD) at week 12 (92.7%). Approximately 24.3% of patients experienced disease relapse and 10.7% required a bone marrow transplant.

Out of the 119 patients included in the study, 15 were excluded due to a lack of DNA for genotyping analysis, resulting in 104 patients being successfully analyzed. Table 1 shows the frequency of SNPs investigated by age group (less than 10 years and greater than or equal to 10 years). No statistical significance was found between groups. The distribution of genotypes according to molecular subgroups is shown in Supplementary Table S2. The data indicate that mutant alleles were present across all molecular subgroups, regardless of their prognostic risk, except for the mutant allele of SNP rs3731217 at CDKN2A, found only in intermediate or high-risk molecular subgroups. An analysis of genotyping data was conducted alongside the occurrence of deletions in the same gene in Table 2. However, despite having information on the SNP, some patients have no information on gene deletion. There was no significant difference in the frequency of SNP genotypes in patients who had deletions or non-deletions in the respective genes, and it was not possible to establish a difference between the groups.

Discussion

The frequency of KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2A-r) is consistent with what is found in the literature since, in our study, we observed KMT2A-r in 5.3% of ALL cases. Other molecular subgroups were detected in a more significant proportion than usually described, such as BCR::ABL1. The literature reports a frequency of 3.0 to 5.0%, while, in our study, we observed it in 15.8% of patients. The same happened for high hyperdiploidy and TCF3::PBX1, which in our study were noted in 6.0% and 11.0% of cases, respectively, while, in the literature, 25.0% and 4.0% are described [11, 12]. Furthermore, our results suggest the need to consider these molecular variations when developing personalized diagnostic and treatment strategies for pediatric patients with B-ALL.

When investigating the relapse rate of patients enrolled in the study, we observed a significantly higher rate in our patient cohort (29.0%) compared to previous studies (15.0%) [13]. This discrepancy may be attributed, in part, to the more significant proportion of patients in our study with the BCR::ABL1 fusion, a marker of unfavorable prognosis in B-ALL. While we recognize the importance of using tyrosine kinase inhibitors for BCR::ABL1 or Ph-like positive patients, the inherent limitations of our retrospective dataset, including the absence of such information, prevented us from conducting a comprehensive and informative evaluation of treatments. Furthermore, a discrepancy may be related to specific genetic characteristics and the presence of germline variants, which may play a crucial role in determining the risk of relapse and promote different development between studies. Despite the higher relapse rate, few patients required bone marrow transplantation, as expected in the literature.

In our study, all mutant genotypes were in a higher proportion in the leukemic population than in the hypothetical healthy population, showing that the presence of this variant plays a role in the development of ALL. We observed that, as described in previous studies, the mutated genotypes SNPs rs37311217, rs11978267, and rs4132601 were more frequent in patients who had B-ALL than in the hypothetical normal population (p-value < 0.05). The study conducted by Sherbone et al. compared 907 ALL cases with 2398 controls and showed that the SNP rs37311217 is a risk factor for ALL [14]. Another study in Egypt compared 128 cases of ALL and 242 controls and showed that the mutants SNPs rs11978267 and rs4132601 in IKZF1 are also related to susceptibility to ALL [15].

Other international studies have studied 247,505 variants, including the mutant genotype of rs3731249, showing a 2.2 times greater risk of developing ALL [16]. Furthermore, the meta-analysis conducted by Hungate et al. evaluated 1210 cases and 4144 controls. It showed that the mutant rs662463 SNP is a risk factor for ALL in African-American individuals [17]. Although our work did not demonstrate this fact, the rs3731249 and rs662463 SNPs are also a risk factor for ALL. This disagreement may be influenced by the small number of cases evaluated in our study. It was possible to note that the leukemic population has a very high frequency of the MM genotype (1.0%) when compared to the hypothetical healthy population (0.1%), and the same occurs when comparing the M allele (6.0% in the leukemic population versus 1.6% in the hypothetical healthy population). The most frequently found mutant variants in the leukemic population of our study were the rs11978267 and rs4132601 variants in IKZF1, where we observed the mutant genotypes of rs11978267 in 13.5% and rs4132601 in 11.0% of the patients studied and these findings are in agreement with the frequencies previously reported in the literature [18–20]. We could not analyze our data by racial or ethnic demographics, as genetic studies have demonstrated that Brazilians should be viewed as 210 million individuals rather than being classified by specific geographic regions or racial groups. A previous study using 40 insertion-deletion polymorphisms effectively distinguishing Amerindians, Europeans, and Sub-Saharan Africans revealed extensive genomic admixture within the Brazilian population. This admixture cannot be accurately assessed through phenotypic traits like skin color [21].

Our findings demonstrated the importance of the homozygous mutated pattern in some of the genes we investigated as potential risk factors for childhood ALL (acute lymphoblastic leukemia). Naturally, the percentage of the allele frequency of the mutated allele in the population of a geographical region determines the frequency of the homozygous genetic pattern. Therefore, each country and its regions may have different mutated allele frequencies, which, as in our population, depend on the contribution of each original population from which the current population resulted.

Conclusions

In summary, our study provides the frequency of SNPs studied in Brazilian pediatric patients with B-ALL, highlighting both agreements and disagreements with existing literature.

The frequency of polymorphisms in IKZF1, CDKN2A/2B is higher in patients with B-ALL compared to the hypothetical healthy population, showing an essential role in the pathogenesis of this heterogeneous and multifactorial disease that is ALL. Our findings highlight the importance of considering ethnic diversity when investigating the etiology of leukemia and other complex diseases.

The identification of specific genetic markers, such as the rs3731249 SNP of the CDKN2A, may allow for more accurate patient stratification and the development of personalized therapies. However, more research is needed to elucidate the complex interactions between this variant and treatment response fully.

These findings contribute to a more complete understanding of B-ALL and can guide future studies. They will also bring new perspectives on personalized therapies that reduce side effects and optimize the effectiveness of B-ALL treatment in children.
