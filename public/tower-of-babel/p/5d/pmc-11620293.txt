Anterior segment ischemia following scleral buckle surgery: The forgotten enemy
Authors: Janani Sreenivasan, Aditi Joshi, Aashul Chawla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Anterior segment ischemia (ASI) is a rare, almost forgotten complication of scleral buckle (SB) surgery. The course varies from mild self-limited to severe type causing significant vision loss. A number of local and systemic factors can precipitate ASI. It is important to be aware of these factors and take the necessary steps to avoid this complication. Despite that, if the patient develops ASI, early recognition is important. Mild cases are managed conservatively whereas severe cases will require removal of the band. We present a case of an 18-year-old male who developed a mild type of ASI post-SB surgery managed conservatively.

Introduction

Anterior segment ischemia (ASI) is a rare but potentially devastating complication following ophthalmic surgeries, especially strabismus surgery and scleral buckling (SB).[123] It also has been reported following extensive laser or cryotherapy in cases of retinopathy of prematurity.[4] ASI is characterized by the compromised blood supply to the anterior segment of the eye, leading to ischemic changes to the anterior segment structures.[123] Here, we aim to remind vitreoretinal surgeons about this almost forgotten complication, through this case of ASI following SB in an 18-year-old male managed conservatively.

Case Report

An 18-year-old male presented with left eye (OS) defective vision accidentally noticed and diagnosed elsewhere as retinal detachment (RD). The patient had nephrotic syndrome and was on oral steroids for 15 years. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of the right eye (OD) was 6/6, N6, and the left eye (OS) was 2/60 with −3.50DS. The anterior segment of both eyes (OU) and fundus examination of OD were normal. OS fundus revealed a rhegmatogenous RD with a large break but minimal proliferative vitreo retinopathy [Figure 1a and b]. On preoperative workup, he was diagnosed with macrocytic anemia and anisocytosis and was treated orally for the same. The patient underwent #279 SB with 5 anchor sutures and #240 encircling band with cryopexy to the break and suspicious lesions. The procedure was a nondrainage type as the fluid was shallow, but paracentesis was done thrice to achieve adequate buckle height without increasing intraocular pressure (IOP). The encircling band was not tightened much and minimal intracameral adrenaline was used for mydriasis. Intraoperatively, indirect ophthalmoscopy was done to check optic nerve head perfusion and finger tension was ok at the time of closure.

(a) represents an ultrawide field pseudocolor OPTOS fundus image of the left eye showing macula off rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RD) with a large temporal break. (b) is the autofluorescence image of the same eye clearly depicting the extent of RD with a hyper fluorescent leading edge

On postoperative day 1, the eye was quiet with a clear cornea, IOP was normal, broke flat, and the retina was attached. On postoperative day 4, image [Figure 2a] he developed chemosis, corneal edema, anterior chamber (AC) cells 4+, and flare 3 + with IOP of 30 mm Hg on applanation tonometry and hazy fundus view. Ultrasonography showed minimal vitreous echoes and attached retina [Figure 2b]. The differentials were toxic anterior segment syndrome due to intracameral adrenaline, endophthalmitis, and severe inflammation due to cryopexy. Strikingly hypopyon was absent and the vitreous cavity was clear on ultrasound, hence the patient was treated in line of inflammation. The patient was started on topical hourly prednisolone, atropine 3 times/day, moxifloxacin 6 times/day, and timolol twice a day. After physician clearance, oral steroids were started at the dose of 40 mg weekly taper. On postoperative day 6, the cornea cleared significantly, enabling a better appreciation of anterior segment details. There was no hypopyon but there was fibrin and streak hyphema. A diagnosis of ASI was made. The patient was suggested to undergo hemoglobin electrophoresis which was normal. On postoperative day 11, the patient was doing better with a clear cornea, contracting fibrin [Figure 2c], and slightly better fundus view showing an attached retina [Figure 2d]. At postoperative week 6, BCVA was 3/60 (−3.50DS), quiet AC, superior iris atrophy but no Neovascularisation of iris/Neovascularisation of angle (NVI/NVA), and clear lens [Figure 3a]. IOP was under control with timolol eye drops. The retina was attached [Figure 3b] and swept-source optical coherence tomography [Figure 3c] showed retinal thinning with outer retinal layers atrophy.

Discussion

The ASI syndrome consists of necrotic changes to the anterior segment structures secondary to ischemia. Striate keratopathy, Descemet’s folds, and corneal edema are frequently the first and most prominent signs.[23] Iris necrosis and cataract formation may begin on the side of greatest hypoxia and is usually complete in severe cases within 3 months. Hypotony is often marked. The AC may become shallow, due to the ciliary body and choroidal detachment.[1] Although the diagnosis of ASI is clinical, recently, optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) has been used to detect iris-filling defects. The most important advantage of OCTA is that gives quantitative iris vessel density values that can be compared pre-and postoperatively.[3]

Systemic risk factors for ASI include older age, generalized atherosclerosis, blood dyscrasias, and hematologic disorders that increase serum viscosity (sickle cell disease/trait/sickle thalassemia).[2] Surgical steps that can cause ASI in predisposed individuals include placement of an encircling band, especially if excessively tightened, IOP elevation, extensive cryopexy, use of sympathomimetics, or some other mechanical disruption of ciliary blood flow (disinsertion of the rectus muscles).[1235] Causes of ASI include the pressure effect of the buckle and surgically induced inflammation, blocking the iris and ciliary vessels.[1] The long posterior ciliary arteries are susceptible to occlusion owing to diathermy or cryopexy;[46] hence it is better to spare the 3 o’clock and 9 o’clock meridians. Literature also suggests that subretinal fluid (SRF) drainage induces hypotony and maintains perfusion.[1] Other less significant causes include vortex vein congestion, 360° peritomy, and retrobulbar block.[57] As per an experimental model, fornix conjunctival incisions in surgery may provide partial protection against ASI by preserving the perilimbal conjunctival-Tenon’s circulation, which is disrupted with limbal conjunctival incisions.[8] A 360° limbal peritomy can be a cause of ASI, and it is recommended smaller more posterior peritomy in predisposed cases.[7] The surgical factors predisposing to ASI and possible corrective measures are summarized in Table 1.

In this case, placement of an encircling band, large SB (#279), nondrainage, use of intracameral adrenaline, and preexisting anisocytosis – all could have precipitated ASI. The final visual outcome was suboptimal due to old RD sequelae. Management of ASI includes topical with/without oral steroids and cycloplegics during the initial phase of severe inflammation.[239] Patients who present with severe ASI may need removal of the explant, especially the encircling band.[2] Since ASI following pars plana vitrectomy is rare although reported,[10] it can be considered over SB in predisposed patients.

Ophthalmologists should be aware of the potential local and systemic risk factors for ASI and modify the surgical decision-making and intraoperative steps. Prompt recognition of ASI should be followed by conservative treatment in mild cases and removal of explant in severe cases.
