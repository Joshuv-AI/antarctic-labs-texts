Explainable Lightweight Model Using Low‐Rank and Convolutional Block Attention for Pancreatic Cancer Diagnosis
Authors: Vishesh Tanwar, Bhisham Sharma, Dhirendra Prasad Yadav, Panos Liatsis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly and accurate pancreatic cancer (PC) detection remains a major clinical challenge.MethodsWe introduce a novel hybrid deep learning framework for automated classification of CT images, which requires fewer computational resources while achieving high diagnostic performance. We integrated a lightweight MobileNetV3Small backbone with a convolutional block attention module and Low‐rank Attention with Shared Efficient Representations (LASER) to enhance feature representation. Feature maps are projected via a 1 × 1 convolution into token sequences and processed through a transformer encoder to capture long‐range dependencies. A parallel global average pooling extracts aggregated features, fused using a cross‐type interaction (CTI) module.ResultsThe model was evaluated on 18,942 CT images and achieved 99.34% accuracy, AUC‐ROC of 0.9996, Cohen's Kappa of 0.9897, and MCC of 0.9859, outperforming ResNet50, EfficientNetB0, and ViT variants with only 1.26 million parameters.ConclusionsExplainability analyses using Grad‐CAM, Grad‐CAM++, and attention visualisation suggest that the model focuses on clinically relevant regions.Keywords: attention, classification, deep learning, pancreatic cancer, transformer

Introduction

Problem Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, characterised by late diagnosis, aggressive progression and limited treatment options, with 5‐year survival rates reported as low as 7% [1]. Despite advances in medical imaging, early detection remains a significant clinical challenge due to the subtle and non‐specific appearance of early‐stage tumours on computed tomography (CT) scans. The dismal prognosis is primarily attributed to late‐stage diagnosis, aggressive tumour progression, and the lack of effective early screening strategies. Although computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in the imaging and diagnostic assessment of pancreatic cancer, early detection remains a formidable clinical challenge. This is due to the subtle and non‐specific nature of early‐stage lesions, low contrast between normal and pathological tissues, and variability in CT acquisition protocols. Even highly experienced radiologists often struggle to distinguish malignant tissue from benign conditions or inflammatory changes, leading to potential misdiagnosis and delayed treatment [2].

Deep learning has emerged as a transformative technology in medical image analysis in recent years, particularly for tasks requiring high‐level feature representation and classification [3]. Unlike traditional ML approaches that rely on handcrafted features, deep learning, specifically CNNs, automatically learns multi‐level hierarchical features directly from raw image data. This capability has already demonstrated state‐of‐the‐art performance in diagnosing diseases using imaging modalities such as chest X‐rays, brain MRI, and mammograms [4].

The use of DL in CT‐based detection of pancreatic cancer is very promising and at the same time, it is associated with certain challenges. Pancreatic tumours have non‐homogenous textures, are irregular in shape and are frequently embedded in complex anatomical backgrounds [5]. Furthermore, there is a lack of annotated images, and class imbalance makes model training and generalisation difficult. Nonetheless, early studies such as those of the PANDA framework and hybrid approaches presented by Zhang et al. have demonstrated that advanced architectures have the potential to enhance sensitivity and specificity. Some of the innovations pushing the limits of diagnostic performance include augmented feature pyramids, self‐adaptive feature fusion and dependency‐modelling modules, which allow more contextualised understanding and higher classification performance [6].

This study aims to build on these advancements by developing a novel deep learning‐based model for PC classification in contrast‐enhanced CT images. The proposed model combines a lightweight MobileNetV3Small backbone for feature extraction with CBAM and LASER attention to refine spatial and channel information, while a Transformer module captures global dependencies. The fused features are then classified through dense layers, enabling efficient and accurate prediction.

Related Work

Reddy et al. [7] proposed a computer‐aided diagnostic system for pancreatic cancer that integrates K‐means clustering for segmentation with Haar wavelet transform for feature extraction on CT and MRI scans. They use triple‐threshold decision logic to validate malignancy, cross‐referencing calculated and software‐derived variance values to distinguish cancerous tissue. The approach demonstrates efficacy in early‐stage detection by isolating the region of interest from surrounding organs. Ozkan et al. [8] developed a CAD system leveraging endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) images to improve pancreatic cancer detection accuracy. Their age‐based classification strategy achieved up to 92% accuracy within specific age groups, indicating that incorporating demographic factors can enhance diagnostic models. Le et al. (2019) [9] introduced a noisy label classification (NLC) method to detect pancreatic cancer from whole‐slide images (WSIs). By dynamically weighting samples from a large noisy dataset alongside a small clean set, their method improved detection performance by up to 9.7% over baseline noisy‐label models and outperformed fully manually annotated models by 2.94%–3.74% AUC in patch‐level testing. Ma et al. [10] constructed a CNN‐based classifier for pancreatic cancer detection using CT images from 222 cancer patients and 190 healthy individuals, achieving over 95% accuracy in binary classification across CT phases. The model demonstrated performance comparable to that of gastroenterologists, suggesting practical utility for screening. Tonozuka et al. [11] developed EUS‐CAD, a deep learning‐based CAD system for detecting pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) using endosonographic images. Trained on 920 images and tested on 470, their model achieved high AUCs of 0.924 (validation) and 0.940 (test), effectively distinguishing PDAC from chronic pancreatitis and normal pancreas. Zhang et al. [12] proposed an advanced tumour detection framework using contrast‐enhanced CT images. Their architecture integrated Augmented Feature Pyramid Networks, Self‐adaptive Feature Fusion, and Dependencies Computation modules, achieving an AUC of 0.9455, outperforming standard CNN‐based approaches in tumour localization and detection.

Sehmi et al. [13] presented an automated pancreatic cancer grading system using histopathological images. They fine‐tuned 14 ImageNet‐pretrained CNN models on two staining types and identified DenseNet as the best performing, achieving 95.61% accuracy. This approach offers the potential to reduce diagnostic time and grading errors in histopathology workflows. Fu et al. [14] developed a deep CNN to detect pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma in whole‐slide histopathological images. Their model achieved 95.3% accuracy at the patch level and 100% accuracy at the WSI level, demonstrating its effectiveness for automated histopathological screening. Rustam et al. (2021) [15] compared LR and RF classifiers for pancreatic cancer classification using data from Al‐Islam Hospital, Indonesia. Their results showed that random forest outperformed logistic regression with a maximum accuracy of 99.38%, supporting tree‐based models for clinical decision support systems. Javed et al. [16] introduced a radiomics‐based AI model to predict pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma up to 3 years before diagnosis. By analysing CT subregions (head, body, tail) using a Naïve Bayes classifier, they achieved 88.2% accuracy, demonstrating that subregional analysis provides superior predictive features over whole‐organ analysis. Noguchi et al. [17] developed deep learning models to predict the 1‐year prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients with positive peritoneal washing cytology. Their CNN and Vision Transformer (ViT) models obtained AUC values of 80.56 and 80.09, respectively. The predictions of the ViT‐based model were significantly related to the survival of patients, thus suggesting possible AI‐based prognostic evaluations in cytology. Shao et al. [18] trained CNN‐based models to identify neoadjuvant chemotherapy responses in pancreatic cancer patients using pre‐chemotherapy CEUS videos. Their models achieved AUCs of 0.892 and 0.908, suggesting strong potential for treatment‐response prediction from ultrasound videos using AI.

Karar et al. [19] proposed a 1D CNN‐LSTM hybrid system for the diagnosis of non‐invasive early PDAC, based on four urine proteomic biomarkers. Their model was tested on 590 urine samples, resulting in an accuracy of 97% and an AUC of 98% and was superior to earlier classifiers, while offering cost‐effective, urine‐based screening. The PANDA deep learning model was developed by Cao et al. [20] to screen pancreatic cancer on a large scale by using non‐contrast CT scans. Achieving AUCs between 0.986 and 0.996 across multicenter datasets, PANDA outperformed radiologists in sensitivity and specificity, demonstrating feasibility for population‐level screening. Ramachandra et al. [21] introduced RIC‐GD, an ensemble learning method for pancreatic cancer detection using urine biomarkers. Their approach achieved 92% accuracy, outperforming traditional classifiers such as Naive Bayes and decision trees, suggesting ensemble techniques can improve non‐invasive diagnostic pipelines. Zhao et al. [22] developed a transformer‐CNN model to detect tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) in H&E‐stained PDAC slides, achieving high F1‐scores of 0.87–0.92. Their study highlighted the maturity of TLS's prognostic value for patient survival outcomes, adding a novel biomarker‐based dimension to cancer prognosis research. More broadly, recent studies have demonstrated the transformative potential of AI in gastrointestinal oncology, spanning early tumour screening, diagnosis, molecular marker identification, treatment planning, and prognosis prediction through deep learning and multimodal data integration [23], including prognostic modelling for hepatocellular carcinoma using machine learning‐based radiotherapy outcome prediction [24].

Systematic literature analysis.

The literature survey on pancreatic cancer detection and classification has demonstrated impressive advances through diverse AI techniques, including CNNs, Vision Transformers, radiomics, and hybrid models, achieving high accuracies (often > 95%) across CT, histopathology, cytology, ultrasound, and biomarker datasets. Notable contributions include annotation‐efficient self‐supervised learning for small datasets, subregion‐based radiomic prediction up to 3 years before diagnosis, and biomarker‐based non‐invasive detection with near‐perfect accuracy, indicating strong potential for early diagnosis, grading, and prognosis prediction. However, these studies face limitations such as small or single‐centre datasets limiting generalisability, lack of multi‐modal data integration, minimal external or prospective validation, interpretability gaps in deep models, and scarce assessment of clinical workflow integration.

Methods

The proposed hybrid framework is shown in Figure 1. We integrated a lightweight MobileNetV3Small backbone with attention modules and a transformer encoder to achieve accurate and efficient pancreatic cancer classification from CT images. MobileNetV3Small first extracts compact yet discriminative spatial features using depth‐wise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks. These feature maps are refined through the CBAM attention module, which adaptively emphasises relevant channels and spatial regions. A 1 × 1 convolution projects features into a lower embedding dimension and reshapes them into token sequences for the transformer encoder, where LASER Attention efficiently models long‐range dependencies with reduced computational complexity. Within the transformer, FFN, Residual Connections, and LN further stabilise and enhance feature learning. Outputs from the Transformer and CNN are pooled using Global Average Pooling, fused, and passed through the CTI module for feature refinement. Finally, dropout and dense layers with L2 regularisation mitigate overfitting, and a sigmoid output layer produces the binary classification (cancer/normal). This design balances computational efficiency with high classification accuracy.

Feature Extraction Using MobileNetV3Small

MobileNetV3Small is used as a lightweight and efficient backbone to extract spatial features from images. MobileNetV3Small is a compact variant of the MobileNetV3 architecture, as shown in Figure 2, specifically optimised for faster inference and lower computational cost on mobile and embedded devices. Like MobileNetV2, it leverages DWS convolutions, which factorise a standard convolution into a depth‐wise convolution followed by a pointwise convolution (1 × 1 convolution to combine outputs). This significantly reduces the number of parameters and floating‐point operations (FLOPs) while maintaining competitive accuracy. In addition, MobileNetV3Small also employs inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks. Instead of compressing and then expanding feature maps (as in conventional residual networks), it first expands the features to a higher dimension, applies depth‐wise convolution, and then projects them back to a lower dimension via a linear bottleneck. This design improves feature reuse and minimises information loss while keeping the model compact. In the hybrid model, MobileNetV3Small serves as the feature extractor backbone. The MobileNetV3Small backbone was initialised with ImageNet‐pretrained weights and fine‐tuned end‐to‐end alongside all added modules. As shown in Table 2, the model contains 1,245,346 trainable parameters (including backbone convolutional weights) and 12,112 non‐trainable parameters, which correspond to batch normalisation running statistics that are updated via exponential moving average rather than gradient descent.

Transformer Encoder With LASER Attention

Low‐rank Attention with Shared Efficient Representations (LASER) attention is a lightweight self‐attention mechanism designed to reduce the high computational and memory costs of traditional Transformer attention, as shown in Figure 4. Standard self‐attention has quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, making it inefficient for large‐scale inputs such as high‐resolution medical images. Several approaches have been proposed to address this bottleneck, including Linformer [25], which projects keys and values to lower dimensions, Performer [26], which uses kernel‐based approximations of softmax attention, Nyström attention [27], which approximates the attention matrix via landmark‐based sampling, and LoRA‐style low‐rank adaptations [28]. Although these methods have been developed and evaluated primarily for long‐sequence NLP or token‐based vision transformers, their application to spatially flattened convolutional feature maps in medical imaging pipelines remains less explored. In this context, LASER is not proposed as a new theoretical class of attention but rather as a practical instantiation of low‐rank attention principles adapted for the hybrid CNN‐Transformer architecture used in this work. Specifically, LASER addresses this challenge by approximating the full attention matrix using a low‐rank factorization, which projects queries and keys into a lower‐dimensional shared representation space. This not only reduces the number of operations required but also minimises memory usage, enabling efficient training and inference. Despite the reduced complexity, LASER maintains strong representational capacity by preserving the most informative relationships across tokens. When integrated with a Transformer encoder, LASER allows the model to capture long‐range dependencies and contextual interactions effectively, while remaining computationally efficient, making it particularly suitable for medical imaging tasks where both global context and efficiency are critical. Layer Normalisation

Layer normalisation (LN) is a fundamental architectural component that significantly improves the stability, trainability, and performance of deep neural networks. Layer normalisation stabilises training by normalising the activations of neurons across the feature dimension for each individual sample, unlike Batch Normalisation, which relies on batch statistics. By computing the mean and variance across features within a layer and then applying learnt scaling and shifting parameters, layer normalisation reduces internal covariate shift, improves gradient propagation, and is particularly well suited for sequential and attention‐based models where batch sizes may vary. Summary of Proposed Model

Table 2 provides a summary of the proposed model, which efficiently combines the strengths of convolutional and transformer architectures for robust PC classification. It uses the MobileNetV3small backbone, pretrained on ImageNet, to process 224 × 224 RGB images inputted and extract rich local features. The CBAM module enhances feature representation by integrating channel and spatial attention mechanisms to highlight key regions. The refined features are reduced from 1280 to 128 channels through a Conv2D layer and projected into tokens for the transformer encoder, which captures global dependencies across the image. Global Average Pooling is then applied to both the CNN and Transformer outputs to generate compact feature representations. These vectors are concatenated to fuse the local spatial details from the CNN with the global contextual information from the Transformer. Subsequently, the fused features are processed through a custom CTI module, followed by dense and dropout layers to facilitate high‐level feature learning and regularisation. Lastly, binary classification is performed by a sigmoid‐activated output layer. Environment Setup

The experiments were carried out using a Lenovo Legion 17th generation laptop with 24 GB of RAM, a 512 GB SSD, and an 8 GB GPU. The Kaggle platform was also used to carry out model training, using a P100 GPU with 32 GB of memory for faster computations. All code was developed and experimented upon in Jupyter Notebooks. The proposed model achieved a classification accuracy of 99.34% with a lightweight architecture of only 1.257 million parameters, representing a substantial reduction in complexity, compared to standard transfer learning models based on attention mechanisms. In terms of computational complexity, the proposed model requires approximately 80–85 MFLOPs per forward pass at an input resolution of 224 × 224, which is only marginally higher than the MobileNetV3Small backbone alone (∼66 MFLOPs), reflecting the lightweight design of the added CBAM, LASER attention, and CTI fusion modules.

Ethical Approval and Use of Human Data

This study was conducted using two publicly available datasets of contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT images for pancreatic cancer classification, that is, the NIH Pancreas‐CT dataset, made accessible via the NCI Imaging Data Commons (IDC), and the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) Task‐07 Pancreas dataset, released under a CC‐BY‐SA 4.0 licence. All images were provided in de‐identified NIfTI format and used for secondary analysis only. No patient contact, intervention, or access to personally identifiable information occurred. In line with standard Institutional Review Board (IRB)/Research Ethics Committee (REC) guidance for research using fully de‐identified imaging data, institutional ethics approval and individual informed consent were not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulations.

Results

In this part, we provide a discussion of the experimental results of the proposed model. The experimental analysis illustrates the relative effectiveness of the hybrid model versus a number of state‐of‐the‐art designs. Performance analysis is conducted using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1‐score and AUC. This demonstrates that the proposed model outperforms the baseline networks in all classification metrics. The analysis of the ROC curve also validates the use of better discriminative ability, with the proposed approach attaining a virtually perfect AUC of 0.9996. Visualisation studies using Grad‐CAM++, Grad‐CAM and LASER attention maps reveal the ability of the model to attend to clinically important areas, which is a robust indication of its interpretability and reliability in pancreatic cancer prediction.

Datasets

In the present study, two datasets were used, that is, the Pancreas‐CT for the normal cohort, and the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) Task‐07 Pancreas for the cancer cohort. Figure 5 shows examples of a normal and a positive CT image. The Pancreas‐CT dataset was curated from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Clinical Centre and made accessible via the NCI Imaging Data Commons (IDC). It contains contrast‐enhanced images of 82 subjects who were scanned with CT (portal venous, about 70 s after injection), comprising 53 males and 27 females with ages between 18 and 76 years (mean: 46.8 ± 16.7 years). Scans were obtained at a tube voltage of 120 kVp using Philips and Siemens MDCT scanners and with slice resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, and slice thickness between 1.5 and 2.5 mm. The cancer cohort comprises 420 patients from the MSD Task‐07 Pancreas dataset, acquired at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Centre. Each volume represents a unique patient undergoing resection of a pancreatic mass, with pathologies including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PNET). All scans were portal‐venous phase CT. In total, the combined dataset includes 502 unique patients (82 normal + 420 cancer). A uniform preprocessing pipeline was applied to all images. Specifically, CT slices were converted using standard abdominal HU windowing (window level 40, window width 400) to enhance soft‐tissue contrast, followed by intensity normalisation to a range of [0,1], and resizing to 224 × 224 pixels. No model‐specific augmentation or preprocessing was applied. This pipeline was used consistently across the proposed model and all baseline comparisons.

Sample images from the dataset (a) normal (negative) (b) tumour (positive).

A strict patient‐level split was performed before any slice extraction. Each patient is assigned exclusively to either the training or test set, and all slices derived from that patient are kept within the same split. This ensures that no slices from the same subject appear in both training and testing, thereby eliminating the occurrence of data leakage. Normal cases (Pancreas‐CT dataset) are split using patient IDs (PANCREAS_Id) prior to slice generation. Cancer cases (MSD Task‐07 Pancreas) are split using volume‐level identifiers (.nii/.nii.gz files), where each volume corresponds to a unique patient. After the patient‐level split, a fixed number of representative slices (up to 10 per patient) are uniformly sampled and stored. In addition to slice‐level performance, we evaluate the model at the patient level by aggregating slice predictions belonging to the same patient using majority voting/mean probability aggregation. Final diagnostic performance is therefore reported at the patient level, which is more clinically meaningful. For reproducibility, we fixed the random seed (random state = 42) during patient‐level splitting.

To bridge this gap, the proposed hybrid model encompassing the MobileNetV3Small and CBAM with LASER Attention achieved an accuracy of 99.34% on CT images.

Explainable AI (XAI)

In this study, we applied three Explainable AI (XAI) methods, that is, Grad‐CAM, Grad‐CAM++ and attention‐based visualisation, to better interpret the decision‐making process of our deep learning model. Gradient‐weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad‐CAM) highlights the most influential regions in the input image by using the gradient information flowing into the final convolutional layers, thereby providing a heatmap that shows which areas contributed most to the model's prediction. This is particularly useful in medical imaging tasks, where it is important to verify whether the model is focussing on clinically relevant regions. Specifically, Grad‐CAM and Grad‐CAM++ are computed using the final convolutional feature map of the MobileNetV3Small backbone (prior to global average pooling), which has a spatial resolution of 7 × 7 pixels. The resulting activation maps are subsequently upsampled to the original input resolution (224 × 224) using bilinear interpolation for visualisation only. We note that these visualizations are qualitative and illustrative. Quantitative saliency evaluation (e.g., localization overlap with expert‐annotated regions) and blinded radiologist assessment were not performed due to the absence of pixel‐level ground truth annotations in the public datasets used, and are identified as important directions for future work. In parallel, attention‐based visualisation leverages the built‐in attention mechanisms of the model, which inherently assign different weights to input features or image patches depending on their importance. By examining these attention weights, we can gain insight into how the model distributes its focus across the input and validate whether its reasoning aligns with domain knowledge. Together, these two groups of methods enhance interpretability by offering both post hoc explanations (Grad‐CAM/Grad‐CAM++) and intrinsic explanations (attention‐based), thereby improving the trust, transparency, and reliability of the model's predictions.

Figure 14 presents Grad‐CAM visualizations of the proposed pancreatic cancer detection model to highlight the regions in CT scans that influenced its decisions. The top row shows the original CT scan slices, while the bottom row overlays the Grad‐CAM heatmaps. The red and yellow coloured regions represent areas where the model focused most strongly when classifying the image. From the results, we observe that the model correctly emphasises clinically relevant regions within the pancreas or surrounding tissues rather than irrelevant background structures. For example, in the first image, the activation is concentrated in a localised area suggestive of a potential tumour region, while in the other cases, activations highlight distinct suspicious regions consistent with abnormal tissue. This indicates that the model is not only achieving high accuracy as previously reported but is also interpretable, providing preliminary visual evidence that its predictions may be based on meaningful anatomical features. While encouraging, such qualitative observations require formal validation through quantitative saliency evaluation and blinded radiologist assessment before clinical conclusions can be drawn.

Statistical Significance Testing

The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test is a non‐parametric statistical method used to compare two related samples. In this study, paired observations were constructed at the individual image level on the fixed test set (5682 images), that is, for each test image, the predicted probability from the proposed model was paired with the predicted probability from each baseline model (MobileNetV3Small, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB0), all trained and evaluated under identical preprocessing and data split conditions. The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was then applied to these paired prediction differences. The resulting Wilcoxon Statistic quantifies the degree of disagreement between the two models' performances. The Table 12 presents the results of the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, comparing the performance of the proposed model against three baseline models (MobileNetV3Small, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB0). The corresponding p‐values are 3.6679e‐40, 2.2365e‐245, and 1.3613e‐21, far below the conventional threshold of 0.05, confirming that the differences are statistically significant. In other words, the improvements achieved by the proposed model over the baseline models are highly unlikely due to random chance. Beyond statistical significance, these findings also demonstrate practical significance, that is, the proposed model consistently achieves higher accuracy and robustness in classifying medical images, which directly translates into more reliable diagnostic outcomes. This means that, in a real‐world clinical setting, the proposed model is not only theoretically superior but also practically more effective in reducing misclassification risks and supporting early disease detection.

Conclusion

This study developed a computationally efficient hybrid deep learning framework for automated pancreatic cancer detection from contrast‐enhanced CT images. By integrating MobileNetV3Small with dual attention mechanisms (CBAM and LASER) and a transformer‐based encoder refined by a Cross‐Transformer Integration (CTI) module, the model achieves 99.34% accuracy with only 1.26 million parameters, significantly outperforming the ResNet50, EfficientNetB0, and Vision Transformer variants. Performance metrics, including an AUC‐ROC of 0.9996, Cohen's Kappa of 0.9897, and an MCC of 0.9859, along with statistically significant improvements (p < 1.4e‐21), demonstrate robust discriminative capability. The framework combines local spatial feature extraction using CNNs with global contextual modelling via the transformer, maintaining low training and inference overhead appropriate for resource‐constrained clinical settings. Explainable AI techniques, such as Grad‐CAM++ and attention visualisation, suggest that the model attends to pancreatic regions consistent with clinical expectations, though formal quantitative validation with radiologist assessment remains an important direction for future work. This hybrid architecture addresses key limitations of current methods, including the detection of small tumours, low tissue contrast, and high computational complexity. Future work will consider extensions to multi‐modal imaging, broader clinical validation across independent multicenter cohorts, demographic subgroup analysis (e.g., stratified by age and sex) using clinically curated datasets with available metadata, systematic evaluation of model sensitivity to input image resolution and CT windowing parameters, and application to other abdominal malignancies.
