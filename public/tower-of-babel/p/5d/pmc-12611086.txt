Effectiveness of information technology-based interventions for diabetes mellitus in glycemic control in Saudi Arabia: A systematic review
Authors: Najwa A. Zabeeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes technology, comprising medical devices and digital health technologies, offers the ability to enhance glycemic control and quality of life. Knowledge of the effects of these interventions could improve the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in Saudi Arabia. The aim of this systematic review was to assess the effectiveness of Information Technology (IT)-based interventions for glycemic control in Saudi Arabia. A total of 126 studies were found (49 publications found in PubMed, 46 in Web of Science, and 32 in ScienceDirect) using database searches. After 117 exclusions, nine studies comprising 5626 patients published between January 1, 2015, and March 24, 2025, were included. The following outcomes were assessed: hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting blood sugar (FBS), and such secondary measures as diabetic knowledge, patient satisfaction, cost-effectiveness, diabetes distress, and behavioral self-efficacy. IT-based interventions, including self-management systems, electronic medical records, and telemedicine, significantly reduced HbA1c and FBS levels. Improvements were also noted in diabetes knowledge, patient satisfaction, and self-efficacy of individuals with diabetes. These interventions also showed benefits in cost-effectiveness and reduced diabetes-related distress, supporting their value in diabetes care. IT-based interventions are effective in improving glycemic control and self-management of patients with T2DM in Saudi Arabia. Their integration into standard diabetes care aligns with national healthcare modernization under Vision 2030.

Introduction

Chronic diseases have become a major global health issue that significantly impacts on individuals, healthcare systems, and the global economy. Of these chronic diseases, diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the most common, and a leading health issue worldwide. According to the projection of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), by 2040, approximately 642 million people, or 8.8% of the global population will be living with diabetes. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of DM among adults was reported as18.7% in 2021, with projections indicating an increase to 20.4% by 2030.[1] With an estimated 7 million diabetics and approximately 3 million pre-diabetics, Saudi Arabia is ranked seventh globally and the second highest in the Middle East in terms of prevalence.[2]

DM is linked to increased mortality, high morbidity, vascular complications, and poor overall health and quality of life.[3] A successful reduction in hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels is known to significantly reduce the risk of complications, including cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, metabolic disease, neurology, nephrology, peripheral vascular disease, and retinopathy.[45]

Information technology (IT) is increasingly being integrated into chronic disease management, DM not excepted.[6] Numerous smartphone applications pertaining to health have been developed as mobile technology has progressed, with the aim of increasing self-management skills, promoting adherence to treatment, and the strengthening of patient-provider communication. However, diabetes self-management is still difficult because of the lack of training, difficulties in maintaining lifestyle changes, and limited access to professional healthcare.[7]

Applications for mobile devices have demonstrated the potential of supporting self-management of chronic illnesses.[8] Smart mobile health information technologies (SMHITs) are applied as a self-management strategy to enhance the quality of life, lower complications, and improve glycemic control.[9] Healthy eating, physical activity, blood sugar monitoring, medication compliance, effective problem-solving techniques, healthy coping mechanisms, and risk-reduction practices are the seven key self-care behaviors that predict positive outcomes for individuals with diabetes, according to the American Association of Diabetes Educators.[10] Better health can result from effective management of role, emotions, and one-self.[11]

Diabetes technology refers to hardware devices and software tools, including lifestyle monitoring and blood glucose control, used by patients to manage their condition. Despite the many benefits of digital health technologies, barriers to their adoption include infrastructure limitations, regulatory challenges, and data security issues. Successful implementation of digital health solutions relies on addressing these challenges and ensuring equitable access to these tools.[12]

The Android-based self-care management health mobile app is an effective educational tool. Participants had more self-confidence and improved control of fasting blood sugar (FBS) after using the app.[13] Li et al., analyzed the effectiveness of a mobile-based intervention on glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes using global population data. This intervention, used by a multidisciplinary team, produced better glycemic control than the usual care.[14] Noh et al., studied the impact of a web-based comprehensive information system, which included both the Internet and cellular phone use, on blood glucose control. This is used for a convenient web-based educational system that may be more effective for glycemic control than traditional education for diabetes patients. In addition, for patients with type 2 diabetes, both computer-based and cellular phone-based education significantly improved A1C levels.[15] A meta-analysis demonstrated that incorporating diabetes apps into routine self-management practices of individuals with T2DM led to a clinically significant reduction in HbA1c levels, a key indicator of long-term glycemic control.[16]

Therefore, the studies mentioned above suggest that Internet-based interventions are associated with improved glycemic control. Despite this evidence, no previous review article has yet been published on the effectiveness of IT on glycemic control in the Saudi population. This systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of IT-based interventions in the self-management of DM by examining their impact on glycemic control (HbA1c and FBS) and other outcomes, including patient satisfaction, cost-effectiveness, diabetes-related distress, and behavioral improvements.

Materials and Methods

Definition of outcomes and inclusion criteria

Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials and observational studies (cross-sectional or cohort) and quasi-experimental studies involving patients of all ages, both pediatric and adult populations, with type 1 or type 2 DM in healthcare settings, where IT-based interventions (e.g., mobile apps/Short message service (SMS), web platforms, electronic medical/health records, telemedicine) were assessed using a pre–post or control–intervention design.

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: conducted within Saudi Arabia, published in peer-reviewed journals between 2015 and 2025, written in English, and employed either quantitative or qualitative research methods. Furthermore, included studies were required to report on at least one of the following outcomes: HbA1c, FBS, and other outcomes such as patient satisfaction, behavioral improvement, and cost-effectiveness. Patient satisfaction was based on direct reports (usefulness, time-saving), while visits, complications, and polypharmacy were analyzed as clinical outcomes related to satisfaction. Exclusion criteria were applied to eliminate case reports, narrative reviews, editorials, or those that involved fewer than 10 patients; studies unrelated to IT-based interventions for diabetes management; patients with T2D managed with multiple daily injections of insulin therapy; research conducted outside Saudi Arabia; non-English publications without available translations; and studies that did not apply a recognized research methodology.

Search strategy

The search strategy for this systematic review was conducted on March 24, 2025, and involved the use of such databases as PubMed, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) to combine these terms, and filters narrowed the search to studies published in English. In addition, reference lists of relevant articles were reviewed to ensure a thorough coverage of the available evidence. The search terms included a combination of keywords such as PubMed: (“Diabetes Mellitus Type 1” [Title/Abstract] OR “Diabetes Mellitus Type 2” [Title/Abstract] OR “T2DM” [Title/Abstract] OR “diabetes” [Title/Abstract] OR “diabetes mellitus” [Title/Abstract])) AND ((“telemedicine” [Title/Abstract] OR “mHealth”[Title/Abstract] OR “Telecare” [Title/Abstract] OR “mobile phone” [Title/Abstract] OR “smartphone” [Title/Abstract] OR “telehealth” [Title/Abstract] OR “eHealth” [Title/Abstract] OR “internet” [Title/Abstract] OR “mobile app” [Title/Abstract] OR “Telemonitoring” [Title/Abstract] OR “e-health” [Title/Abstract] OR “smart phone” [Title/Abstract]) AND (“improvement” [Title/Abstract] OR “healthcare utilization” [Title/Abstract] OR “clinical outcomes” [Title/Abstract] OR “Glycemic Control” [Title/Abstract] OR “HbA1c”[Title/Abstract] OR “self-management” [Title/Abstract]))) AND (“Saudi Arabia” OR “KSA”); ScienceDirect: (“Saudi Arabia OR diabetes”) AND (“T2DM” OR “diabetes mellitus”) AND (“mobile apps” OR “telemedicine” OR “EHR”) AND (“Glycemic Control” OR “HbA1c” OR “self-management”); and Web of Science: (“Diabetes Mellitus Type 1” OR “Diabetes Mellitus Type 2” OR “T2DM” OR “diabetes” OR “diabetes mellitus”) AND (“Saudi Arabia” OR “KSA”) AND (“informatics” OR “telemedicine” OR “mobile phone” OR “telehealth” OR “eHealth” OR “internet” OR “medical informatic applications” OR “technology”) AND (“satisfaction” OR “Glycemic Control” OR “HbA1c” OR “self-management” OR “quality of life”).

Screening and extraction

The total number of articles identified through the searches was: 49 conducted in PubMed, 46 from Web of Science, and 32 from ScienceDirect. Initially, articles with irrelevant titles or abstracts were excluded. The total number of excluded articles was 117. Finally, nine studies comprising a total of 5626 patients were included. In the subsequent phase, the full text of papers was meticulously reviewed to determine their compliance with the inclusion criteria. To streamline the process, titles and abstracts were organized, assessed, and scrutinized for any duplicate entries using reference management software (EndNote X8). To ensure the highest quality of selection, a dual screening approach, involving screening for the evaluation of titles and abstracts and another for the comprehensive examination of the entire texts, was adopted. Once all relevant articles were identified, a structured extraction sheet was created to capture pertinent information aligned with our specific objectives. Two researchers conducted the data extraction process independently.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for nonrandomized studies, including retrospective cohort and cross-sectional designs. One study included was a quasi-experimental study evaluated using the NOS. Scores were interpreted as low quality if they received 0–3 stars, moderate quality if they received 4-6 stars, and high quality if they received 7–9 stars.[17] For RCTs, we had two investigators independently use the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 1[18] to appraise the quality of the studies. This tool provided a systematic approach for evaluating various aspects of study quality, including selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, and reporting bias. Each study was carefully reviewed to determine whether the authors had taken adequate measures to minimize these biases.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the findings of the included studies. Tables presenting quality assessment results, baseline characteristics of selected studies, and study outcomes were included.

Results

Search results

We executed the search methodologies outlined previously, which resulted in the identification of 127 citations. Twenty duplicate studies were removed. After screening titles and abstracts, 107 citations met the eligibility criteria for further consideration. Through full-text screening, this number was further refined to nine articles[192021222324252627] that met the inclusion criteria outlined in the methodology section. Baseline characteristics of included studies

The 12 included studies were conducted in Saudi Arabia between 2015 and 2025. These studies exhibited diverse methodological designs, including retrospective, prospective, cross-sectional, and randomized trials. The sample size was a total of 5626 participants, reflecting notable differences in scale and scope. Most studies focused on individuals with T2DM; only one study included both type 1 and type 2 diabetes populations. The study characteristics extracted included publication year, study setting, design, duration, total number of participants, mean age, gender distribution, type of intervention/technology used, and type of diabetes. This range of designs and participant demographics reflects the varied approaches to diabetes care across different healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia.

The IT-based interventions used in the included studies incorporated various combinations of data transmission, storage, and communication features. Specifically, four studies employed self-management systems;[19202122] and five studies utilized electronic health records.[2324252627] This review identified two broad categories of diabetes management technologies. The first category comprised electronic self-management systems (apps, SMS, web-based platforms) in which patients used smartphones or glucometer-connected devices to upload their glucose readings, which were then transmitted directly to clinicians for monitoring and intervention. The third category, electronic medical records (EMRs)/telemedicine platforms, involved remote consultations and virtual follow-ups, offering continuous support for diabetes management regardless of geographical constraints [Table 4].

Electronic self-management systems (apps, short message service, web-based platforms)

Four studies used electronic self-management tools involving SMS, mobile phones, and telemedicine. For instance, Bin Abbas et al.,[19] reported a reduction in HbA1c from 9.9% ±1.8% to 9.5% ±1.7%. Knowledge scores improved from 46.20 ± 9.62 to 61.10 ± 7.43 (P = 0.0013), and FBS levels fell from 8.60 ± 3.16 to 7.76 ± 3 mmol/L (P = 0.001). Furthermore, Sani et al.,[22] observed a significant improvement in the level of diabetes knowledge following the JILSE program. Alotaibi et al.,[20] found HbA1c reduction from 8.76 ± 0.7 to 7.85 ± 0.70 (P = 0.012), while the control group showed no significant change. The SAED system used in the study was found to be effective and cost-efficient. Similarly, Alanzi et al.,[21] reported a reduction in HbA1c from 8.14 ± 1.20 to 7.54 ± 0.96 (P = 0.000), with no significant improvement in the control group. The SANAD system was deemed acceptable and feasible in diabetes care.

Electronic medical records/telemedicine platforms

Five studies used EMRs either as standalone systems or integrated with web-based personal health records. For instance, Tourkmani et al.,[26] reported HbA1c reduction from 9.98 ± 1.33 to 8.32 ± 1.31 (mean difference = 1.66 ± 1.29; confidence interval (CI) = 1.43–1.88; P < 0.001). The average diabetes duration of participants was 14 ± 7 years. Similarly, Almalki et al.,[25] found that 62.7% of the patients in the telemedicine group achieved satisfactory glycemic control compared to 32.1% in the in-person care group (adjusted odds ratio = 5.123, 95% CI = 3.107–8.447). The study emphasized the potential of telemedicine in chronic disease management.

Many studies also assessed patient satisfaction, particularly regarding the usability of interventions, such as mobile apps or telemedicine platforms. For instance, Almalki et al.,[25] found improved adherence rates with telemedicine.

Other outcomes, such as cost-effectiveness, were also addressed. For instance, Faleh et al.,[23] compared the costs of telemedicine care versus traditional methods and concluded that telemedicine was cost-effective for managing T2DM. Almarzooq et al.,[27] analyzed the outcome of diabetes distress.

Discussion

This systematic review investigated how thriving IT-based interventions improved glycemic control in patients with DM in Saudi Arabia. There is growing interest in implementing digital health tools to support the management of chronic diseases owing to the rising prevalence of diabetes worldwide. Saudi Arabia has one of the highest rates of diabetes prevalence in the world.[282930] This review aimed at examining the effectiveness of IT-based interventions, such as mobile applications, web-based systems, telemedicine platforms, and electronic self-management tools, by synthesizing data from recent studies conducted in Saudi Arabia.

Twelve studies were included, comprising cross-sectional surveys, cohort studies, quasi-experimental studies, and RCTs. These studies evaluated the use of various digital technologies by patients and healthcare providers in managing T2DM and some type 1 diabetes.

There was compelling evidence from all the studies examined that digital health tools significantly improved glycemic control, as indicated by the lower HbA1c levels. Several studies have documented increases in patients’ self-efficacy, diabetes knowledge, treatment adherence, and satisfaction with care, suggesting that these technologies do more than merely reduce HbA1c levels of participants exposed to IT-based interventions, which is one of the most consistent findings. Studies by Alanzi et al.,[21] Tourkmani et al.,[26] Faleh AlMutairi et al.,[23] Almalki et al.,[25] and Alotaibi et al.,[20] showed statistically significant HbA1c improvements following the use of telemedicine consultations and mobile-based self-management tools. Al-Mutairi et al., highlighted the utility of telemedicine in helping T2DM patients maintain glycemic control during the COVID-19 pandemic.[24] These findings are especially significant because persistent reductions in HbA1c are directly linked to lower risks of long-term complications such as cardiovascular events, retinopathy, and nephropathy.[531] By reducing diabetes-related morbidity and healthcare costs, digital tools can help achieve broader public health goals.[3233]

In addition to glycemic control, many reviewed studies have reported secondary benefits of IT interventions. For instance, according to multiple studies, digital platforms improved patients’ comprehension of diabetes care. Trials using self-management applications such as the SANAD, SAED, and JILSE systems, which also had interactive and educational elements, showed improvements in diabetes knowledge scores. Studies by Alanzi et al., and Sani et al.,[2122] linked these components with improved diabetes knowledge, higher self-efficacy, increased physical activity, and healthier dietary behaviors. Such behavioral changes are essential for long-term disease management and demonstrate the potential of IT tools to address both the clinical and psychological aspects of chronic illness care.

Patient acceptance and satisfaction, also frequently assessed in all the studies included, were generally favorable. For example, studies by Almalki et al.,[25] found that patients viewed web-based and telemedicine interventions as practical, efficient, and convenient. The participants conveyed gratitude for improved communication with healthcare providers, expedited access to care, and decreased travel requirements. These findings suggest that digital tools may enhance not only clinical outcomes but also the overall patient experience, which is an essential element in the quality of healthcare.[343536]

The results of this review are generally consistent with evidence from around the world. The effectiveness of IT-based interventions, especially web-based tools and mobile applications, in lowering HbA1c and enhancing self-care practices of diabetic patients has been demonstrated by numerous international meta-analyses and systematic reviews.[16333738394041] In countries such as the USA, Europe, and parts of Asia, mobile-based self-monitoring, real-time feedback systems, and teleconsultation services have consistently shown positive outcomes.[424344] However, in comparison to those utilized in other high-income nations, the instruments utilized in Saudi Arabia, as demonstrated by the studies in this review, were typically less sophisticated and simpler.

A joint paper by the American Diabetes Association and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes states that digital health apps for diabetes management are put into six broad categories.[44]

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses, with a majority from high-income countries, confirm that diabetes can be effectively managed with mobile health apps with robust technological features that improve diabetes outcomes.[1633] This underscores the need to improve digital health infrastructure and digital literacy in the region. Nevertheless, this review shows that even basic tools such as SMS reminders, electronic medical records, and simple mobile applications can yield significant benefits when properly used. This is particularly relevant in healthcare systems like Saudi Arabia’s, where access to specialty care in rural areas remains a challenge. Mobile phone penetration and growing Internet use support the scalability and feasibility of digital interventions.

According to Johnston et al., a review of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) found that they are particularly challenged by diabetes, with fewer patients achieving control of their condition, a younger average age of onset, and increasing prevalence rates, coupled with healthcare system shortages. Furthermore, the study findings suggested that the mHealth intervention was associated with greater reductions in HbA1c, fewer episodes of hypoglycemia, improvedFBS, greater proportions of people achieving target HbA1c, improved LDL, improvements in both adherence to medication and blood glucose testing, and improvements in overall self-care behavior.[45] Furthermore, mHealth interventions in LMICs are associated with clinically significant effectiveness on HbA1c, but as reported by Mokaya et al., have low effectiveness on FBG.[46]

In terms of strength of evidence, most included studies were of moderate-to-high methodological quality. The use of validated outcomes (HbA1c and structured diabetes knowledge tests) increased reliability. While several studies employed pre–post-intervention comparisons, which are less rigorous than RCTs, they still provided valuable insights. Despite being few with small samples, RCTs offered stronger evidence of the effectiveness of digital tools. In addition, Faleh et al.,[23] integrated large retrospective cohort and evaluation of cost-effectiveness, contributing comprehensive information on healthcare utilization and economic impact.

Limitations

While the findings support IT-based management of diabetes, several limitations must be acknowledged. Some studies had small samples, lacked control groups, and had short follow-up durations. Variations in intervention types, delivery methods, and outcome measures limit direct comparisons. The absence of blinding and randomization in some studies raises potential biases. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data for outcomes such as adherence, knowledge, or satisfaction introduces possible social desirability and recall bias. Although a comprehensive database search was conducted, exclusion of grey literature and non-English studies may have introduced publication bias. This review was also restricted to research carried out in Saudi Arabia, which improved contextual relevance but limited generalizability.

Future directions

Despite the valuable guidance for future practice and study offered by the review, large RCTs with longer follow-up periods are necessary to establish the long-term effect of IT-based interventions on glycemic control and other outcomes. Standardized intervention protocols and outcome measures are essential to enable comparison and evidence aggregation and cross-study comparisons. Further research is needed to identify strategies for sustained user engagement and digital adherence, especially in underserved regions or in populations with limited digital literacy.

Expanding research to include diverse populations, such as rural residents, older adults, and individuals with low digital proficiency, is essential. Qualitative studies exploring patient and provider experiences can inform culturally appropriate and user-friendly interventions. Integrating IT-based interventions into current healthcare systems and EHRs is a crucial area for further research. Better data continuity, less service duplication, and more individualized and coordinated care are all made possible with seamless integration. Assessing these integrated systems’ cost-effectiveness, scalability, and interoperability will be essential for directing investment and policy choices.

Given the growing recognition of the psychological impact of diabetes, future interventions should consider incorporating counselling or mental health support. Evidence from Almarzooq et al., suggests that addressing diabetes-related distress through digital tools could further improve outcomes and patient satisfaction.[28]

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the significant potential of IT-based interventions in improving glycemic control and self-management of diabetic patients in Saudi Arabia. Despite some limitations in study design and reporting, the overall findings support the integration of digital health tools into standard diabetes care. These interventions align with Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 goals of healthcare modernization and digital transformation. To reach its full potential, more research-focused funding and inclusive health policies and infrastructure improvements are essential to ensure equitable access and long-term adoption.
