Comparative performance of large language models in answering cornea and cataract surgery questions for resident training
Authors: Seonghwan Kim, Man Ji, Chung Young Kim, Young In Yun, Gahye Lee, Chang Ho Yoon, Mee Kum Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe application of large language models (LLMs) in the medical field has gained increasing popularity; however, their effectiveness in ophthalmology remains uncertain. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of responses generated by various deep learning-based LLMs to questions on cataract and corneal diseases and surgeries, and to verify their educational effectiveness by comparing the performances of LLMs with those provided by ophthalmology fellows and residents.MethodsEighty-one multiple-choice questions on corneal diseases and cataract surgeries were developed based on the standard format of the Korean ophthalmology board examination and categorized into three subtypes: recall-type (n = 27), interpretation-type (n = 27), and problem-solving-type (n = 27). The accuracy and appropriateness of commonly used LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5) were evaluated and compared with one another, and against the performances of three ophthalmology residents and three corneal fellows.ResultsAmong the four LLMs, ChatGPT-5 demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (75/81; 92.59%), followed by Gemini 3.0 Pro (73/81; 90.12%) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (73/81; 90.12%), all of which outperformed ChatGPT-4o (70/81; 86.42%) as well as ophthalmology fellows (86.42 ± 1.23%) and residents (82.30 ± 8.22%). For recall-type questions, ChatGPT-5 achieved the highest accuracy (92.59%), and the other three LLMs (85.19%) outperformed both fellows (82.72 ± 4.28%) and residents (71.60 ± 17.50%). In interpretation-type questions, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved perfect scores (100%), while ChatGPT-4o (92.59%) performed comparably to fellows (92.59 ± 7.41%) and better than residents (87.65 ± 4.28%). However, in problem-solving-type questions, all LLMs scored relatively lower (ChatGPT-5: 85.19%; others: 81.48%) than the mean performance of fellows (87.65 ± 11.91%).ConclusionsAmong the LLMs, ChatGPT-5 demonstrated the highest overall performance; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the models. Compared with ophthalmology trainees, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved significantly higher overall scores than the mean trainee performance. Although LLMs showed good performance in recall- and interpretation-type questions, their relatively lower accuracy in problem-solving-type questions suggests that further advances in LLMs are necessary for their reliable use as educational tools in ophthalmology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04767-z.

Introduction

Since the 1950s, research in natural language processing (NLP) has been widely conducted [1]. Recently, with advancements in neural networks, deep learning-based large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, have emerged [2, 3]. LLMs are capable of NLP tasks including language translation, text generation, answering questions, etc [3]. The capabilities of LLMs enable their application across various fields such as biomedical and healthcare, education, social and business, leading to increased real-world use and significantly impacting society [4–6].

Recently, various studies regarding LLMs have been reported. LLMs were evaluated for extracting information from unstructured clinical notes in electronic medical records and have potential for clinical use [7]. In addition, AI-simulated medical history conversations using LLMs could have a potential use in medical student training [8]. Furthermore, research has also been reported on the effectiveness and accuracy of LLMs on medical board examinations across departments including internal medicine, anesthesiology, rhinology, and ophthalmology, and the performance level was quite high to pass the examination [9–15].

In the field of ophthalmology, various studies have been conducted on the use of LLMs, including the development of educational materials in childhood glaucoma [16], assistance in diagnosing glaucoma [17] and answering frequent questionnaires from patients [18]. LLMs have a potential to be a valuable learning tool for students and ophthalmology residents. However, no previous study has evaluated the performance of widely used LLMs using Korean ophthalmology board-style questions specifically focused on corneal diseases and cataract surgery. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the ability of commonly used LLMs to answer Korean ophthalmology board-style questions and to compare their performance with ophthalmology residents and cornea fellows.

Methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2412-033-1593), and it adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants in the study were provided with informed consent.

LLMs and participants

Four LLMs—ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5—were evaluated. Six volunteers (three ophthalmology residents and three cornea fellows) were recruited between November 1 and November 15, 2025.

Ophthalmology board-style questions

Corneal disease- and cataract surgery-related multiple-choice questions were modified from ophthalmology board-style questions. Multimodal questions containing both images and text were excluded due to limitations in standardized evaluation and potential variability in multimodal reasoning. Answers were verified based on standard ophthalmology reference textbooks, including Cornea (Mannis and Holland, 5th edition) [19] and Steinert’s Cataract Surgery (Garg and Koch, 4th edition) [20]. All questions and answers were independently reviewed and validated by three cornea specialists (SK, CHY, and MKK). Ambiguous questions were removed or revised to ensure clarity and definitive responses. A total of 81 questions were included: 27 recall-type, 27 interpretation-type, and 27 problem-solving-type questions. The questionnaire and corresponding answers are provided in Supplementary File 1.

All questions were translated into English and input into the LLMs on December 1, 2025 (Fig. 1). Each question was asked once for each LLM model. To minimize potential carryover effects between questions, a new chat session was initiated for each question. All questions were entered into each LLM using a standardized prompt: “Please answer the following ophthalmology board-style multiple-choice question. Select the single best answer from the options provided.” No additional contextual examples or explanations were provided. Ophthalmology residents and cornea fellows answered the original Korean-language questions. For LLM evaluation, both the original Korean and English-translated versions of the questions were tested, and identical responses were observed across languages for all questions in all four LLMs. Therefore, the English-translated version was used for the primary analysis to maintain standardized input conditions. Overall accuracy and subtype-specific accuracy were compared among LLMs and between LLMs and human participants.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test and post hoc tests with Bonferroni adjustment were used to compare the performance of LLMs regarding total scores and scores by each question type. A one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted to assess whether there was a significant difference between the scores obtained from each LLM model and those of fellows and residents in total scores and scores by question type. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 software (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the four LLMs, ChatGPT-5 demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (75/81; 92.59%), followed by Gemini 3.0 Pro (73/81; 90.12%) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (73/81; 90.12%), and ChatGPT-4o (70/81; 86.42%) (Fig. 2). However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the LLMs in overall performance.

ChatGPT-5 demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (75/81; 92.59%), followed by Gemini 3.0 Pro (73/81; 90.12%), Claude Sonnet 4.5 (73/81; 90.12%), and ChatGPT-4o (70/81; 86.42%). In recall-type questions, ChatGPT-5 achieved the highest accuracy (25/27; 92.59%), while ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 each scored 23/27 (85.19%) (Fig. 2). No significant differences were found among the LLMs in recall-type performance.

In interpretation-type questions, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved perfect scores (27/27; 100%), whereas ChatGPT-4o scored 25/27 (92.59%) (Fig. 2). The differences in interpretation-type performance were not statistically significant.

In problem-solving-type questions, ChatGPT-5 scored 23/27 (85.19%), while ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 each scored 22/27 (81.48%) (Fig. 2). No statistically significant differences were found among the LLMs in problem-solving-type performance.

ChatGPT-5 (92.59%), Gemini 3.0 Pro (90.12%), and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (90.12%) achieved higher overall accuracy than both residents (82.30 ± 8.22%) and fellows (86.42 ± 1.23%), whereas ChatGPT-4o (86.42%) demonstrated performance comparable to fellows and superior to residents (Table 1). In recall-type questions, ChatGPT-5 achieved 92.59% accuracy and the other LLMs achieved 85.19%, all outperforming residents (71.60 ± 17.50%) and fellows (82.72 ± 4.28%) (Table 1). For interpretation-type questions, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved perfect scores (100%), exceeding both residents (87.65 ± 4.28%) and fellows (92.59 ± 7.41%), while ChatGPT-4o (92.59%) performed comparable to fellows (Table 1). However, in problem-solving-type questions, ChatGPT-5 scored 85.19% and the other LLMs scored 81.48%, all showing lower performance than average fellows (87.65 ± 11.91%) and comparable performance to residents (83.95 ± 2.14%) (Table 1). When comparing the performances of LLMs with overall trainees, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 showed significantly higher overall scores than the mean performance of all trainees (ChatGPT-5 vs. all trainees, p = 0.031; Gemini 3.0 Pro vs. all trainees, p = 0.031; Claude Sonnet 4.5 vs. all trainees, p = 0.031), whereas ChatGPT-4o did not show a statistically significant difference (Table 1).

In recall-type questions, ChatGPT-5 significantly outperformed all trainees (p = 0.031), while ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 did not show significant differences compared with all trainees (Table 1). In interpretation-type questions, ChatGPT-5 (p = 0.031), Gemini 3.0 Pro (p = 0.031), and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (p = 0.031) achieved significantly higher scores than all trainees, whereas ChatGPT-4o did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of four widely used LLMs—ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5—on Korean ophthalmology board-style multiple-choice questions related to corneal diseases and cataract surgery and compared their performance with that of ophthalmology residents and cornea fellows. Overall, ChatGPT-5 achieved the highest accuracy, followed by Gemini 3.0 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5, while ChatGPT-4o demonstrated performance comparable to cornea fellows. Although the differences among LLMs did not reach statistical significance based on Fisher’s exact test, comparisons with human trainees indicated that ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved significantly higher overall scores than the mean trainee performance, suggesting potential educational value of current LLMs in ophthalmology training.

Across question subtypes, LLMs showed characteristic strengths and limitations. In recall-type questions, all LLMs outperformed residents and fellows, and ChatGPT-5 showed a significantly higher score than trainees. This finding suggests that LLMs can effectively retrieve and apply textbook-based factual knowledge, supporting their role as supplementary tools for reinforcing foundational learning. In interpretation-type questions, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved perfect scores and significantly outperformed trainees, while ChatGPT-4o performed comparably to fellows. These results indicate that current LLMs may provide clinically meaningful support in structured case-based reasoning tasks, such as identifying the most appropriate diagnosis or selecting standard management approaches when sufficient clinical information is provided.

However, in problem-solving-type questions, LLM performance was relatively lower compared with their performance in other question formats. Notably, none of the LLMs significantly outperformed trainees in this question subtype, and all LLMs demonstrated lower scores than the average performance of cornea fellows. This pattern may reflect the limitations of current LLMs in advanced decision-making tasks that require nuanced prioritization, procedural judgment, or experience-based reasoning beyond guideline-level knowledge. Therefore, while LLMs may be useful as educational tools, they should be used cautiously in resident training, particularly when addressing complex clinical decision-making cases.

The present findings are consistent with previous studies showing that LLMs can achieve high accuracy on medical licensing or board-style examinations, while still exhibiting limitations in complex reasoning and reliability [21–23]. Kung et al. [21] evaluated the performance of ChatGPT on the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) and demonstrated that ChatGPT can achieve accuracy levels approaching the passing threshold across all steps of the exam, and showed potential for medical education. On the other hand, Liu et al. [22] reported the limitations of LLMs, particularly in consistency and adaptability across diverse examination settings. Our findings, consistent with prior studies, highlighted both the educational potential of LLMs and their limitations in addressing problem-solving-type questions requiring advanced clinical reasoning. Wu et al. [23] conducted a meta-analysis evaluating the accuracy of LLMs in answering ophthalmology board-style questions, and reported that ChatGPT-4 achieved the highest overall accuracy (0.74) compared to other models such as ChatGPT-3.5, Bard, and Bing Chat. In contrast to previous studies that primarily focused on ChatGPT, the present study evaluated a broader range of actively used LLMs, including Gemini 3.0 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5. Furthermore, this is the first study to specifically assess LLM performance on board-style questions related to corneal diseases and cataract surgery, offering novel insights into the applicability of LLMs in subspecialty-level ophthalmology education.

In the present study, both Korean and English versions of the questions were tested in the LLMs, and identical responses were obtained across the two language inputs in all LLMs. This finding suggests that the LLMs demonstrated consistent reasoning performance across languages, indicating that language differences may have minimal influence on the model performance. This observation aligns with previous studies that LLMs exhibit strong multilingual understanding and reasoning capabilities across languages [24, 25].

This study has several limitations. The number of questions was limited, and since the questions were designed to be straightforward and unambiguous, potentially inflating accuracy. Additionally, multimodal questions involving clinical images were excluded, despite the fact that image interpretation constitutes a major component of ophthalmology training and board examinations. This limitation may reduce the real-world representativeness of the evaluation. Moreover, although LLMs showed strong performance in recall-type and interpretation-type questions, their relatively lower accuracy in problem-solving-type questions suggests limitations in complex clinical reasoning. Therefore, current LLMs may be more appropriately regarded as advanced clinical decision support systems rather than autonomous clinical agents. Future studies incorporating multimodal LLMs capable of image interpretation may provide a more realistic evaluation of their clinical applicability. Furthermore, the study was conducted with voluntarily recruited ophthalmology residents and cornea fellows, and the limited number of participants may reduce the representativeness of the broader resident and fellow population. A larger-scale study involving more residents and fellows would be needed to enhance the representativeness and robustness of the results. Another limitation of the present study is that each question was asked only once per model. Previous studies have suggested that prompt design and repeated querying can influence the stability and reliability of LLM-generated responses [26, 27]. Future studies incorporating repeated queries and internal consistency analyses would help better evaluate the reliability of LLM outputs. In addition, the present study did not use in-context learning strategies, such as providing example clinical cases before the evaluation. Future research should investigate whether in-context learning can improve LLM performance in complex problem-solving tasks in ophthalmology education. LLMs sometimes generate incorrect answers and false information to fit their responses. With advancements of LLMs in the near future, they may potentially be able to solve multimodal ophthalmology board-style questions more accurately.

In conclusion, while ChatGPT-5 achieved the highest overall performance, no statistically significant differences were found among the LLMs when answering board-style questions on corneal diseases and cataract surgery. Notably, ChatGPT-5, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved significantly higher overall scores than the mean ophthalmology trainee performance. Although LLMs performed particularly well in recall-type and interpretation-type questions, their relatively lower performance in problem-solving-type questions highlights limitations and suggests that further advances are necessary before these models can be reliably integrated as comprehensive tools for ophthalmology resident education.
