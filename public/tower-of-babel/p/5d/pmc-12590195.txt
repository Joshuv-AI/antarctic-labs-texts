The reality of neonatal nursing work in Kenya and implications for quality and safety: Direct observation of tasks and time utilisation
Authors: Onesmus Onyango, Vincent A Kagonya, Michuki Maina, Kenneth Karumba, Abdulazeez Imam, Sebastian S Fuller, David Gathara, Mike English, the HIGH-Q Hospital Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about how nurses working in care settings affected by remarkably high workloads and workforce constraints manage their work and time to deliver needed care.ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterise nursing workflows and examine time allocated to care in high mortality, intermediate care Kenyan neonatal units.Design and methodsUsing ‘shadowing’ as a time and motion technique, we directly observed 1–2 nurses per 12-hour shift in 8 Kenyan county hospitals. We used an Activity Log Sheet to document their activities during the shift and thematic content analysis of observation notes to classify and group tasks performed. In a second phase, we documented the time spent on specific critical tasks and instances of multi-tasking and care interruptions.ResultsWe directly observed 499 person-hours over 36 day shifts and 15 night shifts. A typical day and night shift had a median of 38 (25–47) and 32 (18–44) babies respectively with a median nursing hours per-patient per-shift of 0.9 (0.5–1.2) hours. We recorded 1891 task episodes comprised of 36 different tasks that we grouped into eight workflow domains. Most tasks involved: direct patient care (37 %) or indirect patient care (23 %) with communications, documentation and reporting, staff or student supervision and mentorship, interruptions, personal breaks, and rest less frequently performed tasks. Nurses commonly devoted up to 20 minutes even to critical tasks while continuing to multi-task and managing more than 2 instances of interruptions during a newborn caring task.ConclusionsKenyan neonatal nurses perform direct and indirect patient caring tasks while grappling with demanding housekeeping, administrative, and clinical teaching and mentorship roles under extremely limited time availability. Time allocated even to complex tasks is minimal and rarely given full focus, threatening patient safety. Our findings highlight opportunities to redistribute basic non-clinical roles for enhanced patient and caregiver experience. Even so, nurse staffing must be substantially improved.Keywords: Workflow, Neonatal, Nursing care, Task performance, Time

Background

Workload, patient acuity, and complexity of individual patients' conditions impact nursing care processes. To account for this, norms and standards for human resources emphasise optimal nurse–patient ratios for different service delivery settings (Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation, 2023, Fernandez-Tunas et al., 2020, World Health Organization, 2020). However, even these norms and standards tend to overlook time needed for administrative work or clinical mentorship roles that are equally essential for smooth running of a hospital unit. Service delivery is also affected by the interconnectedness of patient turnover, shift type, shift times, nurses' experience and competencies, the availability and functionality of equipment, adequacy of consumables and availability of assistive personnel, many of which are incredibly challenging in low and middle-income countries. The Practice Environment Scale of Nursing Work Index (Swiger et al., 2017; Warshawsky and Havens, 2011) has been used to explore differences in nursing care contexts. In our recent work, we developed and used a Nursing Care Index (Gathara et al., 2020, Gathara et al., 2018) that helps to directly quantify bedside nursing care delivery. However, neither of these approaches captures the detail nor potential complexity of an individual neonatal nurse's work. A better understanding of what tasks nurses perform in context is important in shaping how roles are assigned, and shift time utilised for efficient and effective service delivery. Also, helps safeguard staff and patient safety in the case of role creeps (McKenna et al., 2004; Mijovic et al., 2016), moral distress (Salas-Bergues et al., 2024; Mewborn et al., 2023; Boone et al., 2023), task misalignment or workplace constraints among others. Besides, as the nursing discipline becomes highly specialised, there is need to continuously review current scope while reflecting on new frontiers of nursing practice amidst a space where roles are being redefined through task-sharing/shifting and advancements in healthcare technologies (Bauer and Bodenheimer, 2017).

Developing deeper insights into work processes and time utilisation in routine hospital care settings can help examine and optimise efficiency (Kalne and Mehendale, 2022; Lopetegui et al., 2014; Pelletier and Duffield, 2003), or evaluate, standardise and improve workflows (Littler, 1978; Taylor, 2004). For instance, time and motion studies have been used to examine work in intensive care units and adult inpatient care (Butler et al., 2018; Dall'Ora et al., 2021; Michel et al., 2021; Yen et al., 2018), pharmacy (Gjone et al., 2022; Negaard et al., 2020), and emergency departments (Bakhoum et al., 2021), as well as long-term care facilities for geriatrics (Chen et al., 2021; Mallidou et al., 2013; McCloskey et al., 2015). However, most such studies are from well-resourced settings (Dall'Ora et al., 2021; Mathys et al., 2018; Michel et al., 2021) where staffing ratios and resourcing are much better than in low-income settings (Narayanasamy et al., 2018; Wani et al., 2021).

In our previous work in busy Kenyan neonatal units with high crude mortality rates (about 15 %) (Tuti et al., 2022; Irimu et al., 2021), compared to < 1 % crude mortality rates in high-income countries (UNICEF, 2025), we have shown that when nurses care for high numbers of sick babies they typically complete less than 40 % of bedside nursing care tasks (Imam et al., 2025; Kagonya et al., 2025). In this work we explore in greater depth, the reality of delivering nursing care (including type of shift activities, interruptions, and multitasking), and the potential implications for quality and safety, including the adoption of workforce enhancement approaches in Kenyan public hospitals' neonatal units (Gathara et al., 2020; Imam et al., 2022).

Methods

This study addresses two objectives. First, to comprehensively characterise all nursing workflows and related activities in a high-mortality, low-resourced neonatal care setting. Secondly, to examine nurses' time and task allocations in a typical shift. Thirdly, to reflect on the care quality and patient safety implications for shift time allocation and utilisation in intermediate care, low-resourced Kenyan neonatal units.

Study design

We used ‘shadowing’ (Czarniawska-Joerges, 2007) as a direct observation approach to examine neonatal nurses' activities in Kenyan neonatal units. Although direct observations may suffer from the Hawthorne effect and cost implications, we reasoned this approach best suited our aims rather than self-reports that often add additional recall burden on respondents or suffer from recall and social desirability biases (Abt et al., 2022; Baker et al., 2019; Kalne and Mehendale, 2022).

Study setting

This study was nested within the larger Harnessing Innovation in Global Health for Quality Care project described in detail elsewhere (Imam et al., 2022; Maina et al., 2025). Briefly, the study evaluated delivery of bedside nursing care using a Nursing Care Index checklist in eight Kenyan neonatal units and examined the effect of workforce enhancement interventions in 4 of these neonatal units. All eight neonatal units were within public county-level referral hospitals being upgraded to provide intermediate level neonatal care, for instance, respiratory support with continuous positive airway pressure that does not include mechanical ventilation. Typically, these neonatal units have incredibly low nurse-to-patient ratios of 1:15–40. The hospitals are spread in both urban and rural counties.

The within the larger study, workforce enhancements comprised a two-phased intervention. In Phase 1, three additional nurses were introduced to the neonatal unit in four hospitals. In phase II, seven months later, three additional ward (care) assistants (Kagonya et al., 2023) were introduced in the same neonatal units to provide auxiliary support to the nursing staff. The data collected for this study spanned observations in Phase 1 and Phase II, but the focus of this study was characterising neonatal nursing work throughout a typical (day and night) shift, it did not aim to examine the effect of the interventions themselves.

Research assistants

The study recruited eight observers, with one assigned to each neonatal unit. The observers were healthcare professionals with background training in nutrition and familiar with the hospital setting and common newborn care activities. The observers underwent a one-week training program on direct observation methods and note-taking techniques prior to data collection. They were also required to spend one week familiarising themselves with the hospital environment they were assigned to, the neonatal units' staff, and neonatal units processes before commencing actual observations. This period also allowed the neonatal units staff to become acquainted with the observers and develop rapport, helping nurses become at ease with the observations. Furthermore, this enabled the study team to gain support for the research, obtain consent from nurses and caregivers as well as mitigate the potential for Hawthorne effect.

Shifts sampling and participants selection

We randomly selected shadowing shifts within a 6-weeks period, and this comprised of day and night shifts, including weekends. Typically, only 1–2 nurses worked on each shift in these settings and therefore 1–2 nurses on active duty were shadowed per shift (and labelled as Nurse 1 or Nurse 2 to maintain confidentiality). Only the nurses' who had provided an informed consent for the direct observation were ‘shadowed’ during the selected shift.

The study team observed forty-six (46) shifts - 31-day shifts and 15-night shifts. Thirty-three (33) shifts were observed in Phase 1 while thirteen (13) occurred in Phase II. This comprised 499 person-hours shadowing nurses and a total of 1891 task episodes (Table 1).

Data collection

For each shift observed we recorded the number of nurses on the shift, the number of sick newborns in the units and the types of interventions each baby was receiving. Observers took breaks same time as the nurses.

Data were collected from the eight neonatal units in two phases. In Phase I, data were collected from 8 hospitals, and the shadowing focused on mapping discrete nursing activities/tasks as they occurred. This Phase did not investigate the time spent on tasks but rather the range and frequency of activities. In Phase II, the observations were only carried out in 4 hospitals that had received a very modest increase in nursing and assistive personnel (Imam et al., 2022). Data collection in Phase II focused on how nurses allocated their time.

Direct observation and documentation of activities focused on the nurses on duty, commencing at the beginning of the shift (typically with a handover process) and concluding at the end of the shift (the subsequent handover). In all the sites, long day shifts commenced at 0730 h and terminated at 1730–1830 h when night shifts began that ended at 0730 h. In some settings, shorter morning and afternoon shifts complemented these and are included as part of data on day shifts.

In Phase 1, the observers used a notebook to record nurses' shift-level activities narratively as precisely as possible without immediate categorisation or labelling. They paid special attention to changes in tasks such as progression from handover to bedside tasks (e.g. administering medications, checking vital signs). Narrative notes were then transferred onto a nurses' Activity Log Sheet (Supplementary material Table 1) with oversight of the corresponding authors who are professional nurses. This helped promote standardisation of terms used across sites. Subsequent classification of activities into named tasks was reserved for the analysis stage.

In Phase II, the shadowing approach was refined so that observers focused on how nurses allocated time to specific activities/tasks. Timestamps (start and stop times) were applied on a revised Activity Log Sheet (Supplementary material Table 1) while notes were also kept of instances of multi-tasking (where the main task continued while the nurse engaged in a second task simultaneously), interruptions (where the main task was briefly paused but then continued) and task switching (transitioning fully from one task and starting another). If a nurse switched from one task to another, the first activity was considered to have stopped. This approach estimated the median time spent on a task type, which may have involved multiple babies or mothers before the nurse transitioned to a different activity. The time measured is not therefore, equivalent to the time required to complete a specific task optimally such as resuscitation or give intravenous medication to a baby.

Data curation, analysis and synthesis

The free-text (observation) notes, captured on the activity log sheets were then imported into NVIVO 12 (Lumivero International, 2023) for thematic coding and content analysis. Initial inductive coding generated a long list of 36 specific activities/tasks informed by both the authors' professional experience and prior literature (Abbey et al., 2012; Baker et al., 2019; Bakhoum et al., 2021; Myny et al., 2011). These were then grouped into eight broad categories or task domains, also guided by authors' subject matter expertise and synthesis of wider literature. Using the 36 specific task labels we determined the frequency that they were performed. Our report, therefore, covers which of the 8 task domains encompassed the most frequently conducted activities. Noting that this measure of frequency provides an indication of which tasks or activity domains nurses in these units are mostly involved in.

From Phase II of the study, the timestamps were then used to identify how long a nurse spent conducting a specific task. This time measure included time spent multi-tasking amidst (unavoidable) interruptions before the nurse could complete or switch tasks. Next, the shift statistics from activity log sheets were transferred to MS Excel and R software version 4.4.1 (The Comprehensive R. Archive Network, 2024) for descriptive analysis. We report the median activity/task duration noting that this measure might span performance of the same task type for one or several babies or their mothers. For example, time spent on measuring vital signs, medication administration or feeding activities may often involving several babies. We illustrate the frequency or likelihood of multi-tasking and interruptions during an activity qualitatively based on examination of our narrative accounts and provide an example, in Box 1, an abbreviated narrative account to further illustrate the reality of nursing work in these neonatal units.

Lastly, from the shift-level observation data, we further performed descriptive analysis of case mix and patient care intensities for a typical 12-h shift. We calculated the nursing hours per-patient for each shift by multiplying the number of nurses on the shift by their shift durations in hours. The total nursing hours was then divided by the total number of patients on the neonatal units on a shift to measure the time in hours a nurse might have to care for each patient per a standardised 12-h shift (assuming all time is work time).

Results

Range and frequency of nurses' activities

To characterise workflows and related activities among nurses, we used data from the 46 shifts to enumerate and group 36 different tasks into 8 task domains. These were based on a total of 1891 task instances observed over 499 person-hours. Of the 8 broad categories/domains, the greatest number of task episodes [37 %] were related to direct patient care (comprising of 14 tasks with 675 task instances) (Supplementary material Fig. 1). The task distribution across other domains was: indirect patient care (comprising of 7 tasks with 416 [23 %] task instances), communications (comprising of 3 tasks with 202 [11 %] task instances), students' mentorship and supervision (146 [8 %] task instances), personal breaks and rest (122 [6 %] task instances), documentation (226 [12 %] task instances), interruptions (32 [2 %] task instances), and staff supervision and mentorship (23 [1 %] task instances). This broad summary of nursing tasks is provided in Supplementary material Fig. 1. Direct patient care activities

The 14 different direct patient care tasks undertaken by shadowed nurses included feeding, drug and intravenous fluid administration, patient assessment, vital signs monitoring, admissions and discharges, oxygen therapy, cannulation, patient hygiene, and support with last office, among others. Feeding, medication and IV fluids management were the most frequent specific tasks (Fig. 1). We observed some activities that were more common during day shifts than night shifts, for instance, weight check, discharges, and vital signs. Whereas last office and admissions were common during the day-night shift transition periods (Supplementary material Figs. 2 and 3).

Nursing task proportion by categories: Direct and Indirect patient care (values represent proportion of the respective total direct and indirect patient care).

Indirect patient care activities

Indirect patient care comprised 8 different tasks, including handing over, ward rounds, feeds preparation, supplies management, housekeeping and general cleaning, equipment cleaning, portering,2 and interdisciplinary consultations. Of these, handing over and attending ward rounds were the most common indirect patient care processes and mainly happening during day shifts, except for feeds preparations that were common for both day and night shifts as these are pegged on 3-hourly feeding times.

Nurses' workflows, interruptions, and multi-tasking

Exploration of time allocated to different nursing care activities was based on data from a sample of 13 Phase II shifts, comprised of 9-day shifts and 4-night shifts. This observation suggests that, in a typical 12-h shift, nursing handovers occupied the longest time prior to a nurse switching to a new task (median of 33 min) followed by medication tasks (median of 22 min). However, the time spent on an activity/task often involved instances of multi-tasking and interruptions, and such were typical of these busy units. Medication or intravenous fluid administration, feeding, and documentation were subject to most instances of interruptions (Table 3). Of note, nurses were typically only able to devote 15 min to admission of a sick baby and 9 min to activities focused on respiratory support activities before switching to a new task (Table 3).

Across all shifts, consistent patterns were apparent with vital signs measurement predominantly occurring during the day between 11:30–13:30 h and 13:30–15:30 h, and at night between 21:30–01:30 h but rarely at other times at night (Supplementary material Fig. 2). In contrast, feeding tasks were often performed between 11:30 h and 21:30 h and much less at night (despite 3-hourly requirements). Admissions were erratic but discharges were conducted between 11:30 h and 15:30 h (Supplementary material Fig. 2). Case mix and patient intensity in a typical 12-hour shift

The median nursing hours per-patient per-shift, standardised to a 12-h shift, were 54 min for day shifts and 52 min for night shifts with a median number of 38 and 32 babies during day and night shifts, respectively. The average length of hospital stay per baby is 3–7 days. In this context, there are only three patient acuity criteria - critically ill and unstable, moderately ill, and ill but stable. Each shift included babies with varying illness severity requiring different interventions such as Continuous Positive Airway Pressure, oxygen therapy, intravenous therapies, phototherapy, nasogastric tube feeding, incubator care, and kangaroo mother care that remained relatively consistent across both day and night shifts. However, this case mix and patient intensity did not vary significantly between day and night shifts (and Supplementary material Fig. 3) (Table 4).

Discussion

In the Kenyan neonatal care units we studied, the broadest range and proportions of nursing tasks were within the domain of direct patient care (i.e. feeding, hygiene, administering treatments, admissions, patient monitoring, discharges, cannulation, and breathing support), a fifth of tasks were indirect patient care activities (i.e. portering, preparing feeds, housekeeping, supply management, and handing over). Communication tasks, student mentorship, personal breaks, and documentation were infrequent. Our findings reflect task frequency on different workflow domains and this is consistent with other recent reports that nurses spend up to 40 % of shift time on direct patient care (Abbey et al., 2012; Desjardins et al., 2008; Westbrook et al., 2011), 30–40 % on indirect care while the rest is spent on performing unit-level, essential but non-technical nursing tasks. Many of these tasks could be performed by other lower cadre staff. However, support staff such as ward (care) assistants are still few in public hospitals in Kenya (Roche et al., 2017; Yen et al., 2018; Kagonya et al., 2025).

Nurses engage in many activities/tasks away from the bedside, for example, supplies management and housekeeping to ensure the availability of essential medicines and commodities and supervise cleaning. These non-clinical tasks raise questions about alignment of nurses' skillset with roles and also efficient use of skilled nurses' time. While they are essential care-supporting activities, delegating these to other hospital staff could allow nurses to focus more on direct or more complex patient care activities (Arnon et al., 2018; Blay and Roche, 2020; Hewko et al., 2015). However, this would require nurses' to be willing to delegate these tasks, the presence of appropriately trained support staff (Duffield et al., 2019, Duffield et al., 2014; Kagonya et al., 2023; Roche et al., 2017), and that nurses have time for their supervision. Providing such support staff would not be a substitute for clearly needed additional nursing staff who in many of these settings have even wider roles to train and supervise medical and nursing interns and students. Currently, however, the professional norms and values of these professional trainees are being shaped in settings where shortcuts, missed care and limited communication are common (Arreciado Maranon and Isla Pera, 2015; Lee and Chiang, 2021).

While conducting their activities, nurses in Kenyan neonatal units can only focus for short periods (< 20 min) on complex/critical tasks such as intravenous drug or fluid preparation and administration or tailoring respiratory support (< 10 min) for populations of babies with prematurity, extremely low birth weights and varying illness severity. This raises questions on whether critical tasks can be performed properly following standard procedures and good clinical practice, and therefore, quality of care delivered. Even during these short periods nurses frequently have to multi-task and deal with interruptions. At the same time, they are often expected to provide effective communication with the multidisciplinary team and caregivers or their family.

Even though this study shows nurses devote only short periods to a task, we have shown in complementary work on the same neonatal units that qualified nurses deliver only 32 % of babies' expected bedside care (Imam et al., 2025; Kagonya et al., 2025). Just as much care is delivered by inexperienced nursing students and mothers who nurses have almost no time to supervise or support. The limited time that nurses' have to concentrate on tasks, the multi-tasking, interruptions, and inability to provide effective support or supervision have clear patient safety implications. In effect, these factors will also affect the adoption, safe and effective use of technologies especially those that may increase demands on nursing time such as Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (McCollum et al., 2019).

To make efficient use of extremely limited time (< 1 h per baby per 12-h shift), nurses often bundle together some tasks and miss others. For instance, the vital signs monitoring and feeding activities that nurses can undertake occur at set times while multi-tasking and are more likely to be done on day rather than night shifts (Supplementary material Fig. 2). Time tends to be preserved for predictably scheduled nursing handovers and ward rounds that are perhaps core to nurses professional identity (McKnight et al., 2023). These routines are often only broken during unpredictable but critical events such as admissions or sudden change in a baby's condition.

Limitations

Strengths of this study include carefully conducted observational shadowing of about 500 nurse person-hours across 46 shifts spread across 8 hospitals. This approach helps to overcome social desirability and recall bias that can affect related time and motion studies elsewhere (Burke et al., 2000). However, we acknowledge some limitations. The pooled analysis might potentially mask hospital-specific patterns or variations. However, the practices seen across these public hospitals were similar, and we do not feel any hospital-specific variations would be significant enough to affect our conclusions. Second, we only collected time stamp data in Phase II limiting our ability to capture, with rigour, the actual time spent on less common activities, and we did not identify how long a well-conducted high-quality nursing task should take. This methodological approach could be an important focus for future studies.

Conclusion

In Kenyan neonatal units with high workloads and wider resource-constraints, nurses are able to devote limited time and attention to bedside care for severely ill babies, even when performing complex caring tasks. When performing complex tasks nurses have to continue multi-tasking and deal with interruptions even in these short periods. All these factors pose risks to safe patient care. Addressing severe nursing workforce deficits and potentially providing additional support staff are urgently needed if efforts to upgrade neonatal care that include introduction of more advanced technologies are to improve outcomes.
