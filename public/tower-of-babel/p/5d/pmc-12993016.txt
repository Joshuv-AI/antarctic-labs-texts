When brain calcifications are not benign: A calcified metastasis case and literature review
Authors: Omar Ibelkouchene, Assad El Bakkari, Chaimae Abourak, Manal Mernissi, Fatima zahra Laamrani, Youssef Omor, Rachida Latib, Sanae Amalik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcified brain metastases (CBM), though rare and often under-recognized, occur mainly in pulmonary adenocarcinoma but may also arise from other cancers. Their formation is multifactorial, with CT as the reference for detection and MRI (SWI) providing complementary evaluation. Accurate differentiation from other calcified brain lesions is crucial, as CBM may reflect slower disease progression, illustrated by a post-radiotherapy case in a young patient.Keyword: Calcified brain metastases, CT scan, MRI, Radiotherapy, Pulmonary adenocarcinoma, Intracranial calcifications

Introduction

Brain metastases are the most common intracranial tumors in adults, occurring in approximately 20% to 40% of cancer patients during the course of their disease [1]. However, the presence of calcifications within brain metastases remains uncommon. Reported prevalence ranges from 1% to 6% in earlier radiological series to nearly 19% in more recent CT-based studies, reflecting improved detection with modern imaging techniques [2,3].

Calcifications may arise spontaneously, reflect intrinsic histopathological characteristics of the primary tumor, or develop secondary to treatment—particularly radiotherapy or certain chemotherapeutic agents. Several primary cancers have been associated with calcified brain metastases (CBM), most commonly pulmonary adenocarcinoma, breast carcinoma, and small cell lung carcinoma [3,4].

Post-therapeutic calcification may represent tumor necrosis, treatment response, or delayed tissue mineralization.

We report the case of a 36-year-old woman with metastatic lung adenocarcinoma who developed progressive and extensive calcification within multiple brain metastases following whole-brain radiotherapy. This case is noteworthy due to the patient’s young age, the unusual degree of calcification, and the clear interval progression of intraparenchymal calcifications on follow-up imaging, illustrating the evolving radiological spectrum of treated brain metastases.

Case report

We present the case of a 36-year-old woman with no significant past medical history, no known neurological disorders, no history of tuberculosis, and no known metabolic diseases. She was not taking any chronic medications and had no prior neurosurgical or central nervous system conditions. She was followed for invasive acinar adenocarcinoma of bronchopulmonary origin (TTF-1 positive) (Fig. 1), which was metastatic at diagnosis, with osseous involvement of both the axial and peripheral skeleton, treated with systemic chemotherapy.

Axial (A) and coronal (B) chest CT scan demonstrating a left apical pulmonary mass associated with a large pleural effusion.

Three months after the initial diagnosis of the primary tumor, she presented with new-onset focal epileptic seizures. On clinical examination, she was fully conscious with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15. Neurological examination was unremarkable, showing no focal motor or sensory deficits, no cranial nerve abnormalities, and no signs of cerebellar dysfunction or meningeal irritation.

Baseline laboratory tests, including complete blood count, serum electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, calcium and phosphate levels, and inflammatory markers, were all within normal limits. There was no laboratory evidence of metabolic imbalance or infectious/inflammatory disease that could explain her symptoms.

A baseline brain CT performed at seizure onset revealed bilateral supratentorial cortical and subcortical lesions with surrounding perilesional edema (Fig. 2). The patient subsequently underwent whole-brain radiotherapy as part of her oncologic management. At the 6-month follow-up, routine brain CT demonstrated the appearance of intralesional calcifications within previously identified metastases (Fig. 3).

Axial contrast-enhanced CT scan of the brain demonstrating supratentorial cortico–subcortical intra-axial lesions with post-contrast enhancement.

Axial contrast-enhanced CT scan of the brain revealing a decrease in size of the calcified cortico–subcortical intra-axial lesions, with multiple new intraparenchymal calcifications.

The brain lesions were not biopsied due to their typical imaging appearance, the known presence of systemic metastatic adenocarcinoma, and the absence of therapeutic consequences.

Discussion

CBM are considered rare [5]. In an early series, Deck et al. reported calcifications in only 3.5% of 57 patients with brain metastases [2], and Harwood-Nash et al. also described occasional calcifications in irradiated metastatic lesions [5]. CBM are found in approximately 1% of surgical specimens and 6.6% of autopsy series, highlighting their uncommon nature in earlier reports.

More recent studies suggest that CBM may be more frequent than previously believed. For example, Rebella et al. [3] analyzed 1002 brain CT scans in patients with known primary tumors and found CBM in 19% of cases. These findings support the hypothesis that CBM are likely underdiagnosed, particularly when MRI alone is used without complementary CT or when imaging resources are limited.

CBM are most frequently associated with pulmonary adenocarcinomas (particularly non-small cell lung carcinoma), followed by breast carcinoma and small cell lung carcinoma [3,4]. Other reported primaries include colorectal carcinoma, ovarian carcinoma, sarcomas, and non-Hodgkin lymphomas.

Several mechanisms may contribute to calcification. Necrotic or degenerative tumor tissue creates an alkaline environment that favors calcium precipitation, notably through increased local alkaline phosphatase activity [6]. Recent studies on microcalcifications in breast cancer suggest that these deposits are not merely degenerative but may reflect complex cellular mechanisms involved in metastatic potential, with osteopontin playing a role in regulating both microcalcification formation and tumor cell migration [7].

Since the first report by Harwood-Nash et al. [8], calcifications have been recognized as a common radiological manifestation of radiation-induced brain injury. In Rebella et al.’s series [3], approximately 47% of patients treated with radiotherapy developed CBM post-treatment. The exact pathogenesis remains unclear, but radiotherapy is hypothesized to induce tissue hypoxia, disrupting normal calcium gradients across cell membranes and leading to intracellular accumulation. Elevated intracellular calcium may trigger calcification, which subsequently extends to the extracellular space [9,10]. No clear association has been demonstrated with radiation dose, fractionation, or concurrent systemic chemotherapy [11]. Chemotherapy may also influence the calcification process, though the mechanism is not fully understood.

From an imaging perspective, correlation between CT and MRI findings is essential for evaluating brain metastases. CT remains the most reliable modality for detecting parenchymal calcifications, while MRI—particularly susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI)—provides complementary information [12]. Despite widespread use of susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), CT remains superior for detecting and characterizing intraparenchymal calcifications.

On CT, these lesions appear as intra-axial hyperdense areas with measured densities >85 HU to exclude intralesional hemorrhage. The region of interest should be positioned over the most hyperdense portion of the calcification, avoiding peripheral areas to exclude non-calcified tumor tissue or adjacent normal parenchyma. Morphologically, calcifications may appear as punctate (<4 mm), nodular (≥4 mm), ring-like, clustered, or coarse, with punctate being the most common pattern in Rebella’s study [3].

The differential diagnosis of brain calcifications is broad and includes primary brain tumors (eg, oligodendroglioma), vascular malformations (eg, cavernous malformations), infectious granulomas (eg, neurocysticercosis, tuberculosis), meningiomas—which can mimic cortical metastases—and degenerative/dystrophic calcifications such as those seen in Fahr’s disease.

Prognostically, intracerebral calcifications associated with a low proliferative index (low MIB-1) may correlate with a more favorable course and prolonged survival after complete resection [2]. Additionally, a reported case of calcified cerebellar metastasis from ovarian carcinoma showed slow progression over a 10-year interval from initial diagnosis to brain involvement, suggesting a relatively indolent tumor phenotype [5].

Conclusion

CBM are rare but likely under-recognized. Their detection relies primarily on CT, which is more sensitive than MRI for this purpose. They most frequently originate from pulmonary adenocarcinoma, but may also arise from breast, colorectal, ovarian cancers, or sarcomas.

Calcification may result from radiotherapy effects, tumor necrosis, or intrinsic histological features of the primary tumor. The differential diagnosis includes calcified meningiomas and benign or infectious lesions.

Integrating clinical context with multimodal imaging is essential to avoid diagnostic errors. CBM may also be associated with a slower disease course, underlining the importance of their recognition and careful follow-up.
