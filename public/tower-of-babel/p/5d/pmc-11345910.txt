Introduction

Epidermoid cysts are common cutaneous cysts characterized by benign, encapsulated, subepidermal nodules with an epidermis-like epithelial lining (wall) that produces keratin [1,2]. An epidermoid cyst arises from proliferation of surface epidermoid cells within the dermis [1]. Epidermoid cysts are most frequently located on the face, neck, and trunk but can occur anywhere on the body [2]. Two cases of epidermoid cysts located between the two anal sphincters have been previously reported in the literature [3,4]. Here, we present the case of a 32-year-old female with a mass originating from the external verge of the anus which was diagnosed as epidermoid cyst. This case is described in accordance with the criteria of SCARE [5].

Case presentation

A 32-year-old married woman, mother of three children, with a history of two cesarean sections and one vaginal delivery, presented with an 11-year history of a progressively enlarging painless mass originating from the right anal verge. She had no relevant medical history or chronic illnesses to note. Physical examination revealed a soft, well-bordered, smoothly contoured mass. It measured around 4 cm and was located at the 7 o'clock position when viewed in the lithotomy position, covering the anus. Ultrasound imaging identified a homogeneous, hypoechoic, and poorly vascularized lesion (Fig. 1). Due to the patient's financial constraints, no further imaging studies were conducted. The patient's preoperative routine blood tests were normal. A surgical operation was carried out effectively to entirely remove the mass (Fig. 2). Afterward, the operation was concluded by stitching the surgical area with a series of interrupted, absorbable vicryl sutures. Subsequent histopathology analysis of the removed tissue confirmed the presence of a benign epidermoid cyst (Fig. 3). The patient was discharged without any complications.

Ultrasound imaging of the perianal region identified a homogeneous, hypoechoic, and poorly vascularized lesion situated at the anal verge, with dimensions of 27 × 37 mm.

The gross appearance of the excised mass. It measured around 4 cm in diameter, contained necrotic material and was covered by skin.

Histopathological analysis of the surgical specimen predominantly revealed granulation tissue with a significant foreign body reaction, characterized by sporadic foreign-body-type giant cells, focal fibrosis, and regions of transitional epithelium consistent with a benign epidermoid cyst. Discussion

Epidermoid cysts are benign, slowly enlarging lesions that arise from the epidermis of the skin [4]. There are many synonyms for epidermoid cysts, including infundibular cysts, epidermal cysts, and epidermal inclusion cysts [2]. These cysts are more common in adult males and can be either congenital or acquired after trauma or surgery [2,6,7]. Ashcroft et al. have suggested that certain procedures, like colonoscopic examination of diverticulosis, could potentially lead to the formation of an epidermal cyst in the area observed in their patient [3]. Nevertheless, similar to the case documented by Lake et al. [4], our case also did not reveal any probable factors that might indicate a possible cause for the development of the cyst. They may occur at any age but are typically seen in the third and fourth decades of life [2]. The incidence of perineal epidermoid cysts is rare, with the incidence of presacral developmental cysts estimated at around 1 in 50,000 [3]. Skin trauma, inflammation, ultraviolet light exposure, smoking, and the human papilloma virus may cause epidermoid cysts in elderly patients; the occurrence of epidermoid cysts before puberty in unusual locations and numbers raise the suspicion of syndromes such as Gardner syndrome, Gorlin syndrome, and Favre-Racouchot syndrome [1,4]. Epidermoid cysts may be asymptomatic, but in some cases become infected and inflamed as a result of the rupture of the cyst lining, thus may lead to discomfort, erythema, swelling, tenderness, pain upon palpation, and fluctuation [1,3]. This patient did not have any pain or alteration in bowel habits. Radiologic findings of epidermoid cysts depend on the maturation, compactness, and keratin content [8]. In some instances, epidermoid cysts may be small, palpable, and superficial lesions within the subcutaneous tissue seldom necessitate radiologic assessment [8]. Ultrasound is increasingly being employed for the initial assessment of soft-tissue masses and most of the epidermoid cysts (92 %) had a heterogeneously and mildly echogenic appearance, while Computed Tomography (CT) scans of epidermoid cysts reveal hypodensity and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans of epidermoid cysts demonstrate hypointensity on T1-weighted images (T1WI), hyperintensity on T2-weighted images (T2WI) with characteristic diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) [1,7,8]. The patient underwent only an ultrasound due to financial limitations, which showed typical findings of epidermoid cyst. Histopathological examination can confirm epidermoid cysts, showing the cyst is lined by stratified squamous epithelium resembling an epidermis and includes a granular layer and keratin lamellae in the lumen [1,9]. Epidermoid cysts do not usually need treatment if is a small cyst and uncomplicated, removal of the cyst may be accomplished by a local anesthetic around the cyst with epinephrine, and a simple complete surgical excision with the cyst wall intact [1]. Care must be taken by surgeons to avoid excessive excision to prevent the anal canal from stenosis, and avoid spillage of the cyst contents due to the potential for recurrence [3].

Conclusion

Epidermoid cysts are common and can manifest in various locations. When encountering a soft, well-defined mass in an atypical site for epidermoid cysts, it should be included in the differential diagnosis.
