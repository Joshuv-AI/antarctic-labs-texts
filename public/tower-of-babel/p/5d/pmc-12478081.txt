A transdiagnostic, multi-modal approach to understanding apathy: Methodological and analytical framework
Authors: Yunglin Gazes, Hideo Suzuki, Lee-Anne Morris, Seonjoo Lee, Zekai Jin, Edward D. Huey, Bryan B. Chen, Campbell Le Heron, Sarah R. Heibronner, Nora Vanegas-Arroyave
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Apathy is characterized by loss of motivation and manifests as a reduction of goal-directed behavior. Apathy is highly prevalent across neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's Disease (AD) and Parkinson's Disease (PD), and is an important contributor to the disability and reduce quality of life in these conditions. The treatment of apathy remains challenging due to a lack of specific therapies, largely attributed to an incomplete understanding of its cognitive and neuroanatomical underpinnings, crucial for developing targeted interventions. Apathy can be mechanistically studied through effort-based decision-making (EBDM) paradigms, where individuals choose between low- and high-effort tasks for varying reward magnitudes. Anatomically, apathy has been associated with alterations in brain regions previously implicated in EBDM. Using a novel transdiagnostic study design in individuals with AD and PD, we aim to: (1) evaluate the independent effects of reward and effort sensitivity as a mechanistic link between apathy and neurodegeneration of basal ganglia-frontal networks and, (2) in a subset of PD patients receiving deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery, determine whether electrical manipulation of subthalamic nucleus and/or DBS connectivity, directly alter reward and effort information processing and, consequently, goal-directed behavior. Understanding how neurodegeneration—alone or in combination with neuromodulatory interventions—drives apathy, is essential for guiding clinical decision-making and therapeutic development. Given its prevalence across neurodegenerative disorders, apathy provides a unique framework for investigating shared and disease-specific neuroanatomical, functional, and behavioral mechanisms. In this protocol paper, we describe the rationale and methodology of our proposed multimodal approach, to investigate apathy in a transdiagnostic cohort of individuals with AD and PD.

Introduction

Apathy, characterized by a lack of motivation manifesting as reduced goal directed behavior, is a prevalent neuropsychiatric symptom in neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's Disease (AD) and Parkinson's Disease (PD). By limiting engagement in daily activities—both physical and social—apathy significantly diminishes functional independence, heightens caregiver burden, and is associated with an increased risk of mortality (Balestrino and Martinez-Martin, 2017; Bega et al., 2015; Le Heron et al., 2024; van Uem et al., 2016; Weintraub and Mamikonyan, 2019). Apathy is highly prevalent in neurodegenerative conditions, ranging between 60 and 70 % in patients with AD (Diehl-Schmid et al., 2006; Kaufer et al., 1998) and 40 % in those with PD (Pedersen et al., 2009). Despite its high prevalence and impact, effective treatments for apathy remain limited, with current pharmacological and neuromodulatory therapies offering largely inconsistent results (Costello et al., 2024; Padala et al., 2018; Suemoto et al., 2014). Interestingly, apathy may even emerge de novo in PD patients treated with deep brain stimulation (DBS) of basal ganglia targets, such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN).

Understanding how neurodegeneration—alone or in combination with neuromodulatory treatments—contributes to apathy is critical to inform both clinical decision-making and therapeutic development for neuropsychiatric symptoms in aging-related diseases. Notably, regardless of primary neuropathology, apathy represents a common phenotype across neurodegenerative diseases, providing a unique opportunity to investigate shared and disease-specific neuroanatomical, functional, and behavioral mechanisms. This protocol paper aims to describe the rationale and strategy behind our multi-modal approach to examine apathy through behavioral, cognitive and neuroimaging methods in a transdiagnostic cohort of individuals with AD and PD.

Behavioral and cognitive approaches to study apathy

Apathy manifests with difficulties in initiation of and self-motivation toward basic goals (i.e., dressing, bathing) and has been conceptualized as a reduction in goal-directed behavior (Robert et al., 2009). According to the original description by Levy and Dubois (2006), and in the proposed diagnostic criteria for apathy in AD and other neurological and psychiatric disorders (Robert et al., 2009), apathy is thought to be composed of three dimensions: behavioral (auto-activation), cognitive and emotional-affective. Although clinically useful for screening purposes, traditional assessments such as the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) (Cummings, 1997) are not domain-structured in this way. Clinically validated apathy assessments with a dimensional structure include the Lille Apathy Rating Scale (LARS) (Sockeel et al., 2006), the Apathy Evaluation Scale (AES) (Cummings, 1997), the Apathy Motivation Index (AMI) (Ang et al., 2017) and the Dimensional Apathy Scale (DAS) (Radakovic and Abrahams, 2014). These have previously been validated in PD, AD and other neurological conditions. The DAS may be particularly sensitive to the differentiation of predominant dimensions across dementias (Radakovic et al., 2017, 2020; Santangelo et al., 2017) and encompasses the previously proposed domains of behavioral (auto-activation), cognitive and emotional-affective. The initiation subscale of the DAS is similar to the originally proposed behavioral/auto-activation domain, but it is less dependent on motor functions. The executive subscale is most comparable to the cognitive domain. The emotional subscale of the DAS is largely similar to the previously described emotional-affective domain, but it differs by its focus on the integration of emotional behaviors rather than on the facets of expression, processing and recognition. For all of these reasons, the DAS was selected as the primary apathy self/caregiver report questionnaire for the proposed study.

However, although self/caregiver report questionnaires and subscales have been helpful to characterize the multi-faceted nature of the apathetic syndrome during a determined time-frame, they rely on the recollection of behaviors and emotions involving decisions to engage in activities (i.e., physical and social interactions). Furthermore, how these proposed dimensions map to the underlying neurobiological mechanism of goal-directed behavior remains unclear. Alternatively, the use of task-oriented behavioral paradigms allows the real-time examination of core features of goal-directed behavior such as reward/effort valuation or effort-based choice. These components can be mechanistically tested using well-validated effort-based decision making (EBDM) tasks, where individuals are asked to choose to exert effort in exchange for varying reward magnitudes. EBDM paradigms enable us to examine how much value is ascribed to each reward-effort combination and based on the choices made, assess the individual's effort and reward sensitivity. For example, an individual may choose not to act because the rewards offered are not high enough or because the effort costs are too high. One specific example of an EBDM paradigm is the Apple Gathering task (AGt), in which participants are instructed to weigh effort costs against rewards offered for each trial, and to decide if exerting the requested effort is worth it for the offered number of apples (Bonnelle et al., 2015). In this framework, apathy can arise from either insensitivity to potential rewards or overestimation of required efforts. Using the AGt, studies have addressed the cognitive basis of apathy in individuals with PD, Huntington's Disease, cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) (Le Heron, Manohar, et al., 2018; Le Heron, Plant, et al., 2018; Morris et al., 2024), and sporadic cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) (Saleh et al., 2021). In these studies, whilst apathy at a group level in PD and CADASIL was associated with reduced reward sensitivity, whereas apathy at a group level in Huntington's Disease was associated with heightened sensitivity to effort costs, significant individual variability was evident. These findings support the implementation of EBDM paradigms, in addition to self/caregiver report questionnaires, in transdiagnostic research as fruitful models for understanding either shared or divergent mechanisms of apathy across populations with neurodegenerative diseases.

Neuroanatomical underpinnings of apathy across conditions

Neuroimaging techniques have been the most commonly employed tools to explore apathy in a broad range of neurodegenerative conditions, predominantly AD and PD. Existing neuroimaging studies of effort learning, reward learning, and apathy consistently highlight the roles of the frontal cortex, the basal ganglia, and the anterior insula (as part of the salience network). For example, Hauser et al. (2017) described the dissociable anatomical representation of effort vs. reward learning, with the ventral striatum (VS) encoding a reward learning signal and the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) encoding an effort learning signal. Thus, signalling of reward and effort, whilst still closely linked, can also be separated neurally, which may have significance when considering differential changes in reward and effort sensitivity within patients with and without apathy. Baggio and colleagues (2015) found a reduction in functional connectivity (FC) between the frontal cortex and striatum in PD patients with apathy, relative to both healthy controls and PD patients without apathy. Hamada et al. (2021) reported a reduction in FC between the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)/supplementary motor area (SMA) and striatum in elderly participants with apathy compared to those without. Other studies have emphasized the involvement of the anterior insula, orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) in apathy (Jang et al., 2021; Yuen et al., 2014). In our own prior work, we found altered ACC-VS FC in PD patients who later developed apathy (Morris et al., 2023). Taken together, there is a strong evidence base to suggest these neural regions subserve processes involved in goal-directed behavior, as well as being implicated in clinical studies of apathy. Thus, in the present study, we propose a regions-of-interest approach, given a priori hypotheses that these regions in particular would be associated with effort-based decision-making and apathy.

Apathy and DBS

In PD patients who undergo DBS of the basal ganglia for the treatment of motor issues, development of apathy is heterogeneous, ranging from the development of new apathetic behaviors to improvement of existing apathy (Higuchi et al., 2015). Although the development of apathy after DBS has been mainly attributed to the reduction of dopaminergic therapy, larger dopaminergic medication reduction on those who develop apathy after DBS has not been consistently described (Martinez-Fernandez et al., 2016). These observations strongly suggest that post-DBS apathy cannot be exclusively attributed to dopaminergic medication, emphasizing the importance of non-dopaminergic systems in the pathogenesis of apathy after DBS (Martinez-Fernandez et al., 2016). The STN is the most common DBS target and also a particularly interesting structure involved in the regulation of three functional sub-territories: motor, cognitive and limbic/emotional faculties (Alexander et al., 1986). These territories are differentiated by their projections from different frontal regions. Projections to the medial STN from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), OFC, and ACC are known to relate to limbic/emotion abilities and have been strongly implicated in the neurobiological mechanism of goal-directed behavior (Haynes and Haber, 2013). In this context, the STN has also been linked to cost-benefit decision making, further supporting its association with motivational processes (Zenon et al., 2016). Thus, while the effects of DBS on apathy have been primarily attributed to dopaminergic medication reduction (Thobois et al., 2013), DBS electrode location may also contribute to apathy related outcomes after DBS (de Bruin et al., 2025; Zoon et al., 2023).

Value proposition and hypotheses

Taken together, deficits of goal-directed behavior, manifested as apathy, are prevalent across neurodegenerative diseases of aging. We seek to leverage these common behavioral outcomes to investigate the spectrum of structural and functional changes that contribute to apathy, in AD and PD. The proposed study has two main goals: 1) to examine the effects of reward and effort sensitivity as a mechanistic link between apathy and neurodegeneration of fronto-striatal networks and, (2) in a subset of individuals with PD who receive DBS as part of routine care, determine whether electrical manipulation of STN and/or DBS connectivity directly alter reward and effort information processing and, consequently, goal-directed behavior. Drawing from evidence that implicates effort and reward valuation with fronto-striatal and salience networks, we have designed a multi-modal approach to understanding the cognitive and neuroanatomical underpinnings of apathy. EBDM paradigms, in which individuals choose between low- and high-effort tasks with varying reward magnitudes, provide a well-validated framework for studying variability in cognitive aspects of goal-directed behavior. Differentiating between effort and reward sensitivity enables a biologically plausible mechanistic approach to apathy, with testable predictions for each component's distinct neuroanatomical basis. We propose an integrative approach to characterize how apathy-related dimensions and components of goal-directed behavior relate to structural/functional neuroimaging metrics.

In line with the proposed goals, we hypothesize that apathy will be associated with reduced incentivization by reward and/or increased sensitivity to effort costs, and that reward and effort sensitivity differences would be associated with population-level distinctions in the morphology of and/or connectivity of fronto-striatal and salience network regions. The use of this framework in a sample of individuals with AD and PD promises to illuminate the neurobiological basis of apathy in neurodegenerative processes. A clearer understanding of the brain circuits involved in apathy could enable the identification of biomarkers for early diagnosis, prognosis, and patient stratification, allowing for more personalized treatment approaches.

Additionally, given the role of the STN in decision making, we will leverage the use of therapeutic neuromodulation in a subset of patients with PD, to determine whether electrical manipulation and/or white matter connectivity directly alter reward and effort information processing. We hypothesize that as compared to OFF stimulation, stimulation ON will result in changes in reward and effort sensitivities such that participants with stronger structural connectivity between DBS electrodes and cortical structures (e.g., OFC, vmPFC, ACC) will demonstrate a greater change in reward sensitivity. Insights gained from this study may guide the refinement of neuromodulatory interventions, such as DBS, to minimize unintended cognitive and behavioral side effects.

Study design and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Baylor College of Medicine (BCM), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Population

Participant recruitment is currently taking place at BCM and is expected to be completed by the end of 2026. All participants have capacity to consent or have an informant who is willing to provide information about them. There are two main components to the study: (1) cross-sectional examination of neural connectivity underlying apathy in AD/amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) and PD, including also a control group of age-matched healthy individuals and (2) longitudinal examination of the effect of DBS of the STN (and connected frontal-subcortical circuits) on behavioral changes in the AGt (with DBS ‘off’ and ‘on’) in PD subjects who elected to undergo DBS as part of routine care. AD/aMCI participants

100 participants with mild AD or aMCI (20/year) (Schneider et al., 2009) are being recruited at the Alzheimer's Disease and Memory Disorders Center (ADMDC) at BCM. These participants meet a Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) of 0.5–1. As a result of challenges on behavioral task completion in participants with moderate and severe disease, an early decision was made to enrich this cohort with individuals within the likely AD pathology spectrum, particularly aMCI. AD/aMCI diagnoses are made by a panel of physicians and neuropsychologists using the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria (Jack et al., 2011).

PD participants

100 participants with a history of idiopathic PD (20/year) (Postuma et al., 2015), complicated by motor fluctuations, are being recruited from the Parkinson's Disease Center and Movement Disorders Clinic (PDCMDC) at BCM. The PDCMDC has one of the largest PD and DBS clinics in the United States, with an approximate volume of 2000 annual PD patient visits and more than 100 new DBS cases/year. Prospective participants are approached for enrollment once they have been referred for DBS and deemed appropriate surgical candidates as part of routine clinical care by a multi-disciplinary panel of movement disorders specialists, functional neurosurgeons and neuropsychologists. DBS implantation surgery typically occurs approximately one month after enrollment. All PD participants will be recontacted six months after DBS surgery for a follow up visit and completion of study assessments (i.e., physical examination, cognitive, behavioral/psychiatric assessments and AGt with DBS ‘off and DBS ‘on’, in randomized order). While the majority of these patients proceed with DBS, a subset ultimately chooses not to undergo surgery due to personal considerations, such as concerns about potential risks, uncertainty about the expected benefits, or a preference for non-surgical management. This natural variation may provide an opportunity to contextualize clinical outcomes between those who choose to receive DBS and those who do not.

Healthy controls

50 healthy control (HC) participants are being recruited from two primary sources: (1) an existing longitudinal cohort at BCM and (2) through direct outreach to healthy caregivers of patients seen at the PDCMDC. Eligibility criteria include age-matched adults (≥18 years old) with no prior history of neurological or psychiatric diagnoses, as confirmed by medical history review and screening questionnaires.

Exclusion criteria

Patients are excluded if they show (i) moderate or severe dementia (Clinical Dementia Rating Scale >1.0 or Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE, <20), (ii) presence of severe language, psychiatric, or behavioral symptoms that would interfere with the collection of the study measures, (iii) contraindications to undergo a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (e.g., pacemaker), (iv) additional neurological conditions that could affect neuroimaging measures, including previous stroke, traumatic brain injury, or previous brain surgery, (v) radiological (MRI) evidence of cortical stroke, or subcortical infarct or lacune larger than 2 cm in diameter on any slice, (vi) history of claustrophobia, (vii) pre-existing chronic, psychiatric illness defined as serious Axis I illness, including schizophrenia, active major depressive disorder (MDD), or bipolar affective disorder (BAD) (However, those with a history of episodic psychiatric disorders, including MDD, BAD, or previous alcohol or substance abuse, are included in order to maximize the study's generalizability), (viii) active alcohol or substance abuse and (ix) electroconvulsive therapy within the past six months. In addition, patients with any of the following disorders are excluded: (i) central nervous system infection, such as meningitis and encephalitis (ii) post-traumatic dementia, defined as dementia with a temporal relationship to a severe head injury where consciousness was lost, (iii) toxic-metabolic encephalopathy, defined as a syndrome of delirium associated with an identifiable toxin such as a chemical or metabolic disorder (e.g., drug intoxication, hypothyroidism, B-12 deficiency, hepatic encephalopathy or renal disease), (iv) cancer or acute illness (including those with metastases; but, those with a history of successfully treated cancer are included), (v) severe, unstable medical illness (e.g., end-stage liver or renal disease), (vi) multiple sclerosis and (vii) mental retardation.

Clinical and neuropsychiatric assessments

Physical examination

The motor subscale of the Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) is completed in all PD participants (Goetz, 2010), including both in the ‘off’ (>12 h after last dopaminergic medication intake) and ‘on’ dopaminergic medication states (45–60 min after a regular dose of L-Dopa). During this portion of the MDS-UPDRS, an experienced examiner (rater) instructs the patient to complete motor tasks and rates the severity of motor aspects of PD (i.e., tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, gait and posture).

Cognitive assessments

Both the MMSE and Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA) are used. The MMSE and the MoCA are routine cognitive screening tests which assess multiple cognitive domains (i.e., visuospatial, language, memory, attention, executive function, and orientation). Both scales are rated on a 30-point scale with lower scores indicating decreased cognitive abilities. The MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005) is more sensitive to subtle cognitive deficits in patients with PD and is considered superior to the MMSE in discriminating between individuals with MCI and those without cognitive impairment (Pinto et al., 2019). The MMSE (Folstein et al., 1975) is widely used to measure and follow the severity of clinical dementia syndromes over time.

Behavioral/psychiatric assessments

The assembled battery takes approximately 1–2 h to complete. The components of this battery have been selected to comprehensively examine apathy and to capture additional mood-related confounders. Data collected and participant responses are directly entered into a central Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database (https://www.project-redcap.org/). All measures are available in English and Spanish, including:

The DAS (Radakovic and Abrahams, 2014) is completed by both participants and informants. The scale consists of three subscales of apathy: executive, emotional, and initiation. The DAS has been previously validated in populations with PD and AD, and it is considered a reliable tool to assess multidimensional apathy, regardless of the severity of motor symptoms. The scale is composed of 24 items scored on a 4-point Likert response scale. Each 8-item subscale has a minimum of 0 (least apathy) and a maximum of 24 (most apathy). The total score can range from 0 to 72. Published subscale cutoffs are 13.5 for the initiation, 9.5 for executive, 10.5 for emotional, and 28.5 for the total scale (Santangelo et al., 2017).

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Level 1 and 2 Cross-Cutting Measures (DSM-5-CCM) (Narrow et al., 2013) is a sensitive screening measure of other psychiatric symptoms quantified to control for potential confounders, this scale is completed by both, participants and informants regarding the study participant.

The full Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), which serves as a worldwide rating scale to assess cognitive, behavioral, and functional dimensions in individuals diagnosed with dementia. Unlike a conventional mental status examination or inventory, the rater forms a judgment on six categories considering information from the participants and the informants. Although the scoring system for the CDR is strongly influenced by memory scores, it demonstrates reliable consistency among different raters in dementia staging. The CDR allows staging of participants by levels of impairment into five diagnostic categories, ranging from cognitively unimpaired to severe dementia (Berg, 1988).

The Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Questionnaire (NPI-Q) (Cummings et al., 1994) is a highly effective tool for evaluating neuropsychiatric disruptions in individuals with dementia. The examiner typically gathers information from a caregiver, regarding the frequency and severity of symptoms across 12 areas: delusions, hallucinations, agitation or aggression, depression or dysphoria, anxiety, elation or euphoria, apathy or indifference, disinhibition, irritability or lability, motor disturbance, night-time behaviors and appetite and eating changes. An additional feature of the NPI-Q is its capability to capture separate measures of symptom severity and the level of distress they induce.

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (Korner et al., 2006; Yesavage et al., 1982) is a reliable self-report instrument for assessing depression in the elderly. The GDS incorporates items with ecological validity specifically for older individuals, steering away from the predominant focus on somatic symptoms commonly included in other depression instruments. This instrument has shown to be useful in older patients who are cognitively unimpaired of or exhibit only mild dementia.

The Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ) consists of 10 items measuring functional status, which is completed by informants (Pfeffer et al., 1982). FAQ items are rated on a 4-point scale (total score ranging from 0 to 30), with higher scores indicating greater functional impairment. The FAQ has demonstrated to have high clinimetric properties and accuracy for discriminating between MCI and dementia (Gonzalez et al., 2022).

Effort-based decision-making paradigm

Participants are administered an EBDM task, the AGt (Bonnelle et al., 2015). The AGt is a well validated effort-based decision-making paradigm that allows the dissociation of reward and effort effects while controlling for motor deficits. The task is administered in a laptop computer through Psychtoolbox (http://psychtoolbox.org), in MATLAB (MathWorks, USA). For the purposes of this study, the AGt consists of a practice block and 4 testing blocks with 25 trials each. Two factors vary across trials: (1) the amount of reward (1, 3, 6, 9 or 12 apples) given if each trial is successfully completed and (2) the amount of effort required to complete the trial (10 %, 27.5 %, 45 %, 62.5 % and 80 % of each participant's maximum voluntary contraction). Maximum voluntary contraction is measured at the start of task for all participants, to calibrate the required force of contraction to each participant's ability. During the experiment, for each trial, the participant makes a choice of whether to accept the trial or not. They signal their decision via a small squeeze of the dynamometer (left for yes, right for no). If the choice is to accept the trial, the apple tree presented initially in the centre of the screen jumps to either the right or the left, signalling which hand the participant must squeeze with. The participant then squeezes the handheld dynamometer for the indicated hand for >1 s within a 5-s window at the required force level shown on the screen. If a trial is rejected, then the participant is still required to wait the same amount of time as an accepted trial to temporal discounting effects. Completion of the task takes approximately 35 min. To minimize fatigue, the hand used for squeezing is randomized so each hand receives some rest, and breaks are offered between each of the 4 blocks. Raw measures of task performance include the proportion of offers accepted, decision latency, and peak force of squeeze (normalized to each participant's maximal voluntary contraction). These data can then be modeled to estimate the individual's effort and reward sensitivity, through the allocation of effort to obtain rewarding outcomes (see 2.5.1. for additional details regarding the AGt). The AGt has been previously validated across different patient groups with and without motor impairment, including individuals with PD (Le Heron, Plant, et al., 2018), HD and small vessel disease (Le Heron, Manohar, et al., 2018; Morris et al., 2024).

Magnetic resonance imaging protocol

All participants undergo a comprehensive set of MRI sequences to assess brain structural and functional characteristics. All participants are imaged in a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit 3T scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil. The scan sequences include T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo (MP-RAGE) with 208 sagittal volumes, TR = 2.4 s, TE = 2.24 ms, flip angle = 8°, voxel size = 0.8 mm isotropic, and FOV = 256 mm; multi-shell multi-band diffusion-weighted sequences with 92 axial slices × 99 vol (including 7 vol of b = 0 s/mm2 and 46 directions each for b = 1500 and 3000 s/mm2), TR = 3.22 s, TE = 88.4 ms, flip angle = 90°, voxel size = 1.5 mm isotropic, FOV = 210 mm, pixel bandwidth = 1700 Hz, and imaging frequency = 123.25 MHz (two sets of images acquired with opposite phase encoding direction); T2-weighted sampling perfection with application optimized contrasts using different flip angle evolution (SPACE) with 208 sagittal volumes, TR = 3.2 s, TE = 563 ms, voxel size = 0.8 mm isotropic, and FOV = 256 mm; spin echo field maps with 66 axial slices × 2 vol, TR = 6.83 s, TE = 61.2 ms, flip angle = 90°, voxel size = 2.2 mm isotropic, pixel bandwidth = 2186 Hz, and imaging frequency = 123.25 MHz (two sets of images acquired with opposite phase encoding direction); multi-band T2∗-weighted blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) sequences with 66 axial slices × 650 vol, TR = 0.925 s, TE = 37 ms, flip angle = 61°, voxel size = 2.2 mm isotropic, FOV = 228 mm, pixel bandwidth = 2404 Hz, and imaging frequency = 123.25 MHz in the phase encoding (anterior-posterior commissure plane) direction during resting state, in which participants are instructed to remain still with their eyes open and not to think of anything in particular; and 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) with 208 sagittal volumes, TR = 4.8 s, TE = 441 ms, voxel size = 1.0 mm isotropic, and FOV = 256 mm.

Data preprocessing

Drift diffusion models for modelling EBDM behavior

During recent years, there has been a notable increase in the use of accumulation-to-bound models in cognitive neuroscience, assuming that decision-making processes result from the accumulation of evidence towards one option compared to another over time. In particular, drift diffusion models (DDMs) have proven valuable for dissecting the neural and cognitive components of these types of decision processes, including EBDM that requires weighing effort against potential rewards (Myers et al., 2022). Rather than limiting analyses simply to acceptance or rejection rates, by incorporating the time taken to make a decision this approach also allows separating various components of decision-making, such as the speed of evidence accumulation (drift rate), amount of evidence required to make a choice (decision threshold), and the decision bias (tendency towards one choice), offering an opportunity for deeper insights into decision processes. The DDM models human decisions as a generalized Wiener process or generalized Brownian motion. Upon consistent stimulus exposure, a subject gradually accumulates information over time and makes a decision once the process hits one of the two decision boundaries (see Fig. 2). The stochastic process is parameterized by the bias (z), non-decision time (t), threshold (a) and, most importantly, drift rate (v). Anatomical MRI

T1-weighted MR images are pre-processed to quantify cortical thickness and volume, using version 7.3.2 of FreeSurfer (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) (Fischl et al., 2004), which segments and parcellates brain anatomical structures in each participant's native space. Manual corrections of the automatic segmentations from FreeSurfer are made as needed upon visual inspection.

In addition to mean cortical thickness and volume, a priori region-of-interest (ROI) masks are created to conduct the subsequent tractography and functional connectivity analyses. Diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI)

Diffusion-weighted MRI data are preprocessed using MRtrix3 (https://www.mrtrix.org) tools except where indicated. Preprocessing is performed in the following order: (i) denoising (Veraart et al., 2016), (ii) Gibbs ring correction (Kellner et al., 2016), (iii) corrections for geometric distortions followed by motion and eddy current correction (FSL topup and eddy) (Andersson et al., 2016) and (iv) bias field correction using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs; https://picsl.upenn.edu/software/ants) (Tustison et al., 2010).

We choose to use seed-based diffusion tractography data to search for group differences, rather than voxel-wise or white matter ROI analyses, aiming to estimate the integrity of fiber bundles connecting the hypothesized gray matter ROIs. From there, we compute scalar metrics within the bundles. This approach also enables us to link the structural and functional connectivity more directly, harmonizing the multi-modal neuroimaging data.

Preprocessed data are modeled with Constrained Spherical Deconvolution (CSD) (Tournier et al., 2004) from which the fiber orientation distribution is estimated for gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid using the multi-shell multi-tissue approach. ROI masks are registered to each subject by warping the MNI template to each subject's B0-aligned T1 image, and then this transformation matrix is applied to the ROI masks, using ANTs. To extract the WM fibers connecting pairs of ROIs, probabilistic tractography is performed using Anatomy Constrained Tractography (Smith et al., 2015) in MRtrix3 for each of the ROIs in each participant. With FreeSurfer's segmentation of white matter and the warped ROI masks as anatomical constraints, “tckgen” function is used to generate 1 million streamlines (with a maximum angle of 22.5° and a maximum length of 250 mm) that run through each ROI. Then, "tckedit” function is used to select only streamlines that connect pairs of the ROIs. Furthermore, using Automated Fiber Quantification (AFQ; https://github.com/yeatmanlab/AFQ) (Yeatman et al., 2014), we remove tracts that considerably deviate from the group mean of tracts (i.e., Gaussian distance from the core of the tract >4 and length of a tract >4 standard deviation from the mean length), using 30 node points. The resulting tracts for the respective region pairs are then used as masks for which median fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) can be calculated.

Resting-state functional MRI

The T2∗-weighted BOLD fMRI data are used for resting-state functional connectivity analyses and preprocessed with fMRIPrep (https://fmriprep.org), a software package that selects the most robust approaches available in the functional MRI community (https://fmriprep.org/en/stable/). With a vast array of analytical approaches for examining fMRI, fMRIPrep takes advantage of the leading image processing suites and incorporates the most validated steps from each software into a single standardized pipeline, maximizing replicability across studies (Esteban et al., 2019). Preprocessing steps include head motion estimation using FSL's (https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki) MCFLIRT (Jenkinson et al., 2002), followed by slice-timing correction using AFNI's (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov) 3dTShift with timing for the middle of each TR as reference. The order of head motion estimation before slice timing is recommended by Power et al. (2017) for greater accuracy in head-motion estimation. Alignment between the fMRI time series and T1-weighted image is then performed using FreeSurfer bbregister routine (Greve and Fischl, 2009). To minimize loss of information, the fMRI time series are corrected for head-motion and registered to T1-weighted image all in one step by concatenating and applying one interpolation step using the Lanczos resampling. fMRIPrep outputs visual quality assessment reports for each individual in HTML to allow efficient but thorough quality check on the preprocessed data.

Preprocessed data are regressed for nuisance variables estimated from fMRIPrep including motion parameters and global signals. To ensure that the same number of voxels are included in each of the ROIs (see Fig. 3), a sphere of 10-mm diameter is created at the center of mass for each ROI, with the boundary of the sphere ending at GM-WM boundary to ensure the sphere only covers gray matter tissue. The time series for each ROI sphere are calculated as the mean over all voxels in the sphere. Strength of the functional connectivity are assessed by correlating the temporal signals between the two regions in each ROI pair. Correlation coefficients are converted to z-values using the Fisher z-transformation.

Analysis plan

To determine a sample size required to detect expected effects, a power analysis was conducted. A sample size of 250 individuals was considered sufficient to obtain the minimum detectable effect size of r = 0.28–0.32 at Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.05/120 (to account for testing 120 separate hypotheses) with 80 % power. Accordingly, the proposed study plans to recruit 100 patients with aMCI/AD, 100 patients with PD, and 50 HC.

To accomplish our first goal of examining whether altered effort and reward sensitivity serves as a mechanistic pathway linking apathy to fronto-striatal neurodegeneration, we will first determine how trial-level choices (per levels of reward and effort) relate to apathy and diagnosis. We hypothesize that apathy would be associated with reduced acceptance of offers in both groups (aMCI/AD and PD). A logistic mixed-effects regression model will be fitted to trial-level choice data. The model will include fixed effects for reward, effort, disease group (AD/aMCI vs. PD), continuous apathy scores (DAS scores), and their interactions. We will then determine how latent decision parameters (e.g., drift rate by effort) relate to apathy and diagnosis. We hypothesize that apathy will be associated with reduced incentivization by reward and/or increased sensitivity to effort costs. DDM parameters will be analyzed using robust linear regressions to assess effects of apathy, diagnosis, and their interaction, adjusting for age, sex, and cognition (MoCA). Neuroimaging analyses will focus on associations between DDM-derived parameters (particularly those linked to reward and effort sensitivity) and structural and functional metrics from ROIs (see Fig. 3). Neuroimaging measures will include gray matter volume (using T1-weighted images), white matter microstructure (fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity), and resting-state functional connectivity. Linear mixed-effects models will examine relationships between neural metrics, latent decision parameters, diagnosis, and apathy, including interactions.

In a subset of PD participants undergoing STN DBS, we will determine whether electrical manipulation of the STN directly alters reward and effort information processing at six months postoperatively (Visit 2, with DBS ‘on’ and DBS ‘off’). We hypothesize that as compared to ‘off’ DBS, ‘on’ DBS will result in changes in reward and effort sensitivities such that participants with stronger structural (white matter) connectivity between STN-DBS electrodes and cortical structures (OFC, vmPFC, or ACC) will show greater change in reward sensitivity. Trial-level choice data will be analyzed with logistic mixed-effects models including DBS condition (‘off’ and ‘on’), apathy, and their interaction. DDMs will be separately estimated for each DBS state (‘off’ and ‘on’), and resulting parameters will be compared using linear mixed models to assess DBS effects on reward and effort sensitivity. Finally, we will test whether structural connectivity between STN and cortical ROIs (e.g., ACC, vmPFC, OFC; see Fig. 3) moderates DBS-related changes in motivation by including DBS × connectivity interaction terms in linear models, adjusting for age, sex, baseline DAS, motor disease severity (MDS-UPDRS part 3), and MoCA. The effect of dopaminergic medications measured as the change in levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) (Tomlinson et al., 2010) will also be included as a covariate. We anticipate that some eligible patients will decline DBS surgery for reasons such as fear of surgery, motivation, or disease severity. To contextualize our findings, we will compare demographic, clinical, and behavioral characteristics (age, sex, baseline DAS, MDS-UPDRS Part 3, LEDD, and AGt performance) between patients who undergo DBS and those who decline. These comparisons will help assess potential group differences in motivation, cognition, or motor function and inform interpretation of the results.

Discussion

Apathy is a common neuropsychiatric symptom and a strong contributor to the disability and reduced quality of life for patients with neurodegenerative diseases (Aarsland et al., 2007; Massimo and Evans, 2014). Most mechanistic studies on apathy have employed single-level analyses of cross-sectional self-report assessments, combined with either behavioral paradigms or with neuroimaging techniques in single population cohorts. In this study, we leverage apathy as a common behavioral phenotype across neurodegenerative disorders, into a framework to explore both shared and disease-specific mechanisms. By adopting a transdiagnostic, multi-modal approach, we seek to integrate diverse perspectives—behavioral, cognitive, and neural—to capture mechanistic similarities and differences leading to apathy across conditions.

The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework illustrates six domains of human functioning and behavior: negative valence systems, positive valence systems, cognitive systems, social processes, arousal and regulatory systems and sensorimotor systems. For each domain, the RDoC framework invites us to utilize multiple levels of analysis or constructs, including genes, molecules, cells, circuits, physiology, behavior, self-report and paradigms. Our proposed approach integrates positive valence constructs (reward and effort valuation using an EBDM paradigm), self-report (DAS), neurophysiology (functional connectivity and tractography), and circuits (neuroanatomical data) (see Fig. 4). This framework has important implications for developing personalized treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes. By identifying neurocomputational profiles (e.g., effort insensitivity linked to specific circuit dysfunction), results from the proposed work may serve to guide the selection of optimal, targeted treatments matched to individual neural and motivational profiles, regardless of specific diagnosis.Fig. 4Types of data used to approach apathy using positive valence constructs derived from the RDoC framework.The proposed approach integrates multiple levels of information including a domain specific, effort-based decision-making paradigm (i.e., AGt), structural and functional neuroimaging (i.e., DTI and fMRI) and a self-report, dimensional apathy assessment (i.e., DAS). While self-report apathy measures rely on the individual's retrospective recollection of decisions to engage in activities (e.g., exercise, meet with friends, pursue a new hobby), behavioral paradigms allow the real-time examination of goal-directed behaviors during a short time frame (see Fig. 4). The ability to manipulate decision making processes in relation to effort and reward enables the robust examination of positive valence constructs that can then be related to in-vivo measures of physiology (i.e., functional and structural neuroimaging). Although widely accepted by the neuroscience community, task-based approaches are influenced by levels of attention and physical abilities. Thus, considering these pitfalls, this study has implemented cognitive thresholds for the populations examined and accounts for individuals' maximum motor effort levels.

One limitation of using AGt is that it only allows us to assess one aspect of goal-directed behavior – reward/effort valuation or effort-based choice. Goal directed behavior encompasses many other aspects, including foreground/background decision making, executive and attentional processes, all of which may be important for understanding why an individual may lose motivation. However, assessing all aspects of goal-directed behavior in a single study is challenging. Here our approach is to focus on indexing one crucial part of goal-directed behavior across multiple levels (from brain to behavior), but we anticipate this framework can also be applied, using alternative paradigms, to explore additional mechanisms underpinning goal-directed behavior.

Although apathy is also prevalent in psychiatric disorders, such as Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and schizophrenia (Bortolon et al., 2018; Lyngstad et al., 2023; Yuen et al., 2015), the primary focus of the present work is to examine apathy in the context of neurodegenerative diseases. Thus, for this study, individuals with history of pre-existing chronic, psychiatric illness defined as serious Axis I illness were excluded from participation. We acknowledge that the exclusion of psychiatric disorders limits the generalizability of our findings across all diagnostic categories where apathy occurs. Future research should aim to expand our transdiagnostic approach to include both, neurological and psychiatric disorders to delineate shared versus disorder-specific mechanisms of apathy across a broader range of conditions. Additionally, our exclusion of patients with moderate to severe dementia (CDR >1 or MMSE <20) may restrict the generalizability of our findings within this population.

One promise of neuroimaging, whether structural or functional, is that it might give us mechanistic insights into the neural bases of behavior. In this case, neural data are particularly important because of the transdiagnostic nature of our approach. We hypothesize that apathy has significantly overlapping root causes, neurally and cognitively, across AD and PD. However, it is also possible that apathy is generated by variations in different neural circuits across AD vs PD, just as it is plausible that apathy is rooted in different effort vs. reward sensitivity tradeoffs across these diseases. In this case, we would expect the degree of apathy to show different relationships with neural circuits in the AD vs. PD populations. Also, it is likely that apathy has multiple subtypes that can all occur within a given population. In this case, we would expect to observe transdiagnostic clusters that cut across diagnosis.

The advantages and limitations of diffusion and functional MRI have been well-described (Canario et al., 2021; Chung et al., 2011; Lv et al., 2018; Mori and Zhang, 2006). In general, the quality of all MRI imaging data is dependent upon how well participants remain still during the scan, which can be challenging for individuals with movement and cognitive deficits, such as the populations studied here. Furthermore, although dMRI-based tractography analysis and resting-state functional connectivity analysis are powerful tools to estimate large-scale brain connectivity, they are limited in their ability to explore microstructural effects of neurodegeneration. In addition, each type of these analyses has unique limitations in assessing brain connectivity. For example, DTI models can face challenges with crossing fibers (i.e., differently oriented fibers located within a single voxel), which can hinder the accuracy of fiber tracing. Similarly, resting-state fMRI is susceptible to variations in BOLD signals caused by nuisance variables, such as blood pressure, respiratory rate and uncontrollable cognitive processes during the scan. These limitations can impact the reliability and informativeness of neuroimaging data. However, many nuisance factors in diffusion and functional MRI (e.g., head motion, uncontrollable physiological and cognitive processes) can be mitigated by increasing sample size. Moreover, diffusion and functional MRI are non-invasive methods that provide unique measures of the static and dynamic nature of brain networks in-vivo.

In this study, we do not conduct task-related fMRI scans, where functional brain activation can be estimated during task completion (i.e., AGt). Due to the limited time available for each participant and the need to minimize fatigue in our cohort, we prioritize examining intrinsic structural and functional connectivity. In our processing pipeline, we apply the same set of gray matter ROIs to the pre-processed imaging data for both tractography and resting-state functional connectivity analyses. We then estimate the integrity of the connecting white matter tracts, as well as the spontaneous synchronization of functional activity, between these same sets of ROIs. This approach is beneficial for integrating structural and functional connectivity in the brain, effectively bridging multiple neuroimaging modalities across analyses.

The proposed transdiagnostic, multimodal approach seeks to comprehensively characterize apathy across and within neurodegenerative conditions by integrating behavioral, cognitive, and neuroanatomical perspectives. This protocol addresses limitations of traditional methods for studying neuropsychiatric symptoms and offers a robust framework for assessing apathy through diverse lenses. Our approach promises to contribute to the development of personalized, targeted treatments and further propel future research in this field.
