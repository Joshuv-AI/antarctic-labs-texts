Nanoenabled IL-15 Superagonist via Conditionally Stabilized Protein–Protein Interactions Eradicates Solid Tumors by Precise Immunomodulation
Authors: Pengwen Chen, Shangwei Li, Koji Nagaoka, Kazuhiro Kakimi, Kazunori Kataoka, Horacio Cabral
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein complexes are crucial structures that control many biological processes. Harnessing these structures could be valuable for therapeutic therapy. However, their instability and short lifespans need to be addressed for effective use. Here, we propose an innovative approach based on a functional polymeric cloak that coordinately anchors different domains of protein complexes and assembles them into a stabilized nanoformulation. As the polymer–protein association in the cloak is pH sensitive, the nanoformulation also allows targeting the release of the protein complexes to the acidic microenvironment of tumors for aiding their therapeutic performance. Building on this strategy, we developed an IL-15 nanosuperagonist (Nano-SA) by encapsulating the interleukin-15 (IL-15)/IL-15 Receptor α (IL-15Rα) complex (IL-15cx) for fostering synergistic transpresentation in tumors. Upon intravenous administration, Nano-SA stably circulated in the bloodstream, safeguarding the integrity of IL-15cx until reaching the tumor site, where it selectively released the active complex. Thus, Nano-SA significantly amplified the antitumor immune signals while diminishing systemic off-target effects. In murine colon cancer models, Nano-SA achieved potent immunotherapeutic effects, eradicating tumors without adverse side effects. These findings highlight the transformative potential of nanotechnology for advancing protein complex-based therapies.

Introduction

Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are fundamental to cellular functions and physiological processes.1−3 For example, in the immune system, a plethora of protein complexes control far-reaching networks,2 such as antigen presentation through the major histocompatibility complex3 and modulation of immune responses by cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions.4,5 Thus, there is great interest in harnessing protein complexes as promising candidates for therapeutic interventions. Nevertheless, PPIs are driven by noncovalent interactions with uncertain stability in vivo and extremely short effective time.6,7 These inherent intricacies pose significant challenges in leveraging protein complexes for therapeutic purposes.

Engineered fusion proteins and covalently conjugated protein complexes may overcome the challenges associated with the stability and effective duration of PPIs to generate new therapeutics.8−15 However, such approaches face severe hurdles, including the careful design of fusion protein structures and conjugation strategies to attain suitable potency,16,17 and the control of complicated processes to ensure the correct protein folding and purification.18 Moreover, the in vivo stability of such formulations remains vulnerable to proteases in physiological environments.19 Thus, there is a major need for disruptive approaches that facilitate the construction of stable protein complexes for advancing innovative therapeutics.

Nanotechnology has promise for constructing stable protein complexes to spur the progress of next-generation treatments. By precise manipulation of materials at the nanoscale, it would be possible to immobilize and stabilize protein complexes in biological environments. Moreover, the nanostructures can be engineered for improving the pharmacokinetics20−22 and enabling conditional activation,23−25 thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and safety of the protein complexes. To demonstrate this concept, we developed a pH-sensitive polymeric system that wraps around protein complexes to mediate their tumor-targeted delivery. We showcased an example using a complex of interleukin-15 (IL-15) and IL-15 Receptor α (IL-15Rα), as this complex generates a superagonist with superior immune activation over monomeric IL-15.26−28 In fact, a fusion protein formulation engaging IL-15 and IL-15Rα (IL-15SA) has been investigated in the clinic,29−31 though its severe toxicity has limited its approval to locally injected applications.32,33 On such basis, delivery strategies like nanogels and bioconjugates have been proposed to enhance the performance of IL-15SA fusion proteins.34,35

In our nanodriven design, rather than directly altering the protein structure like in fusion proteins, we used a preassembled complex of native IL-15 and native IL-15Rα (IL-15cx) that was coated with a functional block copolymer (Figure 1A). This polymer showed the ability to coat proteins via concurrent ion complexation and pH-sensitive covalent bonds, as shown in our previous study.36 Here, we confirmed that such a coating has a further function to stabilize the PPI between IL-15 and IL-15Rα, which effectively immobilized the complex, isolating it from the surroundings and yielding an IL-15 nanosuperagonist (Nano-SA) with enhanced efficacy. Compared to IL-15SA fusion proteins, the Nano-SA bypassed the complicated engineering on the protein structure and was formed by a straightforward self-assembly process. In mouse models, Nano-SA displayed improved pharmacokinetics and stably delivered intact IL-15cx upon systemic administration. Moreover, due to the pH sensitivity of the polymeric cloak, Nano-SA can sense the acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) to ensure tumor-selective activation. Thus, in murine colon cancer models, Nano-SA profoundly inflamed the TME to attain tumor eradication without inducing immune-related side effects, which are commonly reported in the case of IL-15SA fusion proteins. Our study effectively illustrates the potential of polymeric coatings for stabilizing protein complexes toward developing innovative therapeutic agents.

Results and Discussion

Polymeric Cloak Wraps around a Single IL-15/IL-15Rα Complex

Nano-SA was generated by coating IL-15cx with a pH-sensitive biocompatible polymer. This polymer comprises a protective poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) segment and a poly(l-lysine) (pLL) segment, which was partially modified with carboxydimethyl-maleic anhydride (CDM) (Supporting Figures S1 and S2).36 To prepare Nano-SA, we designed a pH-controlled titration method. The polymers were dissolved in an acidic buffer (pH ≈ 5.0) to avoid cross-linking between the polymer chains and then mixed into a basic buffer (pH ≈ 8.0) containing the preassembled IL-15cx to initiate the coating reactions. In this process, the polymer concurrently anchored IL-15cx through pH-sensitive covalent bonds formed between CDM groups and the amines on the surface of the proteins (Figure 1A), meanwhile the amines in the polymer formed ion complexation with the carboxylates on the proteins. Moreover, the cloak is further stabilized by pH-sensitive cross-linking between the amine moieties and CDM groups in the polymers.

The formation of Nano-SA was first confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), in which Nano-SA dominantly remained at the higher position, indicating the formation of structures with an increased molecular size (Figure 1B). On the other hand, the IL-15 and IL-15Rα exhibited bands at their expected molecular weight positions. The IL-15cx showed separated bands at the positions of IL-15 and IL-15Rα, probably because the association of the two domains relies on the tertiary structure, which is susceptible to the denature process in SDS-PAGE. To analyze the formation of the IL-15cx and the Nano-SA, their diffusion kinetics were measured by fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) after labeling IL-15 and IL-15Rα with AlexaFluor 647 (A647) and AlexaFluor 488 (A488), respectively. The results showed high consistency in the autocorrelation function (ACF) curves and the diffusion time in the two fluorescence channels (Figure 1C,D). Moreover, the association between IL-15 and IL-15Rα in the solution was confirmed by a cross-correlation analysis (correlation coefficient C = 1.050; Supporting Figure S3) of the FCS results. The stoichiometric pairing of IL-15 with IL-15Rα in IL-15cx was further validated by comparing the single molecular fluorescence intensities (count per molecule) with IL-15 or IL-15Rα monomeric standard samples (Supporting Table S1). Comparing to free IL-15cx, Nano-SA showed delayed diffusion kinetics in both fluorescence channels, evidenced by the lagged ACF curves and increased diffusion time (Figure 1C,D), which indicate the incorporation of IL-15cx in larger molecular structures. The FCS-based cross-correlation analysis of Nano-SA also displayed high correlation (C = 1.072) in the two fluorescence channels (Supporting Figure S3). Moreover, Nano-SA exhibited similar count per molecule in both fluorescence channels as free IL-15cx (Supporting Table S1), confirming that one IL-15cx was loaded in one single Nano-SA particle and that both IL-15 and IL-15Rα were coencapsulated as a complex with strict stoichiometric ratio.

The structure of the Nano-SA was further investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Uranyl acetate can stain the poly(amino acid) segments and proteins.37,38 Thus, the cores of Nano-SA were visualized as uniform dark dots with a number-weighted average diameter = 21 ± 5 nm by TEM (Figure 1E,F), while the PEG block of the polymer is invisible due to its low electron density.39 Dynamic light scattering (DLS) determined the hydrodynamic diameter (Z-average size) of Nano-SA to be 39 ± 3.0 nm (Figure 1G,H). The difference between the core size from TEM and the hydrodynamic size from DLS confirmed the PEG shell on the surface of Nano-SA,40 which effectively shields the proteins and masks their surface charge (Figure 1I).

Nano-SA Protects IL-15cx and Regulates Its Bioactivity by Sensing Environmental pH

The ability of Nano-SA to stabilize IL-15cx was studied under physiological conditions. To dynamically monitor the integrity of IL-15cx, we developed a probe system based on fluorescence renascence energy transfer (FRET). This system involves A647-labeled IL-15 and AlexaFluor 555 (A555)-labeled IL-15Rα to form a FRET pair. The FRET signal between A555 (donor) and A647 (acceptor) can reflect the integrity of IL-15cx in real time (Figure 2A). Compared to free IL-15cx, Nano-SA can shield the FRET pair from interference by external proteins even under intratumoral conditions, as demonstrated by the slower decrease in the FRET signal from the Nano-SA sample at pH 6.5 (Supporting Figure S4B and C). We then investigated the stability of the samples in murine plasma to simulate the condition of physiological environment. Clearly, a significant decrease in the FRET signal was observed from free IL-15cx, indicating its instability under physiological conditions (Figure 2B). In contrast, Nano-SA maintained a high FRET signal, confirming its capability to protect IL-15cx. The FCS-based cross-correlation analysis also showed that Nano-SA maintained a high cross-correlation level similar to the initial state (Supporting Figure S5), while IL-15cx exhibited a clear loss of cross-correlation, supporting the ability of Nano-SA to stabilize IL-15cx in physiological conditions. Even in the trypsin-containing environment, Nano-SA still showed resistance against degradation and protected the integrity of IL-15cx (Supporting Figure S4B).

Given the pH sensitivity of the covalent binding between CDM and amine, the polymeric cloak on Nano-SA was expected to exhibit an acidic pH-triggered detachment. This hypothesis was validated by SDS-PAGE of Nano-SA incubated under different pH conditions (Figure 2C), which showed the triggered cargo release from Nano-SA at intratumoral pH 6.5 and 7.0, but not at physiological pH 7.4. Moreover, the changes in the hydrodynamic size of Nano-SA at different pH values were analyzed by FCS. The result confirmed a sudden decrease of the size at pH lower than 7.0 (Figure 2D). In addition, the release profile of IL-15cx from Nano-SA was studied by dialysis at physiological pH 7.4 and intratumoral pH 6.5 (Figure 2E). The release of proteins from Nano-SA occurred at pH 6.5, but not at 7.4. Moreover, the released proteins were unable to pass through the dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) at 20k Da, suggesting the released species existed as IL-15cx rather than IL-15 monomers.

The pH-sensitive polymeric cloak on Nano-SA facilitates switchable regulation of the IL-15cx bioactivity. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) confirmed that Nano-SA efficiently masked IL-15cx from interacting with the detection antibodies (Figure 2F). However, upon activation in an acidic buffer, Nano-SA restored its affinity to the same level as native IL-15cx. In KHYG-1 cells, the intact Nano-SA prevented the binding of IL-15cx to the cell surface, while activated Nano-SA regained the binding affinity to the cells (Figure 2G,H). Thus, Nano-SA promoted a switchable agonistic function in the proliferation of KHYG-1 cells (Figure 2I). Moreover, compared to monomeric IL-15, IL-15cx and activated Nano-SA showed a stronger stimulative effect on KHYG-1 cell proliferation, confirming the superagonistic potency.

Nano-SA Improves Pharmacokinetics to Achieve Stable IL-15cx Delivery

The blood circulation of IL-15cx and Nano-SA following intravenous (i.v.) injection was studied by intravital confocal microscopy (IVCLSM) observing the skin vessels of mice. The IL-15cx and Nano-SA in this study were constructed with IL-15 and IL-15Rα labeled with A647 and A488, respectively, so that both components can be simultaneously traced. After injection, Nano-SA remained in blood circulation and avoided extravasation to the skin (Figure 3A,B). Quantitative blood circulation profile showed that Nano-SA extended the fluorescence intensities of both A647-labeled IL-15 and A488-labeled IL-15Rα in the vessels for more than 2 h (Figure 3C). In contrast, IL-15cx was rapidly cleared and extravasated from the vessels into the skin tissue (Figure 3A–C). Noteworthy, the molecular weight of IL-15cx is around 40 kDa, and macromolecules with this size have shown limited extravasation into the skin.41 Thus, the rapid leakage of IL-15cx from vessels observed here suggests that IL-15cx may break down in the bloodstream. To confirm this hypothesis, we tested the stability of IL-15cx and Nano-SA during circulation in blood by applying a FRET-based probe system to the IVCLSM measurement. After i.v. injection of the IL-15cx FRET pair, only a transient FRET signal was observed in the vessel, which vanished in 10 min (Figure 3D,E). On the other hand, Nano-SA displayed a stable FRET signal in the vessels that lasted for over 200 min. These results indicate that Nano-SA maintains the IL-15 complex during circulation in the blood, whereas IL-15cx is immediately disrupted in the bloodstream.

Because the prolonged circulation of nanomedicines allows leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of solid tumors to achieve a high tumor accumulation,20,42 we evaluated the biodistribution of Nano-SA in mice bearing MC38 tumors after i.v. injection. Clearly, Nano-SA promoted 3-fold higher accumulation of both IL-15 (A647, red fluorescence) and IL-15Rα (A488, green fluorescence) in the tumor compared to IL-15cx (Figure 3F,G). In the organs, Nano-SA showed a comparable distribution of IL-15 and IL-15Rα (Figure 3G). However, the distributions of IL-15 and IL-15Rα after injecting free IL-15cx were greatly different in the lungs, which showed higher IL-15 levels than IL-15Rα levels, and in the kidneys, which presented higher IL-15Rα levels than IL-15 levels (Figure 3H and Supporting Figure S6). Such a discrepancy in the biodistributions of IL-15 and IL-15Rα in different organs further supports the dissociation of the IL-15cx observed in the in vivo FRET study.

Nano-SA Shows Tumor-Specific Activation to Regulate Systemic Exposure

Nano-SA is designed to sense the intratumoral acidification for mediating its activation, i.e., removing the polymeric cloak and presenting IL-15cx in tumor tissues. To visualize the polymer stripping from Nano-SA in situ, we generated an activation indicator of Nano-SA (AI-Nano-SA) that can be traced in in vivo experimental settings (Figure 4A). AI-Nano-SA was achieved by introducing a quencher of A647 (QSY-21) in the PEG-pLL(CDM) polymer (Supporting Figure S7A) and engaging with the IL-15cx made from A647-labeled IL-15 and A488-labeled IL-15Rα. In this formulation, the fluorescence from A647 (red fluorescence) is transiently quenched by QSY-21 under physiological conditions, while the fluorescence from A488 (green fluorescence) is constantly visible. Upon polymer detachment at low pH, AI-Nano-SA is dequenched to retrieve the red fluorescence (Supporting Figure S7B). Thus, the intensity ratio of red-to-green fluorescence (R/G ratio) can be used to indicate the activation level of Nano-SA. AI-Nano-SA was intravenously injected in mice bearing CT26 tumors. The red fluorescence signal was observed at the tumor area by an in vivo imaging system (IVIS), and the signal intensity showed a time-dependent increase (Figure 4B). Twelve-hours post injection, the tissues were collected and imaged ex vivo by IVIS. The green fluorescence thoroughly presented in several tissues including lungs, liver, kidneys, and tumor (Figure 4C), indicating the distribution of the AI-Nano-SA particles there. On the other hand, the red fluorescence, indicating the activated AI-Nano-SA, showed significant intensities from the tumor. Lungs and liver also exhibited slight red fluorescence signals, which could be attributed to the uptake and degradation of AI-Nano-SA by phagocytes in these organs.20 Quantitative assessment of the R/G ratio showed significantly higher activation of the AI-Nano-SA in tumors than in other tissues (Figure 4D), confirming the tumor selectivity of the formulation.

IVCLSM imaging of AI-Nano-SA was used to observe the real-time activation process in healthy tissues and MC38 tumors (Figure 4E). In skin, the green fluorescence of AI-Nano-SA appeared in the blood vessels, whereas its red fluorescence was not detectable in the vessels nor in the peripheral tissues. These results indicate AI-Nano-SA is stable during blood circulation and in healthy tissues. In the tumor, the green fluorescence of AI-Nano-SA was evident within both the blood vessels after i.v. injection, indicating that the formulation reaches the tumor in an intact form. As time passed, AI-Nano-SA extravasated into the tumor tissue, where the acidic intratumoral pH stripped the polymeric cloak, retrieving the red fluorescence (Figure 4E). Both the green and red signals increased with time, as more AI-Nano-SA accessed the tumor and got activated. Quantitative analysis of the R/G ratio confirmed that the activation degree of AI-Nano-SA also augmented with time. Moreover, by doing a three-dimensional reconstruction from Z-stack images of the observation area, we confirmed the thorough distribution of activated AI-Nano-SA in the tumor tissue (Figure 4F). The results confirmed the selective release of IL-15cx into the TME, which would enhance the immune activity in the tumors and reduce systemic exposure to avoid immune-related adverse events.

Nano-SA Elicits Tumor-Targeted Immunomodulation

The tumor specificity of the immune response triggered by Nano-SA was evaluated in mice bearing MC38 tumors and compared with that of IL-15cx. After intensive injection of Nano-SA or IL-15cx (3 i.v. injections; 10 μg IL-15cx equivalence (e.q.) per injection), blood, liver, and spleen were collected from mice for immune analysis (Figure 5A). In mice treated with IL-15cx, downstream cytokines like interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were upregulated in blood (Figure 5B), indicating a systemic inflammatory response.36 Also, antagonists of IL-15 such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) upregulated, suggesting the initiation of counteractive anti-inflammatory signals,43−45 which have been reported for cytokine-based therapies.36,46,47 Conversely, Nano-SA significantly reduced the downstream cytokine responses in the blood (Figure 5B), demonstrating a lower systemic immune response.

The immune cell subtypes in the blood were also screened to reveal the results of immune activation. We first focused on natural killer (NK) cells, which are the main responsive cells to IL-15.48 The results showed that IL-15cx significantly expanded the NK cell number in blood circulation, whereas the level of NK cells for Nano-SA remained close to that of phosphate buffered saline (PBS)-treated mice (Figure 5C). Moreover, IL-15cx upregulated the CD69 expression in NK cells (Figure 5D), indicating that the cells were activated.49 Similar results were observed in the spleen (Figure 5E). In fact, we directly observed splenomegaly induced by IL-15cx (Figure 5F), which is clear evidence of IL-15-associated immunotoxicity.49,50 Increased NK cell frequency was also found in the liver of mice treated with IL-15cx, but not for Nano-SA (Figure 5G). To intuitively evaluate the activity of immune cells in the spleen and liver, we tested the in vitro cytotoxicity against YAC-1 model cells (Supporting Figures S8 and S9). Briefly, the splenocytes and liver-infiltrating leukocytes isolated from the mice were coincubated with YAC-1 cells, a murine lymphoma cell line which is sensitive to be killed by immune cells including NK cells.51 The cytotoxicity of these cells against YAC-1 cells can reflect the activity of the immune cells in the spleen and liver. Clearly, the cells collected from the spleens and livers of mice treated with IL-15cx killed the YAC-1 cells (Supporting Figure S9), inferring the potential risk for inducing organ damage. On the other hand, the cells from Nano-SA-treated mice showed lower cytotoxicity against YAC-1 cells, which suggests a reduced threat to off-target tissues. Another significantly altered cell type by IL-15cx treatment is the T cell. In blood, spleen, and liver, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells showed significantly upregulated CD69 and Ki67 expression (Supporting Figures S10–S13), suggesting the enhanced activation and proliferation. However, Nano-SA treatment induced a much lower response. Together, these results indicate that Nano-SA solved the problem of the systemic immune response induced by IL-15cx, which could reduce the immune-related toxicities (Supporting Figure S14).

In tumors, the effects of IL-15cx and Nano-SA were opposite to those observed systemically. The infiltration of immune cells in tumors after IL-15cx treatment showed no significant improvement comparing to the control group (Figure 5H–J), which could be attributed to the insufficient intratumoral accumulation and instability of IL-15cx upon i.v. administration. In contrast, Nano-SA led to the expansion of effector cells, including NK, NKT, and CD8+ T cells in the TME. Similar results were observed in mice bearing CT26 tumors (Supporting Figures S15–S18), in which IL-15cx induced a stronger systemic response but lower intratumoral immune infiltration compared to Nano-SA. Moreover, we evaluated the cytokine response in CT26 tumor tissue (Supporting Figure S17). Interestingly, IFN-γ and IL-6 were enriched in tumor by Nano-SA treatment, indicating an enhanced intratumoral inflammation response. However, IL-15cx induced higher levels of IL-10 and TGF-β, which could be resulted from the systemic antagonistic reaction. These findings indicate that Nano-SA enhanced the immune agonism in tumor, achieving a tumor-targeted immunomodulation.

Nano-SA Conducts Effective and Safe Immunotherapy in Colon Cancers and Synergizes with ICIs

The therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of Nano-SA was first assessed in a subcutaneous (s.c.) MC38 tumor model (Figure 6A). Nano-SA clearly exhibited effective inhibition of tumor growth, resulting in prolonged survival of the animals and complete response (CR) in 4 out of 5 mice in the group (Figure 6B,C). On the other hand, IL-15cx failed to demonstrate any benefit over the control group. Histological analysis of the tumor sections after Nano-SA treatment showed tissue damage in hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, meanwhile the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay confirmed the stronger apoptotic/necrotic process of tumor cells by Nano-SA treatment (Figure 6D). Moreover, during the IL-15cx treatment, the bodyweight of the animals dropped immediately after the first injection (Supporting Figure S19), indicating the toxicity of IL-15cx.

To further explore the safety profile, we evaluated the serum IFN-γ before and after the treatments as an indicator of systemic immune activation. While IL-15cx induced an averagely 7.5-fold increase in serum IFN-γ over the baseline, the response from Nano-SA was not significantly different to the control group (Supporting Figure S20). Moreover, histological analysis and serum biomarkers measurement were performed to assess the organ damage level. In H&E staining, no clear pathological changes could be found in the heart, liver, lung, and kidney for both IL-15cx and Nano-SA (Supporting Figure S21). However, the morphology of the white pulp in the spleen of the mice treated with IL-15cx was different from the control and the Nano-SA-treated mice. The boundaries between red and white pulps of the spleens in the IL-15cx group were not clear, indicating immunotoxicity from IL-15cx. In addition, the level of serum biomarkers associated with liver damage, including total protein (TP) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), significantly increased in IL-15cx-treated mice, but not in the animals receiving Nano-SA treatment (Supporting Figure S22). These results demonstrated the capability of Nano-SA to achieve an effective and safe immunotherapy against MC38 tumors.

The performance of Nano-SA was further studied in an orthotopic (o.t.) MC38 colon tumor model (Figure 6E). Nano-SA was again able to significantly inhibit the tumors (Supporting Figure S23), leading to a CR of 3 out of 5 mice. Moreover, therapeutic experiments in the CT26 colon tumor model with a lower dose (3 μg IL-15cx e.q. per injection) further confirmed the superior efficacy of Nano-SA over IL-15cx (Supporting Figure S24).

IL-15 treatment has been found to stimulate the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, e.g., PD-L1, on the cancer cells, which could potentially synergize the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).44 In our experiment, we confirmed that Nano-SA also upregulated PD-L1 in the TME (Figure 6F). Thus, we combined Nano-SA with the anti-PD-L1 antibody (aPD-L1) to treat MC38 tumors (Figure 6G). For a clear comparison between the combinational and mono therapies, a lower dose (3 μg IL-15 e.q. per injection) was used. In this dose, IL-15cx, either as a monotherapy or in combination with aPD-L1, failed to show any improved efficacy over the control groups (Figure 6H,I). However, the Nano-SA monotherapy still induced CR in 3 out of 6 mice (Figure 6I). Moreover, combining aPD-L1 with Nano-SA further improved the activity of CD8+ T cells, as suggested by the higher granzyme B level (Figure 6J), promoting a higher CR rate (5 out of 6 mice) (Figure 6I). Notably, no significant difference in bodyweight changes among animals receiving different treatments was noticed, confirming the safety of the treatments (Supporting Figure S25). The cured mice also showed a strong immunological memory, as none of them showed relapsed tumors after rechallenging with a reinoculation of MC38 cells (Figure 6K).

Conclusions

Nano-SA upholds the delicate structure of IL-15cx and proficiently transports the complex to the TME after systemic administration. Nano-SA stably circulated in the bloodstream and selectively dissociated inside tumors, thereby diminishing exposure to healthy tissues while concurrently augmenting intratumoral effects. Thus, Nano-SA achieved tumor-targeted immunomodulation to precisely activate a strong antitumor immunity within the TME. These features facilitated a safe and effective immunotherapy against colon tumor models.

Despite the inherent high affinity between IL-15 and IL-15Rα, marked by a binding constant of 100 pM,52 the association of the complex may not be fully stable in vivo. In fact, our in vivo FRET study demonstrated that IL-15cx is immediately disrupted in the bloodstream. This fast dissociation could be attributed to the dilution of IL-15cx upon iv injections and to the replacement of IL-15 and IL-15Rα in IL-15cx by serum proteins. Some IL-15SA formulations have addressed this problem by covalently bridging IL-15 and IL-15Rα through protein fusion approaches.53,54 However, such strategies require customization and may affect the potency. Instead, our nanobased straightforward approach permits preparing the superagonist without any protein engineering. Nano-SA allows the isolation of IL-15cx, which keeps the local IL-15cx concentration inside the nanoparticle and prevents the dissociation of the complex after dilution into the blood. Moreover, the polymeric cloak avoids interaction of the complex with serum proteins to deliver intact IL-15cx. Thus, Nano-SA circumvents the premature exposure of IL-15cx before reaching the target, thereby mitigating potency loss.

Nano-SA improves the pharmacokinetics and induces an effective intratumoral accumulation. IL-15 monomer has poor pharmacokinetics with a half-life less than 1 h.55 Such fast clearance requires an intensive dosing schedule to achieve effective potency.56 Antibody fragments can be fused in IL-15SA formulations for extending the half-life, but the clinical performance of the strategy was modest in the case of i.v. administration.57−59 Thus, some IL-15SA formulations switched to s.c. administration, though such route faces the problem of low bioavailability.57 Our Nano-SA extended the circulation half-life more than ∼30-fold compared with free IL-15cx in blood after intravenous injection. Moreover, Nano-SA avoided rapid extravasation in healthy tissue and facilitated the intratumoral accumulation due to the ∼40 nm size,60 leading to a higher on-target delivery to the tumor. These factors of Nano-SA ensure a high presentation of IL-15cx in the TME, leading to a strong antitumor efficacy.

The systemic immune response, such as the expansion of peripheral NK and CD8+ T cells, has been used as an indicator of potency in clinical studies of IL-15SA.61−63 However, such an interpretation needs reconsideration. As shown in our study, IL-15cx induced strong activation of peripheral NK and CD8+ T cells but failed to improve the immune infiltration in tumors and promote antitumor effects. In contrast, Nano-SA exhibits efficacy despite low peripheral immune activation. This suggests a shift in the understanding of cytokine-based therapy by emphasizing the importance of delivering a substantial cytokine concentration to tumors. This observation challenges the earlier assumption regarding the importance of extending systemic retention, and support the insights gleaned from recent studies.36,64 What is certain is that hyperactivation of systemic immunity poses risks. Our results confirmed the elevation of NK and CD8+ T cells in the liver after IL-15cx treatment, likely contributing to the increased hematological indicators associated with liver damage. Clinical trials of IL-15SA also reported immune-related adverse events from systemic administration,61−63 which could be attributed to the unspecific accumulation in off-target organs including the liver.65 Thus, local administration, such as intravesical injection IL-15SA, has been used to clinically develop this formulation.27,32 Recently, an enzymatic-sensitive formulation of IL-15 has been developed for enhancing tumor selectivity.66 However, the heterogeneous enzymatic conditions in tumors may pose an obstacle for its clinical translation.67,68 Nano-SA offers an overarching solution by sensing intratumoral acidification, which is a hallmark feature of the TME,69,70 for unleashing IL-15cx activity.

In summary, Nano-SA is a pH-sensitive superagonist formulation of IL-15 that realizes a precise immunotherapy against solid tumors. As the formulation is based on biocompatible materials and validated clinical data, the Nano-SA presents significant potential for translation into clinical studies as an antitumor agent. Moreover, this technology presents a platform for viable tumor-targeted delivery of other protein complexes beyond IL-15cx, offering an innovative design rationale for complex biologics with a tumor-specific activation capability.
