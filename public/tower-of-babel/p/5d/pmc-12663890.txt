Inhibition of Histone Methyltransferase G9a Activates Autophagy and Provides Protection in Dry Eye Disease
Authors: Zeying Chen, Yun Tang, Jiaxuan Jiang, Di Zhang, Yiran Chu, Boda Li, Qi Zhang, Huayong Zhang, Kai Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the expression dynamics and functional role of the histone methyltransferase G9a in dry eye disease (DED).MethodsAn in vitro DED model was established using human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) cultured in hyperosmotic medium (90-mM NaCl). For the in vivo model, mice were treated with 0.075% benzalkonium chloride eye drops twice daily for 14 days. Phenotypic and mechanistic analyses were conducted using quantitative real-time PCR, western blotting, immunofluorescence staining, flow cytometry, and chromatin immunoprecipitation assays. Disease severity was assessed by fluorescein sodium staining and the phenol red thread test. Histopathological analysis included periodic acid–Schiff staining, hematoxylin and eosin staining, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsG9a expression was significantly downregulated in the in vitro DED model, which was mediated by mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of G9a mitigated DED-induced damage in HCECs by reducing inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress while enhancing autophagic flux. Mechanistically, G9a inhibition alleviated transcriptional repression at the autophagy-related 7 (ATG7) promoter, decreasing H3K9me2 occupancy and promoting autophagy. In vivo, G9a inhibition improved disease outcomes, including (1) enhanced ocular surface integrity and increased tear production, (2) restoration of goblet cell density, and (3) reduced expression of corneal inflammatory markers.ConclusionsG9a downregulation confers protective effects in DED by promoting autophagy. Mechanistically, G9a inhibition enhances autophagy through the reduction of H3K9me2-mediated transcriptional silencing of ATG7. This G9a–autophagy axis offers significant protection against DED in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface, primarily characterized by the disruption of tear film homeostasis.1 This condition results from an imbalance in the ocular surface microenvironment, which may arise due to abnormalities in tear quality, quantity, or fluid dynamics.2 These alterations are often accompanied by increased tear osmolality, inflammation of ocular surface tissues, epithelial damage, and neurosensory abnormalities.3 Clinically, patients with DED commonly report symptoms such as ocular dryness, burning sensations, photophobia, foreign body sensation, pain, and visual disturbances.4

The global prevalence of DED ranges from 5% to 50%, with a steadily increasing trend.5 In Asia, the prevalence is approximately 20.1%,6 making DED one of the most prevalent ocular disorders. Among the various risk factors associated with DED, environmental factors play a critical role.7,8 Lifestyle choices such as sleep disturbances, prolonged use of video display terminals, poor dietary habits, and smoking, alongside external conditions such as high-altitude hypoxia, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, low humidity, air pollution,9–11 and prolonged exposure to air-conditioning or heating systems, significantly increase the risk of developing DED.12

DED is characterized by an extremely complex pathological process, with the core features including oxidative stress–induced inflammation and apoptosis of corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells.13–17 These mechanisms synergistically exacerbate one other, creating a self-perpetuating vicious cycle.3 Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting redox balance,18,19 apoptosis inhibition, and inflammation suppression20–22 have emerged as promising approaches to mitigate DED-associated damage.

In response to environmental stresses, the body undergoes reversible changes in gene expression that do not involve alterations in the DNA sequence.23,24 This phenomenon, known as epigenetics, has become a focal point in disease mechanism research. A growing body of evidence suggests a significant role of epigenetic mechanisms in the pathogenesis of various ocular diseases.25–27 Uveal melanoma (UM) and retinoblastoma (RB) are critically regulated by epigenetic homeostasis.28 Emerging evidence indicates that histone methylases and N6-methyladenosine (m6A) hypermethylation-induced histone methylation contribute to UM progression.29,30 Furthermore, histone lactylation has been shown to activate glycolysis in RB via a positive feedback mechanism.31 The corneal epithelium under stress is regulated by several types of epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, microRNA changes, and histone modifications.32–35 Histone modification, a key epigenetic mechanism,36 involves covalent post-translational modifications such as methylation, phosphorylation, acetylation, and lactylation, all of which impact gene transcription and play an important role in cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune diseases, fibrosis diseases, and other diseases.37 Histone H3, in particular, is one of the most modified histones,38 with dimethylation at lysine 9 (H3K9me2) representing a stable mark of gene expression regulation. This modification can reduce chromatin accessibility by restricting the binding of transcription factors and coactivators in the promoter region,39 leading to transcriptional repression of downstream genes.

Environmental stress–induced DED is likely associated with epigenetic modifications, particularly H3K9me2 binding in gene promoter regions. G9a (euchromatic histone lysine methyltransferase 2 [EHMT2]) is a key enzyme that catalyzes the formation of H3K9me2 and functions as an epigenetic repressor,40,41 inhibiting the transcription of downstream genes. G9a has been linked to autophagy regulation,42,43 where its inhibition can upregulate the expression of autophagy-related genes and enhance autophagy flux.44 autophagy-related 7 (ATG7), an E1-like ubiquitin-activating enzyme, is critical for the initiation of autophagy, facilitating the formation and elongation of autophagosomes.45,46 The expression level of ATG7 is directly correlated with autophagic activity and plays a central role in regulating this process. Notably, the transcriptional regulation of ATG7 is influenced by H3K9me2,47,48 a histone modification associated with methyltransferase G9a.

Autophagy is a highly conserved cellular process that mediates the degradation of cytoplasmic components within lysosomes, thereby playing a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis.49 Emerging evidence underscores its essential function in modulating immune and inflammatory responses. It is widely recognized that autophagy exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects, acting as a central regulatory mechanism in immunity and inflammation.50,51 In corneal epithelial inflammatory diseases, emerging evidence suggests that pharmacological activation of autophagy via small-molecule drugs ameliorates inflammatory responses in Aspergillus keratitis and corneal injury.52,53 Conversely, autophagy inhibition through targeted signaling pathways has been demonstrated to induce cell death in herpes simplex keratitis.54 Several studies have demonstrated that controlled activation of autophagy can protect against the main pathological features of DED, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis.55,56 Further upregulation of autophagy can prevent the pathological progression of DED,57–60 making autophagy a critical factor in its pathogenesis. Inhibition of G9a by inhibitors or gene knockdown to activate autophagy has been shown to prevent disease progression in various cancers, including gastric cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, breast cancer, and multiple myeloma.61–64 However, no direct evidence exists to demonstrate the involvement of G9a in the regulation of autophagy in DED.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the expression of G9a in DED and explore the mechanisms by which G9a regulation influences autophagy to protect against DED. This research provides new insights into the pathogenesis of DED and offers potential therapeutic strategies for its treatment.

Methods

Cell Culture and the In Vitro Hyperosmotic Dry Eye Model

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs; CRL-11135; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in Gibco Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. To stimulate a hyperosmotic environment resembling DED, HCECs were exposed to hyperosmotic medium achieved by adding 90-mM sodium chloride (NaCl) to the culture medium.55,56,59 The optimal NaCl concentration for hyperosmotic solutions was determined via cell viability assay (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The experimental design involved three pharmacological interventions: (1) G9a inhibition, 18-hour pretreatment with UNC0642 (T4166; TargetMol, Boston, MA, USA); (2) autophagy blockade, 1-hour treatment with chloroquine (CQ; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA); and (3) mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation, 30-minute pretreatment with MHY1485 (T1908; TargetMol). All treatments were administered prior to 24-hour exposure to 90-mM NaCl–induced hyperosmotic stress.

RNA Isolation and Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using FreeZol Reagent (R711; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Then, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript gDNA Removal RT MasterMix (CW2020; CoWin Biotech, Jiangsu, China). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the MagicSYBR Mixture (CW3008; CoWin Biotech) on the ABI QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), with β-actin serving as an internal control. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method.65,66 Primer sequences are provided in the Table.

Western Blot

Protein extraction was carried out using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (CW2333S; CoWin Biotech). The lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein solution. Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were separated using a Nuclear/Cytosol Extraction kit (P0027; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (CW0014S; CoWin Biotech). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE gels (FD346-100; FDbio, Guangzhou, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Vazyme Biotech). Primary antibodies used included β-actin (81115-1-RR, 1:20,000; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), G9a (DY1109, 1:1000; Abways, San Diego, CA, USA), H3K9me2 (CY5761, 1:1000; Abways), LC3A/B (12741S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p62 (5114S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), ATG7 (CY5658, 1:1000; Abways), mTOR (CY5306, 1:1000; Abways), phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR; CY6571, 1:1000; Abways), TNF-α (HA722022, 1:1000; HUABIO, Woburn, MA, USA), IL-6 (R1414-2, 1:1000; HUABIO), IL-1β (HA601036, 1:1000; HUABIO), and histone H3 (CY6587, 1:1000; Abways). Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (FDM007, 1:10,000; FDR007, 1:10,000; both, FDbio). Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Vazyme Biotech), and band intensity was quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Cellular Immunofluorescence

HCECs pretreated with UNC0642 and exposed to hyperosmotic medium were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. Cells were then permeabilized with Triton X-100 (0.5% in PBS; Abs9155; Absin Bioscience, Shanghai, China) for 1 hour. After blocking with 5% donkey serum for 30 minutes, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with G9a or H3K9me2 monoclonal antibodies (DY1109 and CY5761, respectively; both, 1:1000; Abways). After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) and incubated with Alexa Fluor 568–conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303; ServiceBio, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining for 5 minutes. Images were acquired and analyzed using the Leica Thunder system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Cell Viability Assay

For the cell viability rescue experiment, HCECs were cultured in 96-well plates and were pretreated with different concentrations of UNC0642 (an effective and specific G9a/GLP inhibitor) for 18 hours.48,67 For mTOR activation, cells were pretreated with MHY1485 for 30 minutes before UNC0642 exposure.68,69 Following this pretreatment, the cells were exposed to hyperosmotic medium. After exposure, cell viability was then assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Vazyme Biotech). Then, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added to the medium and incubated for 1 to 2 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite M200; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Assessment of Cellular Apoptosis

HCECs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. Following an 18-hour pretreatment with UNC0642, cells were exposed to hyperosmotic medium (90 mM) for 24 hours. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V–FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KGA1109-20; KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The percentage of late apoptotic cells was analyzed using an Accuri C6 Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and FlowJo v10.

Evaluation of Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species Activity

HCECs were seeded in both six-well and 12-well plates. After treatment with UNC0642 and hyperosmotic medium, cells were stained with a 10-µM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe in the reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (S0033S; Beyotime) for 20 minutes at a 37°C. After washing with PBS, fluorescence intensity was measured at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission of 525 nm using the Leica Thunder system and Accuri C6 flow cytometer. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ and FlowJo v10.

Detection of Superoxide Dismutase, Malondialdehyde, and Catalase

Cells were harvested and lysed by using ultrasonication, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. Levels of oxidative stress markers—superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and catalase (CAT)—were measured according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (S0101, S0131S, S0051; Beyotime).

Autophagic Flux Assay

HCECs were infected with pH-insensitive red fluorescent protein (stubRFP) – pH-sensitive green fluorescent protein (sensGFP)–microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) tandem fluorescent protein lentivirus (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) for 12 hours (viral titer 1.0 × 108 TU/mL, multiplicity of infection [MOI] = 100). After 72 hours,55 cells were treated as described. GFP and mRFP served as markers for the autophagic stages, with GFP+/mRFP+ puncta marking autophagosomes, indicating early autophagy. Upon the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, the acidic conditions within led to the quenching of GFP fluorescence, leaving only the mRFP+ puncta visible, which indicated late autophagy. The GFP+/mRFP+ (yellow) and GFP−/mRFP+ (red) puncta were analyzed using the Leica Thunder imaging system.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the BeyoChIP Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit (P2083S; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were fixed in formaldehyde and subjected to enzymatic digestion with micrococcal nuclease. The supernatants containing immunocomplexes were incubated with anti-H3K9me2 (CY5761; Abways) overnight at 4°C. Protein G–conjugated magnetic beads were added to lysates, and the mixtures were rotated for a further 2 hours at 4°C. After the immunocomplexes were washed six times with washing buffer, the DNA fragments bound to H3K9me2 were eluted, and the abundance was analyzed using qPCR.

Animals and the In Vivo DED Model

The animal studies conducted in this research were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Approval No. 2025AE01001) and carried out under the oversight of the Jiangsu Province Laboratory Animal Management Committee, adhering to the guidelines established by the Vision and Ophthalmology Research Society. Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Yangzhou University and housed at the Animal Experiment Center of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China). Mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 10 per group): (1) control; (2) DED, induced by 0.075% benzalkonium chloride (BAC; B834637; Macklin Biochemical, Shanghai, China) eye drops for 1 week; (3) PBS vehicle; and (4) UNC0642 (treated with 400-µM UNC0642 dissolved in PBS). After 1 week of DED induction, the DED group continued to receive BAC eye drops, whereas the PBS vehicle and UNC0642 groups were given 5 µL drops twice daily, with a minimum interval of 1 hour between modeling and treatment. After an additional week of treatment after DED induction, all mice were euthanized, and samples were collected for further analysis. Given the disease complexity, an in vivo model of early mild-to-moderate disease was established using scopolamine induction. Experimental mice were subcutaneously administered 0.5 mg/0.1 mL scopolamine hydrobromide (Aladdin Scientific, Shanghai, China) four times daily. The resulting experimental data are presented in Supplementary Figure S5.

Corneal Fluorescein Staining Test

Corneal fluorescein staining was performed on each mouse eye on days 0, 7, and 14. A 2-µL drop of 0.25% fluorescein sodium solution (Tianjin Jingming New Technology Development, Tianjin, China) was then applied to the conjunctival sac. After 30 seconds, the eyes were rinsed with PBS, and the degree of corneal damage was assessed and photographed under cobalt blue light. The extent of epithelial damage was quantified by dividing the cornea into four quadrants. Each quadrant was scored as follows: 0 points, no staining; 1 point, slight spot-shaped staining; 2 points, moderate spot-shaped staining; and 3 points, flake staining), yielding a maximum total score of 12, as described by previous studies.55,56,59

Tear Volume Test

Tear production was measured using the phenol red thread tear test (Tianjin Jingming New Technology Development) on days 0, 7, and 14. A cotton thread was gently inserted into the subconjunctival sac at the lateral canthus of each eye and left in place for 3 minutes. The extent of the thread moistened by tears was then measured to assess tear secretion.

Immunohistochemistry

Ocular tissue sections from the different experimental groups were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (G1207; ServiceBio). After the sections were rinsed with PBS, they were treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Absin Bioscience) for permeabilization. Following another PBS wash, the sections were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; T8200; Solarbio, Beijing, China) to block non-specific binding sites. Primary antibodies (DY1109, 1:50; Abways) were applied and allowed to incubate overnight at 4°C. After a subsequent PBS wash, the sections were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, GB23303, 1:1000; ServiceBio). The reaction was visualized using a DAB substrate kit (P0202; Beyotime). The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin (G1004; ServiceBio), dehydrated through a series of alcohol gradients, and mounted for examination under a digital optical microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining of Cornea and Conjunctiva

Ocular tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (diluted in PBS) for 24 hours, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5-µm sections. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a commercial kit (ServiceBio) for histological analysis.

Periodic Acid–Schiff Staining for Conjunctival Goblet Cells

Eyes and surrounding eyelid tissues were excised and fixed in a specialized eyeball fixation solution (G1109; ServiceBio). After dehydration, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 5-µm sections. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining (G1360; Solarbio) was performed to visualize conjunctival goblet cell density. The stained sections were analyzed using a Nikon digital optical microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the t-test, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical evaluations were performed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

G9a Expression Is Reduced in Hyperosmotic-Treated HCECs

To explore the expression of G9a in DED, HCECs were subjected to hyperosmotic stress (90-mM NaCl) to model the hyperosmotic environment associated with DED. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that G9a localized predominantly in the nucleus of untreated HCECs, with no detectable cytoplasmic distribution. Upon hyperosmotic treatment, a significant decrease in G9a fluorescence intensity was observed (Fig. 1A), indicating a reduction in G9a expression under stress conditions.

G9a expression is decreased in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs. (A) Immunofluorescence staining and quantification (n = 3) showing nuclear location of G9a and H3K9me2 in HCECs, with a reduction after hyperosmotic treatment. Scale bar: 200 µm. (B) Western blot analysis and quantification of G9a and H3K9me2 expression in the control and HO groups (β-actin or histone H3 used for normalization, n = 9). (C) qPCR analysis of G9a mRNA levels in the control and HO groups (n = 3). Data are presented as mean ± SD. **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Given that G9a is a key histone methyltransferase involved in catalyzing H3K9me2 at specific histone sites within promoter regions of target genes, we next examined the levels of its catalytic product, H3K9me2. The results showed a significant reduction in H3K9me2 following hyperosmotic conditions (Fig. 1A), indicating impaired enzymatic activity of G9a in addition to its decreased expression. Quantitative analysis confirmed significant differences in fluorescence intensity for both G9a and H3K9me2. Western blot analysis further validated these findings, showing a significant downregulation of both G9a and H3K9me2 protein levels in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs (Fig. 1B). This reduction in protein expression was consistent with a decrease in G9a mRNA levels, as demonstrated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1C).

Together, these results indicate that hyperosmotic stress, which mimics the conditions of DED, leads to a coordinated downregulation of G9a at both transcriptional and translational levels, along with a reduction in H3K9me2 formation. This suggests that the epigenetic regulatory functions of G9a are impaired in HCECs under pathological conditions. The potential activation of additional histone methyltransferases under hyperosmotic stress warrants further investigation. Expression levels of major H3K9-specific histone methyltransferases were quantitatively analyzed by qPCR, and the results are presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

G9a Inhibition Protects HCECs from Hyperosmotic Damage

UNC0642, a highly selective G9a inhibitor, exerts its effects through dual mechanisms: peptide matrix competition and non-competitive inhibition of the cofactor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). This compound demonstrates potent inhibitory activity with minimal cytotoxicity and effectively reduces H3K9me2 marks. To evaluate the optimal concentration and treatment duration of UNC0642 in HCECs, we performed cell viability assays using the CCK-8 reagent (Supplementary Fig. S2A). To determine the optimal concentration, dose–response information was examined for UNC0642, assessing both its inhibitory efficacy against G9a (Supplementary Figs. S2B, S2C) and anti-inflammatory effects (Supplementary Figs. S2D–S2F) across varying doses. Additionally, given the known activity of the compound against the homologous protein glucagon-like peptide (GLP), corresponding transcriptional levels were quantified (Supplementary Figs. S2G, S2H).

Inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress are the primary mechanisms driving cellular damage in DED. The qPCR analysis revealed that pharmacological inhibition of G9a through UNC0642 pretreatment significantly attenuated the hyperosmotic-induced upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, in HCECs (Figs. 2A–C). This suggests a potent anti-inflammatory effect of G9a suppression under pathological conditions.

Protective role of G9a inhibition in hyperosmotic-stressed HCECs via triple modulation of inflammatory, apoptotic, and oxidative pathways. (A–C) The mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in cells following G9a inhibition in cells were measured by qPCR (n = 3). (D) Apoptosis rates in cells after G9a inhibition were examined by flow cytometry using Annexin V–FITC/PI staining (n = 3). (E) The effect of G9a inhibition on ROS was assessed using the DCFH-DA probe and visualized via fluorescence microscopy (n = 3). Scale bar: 200 µm. (F) ROS fluorescence in HCECs after G9a inhibition was measured by flow cytometry. (G–I) Levels of antioxidant enzymes SOD, CAT, and the lipid oxidation product MDA were assessed (n = 3). Apoptotic cell death was quantified using Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) double staining, which showed a significant decrease in late apoptotic cells (Q2 quadrant) in the UNC0642-treated group compared to the hyperosmotic (HO) group (Fig. 2D). These findings indicate that G9a inhibition protects against hyperosmotic stress–induced apoptosis, potentially contributing to the maintenance of ocular surface homeostasis.

Oxidative stress, another critical mechanism in DED pathogenesis, was assessed using multiple approaches. Intracellular ROS levels, measured with DCFH-DA probes and FITC fluorescence detection, were significantly reduced in the UNC0642-treated group compared to the HO group (Figs. 2E, 2F). Additionally, hyperosmotic stress–induced changes in oxidative stress markers were notably ameliorated by G9a inhibition, as evidenced by decreased MDA levels and restored activity of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT (Figs. 2G–I).

Together, these findings demonstrate that G9a inhibition exerts multifaceted protective effects against hyperosmotic-induced cellular damage through simultaneous modulation of inflammatory responses, apoptotic pathways, and oxidative stress mechanisms. The observed downregulation of G9a under hyperosmotic conditions may represent an endogenous protective feedback mechanism, and pharmacological enhancement of this suppression through UNC0642 appears to bolster cellular resilience and promote homeostasis.

G9a Inhibition Enhances Protective Autophagy in Hyperosmotic-Treated HCECs

Given that dysregulated autophagy in DED has been implicated in key pathological mechanisms55,56—including inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress—and considering the reported role of G9a in modulating autophagic activity,61–64 we sought to investigate whether the therapeutic benefits of G9a inhibition in DED are mediated, at least in part, through the activation of autophagy.

To monitor dynamic autophagic flux, HCECs were transduced with stubRFP–sensGFP–LC3 lentiviral reporters. stubRFP–sensGFP–LC3 exhibits yellow fluorescence when located in the autophagosome, and, upon fusion with the lysosome to form the autolysosome, the acidic vesicular environment (pH < 5) causes GFP quenching, leaving mRFP–LC3 to display red fluorescence. Under hyperosmotic conditions, HCECs exhibited an increase in yellow puncta, indicating enhanced autophagosome formation. Treatment with UNC0642 led to a noticeable accumulation of red puncta, suggesting an enhancement in autolysosome formation and autophagic flux (Fig. 2A).

Western blot analysis of the DED model further confirmed these findings, showing that UNC0642 treatment significantly reduced p62 protein levels and increased LC3-II expression compared to the HO group (Fig. 2B). These molecular changes collectively demonstrate robust activation of autophagy following G9a inhibition.

Blockade of Autophagy Reverses G9a Inhibition-Mediated Protection in HO-Treated HCECs

CQ, a well-established autophagy inhibitor, exerts its effects by preventing the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, thereby blocking autophagic flux. As shown in Figure 2, G9a inhibition in HCECs enhances autophagic activity. To investigate whether the protective effects of G9a inhibition in HCECs are mediated through autophagy, HCECs were pretreated with 20-µM CQ for 1 hour prior to UNC0642 administration, pharmacologically inhibiting autophagic processes.

Following autophagy blockade, the anti-inflammatory effects of G9a inhibition were significantly attenuated, as evidenced by the rebound in proinflammatory cytokine expression levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) (Figs. 4A–C). Similarly, the anti-apoptotic effects of UNC0642 were abolished by CQ pretreatment, as indicated by the increased apoptotic cells (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, the antioxidant capacity associated with G9a inhibition was compromised, with intracellular ROS levels failing to return to baseline after autophagy blockade (Fig. 4E).

These findings collectively demonstrate that the protective effects of G9a inhibition against hyperosmotic-induced inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress are dependent on autophagy. The functional relationship between G9a activity and autophagic regulation in HCECs appears to be crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis under pathological conditions. This mechanistic insight suggests that the therapeutic impact of UNC0642 on DED is largely dependent through autophagy.

Mechanistic Validation of the mTOR–G9a–ATG7 Regulatory Axis in DED In Vitro

The regulatory relationship between mTOR and G9a has been reported in previous studies.63,70,71 Notably, mTOR has been identified as an upstream regulator of G9a.48 Our research group has also shown that mTOR expression is downregulated in DED.55 These findings suggest that the observed reduction in G9a levels in DED may be attributable to alterations in mTOR activity. To investigate this hypothesis, we first assessed mTOR levels in an in vitro DED model (Fig. 5A). Although the total mTOR protein levels remained unchanged, p-mTOR levels showed a significant decrease. This prompted us to explore whether restoring p-mTOR levels could modulate G9a expression.

To test this, we utilized MHY1485, a well-characterized mTOR activator68,69 that is cell permeable and inhibits autophagosome–lysosome fusion, leading to LC3-II accumulation and autophagosome enlargement, thereby suppressing autophagy (Supplementary Fig. S3). Pretreatment of hyperosmotic HCECs with 10-µM MHY1485 successfully restored p-mTOR activation (Fig. 5B). This intervention also led to increased G9a protein expression and elevated levels of H3K9me2, a functional marker of G9a activity. In line with these findings, G9a mRNA levels exhibited a significant rebound (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that p-mTOR regulates downstream G9a expression in HCECs.

Given that G9a exerts its biological effects through autophagy regulation, identifying downstream autophagy-related target genes of G9a is of significant interest. Previous studies have established that G9a mediates H3K9me2 deposition and binds to the promoter region of ATG7, thereby suppressing its transcriptional activity.47,48 In line with these findings, our experiments revealed that ATG7 expression was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels following G9a inhibition (Figs. 5D, 5E).

To further elucidate the mechanistic basis of this regulation, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays in HCECs to isolate DNA fragments bound to H3K9me2 and quantify their enrichment at the ATG7 promoter via qPCR. The results revealed a marked reduction in H3K9me2 occupancy at the ATG7 promoter following G9a suppression (Fig. 5F). Together, these findings confirm that, in DED, G9a epigenetically represses ATG7 transcription by catalyzing H3K9me2 deposition at its promoter region.

Inhibition of G9a Alleviates Symptoms in the DED Mouse Model

To investigate the functional role of G9a in DED in vivo, we established a murine DED model through topical administration of 0.075% BAC eye drops (Fig. 6A). Quantitative PCR and immunohistochemical analyses revealed a significant downregulation of G9a expression in both corneal and conjunctival tissues of DED mice. This reduction was further amplified by the topical application of UNC0642 eye drops (Figs. 6B, 6C).

Clinical assessments demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of G9a significantly improved ocular surface parameters in DED mice. Specifically, UNC0642 treatment reduced corneal fluorescein staining scores and restored tear secretion, which helped attenuate corneal epithelial damage (Figs. 6D, 6E). The therapeutic efficacy of UNC0642 was evaluated in a scopolamine-induced murine model, and the results are presented in Supplementary Figure S5. Histopathological evaluation using PAS staining showed partial recovery of conjunctival goblet cells following UNC0642 administration (Fig. 7A). H&E staining revealed characteristic structural alterations in the DED group, including corneal epithelial thinning and disorganized tissue architecture, compared to the control group. Notably, UNC0642 treatment resulted in more pronounced recovery of corneal epithelial thickness and tissue organization compared to the PBS-treated group (Fig. 7B).

Corneal and conjunctival tissues were harvested for mRNA extraction and subsequent analysis of inflammatory gene expressions. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that topical administration of UNC0642 significantly reduced the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, compared to the untreated DED group. The anti-inflammatory effects of UNC0642 were more pronounced than those observed in the PBS-treated group (Figs. 7C, 7D). These in vivo findings corroborate our in vitro observations, demonstrating that G9a inhibition exerts protective effects on ocular surface integrity and function in the murine DED models.

Discussion

DED is a prevalent ophthalmic condition that currently lacks curative treatments, with symptom management remaining the primary therapeutic approach.8 Given its complex pathogenesis, which is strongly influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors,72,73 identifying novel molecular targets for DED intervention is of significant interest. In this study, we investigated the expression and functional role of the epigenetic regulator histone methyltransferase G9a using both in vitro and in vivo DED models. Our results showed that G9a expression was significantly downregulated under DED pathological conditions (Fig. 1). In vitro experiments revealed that the inhibition of G9a showed alleviated DED-associated inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress (Fig. 2). Notably, further suppression of G9a triggered protective autophagy activation (Fig. 3), and this protective effect was markedly diminished upon autophagy blockade (Fig. 4). Mechanistically, we found that reduced G9a levels were associated with inhibition of the mTOR pathway, and G9a regulated autophagy by catalyzing H3K9me2 deposition at the promoter region of the autophagy-related gene ATG7 (Fig. 5). Importantly, therapeutic inhibition of G9a on the ocular surface significantly ameliorated DED symptoms in mice (Figs. 6,7), further supporting its critical role in disease pathogenesis.

Epigenetic modifications play a central role in a variety of environmentally-linked diseases.74,75 In DED, emerging evidence suggests that diverse epigenetic alterations are involved in disease progression. Specifically, methylation-regulated differentially expressed genes have been implicated in both Sjögren's syndrome–associated DED and conventional DED.76 Additionally, promoter methylation changes in circadian rhythm genes have been shown to modulate inflammatory responses in DED.77 The corneal epithelium, as the primary site of DED pathology, undergoes extensive epigenetic reprogramming during wound healing, including DNA methylation–mediated silencing of key genes such as UHRF1, WNT5A, miR-200a, and CDKN2B.32,33,78 Furthermore, regulation by the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 has been observed in DED.34 Beyond DNA methylation, histone modifications also play a crucial role, with the histone methyltransferase SUV39H1 modulating corneal epithelial repair through H3K9me3-mediated gene silencing,35 highlighting the multifaceted epigenetic regulation of wound healing. Despite these advancements, the role of histone methylation modifications in DED remains unexplored. Our study is the first to demonstrate the interaction of H3K9me2 with the ATG7 promoter in DED, thereby elucidating the mTOR–G9a–H3K9me2–ATG7 regulatory axis and significantly expanding the experimental understanding of epigenetic mechanisms in DED pathogenesis.

Autophagy serves as a crucial cellular homeostatic process responsible for the degrading misfolded proteins, damaged organelles, and foreign pathogens.49 Substantial evidence indicates that moderate autophagy activation has protective effects in DED, with enhanced autophagic flux showing therapeutic potential against disease progression.55–59 As a key player in DED pathogenesis, autophagy is negatively regulated by G9a through suppression of core autophagy effectors and upstream regulators.42,43 Mechanistically, G9a inhibition upregulates autophagy-related gene expression and promotes autophagic activity.44 Pharmacological or genetic modulation of G9a expression has emerged as a viable therapeutic strategy for several disorders via autophagy activation.61–64 However, this approach remains unexplored in ophthalmic diseases. Our study provides the first evidence, to our knowledge, that G9a-mediated histone methylation contributes to DED pathogenesis via autophagy modulation, influencing downstream inflammatory, apoptotic, and oxidative stress pathways.

The experimental results revealed several noteworthy observations. UNC0642 partially restored CAT activity under hyperosmotic conditions, although levels remained distinct from controls. This suggests that, whereas G9a inhibition effectively mitigates oxidative stress, additional pathways likely contribute to this regulation due to the complexity of the body. Notably, topical UNC0642 administration improved both ocular surface integrity and tear secretion in two distinct DED models: BAC-induced epithelial damage (Fig. 6E) and scopolamine-induced dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Given that the drug does not directly target lacrimal gland, these effects are hypothesized to occur via the preservation of lacrimal functional unit (LFU) homeostasis.79 The LFU includes the ocular surface (cornea, conjunctiva, meibomian glands, etc.), the tear drainage system (puncta, lacrimal canaliculus, common canaliculus, etc.), the mucosal immune system, and the neural network connecting them. It maintains the ocular surface environment by regulating the production, delivery, and clearance of tears. Dysfunction in one or more components directly affects tear film homeostasis, leading to DED.80

The study employed two complementary DED models (BAC-induced injury representing severe evaporative DED and scopolamine-induced dysfunction simulating early/moderate disease). These models demonstrated the therapeutic effects of UNC0642. However, expanding to other etiologies, such as Sjögren's syndrome models, lacrimal gland excision, or even alternative species, could further strengthen translational relevance and elucidate the complete regulatory landscape, particularly regarding lacrimal gland-specific mechanisms.81,82

UNC0642 is a dual inhibitor targeting both G9a and its homolog GLP, which share 80% sequence homology in their catalytic SET domains and exhibit functional redundancy. These proteins form heterodimeric complexes that have significantly enhanced methyltransferase activity compared to their homodimeric counterparts, indicating strong synergistic effects.83 In the DED model, G9a expression showed a marked reduction, but GLP downregulation was less pronounced (Supplementary Figs. S1B, S1C). Furthermore, UNC0642 treatment did not significantly suppress GLP transcriptional levels under hyperosmotic stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2H). These differential responses between the two homologs, despite their structural and functional similarities, suggest the potential utility of selective inhibitors to dissect the specific contributions of GLP to DED pathogenesis.84,85 The development and application of such targeted compounds would help clarify whether the observed therapeutic effects stem primarily from G9a inhibition or require concurrent GLP modulation.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, due to the current lack of potent G9a activators and considering the established negative correlation between G9a and autophagy levels, we were unable to perform G9a overexpression experiments to achieve bidirectional regulation. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that G9a regulates various biological processes beyond autophagy, including cellular metabolism,86,87 immune responses, immune cell differentiation,88 neural injury responses,67 and pain modulation89—processes that are critically involved in DED pathogenesis. Future studies systematically examining these additional regulatory dimensions could enhance our understanding of the multifaceted roles of G9a in DED. The regulatory mechanisms involving G9a-mediated H3K9me2 formation and subsequent gene silencing raise important questions about potential negative feedback control that might modulate G9a transcriptional levels and terminate its regulatory activity when homeostasis is restored. Previous studies have documented such negative feedback regulation in histone methylases. For example, the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) ligase Su(var)2-10 establishes H3K9me3 modifications that subsequently inhibit its own excessive transcription, thereby maintaining chromatin stability through an autoregulatory loop.90 By analogy, a similar regulatory mechanism may govern G9a activity. The reduction of H3K9me2 could potentially induce local chromatin relaxation, facilitating transcription factor binding at the G9a promoter or releasing inhibitory factors from the G9a locus, thereby establishing a negative feedback circuit. Future studies should directly examine chromatin accessibility patterns and transcription factor binding dynamics at the G9a promoter during H3K9me2 fluctuation to test this hypothesis.

The potential risks associated with prolonged or complete G9a inhibition in ocular tissues remain unexplored. Although preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of G9a-targeting inhibitors, no clinical trials have been reported to date.91 This may reflect concerns that chronic inhibition could disrupt G9a-mediated homeostasis in somatic tissues. Although UNC0642 treatment in this study showed no significant adverse effects on HCEC viability or murine models—with efficacy comparable to sodium hyaluronate, a standard DED therapeutic (Supplementary Fig. S5C)—the long-term consequences of G9a suppression on ocular surface epigenetic homeostasis require further investigation. Future studies should prioritize strategies to enhance therapeutic safety, such as tissue-specific drug formulations, dynamic monitoring of H3K9me2 levels, and genomic stability assessments, to facilitate clinical translation. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that autophagy is implicated in the response to various environmental factors known to trigger DED. For example, UV radiation–induced inflammation and skin tumorigenesis are modulated by the autophagy-related gene ATG7,92 and autophagy regulators have been shown to confer UV protection.93 Similarly, airborne particulate matter (e.g., PM2.5) disrupts homeostasis in pulmonary and cardiovascular systems, a process regulated by autophagy.94,95 Given these findings, investigating potential crosstalk between G9a and autophagy under DED-inducing environmental conditions—such as UV exposure, low humidity, and air pollution—represents a critical avenue for future research.

In conclusion, our study reveals that the pathological downregulation of G9a exerts protective effects in DED. UNC0642 ameliorated DED-induced damage by activating autophagy through epigenetic derepression of ATG7 transcription while simultaneously reducing inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress in hyperosmotic HCECs and improving ocular surface symptoms in DED mice (Fig. 8). These findings establish G9a as a potential therapeutic target for DED, although further investigation is needed to fully elucidate its role through bidirectional regulation studies, exploration of its interactions with other molecular pathways, and clinical validation in patients.
