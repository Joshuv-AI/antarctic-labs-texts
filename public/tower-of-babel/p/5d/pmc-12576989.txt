DHerbKB for CKD: knowledge base of diet and toxic herbal medicines for clinical support of chronic kidney disease
Authors: Huai-Yu Wang, Chi Yuan, Yi-Heng Zhao, Yan Yi, Yi-Nuo Chen, Yelin Li, Dongyuan Chang, Xiaomeng Lin, Ji Wang, Sydney Chi Wai Tang, Qi Wang, Min Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDietary management and nephrotoxic herbal medicine control are essential and sophisticated in chronic kidney disease(CKD) care. Information gap between clinical principles and real-world practice hinders the relevant management. It is necessary to support the care using reliable informatic resources and tools.MethodsPrinciples of CKD dietary management were manually extracted from clinical guidelines. CKD complication-related nutrients data were fully exported from database of the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the China Food Composition Tables. Oxalate contents of food were extracted from Harvard Oxalate Food List Revised November 2023 and Food Composition Table for Bangladesh. Information of toxic Chinese herbal medicine were collected from the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, online databases, literatures, and guidelines, and reviewed by nephrologists and herbalists. By connecting these structured knowledge/data according to clinical principles, the knowledge base named DHerbKB was created along with the recommendation system. To evaluate the DHerbKB, CKD cases with different complications were designed, and full-day dietary schedules covering 136 foods/cuisines were extracted from guidelines/literatures.ResultsThe DHerbKB is accessed by https://dherbkb.hywanglab.org.cn/. Data of protein, energy, phosphorus, potassium, and sodium of 8,725 foods, and that of oxalate of 590 foods were included. A total of 21 herbs and 49 Chinese patent medicines containing aristolochic acids, 39 toxic herbs, 25 herbs with overdose toxicity, 13 herbs and 34 Chinese patent medicines rich in potassium were included. With 128 times of assessment by non-medical professional users, ease of use of the DHerbKB system was proved and only two wrong conclusions were made by the system because the user selected the same food with different processes.ConclusionsThe DHerbKB integrated and transformed the knowledge and data of CKD dietary management and toxic Chinese herbal medicines control into an easy-to-use and reliable system. Resource covered by the DHerbKB are opened for future researches regarding CKD care, toxic herbs control and related artificial intelligence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07178-8.Keywords: CKD dietary management, Toxic chinese herbal medicine, Knowledge base, Recommendation system, Clinical support

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) influenced 9.1% of population worldwide [1, 2]. Prevention and intervention of complications including protein-energy waste, water-sodium retention, hyperkalemia, and mineral-bone disease are almost throughout the whole process of CKD management [1, 3]. Dietary management forms the basis of this care, and inappropriate dietary intake hinders the management of CKD and these complications [4, 5]. Clinical guidelines recommended patients in all stages of CKD to receive routinely individualized dietary consultation and medical nutrition therapy (MNT) by registered dietetic nutritionists (RDNs) [1, 3, 6]. However, only around 10% or less patients received MNT by RDNs [7–9]. Lack of professionals and resources certainly limited the CKD dietary management [7, 8, 10]. Accurately tailoring the dietary schedule according to the dynamic disease status was one of the most concerned issues among CKD patients, while the practice requires patients’ high health literacy and can also poses a challenge to physicians without specialized training [11, 12].

Besides diet, increasing popularity of herbal medicine should be noted. Patients with CKD suffer from multiple comorbidities and complications. Herbal medicine, as an important branch of complementary medicine, attracts patients’ interest by the natural property and the effectiveness on symptom controls [13]. Integration of herbal medicines with strict quality control into the standard disease management has been endorsed by the World Health Organization for decades [14–16]. Avoidance of nephrotoxic herb is the prerequisite. However, due to previously unsatisfactory governance and inappropriate propaganda with limited scientific rigor, patients often ignore the herb’s dosage and toxicity, and they can get pharmacological herbs easily from multiple approaches without the approval of their physicians [1, 17]. The KDIGO clinical guidelines for CKD management (2024) emphasized to review and limit the use of herbal medicines that may harmful for people with CKD [1], while accurate identification of the toxic herb is difficult for patients and health professionals without specialized training. Chinese herbal medicine is one of the commonest herbal medicines used worldwide. Although there are numerous studies and clinical guidelines reported toxicity, warnings, and precautions of Chinese herbal medicines [18–22], the majority studies were written in Chinese, which severely limited knowledge spreading and shaped the huge information-gaps between herbalists and other health professionals. Popularity of herbal medicine and its increasing use worldwide are objective realities. It is necessary to develop a bilingual knowledge base gathering data and precautions of toxic Chinese herbal medicine as much as possible to avoid improper use of herbal medicines.

Therefore, the present study collected multiple resources of CKD dietary management and Chinese herbal medicines, integrated these resources and established a knowledge base along with a recommendation system, to facilitate CKD dietary management, avoid improper use of Chinese herbal medicines, and provide open resources for related studies.

Methods

Principles of diet management in CKD

The KDIGO 2024 clinical guidelines proposed the Practice Point that dietitians or nutrition provider educate people with CKD about dietary adaptations regarding sodium, phosphorus, potassium, and protein intake, tailored to severity of CKD and comorbid conditions [1, 3]. The primary purpose of developing DHerbKB is to promote the management of diet-related complications of CKD, which were water-sodium retention, protein-energy waste, hyperkalemia, and hyperphosphatemia. Therefore, all knowledge regarding the intake of protein, energy, sodium, potassium, and phosphorus described in the KDOQI clinical practice guidelines for nutrition in CKD: 2020 update [3], the Guidelines for Dietary Management of Adult Chronic Kidney Disease (2024 Edition) published by the National Health Commission of China [23], and the KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease was fully extracted and structured as the principles of diet management [1]. (Additional file 1) Recommendations on intake of protein, energy, sodium, potassium, and phosphorus were grouped according to CKD stage, diabetes, age, oliguria, edema, body mass index (BMI), hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, and maintenance dialysis. Common tips, such as the priority of protein source and cooking tips to reduce potassium, were extracted.

Data integration of CKD complication-related nutrients

Based on the FoodData Central of the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) (https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-search?query=&type=Foundation), results of Component Search containing contents of protein, energy, sodium, potassium, and phosphorus per serving size and per 100 unit serving were fully exported and collected. Nutrients of food common in Chinese cuisine were fully collected from the China Food Composition Tables - Standard Edition (6th edition) (CFCT-6th ) [24]. (Additional file 1) To better guide the diet arrangement, food rich in phosphorus, potassium and sodium were labeled as having the content of phosphorus ≥ 210 mg/100 g, of potassium ≥ 250 mg/100 g, and of sodium ≥ 600 mg/100 g, respectively [25].

Considering the involvement of oxalate in kidney stone and the potential harm on kidney function, oxalate content in per serve of food (cup, tablespoon, teaspoon, ounce, per fruit, etc.) was extracted from Harvard Oxalate Food List Revised November 2023 [26], and that in per 100 g was extracted from Food Composition Table for Bangladesh [27]. Given the absence of consentaneous recommendation dosage of oxalate intake for CKD patients in authoritative clinical guidelines, as well as the variation of oxalate content across online resources [28], the DHerbKB presents the oxalate content and category (very high, high, moderate, low, very low, little or none) of the searched food, and does not conclude whether the intake is proper.

Data extraction and normalization of toxic Chinese herbal medicines

Chinese pharmacological herbs previously reported as nephrotoxic were reviewed and categorized into three types: (1) containing aristolochic acid; (2) toxic herbs: for those inherently toxic and not limited to nephrotoxic, such as Areca catechu L. (betel nut), Tripterygium wilfordii Hook. f., bee venom, etc.; (3) overdose toxicity: for those previously reported as nephrotoxic meanwhile clearly stated the overdose and/or excessively prolonged use, such as Aloe ferox Miller, Rheum palmatum L., etc. Data of these herbs were extracted from Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (2020 edition, thereafter referred as Chinese Pharmacopoeia) [29], the Guidelines for Diagnosis and Management of Herb-induced Kidney Injury with Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine [21], China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI, https://www.cnki.net/), Traditional Chinese Medicine System Toxicology Database (http://www.bic.ac.cn/TCMSTD/) [22], China Medical Information Platform (https://www.dayi.org.cn/)(30), Drug database of the National Medical Products Administration of China (https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/datasearch/home-index.html#category=yp), the KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease [1], and Plant Science Data Center of Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://www.plantplus.cn/cn). Regards the herb commonly used as a safe drug but reported with nephrotoxicity, further retrieval and literature review were conducted by one nephrologist and one herbalist independently based on PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), the CNKI, Chinese Pharmacopeia, and the China Medical Information Platform. Herb’s name in Chinese, Chinese Pinyin, and Latin, vernacular names, original plant and its pharmacological part, recommended dose, toxic dose, and representative toxic components were recorded. Toxic dose and/or lethal dose were extracted from clinical reports. If it absents, median lethal dose (LD50) of oral administration detected by animal models were recorded. Chinese patent medicines containing aristolochic acid were collected based on the China Medical Information Platform, the Drug database of the National Medical Products Administration of China, and the Qinghai Provincial Standard of Tibetan Medicine (http://www.tibetmdc.com/, for Tibetan herbal medicine). (Additional file 1) Their names in Chinese and Chinese Pinyin, and ingredients containing aristolochic acid were collected. Given the commercial issues, all Chinese patent medicines were only collected in the knowledge base supporting the retrieval, but not presented in the manuscript.

Data extraction of Chinese herbal medicines rich in potassium

Herbs rich in potassium were collected based on the Guidelines for Diagnosis and Management of Chronic Renal Failure with Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine [31], Modern Chinese Medicine of Nephrology [32], and original studies measured content of potassium in 308 herbs and 109 Chinese patent medicines [33, 34]. (Additional file 1) Decoction of the herb containing potassium over 0.5mmol/g, and decoction of Chinese patent medicine containing potassium over 0.2 g/500mL were defined as the herbal medicine rich in potassium [33].

Development of knowledge base

By connecting multidisciplinary knowledge and data described above, the knowledge base of food nutrients and toxic Chinese herbal medicines for patients with CKD, named the DHerbKB, was created. The manually extracted knowledge of clinical principles and data ensured the relationships among entities being clear and accessible for decision-making. The suggested/improper content of key nutrients mentioned in the principles were split granularly, and name of food and its CKD-concerned nutrients were connected to make the diet knowledge specific and easy-to-use. Similar process of manual structuring and connection was performed to construct the knowledge base of Chinese herbal medicines, so that the principles and precautions of the herb using can be accurately organized for decision-making. Development of recommendation system

The recommendation system was designed to help the users to avoid improper intake of food and herb, rather than to generate ideal dietary schedules de novo. Essential information of disease status is required to support the dietary arrangement (Fig. 1A). The system prompts the user to input name and quantity of food or of 24 h dietary schedule. Both the Chinese and the English input can retrieve and generate the list of food candidates according to semantic similarity, to promote the user-system interaction and remarkably improve the accuracy of food selection. The system quantifies the intake of key nutrients, conducts an in-depth comparison with the established principles, and finally gets individualized advices, outputs the conclusion that whether the dietary schedule is proper for the patient, or whether food rich in CKD-concerned nutrients exists (Fig. 1B).

To support the proper use of Chinese herbal medicine, the system prompts the user to input names of pharmacological herbs or Chinese patent medicine. Similar with the searching process of food, names in Chinese, Chinese Pinyin and Latin can retrieve and generate the list of herb candidates and improve the accuracy of herb selection. For the pharmacological herb, the system reports its detailed warnings and precautions. For the Chinese patent medicine, the system reports whether the medicine containing aristolochic acid or whether it is rich in potassium (Fig. 1B).

Evaluation of knowledge base and recommendation system

To evaluate the accuracy of the DHerbKB system, sixteen cases with different CKD stages and complications were designed by nephrologists. Key factors influencing dietary arrangement, including age, levels of eGFR, dialysis status, BMI, diabetes, electrolyte imbalance, and edema, were considered. Given the real-world situation, different types of item absence were randomly set in these cases (Table 3). According to the guidelines for dietary management of adult obesity (2024 edition) [35] and of adult diabetes (2023 edition) [36] established by the National Health Commission of China, six schedules were randomly selected to represent Chinese diet pattern. According to the principles of the Mediterranean dietary schedule recorded in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in Spain for the prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [37], one Mediterranean dietary schedule was designed by nephrologists. One diet of high-energy breakfast with low-energy dinner from a RCT conducted in Israel for the management of hyperglycemia was adopted [38]. Ultimately, eight full-day dietary schedules covering 136 foods/cuisines were employed (Additional file 2).

Two medical professionals reviewed the CKD cases, quantified key nutrients of the dietary schedules according to the USDA database and the CFCT-6th, then concluded and explained whether the dietary schedule was proper for the case. Consentaneous decision was defined as the golden standard. Dietary schedules and information of cases were inputted into the recommendation system one-by-one by two non-medical professional users. The fluent use of the DHerbKB by general users could demonstrate the ease of use of the system. Accurate rate of these outputs was calculated to demonstrate the system’s reliability.

Knowledge base of CKD complication-related nutrients

Using data from the USDA database, protein content of 13,510 foods, energy content of 13,514 foods, sodium content of 7,888 foods, potassium content of 7,712 foods, and phosphorus content of 7,663 foods were extracted. Taking the intersection, complete nutrients data of 7,000 kinds of food common in the US were included. Using data of the CTCF-6th, content of these nutrients from 1,725 kinds of food common in Chinese cuisine were included.

Oxalate content of 590 kinds of food was extracted and included in DHerbKB. Among 540 kinds of food, oxalate content was measured in milligram per serving, which was extracted from Harvard Oxalate Food List Revised November 2023 [26]. Among 50 kinds of food, oxalate content was measured in milligram per 100 g edible portion of fresh weight, which was extracted from Food Composition Table for Bangladesh [27].

A total of 39 toxic herbs were included in the DHerbKB. Among them, 26 herbs were clearly labeled as having medium or high degree of toxicity in Chinese Pharmacopoeia, 13 toxic herbs were excluded from the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, while they might be currently used in folk.

A total of 25 herbs previously reported as nephrotoxic were categorized into the group of Overdose toxicity after literature review. Among them, 6 herbs were labeled as having mild toxicity in Chinese Pharmacopoeia.

Discussion

CKD dietary management is sophisticated. Careful check and proper use of herbal medicine are challenging for patients and health professionals without specialized training. The present study developed a knowledge base named the DHerbKB, featuring an integrated recommendation system. (https://dherbkb.hywanglab.org.cn/) Clinical principles of dietary management and warnings and precautions of Chinese herbal medicines were comprehensively integrated into the DHerbKB. It provides not only a pragmatic tool supporting the daily care of CKD, but also an open-resource for studies in CKD care, Chinese herbal medicine, and artificial intelligence of clinical support including model training, benchmarking, and knowledge retrieving.

Dietary management is a crucial component of CKD self-management [1, 3, 11]. The KDIGO clinical guidelines recommended to use dietitians and accredited nutrition providers to educate patients with CKD about the intake of sodium, phosphorus, potassium, and protein according to their disease status [1]. Two issues remain to be solved. First, key point of dietitians’ support is to educate the principle of CKD nutrition and to lead the MNT. Relying on professionals to review the food in real-time is impracticable [10]. Second, lack of nutrition specialties and low utilization of MNT among CKD population were common issues worldwide [7, 8, 10]. Currently, many online resources have been used to support CKD-specialized dietary arrangement [40–42]. Generating the dietary schedule de novo [41], showing food list rich in specific nutrients [40] and patient education [42] were common types of online resources, which met different demands of CKD patients. However, CKD dietary management is highly sophisticated, influenced by the dynamic disease status, personal preference, diverse cooking culture, availability of foods, and socioeconomic status. These factors, to varying degrees, limit the feasibility and practical value of the existing online resources. Begum et al. systematically reviewed these resources and found that the variety of food and consistency of recommendations were limited [40]. Different from the previous resources, the DHerbKB gathered the data of food’s nutrients as much as possible, and labelled the food rich in potassium, phosphorus and sodium. By matching the disease status and quantified nutrients of food to the standard principles of dietary management, the DHerbKB concludes whether the food selection is proper and guides the modification accordingly. It can effectively avoid the limitations in the previous resources, and support much flexible retrieval. Based on the current version of the DHerbKB, the present workflow and paradigm of knowledge base, recommendation system and decision support are adaptive for updates in the future.

Avoidance of nephrotoxic drug is important [1]. With the popularity of traditional medicines, represented by Chinese herbal medicines, it is urgent to enhance the support to identify toxic herbs and check the dose and duration of usage [13]. Many patients believe that the natural herbs were innocuous, and they do not even consider herbal medicine as drugs [17, 18]. Hence, they are very likely to overlook the underlying risk of pharmacological herbs and the notice of toxicity in Pharmacopoeia. Over dosage and prolonged use were the most common causes of herb-induced kidney injury [18, 19, 43]. In the bibliometric analysis by Li and Hu, which involved 252 cases of herb-induced kidney injury in China, it was found that 93.25% cases of nephrotoxicity were caused by overdose and prolonged use [43]. It demonstrated the importance to carefully review the herbs and check the dose, as the KDIGO clinical guidelines of CKD suggested [1]. However, for health professionals without specialized training, it is very difficult to guide the proper use of herbal medicines, while arbitrary banning of herbal medicine would block the potential way to improve symptom management. Extra support is urgently needed. The DHerbKB included names of the herb in Latin, Chinese, Chinese Pinyin, and vernacular names, which improved the hit-rate of retrieval and avoided the translation-related misunderstanding. Toxic levels and recommended dose recorded in Pharmacopoeia, as well as toxic components and toxic/lethal dose of the herb reported by literatures, are clearly reported by the DHerbKB, which would be helpful to guide the proper use of herbal medicine.

Additionally, it should be noted that the toxicity of plants used in Chinese herbal medicines was probably different from the original plants without specific processing or in other schools of herbal medicine. Herb processing is an essential discipline in traditional Chinese medicine. The Chinese Pharmacopoeia clearly stated the standard of herb processing and quality control for each herb. Levels of the representative effective components and toxic components of each herb must be measured according to standard protocols [29]. Take Senecio scandens Buch.-Ham/Qian Li Guang/千里光/ Groundsel as an example, groundsel was widely reported being toxic due to pyrrolizidine alkaloids [18, 44]. In contrast, Chinese Pharmacopoeia did not have toxic warning for groundsel, since its standard of quality control requests the content of adonifoline in dried groundsel lower than 0.004% and requests the dosage of groundsel under 30 g [29]. In sum, recommended dose and toxic dose of pharmacological herbs recorded in the DHerbKB are only applicable for Chinese herbal medicine. The proper dose should be further evaluated if the herbs are used in other schools of medicine.

There were some limitations in the present study. First, data update is always needed. Diets are highly varied worldwide. In addition to data of nutrients common in cuisine of the US and China, foods from other regions could be added in the future. Second, performance of the recommendation system was evaluated by dietary schedules used in China and recorded in clinical trials in Spain and Israel. Accuracy and feasibility of the recommendation system for dietary schedules in other regions could be assessed further. Third, nutrients of the same food would be changed with different cooking or processing. Inaccurate input of food would inevitably undermine the accuracy of the DHerbKB recommendation system. Last, recommended dose and toxic dose of the herb used in other traditional medicines, such as in Africa and in Europe, could be added in the future.

Conclusions

To enhance the quality of CKD dietary management and to prevent inappropriate use of Chinese herbal medicine, the present study developed the knowledge base named the DHerbKB along with the recommendation system. The knowledge base incorporated a broad range of CKD complication-related nutrients data, and comprehensively included data on Chinese herbal medicine which may have nephrotoxic effects. The recommendation system of the DHerbKB was demonstrated to be an easy-to-use and reliable tool supporting CKD management. The normalized resources established by the present study provides high-quality open resource for studies on CKD management, nutrition and artificial intelligence of clinical support.
