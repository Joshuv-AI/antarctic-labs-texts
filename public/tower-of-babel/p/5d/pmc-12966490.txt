In-Depth characterization of the shared genetic architecture of suicide attempts with other major psychiatric disorders
Authors: Min Ji Kim, Sophia Gunn, Dong Wang, Fion Shiau, Pedro Lazcano, J John Mann, TJ Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuicide is a significant public health problem that usually co-occurs with major psychiatric disorders. Suicidal behaviors have heritability of 30–50%, and the largest genome-wide association studies identified 12 loci linked to suicide attempts (SA). These findings indicate shared genetic architecture among SA and psychiatric disorders. We analyzed public GWAS summary statistics of SA, major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BIP), schizophrenia (SCZ), and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) to quantify genetic overlap using statistical genetics methods: MiXeR for polygenic overlap, LAVA for locus-specific genetic correlations, and HyPrColoc for multi-trait colocalization. MTAG and conditional false discovery rate (condFDR) identified SA- associated loci, while conjunctional false discovery rate (conjFDR) identified shared loci with psychiatric disorders. Additionally, we used polygenic risk scores (PRS) calculated in the UK Biobank to validate associations between genetic liabilities of psychiatric disorders and SA. SA involve approximately 6.9k (SD = 1.5k) risk variants with substantial overlap between psychiatric disorders, ranging from 53.2% with SCZ to 82.1% with BIP. Using MTAG, condFDR, and conjFDR, we identified 14 and 48 novel risk loci for SA, respectively. Through conjFDR, we identified 78 loci associated with SA and major psychiatric disorders, including one locus shared across all five traits. Genes linked to SA were enriched in synaptic components and signaling pathways. Despite significant genetic overlap, the SA PRS was the single strongest predictor of SA, followed by the MDD and ADHD PRS. The genetic overlap reflects potential common comorbidities complicating the identification of biological processes unique to SA, instead reflecting a complex genetic framework shared with psychiatric disorders.Subject terms: Clinical genetics, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

Suicide is a critical public health priority, with 49,316 lives lost to suicide in 2023 in the United States, most commonly occurring in the context of an episode of major depression [1]. Historically, suicide attempt(SA) has been primarily viewed as a specific symptom and part of the diagnostic criteria of major depressive disorder (MDD) and borderline personality disorder or as a potential adverse outcome of many other psychiatric diagnoses [2]. However, the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) included Suicidal Behavior Disorder as a “condition for further study” to stress its importance as a separate entity with its own specific neurobiology and symptomatology that spans multiple diagnoses in DSM-5 [2].

The etiology of SA remains incompletely understood, but is known to involve social determinants of health, early life adversity, and its pathophysiology includes neurotransmitters such as serotonin, catecholamines, GABA, and glutamate, alongside the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, inflammation, and neuroplastic pathways [3, 4]. Studies report elevated rates of concordant suicidal behavior among identical twins compared with fraternal twins and in adopted children of a parent who died by suicide, indicating 30–50% heritability, suggesting a significant genetic contribution [5–8]. Adoption studies found that the transmission of major psychiatric disorders did not fully explain the transmission of suicidal behavior [9]. Given the frequent and strong association of SA with other psychopathologies, especially mood disorders, it is not clear to what degree the heritability of SA is dependent on the co-occurrence of other psychopathologies. Several GWAS studies have attempted to disentangle this by estimating heritability before and after conditioning on psychiatric disorders [10–12]. However, these analyses typically adjust for one disorder at a time, missing the complexity of comorbidity. Moreover, since most SA occur in individuals with psychiatric diagnoses, conditioning may inadvertently remove SA-relevant genetic variance. Distinguishing shared from unique genetic risk is essential for understanding SA etiology and identifying both pleiotropic and SA-specific risk loci.

Conventionally, assessing shared heritability in complex disorders involves estimating genetic correlations using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) [13]. SA showed a high genetic correlation with psychiatric disorders such as MDD, bipolar disorder (BIP), schizophrenia (SCZ), and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD); however, the results varied depending on whether the meta-analysis was conditioned on MDD and post-traumatic stress disorder [12]. Although these findings suggest that these phenotypes are highly related, LDSC cannot fully capture shared genetic architecture and fails to identify the specific loci-wise correlation and pertinent genes [14–16]. To overcome these limitations, recently developed statistical methodologies, such as bivariate causal mixture model (MiXeR), and local analysis of co-variant annotation (LAVA), which compute the proportion of shared single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and estimate pairwise local genetic correlation, have been applied to more precisely delineate the shared genetic architecture across complex traits [16, 17]. Additionally, the incorporation of analytical approaches like multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) [18], which improves statistical power by jointly analyzing genetically correlated traits, and conditional and conjunctional false discovery rate (condFDR and conjFDR) [19, 20], which identifies genomic loci simultaneously associated with other phenotypes, allows us to leverage genetic overlap more effectively. These methods complement each other, with MTAG and condFDR enhancing the overall detection of trait-associated variants, and conjFDR pinpointing loci with direct pleiotropic effects across suicidal behaviors and psychiatric disorders, thereby providing a more detailed understanding of shared genetic architecture.

Considering the substantial co-occurrence of SA with various major psychiatric disorders, our objective was to find SA risk loci leveraging psychiatric disorders and quantify the genetic relationships between them. We applied different statistical genetics methods, including MiXeR, LAVA, MTAG, cond/conjFDR to assess the genetic convergence with SA and major psychiatric disorders at various levels of resolution - across the whole genome and at each locus. Finally, we validated how much the genetic liability to SA and major psychiatric disorders predicted actual SA in an independent sample using polygenic risk scores (PRS), which aggregated the effects of multiple genetic variants across the genome.

Method

Data acquisition

GWAS summary statistics

We used the largest genome-wide association study (GWAS) available for suicide attempts meta-analyzed by the International Suicide Genetics Consortium (ISGC) [12]. We also obtained GWAS summary statistics for SCZ, BIP, MDD, and ADHD from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC). These GWAS study populations comprised only European ancestry populations (Table 1). We prioritized MDD, BIP, SCZ, and ADHD due to their high heritability, strong genetic correlations with SA demonstrated in prior studies, and the availability of large GWAS summary statistics, ensuring sufficient analytical power [12, 21, 22]. All summary statistics and analyses were based on the GRCh37/hg19 genome build.

UK Biobank

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large, population-based cohort of over 500,000 participants, extensively profiled for lifestyle, physical, and mental health factors. A subset of participants (n = 157,366) completed a self-reported online mental health questionnaire. For this study, we focused on the SA using the variable “attempted suicide” (Field ID 20483). After excluding individuals with missing responses, those who chose not to answer, we also filtered by genetic quality control filters (Field ID 22020). Used for genetic principal components and Field ID 22027 Outliers for heterozygosity or missingness outliers), the final analytic sample included 128,998 participants. The SA phenotype was dichotomized as “yes (n = 2967)” or “no (n = 126,031).”

Statistical analysis

Global and locus-wise genetic overlap

Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) analysis

We employed non-stratified LDSC analysis(v1.0.1) [13] to estimate the global genetic correlation between SA and major psychiatric disorders. After filtering SNPs with the HapMap3 panel, LDSC regression analysis utilized pre-computed linkage disequilibrium (LD) scores from the 1000 Genomes European dataset. Genetic covariance was estimated by regressing SNP statistics, and the genetic correlation (rg) was calculated by normalizing this covariance with SNP heritability.

MiXeR

We conducted pairwise MiXeR analyses to quantify the overlap between SA and major psychiatric disorders, aiming to improve our understanding of the shared genetic architecture across traits [16, 23]. Initially, univariate MiXeR analyses determined the number of genetic variants influencing 90% of the SNP-based heritability, accounting for LD. Subsequently, bivariate MiXeR analyses estimated the number of shared SNPs between pairs of phenotypes, disregarding the direction of their effects. To further quantify the degree of overlap, we calculated mean Dice coefficients, representing the proportion of shared genetic variants relative to the total number of variants influencing both traits.

Local genetic correlation analysis (LAVA)

We employed LAVA to calculate pairwise local rg [17]. Unlike global methods like LDSC, which estimate a single genome-wide correlation, LAVA provides a fine-grained analysis by identifying local rg in approximately equal-sized 2495 semi-independent genetic regions (around 1 Mb each), revealing locus-specific overlaps that may be missed at the global level. LAVA computes the local genetic covariance matrix for each region, incorporating the estimation of local h2 for each trait. Sample overlap was controlled through cross-trait LDSC. To correct for multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was applied independently for each pairwise comparison between SA and psychiatric disorders by dividing the alpha level 0.05 by the total number of loci tested 2495.

Colocalization using HyPrColoc

We conducted colocalization analysis employing HyPrColoc [24] in loci that were significant in LAVA. While LAVA identifies correlated regions between traits, it does not determine whether the same causal variant drives the association across traits.

HyPrColoc, a deterministic Bayesian algorithm designed for efficient multi-trait analysis, addresses this limitation by estimating the probability of colocalization across multiple traits. It outputs posterior probabilities of colocalization, identifies the most likely causal variant within the colocalized region, and reports the proportion of the posterior probability attributed to that variant.

We performed HyPrColoc colocalization analysis using two approaches: one assuming complete independence (no sample overlap or trait correlation), and another incorporating a trait correlation matrix from LDSC while assuming full sample overlap (identity matrix), as recommended by the developers (https://github.com/cnfoley/hyprcoloc). This approach has been shown to improve detection of truly colocalized traits and reduce sensitivity to mis-specified sample overlap matrices.

Discovery of novel and shared genetic variants and their functional annotation

Multitrait Analysis of Genome-Wide Association Studies (MTAG)

The MTAG is an analytical approach that facilitates the concurrent examination of genetically correlated traits, thereby enhancing statistical efficacy in identifying variants associated with each trait [18]. MTAG extends the conventional inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis methodology to the exploration of multiple traits. The method incorporates consideration for sample overlap among GWASs in different traits, employing LD score regression.

Conditional/conjunctional false discovery rate

We utilized condFDR and conjFDR methods to enhance the identification of genomic loci that are associated with both SA and psychiatric disorders. The condFDR method improves the detection of trait-specific associations by re-ranking SNPs in a primary trait based on their association level in a secondary trait [19, 20]. This Bayesian approach leverages cross-trait enrichment to increase statistical power while avoiding the strict multiple testing seen in traditional GWAS meta-analyses. Unlike MTAG, which re-estimates effect sizes across multiple traits simultaneously, condFDR retains the original GWAS statistics and prioritizes SNPs that are more likely to be associated with the primary trait. The conjFDR identifies SNPs jointly associated with both traits by estimating the probability that a variant is null for either, offering a conservative method to detect true pleiotropic loci. While condFDR improves trait-specific discovery using related traits, conjFDR focuses on uncovering shared genetic architecture.

Both condFDR and conjFDR assume independence between GWAS datasets. Sample overlap can inflate pleiotropic enrichment and increase false positives, thus interpretation requires caution when overlapping samples are present.

Functional annotations and gene set analyses

We used the Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA v1.6.1) protocol to annotate SNPs found in MTAG, condFDR, and conjFDR [25]. Initially, genome-wide significant SNPs (P ≤ 5.0 × 10 − 8) were independently identified within a 1 Mb window, ensuring independence (r2 < 0.6) from each other. Subsequently, lead SNPs, a subset of independently significant SNPs in LD (r2 < 0.1) within a 1 Mb window were identified. Genomic risk loci were determined by merging lead SNPs closer than 250 kb apart, allowing for multiple lead SNPs within a locus. The 1000 Genomes European reference panel was utilized for mapping LD. ANNOVAR was then employed to map lead SNPs to their nearest genes, and loci within 250 kb were further merged into a single risk locus [26]. Further analysis involved using FUMA tools for functional annotation and tissue expression with default parameters. We also performed gene-based association analysis, employing MAGMA v1.08 software, which collapses associated signals of SNPs within a gene in the window of 1 kb. MAGMA gene-set analysis is performed for curated 10,678 gene sets (curated gene sets: 4761, GO terms: 5917) from MsigDB v6.2. We obtained the expression of genes identified in 53 different human tissues using Genotype-Tissue Expression v8 (GTEx v8) using MAGAMA tissue expression analysis. All methods that we applied are summarized in Figure S1.

PRS score calculation

The UK Biobank has made available genotypes that have undergone quality control processes, including removing participants with excess heterozygosity or missingness rate and removing markers without information related to batch, plate, genetic sex, and array. The methods for imputing data in the UK Biobank database have been detailed elsewhere [27]. We utilized SA and other psychiatric disorders’ summary statistics that excluded data from the UK Biobank cohort to avoid sample overlap (SA case=33,353, control = 444,626). We used PRS-CS to calculate the PRS for SA, MDD, BIP, and SCZ. PRS-CS employs a Bayesian regression framework with high dimensionality and incorporates a continuous shrinkage prior on SNP effect sizes [28].

We ran PRS-CS using European ancestry reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project and UK Biobank. The global shrinkage parameter (phi) was set to auto mode for automatic optimization. Effect sizes generated in PRS-CS were passed to PLINK v1.9 for scoring.

After PRS calculation, we used logistic regression to estimate PRS associated with SA using models that included each PRS score of the phenotypes and adjusted for genetic sex and 10 genetic principal components (PCs). In addition to individual PRS models, we also performed a full model including all SA PRS and PRSs for MDD, BIP, SCZ, and ADHD, adjusting for sex and 10PCs. Nagelkerke’s R² was computed to estimate the variance explained by each PRS. To convert R² values to the liability scale, we used a population prevalence estimate of 0.024, based on a median lifetime SA prevalence of approximately 2–3% [29], and a case-control proportion of 0.024 from our analytic sample.

Results

Genetic overlap between suicide attempts and other psychiatric disorders

Genome-wide genetic correlations using LDSC showed a robust positive genetic correlation (rg) between SA and four other psychiatric disorders (Table S1). MDD demonstrated the highest correlation (0.887, SE = 0.071), followed by ADHD at 0.653 (SE = 0.055) and BIP at 0.591 (SE = 0.089). SCZ exhibited the lowest correlation of 0.445 (SE = 0.055) among the studied disorders. MiXeR analysis showed a comparable pattern, though with slightly lower rg estimates (Fig. 1A), likely reflecting its focus on the shared effect sizes of trait-influencing variants rather than genome-wide signal.

In the bivariate MiXeR analysis, SA also showed a large genetic overlap with other psychiatric disorders. It was estimated to have many shared variants with MDD (6.8k ± 1.4k), BIP (5.7k ± 0.9k), ADHD (5.3k ± 0.8k) and SCZ (4.2k ± 0.7k) (Fig. 1A). The Dice coefficients indicated a substantial overlap with MDD (72.2%), BIP (82.1%) ADHD (70.8%), and SCZ (53.2%). Most variants influencing traits shared between SA and each psychiatric disorder showed a concordant effect direction, with 97.6% for MDD, 76.2% for BIP, 88.3% for SCZ, and 87.5% for ADHD (Table S2).

The conditional Q-Q plots for cross-trait polygenic enrichment between SA and psychiatric disorders showed successive leftward deflections as levels of SNP associations with the secondary phenotype increased, which implies the presence of polygenic enrichment between SA and each psychiatric disorder (Fig. 1B). This enrichment was also consistent in the reverse conditional Q-Q plots (Figure S2).

Local genetic correlation estimates using LAVA found 481 loci with nominal p < 0.05 correlated between SA and other major psychiatric disorders. Eleven loci remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction with nine positively correlated loci between SAand MDD, and two positively correlated loci between SA and SCZ (Fig. 1C, Table S3-4).

We utilized the Hyprcoloc method to identify candidate SNPs within highly correlated loci in LAVA. Assuming independence among GWAS summary statistics, five loci exhibited posterior probabilities exceeding 0.7 (Table 2). Notably, locus chr 7: 113339387-115321301 manifested two patterns of colocalization: one involving SA, BIP, and SCZ, and the other comprising MDD and ADHD. This observation was consistent with the region plot, which depicted distinct groupings of phenotypes within the locus (Fig. 2B). Additionally, colocalization of SA, MDD, BIP, and SCZ was observed at chr11:112755447–113889019 (Fig. 2C); SA and MDD at chr18:49790997–50372823; and SA, MDD, SCZ, and ADHD at chr18:50372824–51060986 (Fig. 2D). However, when accounting for trait correlation matrix assuming full sample overlap, only two loci retained statistical significance, demonstrating colocalization between BIP and SCZ (chr 11: 112755447-113889019) and between MDD and SCZ (chr 18: 50372824-51060986).

Risk and shared loci for suicide attempt using MTAG and condFDR/conjFDR

The SA GWAS by Docherty et.al., initially identified 8 risk loci [12] (Table S5). However, after applying MTAG to the SAsummary statistics, 114 risk loci were identified(Table S6), including 14 novel loci not previously found in the original GWAS of SA or other psychiatric disorders (Table S6, “MTAG only” column).

The condFDR analyses (condFDR < 0.01) identified 48 loci associated with SA conditional on major psychiatric disorders, which did not contain either SA or other psychiatric disorders GWAS hits(Table S15), including 26 loci for SA | MDD, 11 for SA | BIP, 9 for SA | ADHD, and 4 for SA | SCZ, which were specific to the conditional analysis (Table S7, S9, S11, and S13 “condFDR only” column).

Next, we employed bi-directional cross-trait enrichment analysis to enhance statistical power in identifying shared loci, utilizing conjFDR analysis. We identified 78 genomic loci jointly associated with SA and major psychiatric disorders with a conjFDR < 0.01 (Table S16). Specifically, SA shared 71 loci with MDD, 5 with BIP, 14 with SCZ, and 11 with ADHD(Table S8, S10, S12, S14). Notably, a considerable proportion of these shared loci exhibited overlap across different pairs of traits, as depicted in a Venn diagram (Fig. 3). The locus that all pairs of SA and psychiatric disorder shared was chr 11:113241877-113316102 where the DRD2 gene is located.

Functional annotations and gene set analysis on loci found in MTAG, conjFDR

The results of gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on genes annotated to loci identified through MTAG indicated enrichment of biological processes associated with synaptic specialization, encompassing both presynaptic and postsynaptic structures(sTable 17). Furthermore, gene set analyses conducted on shared loci between SA and MDD, revealed processes such as forebrain neuron fate commitment, cerebral cortex neuron differentiation, as well as core regulatory processes including ribonuclease activities, RNA processing, and histone modification (sTable 18). Similarly, shared loci between SA and BIP were associated with processes related to neonatal hippocampus, alongside regulatory processes involving protein localization, exocytic vesicles, and splicing (sTable 19). Shared loci between SA and SCZ were implicated in lipid metabolism, androgen/progesterone biosynthesis, and peptide biosynthesis (sTable 20). Additionally, shared loci between SA and ADHD were linked to muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, along with core biological pathways such as microtubule regulation, regulatory T cell differentiation, and transcriptional regulation (sTable 21).

PRS for major psychiatric disorders predicting suicide attempt

In individual models,the PRS for MDD, BIP, SCZ, ADHD, and SA were associated with actual SA in the UK Biobank sample when adjusted for genetically determined sex and top 10 PCs with p < 0.001. SA PRS showed the largest effect estimate for SA (beta = 0.326, CI [0.288-0.365]). Other than SA PRS, MDD PRS had the largest effect estimate to predict SA (beta = 0.231, CI [0.194-0.268]) (Fig. 4). SA PRS remained a significant predictor after adjusted for the four disorder PRSs in the full model containing all PRS(sTable 22). Nagelkerke’s R2 and R2 on the liability scale indicated that the largest proportion of variance could be explained by the SA PRS followed by the MDD PRS (sTable 23). The effect size and R2 of ADHD PRS on the liability scale for SA were comparable to those of MDD PRS.

Discussion

Our results precisely quantify and confirm SA as a highly polygenic phenotype influenced by nearly seven thousand genetic variants, showing extensive genetic overlap with other major psychiatric disorders. We identified 5 loci where SA and other psychiatric disorders are correlated and colocalized, implying pleiotropic effects. Through leveraging genetic overlap with major psychiatric disorders, we found a nearly tenfold increase in detected genetic loci associated with SA. This expanded identification of loci, along with the annotation of corresponding genes, unveiled a broader spectrum of biological pathways potentially implicated in the pathophysiology of SA. The polygenic liabilities associated with SA, MDD, BIP, SCZ, and ADHD predicted SA in an independent population-based sample.

The locus located at chr 11:113241877-113316102, proximal to the DRD2 gene, has showed a significant correlation between SA and MDD when using LAVA. Subsequent colocalization analysis revealed a shared causal SNP among SA, MDD, BIP, and SCZ GWAS. The locus was the only common locus among all pairs of SA and psychiatric disorders. The DRD2 gene encodes the dopamine D2 receptor (D2R), a G-protein coupled receptor found on both dopamine (autoreceptors) and non-dopamine neurons (heteroreceptors). DRD2 gene has been extensively associated with psychiatric disorders and psychopathologies such as depression, schizophrenia, alcoholism, neuroticism, aggression, lowered sociability, etc., and its functioning is conserved across evolutionary distant species [30–35]. The DRD2 gene also was associated with suicidal behavior in both the PGC and MVP data sets [12]. Our findings imply a broader pleiotropic impact of the DRD2 gene, indicating that genetic variations within this gene may contribute to SA as well as various psychiatric disorders whose co-occurrence might be explained because they are genetically and pathophysiologically related.

The locus at chr 7:113339387-115321301, where the FOXP2 gene resides, demonstrated shared colocalization across SA, MDD, BIP, and SCZ. Similarly, the locus at chr 18:50372824-5160986, where the MAPK4 gene is located, exhibited shared colocalization among SA, MDD, SCZ, and ADHD. FOXP2 and MAPK4 have been implicated in various GWAS, with FOXP2 associated with cannabis dependence [36], childhood maltreatment [37], post-trauma stress disorder [38], late diagnosed ADHD [39], and MAPK4 has been linked to psychosis [40, 41]. These loci display pleiotropic effects and may be connected to traits specific to or shared among different phenotypes. However, the functional roles of these pleiotropic variants and genes remain largely unknown, and the causal molecular mechanisms underlying the clinical relationships between SA and psychiatric disorders remain incompletely understood.

It is noteworthy that colocalization signals identified under the assumption of independent samples diminished when accounting for trait correlation using Hyprcoloc. Because detailed individual-level sample overlap data were unavailable, we used trait correlation to adjust for sample overlap. While this approach has limitations, it also highlights a broader issue—the challenge of sample overlap in current SA GWAS and its implications for interpreting shared genetic architecture. Current GWAS studies define psychiatric disorders without considering various comorbidities, leading to study populations that are heterogeneous in terms of comorbidities and severity. Furthermore, SA samples included in SA GWAS predominantly originated from preexisting psychiatric genomics consortiums associated with depression, bipolar disorder, eating disorders, major depressive disorder, and schizophrenia [12]. The utilization of samples derived from these disorder cohorts introduces high sample overlap and may introduce bias to the results of SA GWAS, leading to a substantial genetic overlap with other psychiatric disorders.

Functionally, genetic loci implicated in SA have associations with synaptic structure and cellular processes. There is an overrepresentation of synaptic genes in SA, highlighting the potential relevance of synaptic mechanisms and their interplay with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the pathogenesis of suicide [42, 43]. The shared genetic loci between SA and other clinically associated psychiatric disorders may both share diverse biological pathways but also reflect the diversity of suicide’s heterogeneous behavior and its pathogenesis. Specifically, the shared loci between SA and MDD are associated with neuronal differentiation and fate commitment, while those shared between SA and SCZ are more closely linked to lipid metabolism. Previous studies have pointed out that SA among schizophrenia spectrum disorders are associated with aberrant lipid metabolism, explained by cholesterol-serotonin-impulsivity model [44–46]. Given that SA typically occurs in the context of underlying psychiatric conditions, examining its genetic architecture in conjunction with related disorders may help to dissect the biological heterogeneity of SA. These findings suggest that leveraging the genetic architectures of different psychiatric disorders can provide a more nuanced understanding of SA risk and help clarify the biological pathways that may differ depending on the clinical context.

As anticipated, most identified SA loci were found to be shared with MDD. The variations in the number of loci shared with each psychiatric disorder may in part be attributed to differences in the sample sizes and statistical power of the GWAS, with MDD GWAS having the highest power. Additionally, these differences may reflect the substantial clinical co-occurrence of SA and MDD, whereby the diagnostic criteria to determine a major depressive episode include suicidal ideation and SA [2].

In PRS analysis, SA PRS predicted SA the most. The higher predictive power of the SA PRS, despite originating from a GWAS with low power and heritability, may reflect several possible factors. First, the phenotypic specificity of the SA GWAS might capture genetic liability more directly related to SA than broader psychiatric diagnoses such as MDD or ADHD, thereby improving predictive precision. Second, SA GWAS might reflect more severely affected or more deeply phenotyped cases, which can amplify genetic signals through ascertainment bias. In contrast to MDD and ADHD PRSs, which may capture generalized psychiatric vulnerability, the SA PRS may index genetic pathways more proximal to suicidal behavior. These factors might lead to more specific or larger beta estimates, that result in stronger individual SNP contributions to the PRS.

PRS for major other psychiatric disorders were also associated with SA. Among these, MDD PRS and ADHD PRS showed the largest effect sizes in predicting SA, aside from the SA PRS itself. While the association with MDD is expected due to overlapping diagnostic criteria, the strong association with ADHD highlights its significant contribution to the elevated risk of suicidal behavior [47–49]. The theoretical underpinning might be centered on impulsivity, a well-established personality trait, which is a core symptom of ADHD [2].

Limitations

Only using European ancestry constrains the generalizability of the results to other ancestries, as there is a scarcity of GWAS conducted on non-European ancestry individuals with a sample size sufficient to ensure robust statistical power. Also, with the limited inclusion of rare variants, our current investigation has concentrated solely on common variants. The exploration of pleiotropy involving rare genetic loci associated with psychiatric disorders holds the promise of providing a more comprehensive insight into the shared genetic underpinnings among these conditions. In the PRS analysis, we used SA that were collected based on yes or no self-reports for questions that people can choose not to disclose. SA varies in intent and degree of medical injury, and this clinical variance can also likely be reflected in genetic risk [50–54]. More valid and reliable assessment tools to assess suicidal behavior and ideation should be employed for better delineation of suicidal behavior.

Conclusion

We found the genetic architecture of SA revealed a pronounced polygenicity, contributing to the unresolved heritability of SA. By applying recently developed computational methods, we obtained more precise estimates of genetic correlation and colocalization—both at the genome-wide level and at specific loci—thereby offering a more detailed understanding of the genetic overlap and divergence between SA and related psychiatric disorders. This shared genetic architecture may contribute to the coexistence of SA with other psychiatric disorders and underlying neurobiology of comorbidities. Genes associated with SA exhibited involvement in a wide array of biological pathways and displayed distinct tissue expression across diverse tissues. Our findings advance the understanding of the pathophysiology of SA, whether occurring independently or concurrently with other psychiatric disorders. Further research should delve into the genetic and epigenetic foundations of specific SA -related phenotypes.
