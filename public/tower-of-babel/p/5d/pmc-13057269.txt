Influence of pore structure on grain bulk modulus of underground rock masses under hydro-mechanical conditions
Authors: Min-Jun Kim, Junhyung Choi, Eui-Seob Park, Byung-Gon Chae, Myungsun Kim, Jong-Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present an evaluation of the grain bulk modulus of rocks through experimental and theoretical methods. Unjacketed tests were performed on three rock types—Berea sandstone, Idaho sandstone, and Hwangdeung granite—to measure grain-scale compressibility. Theoretical estimates were obtained using mineral composition data and the Voigt–Reuss–Hill averaging method. To examine the role of pore structure, X-ray computed tomography (CT) was employed to visualize and quantify pore geometry, including isolated pores. Experimental results indicated material-dependent variation in grain bulk modulus not directly correlated with conventional mechanical properties. The theoretical values, as represented by the Hill average, were consistently higher than the experimental measurements, with overestimations of 7.2% for Berea sandstone, 37.3% for Idaho sandstone, and 31.7% for Hwangdeung granite. X-ray CT analysis confirmed that isolated pores contribute to additional volumetric deformation, which is unaccounted for in volume fraction-based models. A correlation between theoretical estimates and experimental values was derived to improve the applicability of the model. Conclusively, we present a systematic methodology for accurately evaluating grain bulk modulus by integrating experimental and theoretical approaches, offering foundational data and insight critical for geomechanical modeling in deep subsurface applications such as radioactive waste disposal or underground liquid hydrogen storage.Keywords: Pore structure, Grain bulk modulus, Unjacketed test, Volume fraction-based modelSubject terms: Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Globally, many countries are undertaking national-scale projects to address challenges such as sustainable energy development and climate change mitigation. These efforts involve the exploration and utilization of deep geological environments, including the geological disposal of high-level radioactive waste (HLW), carbon capture and storage (CCS), and underground energy storage systems. The safe and effective implementation of such projects requires advanced techniques for characterizing deep rock formations1–3. Particularly, these deep subsurface environments are subject to complex thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) interactions under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. Accordingly, it is necessary to develop reliable techniques capable of accurately evaluating such THM-coupled processes for accurate long-term performance assessments.

In deep geological environments, porous media saturated with groundwater exhibit coupled interactions between rock deformation and pore pressure, forming the fundamental basis for hydro-mechanical coupled behavior. The bulk modulus, which describes volumetric deformation, is typically categorized into grain, drained, and undrained bulk moduli depending on the experimental conditions4. Among these, the grain bulk modulus represents the intrinsic compressibility of mineral grains5.

The grain bulk modulus represents the volumetric response of rock to changes in pore water pressure, and is defined as the ratio of volumetric strain to pore pressure change6. In deep geological environments, where confining pressure significantly influences rock behavior, this parameter provides critical insight into the mechanical response of mineral grains under varying stress conditions. It also serves as a crucial input in the governing equations of numerical models used to simulate coupled THM behavior of rock formations. Therefore, accurate determination of the grain bulk modulus is crucial for reliable performance assessments and the safe design of deep underground systems, such as geological repositories for high-level radioactive waste7.

Earlier studies proposed a simple and efficient method for estimating the grain bulk modulus by identifying the mineralogical composition of rocks and applying known bulk modulus values to each mineral phase8. In that study, the mineral composition and the volumetric fractions of each constituent were considered to estimate the Voigt and Reuss bounds and the Hill average. As a result, the analysis provides a range of possible values for grain bulk modulus, without offering a detailed discussion on the most representative value or the associated uncertainties and limitations of such estimations. However, this approach often leads to overestimation or underestimation of the grain bulk modulus, as it does not adequately consider boundary interactions such as cementation effects and matrix geometry. Consequently, experimental measurements are widely regarded as the most accurate means of determining the grain bulk modulus9. Nevertheless, no standardized procedure for directly measuring this parameter was established by organizations such as the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) or the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM), highlighting the need to develop reliable experimental methodologies.

Rocks are generally composed of solid grains and pores, with the pore space further classified into connected (i.e., effective pores) and isolated pores10. Connected pores form continuous pathways within the grain framework, enabling fluid transport through the rock matrix. In contrast, isolated pores are void spaces enclosed within the grain skeleton that do not contribute to fluid flow (Fig. 1). While the volumetric behavior of the rock matrix is strongly influenced by connected porosity, additional deformation may result from the intrinsic compressibility of mineral grains and the effects of isolated pores. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of these pore characteristics is essential for accurately determining the grain bulk modulus11,12. The grain bulk modulus can be defined in two different ways: (i) the intrinsic mineral grain bulk modulus, which represents the compressibility of pore-free solid mineral grains and is not affected by the presence of pores, and (ii) the effective grain bulk modulus, which is obtained experimentally and may reflect the influence of pore-scale heterogeneity, including isolated pores. In this study, the term grain bulk modulus is used to refer to the effective grain bulk modulus, as this quantity is more relevant for engineering applications, such as hydro-mechanical numerical modeling.

The grain bulk modulus can be evaluated through theoretical and experimental approaches. The theoretical approach estimates the grain bulk modulus based on the mineralogical composition of the rock, typically determined via X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. In this method, known bulk moduli are assigned to each mineral constituent identified in the rock sample, and the overall grain bulk modulus is subsequently calculated. Earlier studies employed this approach using mathematical formulations derived from mineral composition and concentration, providing a simple and efficient means of estimation8,9,13. However, this approach is limited by its lack of consideration for pore structure characteristics, which can significantly influence the mechanical behavior of the rock.

The grain bulk modulus can also be evaluated experimentally using the unjacketed test, which measures the volumetric strain of rock specimens under high confining pressure. A previous study applied this method to Berea sandstone and Indiana limestone, demonstrating that pore compressibility varies with the presence of compliant clay minerals and microcrack-bearing quartz5. In addition, unjacketed tests were performed on various sandstones and synthetic silica, revealing that the grain bulk modulus remains relatively constant with changes in effective pressure, whereas the pore modulus exhibits strong pressure dependence14. They also observed that non-connected pores influence the measured grain modulus. In a separate investigation, volumetric strain and pore pressure changes in Berea sandstone during lateral deformation were measured, while an acoustic emission (AE) system was employed to monitor AE events and estimate their source locations15. Although these studies reveal important volumetric deformation behaviors through unjacketed testing, this method has limitations in accounting for pore structures influencing the grain bulk modulus. More recently, an experimental approach was used to estimate the grain bulk modulus, and relatively low values were reported, which were attributed to experimental factors such as unconnected pores and the gauge–epoxy layer9. Nevertheless, these factors were not explicitly or quantitatively evaluated in their study.

Here, the grain bulk modulus of rocks was evaluated using both experimental and theoretical approaches. Berea sandstone, Idaho sandstone, and Hwangdeung granite were selected to represent different pore structures. Unjacketed tests were performed as the experimental method, while theoretical estimation was based on mineral composition using the Voigt–Reuss–Hill (VRH) averaging method. By directly comparing VRH-based estimates with experimentally measured grain bulk modulus from unjacketed tests, this study aims to highlight and quantify the discrepancy arising when the idealized assumptions of the VRH method are not fully satisfied in natural rocks. X-ray computed tomography (CT) was employed to analyze pore microstructures and assess their influence on the grain bulk modulus. The methodology and results obtained from the unjacketed tests provide a robust foundation for accurately determining the grain bulk modulus. In summary, this study conducted experiments to quantitatively analyze unconnected pores that were not evaluated in previous studies. A triaxial testing system equipped with an extensometer, which does not induce a gauge–epoxy layer, was employed to reduce experimental artifacts associated with conventional volumetric strain measurements. This approach not only minimizes the limitations inherent in previously proposed methods for measuring the grain bulk modulus, but also enables an analytical investigation of potential theoretical sources of error based on X-ray CT data. Furthermore, understanding the impact of isolated pore structures on the grain bulk modulus may offer valuable insights for future applications in deep underground environments.

Theoretical background

Grain bulk modulus

Poroelasticity provides a fundamental framework for analyzing the hydro-mechanical behavior of deep rock masses. It describes the coupled interaction between fluid flow and deformation of the solid matrix in linear, porous media. This theory describes the mechanical deformation characteristics of rocks under saturated conditions within the elastic regime. In porous media, changes in pore pressure caused by fluid flow induce deformation in the skeleton, which subsequently modifies pore pressure and influences fluid flow. Poroelastic theory quantitatively characterizes this hydro-mechanical coupling between pore pressure and rock deformation.

This assumption was explicitly discussed in17, where it was argued that the unjacketed bulk modulus obtained from a hydrostatic compression experiment could be taken as the bulk modulus of the solid phase. At the granular scale, the volumetric deformation of rock results from the combined deformation of grains and pores. The total volumetric strain is greater than that of the grain component alone. Since Kd < Ks, the Biot coefficient ranges between 0 and 1, indicating the proportion of the total stress carried by pore pressure. The grain bulk modulus directly influences the Biot coefficient and thus plays a crucial role in the hydro-mechanical behavior of rocks. Furthermore, accurate determination of the grain bulk modulus is essential, as it serves as a fundamental input for simulating thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) behavior of rock in deep geological environments18. In addition, in dual-porosity formulations, the grain bulk modulus Ks influences the hydro-mechanical response through Biot-type coupling parameters associated with the porous matrix continuum. Variations in Ks modify the coupling between pore pressure and matrix deformation, thereby affecting pressure evolution and matrix–fracture interactions in dual-porosity systems19. In equivalent continuum hydro-mechanical models, Ks enters the constitutive relations through the Biot tensor and skeleton modulus, which govern effective stress and deformation behavior. Consequently, changes in Ks influence deformation-controlled fracture opening/closure and permeability evolution20. Accordingly, the use of a corrected grain bulk modulus has important implications for both dual-porosity and equivalent continuum approaches by improving the representation of hydro-mechanical coupling in gas flow simulations. Therefore, to obtain parameters relevant to poroelastic characteristics, laboratory tests simulating isotropic confining conditions, such as triaxial compression tests, are essential.

The grain bulk modulus is a key material property characterizing the volumetric deformation of rock grains under increasing confining pressure. It is typically determined through unjacketed testing21. However, in such tests, the transmission of confining pressure depends on the pore structure—specifically, whether the pores are connected or isolated. Under isolated pore conditions, fluid flow is restricted, which can result in measured grain bulk modulus values lower than those of the theoretical predictions14. This deviation arises from localized stress concentrations caused by uneven pore pressure distribution, leading to deformation in limited regions and an overestimation of the volumetric strain.

In rocks with well-developed effective porosity, the confining fluid is uniformly distributed throughout the pore network, allowing for even pressure transmission to the grains and accurate measurement of the grain bulk modulus. Rocks dominated by isolated pores limit fluid penetration, and their internal structure affects pressure transfer to the grains, potentially distorting the measured bulk modulus values.

Therefore, accurate determination of the grain bulk modulus requires quantifying the pore structure and comparing experimental results with theoretical predictions. This approach is essential for understanding the influence of pore structure on the mechanical response of porous rocks under confining pressure.

Unjacketed test

To determine the input parameters of poroelastic theory, including the bulk modulus, it is essential to apply isotropic stress conditions. Such conditions are typically achieved through triaxial compression tests in which the confining pressure can be precisely controlled. Depending on the boundary conditions, triaxial tests for evaluating poroelastic parameters are generally classified as unjacketed, drained, and undrained tests21. Among these, the unjacketed test is specifically employed to measure the grain bulk modulus of the porous medium. In the unjacketed test, the confining fluid is brought into direct contact with the specimen without the use of a membrane or external jacket, allowing the confining pressure to act simultaneously on both the exterior surface and the internal pore space of the sample.

The concept of the unjacketed test was systematically formalized as a key component of poroelastic theory by17. According to Biot’s theory, a porous medium comprises a solid skeleton and a pore fluid, and to understand the mechanical behavior of such a composite under external loading, it is necessary to isolate and characterize the intrinsic volumetric response of the solid matrix. The unjacketed test was designed to satisfy this requirement by imposing an iso-stress condition, in which both the pore fluid and the solid matrix are subjected to an identical pressure increment. These conditions eliminate the influence of fluid flow and drainage, isolating the compressibility of the solid grain structure. Interpreting the unjacketed bulk modulus as the solid-phase bulk modulus implicitly assumes homogeneous deformation at the pore scale, such that strain is uniformly distributed throughout the sample. In addition, unjacketed testing assumes an incompressible pore fluid, non-reactive fluid–rock interactions, purely elastic deformation, and full saturation of the pore space. These assumptions may not be strictly valid for natural rocks, and any discrepancy between the measured unjacketed bulk modulus and the estimated solid mineral modulus therefore requires careful examination. In addition, recent studies have shown that, in micro-inhomogeneous rocks, the Biot coefficient and the effective pressure coefficient governing bulk volume deformation may differ, leading to discrepancies between unjacketed bulk modulus measurements and the intrinsic solid-phase bulk modulus22,23. From this perspective, deviations observed in unjacketed test results may reflect pore-scale heterogeneity rather than experimental uncertainty alone.

In this configuration, the absence of a membrane permits the confining fluid to fully penetrate the pore network of an intact rock specimen. Consequently, equilibrium is established between the externally applied confining pressure and the internal pore pressure, rendering the Terzaghi effective stress equal to zero24, i.e., the entire applied stress is borne equally by the pore fluid and the solid skeleton, leading to deformation originating exclusively from the compressibility of the solid constituents.

Accordingly, under the conditions of the unjacketed test, the observed volumetric strain reflects solely the response of the solid grains. The relationship between the external stress and the measured volumetric strain enables the calculation of the grain bulk modulus25. The test is conducted by placing a fully saturated specimen into a triaxial cell and applying an equal pressure increment to both the confining fluid and the internal pore fluid. The system is then allowed to stabilize under static conditions for a sufficient duration. Since the specimen is unjacketed, the confining fluid is in direct contact with the sample surface and can freely infiltrate the pore space. Once pressure equilibrium is reached, precise measurements of volumetric strain can be achieved. These measurements are then used to generate the pressure–strain response curve, the slope of which directly yields the grain bulk modulus.

Furthermore, by comparing the derived grain bulk modulus with the overall bulk modulus of the saturated rock obtained from conventional drained or undrained tests, the Biot coefficient can be quantitatively assessed. This coefficient represents the relative contribution of pore pressure to the overall mechanical response of the porous medium and plays a critical role in a numerical models of poroelasticity.

The theoretical background of the unjacketed test is intrinsically related to key poroelastic parameters such as porosity and compressibility. These parameters are essential for defining the constitutive stress–strain relationships of geo-materials in deep subsurface. Despite the conceptual and procedural simplicity of the unjacketed test, it remains one of the most direct methods for experimentally quantifying the intrinsic mechanical properties of the solid framework. This makes it an indispensable tool for accurately determining poroelastic parameters and enhancing the reliability of coupled hydro-mechanical models.

Mineral composition and volume fraction-based model: VRH averaging method

The bulk modulus of rock is a fundamental mechanical property describing its resistance to volumetric strain under external loads26. This property is significantly influenced by porosity, saturation level, and the distribution of microcracks. Consequently, experimentally measured bulk modulus values may not accurately reflect the intrinsic elastic behavior of the mineral particles comprising the rock. To overcome this limitation, the concept of the grain bulk modulus was introduced. It refers to the bulk modulus of an idealized, fully dense aggregate of mineral particles, excluding the effects of porosity and structural discontinuities.

The grain bulk modulus of a rock can be theoretically estimated based on the bulk moduli and volume fractions of its component mineral particles; this estimation is typically performed using the Voigt, Reuss, and Hill averaging methods9,27. These methods are based on different assumptions and are designed to provide upper and lower bounds for the bulk modulus28. The VRH model is formulated under idealized assumptions, including continuous and pore-free mineral phases and uniform stress or strain conditions. They are widely used to evaluate the elastic behavior of rocks in theoretical analyses.

The Voigt average is based on the assumption that all component minerals undergo the same strain (i.e., isostrain). It is calculated as the weighted sum of the bulk moduli (Ki) and volume fractions (fi) of the individual minerals (e.g., quartz, albite, and clay). This theoretical estimation represents the ideal elastic response of the rock matrix, excluding the influence of pore-related properties (e.g., porosity), and plays a crucial role in enhancing the accuracy of THM coupling analyses and numerical simulations of rock behavior.

Experimental program

Sample preparation

Unjacketed tests were conducted to determine the grain bulk modulus of rock specimens in this study. To investigate the influence of porosity on the grain bulk modulus, two types of rocks with contrasting porosity characteristics were selected: sandstone specimens with high porosity and granite with low porosity (Fig. 2). The sandstone specimens consisted of Berea and Idaho sandstones supplied by Kocurek Industries, while the granite sample was Hwangdeung granite obtained from South Korea.

Test specimens for unjacketed test.

To ensure accurate measurement of volumetric strain, all specimens were prepared in cylindrical form using standard NX core dimensions (diameter: 54 mm; length: 100 mm). Both sandstone types were macroscopically homogeneous and exhibited porosity values of approximately 18–21% for Berea and 29–30% for Idaho sandstone, respectively. In contrast, the granite specimens exhibited a significantly lower porosity, approximately 0.7–0.8% (Table 1).

Mechanical properties of test specimens.

Prior to the unjacketed tests, the basic mechanical properties of the rock specimens including uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), elastic modulus, and porosity were measured. For UCS testing of the sandstone specimens, a 160-ton capacity rock compression machine manufactured by MTS was used. This closed-loop system is controlled by an automatic servo control mechanism regulating hydraulic pressure in accordance with the test conditions. UCS measurement test was performed following ASTM D293829, in which axial compressive stress is applied until failure, and the peak load is recorded.

To measure axial and circumferential strains during UCS testing, a dial gauge-type displacement transducer was used in the axial direction, and a circumferential extensometer manufactured by MTS was employed to capture lateral deformation. Three specimens per rock type were tested following the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM) guidelines outlined in “Suggested Methods for Determining the Uniaxial Compressive Strength and Deformability of Rock Materials”30.

This method evaluates the effective porosity, defined as the volume of interconnected pores. Specimens were first oven-dried at 105 ± 3 °C for at least 24 h, and the dry weight (Wdry) was recorded. They were then saturated under vacuum conditions (< 800 Pa), and the saturated weight (Wsat) was measured to calculate porosity. Experimental setup and procedures

Unjacketed tests were conducted to determine the grain bulk modulus of rock specimens using the MTS 815 Testing System. The system comprises two intensifier pumps capable of generating confining and pore pressures up to 138 MPa, a 460-ton capacity loading frame, and an integrated controller (Fig. 3). To simulate triaxial loading conditions, a Model 636.60 triaxial cell (MTS Systems Corporation) was installed around the specimen. Within the triaxial cell, confining pressure was applied using hydraulic fluid to maintain a stable confining condition. To minimize potential experimental errors and electromagnetic interference between sensors and the control unit, 7 USP White Oil (Guardsman Inc., USA) was used as the confining medium.

Once the initial setup was complete, the triaxial cell was lowered and sealed by fastening bolts to prevent leakage of the confining fluid. The intensifier pump was subsequently activated to inject hydraulic oil into the triaxial cell. Following the completion of injection, the unjacketed tests were performed using the MTS 793 Controller, operated via the MultiPurpose Testware (MPT) program, which allowed customization of loading protocols.

The confining pressure was incrementally applied from 5 MPa to 50 MPa in 5 MPa intervals. Full saturation and pore pressure equilibration were verified by observing stabilization of the confining-pressure pump displacement, indicating complete pore pressure development within the specimen. Loading was applied only after stabilization of the pump displacement. In addition, loading–unloading cycles were conducted prior to the main experiments to confirm the elastic behavior of the specimens. At each target confining pressure, loading was paused to allow pressure stabilization before moving to the next step. The loading rate was set to 2.5 MPa/min, following the recommendations outlined in the ISRM Suggested Methods33. Each unjacketed test was repeated four times per specimen to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Specifically, the volumetric strain increased from approximately 0.00013 to 0.00169 for Berea sandstone, 0.00013 to 0.00152 for Idaho sandstone, and 0.00012 to 0.00132 for Hwangdeung granite as the confining pressure increased from 5 MPa to 50 MPa. A comparative analysis showed that the grain bulk modulus of Idaho sandstone and Hwangdeung granite were approximately 14.8% and 32.9% higher than that of Berea sandstone (Fig. 7). This variation is more attributed to the mineral composition and grain-level properties such as mineral density, rather than macroscopic mechanical properties. The rock mechanical properties of each rock type—including effective porosity, uniaxial compressive strength, and elastic modulus—exhibited no clear correlation with the grain bulk modulus (Table 1). Instead, earlier studies suggest that the grain bulk modulus is more sensitive to mineral composition and grain-level properties such as density9.

Particularly, a higher proportion of non-swelling minerals (e.g., albite, biotite) tend to increase the grain bulk modulus. These minerals are abundantly present in the Hwangdeung granite specimen, whereas Berea sandstone is predominantly composed of quartz, which exhibits a relatively lower grain bulk modulus34–36. The variation in mineral composition explains the comparatively low value in Berea sandstone and the high value in Hwangdeung granite.

To evaluate the reliability of the experimental results, comparisons were made with available data from previous experimental studies. For Berea sandstone, earlier research reported grain bulk modulus values in the range of approximately 29.1–29.4 GPa, which is within 1.8% difference of the value obtained in this study25. However, no earlier studies experimentally determined the grain bulk modulus of Idaho sandstone or Hwangdeung granite, limiting direct comparison.

Theoretical approach: mineral concentration

Theoretical estimation of grain bulk modulus was performed by combining the mineral composition with the bulk moduli of individual mineral phases. This approach employed the VRH averaging method, as outlined in Eqs. 4–6, which provides bounds on elastic behavior in multiphase materials. Quantitative mineralogical analysis was conducted using XRD, a high-resolution technique for identifying crystalline phases and accurately determining their weight fractions.

In the XRD analysis, characteristic X-rays were directed onto the surface of powdered samples, and the resulting diffraction patterns were recorded. The angles and intensities of the diffracted beams provided information about the crystal structures of the constituent minerals. These patterns were compared with those of the standard reference data from the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) and the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) to identify each mineral phase37. Here, all three rock specimens were pulverized to a particle size of approximately 10 μm for the XRD analysis. The measurements were performed using the X’Pert MPD system (manufactured by Philips Inc.), which provided quantitative results on the mineral composition and corresponding concentrations of each sample.

The mineral compositions and concentrations obtained from XRD analyses of Berea sandstone, Idaho sandstone, and Hwangdeung granite are presented in Fig. 8; Table 2. Berea sandstone was predominantly composed of quartz (80.3%), followed by muscovite (9.0%) and microcline (6.7%). In contrast, Idaho sandstone consisted of quartz (51.0%), albite (26.1%), and orthoclase (22.9%), exhibiting relatively high concentrations of albite and orthoclase compared with those in Berea sandstone. Hwangdeung granite showed the highest albite content (36.1%) among the three samples, followed by quartz (32.6%), microcline (24.4%), and biotite (6.9%).

Discussion: pore structure effect for grain bulk modulus

Isolated pore characteristics

The grain bulk modulus is primarily governed by the mineral composition and concentration of its constituent phases. Additionally, the pore structure—particularly the presence of isolated pores—plays a crucial role in determining the extent of volumetric deformation under external confining pressure14,38,39. Therefore, variations in pore structure are also expected to significantly affect the grain bulk modulus.

To investigate this effect, X-ray CT was employed to acquire and analyze the three-dimensional micropore structure at high spatial resolution. This non-destructive imaging technique allows for the visualization of internal density variations within rock specimens, enabling the identification of pores, cracks, and mineral boundaries through high-resolution 3D images. It is particularly effective for analyzing pore networks and connectivity patterns of porous media40,41.

The pore connectivity of the rock specimens was evaluated through a three-step process involving X-ray CT imaging and pore structure analysis42: acquisition of digital rock images (tomograms), transformation into pore structures, and percolation analysis. For tomographic imaging, cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 5 mm and a height of 40 mm were prepared. The reconstructed images consisted of 1,000 × 1,000 × 1,750 voxels, corresponding to a voxel size of 2.68 μm. This imaging resolution was selected based on preliminary thin-section observations, taking into account the characteristic grain size and pore morphology of the specimens. X-ray CT scanning was performed using a multi-tube X-ray CT system (SEC, South Korea) equipped with three X-ray tubes (120 kV, 225 kV, and 320 kV) and a flat-panel detector. An exposure time of 1 s was applied for each projection, and 800 projections were acquired over a full rotation of 360° for tomographic reconstruction.

Next, the acquired tomograms were processed to transform the grayscale images into pore structures (Fig. 10). The X-ray CT images were expressed as 16-bit intensity values (ranging from 0 to 65,535), representing variations in X-ray attenuation. To convert the CT images into pore structures, binary segmentation (0 or 1) was applied using a single threshold value to distinguish pores (0) from grains (1). The threshold value was determined based on the pore shape and spatial distribution observed from thin-section analysis, ensuring consistent separation between pore and grain phases for each specimen type. This approach enables a clear and consistent classification of the pore–grain domain, which is required for subsequent connectivity analysis. In this study, the single threshold method was employed for CT image binarization to ensure a transparent and reproducible separation between pores and solid grains.

Example of an X-ray CT image of pore connectivity in a rock specimen. (a) X-ray CT scan, (b) 3D pore geometry.

Prior to segmentation, a Gaussian filter was applied to remove high-frequency noise, such as salt-and-pepper noise. The threshold values were determined based on pore shape and distribution observed from thin-section analysis, and were set to 2,093 for Berea sandstone and 2,110 for Idaho sandstone.

Finally, to differentiate connected and isolated pores, the percolation method was applied. This method evaluates the presence of continuous pathways within the segmented pore-grain domain by simulating diffusion across the pore space from one face of the specimen to the opposite face43. Pores forming continuous pathways from one face of the specimen to the opposite face were classified as connected pores, whereas pores that did not form such pathways were classified as isolated pores. To consider directional anisotropy, percolation analysis was separately conducted in the x-, y-, and z-directions. The final dataset comprised 27,350,000 pixels, with each pixel classified as either grain, connected pore, or isolated pore.

Various approaches can be used to extract pore structures from CT images and to characterize pore connectivity. Among them, instance segmentation44–47 and pore network modeling (PNM)48,49 treat pores as discrete objects by separating individual pore bodies and reconstructing their connections as a network of nodes and throats. This enables detailed characterization of pore size distribution, throat geometry, and network topology, and is particularly useful for three-dimensional reconstruction and pore-scale transport analyses.

However, such approaches focus on pore-scale network representation rather than on classifying pore space as a continuous phase. In this study, the objective is to distinguish connected and isolated pore fractions at the specimen scale and to evaluate their influence on the hydro-mechanical grain bulk modulus. Accordingly, pores and grains are classified at the voxel level using binary segmentation, and connectivity is evaluated through percolation analysis to determine whether the pore space forms continuous pathways across the specimen. This voxel-based connectivity framework directly quantifies the volumetric proportions of connected and isolated pores and is appropriate for the connectivity-based evaluation pursued.

We analyzed the influence of pore connectivity on grain bulk modulus using only Berea and Idaho sandstone specimens. Although X-ray CT imaging was initially conducted for the Hwangdeung granite, its extremely low porosity (< 1.0%) and the absence of a continuous pore network prevented a clear identification of connected or isolated pores. In addition, the presence of locally high-density mineral phases caused high-intensity noise in CT images, which limited the selection of optimal scanning conditions. This insufficient pore development prevented a clear separation between pore and grain phases in the digital segmentation process, indicating that a reliable connectivity and isolated-pore analysis was not feasible. Therefore, the granite sample was excluded from pore-structure evaluation in this study, as it was unsuitable for assessing the influence of isolated pores on grain bulk modulus.

The CT analysis results showed that the number of pore pixels in the Idaho sandstone was 9,018,914, which is approximately 55.9% higher than that of the Berea sandstone, which had 5,784,546 pixels (Table 4). Effective porosity, derived from connected pore analysis, was marginally greater in the Idaho sandstone (~ 99.9%) compared with that in the Berea sandstone (99.1%), indicating enhanced pore connectivity in the Idaho sample (Figs. 11 and 12). The number of isolated pore pixels in the Idaho sandstone was only 5,435, representing 9.9% of the 54,806 isolated pore pixels identified in the Berea sandstone. This difference in the distribution of isolated pores and pore connectivity can be observed in the red-highlighted regions in Figs. 13 and 14. These results suggest that the higher porosity observed in the Idaho sandstone is predominantly attributed to connected pores. Consequently, it can be concluded that in cases like the Idaho sandstone, where isolated pores are less developed, volumetric deformation under external pressure is relatively small, resulting in a higher grain bulk modulus.

In particular, isolated pores introduce internal free surfaces and discontinuities within the mineral grain matrix, leading to local stress concentrations around pore boundaries50. Under external loading, these regions undergo preferential deformation through localized volumetric deformation and grain-boundary sliding, thereby reducing the intrinsic grain stiffness (i.e., grain bulk modulus). Consequently, rocks with a higher proportion of isolated pores exhibit a lower grain bulk modulus even when total porosity remains similar.

Theoretically, porosity is influenced less by grain size than that by the geometry of the grain matrix and the degree of cementation between grains51,52. X-ray CT analysis revealed that Idaho sandstone featured a more lattice-like grain arrangement and stronger cementation between grains compared with that in Berea sandstone (Figs. 13 and 14). Consequently, fewer isolated pores were observed in the Idaho sandstone, which in turn reduced localized deformation under confining pressure and resulted in a higher grain bulk modulus.

The results indicate that, although porosity governs the hydro-mechanical behavior of rocks, its influence on the grain bulk modulus appears to be minimal. This finding implies that porosity has only a limited effect on the intrinsic volumetric deformation characteristics of rock grains. In contrast, the bulk modulus of the entire rock mass, which includes pore spaces, is expected to vary significantly with the total porosity.

Modified evaluation model for grain bulk modulus

The comparison between experimental and theoretical approaches revealed that theoretical models tend to overestimate the grain bulk modulus compared with that by experimental results due to the existence of isolated pore. This overestimation may reduce reliability and lead to design errors in practical engineering applications, including the deep geological disposal of radioactive waste, underground energy storage, and tunnel stability analysis.

From the experimental results in Fig. 9, it can be known that the experimentally measured grain bulk modulus values were approximately 69–76% of the theoretical predictions.

These results indicate that calculating the grain bulk modulus based on mineralogical composition and the mineral proportion tends to overestimate the actual values obtained through hydro-mechanical testing. This suggests that theoretical models often underestimate the true extent of volumetric deformation occurring in rock grains under confining pressure. One of the contributing factors to this overestimation is the presence of isolated pores lacking connectivity and the inclusion of reactive clay minerals within the rock matrix5,14. Reactive clay minerals, even in small amounts, may affect the volumetric response under unjacketed conditions due to the chemical response of clay interlayers or time-dependent deformation mechanisms. This additional grain-scale deformability can further contribute to the discrepancy between theoretically predicted and experimentally measured grain bulk modulus. Particularly, isolated pores contribute to additional deformation beyond that caused by the compressibility of individual mineral grains, resulting in a lower grain bulk modulus than that predicted by theoretical models. It should be noted that the X-ray CT analysis is intended to provide microstructural evidence supporting the proposed deformation mechanism, rather than to establish a direct quantitative relationship between isolated pore fraction and grain bulk modulus.

The discrepancy between experimental and theoretical evaluations may lead to differences in the Biot coefficient as defined by Eq. 3, introducing errors in numerical simulation results in the field of rock engineering. Therefore, it is essential to obtain accurate measurements of the grain bulk modulus for the target rock through experimental approaches. Despite its reliability, the unjacketed test is often impractical for all rock types due to limitations related to sample availability, equipment access, time, and cost. Currently, as no standardized method exists for performing the unjacketed test, the results presented here are considered particularly significant. Additionally, we quantify the discrepancy between experimental and theoretical values, suggesting correction factors are introduced to establish a more accurate and practically applicable evaluation model. Although the number of samples analyzed in this study is somewhat limited, the proposed approach is significant in that it presents and experimentally based methodology for evaluating the grain bulk modulus. The primary objective of this study is not to establish a generally applicable correction model, but rather to demonstrate that isolated and unconnected pore structures can systematically bias experimentally measured grain bulk modulus and to propose an empirical correction framework based on experimental measurements. Considering this context, future research will aim to extend this model to various rock types, confining pressure conditions, and environmental parameters to enhance its reliability and applicability.

Conclusion

We investigated the influence of pore structure characteristics on the grain bulk modulus of rocks using both experimental and theoretical approaches. Three rock types—Berea sandstone, Idaho sandstone, and Hwangdeung granite—were selected to represent varying mineralogical and pore structure conditions. Grain bulk modulus was evaluated through unjacketed tests and theoretical calculations using the VRH averaging method based on mineral composition. Additionally, X-ray CT analysis was performed to characterize the effects of pore geometry, particularly the role of isolated pores. The main findings are summarized as follows:

Unjacketed tests revealed a linear increase in volumetric strain with confining pressure. Hwangdeung granite showed the highest grain bulk modulus, followed by Idaho and Berea sandstones. These differences were not explained by conventional mechanical properties such as UCS or elastic modulus. Instead, mineral composition—particularly the presence of albite and biotite, which possess high intrinsic bulk moduli—had a more significant influence.
