Postmastectomy Radiation Therapy on Flap Outcomes in Breast Reconstruction: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Fatima Alameri, Anne-Sophie Reguesse, Pierre Tawa, Philippe Levan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Breast reconstruction is an important aspect of the process of recovery for patients recovering from mastectomy. However, postmastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT) disrupts flap perfusion and cosmetic results. These complications have prompted comparisons of irradiated and nonirradiated flaps, especially in immediate and delayed reconstruction.Methods:Using preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses guidelines, a PubMed and Cochrane Library search from the year 2000 to 2024 was performed to compare the irradiated and nonirradiated flaps. The inclusion criteria restricted the analysis to comparing flap viability, flap complications, aesthetic outcome, or satisfaction level of patients. The meta-analysis was used, and variance was reduced by using fixed- and random-effects models.Results:The analysis involved 12 studies showing that flaps receiving irradiation, particularly in immediate reconstructions, had higher complication rates, including fat necrosis and flap contracture. Nevertheless, delayed reconstruction following PMRT was linked to reduced postoperative complication rates and favorable reconstructive results, whereas flap survival did not differ between immediate and delayed reconstruction. Subgroup analysis demonstrated lower fat necrosis in deep inferior epigastric perforator flaps (≈12%) compared with transverse rectus abdominis muscle (≈25%) and latissimus dorsi flaps (≈40%), suggesting that flap-specific planning is necessary for irradiated patients. These pooled percentages reflect averaged values across included studies.Conclusions:Patients undergoing immediate reconstruction in the PMRT setting have increased rates of postoperative complications, especially fat necrosis, fibrosis, and flap contracture, and worse aesthetic outcomes. On the contrary, delayed reconstruction is associated with lower complications and better cosmetic results, whereas no significant difference is noted in flap survival between immediate and delayed reconstruction.

Takeaways

Question: What are the outcomes of flaps in immediate versus delayed breast reconstruction after postmastectomy radiation therapy?

Findings: Irradiated flaps are associated with increased complications and poorer aesthetic outcomes in immediate reconstruction. However, delaying reconstruction until after postmastectomy radiation therapy seems to reduce these risks and improve long-term results.

Meaning: Preliminary findings suggest that delayed reconstruction may provide significant advantages, emphasizing the importance of tailoring treatment plans to each patient’s unique needs to achieve optimal outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Physical and emotional rehabilitation cannot be complete without postmastectomy breast assessment. It can recontour and redefine the form and image of the postoperative breasts, helping patients regain self-confidence.1 Patients can opt to undergo implant-based reconstruction or autologous reconstruction with the use of flaps, which can include transverse rectus abdominis muscle (TRAM), deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP), or latissimus dorsi flaps.2

When to reconstruct is entirely a matter of patient preference, stage of disease, comorbidities, and the application of adjuvant therapy such as radiation.3 High-risk patients will require radiation to reduce recurrence in the chest wall or the remaining breast tissues.4 However, radiation can cause fibrosis, reduction of tissue pliability, and dysfunctional blood circulation that have negative survival and aesthetic outcomes, especially when using flap arrangements, where perfusion is most critical.5 Despite the aesthetic and psychological benefits of early reconstruction, exposure of healing or maturing tissue to radiation poses the risk of flap losses and poor cosmetic outcomes.6

Reconstruction outcomes are reduced in the short term because radiation impairs wound healing via inflammatory and microvascular injuries.7 Conversely, the long-term outcomes of postmastectomy radiation treatment are less predictable and may include fibrosis, fat necrosis, and aesthetic impairment. These outcomes depend on the timing of reconstruction, type of flap, and patient-specific considerations, rather than a uniform worsening of outcomes.8 When postmastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT) is administered after reconstruction, the reconstructed breast tissue may be exposed to radiation-related changes that variably affect flap viability and aesthetic outcomes over time, depending upon flap type and reconstruction timing.9 The potential to increase the survival rate of flaps and overall prognosis must be balanced against the fact that more complex surgery practices may be required due to the impact of radiation on tissue.10

These challenges prompt the necessity to carefully consider the construction timeframe and the effects of radiation therapy (RT) on the feasibility of flaps and patient satisfaction.11 Surgeons should consider the timing of reconstruction and the possible effects of radiation on flap stability and outcomes.12 The data obtained will be subjected to a systematic review and meta-analysis according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 requirements.13

RATIONALE

The rising cases of the unfavorable effects of RT on both tissue integrity and various outcomes of irradiated and nonirradiated flaps in both immediate and delayed reconstructions make it necessary to test the effects of radiation on the vitality of the flaps and outcomes of patients undergoing breast reconstruction procedures. The results of the study are very important for future clinical decision-making and may ultimately improve patient satisfaction.

OBJECTIVES

This systematic review and meta-analysis presents a conclusive analysis of the effects of irradiated versus nonirradiated flaps in patients undergoing immediate or delayed breast reconstruction. The findings from this study may help inform clinical decision-making and may enhance the quality of care and patient outcomes among individuals diagnosed with breast cancer who undergo reconstruction.

METHODOLOGY

Eligibility Criteria

This review included randomized controlled trials, prospective cohorts, and retrospective comparative clinical studies evaluating autologous or implant-based breast reconstruction with and without postmastectomy radiation. Narrative reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, and studies without primary comparative data were excluded. To avoid duplication, no secondary review articles were included as data sources. Only original clinical studies providing primary patient outcomes were analyzed. Review articles were used solely for contextual discussion.

The inclusion criteria were clinical trials comparing the outcomes of irradiated and nonirradiated flaps in which specific details on the type of flaps were provided (eg, TRAM, DIEP, latissimus dorsi), as well as the timing of reconstruction. The timing of radiation is significant because radiation provided before free flap surgery or after free flap surgery may produce a significant effect on flap viability.

Key parameters such as flap survival, complication rates, aesthetic outcomes, and patient satisfaction were considered primary outcomes. In cases where standardized tools were available, such as the widely used BREAST-Q in the breast reconstruction literature for measuring patient-reported outcomes related to satisfaction with reconstruction and appearance, these tools were applied. Studies included were only those in which sufficient details were provided to allow meaningful comparison of irradiated and nonirradiated flaps.

Animal and in vitro studies were excluded because the purpose of the review was to provide a clinical evaluation of outcomes in humans. Other reports or case series that included fewer than 10 patients were also rejected because such data are unlikely to be generalizable. The studies that did not report outcomes of the irradiated and nonirradiated flaps clearly and/or to an adequate extent were excluded, as well as those in which it was not specified whether reconstruction was immediate or delayed. Additionally, studies that are published in languages other than English were also excluded. The aim of such systematic review and meta-analysis is to provide an overall and objective interpretation of the results of irradiated versus nonirradiated flaps in patients undergoing any form of breast reconstruction with PMRT. Therefore, this review will make a significant contribution by providing evidence-based conclusions to guide clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.

Search Strategy

There was no limitation to the search strategy because PubMed and Cochrane Library were used to be more inclusive. The following search string was used, with the date of publication between 2000 and 2024: (“breast reconstruction” AND “radiation therapy”) AND (“immediate reconstruction” OR “delayed reconstruction”) AND (“irradiated flaps” OR “nonirradiated flaps”). In addition to the database search, references of the included studies were also screened to identify any relevant articles that may have been missed during the initial search. The overall search of PubMed and Cochrane Library revealed 39 records (PubMed, n = 17; Cochrane Library, n = 22). Following the deletion of a duplicate, 38 distinct records remained and were screened by title and abstract. Among them, 21 were eliminated because they were not relevant to the topic, did not compare irradiated with nonirradiated flaps, or did not use postmastectomy autologous or implant-based reconstruction. Eligibility assessment was performed on 17 full-text articles. Five full-text articles were excluded, as they were narrative reviews or other nonprimary studies, involved only oncoplastic lumpectomy or partial breast reconstruction, included case series with fewer than 10 patients, did not have any comparative data between irradiated and nonirradiated, or did not specify the timing of radiation. The other 12 articles were found to be within all the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the quantitative meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Selection and Data Collection

A comprehensive search of PubMed and Cochrane Library databases was conducted. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The search results used Boolean operators such as AND/OR, and the most relevant search results were retrieved by applying appropriate filters on PubMed and Cochrane database.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data necessary for the systematic review and meta-analysis were extracted for irradiated and nonirradiated flaps in women undergoing immediate or delayed postmastectomy reconstruction. Outcomes were evaluated using valid instruments where available, including the BREAST-Q. The review differentiated between the RT types: conventional and hypofractionated, as well as intensity modulated radiation, when specified in the studies. This stratification was necessary to determine the impact of radiation type on early and late reconstruction outcomes. Fat necrosis was reported to be determined by clinical assessment, mammography, and magnetic resonance imaging. Some studies reported instances of fat necrosis that were clinically relevant and required treatment, whereas other studies reported radiographic alterations without symptoms. Owing to this heterogeneity, caution must be exercised when comparing fat necrosis rates, because imaging-proven fat necrosis does not always translate into clinical morbidity.

The variables of intervention that were extracted were the type of flap applied: DIEP, TRAM, or latissimus dorsi; reconstruction parameters: immediate or delayed reconstruction; and RT parameters: time after surgery, total dose, and fractionation. Postoperative cases also recorded the time interval between surgery and the start of radiation to determine its effect on the frequency of complications and final outcomes. Standardized extraction sheets were used following PRISMA 2020 guidance.

This study involved a meta-analysis to compare irradiated and nonirradiated flaps in immediate or delayed reconstruction following PMRT. A meta-analysis was performed using information from various studies on the significant outcomes, such as flap survival rates; complications, including infection, necrosis, and wound dehiscence; and cosmetic outcomes. The effect size summary was calculated, along with the version of the forest plot that provided a summary of the results of individual studies and the overall effect size between irradiated and nonirradiated flaps, conducted with the help of RevMan software. Fixed- and random-effects models were applied to address potential heterogeneity of the included studies, following established meta-analytic methodology14

Risk of Bias and Certainty Assessment

The random-effect and fixed-effect models were used to estimate study outcomes. The primary outcomes of the study were measured on the basis of risk ratio, while data synthesis was conducted through meta-analysis, forest plot, and confidence intervals (CIs) to assess heterogeneity across the included studies in the review.

RESULTS

Characteristics of Studies Included

The characteristics of the included 12 studies are shown in Table 1. The study designs were mainly cohort studies and randomized controlled trials. The total sample size across these studies was 2255, and the total number of flaps analyzed in the immediate reconstruction group was 1123, whereas the number of flaps for the delayed reconstruction group was 1127. The types of flaps examined included DIEP, TRAM, latissimus dorsi, and profunda artery perforator flaps. There were different protocols for RT across the studies, with some focusing on preoperative radiation and others assessing postoperative or adjuvant radiotherapy. Overall, in most of the studies, flap survival rates were quite high, even though irradiated flaps indicated higher complication rates related to fat necrosis and flap contracture. For example, Clarke-Pearson et al15 demonstrated that irradiated DIEP flaps exhibited higher rates of fat necrosis and minor wound complications compared with nonirradiated controls, aligning with our pooled analysis findings. In immediate reconstruction, cosmetic results were poorer, and the rate of complications was greater.

Meta-analysis

Data from 12 studies in Table 1 with irradiated and nonirradiated flaps for breast reconstruction following mastectomy were synthesized using meta-analysis, as shown in Figure 2. The selected studies were different in terms of methods and populations and evaluated flap viability, complications, and aesthetics. According to Figure 2, the pooled effect size was 1.31 (95% CI 1.26–1.36) for irradiated versus nonirradiated flaps. The results indicated that irradiated flaps are significantly more complicated due to fat necrosis, flap contracture, and aesthetic compromise compared with flaps with no irradiation. Most of the conducted studies found higher complication rates in irradiated flaps, as shown in Figure 2. The consistency of results across different study designs and patient demographics strengthens the reliability of the findings. The use of fixed-effect and random-effect models accounted for possible heterogeneity to ensure the robustness of the pooled estimates. Overall, irradiated flaps—particularly in immediate reconstruction cases—were more vulnerable to complications due to impaired perfusion and delayed healing.

Biases and Certainty of Evidence

Confidence in the studies was maintained through consistent outcome reporting. The overall certainty of the studies is considered strong due to the pooled effect sizes of 1.31 and 1.22 in immediate and delayed reconstruction, respectively, indicating the reliability of the studies used, as shown in Figures 3 and 4. Potential confounders of complication rates and aesthetic outcomes were noted to include patient-specific factors such as demographics, body mass index, and smoking status. Irradiated flaps often provide poorer aesthetic outcomes; however, these individual differences must be taken into account when interpreting findings. Moreover, selection of the flap types and radiation protocols may introduce bias, which was managed through statistical approaches.

DISCUSSION

The purpose of the review is to discuss the influence of PMRT on breast reconstruction outcomes. In all evaluated articles, patients who underwent immediate reconstruction during PMRT exhibited worse complication rates, including fat necrosis, fibrosis, infection, and tissue hardening, compared with the nonradiation control group. Indicatively, Ly et al21 reported higher rates of fibrosis and multiple postoperative complications in irradiated free flaps compared with nonirradiated flaps, consistent with our pooled estimates. This supports the detrimental effects of radiation on tissue perfusion, healing, and vascular integrity.23 Conversely, delayed reconstruction, especially using DIEP flaps, was more consistently associated with improved complication profiles and better aesthetic outcomes.17,18 The evidence suggests that there are higher chances of good results after reconstruction following an interval of tissue recovery and rest after radiation. Finally, the delayed–immediate approaches, which entail the implanting of tissue expanders before definitive surgery after PMRT, could provide the psychological benefits of early reconstruction with insignificant hazards of radiation-related complications.24 Despite the psychological advantages of early reconstruction, the irradiated flaps tend to show fibrosis and reduced elasticity.19 On the other hand, delayed reconstruction allows avoidance of irradiating newly transferred tissue, thereby improving the chances of flap survival and resulting in better cosmetic satisfaction among patients.25 These findings therefore imply that patient-centered planning is essential. Patient choice, tumor extent, and the likelihood of requiring PMRT are other issues that need to be considered.

The literature assessing the impact of PMRT on flap-based breast reconstruction has several gaps. Most of the published studies are retrospective and heterogeneous in terms of timing of reconstruction, type of flap, and radiation sequencing, which limits the comparability and strength of inference.25 Radiation-related variables such as dose, fractionation, and timing in relation to reconstruction are not reported consistently; yet, there is evidence that these variables affect reconstructive outcomes and surgical decision-making.3,12 The long-term results are not fully described because most of the research focuses on the early postoperative complications, whereas fewer studies analyse aesthetic stability and patient-reported satisfaction during the survivorship period.10,11 In addition, risk modifiers at the patient level, including body mass index, smoking status, and comorbidities, are reported inconsistently, which restricts the ability to adjust comorbid factors that are known to affect complication rates.21 Prospective study designs, in which reconstruction timing, radiation parameters, and validated patient-reported outcome measures are standardized and reported, are recommended for future research. These are expected to be in line with PRISMA 2020 methodological guidelines, to be more informative on the optimal reconstructive strategy in patients needing PMRT.13

LIMITATIONS

There are limitations in this review. The studies were small, and the follow-up was short-term in some cases. Additionally, variations in surgery techniques, flap types, and radiation treatment may have caused discrepancies in outcome. Future research requires larger populations and patient-reported outcomes. It is, however, interesting to note that some studies did not find it necessary to report some of the major risk factors, such as body mass index and smoking status, which are known to influence complications and outcomes in breast reconstruction. These risk factors should be considered in future research to improve validity. Future studies should focus on standardizing findings in larger populations and including diverse patient groups to better evaluate long-term outcomes of irradiated versus nonirradiated flaps. Additional studies may aid in examining the outcomes of progressive radiation-based procedures and reconstruction therapies on patients and related complications. Although the time interval between completion of radiation and delayed reconstruction was noted in several studies, inconsistent reporting prevented pooled quantitative analysis. Future research should standardize reporting of this variable, as longer recovery intervals may potentially mitigate radiation-related complications.

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review and meta-analysis show that there is no evidence that immediate breast reconstruction is better than delayed breast reconstruction in patients undergoing PMRT; nevertheless, immediate breast reconstruction is associated with a higher incidence of postoperative complications, the majority of which are fat necrosis and fibrosis, and unfavorable aesthetic outcomes. Although delayed reconstruction does not necessarily improve flap survival, it is associated with fewer complications and better cosmetic outcomes. These results highlight the significance of the timing of reconstruction in reducing the morbidity of radiation as opposed to flap viability. In the included studies, poorer reconstructive outcomes were more commonly reported in patients who underwent immediate reconstruction with exposure to PMRT and in those with identifiable risk factors, including high body mass index, current smoking status, and medical comorbidities. These were associated with higher rates of complications, especially fat necrosis and morbidity of the wound. The timing of reconstruction and the use of patient-specific risk profiles should therefore be considered to maximize outcomes in patients who are expected to require PMRT.
