Quality of maternal and child health services in Tanzanian primary healthcare: a 2021–2022 star rating review
Authors: Erick S Kinyenje, Martin M Degeh, Joseph C Hokororo, Syabo M Mwaisengela, Ruth R Ngowi, Omary A Nassoro, Chrisogone J German, Radenta P Bahegwa, Yohanes S Msigwa, Atuganile Musyani, Laura E Marandu, Michael Habtu, Jennifer T Mbise, Habibu Ismail, Janeth Masuma, Faraja Msemwa, Joanita Muruve, Saturini Manangwa, Daudi Msasi, Jimmy Mbelwa, Athman Pembe, Ahmad M Makuwani, Ntuli A Kapologwe, Tumaini J Nagu, Eliudi S Eliakimu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTanzania faces high maternal and infant mortality rates, yet key service quality metrics remain underexamined, limiting targeted improvements. Star Rating Assessment (SRA), introduced in 2015, aims to improve primary health care (PHC) quality, including maternal and child health (MCH) services. This study analyses SRA data to assess the prevalence of PHC quality in MCH and its associated factors.MethodsData for this cross-sectional study were collected from November 2021 to March 2022 across 10 regions. The tool used included 12 MCH-related indicators, each comprising specific criteria. Facilities scoring ≥ 80% across these criteria were considered to meet MCH quality standards, which was the primary study outcome. A descriptive analysis of indicator performance was conducted, followed by multivariate logistic regression to assess characteristics associated with achieving the quality standard. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 2,583 facilities assessed: 2,189 dispensaries (84.7%), 303 health centres (11.7%), and 91 district-level hospitals (3.5%). Only 280(10.8%) met the MCH quality standards. Maternal audit and review practices aligned with guidelines in 2,057 facilities (79.6%). Moderate adherence was observed for family planning guidelines (48.3%), accurate partogram completion (47.9%), and immunisation standards (46.3%). Major gaps included cervical cancer screening, offered by 199 facilities (7.7%), and essential obstetric medicines, available in 424 facilities (16.4%). Among 394 hospitals and health centres, only 60(15.2%) offered postnatal and essential newborn care with dedicated neonatal and Kangaroo Mother Care spaces. Compliance was higher in public, rural, and higher-level facilities. Nurse availability, functional management, and quality improvement teams were key predictors of MCH quality.ConclusionThe quality of MCH services in Tanzania varies widely by facility type, ownership, nurse staffing, and management functionality. Targeted improvements in cervical cancer screening, emergency obstetric care, and newborn care are essential to raise overall MCH service standards.Keywords: Maternal and child health, Star rating Assessment, Primary healthcare, Quality of MCH services, Tanzania

Introduction

Maternal and child health (MCH) is one of the key elements of public health and an essential indicator of healthcare system performance [1–3]. Despite a decrease in maternal and under-five death rates, Tanzania and many other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) still struggle to deliver high-quality MCH services at the primary healthcare (PHC) level [4].

There is increasing evidence that improved health outcomes for maternal and child healthcare are mostly influenced by the quality of care, not just accessibility [5]. In this sense, accountability, mechanisms for continuous improvement, and the systematic monitoring of service quality are necessary to achieve national and international goals such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [1–3].

The quality of MCH services is a crucial factor in attaining both Universal Healthcare (UHC) and SDGs targets on maternal, neonatal and under-5 mortality [1–3]. The quality of MCH services is widely recognised as a measure of effective PHC [1, 6, 7]; the backbone of UHC [2, 8–11]. Many countries, including Tanzania, have established PHC infrastructure to achieve UHC [12]. However, the inadequate quality of services at PHC levels often leads pregnant mothers to bypass these facilities and go to referral hospitals. This results in congestion at referral hospitals, further compromising the quality of services at that level [13].

As of 2020, approximately 800 women worldwide succumb to childbirth-related causes each day [14]. Despite making just 13% of the global population, 42% of maternal mortality occur in least developed countries (LDCs), the majority of which are in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [14]. Similarly, a systematic study conducted in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) revealed that by enhancing the quality of care, 60% of all deaths, 50% of all maternal deaths and 1 million newborn deaths could have been avoided by 2018 [5, 15].

Both structural and process-related obstacles to efficient MCH service delivery have been identified by earlier research conducted in Tanzania and in SSA [16]. Studies have also shown ongoing shortcomings in patient-provider communication, provider competency, and the availability of the necessary supplies, particularly in rural areas [17, 18]. Disparities in maternal and newborn outcomes has also been found to be correlated with differences in service quality at the facility level. For example, despite the wide availability of antenatal services and institutional births in Tanzania, their quality frequently varies, which limits their ability to reduce preventable morbidity and mortality [19, 20].

The quality of maternal health before conception significantly influences pregnancy outcomes [15]. Family planning services during this period support adequate child spacing, which reduces the risk of infant and maternal mortality. Pre-conception also presents an opportunity for screening conditions like cervical cancer that may affect pregnancy [15]. Once pregnant, a mother’s health outcomes depend on receiving quality antenatal care (ANC) in line with national guidelines. Facilities must follow ANC protocols, including Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), checklists, and job aids, while ensuring the availability of essential medicines, supplies, skilled personnel, and a supportive environment.

Complications during childbirth often stem from inadequate emergency obstetric and neonatal care (EmONC). These services, including key signal functions and the proper use of partograms, are critical in preventing maternal and neonatal deaths. Pregnant women may also experience life-threatening conditions like postpartum hemorrhage and pre-eclampsia, which require trained providers and proper clinical tools. The first 28 days after delivery are particularly vulnerable, with many maternal and infant deaths linked to infections and lack of essential care. PHC facilities must implement national guidelines on postnatal and newborn care, including services like Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) [21]. In cases of maternal or neonatal death, conducting the Maternal and Perinatal Death Surveillance and Response (MPDSR) audits helps identify gaps, recommend corrective actions, and improve overall MCH services.

Research in context

Low-quality MCH services are thought to be a bigger challenge than it is estimated because of inadequate statistics in resource-limited countries. Tanzania, as one of these countries, needs more statistics to describe the extent of the difficulties in the quality of MCH services in the areas mentioned above. Word Bank estimates that “238 women die per 100,000 live births due to pregnancy-related causes in Tanzania” by 2020 [22]. Limited studies show nearly 90% of facility-level neonatal deaths occurred in the first week of life by 2015 [23]. However, these statistics also do not indicate which specific areas should be emphasized to improve the quality of MCH services.

Among other initiatives, Tanzania developed a list of MCH indicators to assess and identify areas needing improvement in MCH services. These indicators were formulated considering the aforementioned processes and international standards. They were used to conduct assessments and, rate facilities based on service quality. The MCH indicators were part of a broader set of indicators used to measure the quality of service delivery in PHC facilities under a nationwide quality initiative called “Star Rating Assessment” (SRA). SRA evaluates the quality of the facility across 12 service areas simultaneously [24]. Area 11 focuses on clinical services, encompassing 12 indicators of MCH services. Area 2 assesses staff availability at the facility, while Area 4, among other considerations, evaluates the performance of facility management and quality improvement teams. Both areas 2 and 4 contribute to the performance of other services including MCH services [25]. There is a significant correlation between the quality of MCH services and the adequate performance of management and quality teams at the facility. However, such studies are limited in SSA countries.

The SRA was one of the initiatives under the Big Result Now program, which was established in 2015 to improve facility performance by enhancing management effectiveness and the quality of health services [25–27]. SRA is a quality improvement initiative aimed at assessing, rating and developing quality improvement plans that are implemented to close a quality gap in each primary health facility. The SRA method assesses facilities according to patient-centred care dimensions, service delivery procedures, and structural preparedness.

The objective of this study was to ascertain the prevalence of PHC facilities achieving the recommended status in the quality of MCH services, as well as the performance of MCH indicators and predictors of MCH service quality. The results are intended to drive specific quality improvement measures at the operational and policy levels as well as to feed continuing health system reforms.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study that utilised data collected between November 2021 and March 2022 in 10 out of the 26 regions in the country.

Study setting

The United Republic of Tanzania is a union of two countries, Tanganyika and Zanzibar. After their union in 1964, the area of Tanganyika became known as Tanzania Mainland and covered 945,500 square kilometres [28]. This area is divided into 8 zones, which contain 26 administrative regions.

According to the National Census 2022, the total population in these 10 regions is about 30% (17,554,018) of the entire country’s population (59,517,754) [29].

Study population

This study includes all PHC facilities in the 10 target regions. These10 regions were purposively samped to ensure each of the 8 zones is represented in this research. Logistics challenges led to one region not being reached, resulting in one zone being unrepresented. However, another region with similar characteristics was substituted in the assessment.

In Tanzania, healthcare facilities are divided into the following two main categories: PHC facilities and referral healthcare facilities. PHC facilities account for more than 90% of all healthcare facilities available in the country. Within each region, PHC facilities range from dispensaries and health centres to hospitals at level 1 (council level), in increasing order of expertise [30, 31]. Dispensaries serve populations of about 10,000 and provide outpatient services exclusively that encompass health promotion, preventive, curative, palliative, and rehabilitative services, all adapted to the needs of the communities and based on public health concerns [32]. Health centres act as referral points for dispensaries, offering a broader range of services, in addition to services provided at the dispensary, health centres provide inpatient care and comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care (CEmONC), for populations of approximately 50,000 people [31, 32]. Hospitals at level 1, serve around 200,000-500,000 people, function as referral centres for the lower-level facilities [30, 33] and can provide all basic healthcare services, including medical, paediatric and child health, obstetrics and gynaecology, dental and surgical services [30].

Additional facility characteristics, such as location (urban or rural) and ownership status (private or public), can significantly impact access, management, and service quality. Inclusion and exclusion criteria: This study includes all PHC facilities that completed the SRA conducted in 2021/22. The SRA aimed to include all PHC facilities; however, those found to be non-functional, e.g., closed by local authorities were excluded. We aimed to exclude facilities with no MCH data; however, no facility was excluded, as data were actively collected from all facilities, with quality assurance measures implemented to ensure accurate data entry and reliable storage in the database.

MCH indicators and data management

The data used in this study are stored in the SRA database, part of the DHIS2 platform managed by the Ministry of Health. In collaboration with the University of Dar es Salaam (DHIS2 Project), the Ministry developed the SRA mobile app, which was used for data collection during the assessment. Data collectors from all levels of the health system in Tanzania were gathered and given one week of training on accurate data collection methods, verification, and analysis. At each facility, the team of assessors included representatives from the council, regional, and national levels to eliminate the possibility of unfair assessment. The collected data underwent quality assessment and were directly entered into DHIS2.

The tool used to collect this data was divided into 12 quality assessment areas. Area 11 is called “clinical services” and covers various sub-areas, including MCH. The performance of MCH at each healthcare facility was measured using 12 indicators, as described in Table 1.

Management of study variables and statistical analysis

The dependent variable of this study was “quality MCH services”. Each Facility was required to score ≥ 80% points among all allocated indicator score points to meet the quality service criteria for MCH. This cut-off point is provided in the National Guidelines for Recognition of Implementation Status of Quality Improvement Initiatives in Health Facilities [34]. For description purposes, we calculated the proportion of facilities that met the quality service criteria for MCH. We also calculated the proportion of facilities that achieved each indicator.

Furthermore, we described facility-related variables that could influence the performance of MCH indicators. These variables include the location (urban or rural), ownership (public or private), and facility level (hospital/health centre/dispensary). Additionally, we considered other facility variables such as the functionality of the HFMT, the QIT, and the availability of adequate staffing levels for nurses, clinicians, and all healthcare providers.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the characteristics of PHC facilities associated with this outcome, and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

Results

Description of study participants

The study included 2,583 healthcare facilities: 2,189 dispensaries (84.7%), 303 health centres (11.7%), and 91 hospitals (3.5%). Nearly four-fifths (2,068, 80.1%) were publicly owned- the rest were private facilities, and approximately the same proportion were based in rural areas (2,139, 82.8%) - with the remaining based in urban areas.

Performance of healthcare facilities in MCH indicators

Approximately one out of ten (10.8%) of the 280 facilities met the quality assessment scores for MCH services. Considering individual indicators (Fig. 2), the most notable achievement was that 2,057 facilities (79.6%) successfully implemented maternal audits and reviews following the MPDSR guidelines.

The performance of the healthcare facilities in individual MCH indicators

Excluding the availability of essential checklists, charts, and protocols for integrated management in pregnancy and childcare in 1,467 facilities (56.8%), the remaining indicators were met by less than half of the facilities as follows: 1,247 facilities (48.3%) followed family planning guidelines, 1,238 facilities (47.9%) accurately completed partograms, immunization services adhered to guidelines in 1,197 facilities (46.3%), and 1,035 facilities (40.1%) followed ANC service guidelines. Additionally, staff received BEmONC or CEmONC training in 995 facilities (38.5%), BEmONC or CEmONC services were provided in 816 facilities (31.6%), and quality child growth monitoring, as per IMCI, was offered in 1,054 facilities (40.8%).

Three indicators scored the lowest among others: provision of PNC and essential newborn care (including the utilization of neonatal wards and KMC rooms) in 60 hospitals/health centres (15.2%), cervical cancer screening in 199 facilities (7.7%), and the availability of required medicines and medical supplies for obstetric emergencies in 424 facilities (16.4%).

Facility’s characteristics that predicted quality of MCH services

As presented in Table 2; findings show public facilities were about three times likely to provide quality MCH services compared to private ones (AOR = 2.94, 95% CI 1.80, 5.05). Facilities also were likely to provide quality service in order of increasing level (dispensaries being reference; health centres [AOR = 7.21], hospital [AOR = 34.70). Facilities with nurses according to staffing standards were twice likely to have quality services (AOR = 2.12, 95% CI 1.34, 3.53). Functional management structure at facility level were associated with quality services; as both functional Health Facility Management Teams (HFMT) and Quality Improvement Teams (QITs) were about twice likely to improve quality of MCH [HFMT (AOR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.15, 2.27), and QITs (AOR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.20, 82.43)]. There was no significant difference whether the facility was based on urban or rural areas. Furthermore, presence of clinicians at facility according to staffing standards did not predict quality of MCH, and this was also true for the overall number of staff in the facility.

Discussion

Poor quality of MCH services is a major driver of maternal and child mortality [2]. In this study, we used internationally adopted but nationally approved tools to measure the quality of MCH services in PHC facilities, assessing the availability of services, and their quality using standardized indicators, and key predictors. Unlike the previous studies [35, 36], the current research has examined the state of quality MCH services in PHC using 12 detailed indicators. Our findings reveal a significant gap between actual facility performance and established recommendations, which we present under several thematic areas.

One major theme is inadequate adherence to policies and guidelines related to the quality of antenatal and intrapartum care, which significantly contribute to poor MCH outcomes [37]. This is often reflected in increased facility-based perinatal death rates in developing countries [23]. In these countries, guidelines may exist at the national level, but providers are unaware of their existence [38]. This challenge was also observed in our study, where adherence to ANC, IMCI, immunisation, family planning guidelines, and even completion of partographs was less than 50% in PHC facilities regardless of their availability at the national level. Poor adherence to these essential care standards compromises timely identification and management of complications during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period, leading to preventable deaths and adverse health outcomes for both mothers and infants [39].

The above findings are consistent with findings in other countries in the SSA region [36, 40–43]. Other studies in SSA have found higher utilization of checklists and monitoring tools such as partographs. However, the use was not gauged against the quality of services provided [44]. In our case, we ensured that both the use and quality of data were measured to establish the performance of each facility in filling out the partographs. This contrasts with developed countries, whereby improved quality of MCH services is influenced by digital health interventions, data-driven decisions and health education [45].

Several factors contribute to poor guideline adherence. These include lack of access to the relevant guidelines [38, 44], the perception that the patient lacks risk indicators - making the guidelines appear unnecessary [46], high workload, inadequate management support, limited knowledge, and negative attitude [41]. Further studies indicate that neither experience, the education level of the healthcare worker (HCWs), nor the facility level where HCWs work significantly adherence to guidelines [42]. A previous study at the National Hospital in Tanzania showed that only 14% of partographs were properly completed to monitor uterine contractions [47]. Onsite mentorship, coaching, supervision, staff technical competence, and enhanced availability and accessibility are key factors in improving adherence to guidelines [44, 48]. This suggests that developing countries require robust health systems—similar to those established in developed countries—to holistically improve the quality of services [2, 21].

Training and onsite clinical mentorship lead to significant positive changes in the performance of MCH services [49]. Training in MCH services enhances confidence among HCWs attending to clients. As a result, they are likely to suggest or offer appropriate services needed by their clients [50]. Yet, only 38.5% of the facilities we surveyed had staff trained in either Basic or Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (BEmONC or CEmONC), and just 31.6% provided those services- a difference possibly explained by lack of equipment. EmONC services are a set of life-saving interventions provided to manage complications during pregnancy, childbirth, and the immediate postnatal period. Studies recommend that greater emphasis be placed on workforce development and capacity building for HCWs who provide MCH services to address the underlying causes of health disparities affecting MCH populations [48, 51]. Furthermore, many maternal deaths can be prevented if a woman receives care from skilled health personnel [48, 52].

A second major challenge is the inadequate provision of post-natal and essential newborn care services. This was largely due to the lack of neonatal wards and KMC rooms in hospitals and health centres. The presence of well-equipped neonatal wards and KMC rooms can reduce neonatal mortality by 40% in healthcare facilities [53, 54]. KMC is the most effective intervention for preventing death in underweight infants weighing 2000 g or less at birth [55, 56]. Most deaths among these result from healthcare-associated infections, largely preventable through quality KMC services [57]. Functional neonatal wards and KMC rooms encompass dedicated spaces for KMC, sufficient beds, ample chairs for mothers, measures to ensure privacy and prevent overcrowding, availability of hats for newborns and cloth wrappers for KMC, and functional weighing scales or other monitoring devices [37]. Despite the decreasing under-five mortality rates in SSA countries over the past decade, the neonatal mortality rate has not significantly decreased, primarily due to inadequate infrastructure to prevent infections in healthcare facilities [57]. This underscores the need for health authorities, including those in Tanzania, to focus on managing these critical interventions.

Another finding of concern is that less than 10% of PHC Facilities were providing cancer screening services for women, making this the most MCH-challenging area. Cancer screening is one step towards protecting women from cervical cancer [58–60]. Cervical Cancer is the fourth most common cancer globally, accounting for 266,000 deaths annually as of 2012, with the majority (85–87%) occurring in less developed countries [59–61]. It is the leading cause of cancer deaths in women [62]. East Africa, where Tanzania is located, is the highest-risk region for cervical cancer among less developed countries [61]. Therefore, preventing the disease through expanded cervical cancer screening services is crucial as part of universal health care and attain the “90–70–90 targets by 2030” for its elimination as a public health problem as set out in the WHO strategy of 2020 [63]. One of the major challenges is that cervical cancer often shows no symptoms before it becomes advanced [64].

In contrast, MPDSR adherence emerged as the most implemented MCH quality indicator, as seen in other countries in SSA [65]. However, translating audit findings into action remains limited [65]. This requires functional management teams at facilities and quality improvement teams. Our research showed that only 560 facilities (24%) of 2358 had functional quality improvement teams. Without strong leadership, many MCH service delivery challenges persist [37, 66, 67].

In Tanzania, management and the quality improvement teams directly contribute to initiatives for improving the quality of MCH services and addressing challenges in the quality of MCH services [68–70]. However, previous studies in the country support our findings that the functionality of these teams is still weak [70, 71]. Our study confirms that well-performing facilities are those with functional teams. This proves that effective teamwork is critical to strengthening and promoting quality health services, regardless of infrastructure, staff, and commodities availability.

The provision of quality emergency obstetric care depends on skilled health attendants working in an environment where drugs and medical supplies are available when needed [72]. Only 16.5% of the facilities had the required medicines and medical supplies for obstetric emergencies on the day of the assessment. The essential items missing in many facilities included Hydralazine, Magnesium Sulphate, Calcium Gluconate, and Oxygen Concentrators. These are the common missing items in other developing countries [73–76]. An inadequate supply of these critical supplies puts pregnant women at risk of losing their lives in case of an emergency and compromises quality of MCH services in general [73].

Staffing is another crucial determinant of quality MCH services [37]. Similar to other low and middle-income countries, our findings show that MCH service coverage in PHCs cannot reduce maternal and child mortality without qualified staff [2, 77]. The findings of our study align with this, where adequate staffing of nurses predicted the presence of quality MCH services. Surprisingly, this was not the case for other cadres, as the presence of clinicians or other health workers did not show a significant difference in performance among facilities. The shortage of skilled staff in SSA’s PHCs is very high, and we believe nurses are the most affected group of healthcare staff when the client-provider ratio is too high in the provision of MCH services [78]. This claim is supported by studies showing that the quality of care decreases when nurses have to attend to more mothers than expected [67, 79]. Additionally, there is evidence that crucial MCH services can be provided in PHCs by non-physician staff, who are usually nurses [80].

Facility ownership also influenced performance. As seen in other SSA countries [37, 81, 82], our study also found that the quality of MCH services is more challenging in private facilities compared to government ones. Cost considerations may limit private sector engagement in comprehensive MCH services [81, 82]. In Tanzania, as in many socialist countries, the party that has led the government since independence has a manifesto of providing free MCH services to all pregnant women and children under the age of five [83, 84]. This may be why services are poor in private facilities, as these services lack commercial competition with government facilities, which are the majority. Additionally, facilities owned by the Catholic Church constitute a significant proportion of private facilities in the country. Catholic Church facilities do not offer family planning services, which may contribute to the low performance among private facilities.

Facility level and location were also important. Higher-level facilities performed better in providing MCH services compared to lower-level facilities. This has also been demonstrated in various previous studies in Tanzania, where the quality of services improved with the level of the facility [70, 71, 85–88]. This study presents an interesting aspect regarding the relationship between the quality of MCH services and the facility’s location. While previous studies showed that the quality of various services was affected by the facility’s location [42, 70, 71, 85–88]. This study found no difference between rural and urban facilities. This might be because MCH services are fundamental and have been equally emphasized in both settings. These findings are important for Tanzania, which has intensified efforts to ensure universal health coverage.

A limitation of this study is that we did not interview HCWs specifically about service quality, though they are the primary providers of MCH care. Their perspectives—as well as those of clients and families; could impact how MCH services are delivered [37]. For example, HCWs’ may believe that only equipment saves lives [79] or that preterm babies have a low chance of survival [38]. However, we interviewed clients to gauge their satisfaction on MCH services provided.

Another limitation is that the data were collected during a single-day visits, which may not provide an accurate long-term picture of the facility, especially for consumable commodities like medicines.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical gaps in the quality of MCH services in PHC facilities in Tanzania. Despite the availability of national guidelines and tools, implementation remains suboptimal, especially in areas such as antenatal care, intrapartum monitoring, emergency obstetric services, postnatal and newborn care, and cervical cancer screening. Key challenges include poor adherence to the available guidelines, inadequate staffing—especially nurses, weak functionality of management and quality improvement teams, shortage of key emergency medicines, and infrastructure deficits such as neonatal wards and KMC rooms. Private facilities, in particular, lag behind government facilities in providing comprehensive MCH services.

To address these challenges, we recommend the following policy actions:

i.Reinforce training and clinical mentorship for all MCH service providers, with a focus on guideline adherence, emergency care, and respectful maternity care.

ii.Improve functionality of Quality Improvement and management Teams at all levels of the health system to ensure ongoing monitoring, evaluation, and improvement of MCH service delivery.

Improve functionality of Quality Improvement and management Teams at all levels of the health system to ensure ongoing monitoring, evaluation, and improvement of MCH service delivery.

iii.Ensure continuous supply of essential medicines and supplies, particularly those required for managing obstetric emergencies, across all PHC facilities.

iv.Scale up infrastructure for newborn care, including functional neonatal wards and KMC rooms.

v.Expand cervical cancer screening services and integrate them into routine PHC services to support national targets for cancer prevention and early detection.

vi.Address human resource gaps by recruiting and equitably distributing skilled healthcare workers, prioritizing the retention and motivation of nurses, who are central to MCH service delivery.

The quality and equality of MCH services in Tanzania may be greatly enhanced by putting these policy initiatives into practice, which will ultimately lower preventable maternal and infant mortality and move the country closer to universal health coverage. Future studies could employ research designs capable of establishing cause-and-effect relationships and incorporate qualitative methods to capture stakeholders’ perspectives on the reasons for facilities’ inadequate adherence to MCH quality standards.

Indicator/variableDescription and means of scoringIndicator 1: Antenatal care (ANC) services follow guidelinesThis indicator aimed to determine whether the facility follows ANC guidelines. To assess this, the evaluators asked 3 main questions, each with a possible score of 3 points.The first question was whether routine antenatal services were provided as required. Seven routine services were inquired about in this question, which are: i. Iron supplementation ii. Folic acid supplementation iii. Mebendazole tablets iv. Intermittent preventive treatment (IPT) for malaria v. Tetanus toxoid vaccination vi. Monitoring for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (checking the antenatal card) vii. Syphilis rapid diagnostic testTo verify the above services, the assessor was required to review three ANC visits to ensure that the cards were filled out properly and that the package of services was provided. The cards were selected randomly. There were three possible scores: Yes: 1.00, Partial: 0.50, and No: 0.00. A Yes score was given if the entire package of services was provided and the cards were correctly filled out in all three ANC visits. A Partial score was given if less than the entire package was provided or if the ANC card was incorrectly filled out in one case. A No score was given for less than half of the full package.The second question was whether ANC guidelines, SOPs, checklists, or job aids, including wall charts, were available for reference. The possible scores were Yes: 1.00 and No: 0.00. A Yes score was given if all items were available, and a No score was given if not all items were available.The last question for this indicator was to check if 3 equipment—BP machines, foetoscopes, and adult weighing scales—were available and functioning. The possible scores were Yes: 1.00 and No: 0.00. A Yes score was given if all three items were available and functioning, and a No score was given if one or more items were unavailable for use.Indicator 2: Family planning services follow guidelinesThe goal of this indicator was to determine whether the family planning services provided adhere to guidelines. To assess this, the evaluators asked three main questions, each with a possible score of 3 points.The first question is whether all family planning methods were offered according to the level of health facility and expertise. The assessor was required to check and review records of issues in the previous week. Possible scores were Yes: 1.00, Partial: 0.50, and No: 0.00. Yes was scored if family planning methods offered include oral contraceptives, injectable contraceptives, condoms, and ovulation methods. Partial was scored if fewer than four methods were offered. No was scored if none of the methods were offered.The second question is whether family planning methods were offered according to the client’s informed choice. This was achieved through exit interviews with three family planning clients to ascertain their informed choice of family planning method. Possible scores were Yes: 1.00 and No: 0.00. Yes was scored if all three clients were offered contraceptives according to informed choice. No was scored if fewer than three clients were offered contraceptives according to informed choice.The final question in this indicator is whether there was any healthcare worker trained in family planning methods. At the dispensary level, any method was considered suitable, while at the health centre and hospital levels, the requirement was for at least 2 healthcare workers to be trained on long-term/permanent family planning methods. Examples of long-term methods include intrauterine devices (IUDs), implants, female sterilization, and vasectomy. Possible scores were Yes: 1.00, No: 0.00, NA: 1.00. Yes, was scored if all items were available and functioning on the day. No, if any item was missing (cold chain not fully functional). Ethics approval and consent to participate

The assessment was considered a programmatic quality improvement activity and did not require formal ethical clearance. However, all standard ethical procedures including voluntary participation, informed consent, and data confidentiality were strictly observed. Prior permission was sought from the Ministry of Health, Community Development, Gender, Elderly and Children (Recently renamed as Ministry of Health) before use of the dataset. The Ministry of Health is a custodian of Health Management Information Systems data including the SRA database. Ethical clearance is not necessary for this type of a study because data were collected in the course of implementing an initiative by the government and hence this analysis aims to give feedback on prevailing circumstances after its successful implementation.
