Men’s labour migration and attitudes towards women’s sexual autonomy: evidence from the National Family Health Survey 2019–21
Authors: Vikesh Kumar, Ankit Gupta, Priti, Phanidhar Upadhyaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale labour migration is common across India and often results in extended periods of spousal separation. This absence changes who holds responsibility and decision-making power at home, and can reshape traditional gender roles and women's autonomy. Sexual autonomy, a woman's ability to control her own body, negotiate safe sex, and make her own reproductive choices, is one part of this shift. Because separation changes spousal dynamics, it can also create different expectations around this autonomy. Our study looks at how male labour migration relates to men's own perceptions of women's sexual autonomy.Data & methodsWe use data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5). The sample includes women aged 15–49 and men aged 15–54. After excluding non-working respondents, the final sample is 82,598 individuals. Migration status is defined as an employment-related absence from the place of residence for at least one month, classified as short-term or long-term migration. We use binary logistic regression to examine the link between migration status and men's perceptions of women's sexual autonomy, adjusting for relevant socioeconomic and demographic factors.ResultsOur study finds that 15.3% of men were temporary migrants (8.9% short-term, 6.4% long-term), confirming migration as a major livelihood strategy in India. In unadjusted models, both short- and long-term migrants, along with men who reported substance use, were significantly more likely to hold negative attitudes toward women's sexual autonomy. After adjusting for sociodemographic factors, the odds of holding negative attitudes were significantly lower among middle-aged men (45–54 years), men from the Scheduled Castes, and men with higher education.ConclusionMigration status has a significant role to play in the male perspective on the sexual autonomy of women. Both short- and long-term labour migrants are more likely than non-migrants to hold negative attitudes toward women's sexual autonomy. Our findings point to the need for targeted social and behavioural interventions aimed specifically at younger male migrants who are engaged in substance abuse.

Introduction

Migration is a social and economic force that reshapes family structures, gender relations, and community norms. Male labour migration, especially in low- and middle-income countries, often leads to long spousal separation and shifts how households make decisions [10]. This separation can weaken closeness and intimacy in a marriage [20]. At the same time, it changes what is expected of the wife left behind: she takes on more unpaid labour and domestic duties, but she also gains more room to make decisions in the home. These two outcomes, the added burden and the added autonomy, are distinct. Burden refers to the rise in unpaid work and household duties; autonomy refers to a woman’s actual decision-making power. Male absence makes both possible at once, because it redistributes roles within structural limits that remain firmly in place. In India, women are more likely to have a say in daily cooking decisions, spending on expensive household items, and decisions about their children’s marriage and healthcare when their husbands are away [10]. In Mozambique, women whose husbands have migrated report greater freedom to visit friends and family, look for work, travel to the city or district capital, and get tested for HIV [31]. Across these contexts, men’s migration tends to increase their spouses’ independence and decision-making power [31]. This is a meaningful shift in countries where women have historically held less personal autonomy under patriarchal norms [6], with less access to resources and less control over decisions than men [14, 22, 26]. But this body of research stops at household decision-making

Sexual autonomy refers to a person’s ability to negotiate safer sex, reduce unwanted sexual contact, make informed and voluntary decisions about sexual activity, and exercise control over their own body and reproductive choices [2, 19, 30]. It is widely recognised as a basic human right and a core part of sexual and reproductive health and well-being [12]. Women’s sexual autonomy remains a serious concern in patriarchal settings, where social norms often limit it directly. Studies show that women with greater sexual autonomy are more likely to negotiate condom use, use contraception, and avoid negative reproductive health outcomes such as STIs and unwanted pregnancy [1, 8]

Power dynamics within sexual relationships are shaped by conventional ideas about masculinity, femininity, and gender inequality more broadly [5]. Migration adds another layer to this: some studies find that male out-migration increases women’s autonomy at the household level, but this does not automatically extend to sexual autonomy — patriarchal norms, social surveillance, and economic dependency on the husband can persist regardless of his physical absence [18, 20, 31].

Male labour migration could be associated with male attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy through several pathways. First, economic mobility and remittances may reinforce a man’s role as provider, strengthening traditional patriarchal authority at home [10]. Second, long spousal separation may also weaken emotional intimacy and communication between partners, which can increase suspicion or a need to control the wife’s sexuality [20]. Third, exposure to urban or destination settings may introduce different gender norms and more liberal attitudes toward women’s decision-making and sexual rights [16]. Fourth, difficult migration experiences, such as poor working conditions and social isolation, may raise stress, alcohol use, and risk-taking, all of which are linked to more controlling and coercive sexual attitudes [13, 21]. Because these pathways pull in opposite directions, the actual effect of migration on attitudes is an open empirical question.

Two competing theoretical frameworks help explain why migration could push attitudes in either direction. Social remittances theory [17] suggests that migrant men bring back new ideas and values from their destination, which can shift them and their households toward more gender-equitable attitudes. Hegemonic masculinity theory suggests migration can deepen patriarchal attitudes if it strengthens a man’s role as economic provider, or if separation creates insecurity about his authority and his wife’s fidelity. These two frameworks predict opposite outcomes, which is part of why this needs empirical testing rather than assumption

Most existing research has examined how male migration affects women’s economic autonomy and household decision-making, as well as the consequences for women left behind. Far less attention has gone to the men themselves: how migration shapes their own gender attitudes, and specifically their attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy. This matters because men’s attitudes directly interact with sexual negotiation, reproductive health outcomes, and the balance of power within marriage. Studies also treat migration as a single, uniform experience, without separating short-term from long-term absence, so we know little about whether migration duration itself changes these attitudes. Our study addresses that gap. Using nationally representative data from the NFHS-5, we examine the relationship between men’s labour migration and their attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy in India, distinguishing between short- and long-term migration and accounting for substance use and socio-demographic factors

Methodology

Data sources

This study draws on data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), carried out between 2019 and 2021. NFHS-5 is a nationally representative, large-scale survey that provides detailed information on population characteristics, family planning, maternal and child health, nutrition, morbidity, and socio-economic and demographic conditions across all states and union territories of India. The survey used a two-stage sampling design in rural areas and a three-stage design in urban areas. Conducted in two phases, it covered 636,699 households and included 724,115 women aged 15–49 and 101,839 men aged 15–54. Full details on the survey methodology are available in the NFHS-5 India report (International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) and ICF, 2021).

Study sample

For this study, we use the men’s sample. We first exclude men who were not engaged in any economic activity, since labour migration status is only meaningful for working men. Among the remaining working men, anyone away from home for one month or more was classified as a labour migrant. Outcome variable

The main outcome variable is men’s attitudes toward the sexual autonomy of women. In NFHS-5, men were asked whether, if their wives refuse to have sex, the husband has the right to (a) get angry, (b) refuse financial support, (c) use force for sex, and (d) have sex with another woman. We built the outcome variable by combining responses to these four questions: men who answered positively to at least one was coded as holding a negative attitude (1); all others were coded 0 (scale reliability coefficient: alpha = 0.84).

Main explanatory variables: migration status

NFHS-5 does not ask about migration status directly, but it does ask two related questions. First, respondents were asked whether, in the last 12 months, they had been away from home for one month or more at a time. Those who answered yes were then asked whether they had been away for six months or more at a time. Based on these two questions, we classified respondents into three groups: men who had never been away from home for at least one month were considered non-migrants; those away for at least one month but less than six months were considered short-term or temporary migrants [7, 15] and those away for six months or more were considered long-term or semi-permanent migrants (NSSO, 2010)

Other explanatory variables

Other explanatory variables include respondents’ age (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, and 45–54), education (no education, primary, secondary, and higher), marital status (never married, married, others), occupation (professional, clerical, sales, services, agriculture, production worker, and other), residence (rural, urban), religion (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, and others), social group (Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, and Others), wealth index (poorer, poor, middle class, rich, and richest), media exposure, smoking behavior, and alcohol use (no, yes).

Methods

Descriptive statistics were used to present the distribution of outcome and explanatory variables. The analysis included a total sample of 82,598 men aged 15–54 years, comprising 70,039 non-migrants, 7,253 short-term migrants, and 5,306 long-term migrants. The outcome variable of the study is men’s attitudes towards women’s sexual autonomy (MASAW),

Statistical methods

We used descriptive statistics to show the distribution of the outcome and explanatory variables across the full sample of 82,598 men aged 15–54, 70,039 non-migrants, 7,253 short-term migrants, and 5,306 long-term migrants. We then ran a bivariate analysis to examine how the outcome variable, men’s attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy (MASAW), varies by background characteristics, using a chi-square test to assess significance. Finally, we ran a binary logistic regression to examine the association between migration status and MASAW. We report two models as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs): Model I provides unadjusted estimates (UORs), and Model II provides adjusted estimates (AORs) for migration status after controlling for covariates. All analyses were run in Stata MP 17.

Results

Long-term migrants were also more educated, 18.2% had completed high school or above, compared to 15.5% of short-term migrants and 15.7% of non-migrants. About 39.8% of men overall worked in agriculture, with smaller shares in other occupations. Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes were more represented among short-term migrants than long-term migrants, while Other Backwards Classes were more represented among long-term migrants. Short-term migrants were the poorest group: 26.9% fell in the poorest category, compared to 23.9% of long-term migrants and 19.7% of non-migrants. Associations between temporary migration status and men’s attitudes towards the sexual autonomy of women

Table 3 shows the estimates of logistic regression to examine the association between temporary migration status and men’s attitudes towards the sexual autonomy of women. Approximately 28.3% of men exhibited negative attitudes towards women’s sexual autonomy. In the unadjusted model, the results show that men who were migrants (both short-term and long-term) were significantly more likely to have negative attitudes towards sexual autonomy. After adjusting for the covariates, Model II shows that short-term migrants (AOR: 1.13; CI: 1.07–1.19) and long-term migrants (AOR: 1.41; CI: 1.32–1.49) were more likely to have negative attitudes than non-migrants. Similarly, men who used substances were 1.16 times more likely (AOR: 1.16; CI: 1.12–1.20) to have negative attitudes. Logistic estimates in Model II further show that men belonging to the older age group (45–54 years), those with higher education, and those belonging to Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes had significantly lower odds of negative attitudes. On the other hand, men belonging to the Muslim religion and those in higher wealth categories had significantly higher odds of having negative attitudes towards the sexual autonomy of women. Discussion

This study examined the association between temporary labour migration and men’s attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy in India. The results demonstrate that, in comparison to non-migrants, migrant men—especially those who have been migrants for a long time—are more likely to have negative opinions about women’s sexual autonomy. This pattern contradicts the widely held theoretical belief that social learning and exposure to diverse social contexts during migration and urbanisation lead to more progressive gender norms [3, 9].

Several interrelated mechanisms may explain this unexpected finding. First, many migration destinations for low- and semi-skilled workers are characterised by economic precarity, informal employment, and male-dominated social networks rather than serving as places of liberal norm diffusion [29]. Migration may strengthen masculine authority and patriarchal expectations in these contexts, especially at times of social fragility and economic strain. In situations where traditional provider roles are challenged, maintaining control over women's sexuality may act as a symbolic declaration of authority [4].

Several mechanisms may explain these findings. First, migration destinations for low- and semi-skilled workers tend to involve economic precarity, informal employment, and male-dominated social networks, rather than offering exposure to more liberal norms [29]. In this kind of setting, migration may actually strengthen masculine authority and patriarchal expectations, especially when migrants face economic strain. When a man’s role as provider is under pressure, controlling his wife’s sexuality may serve as a way of asserting authority elsewhere [4]. Second, in cyclical and short-term migration, the assumption that migrants gain real exposure to egalitarian norms may not hold. Migrants often have limited social integration at their destination and remain within similar peer networks rather than encountering different gender norms [11]. Third, mobile communication dilutes the idea that physical distance reduces a husband’s control over the household decision making. Migrant husbands can stay in constant phone contact, monitoring and directing household decisions from a distance. Therefore, migration does not necessarily reduce control, even when it creates physical separation. Fourth, selection may also play a role. Men who already hold more conservative gender attitudes may be more likely to migrate in the first place, due to structural and economic pressures [25]. This would mean migration is not a neutral experience that changes attitudes, but one that is shaped by the attitudes men already hold before they leave.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the social remittances theory only partly explains what we see in internal labour migration in India. Our results align more closely with hegemonic masculinity theory, which expects male control to persist and adapt even as economic and social circumstances shift, rather than with social remittances theory, which expects migration to liberalise attitudes. The findings also show that age, education, and wealth shape gender attitudes alongside migration status [23]. Younger men held more regressive attitudes than older men, while more education was linked to more gender-equitable attitudes. These findings point to interventions that go beyond the economic side of migration. Programs should include gender-sensitisation efforts and approaches that account for how digital communication can be used to maintain control within migrant households. This is also found in existing evidence that migration affects social norms and family dynamics in gender-structured settings [3, 9]

There are a few limitations to our study. The cross-sectional design means we cannot make causal claims about these relationships. Reporting bias may also affect how men answer attitudinal questions. Future studies using longitudinal designs and more detailed measures of migration experience could help clarify how migration shapes gender attitudes over the life course.

Conclusion

Our study shows that migration is a significant determinant in shaping men’s attitudes toward women’s sexual autonomy in India. Both temporary and long-term migrants are more likely than non-migrants to have negative attitudes toward women's sexual autonomy, defyinthe widespread belief that migration and exposure to metropolitan environments inevitably result in more progressive viewpoints.

Additionally, we find that these attitudes are influenced by important social and economic variables, such as education, income, occupation, and religion, and are associated with substance use. Younger men seem more likely to hold less progressive opinions, whereas men with greater education tend to hold more gender-equitable attitudes. Overall, these results imply that migration may not always improve gender attitudes and, in certain situations, exacerbate existing disparities. This highlights the need for more targeted and context-specific interventions, particularly for migrant men. These include initiatives that directly address enduring patriarchal practices in homes and social networks, as well as gender awareness programs in migrant communities and workplaces.
