Mitral Edge-to-Edge Repair Resolves Hemolytic Anemia After Surgical Mitral Valve Repair
Authors: Jinny Scheffler, Stefan Schoensteiner, Annika Hanrieder, Johannes Mörike, Wolfgang Rottbauer, Leonhard Schneider, Michael Paukovitsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemolytic anemia after surgical valve repair is rare but potentially life-threatening and has mostly commonly been described in the context of paravalvular regurgitation.Case SummaryA 78-year-old woman with prior surgical mitral valve (MV) repair presented with progressive dyspnea and severe hemolytic anemia. Echocardiography revealed severe eccentric, valvular mitral regurgitation (MR) caused by anterior leaflet prolapse with a high-velocity regurgitant jet striking the annuloplasty ring. The patient underwent transcatheter mitral edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER), achieving reduction of MR to mild and resolution of hemolysis.DiscussionThis case highlights hemolysis as a rare complication after surgical MV repair and the utility of M-TEER for patients at prohibitive surgical risk.Take-Home MessagesM-TEER may represent a safe and effective option for managing hemolytic anemia due to recurrent MR. Besides paravalvular regurgitation, valvular regurgitation in the context of prior surgical MV repair should be recognized as a potential cause of hemolytic anemia.

History of Presentation

A 78-year-old woman was initially referred to the hematology department of our tertiary center by her family doctor for diagnostic work-up and treatment of hemolytic anemia of unknown cause. At presentation, the patient reported experiencing progressive fatigue, dyspnea (NYHA functional class III), and palpitations over the preceding 3 months. Physical examination revealed mild scleral icterus, a holosystolic murmur, and no peripheral edema.Take-Home Messages•Hemolytic anemia is a rare and serious complication of recurrent mitral regurgitation after mitral valve repair.•Echocardiography is essential for identifying regurgitant jets causing mechanical red blood cell destruction.•M-TEER can be an effective treatment strategy for patients with recurrent, valvular mitral regurgitation and mechanically induced hemolysis.

Past Medical History

Nine months prior to presentation at our center, the patient had been admitted to an external hospital with decompensated heart failure and diagnosed with severe mitral regurgitation (MR) due to bileaflet mitral valve prolapse. Coronary angiography excluded relevant coronary artery disease.

The patient subsequently underwent endoscopic minimally invasive surgical mitral valve repair via mini-thoracotomy. Intraoperative inspection confirmed leaflet flail of the A2 and P3 segments, prolapse of the P2 segment, and a cleft between the P1 and P2 segments. Chordae of the A1, A2, P2 and P3 segments were reconstructed using Gore-Tex loops, an annuloplasty ring (38-mm Edwards Physio II ring) was implanted, and the cleft was surgically closed. The postoperative course was initially uneventful.

Approximately 8 months after surgery, the patient was acutely readmitted via the emergency department with rapidly progressive dyspnea and severe hemolytic anemia. Hematologic evaluation was inconclusive, with negative antibody testing and no evidence of autoimmune hemolysis. An extensive diagnostic work-up, including gastroscopy and colonoscopy, did not identify a bleeding source. Empirical glucocorticoid therapy was initiated, without improvement. The patient required repeated blood transfusions and was discharged without a definitive diagnosis.

Subsequent outpatient laboratory monitoring demonstrated persistent anemia with continued transfusion requirements, prompting referral to our tertiary center for further evaluation.

Relevant comorbidities included hypertension, dyslipidemia, and persistent atrial fibrillation treated with oral anticoagulation.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed mild hepatic steatosis and a slightly dilated common bile duct; hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were ruled out. Transthoracic echocardiography suggested high-grade eccentric MR. In addition, transesophageal echocardiography was performed and revealed severe, eccentric valvular mitral regurgitation due to anterior leaflet prolapse in the A1/A2 segments (effective regurgitant orifice area: 0.3 cm2, mitral valve orifice area: 5.0 cm2, mean pressure gradient: 3 mm Hg). A pseudo-prolapse was present in the P3 segment with cleft in P2/P3. Management

The case was discussed with a multidisciplinary heart team owing to transfusion-dependent hemolysis and severe recurrent MR. Given the high surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons score: 8.4%) and comorbidity burden, redo mitral valve surgery was deemed prohibitive. The patient underwent transcatheter mitral edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) using the MitraClip system. One device (MitraClip XT, Abbott) was successfully implanted, reducing MR from severe (grade IV) to mild (grade I). Stenosis was excluded, with mean pressure gradient of 5 mm Hg. Outcome and Follow-Up

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, with normalized hemolysis parameters and mild residual MR on echocardiography.

Discussion

Hemolytic anemia after surgical valve repair is a known but rare complication (<1%) and occurs most frequently in the setting of severe paravalvular regurgitation.1 In most cases, hemolysis occurs within the first 3 months after surgery.1 Notably, there is increasing evidence for hemolysis even in valvular regurgitation2, 3, 4 given the interaction of high-velocity jets with prosthetic material, similar to paravalvular regurgitation.1 Blood acceleration and contact with the prosthetic material may increase shear stress, leading to erythrocyte destruction.3,4 Another potential mechanism described in previous cases is the collision of the regurgitation jet with structures of the valvular apparatus, including potential shear stress increasing interference with residual valvular structures as well as cell fragmentation caused by a dehisced rigid annuloplasty ring.3

Diagnosis is based on laboratory evidence of intravascular hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, and echocardiographic identification of the regurgitant jet mechanism. Management depends on the severity of hemolysis and the surgical risk. While redo surgery has been the traditional approach, M-TEER has emerged as an effective alternative. Recent case series have reported resolution of hemolysis after MR reduction with M-TEER,2, 3, 4 aligning with the outcome observed in this patient.

Conclusions

In patients with prior mitral valve repair presenting with hemolytic anemia, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for recurrent MR as the cause. Paravalvular regurgitation as well as valvular regurgitation should be recognized as a potential cause of mechanically induced hemolytic anemia. Besides ruling out other causes of hemolytic anemia, echocardiographic evaluation is critical for diagnosis. M-TEER may offer a safe and effective treatment option for high-risk surgical candidates with recurrent MR and hemolysis induced by valvular regurgitation.
