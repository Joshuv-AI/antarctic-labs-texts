Bridging the species divide: The limits of rat models in capturing human PVOD mechanisms
Authors: Frédéric Perros, Cédric Chaveroux, David Montani
Published: 2024

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent paper by Prabhakar et al., “Mechanisms underlying age‐associated exacerbation of pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease,” 1 which investigates the role of the integrated stress response (ISR) in pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease (PVOD). Their findings, focusing on the inhibition of the ISR and protein kinase R (PKR) in an experimental rat model, provide compelling evidence for potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate pulmonary vascular remodeling in PVOD, in both young and aged rats. The study is a significant step forward in understanding disease mechanisms, and the authors have executed an impressive investigation into the ISR's role. However, in light of our study, 2 which explores human PVOD, we wish to highlight several critical distinctions between rat models and human disease. Specifically, we aim to address the differential roles of PKR and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) activation, the bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 (BMPR2)–SMAD‐mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD) axis, and the broader implications of these differences for translational research.

Prabhakar et al. 1 demonstrated that in rats, mitomycin C administration triggers a strong activation of the PKR–ISR axis, leading to phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) and subsequent upregulation of ATF4. This pathway played a pivotal role in vascular endothelial (VE) remodeling, disrupting barrier integrity via the loss of VE‐cadherin and RAD51. They found that pharmacological inhibition of PKR using C16 or ISR using ISRIB prevented this damage and reversed PVOD‐like phenotypes in rats, particularly in aged populations, where ISR activation was more pronounced due to reduced activity of protein phosphatase 1, leading to prolonged eIF2α phosphorylation. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the PKR–ISR axis to manage PVOD.

However, in our study “Comparison of Human and Experimental Pulmonary Veno‐Occlusive Disease,” 2 the analysis of human PVOD suggests that the ISR pathway, particularly the PKR–ATF4 axis, may not be as prominently activated in human cases. In contrast to the robust ATF4 activation observed in rats, we did not detect significant upregulation of ATF4 in human lung samples. While the PKR–ISR axis is crucial in rats, its activation may not be a central driver of human PVOD pathogenesis. Instead, we found that other pathways, including those dependent on general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2), might be involved in human PVOD. This divergence between species presents a significant challenge for directly translating ISR‐targeted therapies to human patients.

A particularly important aspect highlighted in another recent Prabhakar et al.'s study using the same model “Reversal of pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease phenotypes by inhibition of the integrated stress response” 3 and our research 2 is the role of the BMPR2–SMAD signaling axis. BMPR2 mutations, commonly associated with pulmonary arterial hypertension, contribute to disease severity. 4 Prabhakar et al. showed that rats with a heterozygous BMPR2 mutation experienced more severe vascular remodeling and right ventricular hypertrophy than wild‐type rats. 3 The exacerbation of PVOD phenotypes in these BMPR2‐mutant rats stresses the importance of BMPR2 signaling in maintaining endothelial function and vascular homeostasis. Furthermore, they found that ISR inhibition using ISRIB restored the levels of VE‐cadherin and RAD51, even in BMPR2‐mutant rats, suggesting that the therapeutic benefits of targeting the PKR–ISR axis could extend to patients with BMPR2 mutations.

Our study similarly found that BMPR2 signaling plays a critical role in PVOD pathogenesis. However, we observed that GCN2 may modulate the BMPR2–SMAD axis in human PVOD through alternative pathways, as inhibition of GCN2 affected SMAD signaling without fully abolishing it. This suggests that the interplay between BMPR2 and GCN2 is more complex in human disease than in rodent models, potentially contributing to the differences in ISR activation observed between species. 2

The contrast between these findings in rat models and human PVOD emphasizes the broader challenge in translational medicine: the significant species‐specific differences in disease mechanisms. Prabhakar et al.'s studies 1 , 3 are important in advancing our understanding of PVOD. Still, the results also raise important questions about the relevance of preclinical models when studying human disease. While the inhibition of the PKR–ISR axis showed promise in reversing PVOD phenotypes in rats, the lack of significant ISR activation in human PVOD raises concerns about the direct translational relevance of these findings. Targeting the PKR–ISR axis in humans may have limited efficacy, and future research should focus on identifying alternative pathways, such as GCN2‐dependent mechanisms, that are more directly involved in human disease progression. Additionally, therapies that target the BMPR2–SMAD axis could hold promise in PVOD patients.

As the scientific community continues developing innovative therapeutic approaches for PVOD and other pulmonary vascular diseases, we must re‐evaluate and brainstorm how best to model these complex human conditions. Animal models remain invaluable tools in studying disease mechanisms. Still, the divergence in findings between rat models and human PVOD underlines the necessity of developing models that more closely mimic human disease. A collaborative effort to establish improved in vivo models is essential to ensure that new therapeutic interventions are relevant and effective in the human context. 5

In conclusion, while Prabhakar et al.'s works provide valuable insights into the role of the ISR in experimental PVOD, our study indicates that the PKR–ISR pathway may not play as central a role in human PVOD. Instead, other pathways, particularly those involving GCN2 and the BMPR2–SMAD axis, may be more critical in the human context. This divergence highlights the limits of current preclinical models and the need for further research into human‐specific disease mechanisms to develop in vivo models mimicking the complexity of the disease in humans and ensure that therapeutic interventions are tailored to the pathways most relevant to human patients.
