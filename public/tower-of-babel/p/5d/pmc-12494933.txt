Camizestrant causes reversible pharmacological effects on retinal responses in rats
Authors: Gregory Hamm, Gareth Maglennon, Stuart Purbrick, Lucy Flint, Glen Hawthorne, James Atkinson, Ruth Macdonald, Alexander Harmer, Stewart Jones, Yelena Krakova, James Ver Hoeve, Andrew Walding, Lindsay Wright
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Some patients treated with camizestrant experienced mild, reversible visual effects.•This nonclinical study examined camizestrant in rats using electroretinography.•The results supported a reversible, pharmacological effect on retinal response.•No structural changes to the retina were observed.

Introduction

Camizestrant, the next-generation oral selective estrogen receptor (ER) degrader, is currently under investigation in patients with ER-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative breast cancer. Data from the SERENA-1, SERENA-2, and SERENA-3 trials have demonstrated that camizestrant is well tolerated as both a monotherapy [[1], [2], [3]] and in combination with abemaciclib [4], capivasertib [5], palbociclib [6], or ribociclib [7].

During the SERENA-1 and SERENA-2 trials, dose-related incidence of mild visual effects was reported in some patients, most commonly photopsia [1,3]. In the SERENA-2 trial, photopsia was reported in 12 % of patients at the recommended phase 3 camizestrant dose (75 mg once daily) [3]. Photopsia can manifest as momentary flashes, strobing, or flickering light in the vision [8,9]. Visual effects were transient and associated with transitions from darkness to bright light [1]. All symptoms resolved within a few weeks of treatment end [1,3], and there was no evidence of any structural changes in the eyes [1]. The association of reported visual effects with light transitions and the absence of any structural changes in the eye suggested that these effects may originate in the retina, a tissue primarily involved with detecting and adapting to changes in light conditions [10].

It is important to understand the origin of visual effects arising in some people receiving camizestrant to inform guidance for patients and healthcare professionals. Therefore, we sought to determine whether camizestrant is present in the eye after oral administration using mass spectrometry imaging, and what effect camizestrant might be having on the retina using electroretinograms (ERGs).

Mass spectrometry imaging can be used to spatially resolve drug biodistribution in complex and heterogeneous tissues such as retinal layers [11]; it can therefore establish whether and where a drug localizes in the eye. ERGs can be used to examine changes in retinal response under dark-adapted (scotopic, mediated by rod photoreceptors) and light-adapted (photopic, mediated by cone photoreceptors) conditions [12,13]. ERGs measure the electrical activity of the retina in response to stimulation with light, and generate a characteristic waveform (Fig. 1A) [14]. If the retina is strongly stimulated, high-frequency, low-amplitude wavelets, known as oscillatory potentials, can appear superimposed on the waveform response [15]. The different components of the ERG waveform correspond to activity in different parts of the retina. Thus, deviations from the standard ERG response can be used to localize potential effects of a drug to specific retinal layers and/or cell types [12,13].Fig. 1A, Typical ERG waveform response to stimulation with a single white flash of light. By combining mass spectrometry imaging and ERG in a nonclinical model, we examined the retinal exposure of camizestrant and the impact of that exposure on retinal response to establish whether the visual effects observed in clinical trials are likely to be functional or structural in origin.

Materials and methods

All procedures followed applicable animal welfare acts and were approved by the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Laboratory procedures were conducted in accordance with the applicable Test Facility standard operating procedures, which align with international standards of Good Laboratory Practice.

Animals

Female Long Evans Blue Spruce (HsdBlu:LE; RRID: RGD_5,508,398) rats were procured for this investigation (Part 1: Envigo RMS, Indianapolis, Indiana; Part 2: Envigo RMS, Livermore, California). Animals received water and Certified Rodent Diet #2014C (Envigo RMS, Inc.) ad libitum, and were identified via one or more of the following methods: an implantable microchip identification device, tail marking, HT IPTT-300 implantable programmable temperature transponders, and/or cage card.

Assessment of retinal distribution of camizestrant using mass spectrometry imaging

Mass spectrometry imaging to assess the retinal accumulation of camizestrant took place as a separate study that preceded the ERG assessments. Two groups of Long Evans rats (n = 3 per group) received daily doses of either camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day or control over a 7-day period. The control was Milli-Q water that was pH-adjusted to 4.8–5.5 using 1 N HCl/NaOH.

Animals were euthanized 2 h post-dose on Day 7.

Camizestrant levels in the retina were assessed through mass spectrometry imaging (RapifleX MALDI; RRID: SCR_026525). Low (40 µm) and high (10 µm) spatial resolution mass spectrometry images were taken. Relative abundance, distribution, and relative quantitation of camizestrant were analyzed using Fleximaging v5.1 and SCiLS Lab 2022a (RRID: SCR_014426) software. Further details of the imaging procedures can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

ERG dosing and dose selection

The ERG investigations were divided into two parts, each using similar methodology and the same animal model but assessing different doses of camizestrant. Part 1 was used to inform the doses investigated in Part 2.

Part 1 examined once-daily administration of camizestrant 25 mg kg-1/day or 75 mg kg-1/day versus vehicle (control) (n = 6 per group). To ensure full characterization of the dose–response relationship, Part 2 examined once-daily administration of camizestrant 5, 12, or 25 mg kg-1/day versus control (n = 6 per group). The control in both parts was deionized purified water, pH-adjusted to 4.8–5.5 with 1 N HCl. Details on how study materials were stored can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

The highest dose of camizestrant (75 mg kg-1/day) was selected following the mass spectrometry imaging, which showed that this dose level achieved retinal exposure in rats (primarily in the choroid and retinal pigment epithelium [RPE]) and was tolerated for 7 days of dosing (Fig. 2). The lowest dose of 5 mg kg-1/day was used as it was anticipated to be equivalent exposure to the intended clinical dose of 75 mg/day in humans. Animals were assigned sequentially to dose groups using a computerized procedure and statistical analysis designed to achieve body weight balance between groups. Dosing was administered via oral gavage at a dose volume of 10 mL/kg over 7 consecutive days. The oral route of administration was selected as this is the human therapeutic route for camizestrant. Animals were then observed for 7 days without further dosing prior to euthanasia on Day 15.

Assessment of retinal response using ERGs

Electroretinography was conducted by Ocular Services On Demand (OSOD, LLC, Madison, Wisconsin), with assistance from Labcorp technical staff.

Full-field ERGs were recorded once during the pre-dose phase (baseline), at approximately 90 min post-dose on Days 1 and 7, and 7 days after the last dose on Day 14 (Fig. 1B). ERG stimuli included dark-adapted and/or light-adapted single flash and flicker stimuli, and a temporal frequency–response profile to sinusoidal stimuli recorded from each eye, detailed below (Fig. 1C). Where relevant, the amplitude (size) and latency (speed) of responses were recorded. Further details of ERG procedures can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Prior to any dark-adapted assessments, animals were dark-adapted for a minimum of 1 hour. Prior to any light-adapted assessments, animals were light-adapted for approximately 10 min.

Single flash stimuli

Pulsed (4 ms duration, with a minimum inter-flash interval of 5 s) white flash stimuli were used to record the a- and b-wave responses of the retina (Fig. 1C). In Parts 1 and 2, dark-adapted stimuli comprised a standard single white flash of light, with intensities of −36, −30, −24, −16, −6, 0, 6, and 10 dB.

Oscillatory potentials, which reflect modulation of signaling between the photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells, can be examined by filtering ERG responses to pulsed white flashes of light. In Parts 1 and 2, oscillatory potentials were assessed by filtering high-frequency components of the responses recorded from the dark-adapted single white flash stimuli.

In Part 2 only, responses to pulsed white flashes were recorded during a 10-minute light-adaptation period at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min from start of light adaptation.

Light-adapted flicker stimuli

Pulsed white flash stimuli flickering at different frequencies were used to record the b-wave response of the retina only (Fig. 1C). Response to light-adapted flicker stimulation primarily comprises cone photoreceptor activity [12]. In Parts 1 and 2, light-adapted stimuli comprised pulsed white flashes of light of 0 dB intensity, flickering at a rate of 1, 3, and 30 Hz.

Light-adapted sinusoidal stimuli

Sinusoidally modulated light stimuli were used to assess the temporal frequency–response relationship of the retina (Fig. 1C). In Parts 1 and 2, light-adapted stimuli comprised single green flashes of light at temporal frequencies between 0.5 and 30 Hz to capture a sinusoidal stimulation pattern. Sample lengths were lengthened for low-frequency stimuli in order to record at least two full cycles.

Toxicity and exposure assessments

Animals were checked twice daily (am and pm) during the study. Detailed observations were conducted for each animal up to four times during the pre-dose phase (including on the day before dose initiation), on several occasions throughout the dosing phase, and on the day of scheduled euthanasia.

Assessments of toxicity were based on observations, body weight, and ophthalmic examination. On each day of dosing, cage-side observations were conducted for each animal at approximately 1 hour post-dose.

Ophthalmic examinations were conducted once during the pre-dose phase and on Days 8 and 15 (prior to necropsy; further details can be found in the Supplementary Materials). The ocular fundus of both eyes was examined using an indirect ophthalmoscope. Histopathologic examination of the eyes was conducted post-termination.

Camizestrant exposure was assessed through plasma samples that were taken following ERG on Days 1 and 7 (approximately 2 h post-dose). Further details on how the samples were stored can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analyses

ANCOVA was used to assess the ERG data (camizestrant groups vs control). Where available, observations from both eyes of the same animal were averaged prior to analysis. T-tests were used for group comparisons of animals receiving camizestrant versus those receiving the control, using the following statistical model: response = covariate + treatment.

A combination of ANOVA and pairwise comparisons were used to examine bodyweight data. Prior to any group comparisons by ANOVA, Levene’s test was performed to assess equality of variance between groups. If Levene’s test was significant (P ≤ 0.05), a rank transformation was applied to the data before analysis. If the group effect of the ANOVA was significant (P ≤ 0.05), Dunnett’s test was used to conduct pairwise comparisons for each treated group and the control group. Where only two groups were available for analysis, a two-sample t-test was conducted. Data with values above or below the limit of quantification were not analyzed.

Results

Data on group body weight and age can be found in the Supplementary Materials. No ophthalmic, histopathologic, or macroscopic observations were noted, consistent with findings from previous nonclinical toxicology studies (Supplementary Table S1).

Retinal distribution of camizestrant

Mass spectrometry imaging showed that camizestrant accumulated primarily in the choroid and RPE for rats receiving 75 mg kg-1/day (Fig. 2A, B).

Across all camizestrant groups, analysis of the relative abundance of camizestrant and ratio of abundance in the extra-ocular muscle found the greatest levels of camizestrant in the choroid and RPE, followed by the photoreceptor outer and inner segments (Fig. 2C, D).

The effect of camizestrant on retinal response under dark-adapted conditions

ERG response data are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 and detailed below.

Following at least 1 hour of dark adaptation, single white flashes of light were administered to each animal across a range of intensities to assess ERG a- and b-wave responses and oscillatory potentials (corresponding to activity in the retinal photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and horizontal/amacrine cells, respectively) under dark-adapted conditions. The amplitude and latency of response were recorded.

Administration of camizestrant at ≥25 mg kg-1/day significantly reduced the amplitude of the ERG b-wave versus control at Day 1 and Day 7 across all light intensities (Fig. 3; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S3; P ≤ 0.05). The reduction in b-wave for ≥25 mg kg-1/day at Day 1 and Day 7 of dosing was similar in magnitude (Fig. 4C) and was reversible, with amplitude returning to baseline levels following 7 days of washout (Day 14) (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S1B).

The largest depression in b-wave amplitude versus control was seen with camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day (Fig. 3A); a peak decrease of 77 % at −24 dB versus control was observed on Day 1 (Supplementary Table S3). Most animals receiving camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day had “electronegative” b-waves (at 10 dB, −24 dB, and −30 dB), in which the b-waves did not rise above pre-stimulus measurements. Exposure–response relationship

Assessment of mean plasma concentrations of camizestrant on Days 1 and 7 demonstrated systemic, dose-proportional exposure (Supplementary Table S4). At Day 1, mean unbound plasma concentration was 1590, 694, 425, 199, and 46 nmol/L for camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day, 25 mg kg-1/day (Part 1), 25 mg kg-1/day (Part 2), 12 mg kg-1/day, and 5 mg kg-1/day, respectively. At Day 7, mean unbound plasma concentration was 1570, 560, 533, 188, and 52 nmol/L for camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day, 25 mg kg-1/day (Part 1), 25 mg kg-1/day (Part 2), 12 mg kg-1/day, and 5 mg kg-1/day, respectively. Human exposure with camizestrant 75 mg has been reported as an unbound maximum concentration (Cmax) geomean of 0.034 µM (equivalent to 34 nmol/L) [1].

There was a clear sigmoidal exposure–response relationship between camizestrant exposure and dark-adapted b-wave and oscillatory potential amplitudes (Fig. 7A, B, respectively), with lower amplitudes being seen at greater exposures of camizestrant. Discussion

This study was prompted by the observation that some patients treated with camizestrant experienced mild visual effects during clinical trials [1,3]. We hypothesized that these likely resulted from effects of camizestrant on retinal responses and reasoned that any changes in retinal response would be detectable by ERG. The retina is a layered, light-sensitive tissue that, in simplified terms, encodes the light detected by photoreceptors into changes in neuronal signaling, which are conveyed via the bipolar cells to the retinal ganglion cells and directed out through the optic nerve. ERG waveforms reflect changes in electrical activity across the retina in response to light, the subcomponents of which correspond to the function of specific cells in different retinal layers. ERG a-waves primarily correspond to photoreceptor activation in the dark-adapted retina, with some contribution from the bipolar cells under light-adapted conditions [12]. B-waves correspond to bipolar cell function [12], and oscillatory potentials reflect activity primarily in the retinal horizontal and amacrine cells [15]. Retinal horizontal and amacrine cells modulate signaling between the photoreceptors and bipolar ganglion cells of the retina [12,16]. As it is impractical to conduct ERGs on human patients during clinical oncology trials, we identified an appropriate nonclinical model to examine our hypothesis.

First, we reported that camizestrant is present in the rat retina, providing evidence of retinal exposure and validating the use of rats to assess the effect of camizestrant on ERG responses. Interestingly, camizestrant was predominantly localized to the pigmented tissues of the eye (i.e., choroid and the RPE layer of the retina together with the iris and ciliary body regions). High concentration in pigmented regions of the eye is not uncommon for small molecules and is often a consequence of drug–melanin binding [17,18].

Second, we used ERG to assess functional changes in retinal responses resulting from camizestrant exposure to a range of flashing light stimuli under dark- and light-adapted conditions. Our principal ERG findings were a dose- and exposure-dependent reduction in the amplitude of b-waves and oscillatory potentials under dark-adapted conditions, and enhanced b-wave amplitude and latency under light-adapted conditions. The effects were reversible, and response levels returned to baseline within 1 week of post-dose washout. No effects on a-wave amplitude or latency were observed at any dose or light intensity. These data therefore suggest that camizestrant affects bipolar cell and retinal horizontal/amacrine cell activity in the retina but does not affect photoreceptor response. Furthermore, the return of responses to baseline levels within a week of post-dose washout aligns with clinical reports that visual effects resolve within a few weeks of treatment end [1,3], further supporting that they are reversible and do not reflect any structural change in the eye.

As b-wave response (i.e., bipolar cell activity) was decreased under dark adaptation but enhanced under light adaptation, this raises the question of how ERG responses change during the transition from dark to light conditions. To address this, we assessed ERG responses during light adaptation and found that administration of camizestrant at ≥12 mg kg-1/day significantly altered the dynamics of light adaptation in the retina for several minutes, recovering by the end of the 10-minute adaptation period. The marked difference in dark- and light-adapted b-wave responses and the altered dynamics in light adaptation indicate that camizestrant might affect visual function specifically during light transitions. The ERG effects reported here are consistent with the transient and reversible visual effects (e.g., photopsia) that were reported specifically during transitions between different lighting environments in clinical trials of humans receiving camizestrant [1].

The mechanism underlying the effects on retinal response is unclear, and there is little published data available on how the effect of selective ER degraders on ERs could affect retinal function. However, long-established ER degraders and modulators (tamoxifen, fulvestrant and lacustrine) are not associated with similar visual effects in the clinic as observed with camizestrant. This indicates that the ER does not mediate the visual effects of camizestrant. Based on the retinal distribution and response data reported here, possibilities include a direct or indirect effect on the bipolar, amacrine, or retinal horizontal cells, although the data presented here do not provide evidence for any specific molecular mechanism. Another interpretation of the observed change in ERG response pattern is that camizestrant treatment removes suppressive influence of the rod photoreceptors on cone photoreceptor function [19], resulting in enhancement of light-adapted responses.

One key limitation of this study is that we have no ERG data from human studies to compare with the data generated in rats. There are known differences in retinal composition between rats and humans; most notably, the rat retina contains a higher percentage of rod photoreceptors when compared with the human retina (99 % vs 95 %, respectively) [20]. Therefore, we can only speculate that the effects of camizestrant on the rat ERG reflect changes in patients who experience visual effects while taking camizestrant. However, several pieces of evidence support the premise that these nonclinical ERG effects translate into the visual effects observed in humans. First, the exposure–response curves reported here are consistent with the exposures achieved in patients; in the SERENA-1 trial, visual effects of any severity were observed in 46 % of patients at 75 mg and 64 % at 150 mg [1] – doses that correspond to unbound plasma Cmax geomean values of 0.034 μM and 0.086 µM, respectively (similar to the exposure at 5 mg kg-1/day in the non-clinical model presented here). However, in these non-clinical experiments, effects were primarily noted at ≥25 mg kg-1/day. Therefore, while there was an overlap in the exposure levels examined, caution should be exercised when drawing any conclusions as it is unclear how precisely the exposures correspond between the rat model and humans. It is somewhat speculative to make direct comparisons between how changes in ERG and patient-reported experience of visual effects relate to camizestrant exposure. Secondly, as already discussed, the rat ERG data suggest a potential effect on retinal responses during light transitions, which corresponds with the experience of patients with visual symptoms when taking camizestrant. Next, there is evidence that the effects of human congenital disorders associated with changes in ERG function can be replicated in mouse models of the disease, suggesting correspondence between human and rodent ERG responses [21,22]. Finally, there are numerous examples of drugs associated with clinical visual dysfunction that are also associated with retinal and ERG changes in nonclinical species (e.g., AUY922, crizotinib, voriconazole) [[23], [24], [25]]. Therefore, there is good circumstantial evidence that the effects of camizestrant on the rat ERG are potentially clinically relevant.

Conclusions

All evidence collected to date strongly supports that the low-grade visual effects of camizestrant observed in some patients during the SERENA-1 and SERENA-2 clinical trials are both pharmacological and reversible in nature. In rats, administration of camizestrant affects the ERG response within 90 min of dosing. These effects were reversible, dose dependent, and showed a classical sigmoidal relationship when plotted against plasma exposure. Mass spectrometry imaging in the same rat model demonstrated that camizestrant 75 mg kg-1/day accumulated primarily in the choroid and RPE of the retina. There were no indications of any structural eye changes in this study, and previous toxicology studies have shown that there are no eye or optic nerve changes by histology following up to 9 months of daily camizestrant administration in nonclinical species. Finally, these observations are consistent with the clinical presentation, where transient visual effects in patients had a dose-related incidence, were associated with changes in light conditions, fully resolved following treatment cessation, and had no evidence of structural changes in the eye.

These data offer valuable insights into the physiology underlying visual effects such as photopsia experienced by some patients who receive camizestrant and indicate that the mild visual effects reported while taking camizestrant do not represent long-term impact on the eye or visual function.
