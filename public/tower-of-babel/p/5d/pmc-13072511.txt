Maternal perinatal vulnerability and its influencing factors in different stages
Authors: Zeng Feng, Luan Xiao, Wang Junzhi, Chu Liangliang, Cheng Xue, Ming Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerinatal is a special and critical period in female life, maternal in the perinatal vulnerable to various physiological, psychological and social stress, perinatal vulnerability rate is higher, seriously affect the physical and mental health, existing research pay more attention to a single stage, and more qualitative research, the lack of cross-stage comparison based on the same cohort, focus on perinatal vulnerability, help to reduce the risk of maternal and infant adverse outcomes.ObjectiveTo explore the differences in perinatal vulnerability between 28 and 42 weeks and 1 postpartum.MethodsThis study was designed as a repeated-measure observational investigation. A convenience sample of 180 pregnant women receiving regular antenatal care or hospitalized in the obstetrics department of a tertiary hospital in Shandong from January to April 2025 was recruited. Data were collected at two time points (28–42 weeks of gestation and 1 week postpartum) using a general information questionnaire, the Visual Analogue Scale, the Fatigue Scale−14, the Personal Mastery Scale, and the Family APGAR Index. Data analysis was performed with SPSS 25.0.ResultsA total of 178 valid questionnaires were collected (effective response rate:98.9%). Perinatal vulnerability scores at one week postpartum (6.79 ± 1.63) were significantly higher than those at 28–42 weeks of gestation (6.43 ± 1.68) (P < 0.05). At both time points, fatigue was positively correlated with vulnerability (T1:r = 0.290; T2:r = 0.250, P < 0.01), while personal mastery and family care were negatively correlated with vulnerability (P < 0.05). Multiple linear regression indicated that the main influencing factors for vulnerability at 28–42 weeks of gestation included education level, monthly household income, history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, parity, fatigue, personal mastery, and family care (R²=0.280, F = 8.234, P < 0.001). At one week postpartum, the main influencing factors were place of residence, employment status, monthly household income, fatigue, personal mastery, and family care (R² =0.298, F = 8.956, P < 0.001).ConclusionPerinatal vulnerability escalates postpartum, with stage-specific risk factors. Tailored interventions addressing socio-demographic, psychological, and social support factors are essential to mitigate vulnerability and enhance maternal-infant outcomes.Keywords: Maternal health, Perinatal vulnerability, Fatigue, Personal mastery, Family care, Influencing factors

Contribution of this study to the existing literature

Most existing literature on perinatal vulnerability primarily focuses on isolated stages of pregnancy or postpartum, predominantly employing qualitative research methods. These studies lack quantitative tracking of dynamic changes in perinatal vulnerability, making it challenging to reveal evolutionary patterns across different perinatal stages or identify variations in influencing factors. This study not only validates the objective pattern of worsening perinatal vulnerability over time, but also fills the gap in longitudinal data on vulnerability changes from pregnancy through early postpartum, providing a foundation for future research.

Introduction

The perinatal period is defined as the period from the 28th week of gestation to the first week after delivery, encompassing a delicate phase of physiological and psychological changes for the mother, and representing a critical interval with elevated health risks for both mother and infant [1]. During this stage, pregnant and postpartum women often undergo significant physical and psychological adjustments and are susceptible to various stress factors, making it a specific period of psychological crisis [2]. Among these, psychological stresses such as anxiety and depression not only threaten the health of the mother but may also negatively affect fetal development and family functioning, necessitating multidimensional attention and intervention [3]. With the refinement of perinatal care systems and shifts in medical models, the psychological well-being of pregnant and postpartum women during the perinatal period has received increasing attention. Perinatal vulnerability refers to a state in which pregnant and postpartum women, influenced by physical, psychological, cognitive, and social risk factors, exhibit heightened sensitivity to health risks and a relative insufficiency in coping capacity during the perinatal period [4]. This concept encompasses multiple dimensions. At the physiological level, perinatal women often experience varying degrees of fatigue, which may lead to adverse maternal and infant health outcomes [5]. If such fatigue is not effectively alleviated through self-regulation, it can persist and develop into postpartum fatigue [6]. This may further result in delayed physical recovery [7, 8], reduced quality of life, prolonged lactation onset [9], decreased breastfeeding willingness, and difficulties in newborn care, thereby increasing physiological vulnerability. At the psychological and social levels, pregnant and postpartum women often face an imbalance between available personal and social resources and their needs during perinatal care. They also encounter numerous uncertainties and stressors, such as fear of labor pain, concerns about fetal health, and psychological challenges related to role transition, all of which can exacerbate perinatal vulnerability [10]. Studies [11, 12] indicate that up to 78.33% of pregnant and postpartum women experience perinatal vulnerability, which not only affects their mental health but may also prolong labor, increase the risk of preterm birth, and have long-term impacts on neonatal development as well as maternal-infant bonding. Currently, research on perinatal vulnerability in the international literature has focused primarily on qualitative investigations of experiences and perceptions, and has led to the development of nursing quality indicators and clinical guidelines for perinatal vulnerability [13–15]. However, quantitative studies remain relatively scarce, and the dynamic changes in perinatal vulnerability across different stages still require in-depth exploration. To address this gap, this study selects three key dimensions for analysis: fatigue, personal mastery, and family care. Fatigue, as a common psychophysiological symptom, directly reflects the physical and mental load of pregnant and postpartum women; personal mastery represents an individual’s psychological resources for coping with stress and serves as an important internal buffer against vulnerability; and family care reflects the core of external support systems, directly influencing adaptation and recovery. Together, these dimensions provide a multidimensional framework for understanding perinatal vulnerability, contributing to a comprehensive identification of its underlying mechanisms. Moreover, this study was conducted in Shandong, China, and its findings should be understood within the specific sociocultural and healthcare context. In China, the perinatal care system is predominantly hospital-centered, with a high coverage of regular prenatal checkups, which helps ensure the monitoring of physical health. However, psychosocial support and continuity of care systems are still under development. At the same time, traditional customs such as the practice of “confinement period”, high societal expectations of motherhood, and family-centered collectivist values profoundly shape how pregnant and postpartum women perceive support needs, cope with stress, and attribute their own vulnerability. Within this context, exploring the impact of factors such as fatigue, personal mastery, and family support on perinatal vulnerability holds significant importance for developing interventions that align with local cultural and psychological characteristics. This study employs Visual Analogue Scales (VAS) through questionnaire surveys to assess maternal vulnerability during these two critical phases. It aims to (1) characterize vulnerability profiles at 28–42 gestational weeks and the first postpartum week, and (2) identify stage-specific influencing factors from three dimensions: perinatal fatigue, personal mastery, and family support. The findings may facilitate early identification of high-risk populations, inform interdisciplinary interventions integrating psychological support and social services, and guide healthcare resource allocation (e.g., expanding prenatal care accessibility in underserved areas) and public health policymaking. Ultimately, this research seeks to enhance perinatal care equity, improve maternal-infant health outcomes, reduce vulnerability severity, and provide theoretical foundations for optimizing perinatal healthcare systems.

Methods

Design and study subjects

This repeated-measures observational study adopted a convenience sampling method and recruited pregnant and postpartum women who attended regular prenatal check-ups or were hospitalized in the obstetrics department of a tertiary grade A hospital (the highest level in China’s hospital classification system) in Shandong between January and April 2025.Inclusion criteria: (1) pregnant women who meet the perinatal range (28–42 weeks of gestation and 1 week postpartum).(2) Age > 18 years old; (3) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) a history of mental illness, family history, or application of psychotropic drugs; (2) Individuals with severe communication barriers (such as severe auditory, linguistic, or cognitive impairments) that prevent them from completing the assessments or participating adequately in the study. Sample size calculation method: According to the requirements for sample size in multiple regression analysis, it is generally recommended that the sample size be 5–10 times the number of independent variables to ensure statistical power and reduce the risk of model overfitting [16]. There were 15 independent variables in this study, and considering a sample loss rate of 20% determined a final sample size of 180 cases.

Measurements

General data questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was developed by the researchers, including general demographic characteristics (e.g., age, residence, marital status, educational level, BMI, monthly family income, medical expenses, work status) and obstetric related data (e.g., pregnancy complications, adverse pregnancy history, labor status).

Visual Analogue Scale(VAS)

The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was developed by Huskisson [17] in 1974 and originally used for pain assessment and is now used in pregnant women to evaluate their perinatal vulnerability [18]. The total score of the scale is 0 to 10,0 indicates feeling not fragile, and 10 means feeling very fragile. Specifically, scores of 1–3 indicate mild vulnerability, 4–6 indicate moderate vulnerability, and 7–9 indicate severe vulnerability [19]. This tool requires pregnant women to use a scale of 0 ~ 10 to answer the perinatal vulnerability situation, which is differs from the scoring method of traditional multi-item scales (e.g., Likert scales), so the reliability and validity test cannot be carried out.

Fatigue scale (fatigue scale−14, FS−14)

The Fatigue Rating Scale is used to assess the fatigue symptoms and their severity in participants. It was developed by Chalder in 1993 and [20] translated it into Chinese. The scale includes two dimensions: mental fatigue (8 items) and physical fatigue (6 items), totaling 14 items. Items with positive scoring are scored as 1 point for “yes” and 0 points for “no”; items 10,13, and 14 are scored with reverse scoring, with a total score ranging from 0 to 14 points. Higher scores indicate more severe fatigue, and a score of ≥ 3 indicates the presence of fatigue. The scale has good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.809 and a split-half reliability of 0.676 [21].

Personal Mastery Scale( PMS)

The scale was developed by Pearlin and Schooler [22] and later translated and adapted into Chinese by Yu Yibing and Zou Hong [23]. It is a single dimensional scale with a total of 7 items. The Likert 5-level scoring method is adopted, with 1 to 5 points from “full inconformity” to “full compliance”. The total score was 7 to 35, and the higher the score, the stronger the personal sense of control. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.810, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.787.

Family care scale (family APGAR index, APGAR)

The Family Care scale was compiled by Smilkstein G [24] to assess the care level of the family. The scale includes family fitness, cooperation, length, affection and intimacy into five aspects, a total of five items, each item using 0 ~ 2 scoring method, from “rarely” to “often” in turn 0 ~ 2 points, the total score of 0 ~ 10 points, the higher the score said respondents family care degree is higher, 0 ~ 3 into serious family dysfunction, 4 ~ 6 is divided into moderate family dysfunction, 7 ~ 10 divided into good family function. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.729.

Data collection

A questionnaire survey was conducted. Two trained investigators distributed paper-based questionnaires to eligible participants during late pregnancy (28–42 weeks) and within one week postpartum. Before the survey, standardized instructions were given to explain the purpose, significance, and completion method of the study. After obtaining signed informed consent, the questionnaires were distributed. Participants completed the questionnaires independently and anonymously. After completion, the investigators checked the questionnaires for any missing items, which were filled in on the spot before collection. A total of 180 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 178 valid questionnaires were collected. Two of the questionnaires were excluded due to logical inconsistencies in the responses, resulting in a final count of 178 valid questionnaires and an effective recovery rate of 98.9%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, Q-Q plots, kurtosis, and skewness coefficients. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Independent-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used for univariate analysis of perinatal vulnerability. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between vulnerability and fatigue, personal mastery, and family care. Multivariate linear regression analysis was employed to identify influencing factors of perinatal vulnerability. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No.: YXLL-KY−2025(175)). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit eligible pregnant and postpartum women from the obstetrics outpatient and inpatient departments of a tertiary grade-A hospital in Shandong Province between January and April 2025. Prior to the study, the investigators provided detailed explanations to all potential participants regarding the research purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, the right to voluntary participation and withdrawal at any time, as well as data confidentiality measures. All participants provided written informed consent after fully understanding and agreeing to the terms. The questionnaires were completed anonymously, and the collected data were de-identified for statistical analysis, used solely for academic purposes. Refusal to participate or withdrawal from the study would not affect any medical services or rights of the participants at the hospital.

Discussion

Analysis of perinatal vulnerability status in women

This study observed significantly higher vulnerability scores at one week postpartum compared to 28–42 weeks of gestation, reflecting an increased vulnerability risk in the early postpartum period. This aligns with the findings of Fontein [18]. Analyzing the reasons from a biopsychosocial perspective: at the physiological level, pregnant women undergo a series of physiological changes under the influence of pregnancy hormones—such as back pain, frequent urination, and uterine enlargement—to support fetal growth and development [25]. During pregnancy, although expectant mothers face sustained physiological demands, the body gradually enters a stage of compensatory adaptation. In contrast, one week after delivery, postpartum women experience multiple stressors including birth-related exhaustion, abrupt hormonal shifts, and tissue repair. These factors can lead to delayed recovery and accumulated fatigue, thereby exacerbating feelings of vulnerability [26]. Negative psychological factors such as stress and depression can contribute to the occurrence of perinatal vulnerability [10]. Research [27, 28] indicates that the incidence of postpartum depression is significantly higher than during late pregnancy, while postpartum role adaptation, parenting anxiety, and emotional regulation difficulties may further amplify psychological vulnerability. At the social level, the gap between the actual effectiveness of postpartum support systems and maternal expectations can also intensify feelings of helplessness [10]. Therefore, perinatal care should consistently address psychosocial adaptation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. It is essential to strengthen comprehensive assessment and support in the early postpartum phase, timely identify factors influencing perinatal vulnerability at different stages, and implement targeted interventions accordingly.

Sociodemographic variables and perinatal vulnerability

This study confirms that monthly household income is a common influencing factor for vulnerability at both 28–42 weeks of pregnancy and 1 week postpartum, with the low-income group (monthly income 3,000–5,999 RMB) showing a higher risk of vulnerability, consistent with the report by Molenaar et al. [29].Pregnant individuals from low-income families often experience financial stress, which may restrict their access to high-quality medical and social resources, thereby compromising perinatal care quality and psychological well-being [30]. Whereas, individuals from high-income backgrounds are generally buffered from these risks [31]. Therefore, clinical healthcare providers should strengthen resource linkage and social support for low-income families. Educational level, adverse pregnancy history, and childbirth are independent influencing factors for vulnerability at 28–42 weeks of pregnancy. One possible explanation is that pregnant women with lower educational attainment may have poorer health management ability and psychological resilience. This can impair their ability not only to cope with prenatal psychological stress effectively but also to access and comprehend essential pregnancy-related health information (e.g., on nutrition, the importance of prenatal examinations, and danger signs), thereby contributing to cognitive vulnerability. Songco [32] et al. explored whether there was consistency in cognitive vulnerability characteristics between pregnant women and non-pregnant women, and the results showed that the personal cognition of pregnant women was more likely to affect perinatal vulnerability. Therefore, when receiving perinatal women with low education level, medical workers should try to avoid consultation with medical terms and carry out vivid, easy to understand and various forms of health education. In this study, women with a history of adverse pregnancy were significantly more vulnerable at 28–42 weeks of gestation than at 1 week postpartum, which may be compared with women with a history of adverse pregnancy and often leave psychological trauma. After getting pregnant again, the past painful experiences tend to come to mind, leading them to fear and worry about the pregnancy, fearing that similar tragedies will happen again. This psychological state is similar to post-traumatic stress disorder, which can make pregnant women more emotionally sensitive and vulnerable during pregnancy. Wang Ruiying [33] et al. pointed out that perinatal women with a history of adverse pregnancy are in the stress-adaptive state of pregnancy for a long time, and are prone to self-regulation fatigue, which is difficult to recover from the continuous fatigue state, and finally form a vicious circle. This study confirms that first-time mothers exhibit significantly higher levels of vulnerability during 28–42 weeks of pregnancy compared to multiparous women. Women experiencing their first pregnancy undergo a major physiological transition from a non-pregnant state, which makes them more susceptible to stress reactions than multiparous women. Moreover, due to a lack of childbirth and parenting experience, along with challenges in adapting to their new maternal role, first-time mothers generally have lower awareness of pregnancy-related changes. Their younger age further contributes to a higher risk of perinatal psychological vulnerability [34]. The study also identifies place of residence and employment status as independent factors influencing vulnerability at one week postpartum. Postpartum women living in rural areas are at a higher risk of vulnerability. Within a gendered health system, women often bear greater family care responsibilities while having access to fewer economic and social resources. This imbalance between resources and responsibilities is particularly pronounced during the perinatal period [35]. In rural areas, maternal health knowledge is limited due to regional economic development, resulting in insufficient perinatal healthcare promotion. Maternal parenting skills often rely on family experience rather than modern medical guidance. Additionally, rural mothers have little exposure to postpartum psychological adjustment knowledge and lack coping strategies for depressive emotions, making them more vulnerable. This is linked to disparities in medical resources and health education penetration across regions. This study found that employment status has a complex impact on maternal vulnerability at one week postpartum. Results indicate that long working hours during pregnancy may delay postpartum recovery and increase the risk of vulnerability, partially aligning with the views of Grenier et al. [36]. Pregnancy is physically demanding, especially in the mid-to-late stages, with physiological changes such as fetal growth, weight gain, frequent urination, and lower limb edema. Physical vulnerability thus becomes a prolonged symptom throughout childbirth [26], delaying the recovery of bodily functions. However, some studies [37] suggest that longer working hours during pregnancy may enhance psychological resilience, thereby reducing postpartum vulnerability risk. This contradiction indicates that the impact of employment on perinatal vulnerability requires more detailed exploration, considering factors such as job nature, social support, and personal resources.

Fatigue, personal mastery, family care, and perinatal vulnerability

This study reports that maternal fatigue scores were higher at one week postpartum compared to 28–42 weeks of pregnancy, and fatigue at both stages was positively correlated with perinatal vulnerability. Fatigue is a persistent, complex physiological state involving physical and psychological discomfort and is influenced by multiple factors [38, 39]. Research [40, 41] indicates that fatigue in late pregnancy can impair cognitive function and self-care abilities, and may even affect the delivery process. If prenatal fatigue is not alleviated, postpartum fatigue may persist or worsen, thereby exacerbating perinatal vulnerability through mind–body interactions. Specifically, physical fatigue can trigger or intensify emotional issues such as anxiety and depression, while prolonged fatigue may reduce a mother’s capacity to cope with childcare and postpartum recovery, creating a vicious cycle of physical and psychological strain [42]. This renders individuals more vulnerable when facing perinatal challenges. Our findings support the view of Bakker et al. [43] that perinatal vulnerability is a significant predictor of adverse physical and mental states after childbirth. Therefore, healthcare providers should emphasize early identification and intervention for perinatal vulnerability, particularly in the postpartum phase. This study also confirms that personal mastery scores were lower at one week postpartum than at 28–42 weeks of pregnancy, and mastery at both stages was negatively correlated with perinatal vulnerability. This aligns with Pearlin’s theory [22] on the regulatory role of mastery in the stress process. Personal mastery is an important psychological resource that influences maternal emotions, behavior, and physiological state [44]. Within the biopsychosocial model, personal mastery can be regarded as a key factor in coping with multiple perinatal stressors. The transition from pregnancy to postpartum involves physiological changes, shifts in family roles, newborn care, and self-recovery [31]. Pregnant women and new mothers with lower personal mastery may experience anxiety and helplessness due to insufficient self-efficacy and lack of confidence in their bodily changes and fetal development. They may tend to rely passively on others in situations such as birth-related decision-making. After childbirth, they may struggle to adapt to the maternal role, feel overwhelmed by newborn care, and experience frustration and helplessness, thereby aggravating their perinatal vulnerability. Furthermore, our research reveals that family support scores were higher at one week postpartum than at 28–42 weeks of pregnancy, and family support at both stages was negatively correlated with perinatal vulnerability. This is consistent with Hamelin‑Brabant et al. [45], who emphasize the crucial role of social support in the postpartum period. From the perspective of physiological and psychological transition, postpartum women often face multiple pressures, including physical recovery, hormonal fluctuations, and newborn care. During this time, family support becomes more prominent in providing direct care and emotional connection. This may be closely related to the traditional Chinese practice of “doing the month” (zuo yuezi), during which family members concentrate care on the mother [46]. Such support alleviates feelings of vulnerability to some extent and serves as a primary channel through which external support systems directly influence vulnerability.

Limitations

The relationships of fatigue, personal mastery, family care and perinatal vulnerability between 28 and 42 weeks of pregnancy and 1 week postpartum still have the following limitations: firstly, this study focused solely on three variables—fatigue, personal mastery, and family care—and examined perinatal vulnerability changes at only two time points, excluding common perinatal psychological factors like anxiety and depression. This limitation may restrict the comprehensiveness of the findings. Future research should incorporate more psychosocial variables and extend the time span to systematically reveal the multidimensional factors influencing perinatal vulnerability and its evolving patterns across different time periods; secondly, the assessment tool for perinatal vulnerability used in this study is the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). While this scale is easy to understand, simple to score, and time-efficient, it is a single-item subjective measure. Respondents are asked to rate their perinatal vulnerability on a 0–10 scale, which differs from traditional multi-item scoring methods. Consequently, it may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of vulnerability, nor is it suitable for reliability and validity testing. In future studies, a targeted perinatal vulnerability questionnaire could be developed to further assess perinatal vulnerability in pregnant and postpartum women; thirdly, this study was conducted within a specific sociocultural context in China, with the sample drawn from a single hospital in Shandong Province. Traditional practices such as zuo yuezi (the month-long postpartum confinement) have shaped a culturally distinct pattern of family support for postpartum women, which may influence how participants perceive and report perinatal vulnerability. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to non-Chinese or non-Asian populations, limiting the broader applicability of the results. Future research should therefore aim to encompass diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds by adopting multicenter, large-sample designs and integrating multiple research methodologies. Such approaches would facilitate a more comprehensive investigation into the influencing factors and underlying mechanisms of perinatal vulnerability, thereby providing more robust theoretical support and practical guidance for perinatal care.

Conclusion

Perinatal women exhibit a sense of vulnerability as early as 28–42 weeks of gestation, and this vulnerability tends to intensify one week after delivery. The unique contribution of this study lies in its repeated-measures design, which systematically reveals differences in the influencing factors of perinatal vulnerability between the two stages—28–42 weeks of gestation and one week postpartum. Specifically, it identifies lower education level, household monthly income, history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, and parity as key factors during late pregnancy, while employment status during pregnancy, place of residence, and household monthly income emerge as significant influences in the postpartum period. These findings reinforce the perspective that perinatal vulnerability is dynamic and stage-specific, providing a basis for targeted interventions at different phases. In clinical practice, it is recommended to establish a dual-phase assessment pathway covering both the antenatal and postnatal periods. During pregnancy, emphasis should be placed on screening individuals with low personal mastery, inadequate support, or a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, and providing psychological empowerment and family-involved guidance. After delivery, efforts should be strengthened in physical recovery support, fatigue management, and enhancing social resource connectivity for women in rural areas. At the policy level, structured psychosocial support programs could be integrated into maternal health service packages, with particular focus on resource-limited regions, to better meet the perinatal healthcare needs of women and improve overall quality of care.
