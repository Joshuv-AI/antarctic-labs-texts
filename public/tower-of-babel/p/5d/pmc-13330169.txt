Extending the starter-unit selection of a collaborating non-reducing polyketide synthase through rational domain swaps
Authors: Ruya Yin, Mengwei Zhang, Ziqi Zhai, Yifei Qin, Xingrui Liang, Dan Xu, Ligang Zhou, Daowan Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungal non-reducing polyketide synthases (NR-PKSs) use starter-unit acyl transferase (SAT) for selection of the starter unit. Here, we swapped a C6-loading SAT of the sorbicillin NR-PKS with an acetyl-loading SAT, resulting in the generation of non-native aromatic polyketides with a shorter chain length. This extends the starter-unit selection of a collaborating NR-PKS to C2. A general rule was summarized to predict the product chain-length after SAT-exchange. This study provides new insights towards the chain-length control in NR-PKS programming.

Fungal polyketides are intriguing metabolites with diverse structures and bioactivities, ranging from pharmaceuticals to mycotoxins. They are mostly biosynthesized by iterative type I polyketide synthases (iT1PKSs). iT1PKSs are multidomain megaenzymes that can be classified as non-reducing (NR), partially reducing (PR), or highly reducing (HR) PKSs according to different domain organizations.1 Among them, NR-PKSs do not have any modification on β-carbonyl, resulting in the generation of poly-β-ketones or aromatic products. NR-PKSs encompass multiple catalytic domains including starter-unit acyl transferase (SAT), β-ketoacyl synthase (KS), acyl transferase (AT), product template (PT), acyl carrier protein (ACP) domains, product-releasing domains, and the nonobligate C-methyltransferase (CMT). Among them, SAT and PT are unique to the fungal NR-PKS. The SAT domain has been demonstrated to exhibit selectivity for starter units, initiating polyketide biosynthesis by transferring the starter unit to the ACP domain.2 The KS domain extends the starter unit to a fixed length, and then, the PT domain catalyzes specific aldol cyclization(s) and aromatization, followed by product release by the action of product-releasing domains, such as thioesterase (TE) or reductase (R).3,4 Moreover, sophisticated combinational domain-swap experiments have provided insights into the rules governing NR-PKS programming.5,6

SAT domain exchange is useful in generating new aromatic polyketides. For example, replacing the SAT domain (C8 acyl-primed) of AfoE with StcA_SAT (C6 acyl-primed) led to the production of a new aromatic product with the same chain length (C16) as that of the native AfoE product (Fig. 1A), suggesting that the chain length might be controlled by the KS and PT domains.7 Further replacement of the SAT domain of AfoE with AN3386, natively primed with a C10 starter unit, gave rise to a major product still with the same chain length (C16) as the natural AfoE product, and a minor product with two more carbon atoms (Fig. 1A).8

Acetyl unit is the most commonly seen starter in NR-PKSs; however, productive biosynthesis has not yet been achieved by swapping it with the SAT of NR-PKSs that accepts complex starter units from partner HR-PKS or FAS (fatty acid synthase). For example, exchanging the AfoE_SAT (C8-starter unit) with eight SAT domain donors associated with the acetyl starter unit resulted in unproductive hybrid NR-PKSs (Fig. 1A).8 Nevertheless, it is unclear whether the chimeric NR-PKS is productive or not when the C6-primed SAT is swapped with the acetyl-loading SAT. We reasoned that the less difference in the size of the starter units (C6vs. C2) would maximize the chances for successful interactions between noncognate SAT and KS, thereby leading to a productive hybrid NR-PKS.

For this purpose, the sorbicillin PKSs caught our attention,9–12 which include the HR-PKS UvSorA and the NR-PKS UvSorB (Fig. 1B). UvSorB has a SAT-KS-AT-PT-ACP-CMT-R domain organization, and it accepts a sorboyl starter unit (C6 acyl) biosynthesized by UvSorA, extends the starter intermediate three times with malonyl CoA, and methylates it twice by the CMT; this is followed by reductive release through the R domain to afford an aldehyde, which undergoes a Knoevenagel cyclization reaction to generate the phenol product, sorbicillin (Fig. 1B).

In this study, the acetyl-CoA loading SAT of the YWA1 PKS (UvPKS1),9 which has a SAT-KS-AT-PT-ACP-TE domain architecture (Fig. 1B), was used as a SAT donor. The exchange of the SAT domain of UvPKS1 and UvSorB was performed to create hybrid NR-PKSs, namely, UvPKS1* and UvSorB* (Fig. 1B). To achieve this, the SAT domain of UvPKS1 and UvSorB were determined by sequence alignments with homologous PKSs whose SAT domains were known.13 As a result, the SAT domain of UvPKS1 was clearly assigned (1∼378 AA) (Fig. S1, see ESI†), while three possible SAT-KS linker sites of UvSorB were predicted (Fig. S2) by aligning with the SAT domain of CazM14 (P395), StcA7 (T363), and PksA2 (G370), which natively loads 4-methyl-2-hexenoyl, hexanoyl, and hexanoyl starter unit, respectively. The three versions of UvSorB_SAT (i.e., SAT1-377, SAT1-384, and SAT1-397) were swapped with that of UvPKS1_SAT, resulting in six hybrid PKSs, named UvPKS1*-1∼3, and UvSorB*-1∼3 (Fig. 1B). The hybrid UvSorB* was expressed in A. oryzae alone, while UvPKS1* was co-expressed with UvSorA in A. oryzae, which supplied the sorboyl starter unit in vivo.

The metabolic profiles of each chimeric PKS were analyzed by HPLC-MS. Among these chimera, no products could be detected for the UvPKS1* hybrids (Fig. S7), possibly due to the fact that the native UvPKS1 is a standalone NR-PKS, which is not like the collaborating NR-PKS (UvSorB) that accepts a starter from its partner HR-PKS or FAS. One of the many possible reasons for the unproductive chimera might be that an incompatible interaction occurred between the surrogated SAT and the downstream (native) KS or other domains, which required future investigations. Also, to our knowledge, no previous reports can be found in the literature describing the productive transformation of a standalone NR-PKS into a collaborating one by SAT domain exchange alone, although a recent work by Zhang and co-workers reconfigured the MonAzP NR-PKS to collaborate with an HR-PKS by replacing its SAT domain, which gave rise to pyrones exclusively, the derailed products of the PKS assembling line.15

On the contrary, all the three UvSorB* hybrids produced detectable new products (1a and 1b) (Fig. 2), with 1a being the most abundant one. Comparison of the expression level of the hybrid PKS by qRT-PCR (Fig. S5) as well as the production of 1a (Fig. S6) revealed that the UvSorB*-1 chimera was the most efficient one among the three hybrid UvSorB* PKSs. The reduced compound yield in UvSorB*-2 and −3 was consistent with their significantly lower transcription level compared to that of UvSorB*-1 (Fig. S5). Among these chimera, UvSorB*-1 (UvPKS11−378:UvSorB378-2641) has almost the same length as the native UvSorB1-2641, while UvSorB*-2 and −3 were 6 and 19 AA shorter than that of the native UvSorB, respectively, which might explain the better performance of UvSorB*-1.

The new products were then isolated and purified from the large-scale fermentation of UvSorB*-1 but with low yield. Ca. 4 mg of 1a and 1 mg of 1b were isolated from a total of 45 liters of culture media. We further optimized the production of these metabolites by adding various macroporous adsorption resins to the media (Table S5 and Fig. S4). As a result, the polar S-8 resin was found to display the highest adsorption effect, where the yield of 1a was increased by 3.2-fold compared to the no-resin control. Subsequently, a large-scale re-fermentation with the addition of S-8 resin greatly improved the isolated yield of 1a (0.35 mg L−1) and 1b (0.17 mg L−1). With sufficient quantity in hand, the structure of these products was identified by a combined analysis of the NMR and HRMS spectra.

Compound 1a has a molecular formula of C12H16O4, as determined by HRESIMS (Fig. S9). The 1H NMR spectrum of 1a (Fig. S11) revealed the presence of one aromatic proton (δH 7.47), one oxygenated methine (δH 4.32), one methylene (δH 3.12, 2.94, each dd), two olefinic methyl (δH 2.16, 2.05), and one doublet methyl (δH 1.25, d) groups. This suggested the presence of a penta-substituted benzene ring, similar to that of sorbicillin. The final structure of 1a was elucidated by further analysis of the 13C NMR and HMBC spectra (Fig. S12, S13 and Table S6). It was a new compound, which shared a same core structure as sorbicillin, the native product of UvSorB, but with a side chain shortened by two carbon units (Scheme 1). The specific rotation value of 1a is zero, implying it is a racemate.

The structure of 1b was determined to be a chromone derivative, related to 1a (Scheme 1A), by spectroscopic analysis (Table S7 and Fig. S14–S18).

The presence of 1a and 1b hinted the formation of a 9-keto product (1) from the chimeric PKS, which gave rise to 1avia hydrogenation, catalyzed by endogenous reductase(s), or 1bvia dehydration. We deduce that UvSorB* accepts an acetyl starter unit, which is extended three rounds with malonyl-CoA and methylated once at C4 (labeling according to the final product), before a further round of extension and methylation at C2 to give a polyketide intermediate with a 10-carbon backbone (not including the methyl groups). This is followed by a reductive release catalyzed by the R domain, and a Knoevenagel cyclization to generate 1 (Scheme 1A).

It was found that the PksA_SAT preferred the native hexanoyl starter unit over the acetyl one by a difference of 8.9 fold in transacylase rate;2 on the contrary, the WA SAT preferred the native acetyl starter unit over the hexanoyl one by 57.3 fold.13 The results found in this study was consistent with such a preference, though it might have subtle differences in the C6-acyl between sorboyl and hexanoyl.

Previous works have demonstrated that the chain-length of the SAT-swap product is the same as the native product of acceptor PKS when the primed acyl groups were differed only by two carbons in size.7,8 In this study, a productive switch of a non-acetyl-loading SAT to an acetyl-loading SAT was achieved for the first time. Our results revealed that the chain-length does not keep constant to the native product when the donor and acceptor SAT are primed with acyl groups that differed in size by more than two carbons. Notably, the chain length is controlled by KS to a larger extent, as PT is inactive in SorB, different from the previous proposal.7

Based on these and previous results, we proposed a general rule to predict the product and its chain-length of hybrid NR-PKSs created by SAT exchange (Fig. 3). It has a prerequisite that the recipient should be a collaborating NR-PKS, rather than a standalone NR-PKS. As could be seen from this figure (Fig. 3A), it could be deduced that KS first scrutinizes the surrogated starter unit to check if this unit is shorter than the native one (C2vs. C6 herein; C6vs. C8 in ref. 7), and then, one round of extension (at most) will be initiated to elongate the starter, before further programming; if this unit is longer than the native one (C10vs. C8 in ref. 8), either the first extension will be skipped before further process to retain the chain length in most cases or the elongation and programming is not affected in some cases (minor product). Since exchange between C8 and C2 is effortless,8 to achieve a productive chimera PKS, the difference between the size of the acyl starters has to be not greater than four (Fig. 3B).

Generation of hybrid NR-PKSs by replacing the native SAT domain with surrogated SATs (A), and a general rule to predict the productivity of a chimera NR-PKS after SAT-exchange (B). Ls and Ln denote chain-length (L) of the acyl starter unit primed by surrogated and native SATs, respectively.

Interestingly, despite that the chain length of the product (compound 1) of the hybrid PKS is less than the native one by one acetate unit, methylation occurs exactly at the same position (C2 and C4, numbering from the end of a polyketide chain) (Scheme 1). Since it is known that the chain extension by KS and the C-methylation by CMT is a competing process, and the methylation pattern is primarily controlled by the CMT,16 it is unclear how these two domains cooperate to assemble the final product in this case, which requires further investigation in the future. Finally, the increasing knowledge of the exact programming in NR-PKS will undoubtedly facilitate the engineering of new nonreduced polyketides.
