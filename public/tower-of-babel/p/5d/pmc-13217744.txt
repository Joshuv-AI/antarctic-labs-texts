How can community pharmacists support people living with eating disorders? identifying evidence gaps and practice opportunities in Australia
Authors: Elissar Mansour, Claire L. O’Reilly, Stephen Touyz, Sarah Maguire, Sarira El-Den
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eating disorders (EDs) are mental illnesses which greatly contribute to disease burden. While binge-eating disorder is the most common ED, anorexia nervosa accounts for one of the most life-threatening mental illnesses. People living with EDs often wait years before receiving a diagnosis and medical support, consequently experiencing poorer health outcomes and longer hospital admissions. As frontline healthcare workers, pharmacists are amongst the most accessible and trusted healthcare professionals. Pharmacists’ roles in mental healthcare are expanding with recent evidence demonstrating their contribution to mental health screening and medication management. Pharmacies are frequently visited by the community, reflecting the potential opportunities for pharmacists to be trained to engage with and provide support to people experiencing EDs and their carers. Furthermore, considering people living with EDs have altered pharmacokinetics and may benefit from tailored medication counselling and advice, pharmacists’ expertise in medicines and their skills in supporting medicine use may be especially important in this population group. This commentary highlights evidence gaps, and ways trained pharmacists may contribute to supporting people living with EDs. It is evident that there is a need for research exploring pharmacists’ current contribution to ED care, the co-design of training to upskill the community pharmacy workforce in providing ED care, and the feasibility and impact of pharmacist-delivered support for people experiencing EDs.

Introduction

An eating disorder (ED) is an umbrella term for conditions affecting a person’s eating or eating-related behaviours which are accompanied by significant distressing thoughts and emotions often leading to physical health deteriorations [1]. EDs are prevalent globally in both women and men, with approximately 9% of the American population [2] and 4.5% of the Australian population [3] living with an ED. Anorexia nervosa is one of the most life-threatening mental health conditions and studies have shown an increasing trend in EDs globally with a surge during the COVID-19 pandemic [4, 5]. Binge -eating disorder is concerningly on the rise in Australia, with a six-fold increase in its prevalence from 1995 to 2015 according to a longitudinal study conducted in South Australia [6]. Despite their prevalence, a recent systematic review showed that EDs are often left untreated between 2 and 3 years for anorexia nervosa and 4–6 years for binge-eating disorder and bulimia nervosa [7]. This is a significant concern given that poorer outcomes are observed the longer EDs are left untreated [8]. Furthermore, worsening clinical symptoms are associated with an increase in use of healthcare resources leading to higher direct and indirect costs [3]. The treatment of EDs has a considerable economic impact associated with healthcare costs and reduced wellbeing, with EDs costing approximately $4.7 billion in Australia [3] and $64.7 billion in the United States (US) each year [9]. While general practitioners (GPs) are well placed to address EDs, recent data suggests that only a fraction of EDs are addressed in these settings [10]. As such it is important to consider how other members of the primary healthcare team can help contribute to the care of people living with EDs.

Pharmacists are internationally considered as some of the most accessible and trusted primary healthcare professionals [11, 12]. For example, 89.2% of people living in England live within a 20-minute walk of a pharmacy [13]. In line with this, the average person visits a pharmacy 18 times per year in Australia [14]. While often not recognised for their roles in supporting mental healthcare, pharmacists do have current and emerging roles in this area. For example, 85% of pharmacists in Canada and Australia have interacted with people at risk of suicide [15]. While there is need for further training, pharmacists are well-placed to assist people living with mental illnesses, with roles in medication safety and mental health screening [16]. There is minimal research exploring pharmacists’ roles in supporting people living with EDs, suggesting that their potential role in this area may be limited and not well-understood. This commentary focuses on identifying evidence gaps, highlighting the potential for pharmacists to contribute to ED identification and care and outlining the necessary training and system requirements needed for pharmacists to be involved in the multidisciplinary care of people living with EDs.

Barriers to care

People with EDs often go undiagnosed for many years, worsening their quality of life and prognostic outcomes [17]. Delays to diagnosis and management of appear to be due to patient-related factors in the early stages where patients have not yet recognised their symptoms [18], followed by service-level delays in later stages where patients are waiting for treatment [19]. In Australia, barriers to care include lack of knowledge of available treatment services by both patients and carers, long waitlists to access treatment and strict entry criteria, such as requiring specific body mass indices (BMIs) [20]. In the US, physicians have also reported finding it difficult to support people living with EDs due to lack of evidence-based pharmacological treatments, limited health services available, time constraints and discomfort [21]. Additionally, access to health resources is extremely limited in regional and remote areas, making it difficult for patients to access equitable care [20]. This is a common issue globally and often includes difficulty in accessing pharmacies as well. For example, 98.3% of the population in England living in urban areas live within 20 min of a pharmacy but only 18.9% of the population living in rural areas do [13]. However, in Australia, the Australian Institute for Health and Welfare highlights that for all health professionals, including GPs and psychologists, their distribution relative to the population was the lowest in smaller towns compared to other areas, except for pharmacists, thereby highlighting their accessibility and potential to help in early intervention in these communities [22].

To facilitate access to care in Australia, organisations, such as the Inside Out Institute and the Butterfly Foundation, have a database of ED-trained professionals including psychologists, dieticians, GPs, social workers, nurses, exercise physiologists and dentists; however, this does not include pharmacists [23, 24]. This is understandable given pharmacy education focusing on EDs has been minimal; however, the lack of sufficient education in tertiary curricula in relation to EDs is not unique to pharmacy with research from other disciplines also demonstrating this issue [25]. Nonetheless, with the appropriate training, evidence demonstrates that community pharmacists are capable of supporting people living with mental illnesses [26] including severe and persistent mental illnesses [27].

Early identification and screening in community pharmacies

Early intervention in mental health has often been associated with more positive health outcomes and reduced cost especially in psychosis [28] and more recently EDs [29, 30]. The First Episode Rapid Early Intervention for Eating Disorders service model, which aims to reduce service-based delays in accessing treatment, was shown to significantly improve clinical outcomes, such as faster weight restoration, reduction in hospital stays and increased cost savings per patient [31]. As acknowledged by the Australian National Eating Disorders Collaboration (NEDC) ‘a pharmacist is often the first health professional that a person with an ED will come in contact with’ [32]; hence, pharmacists who complete further training and education in EDs may have a unique health care encounter opportunity to help identify patients with possible EDs early and play a key role in linking them to treatment.

There are a number of ways community pharmacists may be able to identify warning signs (Table 1), such as the misuse of over the counter (OTC) medications (e.g. laxatives, diuretics or diet pills), physical signs (e.g. knuckle calluses or lanugo hair) or requests for OTC products which could indicate an attempt at managing an ED-induced complication (e.g. poor dental hygiene, sleep disturbances or gastrointestinal complaints). The NEDC has published a guide for pharmacists to help recognise EDs in the community and refer them accordingly [33]. However, it is unclear whether pharmacists are aware of this guide and the extent of uptake within the profession.

Preventative programs have been shown to reduce modifiable risk factors for EDs and improve eating behaviours in society with the most effective approaches being community-based [34]. According to the Australian Code of Ethics For Pharmacists, pharmacists are expected to promote and participate in public health initiatives [35]. Pharmacies have been home to a range of well-known public health campaigns such as those for smoking cessation, screening or “healthy living” services [36]. This can be extended to include body positivity and “health at every size” campaigns in the pharmacy to destigmatise, provide resources and encourage people with EDs to seek help. The Australian NEDC has a guide for effective prevention programs which include health promotion approaches focused on promoting a balanced relationship with nutrition and exercise, and a guide for the development of appropriate materials for consumers [37], all of which may be achievable in pharmacies and are within the scope of practice of pharmacists.

Additionally, pharmacists are involved in a range of screening services for conditions such as sleep apnoea through home screening kits [38], osteoporosis through bone mineral density scans [39] or skin cancer through skin checks [40]. In the context of mental health, a systematic review of 26 studies demonstrated that trained pharmacists can also perform risk assessments and mental health screening in a primary healthcare setting, as well as appropriately refer patients to the relevant health professionals, with most studies conducted focusing on depression and substance use disorders [41]. While multiple evidence-based ED screening tools, such as the Eating Disorder Screen for Primary Care (EDS-PC) [42] and the InsideOut Institute Screener (IOI-S) [43], have been validated for use in primary healthcare settings, there are no studies exploring their use in community pharmacies indicating the need for further research exploring pharmacist-delivered screening for EDs.

Medication management and support

The gold standard for ED treatment is psychological therapies such as enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy or Family Based Treatments [52]. There is only one medication, lisdexamphetamine, approved for the treatment of EDs in Australia for binge-eating disorder [53]. Being a controlled substance with strict legal requirements for dispensing in Australia, there are opportunities for pharmacists to counsel on use and monitor for possible misuse. Additionally, there are now increasing studies investigating the efficacy of glucagon-like-peptides-1 (GLP-1s), such as semaglutide, in both bulimia nervosa and binge -eating disorders. The evidence is conflicting as these medications have shown a reduction in binge-eating symptoms [54]. However, studies have yet to demonstrate its effect on recovery rates, with some evidence also showing misuse of GLP-1s for contraindicated weight loss [55]. GLP-1s are currently not registered for use for binge -eating disorder and bulimia nervosa in both the United States and Australia. Should GLP-1s receive Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and Therapeutics Good Administration (TGA) approval for EDs, pharmacists would be well placed to provide people with EDs necessary clinical information and support while considering both evidence for efficacy as well as risk of misuse.

A few psychotropic medications are recommended in the Australian Therapeutic Guidelines for off-label use in the context of EDs (e.g. antidepressants or topiramate for moderate to severe bulimia nervosa and binge -eating disorder) [56, 57]. A study consisting of 604 young women with different EDs showed that one-third took at least one psychotropic medication, such as antidepressants (25.9%), antipsychotics (7.5%) or anxiolytics (2.9%) used to manage both EDs and other comorbidities [58]. Nutritional supplements and vitamins are also often prescribed, notably in the context of preventing refeeding syndrome in patients with anorexia nervosa and other restrictive EDs [59]. Hence, it is evident that a range of medications are frequently used by people living with EDs, and there may be a range of opportunities for pharmacists to provide medication management and support. This would be especially important considering people with EDs were shown to have more negative attitudes regarding medications compared to people with other psychiatric conditions [60].

Pharmacists are well-placed to support medication use among people living with EDs by clarifying misconceptions relating to medications, tailoring counselling to improve adherence, advising on safe re-dosing or personalising a tailored dosing schedule in the context of purging and providing dosing advice specific to people living with EDs considering their fluctuating weights and possibly altered pharmacokinetics (Table 2). They may also play important roles in advising on and helping manage the side effects of medications, some of which may require unique considerations when caring for people living with EDs. For example, weight gain, with some antidepressants and antipsychotics, or gastrointestinal side effects, such as constipation/bloating with iron supplements, antidepressants and antipsychotics [61], could be specifically challenging for people with EDs [50]. Additionally, some medications may increase the risk of medical complications which are already compromised in people with EDs, such as increased QTc interval in patients with anorexia nervosa which is also seen with the use of some antidepressants [62]. As such, pharmacists are in an ideal position to contribute to holistic care by educating patients and advising prescribers on side effect management and considerations.

Examples of how trained pharmacists can support medication use in people with EDs

Psychotropic medications

Some medicines, such as amitriptyline, mirtazapine and many antipsychotics, may have the side effect of weight gain [63] which may be distressing for people with EDs.

Many antidepressants, notably selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, may increase the risk of suicide [64], which should be closely monitored especially in a population group that is already at higher risk [65].

Serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors may cause hyponatremia [66] in a population group which is at high risk of electrolyte disturbances [67].

Some medicines, such as fluoxetine, citalopram, tricyclic antidepressants, and some antipsychotics, may prolong QT interval, which people with anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa are already at risk of [68].

- Identification of contraindications, such as prolonged QT interval or electrolyte disturbances, to prevent further complications;

- Monitoring patients to reduce risk of complications from psychotropic medicines;

- Advising prescribers on medication choice and monitoring points for psychotropic medicines;

- Tailoring medication counselling to help alleviate anxiety around starting new psychotropic medicines; and/or

- Improving compliance, patient-centred care and education on expectations including effectiveness and side effects of psychotropic medicines.

Medications for the management of nutritional deficiencies

People living with EDs may have nutritional deficiencies and fluid fluctuations as a result of chronic food and fluid restriction, self-induced emesis, excessive exercise or laxative/diuretic misuse. Additionally, minerals and vitamins, such as phosphate magnesium and thiamine, are prescribed for short-term use in the context of preventing refeeding syndrome [67].

- Monitoring of patients who take vitamins and supplements (as most of them are available OTC) to ensure compliance or appropriate length of use as some are only required short-term in the context of preventing refeeding syndrome;

- Safe rehydration advice to those with a history of fluid restriction, self-induced emesis, excessive exercise or laxative misuse;

- Advising the healthcare team on choice of vitamins and minerals based on patient profile and preferences; and/or

- Counselling patients on side effects associated with the use of supplements and vitamins, especially those that may be distressing or triggering for people with EDs such as nausea, diarrhoea (e.g., caused by magnesium) or constipation (e.g., caused by calcium) [69].

Medication Misuse

Lisdexamphetamine and GLP-1s may be misused by patients in the aim of achieving weight loss [55]. Additionally, diuretics and laxatives are falsely believed to increase gastrointestinal motility and therefore reduce absorption of unwanted calories [70]. However, the weight loss observed is actually transient and is due to dehydration, followed by fluid shifts and retention, which leads to a vicious cycle where the patient increases their laxative use for further fluid excretion [70]. Despite some studies showing that laxative weaning protocols lead to successful outcomes [71], they are currently lacking and rarely used in practice.

- Lisdexamphetamine and GLP-1s are prescription-only medications putting pharmacists in an ideal position to support and assess patients for risk-benefit.

- Educating patients on the safe use of medicines and clarification of misconceptions such as associating the use laxatives and diuretics with weight loss;

- Implementing weaning protocols and supporting patients to wean off their laxatives with a study demonstrating that that five out of seven participants were able to reduce their laxative intake by at least 50% of which three completely stopped using laxatives with the support of a pharmacist [72]. The study described this method as less costly due to the involvement of pharmacists, and all the participants in the study appreciated the support provided from counselling on normal eating behaviours, bowel habits and the consequences of overusing laxatives [72]. There is evidence to support the convenience of having pharmacists supporting patients during laxative weaning.

Medicines which are dosed based on weight

Some medicines are dosed based on weight (e.g. antimicrobial agents, anticoagulants) and this may be overlooked in people with EDs whose weights may be fluctuating [73].

Medicines metabolised by CYP3A4 and CYP1A2

A study identified that the activity of the drug-metabolising enzymes CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 is altered in patients with anorexia nervosa and low body weight, which may alter drug plasma concentrations and effectiveness of their substrates (e.g. quinine and CYP3A4, caffeine and CYP1A2) [74].

All medicines

Compensatory behaviours, such as emesis or excessive laxative use within a few hours of having ingested an orally-administered drug, can affect intestinal transit time and absorption into systemic circulation [75].

- Educating patients on the effect of ED behaviours on drug absorption using their knowledge of basic pharmacokinetics; and/or

- Having an open discussion with patients to advise on safe redosing after a purging episode or collaboratively deciding on the optimal dosing time of a patient’s regular medications if purging patterns are predictable (e.g. taking all medicines two hours before a meal to ensure optimal absorption should the patient have purging behaviours after meals).

Supporting complications and recovery

People with EDs may experience multiple mental and physical complications, such as gastrointestinal issues, poor dental hygiene and cardiovascular abnormalities, which are associated with poor nutrition and eating habits. While these conditions should be managed by the relevant medical specialists, Table 3 shows how pharmacists may be able to support the management of complications and recovery as part of a multidisciplinary healthcare team. Pharmacists may be able to provide in-scope outpatient health monitoring (e.g. blood pressure, heart rate, blood glucose levels), advice on effective and safe symptomatic relief options (e.g. OTC antacids, anti-emetics, laxatives), counselling and education (e.g. dental hygiene, rehydration advice) and early referrals for complications that warrant immediate medical attention (e.g. suicide, concerning vital signs) or may be concerning (e.g. reproductive malfunction, skin abnormalities).

Pharmacists could also be well placed to support people with EDs during their recovery as it may take several years with relapses commonly occurring. On average, physical recovery from anorexia nervosa was found to take 4.7 years and psychosocial recovery was found to take 6.5–9 years [76]. Training pharmacists to identify early signs of relapse is likely to be most critical between 4 and 9 months post-hospital discharge as this period of time was shown to carry the highest risk of relapse [77]. Being easily accessible, pharmacists can arrange routine check-ups in the community and referral to physicians upon identification of signs of relapses. This would be quite reasonable given pharmacists have often been involved in successful interventions in referring and managing patients with other illnesses such as type 2 diabetes [78], sleep apnoea [79] or even depression [80]. In Australia, pharmacists are also involved in the monitoring of clozapine for patients with schizophrenia, which includes determining appropriateness of dosing based on blood test results and medication adherence [81].

Future directions

The prevalence and the impacts of EDs on the individual, carers, communities and governments underscore the need for immediate attention by all health professionals. With the concerning rise in workforce shortages in mental health services [100], there are increasing opportunities to support non-mental health practitioners to complete training to support people experiencing mental illness, especially in primary care and community settings. While a minimum standard of mental health and/or crisis education for all pharmacists is important, such as MHFA® training which is integrated in various pharmacy curricula in Australia and recommended for pharmacists in the US [54, 101], pharmacists also need further, targeted education to support people living with EDs, specifically given that Australian pharmacists have demonstrated a willingness and a commitment to upskilling and expanding their scope of practice [26, 82]. When exploring the acceptability of healthcare interventions, it is important to consider willingnesse, barriers and enablers from the perspective of all stakeholders, such as people with lived experience, their caregivers and health professionals. For example, recent research exploring pharmacists in perinatal mental health has explored the perspectives of pharmacists and perinatal women [102, 103]. A similar approach involving interviews with and surveys of key stakeholders in ED care, with a focus on people with lived experience and healthcare professionals with expertise in the field, may be appropriate to inform further research and inform co-design of training, resources and services in this area.

Previous studies have shown that pharmacists are capable of supporting people with mental illnesses if they receive adequate training [26]. This could include a higher level of education in universities as well as specialised training for members of the existing workforce who wish to upskill themselves. The Australian and New Zealand Academy for EDs currently provides credentials to GPs and other health professionals such as dieticians, occupational therapists, psychiatrists and psychologists to be recognised as ED-trained practitioners [104]. Similar credentials could be made available to pharmacists who undergo specialised training which could allow for them to be listed on the searchable directory of trained professionals. Hence, there is a need for further research in this area, specifically focusing on the co-design of a tailored training program for the community pharmacy workforce, and its evaluation. While barriers to pharmacists’ potential roles in ED care, such as lack of time and the need for remuneration [80, 103], are acknowledged and likely to be consistent with barriers associated with their roles in mental health care, in general, these are not insurmountable. In fact, with the right training, health system changes, remuneration and support, pharmacists can provide important services to communities, such as medication reviews, MedsChecks and dose administration aids, many of which are remunerated by the Australian Government [105].

Conclusion

EDs have a significant impact on a person’s health and quality of life. There are multiple gaps in the healthcare system which delay the diagnosis of EDs leading to poorer health outcomes. With adequate training and support, pharmacists may be able to help fill these gaps and could potentially play an important role in the prevention and recovery journey of people with EDs, but further research exploring feasibility, acceptability, effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of pharmacist-delivered ED care is needed.
