Neonatal gene therapy with AAV2/8-LSPhGAA improves hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in the Gaac.1826dupA knock-in murine model
Authors: Shih-hsin Kan, Jerry F. Harb, Songtao Li, Agatha Kliman, Perla Andrade-Heckman, Chloe L. Christensen, Sang-oh Han, Dwight D. Koeberl, Raymond Y. Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pompe disease (PD) results from lysosomal acid α-glucosidase (GAA) deficiency, causing lysosomal glycogen accumulation in cardiac and skeletal muscles. We previously characterized a murine model carrying the orthologous human infantile-onset PD (IOPD) pathogenic variant, c.1826dupA (p.Y609*), introduced into the mouse Gaa gene. Compared to wild-type (WT; C57BL/6NJ) controls, Gaac.1826dupA mice exhibit reduced GAA activity and develop early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy—evidenced by increased left ventricular wall thickness and left ventricular mass index (LVMI)— as well as impaired grip strength and gait abnormalities.To benchmark the model’s disease fidelity and assess its responsiveness to established therapeutic intervention, Gaac.1826dupA mice received a single retro-orbital dose of AAV2/8-LSPhGAA (2 × 109 vg/g body weight) at postnatal day 12–14. Twelve weeks post-treatment, mice exhibited supraphysiological GAA enzymatic activity in the heart (550% of WT) and liver (400% of WT) with a 93% reduction in cardiac glycogen. No sex-dependent differences in therapeutic efficacy were observed. Echocardiography revealed robust reversal of cardiac pathology, with wall thicknesses and LVMI values approaching WT levels. In contrast to this profound cardiac rescue, skeletal muscle improvements were modest; while forelimb grip strength remained unchanged, automated gait analysis showed benefit limited to hind paw base of support.These findings demonstrate that the Gaac.1826dupA model mirrors the critical cardiomyopathy characteristic of IOPD. While systemic AAV treatment yields definitive cardiac correction, the partial skeletal muscle response highlights a clear need for optimization. Consequently, the Gaac.1826dupA mouse serves as a high-fidelity platform for evaluating next-generation genomic correction strategies targeting both cardiac and refractory neuromuscular manifestations of PD.

Introduction

Pompe disease (PD), also known as glycogen storage disease type II (GSD II; OMIM: 232300), is a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by absent or reduced activity of acid α-glucosidase (GAA; EC: 3.2.1.20), an enzyme essential for glycogen degradation within lysosomes [1,2]. This enzymatic defect leads to abnormal glycogen accumulation, resulting in progressive muscle weakness, respiratory impairment, and, in severe cases, cardiomyopathy among other clinical outcomes [3]. The clinical presentation of PD is heterogeneous, with variations in age of onset, extent of organ involvement, and life expectancy [4]. Based on age at symptom onset, patients are generally classified into infantile-onset PD (IOPD) or late-onset PD (LOPD) [1]. IOPD is characterized by severe cardiac involvement with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypotonia, and respiratory insufficiency, which is often fatal within the first year of life without treatment. In contrast, LOPD primarily affects skeletal muscle and follows a more gradual course, with onset of myopathic symptoms ranging from late infancy to adulthood [1].

Currently, the standard-of-care treatment for PD is intravenous (IV) enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human GAA enzyme (rhGAA) [1,5]. However, ERT requires lifelong weekly or biweekly infusions, cannot cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and often elicits strong neutralizing immune responses against rhGAA [6–8]. Therefore, the development of alternative therapeutics such as gene therapy is essential to achieve durable, endogenous, high-level GAA production, which aims to restore normal cellular processes to alleviate PD symptoms. Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that liver-based GAA expression requires lower vector doses and results in a higher degree of efficacy than ERT while inducing immune tolerance to GAA, thereby preventing or suppressing anti-GAA antibody formation [9,10].

To further advance the development of alternative preclinical therapeutics for PD, we generated a knock-in (KI) murine model by introducing orthologous human IOPD pathogenic variant c.1826dupA (dbSNP: rs786204727) into the mouse Gaa gene. This murine model has been comprehensively characterized and shown to reflect some hallmark features of human IOPD such as cardiomyopathy and skeletal muscle weakness [11,12].

To evaluate the therapeutic responsiveness of this KI model, infant Gaac.1826dupA mice received intravenous administration of the AAV2/8-LSPhGAA (adeno-associated virus serotype 2/8 with proprietary Liver-Specific Promoter / human GAA cassette) at postnatal day 12–14. This vector, previously validated in Gaa knockout (KO; Gaatm1Rabn) mice, employs a liver-specific promoter to drive sustained expression and systemic secretion of human GAA. Upon secretion, the enzyme enters the systemic circulation and is subsequently internalized by peripheral tissues, such as the cardiac and skeletal muscle, via the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptor-mediated lysosomal trafficking pathway [13]. This phenomenon known as “cross-correction” [14], enables the liver-derived enzyme to clear the systemic glycogen accumulation resulting from endogenous GAA deficiency [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that this approach restores enzyme activity, reduces glycogen storage, and improves functional outcomes in PD models [10,16–18].

While the KO model has been instrumental in establishing proof-of-concept for liver-directed gene therapy, its complete absence of endogenous Gaa limits its utility for evaluating mutation-specific therapeutic approaches that require preservation of the native genomic target. In contrast, the Gaac.1826dupA KI model retains the disease-causing sequence and therefore provides a clinically relevant platform for the development and assessment of next-generation therapeutic strategies, including site-specific genome editing.

The present study was designed to determine whether the Gaac.1826dupA KI model exhibits therapeutic responsiveness comparable to that reported in established PD models. To this end, we evaluated cardiac, biochemical, histological, and functional outcomes 12 weeks after AAV2/8-LSPhGAA administration and compared treated KI mice with untreated KI and wild-type controls. Collectively, these studies establish a functional benchmark for the Gaac.1826dupA model, validating its disease fidelity and supporting its use as a preclinical platform for future genome-editing and mutation-specific therapeutic development.

Materials and methods

Animal care, husbandry and usage

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the CHOC Children’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol #160902). Gaac.1826dupA mice (C57BL/6NJ-Gaaem1Jhng/J; JAX#034609), carrying the orthologous human GAA mutation c.1826dupA (dbSNP: rs786204727), were on a C57BL/6NJ background [11]. Heterozygous breeders were crossed to generate homozygous mutant (Gaac.1826dupA/c.1826dupA) and wild-type (WT) control mice. Genotyping was performed by PCR using primers flanking exon 13: 1826dupA-F (5′-CACTCAGGGCCCTGGTCAAGA-3′) and 1826dupA-R (5′-GTGCCACATGAGATGGCGCATG-3′) followed by RsaI digestion, which distinguishes the mutant (GTAAC) from the WT (GTAC) allele as previously described [12]. Mice were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to Teklad Global 16% Protein Rodent Diet (Envigo, Indianapolis, IN) and water.

A total of nine male and nine female Gaac.1826dupA pups were treated in this study. Both sexes were included to assess potential sex-dependent effects on treatment outcomes. Sex-stratified echocardiographic analysis revealed no significant differences between male and female mice in the WT or Gene Tx groups. Within the untreated KI cohort, male mice exhibited significantly greater IVSd and LVPWd compared with female KI mice (p = 0.0062 and p = 0.0004, respectively), indicating a modest sex-dependent difference in baseline cardiac disease severity. As treatment efficacy was not sex-dependent, sex-stratified echocardiographic data are presented as the primary analysis (Fig. 1), with combined-cohort data provided for reference (Supplementary Fig. 1). Echocardiography and behavioral assessments (grip strength and gait analysis) were performed prior to final tissue collection at 12 weeks post-treatment. Age-matched historical controls from untreated KI and WT mice were used for comparison, as these cohorts were characterized and maintained under identical housing and husbandry conditions.

Production of AAV2/8-LSPhGAA and retro-orbital administration

The details of viral vector production and subsequent in vivo delivery were performed as follows: The vector plasmid, pAAV-LSPhGAA, contains the liver-specific promoter (LSP) regulatory cassette, WT human GAA cDNA, and a human growth hormone polyadenylation signal, all flanked by adeno-associated virus serotype 2 (AAV2) inverted terminal repeats. The LSP regulatory cassette–subcloned from pAV-LSP-cFIX (courtesy of Dr. Inder Verma, Salk Institute, La Jolla, CA; sequence available upon request)—comprises a thyroid hormone-binding globulin promoter sequence downstream of two copies of an α1-microglobulin/bikunin enhancer sequence. This cassette previously demonstrated long-term efficacy in hemophilia B mice when utilized within an AAV vector encoding coagulation factor IX [19].

Briefly, the AAV2/8 viral vector was prepared using a standard triple-transfection method: HEK293 cells were co-transfected with the pAAV-LSPhGAA vector plasmid [20], the AAV8 packaging helper plasmid [21] (courtesy of Dr. James M. Wilson, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA), and pAdHelper (Stratagene, now Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles, and isolated via sucrose cushion pelleting followed by two successive cesium chloride density gradient centrifugation steps. Purified AAV stocks were dialyzed against three changes of Hanks’ balanced salt solution, with 5% sorbitol added to the final dialysis change, and stored in aliquots at −80 °C. The vector genome titer was determined by DNase I digestion, DNA extraction, and Southern blot analysis. All viral vector preparations were handled in strict accordance with the Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) guidelines published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Recombinant AAV2/8-LSPhGAA vector aliquots were previously prepared and stored at −80 °C [16] until needed for administration, after which the aliquots were thawed on ice and the vector was diluted with sterile PBS to a working concentration of 2 × 108 vector genomes (vg) / μL).

A final dose of 2 × 109 vg per gram of body weight (BW) was administered intravenously via retro-orbital injection [22] into Gaac.1826dupA mice between postnatal days 12–14. Prior to injection, pups were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane, and the vector solution was delivered in a total volume of 10 μL per gram mouse BW using a 29-gauge 1 mL insulin syringe. After injection, mice were monitored until full recovery from anesthesia and returned to their home cages with dams.

Murine echocardiography

One day before echocardiographic imaging, thoracic fur was removed using depilatory cream. Before scanning, lubricant ointment was applied to the eyes to prevent corneal drying. Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for 10–15 s, then maintained at 0.5% on a heated platform with ECG electrodes placed on the thorax to monitor heart rate (HR), which was kept at ≥500 bpm. Transthoracic echocardiography (M-mode and B-mode) was performed using a Vevo 3100 high-resolution ultrasound system equipped with a 25–55 MHz linear transducer (FUJIFILM VisualSonics Inc., Toronto, Canada). Image analysis with Vevo LAB software enabled quantification of interventricular septum (IVS), left ventricular internal diameter (LVID), and left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW) during systole and diastole. Reported values represent the average of at least three independent measurements. Left ventricular mass (LVM) was determined using the linear method in Vevo LAB software, and the LVM index (LVMI) was calculated as LVM/BW. Fractional shortening (%FS) was calculated as (LVIDd − LVIDs) / LVIDd × 100.

All echocardiographic image acquisition and quantitative analysis were performed by a single experienced operator who was blinded to treatment group assignment. Measurements were conducted across multiple concurrent studies, further minimizing potential operator bias.

Tissue harvesting, processing, and histological staining

Animals were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation and cervical dislocation, then transcardially perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Heart, liver, diaphragm, quadriceps and gastrocnemius samples were harvested in this study. Tissue samples for biochemical studies (heart, liver, diaphragm and quadriceps) were rapidly frozen and stored at −80°C; tissues for histological staining (heart, quadriceps and gastrocnemius) were fixed in formalin before sending them to Experimental Tissue Resource Center at University of California, Irvine (UCI) to embed in paraffin blocks to perform periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining counterstained with hematoxylin on 4 μm-thick sections.

Imaging quantification

To quantify glycogen accumulation, the optical density (OD) of PAS-stained sections was measured across various tissues. Representative images were captured at 20× magnification using the SL5 Real-Time Telemicroscopy Imaging System (Mikroscan Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). To determine staining intensity, the RGB channels of PAS-stained images were processed using established color deconvolution protocols [23,24]. Briefly, images with homogeneous backgrounds from tissues (heart, quadriceps, and gastrocnemius) were performed using defined color vectors [23] via the Fuji software (ImageJ version 2.0) [25]. After applying the H-PAS deconvolution function, images were converted to an 8-bit format. The OD was calculated from the mean gray value using the formula: OD = log (max intensity / mean intensity), where the maximum intensity was set to 255. The average mean OD values were then compared across experimental groups to assess glycogen clearance.

All image quantification was performed by an investigator blinded to group assignment, with samples processed across multiple concurrent studies.

Biochemical evaluations

GAA activity and glycogen content were measured in tissue lysates using established protocols [6,20,26]. Tissue samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, homogenized in water, and centrifuged to remove insoluble material. Protein concentration of the resulting lysates was determined by Bradford assay. GAA enzymatic activity was measured by quantifying the cleavage of 4-methylumbelliferyl-α-D-glucoside at pH 4.3, with one activity unit (U) defined as 1 nmol of converted substrate per hour; specific activity was expressed as U per mg of protein. Glycogen content was determined by measuring glucose released from boiled tissue homogenates following digestion with Aspergillus niger amyloglucosidase, quantified spectrophotometrically via NADH absorbance using a linked enzymatic assay with glucokinase and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, and expressed as μmol of glycogen per mg of protein.

Forelimb grip strength assay and gait analysis

Muscle strength was evaluated using a forelimb grip strength test to measure peak tensile force (N, Newton) as an indicator of neuromuscular function. Mice were acclimated for at least one hour before testing. Grip strength was measured with a horizontally oriented pull bar connected to a digital force transducer (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH, USA). During testing, mice were oriented in a manner where forepaws grasped the pull bar, and gradually retracted to elicit a peak force. A valid trial required a full forelimb grip, steady resistance, and consistent pulling motion. Gait analysis was conducted using the CatWalk XT system (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA) to assess locomotor coordination and paw placement. Mice were acclimated in a dark testing room for at least one hour before testing. Run criteria were defined as a duration between 0.50 and 12.30 s with a minimum of three compliant runs, where any runs outside these parameters were excluded. The walkway was calibrated, and background images were captured prior to testing. During each trial, mice traversed the walkway from one end to the other while being recorded from below under standardized illumination settings (camera gain, 22 dB; green intensity threshold, 0.10; red ceiling light, 17.7 V; green walkway light, 16.5 V). Raw gait data were automatically generated and processed using CatWalk software (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA).

All grip strength measurements and gait data analysis were performed by an investigator blinded to group assignment, with assessments conducted across multiple concurrent studies.

Statistical analysis

All graphs and statistical comparisons were generated using GraphPad Prism 11.0.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Restoration of cardiac structure and function in KI mice following neonatal AAV-mediated gene therapy

Cardiac function was evaluated by echocardiography. By 12 weeks post-treatment, untreated KI mice exhibited significant alterations in cardiac structure, including increased IVS and LVPW thicknesses, accompanied by a reduction in LVID (Fig. 1A). These changes are characteristic of left ventricular hypertrophy and were associated with an elevated LVMI. Such structural remodeling leads to hyperdynamic left ventricular systolic function, as reflected by increased %FS compared with WT controls [12]. Given the observed sex-dependent difference in baseline wall thickness within the untreated KI cohort (Section 2.1), echocardiographic findings are presented stratified by sex.

Treatment of KI mice with AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy led to marked improvements of all echocardiographic parameters across both sexes. In male treated animals, the end-diastolic thicknesses of the IVS (IVSd) and LVPW (LVPWd) were nearly normalized (0.72 ± 0.05 and 0.67 ± 0.05 mm, respectively), showing no significant difference compared to male WT values (0.70 ± 0.07 and 0.64 ± 0.07 mm; p > 0.05) and substantially reduced compared with untreated KI mice (1.10 ± 0.11 and 1.07 ± 0.09 mm; both p values <0.0001). Similarly, female treated animals demonstrated near-normalization of IVSd and LVPWd (0.71 ± 0.11 and 0.65 ± 0.07 mm, respectively), with no significant difference from female WT controls (0.66 ± 0.05 and 0.59 ± 0.09 mm; p > 0.05) and significant reductions compared with female untreated KI mice (0.95 ± 0.10 and 0.88 ± 0.10 mm; both p < 0.0001). Concurrently, end-diastolic LVID (LVIDd) was significantly increased in treated animals of both sexes–males: 3.23 ± 0.50 mm vs. untreated KI 2.61 ± 0.40 mm (p < 0.001); females: 3.14 ± 0.37 mm vs. untreated KI 2.64 ± 0.37 mm (p < 0.01) — becoming comparable to respective WT values (males: 3.34 ± 0.43 mm; females: 2.87 ± 0.32 mm; both p > 0.05), indicating restoration of near-normal ventricular geometry (Fig. 1A, B). Combined-cohort values were consistent with these findings (Supplementary Fig. 1; IVSd: 0.71 ± 0.09 mm; LVPWd: 0.66 ± 0.06 mm; LVIDd: 3.18 ± 0.42 mm).

Consistent with these structural improvements, %FS and LVMI were markedly improved following gene therapy in both sexes (Fig. 1C). %FS was significantly reduced in treated males (38.20 ± 4.86%) relative to untreated KI males (47.13 ± 4.31%; p < 0.001) and was not significantly different from male WT levels (38.19 ± 5.09%; p > 0.05). In females, % FS was likewise reduced in treated animals (41.85 ± 2.73%) compared with untreated KI females (48.27 ± 7.02%; p < 0.05), normalizing to levels comparable with female WT controls (42.86 ± 5.57%; p > 0.05). LVMI was significantly reduced in treated males (3.01 ± 0.51 mg/g BW) compared with untreated KI mice (4.20 ± 0.65 mg/g BW; p < 0.01), approaching WT levels (2.84 ± 0.73 mg/g BW; p > 0.05). In females, LVMI was not significantly reduced in treated animals (3.52 ± 0.34 mg/g BW) compared with untreated KI mice (3.98 ± 0.82 mg/g BW; p = 0.236) and remained significantly elevated relative to female WT controls (2.54 ± 0.56 mg/g BW; p = 0.0086), suggesting incomplete attenuation of cardiac hypertrophy in female KI mice. Taken together, these findings indicate near-complete normalization of left ventricular structure and systolic function in male KI mice following gene therapy. In females, while %FS was fully normalized, LVMI remained significantly elevated compared with WT controls, suggesting a sex-dependent difference in the completeness of cardiac hypertrophy reversal.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy robustly mitigates heart disease in the Gaac.1826dupA KI model, preventing key features of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and improving both cardiac structure and function. Sex-stratified analyses confirmed that treatment efficacy was not sex-dependent across all echocardiographic parameters in WT and Gene Tx groups (Supplementary Table 1). Combined-cohort data are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Biochemical evaluation of KI mice following neonatal AAV-mediated gene therapy

Previously, we reported that Gaac.1826dupA KI mice exhibit reduced GAA enzyme activity and elevated glycogen content compared to WT controls [11,12]. Twelve weeks following IV AAV2/8-LSPhGAA treatment, supraphysiological levels of GAA activity were observed in both liver and heart. GAA activity in the livers of treated KI mice reached approximately 400% of the WT mean (263.80 ± 183.92 vs. 63.71 ± 3.47 U/mg protein), while cardiac GAA activity was even more pronounced, reaching nearly 550% of the WT mean (89.45 ± 53.28 vs. 16.36 ± 2.41 U/mg protein) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, no significant increase in GAA activity was detected in the quadriceps or diaphragm of treated animals relative to untreated KI controls (Fig. 2A).

The robust enzyme expression observed in the heart was complemented by a marked reduction in cardiac glycogen accumulation. Glycogen content in treated KI mice (0.20 ± 0.17 μmol/mg protein) approached WT levels (0.03 ± 0.00 μmol/mg protein; p > 0.05), representing an approximately 93% reduction compared to untreated KI mice (2.55 ± 0.42 μmol/mg protein; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy in restoring GAA activity and ameliorating glycogen storage pathology in cardiac tissue.

A significant reduction in glycogen content was observed in quadriceps samples from treated mice (0.78 ± 0.20 μmol/mg protein), representing an approximately 40% reduction compared with untreated KI controls (1.29 ± 0.23 μmol/mg protein; p < 0.001), although levels remained elevated relative to WT mice (0.07 ± 0.01 μmol/mg protein; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B). This partial biochemical correction in the absence of detectable GAA activity in skeletal tissue suggests that cross-correction via systemic secreted liver-derived GAA may contribute to glycogen clearance in skeletal muscle. No measurable reduction in diaphragmatic glycogen storage was observed (1.46 ± 0.65 μmol/mg protein vs. untreated KI 1.65 ± 0.19 μmol/mg protein; p > 0.05).

One male treated KI mouse exhibited no detectable increase in GAA activity in heart or liver and no reduction in glycogen content across tissues, consistent with a lack of therapeutic response. This animal has been retained in all analyses; its inclusion did not affect the statistical significance of any primary outcome measured.

Grip strength and gait analysis results in KI mice following neonatal AAV-mediated gene therapy

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy on motor function, we assessed forelimb grip strength and gait parameters in treated KI mice compared with untreated KI and WT controls. Consistent with previous reports [12], 12-week-old KI mice started to exhibit a marked reduction in forelimb peak tensile force compared to WT (0.81 ± 0.13 N vs WT 0.95 ± 0.09 N; p < 0.001), followed by progressive muscle weakness over time relative to WT mice (Fig. 3A). They also displayed a narrower hind paw base of support (2.32 ± 0.40 cm vs WT 2.71 ± 0.27 cm; p < 0.0001), indicative of impaired motor function and coordination. Functionally, gene therapy–treated mice demonstrated a smaller, but not significant trend of forelimb strength improvement compared to untreated KI mice (0.84 ± 0.11 N vs KI 0.81 ± 0.13 N; p > 0.05) approaching WT controls. A significant improvement in hind paw base of support was observed (2.54 ± 0.27 cm; p < 0.001 compared to KI), suggesting enhanced postural stability and a partial normalization of gait dynamics following AAV2/8-LSPhGAA treatment (Fig. 3B).

Histopathological evaluation demonstrates reduced cardiac muscle PAS staining intensity in KI mice following neonatal AAV-mediated gene therapy

To visualize and assess glycogen accumulation in treated mice, PAS staining was performed on cardiac and skeletal muscles (gastrocnemius and quadriceps) from treated KI mice, untreated KI mice, and WT controls. Qualitatively, PAS staining was markedly more intense in KI mice across all examined tissues compared with WT controls, indicating significant glycogen deposition (Fig. 4A).

Quantitative analysis corroborated these findings, revealing statistically significant increases in OD in all tissues from untreated KI mice compared with WT controls (0.25 ± 0.02 OD and 0.17 ± 0.02 OD, respectively; p < 0.0001). Cardiac muscle from treated KI mice demonstrated a significant reduction in PAS staining intensity (0.13 ± 0.02 OD, p < 0.0001 relative to untreated KI), reflecting diminished glycogen burden and consistent with the biochemically measured reduction in cardiac glycogen content. No significant reduction in tissue OD was observed in PAS-stained gastrocnemius or quadriceps muscles when compared with untreated KI animals. Due to the limited amount of diaphragm tissue in each mouse, all diaphragm tissue were allocated for biochemical assays; consequently, histopathological evaluation was not performed for this tissue.

Discussion

This study represents the first therapeutic application performed in the Gaac.1826dupA KI mice since their initial characterization in 2020 [11]. We utilized a well-characterized gene therapy vector, AAV2/8-LSPhGAA, which employs a liver-specific promoter that drives sustained hepatic expression and secretion of human GAA into the bloodstream for cross-correction [16–18]. Previously validated in Gaa KO mice, studies have demonstrated elevated plasma GAA activity and high vector genome copy numbers in the liver, supporting effective hepatic transduction and enzyme delivery [18]. Importantly, the gene therapy experiment presented here was not designed to demonstrate superiority over prior KO-based studies, but rather to serve as a functional benchmark — confirming that the Gaac.1826dupA KI model responds to therapeutic intervention in a manner consistent with the established literature, thereby validating its utility as a preclinical platform. This liver-depot gene therapy strategy restored GAA enzyme activity in key tissues and reduced glycogen accumulation in heart and skeletal muscles, thereby ameliorating PD cardiomyopathy and skeletal myopathy in Gaa KO mice. Furthermore, the liver depot strategy has been shown to achieve efficacy even at a low vector dose (0.8 × 109 vg/g BW) [16], achieving biochemical correction comparable to that attained with long-term ERT [27]. Building upon these promising preclinical results, the AAV2/8-LSPhGAA vector was subsequently advanced to a clinical trial in LOPD patients (NCT03533673) [9,28]. Liver-directed gene therapy successfully eliminated the need for ERT by establishing sustained GAA production. Following baseline administration and 26 weeks of co-treatment with ERT, ERT was discontinued as participants maintained elevated serum GAA activity (101–235% of baseline) without serious adverse events or anti-capsid T-cell responses. By week 52, most participants demonstrated significantly increased muscle GAA activity and stable glycogen levels, confirming the efficacy of the liver-depot strategy as a long-term ERT alternative [28].

In this study, we applied the AAV2/8-LSPhGAA vector to P12-P14 Gaac.1826dupA KI mice and evaluated disease pathology, biochemical markers, and histological outcomes 12 weeks post-treatment. Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated marked improvement of cardiac structure and function in both male and female KI mice. Although LVMI remained significantly elevated relative to female WT controls, direct comparison of treated male and female cohorts revealed no statistically significant sex-dependent differences in treatment outcomes. Notably, untreated male KI mice exhibited greater interventricular septal and posterior wall thickening than untreated females, indicating more severe baseline cardiac disease. Thus, the residual LVMI elevation observed in treated females likely reflects baseline phenotypic variation rather than a differential response to therapy.

This observation is of interest in the context of well-established sex differences in AAV-mediated liver transduction, where liver-directed expression has been shown to be higher in male mice through androgen-dependent mechanisms [29,30]. Despite this reported transduction bias, comparable therapeutic benefit was observed in both sexes in the present study, suggesting that the administered dose achieved the therapeutic threshold required for effective cardiac correction in both male and female animals.

Biochemical analyses further supported the echocardiographic findings, demonstrating supraphysiological cardiac GAA activity, normalization of glycogen accumulation, and marked reduction of PAS-positive staining in treated animals. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy effectively reverses hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in Gaac.1826dupA KI mice and provides comparable cardiac benefit in both sexes under the conditions evaluated in this study.

However, these therapeutic effects were not uniformly observed across other muscle tissues. No significant increase in GAA enzyme activity was detected in diaphragm or quadriceps tissues, likely attributable to the significant downregulation of cation-independent M6P receptors in adult mammalian skeletal muscle, which limits efficient lysosomal targeting [27,31]. Nevertheless, a partial glycogen reduction was observed in the quadriceps. Although glycogen levels were not fully restored to WT levels, they were significantly lower than those in untreated KI mice. Glycogen content has been reported to serve as a more sensitive indicator of biochemical correction than GAA enzyme activity [16,17], as glycogen accumulation in PD-affected muscle can be substantially cross-corrected through hepatic secretion of GAA, serving as an endogenous source of enzyme replacement. Interestingly, glycogen reduction was observed despite the absence of detectable increases in local GAA activity, suggesting that low-level cross-correction may provide measurable biochemical benefit even when enzyme activity remains below the assay detection threshold. Consistent with this interpretation, a single gait parameter—hind paw base of support—showed modest improvement, accompanied by partial glycogen reduction in quadriceps, despite undetectable GAA enzyme activity in skeletal tissues. Forelimb grip strength showed no significant improvement. These findings suggest that while liver-directed gene therapy can partially ameliorate skeletal muscle pathology, extended treatment duration or higher vector doses may be required to achieve more robust neuromuscular correction.

In addition, the dose requirement for liver depot gene therapy appears to be approximately three-fold higher for infants compared to adult mice, due to the rapid loss of vector genomes early in life. One study comparing liver depot treatment in P60 (adult) mice to P12 (infant) mice showed that adult mice had stronger and longer-lasting correction of GAA deficiency and glycogen clearance than in infants, which demonstrated more pronounced GAA restoration in the liver and heart [18]. This age-dependent difference in vector retention may partially explain the limited therapeutic efficacy observed in skeletal muscle in the present study.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AAV2/8-LSPhGAA gene therapy effectively reverses hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in Gaac.1826dupA KI mice following neonatal administration, consistent with the known cardiac tropism of the liver-depot strategy at this developmental stage. While these results confirm the high therapeutic utility and potency of the AAV2/8 vector, the broader significance of this study lies in the characterization and potential applications of the animal model. Specifically, the successful restoration of cardiac function provides proof of concept that Gaac.1826dupA KI mice are a robust and reliable platform for the preclinical evaluation of next-generation therapeutics for PD.

Notably, the comparable therapeutic outcomes observed following AAV gene augmentation in the Gaac.1826dupA KI model relative to classical Pompe KO models serve as an important validation of this KI model’s disease fidelity. Critically however, the KI model was specifically developed to support genotype-specific genome-editing strategies, such as prime editing, which require the presence of the endogenous pathogenic sequence and therefore cannot be evaluated in KO models.

The importance of such precise genetic models is underscored by the rapid evolution of genomic/personalized medicine [32]. Over the past decade, the characterization of Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) and their associated (Cas) nucleases has catalyzed a paradigm shift in genomic manipulation. This transformative technology offers far-reaching applications in biotechnology and holds profound implications for human therapeutics, particularly in the advancement of personalized medicine [33]. The diversification of the CRISPR–Cas toolkit—encompassing nucleases, base editors, transposases, and prime editors—has significantly broadened the scope of genomic surgery by offering distinct advantages in precision targeting and versatile DNA-modification. Continued optimization of these platforms has improved editing efficiency and minimized off-target effects, accelerating their transition toward clinical application and reinforcing their potential in precision medicine [34].

Successful translation of CRISPR-based therapeutics, however, requires animal models that faithfully recapitulate human disease genotypes. Historically, PD research has relied predominantly on KO rodent models generated through insertion of neomycin resistance (Neo) cassettes into critical exons of the Gaa gene, thereby abolishing functional enzyme expression [35,36]. While fundamental research in ERT, gene therapy and cell therapy has been built upon these KO models [31,37,38], they possess inherent limitations for modern genetic medicine.

More recently, several Gaa KI models harboring disease-relevant patient mutations have been established [11,39,40], providing a crucial preclinical framework for evaluating next-generation, site-specific gene-editing strategies. Unlike KO models, which lack the endogenous genomic architecture required for homology-directed repair or base and prime editing, KI models preserve the precise disease-causing alleles. This feature enables rigorous validation and optimization of corrective genomic interventions within a physiologically and translationally relevant context.

Previous work from our group evaluated the therapeutic potential of prime editing in a C2C12 mouse myoblast model carrying biallelic Gaa c.1826dupA mutations [11]. Prime editing successfully corrected the mutant allele to the WT sequence, resulting in a statistically significant increase in GAA enzymatic activity compared to untreated mutant controls [41]. This genome-editing strategy will next be translated into this Gaac.1826dupA KI model to assess therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Beyond confirming genomic correction, restoration of enzymatic activity, and the clearance of glycogen, these studies will enable comprehensive evaluation of systemic physiological outcomes, including recovery of cardiac and skeletal muscle function. Together, this work establishes a foundation for the development of definitive, genotype-specific therapies for PD.
