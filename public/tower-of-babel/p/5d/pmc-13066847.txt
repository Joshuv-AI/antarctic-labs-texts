Y-shaped pulmonary artery in modified Fontan surgery: a multimodality imaging case
Authors: Rui Lv, Zhenglun Alan Wei, Xiaona Lin, Chenze Tian, Bin Qiao
Published: 2026

Introduction

A 16-year-old male patient with single ventricle physiology (right ventricular type) and dextrocardia underwent a Qiao-modified Fontan procedure in 2015. The procedure featured a novel Y-shaped pulmonary artery (PA) configuration distinct from conventional Y-grafts which contained bilateral superior vena cava (SVC).

Case presentation

Figure 1 illustrates the surgical details, encompassing ligation of the main pulmonary artery (MPA) (Figure 1A,1B), the right SVC (RSVC) was disconnected and anastomosed to the right PA (RPA); the innominate vein (IV) was disconnected and anastomosed to the left PA (LPA) as left SVC (LSVC), and anastomosis was performed of the conduit with the MPA and inferior vena cava (IVC). Notably, in this case, the PA diameter was within expectation, and no PA augmentation was performed.

When necessary, the MPA was incised proximally along the bifurcation of the RPA and LPA for the purpose of lengthening the RPA and LPA, preserving sufficient tissue for ligation. At this point, the RPA, LPA, and MPA formed a Y-shaped structure (Figure 1B).

The MPA was ligated to prepare for the connection of the total cavo-pulmonary connection (TCPC) conduit (see Figure 1A). A 1.2 cm Gore-Tex conduit with a length of 4.5 cm was selected, with the left and right ends trimmed into arcuate shapes. The upper end of the conduit was anastomosed to the MPA of the Y-shaped structure (Figure 1E). The IVC was lifted above the diaphragm and anastomosed to the lower edge of the conduit. After suturing, the IVC naturally fell back into the abdominal cavity, with the conduit trapped above the diaphragm (Figure 1F, also Figure 2A). The overall Y-shaped PA and conduit structure should be free of angular malformations or torsion. Figure 2B provides a conceptual diagram illustrating this modified Fontan structure. A 10-year follow-up conducted in 2025 was evaluated using multimodal imaging. Postoperative assessments revealed stable sinus rhythm, a normal cardiothoracic ratio, and Fontan conduit flow velocity of 23 cm/s by echocardiography. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed a patent, undistorted Y-shaped PA (Figure 2C). Left atrial volumes were measured from cine cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) images and indexed to body surface area (BSA) to obtain the left atrial maximum volume index (LAVImax/BSA) 54.34 mL/m2 and minimum volume index (LAVImin) 35.52 mL/m2. Peak velocities measured by four-dimensional (4D)-flow MRI were 32.5 cm/s (conduit), 27.8 cm/s (RSVC), and 34.1 cm/s (LSVC) (Figure 2D). Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations indicated laminar flow directed toward both pulmonary arteries with power loss 0.79 mW, indexed power loss 0.25 and resistance 0.09 WU (Figure 2E). Angiography showed no stenosis (Figure 2F), with bifurcation angles of 99°/115°/146° (Figure 2A) and 93°/131°/136° (Figure 2F). The diameters were 1.20 cm (position 2, conduit), 1.57 cm (position 1, IVC, 23.6% difference), and 1.10 cm (position 3, RPA). No catheter-related thrombotic events reported.

Discussion

Our Y-shaped PA reconstruction technique shares the fundamental hemodynamic design goal with the traditional total synthetic, both Y-graft Fontan procedure (1-5), both aiming to achieve an energy-efficient pulmonary circulation. However, there is an essential distinction in their core construction. The conventional Y-graft relies entirely on prosthetic vascular material. CFD analyses have indicated that the connecting bridge segment in the mid-portion of such grafts can generate dissipative secondary flow patterns, which are detrimental to energy efficiency (3). More importantly, the extensive use of synthetic materials theoretically elevates the long-term risk of thrombosis (4).

The core innovation of the technique reported here lies in the maximized utilization of autologous pulmonary arterial tissue. By fully mobilizing and inferiorly translocating the patient’s own left and RPAs, we fashioned them into the two branching limbs of the Y-configuration, using only a short segment of prosthetic conduit as the central stem. This autologous tissue-dominant structure significantly reduces the volume of implanted foreign material, which may confer superior biocompatibility and long-term durability. By omitting the mid-graft connecting bridge inherent to traditional synthetic Y-grafts, our design, based on CFD analysis and imaging follow-up (including 4D flow MRI), promotes laminar flow, reduces turbulence, and minimizes energy loss (6-8). For instance, the bilateral SVC flow rates (1.6–1.96 L/min) were within a favorable range (1). The growth potential of the native PA tissue, coupled with improved compliance matching between the prosthetic conduit and the autologous vessels, may reduce the risks of conduit distortion, traction, or compression on surrounding structures associated with somatic growth. In this case, cardiac MRI confirmed the absence of hemodynamically significant compression on the heart by the conduit.

As a case report, this technique has evident limitations. First, the conduit diameter, chosen to match the native PAs, is relatively small, and an inverted funnel shape was observed at the IVC anastomosis. Although no typical signs of Fontan-associated liver disease have been identified to date, long-term follow-up is necessary to determine whether this IVC morphology leads to chronic regional complications. Second, the successful application in this case benefited from specific anatomical conditions. For patients with different anatomies, alternative conduit pathways (left-sided, right-sided, or paraspinal routes) would need to be planned to achieve an analogous Y-configuration. The reproducibility and broader applicability of this technique require validation in larger, more anatomically heterogeneous patient cohorts. Formal cardiopulmonary exercise testing has been included as a key component of the planned long-term follow-up protocol.
