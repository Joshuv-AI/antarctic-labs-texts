EMG hysteresis patterns in human elbow muscles under simultaneous force and length changes
Authors: Andriy Gorkovenko, Oleksii Lehedza, Andriy Maznychenko, Inna Sokolowska, Alexander Kostyukov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated signal transmission in the human motor system, focusing on the influence of coinciding and opposing directions of change in key mechanical parameters – muscle length and force – on electromyograms (EMGs). The goal is to understand how these mechanical changes affect muscle activation during movement, a crucial aspect for developing a comprehensive model of motor control. Eight participants performed movement tests using a robotic-mechatronic device. Each test was repeated ten times to obtain averaged EMG signals from the elbow muscles. The participants developed active elbow flexor contractions while pressing a force vector-measuring manipulator in biofeedback mode. The manipulator’s movement trajectory and visual biofeedback were preprogrammed to create time profiles of muscle length and force changes using a double trapezoid waveform. The EMG data were compared for opposing pattern (OP) and coinciding pattern (CP) movements in terms of length and force changes. During OP movements, the averaged EMG waveforms followed programmed force changes, with some natural distortions. However, CP movements strongly compensated for the differences in force and length, reducing EMG variation. Statistical analysis revealed that the amplitude of the force factor did not significantly affect the integrated areas of EMGs or their peak amplitudes, but the directional force factor and interaction of factors were effective. Interestingly, muscle hysteresis effects, where higher EMG activity typically corresponds to muscle shortening, were violated by some agonists but preserved by others. This study highlights the complex mechanical interactions and redistribution of forces among synergistic muscles during different movement patterns. These findings may help refine the “thermodynamic” model of muscle activation, providing insights into how central commands are modulated during motor tasks involving both coinciding and opposing mechanical changes in muscle length and force.

Introduction

Attempts to analyze central commands coming to the muscles of human limbs, as a rule, are limited to the analysis of surface EMG signals during motor activity. In this case, there is a need to record the maximum possible number of biomechanical indicators of the act of movement, such as joint angles and moments of force (torques) around the joints, and it is desirable to use multiple repetitions of the motor program for the purpose of a subsequent attempt to establish functional relationships and correlation dependencies between a large number of variables1. Notably, any analysis of targeted, highly coordinated movements of human limbs is based on extrapolation to a specific motor act of three fundamental parameters of muscle contraction: muscle length, developed force, and the level of muscle activation. Since there are usually many muscles providing a specific movement, a certain simplifying assumption is introduced about the possibility of dividing all muscles into groups with similar changes in the above parameters, or “synergies”2.

In various human movements, three supposedly partially independent types of muscle synergy are usually considered. Kinematic synergy is represented by covariances (correlations) between simultaneous changes in joint angles3; it has been used to describe distal movements such as different types of manual exploration4 and typing5. Force (kinetic) synergy is described as the forced contractions of distal hand muscles during grip and voluntary interaction of the fingers3,6. Activation (muscle) synergy is explored, for example, during static positioning of the hand7,8 or during creation of active forces by the fingers of the hand9,10. Information about the application of methods of theoretical mechanics to the analysis of synergistic control can be found in the monography of Valero-Cuevas9. An overview of different approaches for analyzing the synergy in motor control can be found in the review of Bruton and Dwyer11.

The present study is, at least in part, concerned with the analysis of the role of muscle contraction hysteresis in forming central commands to muscles during real movements. Although most nonlinear effects on muscle dynamics are well known, they are often not taken into account in the analysis of various problems related to motor control. This is especially noticeable in attempts to implement the principles of biological systems in technical devices, where the Hill model is mainly used12. Modern models considering human movements inevitably include the thixotropic properties of real muscles13–15, residual force enhancement16–18, and various hysteresis effects19–21. Research on hysteresis in human motor control may be particularly important from an applied perspective in robotics, given that this effect is observed in artificial muscles22.

The development of more sophisticated experimental methods allows us to obtain more detailed information about real changes in muscle length and force, thereby revealing the “internal” parameters of a moving limb, in contrast to the “external” kinematic and kinetic parameters, such as the angles of the joints under study and their moments of force, which are more accessible to experimental study. An example of such an approach is described for externally imposed circular planar movements of the hand, which are produced in parallel with the generation of tangential forces in the biofeedback mode23. In addition to the implementation of end point trajectories provided by a robotic mechatronic device (RMD), traces of length and force in the muscles under study were reconstructed using previously developed identification methods24. When analyzing synergistic interactions between muscles belonging to different joints, a close relationship was shown between the force and activation synergies, as well as the priority of the former in this combination, while the influence of kinematic synergy was rather modulating in nature. Moreover, we have shown that for a real analysis of the interaction between force and length-dependent factors on muscle activation, it is currently better to use simpler experimental paradigms, such as, for example, a single-joint experimental model involving reactions involving close agonists in the same joint. It seems to be most interesting to compare the results of simultaneous changes in muscle length and force. As shown in the above-cited study23, in two-joint movements, such changes can have both coinciding and opposing effects, whereas similar effects have not yet been studied on nerve-muscle preparations, which were traditionally restricted to studying muscle dynamics in isotony and isometric conditions, during slow length changes and in transient records under stepwise force or length changes (for review, see Kostyukov25. The present study combining external muscle movements evoked by RMD with active biofeedback control of muscle force may also expand the possibilities for studying nonlinear muscle dynamics in humans.

The following hypothesis is considered in the study. The interaction of the basic mechanical parameters of a large number of muscles involved in complex single-joint or multijoint movements remains a major challenge, particularly in finding fundamental relationships between these parameters and the corresponding central commands estimated from EMG signals. Theoretical modeling predicts minimal versus large changes in central commands for coinciding and opposing changes in muscle length and force, and the main aim of this study was to test this quantitatively. Previously, for two-joint circular movements23, we observed frequent violation of the standard direction of hysteresis effects in actively contracted muscles, and we tested the hypothesis that such a violation may be connected with a redistribution of activation intensities between agonist muscles.

Methods

Participants

Eight healthy right-handed men (aged 29.1 ± 6.9 years) participated in the experiments. The experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Bogomoletz Institute of Physiology, National Academy of Sciences, Kyiv, Ukraine, (protocol #5/13; 25.11.2023) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 1964. The experimental procedure did not exceed 1.2 h. Before the main session, participants practiced performing various tests up to ten times to become familiar with the setup and to correctly track the force command in the visual feedback mode.

EMG recording and data processing

The surface EMGs were registered from the following muscles: m. biceps brachii caput longum (BBcl), m. biceps brachii caput breve (BBcb), m. brachioradialis (Br), m. triceps brachii caput laterale (TBclat), and m. triceps brachii caput longum (TBcl); the abbreviations used throughout the remainder of the paper are given in brackets. The EMG signals were recorded by pairs of electrodes (Biopac System EL 503, USA). To ensure reliable EMG recording, electrodes were placed over the belly of each target muscle parallel to the fiber direction after skin preparation (alcohol and abrasive) and palpation during voluntary contractions, with a fixed interelectrode distance of 25 mm, corresponding to the range of 20–40 mm in accordance with Hermens et al.. recommendations26. Electrodes were positioned ≥ 2–3 cm from the borders of adjacent muscles to minimize crosstalk, with activation of the isolated muscle confirmed by preliminary motor tests preceding the main experimental program. The recorded activities were amplified via a 16-channel amplifier (CWE, Inc., PA 19003 USA) with 10–5000 Hz bandwidth for each channel. The following low-pass 10 Hz filtering attenuated distant interference, while preliminary movement tests confirmed discriminable EMG patterns for each muscle. The EMGs together with the position signals from the RMD were collected via a CED Power 1401 data acquisition system using the Spike 2 (Cambridge Electronic Design, UK) program. The signals were digitized at 2000 Hz, and software Origin 18 (OriginLab Corporation, USA) was used for off-line data analysis. The EMG signals were fully rectified and filtered (Batterworth filter of 4th order without a time shift and low-pass10 Hz filtering) in an off-line regimen. All tests were repeated 10 times to obtain the average corresponding records. At the end of each experiment, we registered the maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) of each muscle undergoing study. For this purpose, the average EMG signals during steady-state maximal isometric contractions of the muscles were recorded when the shoulder and elbow angles were near 70° and 90°, respectively. The MVC tests consisted of two - three maximal isometric muscle contractions lasting 20 s, which began after a short rest period of 4–5 min after the end of the main tests. Similarly, the minimal levels of EMG activity in fully relaxed muscles were evaluated. The average EMG activity registered in the main part of the experiments is shown as a percentage scale, which ranges from the above-defined minimal level of activity (0%) to the MVCs (100%).

Reconstruction of the transition trajectory and speed of movement of the RMD manipulator to ensure the target change in muscle length

It is clear that in a real single-joint movement, an experimenter can control only the joint angle changes and the force created by a participant’s hand. Earlier, it has been proposed several biomechanical models of the human arm with using MRI images of the bones of the participant’s arm with a real reproduction of their shape by 3D printing23,24,26. Based on these models, an approach has been developed to assess changes in the length of arm muscles during two-joint movements and to predict their forces, which are dependent on changes in the torque lever. In the present study, the shoulder joint angle did not change, which somewhat simplified the task. In reality, the shoulder was not actually fixed by any external restraints, but it also did not move at all, maintaining a stable position of the elbow due to the special shape of the trajectory of the participant’s hand holding the RMD handle. At the same time, the activity of the shoulder joint muscles, including the BBcl, as well as the moments of force associated with them were not taken into account in this study. The length changes in the BBcl during the test movements of the hand in the operational space were primarily considered. Reconstruction of the target force vector trajectories for biofeedback

In parallel with the imposed movement program considered above, a subject should generate a target force vector by using visual feedback (Fig. 1D). This force occurs when the subject’s hand presses the RMD manipulator containing a force vector sensor27. For simplicity, it was also initially assumed that the force is exerted by isolated contractions of the BBcl, and the force vector changes over time according to the given active force program. In particular, it was assumed that the muscle force would change similarly to externally imposed piecewise linear changes in its length, and the direction of these changes must coincide or oppose the muscle movement direction (the opposing forces are shown in Fig. 1E, F).

Statistical analysis

For statistical evaluations, we used analysis of variance with a repeated measure (RM ANOVA) with post hoc Bonferroni correction. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The specific statistical models used for analysis will be provided in the description of the respective results. IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software was used for the statistical analysis.

Results

A general description of the test movements

An example of a standard experiment is shown in Fig. 2. The main objective was to compare the EMG responses of three flexor muscles in two different movement paradigms with the same temporal changes in muscle length and force, directed oppositely or coinciding with each other, thereby representing opposing and coinciding patterns of movement, OP and CP (Fig. 2; see lines I and II). Muscle length trajectories remained unchanged across all tests and represented an inverted double trapezoid; force signals with a shape similar to the length had different amplitudes (A1, A2, A3) and corresponded to the OP and CP forces directed opposite to each other.

To compare EMG signals with respect to different movement patterns, OP and CP, we further labeled several elements on the movement trajectories (Fig. 2, II, Br). The entire trajectory of the reciprocating length change is divided into two sectors: s1 and s2; successive phases of uniform linear motion and length fixation are designated p1, p2, p3, and p4; and f1, f2, and f3, respectively. To visualize the hysteresis effects of contracted muscles, in addition to the normalized time records of muscle force and length (lines I and II) and averaged EMGs (line II) corresponding to three amplitude levels of force (A1–A3), the EMG signals were also presented as a function of the normalized length (line III). In this representation, it was possible to obtain a general impression about the form of hysteresis loops in the s1 and s2 sectors; however, for a detailed assessment of the hysteresis dynamics, we added time records of the differences in EMG signals in the phases of muscle shortening and lengthening: Ep1(t)-Ep4(t) and Ep2(t)-Ep3(t) (line IV). At the beginning of the above differences are the EMG signals of the muscles during shortening contractions; therefore, one can expect that positive and negative hysteresis values will correspond to “normal” or “abnormal” types of reactions, respectively.

By comparing temporary EMG recordings obtained in identical experiments on four different participants (Fig. 2, line II; 3, lines I, III; 4), we can conclude that among the various parameters that determine the active force of muscle contractions, the most powerful influence is exerted by the direction of the force waves, while the effect of changing their amplitude is not as significant. When the force waves are directed upward, i.e., opposite to the waves of changes in muscle length (OP columns in the figures), EMG responses tend to follow the force changes, introducing some natural distortions. In another case, when the directions of the length and force waves coincide (CP columns in the figures), one may obtain the impression of mutual compensation for their actions, which leads to a certain stabilization of EMG activity at a fairly high level. On the other hand, both the above following and compensation effects in the OP and CP in many cases are well supported by a corresponding well-expressed splitting of EMGs related to different amplitudes of the FWs. It was best of all seen in the s2 sectors of the test movements relating to a range of maximal elbow flexion. On the other hand, both the force following in the OP and the effects of force length compensation during CP are in many cases further enhanced by proper amplitude-dependent splitting of EMG recordings. This can best be seen in the s2 sectors of the test movements within the range of maximum elbow flexion. However, this “correct” order of EMG trajectories (higher EMG levels corresponding to higher force amplitudes) is often violated, sometimes significantly (see, for example, Fig. 2, II, BBcl, CP; Fig. 3, I, BBcb and BBcl).

An intriguing finding of the present study is that we often observed a violation of the standard hysteresis type of muscle response. Standard hysteresis can be recorded by experiencing muscle contractions in the so-called state of neuromuscular preparation (muscles with a portion of the nerve supply for stimulation)19 or by experimentally simulating real joints using a mechanical connection toward each of two separate muscles in the above states30. It has been shown that in these participants, actively contracting muscles will always require a greater intensity of efferent activation when they are shortened than when they are lengthened. For the present experimental paradigm, this approach requires that the muscle length-EMG loops have a counterclockwise direction and that the sectoral (s1, s2) difference between EMG records along the muscle shortening and lengthening branches be strictly positive. However, one can notice numerous departures from these rules (Figs. 2, III, IV and 3, II, IV). As a bright example, we can consider reactions of the BBcb muscle under OP conditions in the experiment presented in Fig. 2. The responses of this muscle exhibit large hysteresis loops in the OP paradigm, with these loops directed counterclockwise and approaching each other at different amplitudes of force change. This direction means that the muscle is activated by a less intense efferent influx during shortening (phases p1 and p2 of movement); on the other hand, it receives a significant increase in activation during reverse lengthening movement (phases p3 and p4). Interestingly, in this study, the “reverse” direction of hysteresis was observed in all flexor muscles studied; it is obvious that “normal” types of reactions in flexor muscles that were not accessible for recording cannot be excluded. At least in BBcl, anticlockwise oscillating elements of hysteresis were observed in sector s2 in OP movements (Fig. 2, IV, BBcl, OP).

Both the EMG signals themselves and their hysteresis properties during CP movements were noticeably different from those of similar reactions associated with OP. Along with the general stabilization of EMG reactions and the obvious decrease in the amplitude range of their changes throughout the entire cycle of test movements, it is likely that the hysteresis itself has become less pronounced for CP movements. Overall, the hysteretic difference between shortening and lengthening EMG signals often fluctuated around zero, but clear positive responses could also be observed, which was therefore consistent with the natural behavior of isolated muscles (Fig. 3, II, BBcb and BBcl).

The efferent commands sent to the elbow extensor muscles in the above tests were much simpler in nature than those sent to the flexor muscles (Fig. 4). In contrast to the type of EMG flexor described above, the responses of both extensors in our tests are very similar and quite predictable. These responses corresponded well to the force profiles produced by flexor contractions, and sometimes, in contrast to agonist muscles, EMGs recorded from extensors were very sensitive to changes in force amplitude. This was very clear in the EMG recordings associated with the minimum and maximum force amplitudes (Fig. 4 IV, V). The severity of the hysteresis effects in the extensors was apparently not noticeably different, and nearly coincident shortened and lengthened EMG signals were observed. Hysteresis effects in extensors are best observed at maximum force amplitudes, and in these cases, it is likely possible to indicate their “correct” direction, with higher EMG intensities corresponding to shortening movement (Fig. 4 IV, V).

Hysteresis relationships, including force, length, and EMG intensity, are among the three main parameters that determine the state of active contracting muscles in human limbs and constitute an important group of nonlinear effects in the human motor system. When comparing the length-EMG hysteresis loop intensities between movement sections s1 and s2 (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), significant differences can be noted, which are observed both in their amplitude range and in their direction. For this reason, for statistical analysis, we added additional factor S as the sector binding factor; the respective EMGs were distinguished by hatching the s2 columns with the same color (Fig. 7). Hysteresis coefficients (separately for each sector) were calculated as the difference between the corresponding mean EMG signals at the shortening and lengthening movement phases: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{H1}={M}_{p1}-{M}_{p4}$$\end{document}(s1) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{H2}={M}_{p2}-{M}_{p3}$$\end{document}(s2). The positivity (negativity) of the hysteresis coefficients corresponds to the normal (atypical) difference between EMG intensities at the trajectories of muscle shortening and lengthening.

Model comparison of OP and CP movements

Previously, we proposed a “thermodynamic” model, which attempted to consider the central commands (interpreted as EMG intensities, E) entering the muscle in terms of a nonlinear function of two variables: muscle length (L) and force (F)31,32. Discussion

When studying central commands in various types of two-joint human movements23,33,34, we noted the importance of switching points at which trajectories of length and/or force reverse their directions. Consequently, in real movements, various combinations of these directions can continuously occur, but an adequate interpretation of these effects can often be difficult due to insufficient information about the passage of these processes in an individual muscle. Studies on muscle dynamics were restricted predominantly by a constant rate of efferent stimulation in isometry and isotony, slow lengthening and shortening movements, transient movements during transitions from isometry to isotony, and muscle length changes under rate-modulated stimulation (for an overview, see the monograph by Kostyukov25. In these studies, to determine the functional relationship between two of the three parameters of the state of an actively contracting muscle, length, force and stimulation rate (considered analogous to activation intensity, or EMG), it was necessary to fix a third parameter. Abandoning this rigid classical framework for analyzing a nonlinear dynamic system of three variables, this approach attempts to estimate one of them, the central commands of the muscle, under the condition that both other variables, length and force, vary simultaneously. Thus, our experimental task consisted of localization of two independent types of control: external passive control of muscle length using RMD and internal active control of muscle force by the subject himself using visual biofeedback. Therefore, we try to obtain information about central commands coming to muscles during simultaneous changes in both the length of the muscle and its force. More details about this approach are described elsewhere23,28.

This study was restricted by examining identical profiles of length and force waveforms consisting of a double trapezoid with symmetrical biphasic linear leading and trailing edges. EMGs related to OP and CP movement patterns were compared. During OP movements, the averaged EMG waveforms follow the programmed time changes of force, naturally distorting them to some extent, whereas during CP movements, rather strong mutual compensation of the effects of force and length occurs, which leads to a decrease in the range of EMG changes (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Statistical analysis revealed the absence of influence of the amplitude force factor on the integrated areas of the EMGs and mean amplitudes at their apexes, whereas the directional force factor and interaction of both factors were mostly effective. A weak dependence of flexor EMG signals on the amplitude of the sweep of force could look slightly strange; however, at present, we can propose only a standard hypothesis about some redistribution of activity between agonists and incomplete EMG information related to the inapproachability of deep elbow flexors. On the other hand, in experiments with isotonic linear flexion movements in the elbow joint, complex EMG patterns were recorded, including nonmonotonic declines and rises, as well as a complex redistribution of EMG activity between different areas of the same muscle35.

Despite the relative closeness of the integral characteristics of the EMG responses of the elbow flexors in the OP and CP motion profiles (Fig. 5, lines I, II), most of the other parameters demonstrated striking differences. This is concerning of their amplitudes and the apexes of the length and force changes (Fig. 5, lines III, IV), asymmetry properties of the EMG waves (Fig. 6, lines I, II), uncertainty of the mean EMGs at the intermediate level of the length fixation (Fig. 6, lines III, IV), and an expression of the hysteresis effects at both sectors of movement, s1 and s2 (Fig. 7). The stabilizing effect of CP movements on EMG reactions can be observed as their low slopes of change in both sectors (Fig. 8). The asymmetric properties of EMG responses, which depend heavily on the direction of movement to steady states under force interaction with the environment, can be seen as further evidence against simplistic equilibrium state concepts in movement regulation36–38. The obtained results indicate that the prehistory of changes in both the lengths of the muscles involved in a given movement program and their forces can be a source of uncertainty in the incoming central commands; therefore, it is unproductive to extrapolate some changes in mechanical parameters to others, even for cases when their final steady states coincide39.

An important question that may arise in any study of motor control is whether the experimental results obtained are consistent with evident and well-known results of neuromuscular physiology established in experiments on single muscles. In the context of the present study, this phenomenon might be known as hysteresis of muscle contraction, in accordance with which any shortening of an active muscle requires higher activation intensities than lengthening. In particular, this was demonstrated in experiments on the cat soleus40, in which, using linear reciprocating changes in the rate of distributed stimulation of efferents and isotonic loads, counterclockwise loops were registered in coordinates: length-stimulation rate. In the present experiments, we can often observe oppositely directed hysteresis. In the length-EMG intensity coordinates, which can be considered a direct analog of the length-stimulation rate coordinates discussed above, opposite directed loops were recorded during OP movements (Figs. 2 and 3). Moreover, for all elbow flexors studied, striking differences were found when comparing the statistical parameters of the hysteresis coefficients in OP and CP movements, which were predominantly negative in the first case and positive in the second. Therefore, within the agonist muscles, slow linear changes in muscle length occur, first leading to muscle shortening and lengthening, which may be considered atypical reactions. However, in addition to the differences between animal and human muscles, two types of differences exist between the experiments being compared. First, in the present study, more complex patterns of muscle activation were used than isotonic muscle loading in the animal experiments. Second, instead of a single muscle in the case of neuromuscular preparation, human experiments involve reactions of several agonist muscles. It seems likely that the first type of difference could be re-examined experimentally using appropriately controlled changes in muscle length and different rate-modulated stimulation patterns. For the second type, it seems necessary to conduct tests with the largest possible coverage of EMG recordings and/or select joints with fewer agonists for analysis. It seems to be generally accepted that nonlinear characteristics of muscle contraction can influence the organization of motor control16,19. The idea of synergistic motor control usually arises from the fact that the nervous system can combine muscle activity into certain groups of synergies. The present study demonstrates similar EMG patterns in the elbow flexor group, which form part of broader groups of synergies in multijoint arm movements.

The stability of the synergies in motor actions convincingly confirms the existence of this concept of motor control in humans. Accordingly, various studies have confirmed the possibility of such control mechanisms in movements of the upper extremities in humans41–43. It has been demonstrated that muscle synergy can probably reconstruct muscle activity during virtual trajectories of arm movement or force generation, and synergy models can sometimes satisfactorily reconstruct variations in EMG data44,45.

Interestingly, according to generally accepted ideas about the contractile properties of muscles, with an increase in EMG as the muscle contracts, one would expect some autoamplification of EMG due to the supposed effects of additional internal shortening of the contractile elements of the muscle46. Instead, in many cases, we can observe oppositely directed effects at the returning phase of movement. However, these results need further experimental substantiation on isolated muscle. At present, we are able to speculate only about the hypothetical existence of some kind of “redistribution” process between the activities of the agonist muscles and the action of these processes on the overall force. In favor of this assumption, we can point to frequent observations of random changes in the amplitudes of EMG signals related to certain force amplitudes (A1–A3), provided that more natural reactions are restored in subsequent tests.

In the present study, we concentrated our attention on the simplest single-joint movements. In multijoint movements or highly coordinated movements, including movements of the entire body and several limbs, such as walking, three partially independent types of muscle synergy are usually considered: kinematic synergy3–5, force (kinetic) synergy3,6, and activation (muscle) synergy7–11,47–49. It seems that the above divisions of synergistic connections between muscles for complex movements of the body and limbs in many cases may be more declarative and descriptive in nature than have a real justification in the anatomical and physiological context. Much more realistic and useful is the consideration of synergistic interactions between muscles acting around a single joint; this approach involves not only the close similarity of muscle behavior within two functional groups, flexors and extensors but also the real mechanical and central interactions of these groups. In the context of the present experimental paradigm, we can point out a predominant coactivation pattern in the activity of extensor muscles during pure flexor-dependent movements. Recording the same movement-dependent parameters in flexor and extensor muscles often demonstrated their rather close resemblance (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8). Moreover, in contrast to the more variable nature of the EMG changes in the flexors, the responses of the extensors are more predictable and correspond mainly to the profiles of the forces created by the flexors when performing the task. Interestingly, hysteresis effects in extensors were not noticeable, indicating that they were in the “correct” direction, with higher EMG intensities corresponding to shorter movement. Coactivation of the antagonist muscles belonging to the same joint is widely used in a variety of motor tasks37,50,51. This pattern can appreciably increase the joint stiffness that is of paramount importance for complex limb movements when precise positioning of distal segments requires additional fixation of more proximal joints52–55. In a study by Gorkovenko et al.30 using an experimental joint model consisting of two muscles simulating an agonist–antagonist pair, it was shown that coactivation patterns can clearly reduce hysteresis effects; however, reciprocal activation of antagonists was more suitable for precise regulation of both the amplitude and velocity of joint movements.

Conclusions

The interaction of the basic mechanical parameters of a large number of muscles involved in complex single-joint or multijoint movements remains a major challenge, particularly in searching for fundamental relationships between these parameters and the corresponding central commands estimated from EMG recordings. The present study provides experimental support by theoretically predicting, using the T-model, the existence of small and large changes in central commands for coinciding and opposing changes in muscle length and force. The study showed frequent violation of the standard direction of hysteretic effects in actively contracting muscles; such disruptions may be due, at least in part, to redistribution of activation intensities between agonist muscles; however, these results will probably need to be reconsidered in acute animal experiments given that approaches involving simultaneous changes in muscle length and force have not been previously used.
