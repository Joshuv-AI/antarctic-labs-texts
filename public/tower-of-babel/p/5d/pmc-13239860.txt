Re‐Evaluating Springtime as Southern Arizona's Dust Season
Authors: Ellis S. Robinson, Labiba J. Noushin, Carolina Martinez Guajardo, Chris Chaeha Lim, Avelino Arellano, Armin Sorooshian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe existing literature largely identifies spring as Arizona's predominant dust season, when synoptic‐scale dust events are most frequent and “Fine Soil” measurements from the IMPROVE (Interagency Monitoring of Protected Visual Environments) network reach a yearly maximum. Because IMPROVE observations are primarily derived from remote monitoring sites, however, they may not reflect exposure‐relevant dust seasonality in Arizona's urban areas, where anthropogenic activities can strongly influence coarse particulate matter (PM) concentrations. Consistent with this possibility, the notion of “spring as dust season” is less clear in other surface data sets that serve as dust proxies, including PM coarse and PM10. Here we analyze (a) regulatory monitoring of hourly PM10 and PM coarse concentrations, (b) IMPROVE measurements of 24‐hr Fine Soil and PM coarse concentrations, and (c) sub‐hourly PM coarse concentrations from a low‐cost urban sensor network (SUNSET, Sonoran Unified Network of Sensors for Environmental Tracking). Our analysis reveals a different picture of dust seasonality. Summer has the highest concentrations of regulatory PM10 and PM coarse, followed by fall and then spring, whereas IMPROVE‐measured Fine Soil peaks in spring. Summer also has the most PM10 NAAQS exceedances. Seasonal patterns differ between urban and rural environments, and concentrations are generally higher in more anthropogenically influenced areas. Diurnal cycles, weekday–weekend differences, a COVID‐era anomaly in 2020, and urban spatial gradients further indicate strong anthropogenic influences. Together, these results suggest that while springtime dust is climatologically important at remote sites, it does not represent exposure‐relevant dust seasonality in Arizona's urban areas.

Introduction

Dust is one of the most abundant aerosol types by mass globally, accounting for potentially the majority of total atmospheric particulate loading (Kok et al., 2017). Dust influences the Earth's radiative balance through both scattering and absorption of both short‐ and long wave radiation, and affects cloud microphysical processes (Mahowald et al., 2014) by acting as both cloud condensation nuclei (Kumar et al., 2011) and ice nuclei (DeMott et al., 2003). In many arid and semi‐arid regions, dust also degrades visibility and contributes significantly to regional haze conditions (Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023). Human exposure to airborne dust is linked to adverse respiratory outcomes (Gross et al., 2018), cardiovascular disease (Dominguez‐Rodríguez et al., 2021), stroke (Kamouchi et al., 2012), and increased mortality (Tong et al., 2023). In the southwestern United States, dust additionally serves as a carrier for pathogenic bioaerosols such as Coccidioides spores, the causative agent of Valley fever (Tong et al., 2017), highlighting its importance for both environmental and public health.

Dust is a complex chemical mixture that can be challenging to measure directly and quantify comprehensively. Dust particles span over four orders of magnitude in diameter—from sub‐micron clays to coarse, sand‐sized grains—and exhibit broad variability in composition, morphology, and optical properties that can depend on source geology, weathering state, and transport history (Bondy et al., 2018; Fitzgerald et al., 2015; Kandler et al., 2009; Maring et al., 2003). Because dust spans a wide range of particle sizes and compositions, individual measurement techniques typically characterize only a subset of the dust population (Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2023; Reid et al., 2003). Thus, studies aiming to understand dust typically rely on proxy‐measurements that capture some fraction of dust or quantities that are assumed to correlate tightly with “dust” as a whole. These can include calculated “Fine Soil” mass from chemical speciation networks (e.g., Interagency Monitoring of Protected Visual Environments or IMPROVE, Malm et al. (1994)); coarse‐mode particulate matter (PM) mass concentrations, such as total suspended particles (TSP), sub‐10 μm PM (PM10), or PM between 2.5 and 10 μm (PM10−2.5 or “PM coarse,” Aryal and Evans (2022)); tracer measurements of crustal elements (e.g., particulate calcium, aluminum, silicon, iron, or titanium), aerosol optical depth, and others. Each proxy captures a different facet of the dust burden, and collectively can provide a more complete picture of dust behavior.

The American Southwest, including Arizona, is the most dust‐impacted region in North America (Kok et al., 2021) due to its arid climate, sparse vegetation, exposed soil surfaces, and frequent high‐wind events (Sorooshian et al., 2011). Regional aerosol climatologies indicate that coarse‐mode PM constitutes a substantial fraction of total particulate matter in the region, with PM10‐to‐PM2.5 ratios frequently much higher than in other parts of the United States (J. Hand et al., 2019). Episodic, high‐concentration dust “events” driven by strong frontal passages, pre‐monsoonal troughs, and convective outflows are well documented and are known contributors to exceedances of the PM10 National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) in Arizona and surrounding states (Ardon‐Dryer, 2025; Hyde et al., 2018). Dust events can be broadly categorized as either “synoptic” or “convective” based on their meteorological drivers; the latter typically are shorter in duration and localized in scale, while the former tend to be longer and more regional (Sandhu et al., 2024). Here we use the term “dust event” to denote episodic periods of elevated airborne dust concentrations, while “dust storm” refers to the most intense of these events associated with strong winds and widespread dust mobilization. The regulatory relevance of dust events underscores the need to better understand dust seasonality not only from a climatological perspective but also in terms of population exposure and compliance with health‐based standards, especially in the fast‐growing U.S. Southwest. Arizona cities are among the fastest‐growing urban areas in the country; Maricopa County (population 4,420,568, per 2020 Decennial Census (U.S. Census Bureau, 2021)), where Phoenix is located, grew 16% between 2010 and 2020, and Pima County (pop. 1,043,433), home to Tucson, grew 6% during the same timeframe.

A prevailing narrative in the literature identifies spring as the dominant dust season in Arizona and the broader Southwest (Aryal & Evans, 2022; J. L. Hand et al., 2016, 2024; Sorooshian et al., 2011, 2013). IMPROVE Fine Soil routinely exhibits a springtime maximum at remote (rural) monitoring sites in the U.S. Southwest (J. L. Hand et al., 2016, 2024; Sorooshian et al., 2011). Spring also marks a meteorological transition period characterized by strong synoptic‐scale forcing capable of mobilizing surface sediments. Deep mid‐latitude troughs, lee cyclogenesis, intense cold frontal passages, and associated surface pressure gradients drive elevated wind speeds that enhance dust entrainment over dry, unvegetated surfaces (Luo et al., 2004). Increasing solar heating and atmospheric destabilization during the pre‐monsoon (May–June) period further promote vertical mixing and dust lofting (Pandithurai et al., 2008). Additionally, springtime circulation patterns can facilitate long‐range transport of dust from regional (e.g., Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts) and occasionally intercontinental sources (e.g., Asia) (Kavouras et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2021). These factors all contribute to an established perception of spring as the climatologically significant “dust season” in Arizona.

However, other evidence complicates this paradigm. Several recent studies suggest that dust seasonality can vary significantly across land‐use types, especially when considering urban areas where anthropogenic activities such as vehicle‐induced road dust resuspension, construction, and agricultural operations play a major role in coarse PM emissions (Adebiyi et al., 2025; Clements et al., 2017; Fitz & Bumiller, 2021; Rienda & Alves, 2021). Furthermore, the frequency of high‐wind synoptic dust storms may be lower than the cumulative impact of recurring, moderate‐intensity dust resuspension events in summer or fall (Sandhu et al., 2024). Given that the aerosol size distributions for synoptic dust storms tend toward smaller particles compared to convective storms (Ardon‐Dryer & Kelley, 2022), there are open questions about how the cut‐size diameter for dust proxy measurements (from e.g., IMPROVE) may bias conclusions drawn about dust as a whole (Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2023). Dust size distributions tend to shift toward smaller sizes over the course of atmospheric transport, due to preferential removal of larger particles by gravitational settling (Maring et al., 2003). And, particularly in densely populated regions, there is regular exposure to airborne dust outside of these high‐concentration dust events, which may have strong anthropogenic drivers (Murphy et al., 2008) that are decoupled from the seasonality of meteorological conditions.

There is thus a critical question about whether the established spring dust season narrative adequately reflects population‐weighted exposure to dust in Arizona's urban airsheds, and this knowledge gap has important implications for air quality management and health risk assessment. While large synoptic dust storms may dominate remote, fine‐mode dust climatologies, urban populations may be more likely to be affected by coarse‐mode aerosol from locally‐generated or regionally‐transported dust influenced by anthropogenic activity. As PM10 NAAQS exceedances increasingly involve coarse dust contributions (Pinal County Air Quality Control District, 2023; Sharratt & Edgar, 2011), a refined understanding of dust seasonality in populated areas is necessary for effective mitigation and exposure assessment.

Here we address this gap by examining temporal patterns in dust proxy measurements in Arizona using three complementary data sets: (a) hourly PM10 and PM coarse concentrations from regulatory monitors, (b) 24‐hr Fine Soil and PM coarse concentrations from the IMPROVE network, and (c) sub‐hourly PM coarse concentrations from a dense, low‐cost sensor network in urban Tucson (SUNSET: Sonoran Unified Network of Sensors for Environmental Tracking). By comparing seasonal signals across data sets, land‐use categories, and location settings (e.g., Urban vs. Rural), we evaluate whether the canonical spring dust paradigm represents exposure‐relevant dust trends. We further illustrate the variation of strong anthropogenic influences through examining diurnal cycles, weekday–weekend differences, and anomalous behavior during COVID‐19 (year 2020) across location settings, and examine the spatial variability of coarse PM across an urban environment at high spatial resolution.

Methods

Hourly PM10 and PM2.5 Mass Concentrations

Data Download

Hourly PM10 and PM2.5 mass concentration data come from regulatory monitoring sites. We downloaded pre‐generated data files from the EPA's AirData database for both hourly PM10 and PM2.5 (“Hourly Data—Particulates,” https://aqs.epa.gov/aqsweb/airdata/download_files.html#Raw; also accessible via https://www.epa.gov/outdoor‐air‐quality‐data and then clicking “Pre‐generated Data Files”). We only consider Federal Reference Method (FRM) or Federal Equivalent Method (FEM), and do not include “non‐FRM/FEM” data. All FRM and FEM PM data are reported at ambient temperature and pressure (ATP), meaning that there is no correction of the air volume sampled to some other reference conditions (e.g., standard temperature and pressure (STP)). In other words, these data, along with all other data analyzed and reported in this manuscript, represent ambient PM concentrations at each site.

While data reported to EPA's AirData and Air Quality System (AQS) databases tend to be collected and managed by state, local, and tribal environmental agencies, for brevity we refer to this data set throughout the manuscript using “EPA” because of where we downloaded the data. The EPA AQS parameter codes for PM10 and PM2.5 measurements considered here are 81,102 and 88,101, respectively. These are the primary EPA AQS parameter codes for FRM/FEM PM10 and PM2.5 measurements, and were selected to ensure consistency across monitoring sites. While the webpage above does provide PM coarse data sets, those data are 24‐hr averages. Instead, we calculate PM coarse for use in this analysis from the hourly PM10 and PM2.5 data (PM coarse = PM10—PM2.5) at sites with both measurements.

Inclusion Criteria

All hourly PM10 and PM2.5 measurements contain metadata used for quality control (QC), namely the “Qualifier” variable. Qualifiers are used to indicate when measurements suffered from operational issues (e.g., “Flow Rate Average out of Spec.”) or anomalous ambient conditions/events (e.g., “High Winds” or “African Dust”). In general, we did not include those qualifiers indicating abnormal operating issues, but did include qualifiers indicating anomalous ambient conditions (e.g., “High Winds”), though the vast majority (98.5%) of the data set did not have any noted QC concerns. Of the qualifiers referring to anomalous ambient conditions, “High Winds” was by far the most prevalent. We chose to include this qualifier and others like it because they do not indicate a problem with the measurement itself, and we do not want to bias our data set by not including data where for example, wind‐blown dust is present. Qualifiers like “High Winds” are applied during agency QC review rather than generated automatically from meteorological thresholds; therefore their frequency may vary across monitoring programs and should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of wind conditions (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2026). Table S1 in Supporting Information S1 shows the full list of which qualifiers are found in the data set and those used for inclusion. Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1 shows a time series of the total number of PM10 observations in our data set colored by qualifier codes.

A small fraction (less than 0.5%) of the PM10 and PM2.5 data sets that we downloaded contain negative concentration values. While negative PM concentrations have no physical meaning, they typically reflect high instrumental noise relative to low PM concentrations. EPA AQS accepts negative values that are greater than the negative of the method detection limit (MDL) (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2011), and we keep these negative values in our final data set assuming they were not otherwise discarded per the above discussion of qualifiers.

Daily Aerosol Composition

Data Download

Aerosol composition data comes from the IMPROVE monitoring network, and consists of 24‐hr concentration measurements of aerosol elements and ion species, measured every one‐in‐six or one‐in‐three days. We also use PM coarse mass concentrations from the same data source. We downloaded IMPROVE data from the Federal Land Manager Environmental Database (https://views.cira.colostate.edu/fed/QueryWizard/) for all sites in the state of Arizona from 2000 through 2024. A full list of IMPROVE sites used in this analysis along with relevant details (e.g., name, latitude/longitude, location setting, elevation, etc.) can be found in Table S2 of Supporting Information S1.

While it has been described in full detail elsewhere (see e.g., Malm et al., 1994), briefly: IMPROVE monitoring sites measure PM across four different channels (J. L. Hand, 2023). Three of the channels (A, B, C) use 2.5 μm cyclones, each collecting PM on a unique filter substrate employed for different offline analyses (e.g., nylon filters are used for offline ion chromatography). The fourth channel (D) uses a 10 μm cyclone. Channels A and D are used for gravimetric analysis of PM10 and PM2.5 mass concentration, respectively. Importantly, all chemical composition information from IMPROVE is performed only on the fine (sub‐2.5 μm) particulate fraction, and not on coarse particles.

Parameter Description

As mentioned above, channels A, B, and C each collect fine particles on different substrates for offline analysis. For this paper, we are concerned with the chemical composition information from channel A, which provides elemental analysis for particles collected on teflon filters via X‐ray fluorescence (XRF). Measurements of aluminum (Al), silicon (Si), calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), and titanium (Ti) are used to estimate total “Fine Soil,” aka “PM2.5 dust,” with the following parameterization (J. L. Hand, 2023): (1) FineSoil=2.53⋅Al+2.86⋅Si+1.87⋅Ca+2.78⋅Fe+2.23⋅Ti

These key crustal elements are assumed to exist as common oxides found in dust (e.g., SiO2, Al2O3, etc.) and are scaled by the appropriate corresponding stoichiometric factors. We also use PM10 and PM2.5 measured from IMPROVE to calculate PM coarse.

Inclusion Criteria

We use the conservative approach of filtering the data set based on the metadata (“status”) flags where all four channels are “normal” (“NM” flag). We examined whether there were substantial seasonal or site biases for filtering the data this way, and found that there were not. Roughly 77% of the data has NM status across all four channels, and these were relatively evenly distributed across season, year, and site. Table S2 in Supporting Information S1 summarizes the overall data coverage across IMPROVE sites for our data set.

PM Coarse From an Urban Low‐Cost Sensor Network

The Sonoran Unified Network of Sensors for Environmental Tracking (SUNSET) consists of MODULAIR sensors (QuantAQ, Inc.) placed outdoors at 20 different Pima County, Arizona schools to provide real‐time, open access air quality information. MODULAIR sensors report PM mass concentration estimates and criteria gas pollutant mixing ratio estimates, and have been described in detail elsewhere (Hagan & Cross, 2024). Briefly, we describe the PM measurement, which is relevant to the analysis presented herein.

MODULAIRs measure PM across a wide size range using a combination of nephelometer and optical particle counter (OPC) sensors. The nephelometer estimates mass at smaller particle sizes (<350 nm), while the OPC sizes larger particles (>350 nm) using single particle light scattering. Importantly, unlike some other widely‐used lower‐cost PM sensors (e.g., PurpleAir, see e.g., Rueda et al., 2023), MODULAIRs are able to appropriately measure larger particles due both to minimal inertial losses and relying on OPC‐based detection instead of nephelometry. In short, MODULAIRs can accurately estimate mass concentrations of for example, PM10 and PM coarse (Westgate et al., 2025).

For our analysis, we retrieve 1‐min average PM10 and PM2.5 measurements from 18 of the Tucson‐area SUNSET sensors, and use these to calculate PM coarse. This data set spans August 2024 to October 2025. Due to this limited timeframe compared to the multi‐decade EPA and IMPROVE data sets, we do not use SUNSET to assess seasonal variability.

We performed the following QC procedures on the MODULAIR data sets: We filtered out periods potentially impacted by fog per the flagging procedure outlined by McClosky and Hagan (2024). On account of Tucson's arid climate, very few measurements (<0.001% of the data set) are potentially impacted by fog artifacts, and do not impact any of the results we present herein. We also include only measurements where the “device_state” variable was “ACTIVE,” which pertains to the vast majority (>99.99%) of the data set.

IMPROVE Sites

We designated each IMPROVE monitoring site as either being in an “Urban” or “Rural” location setting, using a 10 km buffer around U.S. Census Bureau “urban area” designations (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022) to include sites as Urban. All sites not within 10 km of an urban area are designated as rural. The Phoenix sites (“PHOE1” and “PHOE5”) are unambiguously urban in their setting, located roughly ∼5 km north of the downtown core at “JLG (James L. Guyton) Supersite” (site ID: 040139997; coordinates: ‐112.0958, 33.5038). The JLG Supersite monitoring station is part of both IMPROVE and the EPA National Core network, and is operated by Arizona Department of Environmental Quality (Arizona Department of Environmental Quality, 2024). The two Tucson‐area sites, each located in National Park Service parks (Saguaro East (“SAGU1”) and Saguaro West (“SAWE1”) National Parks), are also designated as Urban. These two sites are far less urban in character compared to the Phoenix sites, and may better be described as “urban proximate” or “near‐urban”; however, they fall within the 10 km buffer, and keep the number of categories to two for simplicity. In the context of Arizona, where there are only two major population centers, it is reasonable for these sites not to be included in the Rural category, which is typified by remote locations much further removed from urban areas. The Nogales site (“NOGA1”) is also categorized as Urban, as it is located within the Nogales urban area. While Nogales is by far the smallest of the three urban areas discussed, aerosol composition at this site is nominally similar to the Phoenix sites, which are distinct from all of the Rural IMPROVE sites in Arizona, and so categorization as Urban here also is sensible. Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1 shows seasonal composition for Nogales in comparison with Phoenix and Rural sites, and Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1 shows a map of the urban areas and 10 km buffer relative to each site designated as Urban. Table S2 in Supporting Information S1 contains details for all IMPROVE sites, including their location setting designation.

Regulatory Sites

Regulatory (“EPA”) sites shown in the map in Figure 1 have either PM10 data collection only or both PM10 and PM2.5, allowing for the calculation of PM coarse at hourly resolution, as noted in a previous section. We refer to sites based on this distinction as making up the either the “PM10” or “PM coarse” data sets used in this analysis. Regulatory sites are also classified according to surrounding land‐uses (“Land‐use category”) and population density (“Location setting”), per EPA designations. This site information was found under the “Site and Monitor Descriptions” part of the EPA Data Download webpage (see link in “Data Download” section above), and joined to data via the “Site ID” variable. Table S3 in Supporting Information S1 contains a list of all EPA sites used with their land‐use category, location setting, and data set designations.

Data Analysis

We categorize data throughout the paper into four seasons (Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall), using meteorological season definitions where each season is comprised of three round months (U.S. National Centers for Environmental Information, 2023). For example, Winter is comprised of the months of December, January, and February and is referred to as “Winter (DJF)” in each figure we present, and so on for example, “Spring (MAM),” etc. We exclude year 2020 from the calculation of seasonal averages presented in Figure 1. We also only include data from monitoring sites that have close to full coverage across the calendar year, defined by data coverage in at least 10 individual months at each site in each year.

Diurnal patterns (shown in Figures 3, 6 and Figures S6, S7 in Supporting Information S1) are hourly mean values calculated for all data points within each group or monitor (e.g., rural‐residential in winter). For the diurnal plots for EPA monitoring sites (e.g., PM10 in Figure 3) we only include data from 2015 onwards, excluding year 2020, in an effort to reduce the influence that long‐term trends or anomalous behavior during the height of COVID may have on ambient concentrations.

Box and whisker plots (shown in Figures 2 and 4) are “Tukey‐style:” they show a central box from the 1st to 3rd quartile (Q1–Q3) with a line at the median (Q2) and whiskers that extend to the most extreme data point within 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) from Q1 and Q3, extending down (e.g., Q1–1.5 × IQR) and up, respectively. All values beyond the boundaries of the whiskers are plotted individually as outliers.

Box plots of Fall‐to‐Spring concentration ratios for different variables (Fine Soil, PM coarse, PM10) for different land‐use categories within each of the IMPROVE and EPA data sets. We compare non‐year 2020 data for each land‐use category with year 2020 data across all categories.

To quantify the weekday‐weekend effect (shown in Figure 4), we calculated a “Normalized concentration” value, similar to Murphy et al. (2008) and others (Lopez et al., 2015). Normalization here is meant to assess the day‐of‐week influence on concentrations relative to an appropriate baseline. For each monitoring site, we calculated: (a) seasonal average values within a multi‐year time window (e.g., 3 years) using all days of the week, and (b) seasonal average values within the same time window for each individual day of the week. Each data point displayed in Figure 4 represents the latter of these quantities divided by the former. This approach is meant to reduce any conflation of seasonal variation or long‐term year‐over‐year changes in concentrations as day‐of‐week difference. The results presented in Figure 4 use a 3‐year time window, but our results are not sensitive to this choice for time windows (between 2 and 10 years). We also exclude year 2020 from this calculation for the same reason as above.

For assessing differences in normalized concentrations for weekdays versus weekends (e.g., Figure 4), we used Welch's t‐test. These data are relatively normally distributed, but had unequal variances. We use a p‐value threshold of 0.05 to determine significance, but report the calculated p‐value for each comparison. We also omit Monday data from our statistical tests comparing weekday‐weekend differences, as Monday exhibits a minimum that may be influenced by activities (or the lack thereof) from Sunday.

For assessing differences between the Fall‐to‐Spring ratios (Figure 2) of different quantities we use Wilcoxon rank‐sum testing, which does not require normality or equal variances and is robust to extreme outlier values, and so suits these right‐skewed data. Again we use a p‐value threshold of 0.05 to determine significance, but report the calculated p‐value for each comparison.

Using the EPA PM10 data set, we calculate the number of days (defined as 24‐hr periods starting at midnight) with average concentrations above the 24‐hr PM10 NAAQS of 150 μg m−3, and refer to these as “violation days.” We only included days with complete (n = 24) hourly measurements. It is likely that our tabulation of these violations is not directly comparable to the regulatory record, which allows for some “exceptional events” to be discounted as violations; our tabulation would presumably include these. Exceptional events are defined by the EPA as “unusual or naturally occurring events that can affect air quality but are not reasonably controllable,” which can include for example, dust storms and blowing dust events. While it may make sense to exclude these events in a regulatory context, here we are interested in how exceptional events contribute to dust exposure and seasonality. We included year 2020 data for the violation day calculation, though the conclusions we draw from this analysis were not sensitive to this decision.

Results

This analysis presents seasonal, diurnal, and day‐of‐week patterns for measured quantities commonly used as proxies for dust: calculated Fine Soil, PM coarse, and PM10. We also calculate the number of days in violation of the PM10 NAAQS by season, categorized by land‐use category and location setting. As discussed in the Introduction, none of these quantities are direct measurements of dust, and each may have its own limitations in representing dust as a whole.

The coarse fraction of PM10 in this region, calculated using qualifying sites in the EPA data set, is relatively high compared to other environments (e.g., mid‐latitude humid continental): the median coarse fraction from our EPA data set is 75% (min. 67%‐max. 84%). Given this high coarse fraction and that PM10 is much more widely measured in the EPA network in Arizona than sites where both PM10 and PM2.5 measurements are co‐located (allowing for the calculation of PM coarse), we also present PM10 measurements here as a proxy measurement for dust.

Seasonal Patterns of Fine Soil, PM Coarse, and PM10

Different data sets and metrics support variable conclusions on the seasonality of dust in southern Arizona. The tile plot in Figure 1 presents average concentrations of Fine Soil, PM coarse, and PM10 by season for each of the IMPROVE and EPA data sets. IMPROVE Fine Soil has considerably higher concentrations in spring compared to the next‐highest season (summer) for both Rural and Urban sites (1.4× and 1.2× higher, respectively). Spring is not the dominant season for IMPROVE PM coarse, however. PM coarse is highest in spring for Rural sites, though by only a very slim margin compared to summer, while PM coarse is highest in fall at Urban sites.

Spring as the dominant dust season is even less discernible when considering the EPA data set. We computed seasonal averages for all combinations of location setting–land‐use category for each of PM10 and PM coarse; only for one combination of seven was spring the highest season for PM coarse (14%), and for two combinations of 11 for PM10 (18%). Summer was the dominant season for each: six of 11 (55%) for PM10, and four of seven (57%) for PM coarse. Second to summer is fall in terms of the number of combinations where concentrations are highest, both for PM10 and PM coarse, followed then by spring. Figure 1 also indicates the season with the second‐highest concentrations, and fall is more represented for this category than spring. For most rows in the Figure 1 tile plot, winter has the lowest concentrations, which is consistent with spring as the beginning of dust season, if not the dominant period of dust season.

The results in Figure 1 indicate two important things: First, some metrics (e.g., IMPROVE Fine Soil from rural sites) point to spring as the dominant dust season, but most do not. The EPA data set, using either PM coarse or PM10 as proxies for dust, highlight summer as the predominant dust season, followed next by fall, and then by spring. Secondly, Figure 1 shows how much higher dust proxy measurements are in location settings and land‐use categories indicative of higher population and/or other anthropogenic activity (e.g., compare values in “Annual Average” column between Urban and Rural for IMPROVE Fine Soil, or Rural‐Desert vs. Urban‐Industrial for EPA PM10). Despite IMPROVE Fine Soil indicating spring as the dominant dust season across each location setting, the absolute Fine Soil concentrations are significantly higher for Urban sites compared to Rural (2×), which is true for IMPROVE PM coarse as well (2.7×). Similarly, for EPA PM10, the Rural‐Desert category had the lowest concentration, while Rural‐Agricultural was highest, followed second by Urban‐Industrial.

For each measurement and location setting and/or land‐use category, we tested whether site‐year seasonal mean concentrations differed significantly between the two highest seasons using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Only for IMPROVE Fine Soil were the highest and second‐highest seasons statistically distinguishable (p<0.05), which was true within both Urban and Rural categories. In contrast, the two highest seasons were not significantly different for any categories of IMPROVE PM coarse, EPA PM coarse, or EPA PM10. These non‐significant differences suggest that seasonal maxima are broadly comparable across multiple seasons, which is consistent with the conclusion that spring is not the uniquely dominant dust season.

We also calculate the ratio of fall and spring concentrations of each component for each site and each year, and show the distribution of these values within their respective location setting or land‐use categories in Figure 2. Year 2020 Fall‐to‐Spring values are separated from non‐year 2020 values for each category, because the behavior is systematically anomalous in this year, where Fall‐to‐Spring is much higher compared to other years. For each IMPROVE variable, differences between each category in Figure 2 are significant (p<0.001), including non‐2020 Rural versus Urban, as well as the year 2020 compared to each non‐2020 category. EPA PM10 and PM coarse Fall‐to‐Spring values are centered around one for most land‐use categories with the exception of the Desert category, where values are less than one. Differences between year 2020 values and all non‐2020 categories are significant (p<0.001), as are differences between non‐2020 Desert and all other non‐2020 categories (p<0.001). Pairwise differences between non‐Desert, non‐2020 categories (e.g., Residential vs. Commercial, etc.) were not significant (p>0.05). Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1 contains an annual time series of Fall‐to‐Spring values for all monitoring sites.

Figures 1 and 2 indicate that spring is not the dominant dust season across all monitoring locations when using PM coarse or PM10 as proxies for dust. In fact spring really only dominates when looking at IMPROVE Fine Soil at Rural sites. Taken together, Figures 1 and 2 point to a very strong connection between dust concentrations and anthropogenic activity.

Diurnal Patterns of PM10 and PM Coarse

Diurnal patterns of dust proxy measurements strongly indicate an anthropogenic influence on hourly concentrations. Here we focus on PM10 due to the much wider coverage of sites in the network, but the patterns discussed below broadly hold for PM coarse. Figure 3 shows hourly mean PM10 concentrations for data grouped by location setting and land‐use category combinations. Across all categories, we observe a pronounced early morning (6–8 AM) peak, as well as an evening (7–9 PM) peak. The magnitudes of these peaks vary considerably across categories. Concentrations also reach a mid‐day minimum (12‐3 PM) across all categories. These early morning and late evening peaks are similar to the diurnal patterns of canonical tailpipe‐emitted primary species (e.g., carbon monoxide, black carbon, and hydrocarbon‐like organic aerosol; Baumgardner et al., 2002; Raman & Arellano, 2017; Zhang et al., 2005).

Diurnal plot of hourly‐mean PM10 mass concentrations (μg m−3) grouped by season, location setting, and land‐use category. Horizontal dashed lines are the seasonal average for each land‐use category‐location setting combination.

While there is broad consistency in diurnal patterns discussed above across all categories and seasons shown in Figure 3, there are also considerable differences in the magnitude of hourly concentrations between categories. For example, the Rural‐Agriculture category has the highest AM peak concentration across all seasons (e.g., 115 μg m−3 in summer) except winter (54 μg m−3 in winter). In winter, the Urban‐Industrial category has the highest AM peak (62 μg m−3, 7 AM). While Rural‐Agricultural has a peak hourly mean concentration of nearly 115 μg m−3 in summer (6 AM), Rural‐Desert has the lowest summer AM peak concentrations (33 μg m−3 in summer) of all categories. This highlights the significant variability of PM10 concentrations within rural areas, driven in significant part by the presence of agricultural and other anthropogenic activity.

Unlike most location setting‐land use category combinations, which have their highest AM peaks sometime between spring and fall, some urban land‐use categories have their highest AM peaks during winter. The Urban‐Commercial category has its highest AM peak in winter (55 μg m−3), and its lowest in summer (44 μg m−3). Urban‐Residential is also higher in winter compared to summer. These seasonal differences between location settings may reflect either differences in boundary heights (e.g., higher in summer than winter) and/or differences in emission source strength (e.g., higher in drier/hotter periods).

We also see interesting differences in the timing of peak concentrations between categories and seasons as well. For example, within the Urban location setting category, the time of the maximum AM peak is roughly 1 hour earlier for the Industrial land‐use category relative to the Commercial or Residential categories across all seasons. This likely reflects differences in the temporal patterns of emission strengths between these categories. On the other hand, for a given location setting‐land use category combination, the timing of the peak can vary by season, with wintertime peaks tending to be later in the morning (e.g., 8 AM) compared to summer (e.g., 6 AM). These within‐category seasonal differences in the timing of the morning PM10 peak could reflect either seasonal differences in daily emissions patterns or seasonal meteorological differences, such as the time of boundary layer breakup. Boundary layer breakup starts 8–9 AM in the winter compared to 6–7 AM in the summer (See Figure S5 in Supporting Information S1 for a comparison of the January vs. July diurnal patterns in planetary boundary layer height for Southern Arizona).

The diurnal patterns noted above are broadly similar for PM coarse (see Figure S6 in Supporting Information S1). While the interactions with meteorological conditions and any pollutant are critical for controlling atmospheric concentrations, that there is a sharp increase in PM10 (and PM coarse) means that emissions are increasing during this time. Given that this time is coincident with the start of early‐morning human activities (e.g., rush hour traffic, beginning of the construction and farm workdays, etc.), the data strongly suggests that hourly concentrations of dust are strongly influenced by anthropogenic emissions to varying degrees across all monitoring site categories in the region.

Day‐of‐Week Patterns for Fine Soil, PM Coarse, and PM10

Each of Fine Soil, PM coarse, and PM10 share a broadly similar day‐of‐week pattern, where concentrations tend to be higher during weekdays, peaking on Thursday‐Friday, compared to weekends. Figure 4 shows the distribution of normalized concentration values, represented with box and whisker plots, for each combination of dust proxy, land‐use category, and location setting across each data set for each day of the week. Across all land‐use categories and location settings, weekdays were 1.14×, 1.07×, and 1.13× higher than on weekends for each of EPA PM10, IMPROVE PM coarse, and IMPROVE Fine Soil, respectively. Weekday‐weekend differences were statistically significant for all groups shown in Figure 4 (p<0.001) except for PM10 in the Rural‐Desert group (p=0.056).

Boxplot of normalized concentration values for EPA PM10 (left panels) and IMPROVE Fine Soil (top‐right panel) and IMPROVE PM coarse (bottom‐right panel) across days of the week for each land‐use category and/or location setting.

There are notable differences between the sub‐categories shown in Figure 4 as well. For example, the weekday‐weekend difference for both IMPROVE Fine Soil and IMPROVE PM coarse are larger for Urban sites than Rural sites. For Fine Soil, weekday normalized concentration is 1.21× higher at Urban sites compared to 1.07× at Rural sites. For PM coarse, weekdays are 1.13× higher for Urban compared to 1.03× to Rural. If we interpret these weekday‐weekend differences as relative indicators of how much anthropogenic activities (e.g., vehicle traffic, construction, agricultural practice) influence ambient concentrations of dust proxies, both Fine Soil and PM coarse are influenced by anthropogenic activities, though Fine Soil appears to be more impacted than PM coarse. Weekday‐weekend differences are magnified at Urban sites compared to Rural sites.

In the EPA data set, we see interesting behavior when slicing by land‐use and location setting as well. While the weekday‐weekend difference between location settings is similar, when not considering land‐use differences (Urban, Suburban, and Rural are 1.14×, 1.14×, and 1.13×, respectively), the Industrial land‐use category has the highest difference of all other land‐use categories for each location setting. Of all location setting‐land use combinations, Rural‐Desert had the lowest weekday‐weekend difference (1.06×), while Urban‐Industrial had the highest (1.3×). This is consistent with the results above where Rural IMPROVE sites have a lower weekday‐weekend difference than Urban sites.

There are some interesting differences within day type categories (weekday vs. weekend) as well. For example, normalized concentration values tend to increase over the course of the week, peaking on Thursday or Friday, as others have also shown for crustal elements for this region (Murphy et al., 2008; Prabhakar et al., 2014). This is most pronounced when looking at Urban sites for either IMPROVE‐measured variable, though is evident within some of the EPA sub‐groups as well (e.g., Urban‐Residential, Urban‐Commercial, Suburban‐Industrial). We do not offer a complete explanation for why some data sets (e.g., IMPROVE Fine Soil) have highly‐variable non‐weekend concentrations peaking on Thursday‐Friday.

Days Violating PM10 NAAQS

The PM10 NAAQS is defined by the U.S. EPA as a 24‐hr (midnight to midnight) average concentration of 150 μg m−3, which must not be exceeded more than once per year on average over 3 years. We calculated a total of 150 violation days (avg. 3.3 violations per monitor per year) from the available monitoring data in our EPA data set.

Figure 5 shows the violation days by season for each land‐use category and location setting, both in absolute number and relative contribution. First, this figure shows that summer is the season with the most violation days by far; there were a total of 518 violations in summer, 1.64× more than the next‐highest season (fall, 315 violations). Spring has the third‐highest number of violation days with 291. Using PM10 NAAQS violations as a metric for assessing the impacts of dust does not support springtime as the dominant dust season.

Seasonality of days in violation of the 24‐hr PM10 NAAQS of 150 μg m−3 for all Arizona regulatory PM10 monitoring sites considered (data set spans years 2000–2023). Top panel shows the number of violating days by season, colored by land‐use category and shaded by location setting, and bottom panel shows the relative contribution of each land use category‐location setting combination.

By land‐use category, Residential had the largest number of violations (465, 36% of total), followed by Commercial (323, 25%), Agricultural (289, 22%), Industrial (219, 17%), and Desert (nine, 1%). Commercial was the only land‐use category where violations were not the highest in summer; both fall and winter violations were higher for Commercial. Indeed, the majority (51%) of all violations during winter were from Urban‐Commercial monitoring sites, which is the highest seasonal share of any location setting–land‐use category combination. Comparatively, there are very few violations from monitors in the Agricultural category (dominated by Rural‐Agricultural) during winter (7%), compared to 25% on average across all other seasons. During summer, Residential sites account for the most violations (46%) of all land‐use categories, which is relatively evenly spread across Rural, Suburban, and Urban location settings.

PM Coarse Spatial Variability in Tucson

Figure 6 shows the spatial variability of PM coarse mass concentrations in the Tucson, Arizona metropolitan area, as measured by MODULAIR sensors in the SUNSET network. The left panel of Figure 6 presents a map of PM coarse concentrations at 18 monitoring sites averaged across the full data set (see Table S4 in Supporting Information S1 for a lookup table matching full site names with the labels used in the map). There is a high degree of spatial variability of PM coarse across the SUNSET network, varying by as much as a factor of 2.2× (max: 26 μg m−3 at site “A,” min: 11.9 μg m−3 at site “R”). Interestingly, sites closest to the downtown core (“O,” “N,” “M”) are on the lower‐end (average 14 μg m−3) of the spectrum, while sites around the periphery (e.g., “A,” “C,” “K,” “L,” “Q,” “P”) are quite variable, representing nearly the full range of values. This pattern may reflect differences in local surface characteristics (e.g., predominantly paved roads in the urban core vs. more exposed and unpaved surfaces in suburban areas), although it may also indicate that transport and mixing influence PM coarse concentrations on urban scales.

PM data from the Tucson metropolitan area‐based SUNSET sensor network. Left panel: map of dataset‐average PM coarse (“PMc”) mass concentrations at each site. Letters on the map serve as short‐hand labels; Table S4 in Supporting Information S1 contains a lookup table for full site names that go along with the short‐hand label. Right panels: diurnal patterns of hourly‐average PM coarse (top‐right) and PM2.5 (bottom‐right) for all sites.

In addition to illustrating how PM coarse concentrations can vary widely over kilometer length scales, the map also shows concentrations do not seem to correlate with nearby land‐uses in a simple way; nearness to the downtown core or major roads do not qualitatively appear to be strongly‐correlated with average concentration. For example, compare sites “R” and “B,” which are roughly 5 km away from eachother. While each site is roughly 1 km or less west of Interstate‐10, they have the lowest and second‐highest average concentration in the network, respectively.

To further contextualize the spatial variability of PM coarse, the two panels on the right of Figure 6 present the mean hourly concentrations of both PM coarse and PM2.5 for each site. During the morning peak (6–9 AM), mean PM coarse concentrations vary by a factor of 2.83× and span a range of 15–42.4 μg m−3. For comparison, PM2.5 only varies by up to a factor of 1.97×, spanning a range of 3.7–7.3 μg m−3. While both exhibit the morning rush‐hour signature of a pollutant emitted by vehicular activities, the relative importance of that signature appears to be much higher for PM coarse than for PM2.5. Indeed, during this morning peak period the fraction of PM coarse to PM10 increases rather sharply, indicating that PM coarse emissions are relatively higher than PM2.5 emissions on a mass basis between 6 and 9 AM (see Figure S7 in Supporting Information S1).

The spatiotemporal variability in PM coarse observed by SUNSET in Tucson across times of day and monitoring sites is broadly similar to that in Phoenix, as assessed by EPA hourly data. Discussion

Of the data examined here, only IMPROVE Fine Soil supports the idea that spring is the dominant dust season in Arizona. Within IMPROVE monitoring sites, the dominance of spring is much more obvious for Rural sites compared to Urban sites for Fine Soil. Regulatory measurements of both PM10 and PM coarse, on the other hand, do not show a springtime maxima. Instead, summer emerges as the season with the highest dust concentrations and most frequent PM10 NAAQS exceedances, followed next by fall and then by spring, though seasonal patterns differ across location settings and land use categories. This analysis indicates that the canonical “spring dust season” may accurately describe conditions at remote, sparsely populated sites dominated by fine‐mode, synoptically‐mobilized dust, but not exposure‐relevant dust seasonality of Arizona's urban airsheds.

A major limitation of this study is the broad treatment of all coarse PM mass as “dust.” While this assumption is routinely made (see e.g., Aryal & Evans, 2022), it is an inference based on common understandings of how coarse‐mode aerosol particles are generated and emitted into the atmosphere in the U.S. Southwest. Composition measurements of coarse aerosol in the region are unfortunately rare, but do not always support the assumption that all coarse mass is dust (Clements et al., 2017). Those few speciated coarse PM measurements in the region do show that most coarse mass is related to dust, however (Clements et al., 2017; Malm et al., 2007; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2015). Additional composition measurements of coarse PM in Arizona with accompanying source apportionment would strengthen conclusions about dust behavior drawn from fine‐mode composition measurements and/or coarse PM mass.

A number of features of the behavior of dust proxy quantities observed across all of these data sets point toward a pervasive influence of anthropogenic activities on dust concentrations. Consistent morning and evening peaks in the diurnal behavior of PM coarse and PM10 concentrations correspond closely to traffic and workday activity cycles, while midday minima coincide with boundary‐layer growth and dilution. Weekday PM10 concentrations typically exceed weekend levels by roughly 5%–15%, with the strongest contrasts in urban and industrial location settings—patterns consistent with the predominance of road dust and mechanical resuspension sources (Adebiyi et al., 2025; Clements et al., 2017; Fitz & Bumiller, 2021; Murphy et al., 2008; Rienda & Alves, 2021). Similar weekday‐weekend contrasts exist even for Fine Soil at Rural IMPROVE sites, highlighting how pervasive anthropogenic influences appear to be for the regional dust aerosol burden. Additionally, PM coarse concentrations are highly variable across the Tucson metropolitan area, which indicates how important local activities are for determining airborne dust concentrations.

The divergence between data sets underscores how the apparent seasonality of dust depends strongly on both particle size and monitoring context. IMPROVE Fine Soil data primarily reflect smaller dust particles that are often associated with large‐scale synoptic storms and long‐range transport events that are climatologically favored in spring (Kavouras et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2004). By contrast, PM10 and PM coarse measurements include a larger contribution from locally generated, short‐lived emissions that are more responsive to surface conditions and human activity. Elevated concentrations in summer and fall likely reflect enhanced resuspension from hot, dry surfaces, coinciding with intensified vehicular traffic, construction, and agricultural operations (Clements et al., 2017; Fitz & Bumiller, 2021; Rienda & Alves, 2021). These findings reinforce recent observations that natural and anthropogenic dust emissions are intertwined, and that anthropogenic drivers substantially modulate coarse‐particle concentrations even in regions traditionally viewed as natural environments.

From an air‐quality management perspective, these results have important implications. The summer predominance of PM10 violations suggests that health‐relevant dust exposure and regulatory violations are more often linked to recurring, moderate‐intensity local events than to infrequent springtime synoptic storms. This distinction matters because exposure mitigation strategies targeting only exceptional dust storm events will overlook the chronic exposures that dominate annual dose and drive cumulative health impacts (Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023). Moreover, summer and fall conditions coincide with heightened vulnerability to Valley fever (Gorris et al., 2018; Head et al., 2022), whose causative fungal spores are mobilized by the same mechanical and surface‐disturbance processes responsible for anthropogenic dust (Adebiyi et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2017). The strong dependence of dust concentrations on land‐use category—particularly high values at Industrial, Commercial, and Agricultural sites—further underscores that mitigation policies addressing fugitive dust from vehicles, construction, mining, and soil management could yield tangible benefits even in regions with abundant natural dust reservoirs, like Arizona.

Together, these findings argue for a revised framework for understanding dust seasonality in Arizona. Rather than a single, climatological “spring dust season,” Arizona's dust dynamics are better described by two overlapping regimes: a springtime synoptic dust season, dominated by regional transport of fine‐mode mineral dust, and a summer–fall anthropogenic dust season, dominated both by local resuspension of coarse particles under persistently arid conditions and “haboob” dust events linked to monsoon activity (Eagar et al., 2017; Raman et al., 2014). Recognizing these distinctions can help reconcile conflicting observations across data sets and ensure that public‐health and regulatory responses target the periods and processes most relevant to human exposure.

Looking ahead, integrating high‐resolution meteorological modeling and remote sensing with continuous speciation of coarse PM will be crucial for disentangling natural and anthropogenic contributions across these regimes. Dense low‐cost networks such as SUNSET offer the opportunity to characterize spatial exposure gradients at neighborhood scales and to evaluate the effectiveness of dust‐control measures. Future work that combines coarse mode chemical composition with particle‐size‐resolved data could further illuminate the sources and transformation pathways that govern Arizona's evolving dust climatology. Ultimately, reframing “dust seasonality” around exposure rather than solely climatological timing will improve both scientific understanding and the design of policies aimed at protecting public health in the arid Southwest.
