Assessment of coastal bedrock groundwater quality in Jiaodong Peninsula via extension cloud model and resampling strategy
Authors: Wenzhou Dong, Beibei Zhang, Xin Hu, Yu Yang, Zehui Wang, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Groundwater safety in coastal tourism regions is increasingly compromised by the conflict between rigorous quality standards and diffuse agricultural pollution. Traditional linear assessment methods often mask specific high-risk indicators through mathematical averaging. To address this challenge, this study applies the Extension Cloud Model using data from 18 monitoring wells in the Jiaodong Peninsula. To robustly quantify uncertainty despite the limited sample size, the framework is integrated with the Multi-step Backward Cloud Transformation based on Sampling with Replacement algorithm. While hydrochemical analysis indicates that rock weathering controls the regional baseline, water quality is significantly degraded by where potassium shows a strong correlation coefficient of 0.90 with chemical oxygen demand. The assessment results indicate that 38.9% of the groundwater samples fall into Class IV or V which are unsuitable for drinking purposes. A comparative analysis confirms that the Extension Cloud Model successfully identified critical contamination risks masked by the eclipsing effect in the Entropy Weighted Water Quality Index. Furthermore, the resampling algorithm calculated a regional expectation value of 0.3609 quantifying that the overall water quality is in a critical transition state between Class III and IV. This framework offers a scientifically robust tool for diagnosing groundwater safety in data-scarce coastal zones.

Introduction

The sustainability of coastal aquifers in tourism-intensive regions is increasingly compromised by the structural conflict between the rigorous water quality standards required for tourism and the diffusive pollution inputs from agricultural hinterlands. Recent hydrogeological assessments indicate that these dual-pressure zones are exceptionally vulnerable to anthropogenic contamination1,2. This vulnerability is particularly acute in bedrock fissure aquifers, such as those in the Jiaodong Peninsula, where groundwater flow is governed by complex fracture networks rather than uniform porous media. In such highly heterogeneous hydrogeological settings, water quality exhibits significant non-linearity and spatial discrete characteristics, rendering accurate risk diagnosis a challenging task3,4. Failure to identify specific contamination sources extending from diffuse agricultural runoff to localized geogenic anomalies can lead to irreversible degradation of the aquifer systems that underpin regional economic stability.5.

To quantify these risks, scholars have developed various GIS-based statistical models and vulnerability indices. Notably, integrated statistical models6 and modified DRASTIC frameworks7 have been widely applied to map nitrate contamination risks in agricultural zones, while region-specific adaptations have proven necessary to address local hydrogeological variability8. However, establishing foundational baselines in diverse geological contexts reveals that these largely deterministic approaches often struggle to address the structural uncertainty inherent in fractured media9,10. Nevertheless, the application of these conventional deterministic protocols to fractured coastal aquifers presents a fundamental hydrogeological challenge. The complex anisotropy inherent in fissure networks, combined with the common constraint of sparse monitoring data, creates significant structural uncertainty that linear aggregation algorithms cannot accurately quantify. Standard indices, such as the Entropy-Weighted Water Quality Index, frequently suffer from the “eclipsing effect,”where the discrete and extreme pollution signals typical of heterogeneous aquifers are mathematically masked by the averaging of majority parameters11–13. This deficiency is further amplified when linear index values are subjected to spatial interpolation in data-scarce regions; conventional algorithms tend to smooth out extreme values to minimize estimation variance, thereby causing localized pollution hotspots to disappear in regional risk maps14–16. Consequently, a critical deficiency exists regarding probabilistic assessment frameworks capable of extracting robust regional characteristics from limited observations in such data-scarce environments.

Addressing the combined challenges of numerical eclipsing and spatial smoothing in bedrock aquifers characterized by data scarcity necessitates a shift from rigid deterministic models to nonlinear frameworks rooted in uncertainty theory. Recent methodological advances have demonstrated the efficacy of hybrid approaches, such as combining statistical methods with meta-heuristic optimization17 or utilizing adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems (ANFIS) coupled with multi-criteria decision-making18, to enhance assessment accuracy. The Extension Cloud Model, rooted in probability and fuzzy set theory, offers a robust solution by utilizing three numerical characteristics, namely Expectation, Entropy, and Hyper-entropy, to quantify the randomness and fuzziness inherent in hydrochemical data19,20. Unlike traditional statistical methods that require large datasets to satisfy probability distribution assumptions, the Cloud Model is particularly effective in handling data-scarce systems characterized by limited samples21,22. Furthermore, recent studies highlight the superiority of hyper-entropy enhanced models in quantifying uncertainty within multi-criteria evaluation systems23. To further overcome the statistical bias caused by the limited number of monitoring wells in this study, we integrated the Multiple Backward Cloud Transformation based on Sampling with Replacement algorithm. This resampling technique generates stable probability distributions from small datasets, allowing for the rigorous quantification of uncertainty even in sparse monitoring networks24–26.

This study integrates the Extension Cloud Model with hydrochemical analysis to diagnose groundwater safety in the Jiaodong Peninsula. Deviating from conventional linear assessments, this research aims to: (1) identify the dual-source pollution mechanisms driving water quality degradation by coupling hydrochemical indicators with the Extension Cloud Model; and (2) reveal the eclipsing phenomena through a spatial comparative analysis between the proposed model and the Entropy-Weighted Water Quality Index. By exposing the hidden risks masked by traditional averaging methods, this study provides a scientifically robust paradigm for groundwater management in complex, data-scarce coastal aquifers.

Study area

The study area is situated in the eastern coastal region of the Jiaodong Peninsula, China (Fig. 1). Geomorphologically, the region is classified as a coastal hilly-montane zone with an average elevation of 70–100 m. As a pivotal tourism hub in eastern China, the area hosts numerous natural landscapes and cultural heritage sites, leading to high population density and substantial seasonal water demand. The region experiences a warm temperate continental monsoon climate with distinct maritime influences. Meteorological records for 2021 indicate a mean annual temperature of 14 °C and precipitation of 916.2 mm, while long-term decadal averages show an annual precipitation of 739.3 mm and 2239 sunshine hours.Fig. 1Location of study area.

Location of study area.

The study area forms a marginal peninsular drainage system with no external water inflow. The surface hydrographic network is predominantly characterized by short, seasonal streams that flow directly into the sea. Due to the limited catchment area and rapid runoff response, most rivers experience flow cut-off during the winter and spring seasons. In recent decades, persistent rainfall shortages have exacerbated this situation, causing many surface watercourses to dry up year-round. Consequently, the urban and agricultural water supply has shifted almost entirely to groundwater extraction, making the protection of groundwater quality critical for regional sustainability.

The groundwater system in the study area is dominated by bedrock fissure water, which creates a highly heterogeneous hydrogeological environment. Based on lithology, the aquifers are categorized into three subtypes: (1) fissure water in metamorphic gneiss; (2) fracture network water in massive granite; and (3) pore-fissure water in volcanic andesite. Groundwater is primarily recharged by atmospheric precipitation. The discharge dynamics are controlled by intensive anthropogenic extraction and seasonal agricultural irrigation. This hydrogeological structure, governed by complex fracture networks rather than uniform porous media, creates significant spatial variability in water quality, necessitating robust risk assessment methods tailored to data-scarce bedrock aquifers.

Materials and methods

Groundwater sample collection and determination

During July 2022, this study leveraged the existing regional groundwater monitoring network to select 18 representative observation wells. These sites were strategically screened to capture hydrochemical gradients across diverse land-use types, specifically covering the spatial transition from the inland agricultural hinterland to the coastal residential zones. This spatial configuration ensures the simultaneous capture of distinct pollution signatures from both diffuse fertilizer leaching and point-source domestic discharges. The sampling depths ranged from 0.3 to 18 m to ensure hydraulic connectivity among sampling points. A bailer sampling device was employed for each collection. Subsequently, samples were immediately labeled and preserved in cryovials wrapped with anti-static foam packaging within insulated containers containing blue ice refrigerant. In situ measurements of temperature, pH, and electrical conductivity were conducted within 15 minutes post-collection using field-deployable instrumentation. All sampling protocols strictly adhered to the Technical Specifications for Environmental Monitoring of Groundwater, specifically standard HJ 164-2020 of China.

Laboratory analyses were performed by a facility holding China Metrology Accreditation certification. The procedures maintained full analytical compliance with the Chinese Groundwater Quality Standards GB/T 14848-2017 and the Sanitary Standards for Drinking Water GB 5749-2022. Quantitative determinations included pH via the potentiometric electrode method EPA 150.1, total hardness through the EDTA titrimetric method ISO 6059, and total dissolved solids using gravimetric analysis SM 2540 C. This technique systematically integrates both variability magnitude and inter-indicator correlations, leveraging inherent dataset properties for bias-free evaluation. Extension cloud model

The Cloud Model is a statistical method bridging probability and fuzzy set theory, designed to address the uncertainty inherent in transforming qualitative concepts into quantitative data. By integrating fuzziness and randomness, it quantifies qualitative concepts into stochastic distributions characterized by three numerical parameters: Expectation (Ex), Entropy (En), and Hyper-entropy (He). In the specific context of constructing standard clouds for the five water quality grades, this study advances beyond traditional semi-empirical estimation methods by deriving parameters directly from weighted standard limits. Explicitly, to address the dimensional heterogeneity of the evaluation criteria, the multidimensional national standard limits were transformed into dimensionless comprehensive score boundaries (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_k$$\end{document}). Regional characteristic extraction in MBCT-SR

In this study, the limited sample size poses a potential risk of estimation bias when using traditional statistical methods. To address this specific data constraint and robustly quantify the regional water quality characteristics, the Multi-step Backward Cloud Transformation based on Sampling with Replacement (MBCT-SR) was adopted. This algorithm integrates the randomness of the Cloud Model with the Bootstrap resampling technique, enabling the objective inference of population features from the finite observation dataset36,37.

The MBCT-SR method transcends discrete data points by simulating the population distribution, thereby extracting three numerical features that define the“Regional Cloud”: Expectation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ex_{r}$$\end{document}), Entropy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$En_{r}$$\end{document}), and Hyper-entropy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$He_{r}$$\end{document}). The algorithm operates on the set of comprehensive scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = \{S_1, S_2, \dots , S_{n}\}$$\end{document} derived from the fuzzy evaluation in Section “Sample-centered cloud generation and evaluation”. The specific implementation steps are as follows:

Step 1: Bootstrap Resampling Strategy

To mitigate the uncertainty associated with the small sample size, a bootstrap resampling technique was employed. From the original score set S, M independent bootstrap samples (set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M=1000$$\end{document} to ensure statistical convergence) were generated using sampling with replacement. Identification of pollution sources

Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 8) was employed to decouple geogenic baselines from anthropogenic superpositions.A coherent geogenic cluster is formed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{Ca}^{2+}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{Mg}^{2+}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\textrm{HCO}_{3}}^-}$$\end{document}, and TH, which exhibit strong positive inter-correlations with coefficients exceeding 0.77. Response of water quality to pollution sources

Methodological comparative analysis and global applicability

Comparative analysis of model performance

A comparative analysis with the traditional EWQI reveals a fundamental divergence in assessment outcomes, as detailed in Table 4. For the unpolluted reference Sample S02, both methods consistently assign Class I, verifying the baseline reliability of the Cloud Model in the absence of anomalies. However, a sharp contrast emerges in contaminated samples: while EWQI rigidifies to Class II for Samples S06, S10, S12, and S13, the Cloud Model identifies them as Class IV or V. Quantitatively, this leads to a discrepancy rate of 38.9% across the region. This discrepancy highlights the intrinsic flaw of linear weighted indices known as the eclipsing effect, where critical single-factor pollution is mathematically masked by the high quality of other parameters. More critically, regarding risk sensitivity, the EWQI exhibits a 100% false-negative rate for these limit-exceeding samples, whereas the Cloud Model achieves a 100% detection rate, transforming the false safety of linear averaging into accurate risk diagnosis. This limitation of EWQI becomes particularly dangerous when assessed against international health standards. For instance, samples in the northern and southwestern areas exhibit fluoride concentrations exceeding the World Health Organization (WHO) drinking water guideline (1.5 mg/L). Under the linear EWQI framework, the pristine background of major ions dilutes this toxic signal, resulting in a false negative error that ignores the potential risk of skeletal fluorosis; in contrast, the Cloud Model accurately identifies these samples as Class IV/V by prioritizing the limit-determining factor. Scientific value and global applicability

By overcoming the rigid threshold dependence of traditional methods, the Extension Cloud Model offers a robust tool for identifying specific health risks and early stage degradation trends. This methodological innovation transforms the regional assessment from a static, mathematically averaged false safety to a dynamic, risk-oriented diagnosis. Consequently, this capability renders the model theoretically more suitable than linear indices for managing intensive agricultural regions worldwide, where capturing the uncertainty of diffuse pollution driven by fertilizer overuse and preventing misjudgments of water safety is paramount for public health protection.

Limitations and future research

This study analyzed groundwater quality using data from 18 monitoring wells in a complex bedrock aquifer. While this dataset was sufficient to identify general water quality trends, the limited number of sampling points makes it difficult to map the precise shape of pollution zones in such uneven rock structures. To reduce the uncertainty typically caused by small datasets, this research applied the MBCT-SR algorithm. This method uses a resampling technique to generate stable data distributions, which ensures that the regional risk assessment remains reliable despite the small number of observations.

Regarding the identification of pollution sources, the current approach relies on statistical relationships to distinguish between agricultural inputs and domestic wastewater. Although the patterns of nitrate and salinity provide strong evidence for these sources, the assessment lacks direct verification from specific tracers such as nitrogen and oxygen isotopes. Therefore, distinguishing between mixed pollution sources in transition zones is currently based on estimation rather than precise calculation. Future research should use these chemical tracers to accurately measure the contribution of each pollution source.

Finally, this assessment is based on data collected at a single point in time. Since groundwater in fractured rock responds quickly to rainfall and pumping, relying on static data may miss seasonal changes in water quality. Future studies should aim to combine the Extension Cloud Model with physical computer models that simulate water flow. Integrating statistical analysis with physical simulations will help create an early warning system that can predict how pollutants move under different weather conditions.
