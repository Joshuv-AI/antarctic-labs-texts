The relationship between childhood trauma and psychological security: the mediating role of self-disclosure and perceived social support in Iranian medical students
Authors: Zahra Saedi, Elham Taheri, Elham Azmoude
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological security, defined as an individual’s sense of safety, stability, and freedom from threat, is a fundamental aspect of mental health, especially for medical students experiencing high academic stress. While childhood trauma is known to undermine psychological security, its mechanisms remain underexplored in non-Western contexts. This study tested whether self-disclosure and perceived social support mediate this relationship in Iranian medical students.MethodsThis cross-sectional correlational study (March 2023–January 2024) was conducted on 414 medical students (248 females, 166 males) recruited through stratified random sampling at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. The study population included undergraduate medical students (semesters 1–14). Validated Persian versions of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, the Psychological Security Questionnaire–Short Form, the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, and the Chelune Self-Disclosure Scale were administered in a 25-minute session. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26 and SmartPLS 3.3.9 (PLS-SEM) to test mediation effects, considering the non-normal distribution of variables.ResultsThere was a moderate negative correlation between childhood trauma and psychological security (β = − 0.368, 95% CI [–0.42, − 0.31], f² = 0.15, p < .001). Self-disclosure (β = − 0.234, CI [–0.29, − 0.18]) and perceived social support (β = 0.213, CI [0.16, 0.27]) mediated 27.7% and 24.4% of the total relationship between childhood trauma and psychological security, respectively. The model explained 48.1% of variance and demonstrated good fit (SRMR = 0.062).ConclusionChildhood trauma erodes psychological security in medical students through impaired self-disclosure and social support. Recommended interventions include trauma-informed faculty training, anonymous peer support platforms, and regular mental health screening.

Introduction

Medical students are often expected to perform in emotionally taxing environments while concealing unresolved psychological wounds from their past. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses show markedly elevated depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among medical students compared with peers, underscoring a substantial hidden psychological burden [1–3]. One such wound, childhood trauma, represents a critical yet often overlooked factor that may undermine students’ mental health, emotional regulation, and academic functioning in the highly stressful setting of medical education [4]. Psychological security, originally described by Maslow (1942) as a fundamental human need, has since been elaborated as an internal sense of emotional safety, stability, and freedom from threat in social and academic contexts. It encompasses confidence in one’s coping abilities to speak up, trust in others to respond supportively, and a stable sense of self-worth that is culturally constructed [5–7]. A growing body of research, including studies on populations under significant stress, emphasizes that psychological security functions as a foundational protective factor [8, 9]. It is recognized for promoting adaptive coping and resilience, enhancing academic satisfaction [10], and improving life satisfaction in vulnerable populations [11]. This sense of security is foundational for adaptive functioning and resilience, particularly in high-pressure environments such as medical school [12, 13]. Medical students experience unique stressors including exposure to human suffering, competitive academic demands, and a professional culture that may discourage emotional vulnerability [14, 15]. Under such pressures, individuals with prior emotional wounds, particularly those stemming from adverse childhood experiences, may find their psychological security significantly compromised [16].

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), including emotional, physical, or sexual abuse, neglect, and exposure to violence, are known to disrupt early attachment and undermine the development of emotional regulation and interpersonal trust [17–19]. According to attachment theory, early interactions with caregivers form “internal working models” that guide how individuals relate to others and perceive emotional safety in adulthood [20, 21]. When these early experiences are negative, they can calibrate the stress-response system toward hypervigilance, leading individuals to develop persistent feelings of insecurity and mistrust, a vulnerability that becomes particularly salient when re-exposed to stress, such as during medical training [22–24].

Despite the long-term impact of childhood trauma, psychological outcomes are not fixed. Protective factors, including perceived social support and self-disclosure, may buffer the negative effects of trauma and help restore psychological security. Social support refers to the perception of being cared for and understood by others, and is consistently linked with emotional well-being and resilience [25, 26]. Self-disclosure, defined as the voluntary expression of one’s inner experiences, promotes emotional relief and stronger social bonds [27–29]. These two factors are deeply interconnected, as those who feel supported are more likely to disclose emotional difficulties, and those who disclose often elicit greater support in return [30–32].

However, in emotionally restrained environments such as medical schools worldwide, and particularly in collectivist cultures like Iran, self-disclosure may be stigmatized or discouraged [33, 34]. Social norms that emphasize emotional control, reputation, and group harmony are recognized cross-culturally as drivers of stigma [35], which in turn may create barriers to seeking help or expressing vulnerability, especially among students with traumatic backgrounds [36, 37].

While there is a growing literature on childhood trauma and mental health, systematic reviews highlight that this evidence base remains largely focused on Western populations [38], and limited attention has been given to the mechanisms through which trauma affects psychological security, particularly in non-Western high-stress academic contexts [39, 40].

Most existing studies do not explore cultural dynamics that shape emotional expression and coping. Addressing this gap is critical, as unresolved trauma may increase students’ risk for depression, emotional exhaustion, and academic failure [41, 42].

The present study addresses this gap by examining how self-disclosure and perceived social support mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and psychological security among Iranian medical students. By situating this inquiry within a culturally sensitive framework and drawing upon established theoretical models (e.g., attachment theory, social support theory), the study aims to identify modifiable pathways that can inform future psychological interventions in academic settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional correlational study aimed to examine the relationships among childhood trauma, psychological security, self-disclosure, and perceived social support among Iranian medical students. Data were collected at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences during the 2023–2024 academic year.

The study population included all undergraduate students enrolled in the general medicine program (semesters 1–14). Stratified random sampling was employed using academic semester as the stratification variable. The number of participants selected from each semester was proportional to the number of enrolled students in that stratum. Within each stratum, participants were randomly selected using a computerized random number generator. This sampling strategy follows standard methodological guidelines for stratified designs in educational and psychological research [43].

The final sample consisted of 414 students (248 females and 166 males), which approximated the overall gender distribution of the medical faculty (60% female, 40% male). The mean age of participants was 22.4 years (SD = 2.1), with females having a mean age of 22.2 years (SD = 2.0) and males 22.5 years (SD = 2.3).

All questionnaires were administered in Persian, the participants’ native language. Completion time ranged from 20 to 25 min with no time constraint.

Sample size justification

A priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 for SEM with mediation (medium effect size f² = 0.15, α = 0.05, power = 0.95, four latent variables) indicated a minimum required sample size of 204. This approach is consistent with recommendations by Cohen (2013) and Kline (2023) for sample size estimation in SEM [44, 45]. To improve model stability, account for model complexity, and mitigate risks of Type I and Type II errors, we recruited 414 participants.

Participant eligibility

Participants were eligible if they were enrolled in the general medicine program, aged between 18 and 30 years, and willing to participate voluntarily. Students were excluded if they had a clinical diagnosis of major psychiatric disorders (e.g., bipolar disorder or schizophrenia) confirmed by a licensed psychiatrist, or if they had participated in similar psychological studies within the past 12 months.

Recruitment and ethical considerations

Participants were recruited through classroom visits, digital student platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram), and institutional announcements. Of 460 invited students, 414 completed the study (response rate: ~90%).

Questionnaires were administered both online (via Porsline.ir) and in paper format. Participation was voluntary; informed written consent was obtained. Participants could withdraw at any point without penalty.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1403.344) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki. An independent monitoring committee reviewed procedures biannually.

Data collection spanned from March 2023 to January 2024. Responses were anonymous and stored on password-protected servers. Data will be deleted after 10 years in accordance with institutional policy. Public access to raw data is restricted; however, anonymized datasets are available upon request. A summary of study results will be shared with participants via university channels.

Instruments

Participants completed four validated Persian instruments. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF) is a 28-item scale measuring five domains of childhood trauma (emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect) [46]. In this study, internal consistency was excellent (α = 0.89). The original development and validation were conducted by Bernstein et al. [46], and the Persian version has been previously applied and validated among Iranian populations [47].

The Psychological Security Questionnaire – Short Form assesses emotional safety and psychological stability through 18 items [48]. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.82. The scale was originally developed as part of organizational and psychological research [48, 49], and its Persian adaptation has been validated in Iranian samples [49].

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) measures perceived social support from family, friends, and significant others using 12 items [50]. Internal consistency in this study was high (α = 0.91). The original scale was developed by Zimet et al. [50] and has been adapted and validated in Iranian populations [51].

Finally, the Chelune Self-Disclosure Scale is a 6-item measure of openness in interpersonal communication [52]. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.79. The scale was originally developed by Waring and Chelune [52], and the Persian version has been validated among Iranian students [53].

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 2 presents the demographic information of the 414 participating medical students. The sample included 59.9% females and 40.1% males, with the vast majority (95.65%) being single. The average ages were 22.00 ± 2.00 for male students and 22.50 ± 2.30 for female students. Most students (52.1%) were in semesters 2 to 8, representing the basic sciences and early clinical training phases. Gender and academic semester were statistically controlled as covariates in the structural model.

Clinical note

The sample is predominantly composed of young, unmarried female students. This demographic imbalance may influence both the expression and perception of emotional needs. Prior studies suggest that unmarried women, particularly in collectivist societies, tend to report higher levels of emotional openness and seek more social support. Conversely, single men—especially in similar cultural contexts—often report lower emotional expressiveness, possibly due to gender-role expectations that discourage vulnerability or self-disclosure (e.g., Kennedy-Moore & Watson, 2001; Wong et al., 2017). Therefore, the overrepresentation of unmarried females and relative underreporting by males may contribute to gender-based differences in reported self-disclosure and perceived support, potentially influencing structural model outcomes.

Gender differences

Multi-group analysis (MGA) revealed significant gender differences in the mediating pathways. Although no mean-level difference was observed in psychological security itself, females reported significantly higher levels of self-disclosure (d = 0.32) and perceived social support (d = 0.28). Furthermore, the strength of the relationship between childhood trauma and both self-disclosure and social support was significantly stronger among male students, suggesting distinct gendered patterns in how trauma influences psychological outcomes.

Clinical implications

A one SD increase in trauma score (CTQ = 85) predicts a ~ 5-point reduction in psychological security, indicating a clinically meaningful decline. Students with high trauma and low support may exhibit deficits over 10 T-points, raising burnout risk substantially.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between childhood trauma and psychological security, focusing on the mediating roles of self-disclosure and perceived social support among Iranian medical students. Psychological security refers to an individual’s sense of emotional stability and confidence in navigating social and academic challenges without excessive fear or self-doubt [5, 6]. This concept, foundational to mental well-being, has been studied in various contexts, including times of widespread stress such as the COVID-19 pandemic [8] and in populations managing chronic illnesses [9]. Self-disclosure was defined as the willingness to share personal thoughts and emotions with others [27–30], and perceived social support reflected the belief that one has access to dependable help from peers, family, or institutions [25, 26, 31].

Findings supported the first hypothesis, indicating that childhood trauma was negatively associated with psychological security [16–19, 22–24]. This aligns with a growing body of empirical evidence suggesting that early adverse experiences undermine emotional safety, trust, and self-regulatory abilities [17–19], as predicted by attachment theory [20, 21]. According to attachment theory, unresolved childhood trauma may increase vulnerability to insecurity, particularly in stressful interpersonal or academic contexts [20–22].

The second and third hypotheses were also supported: childhood trauma was associated with lower levels of self-disclosure and perceived social support, which were, in turn, linked to reduced psychological security [27–32]. These findings are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the protective role of emotional expression and interpersonal connectedness in psychological resilience [25, 26, 30]. Importantly, these results expand the literature by providing evidence from a non-Western, culturally conservative society, where mental health stigma and emotional restraint may inhibit help-seeking behaviors [33–37].

Cultural context is critical for interpreting these patterns. In Iran’s collectivist society, values such as modesty, emotional restraint, and group harmony often discourage open emotional expression—particularly among women [33, 37]. Research consistently links child maltreatment to long-term psychological vulnerability, including an increased risk of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [16, 38]. Studies conducted in diverse cultural contexts, such as China [39], further underscore that adverse childhood experiences are a significant public health concern globally. In the Iranian context, this is compounded by barriers such as fear of social judgment, traditional gender norms, and mistrust in institutional support [34, 36]. For example, students may hesitate to discuss emotional difficulties with peers or mentors out of concern for appearing weak or disruptive—a phenomenon confirmed in qualitative research [34, 36]. These norms contrast with therapeutic practices in Western cultures, where emotional disclosure—such as expressive writing or verbal sharing—is encouraged for psychological healing and self-regulation [27–29].

The pattern of results aligns with Markus and Kitayama’s theory of interdependent self-construal, which suggests that individuals in collectivist cultures define themselves through relationships and strive to maintain group harmony—even at the cost of personal expression [7].

The gender distribution in the sample (59.9% female, 40.1% male) closely reflects the overall demographics of Iranian medical schools (approximately 60% female, 40% male), enhancing the representativeness of our findings. However, the absolute number of male participants (n = 166) may still limit the statistical power to detect subtle gender differences in trauma response, a well-documented phenomenon in medical student well-being research [1–3]. Prior research suggests that men and women differ in emotional processing—with men more likely to suppress emotions or adopt avoidant coping strategies. Future studies with larger overall samples are needed to ensure sufficient numbers of male participants for robust gender-based comparative analyses.

One unexpected result was the non-significant serial mediation effect. While both self-disclosure and perceived social support independently mediated the link between trauma and psychological security, their sequential pathway was not significant. This may reflect cultural dynamics common in collectivist societies, where emotional disclosure does not always elicit supportive responses [33, 37]. Similar patterns were observed among university students in conservative cultural contexts such as Pakistan, where shyness and perceived social support significantly influenced self-disclosure behaviors [33]. In Iranian academic settings, students may lack trust in institutional systems, such as university counseling centers, further weakening the disclosure-support link [34, 36]. These findings suggest a need for culturally adapted psychological models rather than direct application of Western frameworks [7, 33].

Several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation [1–3]. Longitudinal designs are required to assess how trauma affects psychological security over time. Second, self-reported data may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability, especially concerning sensitive issues such as childhood abuse [16–19]. Cultural stigma, particularly toward women reporting abuse, likely contributed to underreporting [33, 36]. Third, measurement relied on the Self-Disclosure Index and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support [25–29, 31]. Although these instruments demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, they may not have been fully validated in Persian-speaking populations, potentially affecting construct validity [25–29].

Additionally, data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic. These conditions may have influenced students’ willingness to disclose emotions or their perceptions of available support systems, as pandemic-related stress has been shown to exacerbate existing mental health challenges [8, 9]. Contextual factors such as current mental health status, family functioning, and socioeconomic background were also not included in the model. These variables could confound observed relationships—for example, students from well-functioning families may report high psychological security regardless of past trauma.

Despite these limitations, the study offers meaningful recommendations for medical education in culturally conservative environments. Implementing trauma-informed and culturally sensitive interventions could significantly enhance students’ emotional well-being [6, 7, 33]. Specific actions include: peer mentorship programs to foster safe and trusting emotional dialogue; communication skills workshops led by trained counselors to promote healthy self-expression; and psychoeducation campaigns to address stigma around emotional vulnerability and challenge restrictive gender roles [6, 7, 33].

Research on stigma in Iran suggests that interventions focused on peer training in basic psychological support could hold promise for reducing stigma and improving help-seeking behaviors [36]. Implementing a pilot program, such as a Mental Health First Aid initiative, could be a logical next step to evaluate this approach. Initial implementation could focus on one medical faculty, with evaluation after 6–12 months before wider rollout.

At the institutional level, universities should offer confidential counseling services accessible without bureaucratic hurdles, launch anti-stigma campaigns across campuses, and provide faculty training in trauma-informed education and mental health literacy. Support from national educational and health policymakers—through curriculum reforms, targeted funding, and policy frameworks for student well-being—can ensure sustainable impact [6, 7, 36].
