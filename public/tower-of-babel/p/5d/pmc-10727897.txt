Traumatic Neuromas of the Penis: A Case Report
Authors: Minah Cho, Yu Ri Woo, Sang Hyun Cho, Jeong Deuk Lee, Hei Sung Kim
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

Traumatic neuromas result from the exuberant regeneration of injured nerves. Although they can occur at any age or site, they are often reported in young people with severe accidental trauma or in older people following limb amputation1. They are rare in the genital area. Herein, we report a rare case of traumatic neuromas on the penis that resembles condyloma acuminata, showing skin-colored verrucous papules on the penis.

A 29-year-old man presented with asymptomatic, skin-colored papules on the penis. He noticed these papules one month ago. He had no genital trauma or surgical history except for circumcision more than 10 years ago. He had had sexual intercourse more than once a week recently. He was very concerned that the lesion might be a sexually transmitted disease. Physical examination revealed grouped 2- to 3-mm-sized skin-colored verrucous papules on the penile shaft (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests for syphilis and human immunodeficiency virus were negative.

Grouped skin-colored verrucous papules on the penile shaft. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

A biopsy was performed with a 3-mm punch under suspicion of condyloma acuminata. Histopathologic evaluation revealed an increased number of nerve bundles and multiple Meissner corpuscle-like structures in the papillary dermis without evident epidermal changes (Fig. 2). We also performed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect the presence of human papilloma virus (HPV). No HPV was detected in the biopsied tissue. From the findings, we made a diagnosis of traumatic neuromas. We recommended the patient to have surgical excision which he refused.

An increased number of nerve bundles and multiple Meissner corpuscle-like structures present in the papillary dermis (H&E, ×200).

Although traumatic neuromas of the penis are rare, there have been some reports in connection with trauma, circumcision, or genital surgery. Penile traumatic neuroma was first reported in 1990 by Montgomery et al.2. The authors described a solitary tender papule on the penile shaft of 58-year-old man who had a history of penile trauma that occurred while walking through a glass door. In one study analyzing 17 cases of penile traumatic neuromas, all patients were clinically diagnosed with condyloma acuminata. They also all had a trauma history such as previous penile biopsy or circumcision3. In addition, one case of penile traumatic neuroma was reported as a late complication of penile dorsal neurotomy to treat premature ejaculation4. However, there was also a report of penile traumatic neuroma occurring in sexually active young men without any history of penile trauma. The author suspected that it might be caused by minor trauma during sexual intercourse5.

In conclusion, genital traumatic neuromas can be easily misdiagnosed without biopsy. Differential clinical diagnoses of genital papules include sexually transmitted diseases (i.e., condyloma acuminata, condyloma lata, and bowenoid papulosis), molluscum contagiosum, pearly penile papules, Fordyce spots, and lichen nitidus. Thus, traumatic neuroma should be considered for penile papular lesions, especially in patients who have been circumcised or have had invasive procedures on the penis. Treatment of choice is surgical excision. Its prognosis is good with clinical follow ups.
