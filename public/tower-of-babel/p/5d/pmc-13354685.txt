Complementary Microscopic and Metabarcoding Studies Allow for a Better Understanding of the Symbiotic Microbiome of Leafhopper Species Iassus lanio (Hemiptera, Cicadellidae)
Authors: Michał Kobiałka, Dariusz Świerczewski, Marcin Walczak, Aleksandra Pisarek-Pacek, Rafał K Wóycicki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeafhoppers’ microbiome patterns were shaped by deep co-evolutionary adaptation driven by dietary specialization. Their microbiome is dominated by obligate symbionts that supplement their nutrient-poor phloem-sap diet, as well as facultative symbionts, including both bacterial and fungal microorganisms. In this study, NGS metabarcoding techniques were performed, supplemented by confocal and electron microscopy, to thoroughly investigate the symbiotic system of the Auchenorrhyncha species Iassus lanio, a representative of the poorly studied leafhopper subfamily Iassinae. The obtained results include descriptions of the composition, distribution, and ultrastructure of microorganisms, as well as the phylogeny of ancient symbionts. Two obligate symbionts were found: the ancient Auchenorrhyncha symbiont Karelsulcia bacterium and the yeast-like symbiont Ophiocordyceps. Karelsulcia bacteria occur exclusively in specialized organs called bacteriomes, while fungal microorganisms inhabit mycetocytes within the fat body. Both symbionts are transmitted transovarially from mother to offspring. The presence of Wolbachia, Sodalis and Cardinium was detected. Sodalis and Cardinium were observed in the fat body. The ultrastructure of Cardinium showed a characteristic microtubule crest inside. The obtained phylogeny of Karelsulcia bacteria indicates Iassinae affinity with the Coelidiinae and Deltocephalinae subfamily symbionts. Taxonomic profiling revealed that both sequencing methods detected the same range of bacterial taxa, while ONT exhibited improved resolution for dominant species. Differential abundance analysis emphasized platform-specific biases. These studies highlight the complementary roles of different microscopy and metabarcoding techniques, demonstrating the complexity of symbiotic systems in leafhoppers and thereby improving our understanding of the host-symbiont relationship and expanding our knowledge of the structure and localization of insect microorganisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02790-7.Keywords: leafhoppers, symbionts, microbiome, bacteria, fungi

Introduction

Many animals are host to symbiotic microbes, which play various roles with ecological and evolutionary consequences [1–3]. Insects, in particular plant-sucking ones of the order Hemiptera, live in obligate symbiotic associations with microorganisms and possess well-developed symbiotic systems in their specialized tissues, cells or gut regions [4–7]. Since plant sap usually lacks proteins, essential amino acids and vitamins, insects depend on symbiont-provided nutrients for their growth and survival [8–10].

Leafhoppers (Hemiptera, Cicadomorpha, Cicadellidae) are a highly diverse and abundant family of insect herbivores in the Hemiptera, comprising over 23,000 described species across 19 extant subfamilies [11, 12]. They are cosmopolitan and found in various ecosystems, including agricultural and horticultural ones, however, recently, as other insect groups have revealed alarming drops in biomass and abundance, driven mainly by human-origin environmental changes, e.g., habitat destruction, climate change, introduced species, and pollution [13, 14]. Morphological identification is challenging, especially for nymphs and female adults. Molecular tools, such as mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase I (mtCOI) gene markers or whole mitochondrial genome (mitogenomes), are increasingly used for accurate species identification [15, 16].

Leafhoppers’ symbionts are broadly categorized into bacterial and fungal symbionts, each contributing differently to insect physiology. Candidatus Karelsulcia muelleri, Bacteroidota (formerly Candidatus Sulcia muelleri), hereafter Karelsulcia, and Candidatus Nasuia deltocephalinicola, Betaproteobacteria (species name proposed by Noda and co-workers for a Deltocephalinae leafhoppers [17]), hereafter Nasuia, are the primary bacterial symbionts housed in specialized cells called bacteriocytes, which form large organs known as bacteriomes [18–20]. Bacteriomes are organs of mesodermal origin found in many representatives of the Auchenorrhyncha [21–23]. Genomic analysis revealed that these symbionts have the smallest known genomes (112 kb), next to those of Candidatus Vidania, a betaproteobacterial symbiont of planthoppers (50-52 kb) [24–27]. Both originated with their hosts over 260 million years ago [17, 28]. They are transovarially transmitted, meaning they are passed from mother to offspring through the ovaries, ensuring their persistence across generations [23, 29, 30].

In some leafhoppers, such as Macrosteles laevis (Deltocephalinae), the bacterium Arsenophonus is found within the bacteriocytes containing Karelsulcia bacteria and, interestingly, within the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia, indicating a complex symbiotic relationship [31]. Other types of Gammaproteobacteria, such as Sodalis, may also be present in the body of these insects, in bacteriomes or fat body [32–34]. Leafhoppers also harbor other bacterial symbionts, such as Asaia (Alphaproteobacteria), which has been studied for its potential use in symbiotic control strategies [35]. Alphaproteobacteria Wolbachia and Flavobacteriia Cardinium (Bacteroidota) have been identified in some leafhoppers (Deltocephalinae, Eurymelinae, Typhlocybinae) and planthoppers (Delphacidae), influencing host characteristics and potentially playing a role in the reproduction manipulation [20, 36–39]. Some leafhoppers, particularly those in the subfamily Ledrinae and some from Deltocephalinae and Eurymelinae, have replaced their bacterial symbionts with yeast-like fungal symbionts. These fungi are related to the entomoparasitic genus Ophiocordyceps and are found within specialized cells of the leafhoppers’ fat body [20, 40, 41]. Understanding the complex interactions between insects and their symbionts can provide insights into pest control strategies and the management of plant diseases [42–44]. The presence of horizontally transferred genes in leafhoppers supports the symbionts’ roles in enhancing the host’s ability to detoxify plant chemicals and resist environmental stress. The adaptation of leafhoppers to their symbionts is evident in their genome, which shows enrichment for genes involved in microbial population regulation and immune responses [45, 46]. In conclusion, leafhoppers maintain intricate relationships with both bacterial and fungal symbionts, which are essential for their survival and ecological success.

The worldwide distributed Iassinae is one of the subfamilies of Cicadellidae with 12 tribes, over 160 genera, and more than 2,200 species known to date [47]. Despite the fact that most species in this subfamily are arboreal, the host relationships of most known taxa are poorly studied [48–51]. With piercing-sucking mouthparts, Iassinae insects can directly cause injury to plants, but they can also transmit viruses, resulting in slow growth or even the total destruction of the plant [50]. The endosymbionts of Iassinae leafhoppers are practically unknown. So far, Karelsulcia symbiont genes have been retrieved from the transcriptomes of two species: Penestragania robusta and Ponana quadralaba to elucidate its position relative to other members of the phylum Bacteroidetes and the degree of congruence with the phylogeny of its Auchenorrhyncha hosts [52]. Taking into account this critical research gap, an attempt was made to determine the composition, distribution, morphology, and ultrastructure of the microbiome of another species of Iassinae leafhopper – Iassus lanio (Linnaeus, 1761). Moreover, due to the increasing interest in the phylogenetic affiliation and internal classification of this group [49, 53] the relationship of the Iassinae symbiont Karelsulcia to the symbionts of other Cicadellidae subfamilies was uncovered.

Material and Methods

Insect Individuals

Iassus lanio is the species of the genus Iassus Fabricius, 1803, belonging to the tribe Iassini. It is a monophagous leafhopper species with a European distribution, however, it has been recorded locally and usually in a low number of specimens. The species feeds on oaks (Quercus robur and Quercus petraea, family Fagaceae) in the interior of forests as well as along their margins [54]. The individuals collected from Quercus trees in southern Poland and the Czech Republic were studied. The species prefers to reside high in the tree canopy, so only a few specimens were collected using a standard sweep-net and an insect aspirator. Details on specimens are in the appendix (Table S1).

Molecular Identification

For molecular analysis, 5 adult female specimens were fixed in ethanol, and DNA was extracted from whole individual insects with EURx GeneMATRIX Bio-Trace kits, then stored at 4 °C. The same specimens were sequenced for both bacterial and fungal communities. For insect identification, the mtCOI gene marker was used. COI gene sequences of the host were amplified, confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis, sequenced via the Sanger method and confirmed using BLAST [55] and the NCBI GenBank database [56]. Symbiotic microorganisms were identified using their 16S rDNA (bacteria) and ITS1, ITS2 (fungi) sequences. DNA quantity was measured with a Qubit and isolation was confirmed by amplifying 16S rDNA and 18S rDNA with primers V3-V4 (F357, R805) and NS1, FS2 (Table S2), visualized via agarose gel electrophoresis. Two methods for bacteria metabarcoding were used: Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT, https://nanoporetech.com). Illumina sequencing was used to identify fungi. SEQme s.r.o. sequenced samples using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 SF Flow Cell, prepared with Nextera Technology kit primers (Table S2). Reads were verified with FastQC and MultiQC, then joined using Fastq_join, trimmed, filtered and classified into Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) using DADA2 1.38 (https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/tutorial.html) in RStudio (https://cran.rstudio.com), assigned to genus level using Silva 138.2 [57] and Unite 10 [58] databases. ONT sequencing was performed in the Laboratory of Genomics at the Institute of Zoology and Biomedical Research, Jagiellonian University. The ONT libraries were prepared using 27 F-1492R ONT 16S Barcoding Kit 1–24: (1) SQK-16S024 for Flow Cell FLO-MIN106DNA with 9.4.1 chemistry and (2) SQK-16S114-24 for Flow Cell FLO-MIN114DNA with 10.4.1 and sequenced with a MinION Mk1B sequencer. Basecalling of 9.4.1 chemistry data was processed using Dorado Software v.0.9.6 basecaller command and dna_r9.4.1_e8_sup@v3.6 model. Basecalling of 10.4.1 chemistry data was performed in a duplex-aware manner (allowing better read quality) using Dorado software v.1.1.0 duplex command and dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.2.0 model. Demultiplexing and trimming of both chemistry ONT data were done using Dorado “demux” and “trim” commands, respectively. Simplex reads from 9.4.1 chemistry data and simplex/duplex reads from 10.4.1 chemistry data were joined together, quality checked using NanoPloy software v. 1.44.1 and trimmed to include only reads with Phred quality score over 10 and of length between 1300 and 1800 bp. Such prepared reads were submitted for taxonomic classification, which was performed using the EMU package [59] and the Silva 138.2 database. Statistical tests for comparing bacterial abundance were performed using RStudio (Shapiro-Wilk, Levene, Anova). Sequences were deposited in the Figshare database under the project “Iassus lanio microbiome”.

Symbionts Phylogenetic Analyses

Reference sequences were downloaded from NCBI GenBank (numbers in Fig. 4), and for Eurymelinae subfamily sequences were obtained from Figshare databases (link in Fig. 4 description). Short sequences from the V3-V4 region of 16S rRNA were gathered due to the limited availability of sequences from different representatives of Cicadomorpha. Nucleotide sequences were aligned using ClustalW [60]. Phylogenetic analyses of the 16S rRNA genes of Karelsulcia symbionts were performed using MrBayes [61]. For Bayesian inference, four MCMC chains (3 heated, 1 cold) ran for five million generations, sampling trees every 1000 generations. The results were visualized using iTOL software [62].

Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH)

Adult females, preserved in ethanol, were rehydrated, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and dehydrated with ethanol and acetone. Samples were embedded in Technovit 8100 resin and cut into 1 μm cross sections at the height of the first and second abdominal segments. Hybridization buffer (based on [63]) was composed of Tris-HCl, NaCl, SDS, formamide, and distilled water. Samples were incubated overnight with hybridization solution, washed in PBS, dried, and mounted with DAPI fluorochrome (ProLong Gold). Images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Observer LSM 710 confocal microscope. FISH experiments were repeated on two adult female individuals from Poland (for details, see Table S1). As a negative control, insects of the species Kybos virgator (subfamily Typhlocybinae) were used, devoid of symbionts, possessing only Wolbachia and Rickettsia bacteria (verified by sequencing [20]). Probes and fluorochromes are listed in Table S2.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Specimens were dissected and preserved in 2.5% glutaraldehyde with 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Fixed material was stored at 4 °C for three months, rinsed in the same buffer with added sucrose, and postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1.5 h. After dehydration in graded ethanol and acetone, specimens were embedded in epoxy resin (Epoxy Embedding Medium kit, Merck Sigma-Aldrich). Sections from epoxy blocks were cut using an ultramicrotome. For histology, semithin sections (1 μm) stained with 1% methylene blue in 1% borax were examined under a Nikon Eclipse E200 light microscope. Ultrathin sections (70 nm), contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, were studied under an electron transmission microscope Jeol JEM 2100 (80 kV). Experiments were repeated on three adult female individuals from Poland (for details, see Table S1).

Results

Bacterial Symbionts Composition

The symbiotic system in all studied samples consists of both bacterial and fungal microorganisms (Fig. 1A-C). Using Illumina sequencing, about 160k bacterial reads were obtained for all samples (25-37k per sample), about 74k fungal reads (10-15k per sample), and about 340k using ONT (50-80k per sample).

The percentage of relative abundance of bacteria (class and genus) for each sample based on the number of sequences reads, 16S rRNA metabarcoding using Illumina (A) and Oxford Nanopore Technology (B). The Karelsulcia bacterium was present in all samples, although a smaller number of reads were obtained from Illumina sequencing (<1%, therefore abundances were not shown in Fig. 1A as a separate group but were included in "Other"). Using BLAST and the Silva database, the obtained sequences showed the greatest similarity (97%) and coverage (100%) to the Karelsulcia sequence from the Iassinae species Hamana dictatorial [DQ066638.1] [28]. A significantly higher number of reads (2-32%) for Karelsulcia bacteria was obtained using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) (Fig. 1B) (Anova test with White adjust: p<0,05).

The most abundant bacteria were the Gammaproteobacteria Sodalis (Illumina: 75-99%; ONT: 86-98%) and Alphaproteobacteria Wolbachia (Illumina: 72-74%; ONT: 66-68%), though neither was present in all samples, also the presence of both bacteria in one sample was not observed. The Cardinium bacterium was present in all samples from Poland (samples Ias_lan_1-4), but not from the Czech Republic (sample Ias_lan_5, Fig. 1A and B). The Cardinium sequence showed the greatest similarity to the Cardinium bacterium found in the mite Tyrophagus putrescentiae [64]. In contrast to Karelsulcia bacteria, a higher number of Cardinium bacteria reads was observed using Illumina sequencing technology (Illumina: 0-25%; ONT: 0-1%) (Fig. 1A and B). The remaining bacteria, mainly Sphingomonas (entered as "Other" in Fig.1A and B) were in tiny quantities and might have come from the insect's gut [65, 66]. Their highest abundance was observed in the Ias_lani_3 sample, though this was mainly observed in Illumina results, than ONT.

Fungal Symbionts Composition

Among fungal microorganisms, the most abundant were representatives of Hypocreales – the genus Ophiocordyceps (31–97%) (Fig. 1C). The obtained reads were most similar to the genus Ophiocordyceps sp. [67], then to Hirsutella subulate [68]. A high number of sequence reads belonging to the Cladosporium genus were also observed, especially in Ias_lan_2 (50%). Other fungi, including the genera: Alternaria, Microcyclospora, Bacidina, Pseudoveronaea and others, constituted a minority of the reads.

Symbionts Localization

Using the FISH technique and confocal microscope (Fig. 2), it was discovered that Karelsulcia bacteria are the only ones that inhabit specialized organs called bacteriomes. The bacteriomes occur in pairs, on both sides, close to the muscles and cuticle, in the first and second abdominal segments (Fig. 2A). Confocal microscope images (Fig. 2A-C) show the bacteriome structures. These are relatively large organs, surrounded by a single layer of epithelium called the bacteriome sheath, composed of bacteriocytes filled with Karelsulcia bacteria (green).

Bacteria of the Sodalis and Cardinium genera were observed in some places within the insect's fat body (Fig. 2D and E). Sodalis microorganisms (Fig. 2D, yellow) are rod-shaped bacteria, more elongated than Cardinium residents (Fig. 2E, red). Wolbachia bacteria were not observed on the tested insect sections.

Using a specific probe, we noted that fungal microorganisms belonging to the genus Ophiocordyceps, called yeast-like symbionts (YLS) due to their shape, settle the fat body of the studied insects (Fig. 2A, C, F). Within the fat body, yeast-like fungi sometimes occupy isolated cells; these cells are sometimes called mycetocytes, and when they form organs, they are called mycetomes (Fig. 2A, C, F).

The presence of Karelsulcia bacteria and Ophiocordyceps yeast-like symbiont was observed in ovaries: inside the follicular cell surrounding the posterior pole of the terminal oocyte (Fig. 2G) and in the perivitelline space, separating the epithelium from the oolemma (Fig. 2H). In the oolemma invagination, microorganisms form a specific structure called a symbiont ball, although in this case it has a flattened, cap-like shape.

Symbionts Ultrastructure

The use of electron microscopy (Fig. 3) enabled visualization of the morphology and ultrastructure of the microorganisms inhabiting the bodies of the studied insects. Bacteriome resident, Karelsulcia symbiont is a pleomorphic, amorphous, slightly elongated bacterium, electron dense, therefore dark on electron microscope images, quite large, measuring from 6 to even 12 micrometers in length (Fig. 3A and B). These associates almost completely fill the cytoplasm interior of the bacteriocytes, specialized cells that constitute the symbiont's living environment. There are also clearly visible polyploid nuclei and cellular organelles such as mitochondria (Fig. 3A and B).

Ophiocordyceps yeast-like symbionts settle the insect fat body (Fig. 3C-E). Fungal microorganisms possess a typical elongated yeast-like shape, a thick cell wall, and relatively large vacuoles inside. Next to them, in the fat body space, there are small, rod-shaped, more or less elongated bacteria (Fig. 3D-H). The elongated ones represent the genus Sodalis (Fig. 3D, E, H), as reported in previous ultrastructural studies [32, 69]. The Cardinium bacteria are slightly smaller, more rounded, and have a characteristic comb-like structure composed of microtubule-like elements (Fig. 3D and F, high magnification: F and G) [39, 70].

Consistent with our confocal microscopy observations, we also detected, in TEM-examined I. lanio individuals, the presence of Karelsulcia bacteria and Ophiocordyceps fungi in the cytoplasm of follicular cells. (Fig. 3I). The presence of Wolbachia, Sodalis and Cardinium bacteria was not observed in the gonads of the tested insects.

Symbionts Phylogeny

The cladogram shows a Bayesian analysis of the most abundant 16S rDNA sequence of the Karelsulcia obligate symbiont of the I. lanio and other Auchenorrhynchans (Fig. 4). The outgroup consists of planthoppers (Fulgoromorpha) symbionts; the ingroup includes the symbionts of many insects from Cicadomorpha: superfamily Cicadoidea (cicadas), Cercopoidea (spittlebugs) and Membracoidea: family Membracidae (treehoppers) and Cicadellidae (leafhoppers): including subfamily: Deltocephalinae, Iassinae, Cicadellinae, Eurymelinae, Ledrinae and others. For the subfamilies, the symbionts form compact clades. The Karelsulcia sequences from I. lanio constitute a separate sister clade to the Iassinae species symbionts of Penestragania robusta (tribe Hyalojassini) and then symbionts of Gyponini representatives: Ponana quadralaba and Hamana dictitoria. The symbionts of the Iassine subfamily show the closest relationship to the symbionts of the subfamilies Coelidiinae and Deltocephalinae (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Obligate Symbionts

Bacteria of the genus Karelsulcia were the only obligate bacterial symbionts of the species I. lanio. They occurred in all studied specimens, inhabited bacteriomes, and were transmitted transovarially from generation to generation. The abdominal localization of bacteriomes in the first and second segments is typical for leafhoppers, described in Paramacrosteles nigromaculatus, Deltocephalus brunnescens, Balclutha sp. [23], Yamatotettix flavovittatus [71], Macrosteles striifrons and Macrosteles sexnotatus (Deltocephalinae) [46]. Karelsulcia bacterium is not the only obligatory symbiotic microorganism, as in the case of the leafhopper Dalbulus maidis (Deltocephalinae) [72], but is supported by the yeast-like fungus Ophiocordyceps, both of which are involved in amino acid synthesis. Previous studies on Auchenorrhyncha insects have indicated that in some lineages, the second bacterial symbiont was replaced by a fungal microorganism, which, together with the primary Karelsulcia symbiont, is responsible for supplementing the insect’s diet [7]. Phenomenon was particularly observed in planthoppers and some leafhoppers [19, 20, 33, 39–41, 72, 73]. Furthermore, the long evolution of symbiotic fungi with insect hosts is confirmed by the formation of specialized fat-body cells called mycetocytes. Yeast-like symbionts, along with Karelsulcia bacteria, are present in all individuals of the species and are also passed via the ovaries to the offspring. This transfer occurs in a manner similar to that reported in other leafhoppers and planthoppers [20, 33, 34, 73, 74]. The microorganisms leave the bacteriocytes and mycetocytes, then pass through the cytoplasm of the follicular cells in the region of the posterior pole of the terminal oocyte, enter the perivitelline space, and finally form a symbiont ball within an invagination of the oolemma. In planthoppers and leafhoppers, during embryonic development, these symbionts migrate to the first and second abdominal segments, Karelsulcia inhabits the bacterium, and fungal symbionts inhabit the fat body [23].

Facultative Symbionts

The Sodalis and Wolbachia bacteria are facultative symbionts, transferred between populations, since they are not present in all individuals of the species. They could be intracellular commensals or pathogens. Sodalis is a common insect microorganism with a broad spectrum of effects on its host, first identified in tsetse flies [75, 76], reviewed by Renoz et al. [77]. The ONT results indicate that the detected bacteria are mainly related to Sodalis praecaptivus (99.5% sequence similarity). S. praecaptivus is a free-living precursor of insect symbionts [75, 76]. Sodalis bacteria have been described in some insects’ as an obligate symbionts with reduced genomes [78, 79]. It is possible that the bacteria present in I. lanio are in a temporary stage between facultative and obligate symbionts. Bacteria of the genus Sodalis were discovered in the bodies of representatives of Auchenorrhyncha: planthoppers [72, 73, 80], leafhoppers [20, 32–34] and spittlebugs [78]. Co-infection of Sodalis and Wolbachia bacteria in the insect population was observed in tsetse flies, with no or low participation of them both [81, 82].

The Wolbachia bacterium is a ubiquitous microorganism in insects and other arthropods [37, 83–85]. The exact location of Wolbachia in the FISH-studied individuals was not observed, it can be found in many different tissues. The bacterium is known to be associated with the influence on sex determination, causing the cytoplasmic incompatibility of reproductive cells, feminization and even death of male embryos [37, 83, 86], also applies to the leafhoppers (Typhlocybinae) [38].

Cardinium bacterium, sometimes described as symbiotic, similarly to Wolbachia, may interfere with the host’s sex determination [36, 87–89], reviewed by Mathieson et al. [89]. The ultrastructure of these bacteria has been well described in the Scaphoideus titatus (Deltocephalinae) [39]. The microtubule crest is performing cytoskeletal and transport functions. Its transovarial transmission was not observed, indicating that, like the other bacteria, it is not a mutual symbiont. To understand the exact function of bacteria, it would be necessary to sequence a larger part of the bacterial genomes and perform genomic analyses. It is possible that the studied bacteria also occur in other, unexamined tissues of the insect, i.e., the remaining segments of the abdomen or tissues outside the abdomen. The DNA used for metabarcoding was isolated from whole individuals.

Metabarcoding Methods

In this study, two types of modern NGS metabarcoding methods for bacterial identification were performed. The results of both methods revealed the presence of the same bacterial taxa in the samples. The difference in abundance is most evident for the Karelsulcia and Cardinium bacteria. The Illumina sequencing results show a low abundance of Karelsulcia bacteria and a higher abundance of Cardinium bacteria, whereas the ONT sequencing results show the opposite. Bacterial abundance results largely depend on methodological aspects, including first of all the differences in the length of sequenced fragments (Illumina: V3-V4 region vs. ONT: all 16S rDNA), but also conditions of carried out reactions, e.g., the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), based on which amplicon sequencing was performed. Ultimately, it is difficult to compare the actual amount of bacteria in the sample. It seems that Karelsulcia bacteria should have a higher content since they occupy relatively large bacteriomes, one of the largest organs in the insect’s body. The Cardinium bacteria are much smaller and have a lower volume than bacteria (Fig. 3A, B, F, G). However, the number of Cardinium bacteria in the fat body can also be high.

Phylogenetic Remarks

Due to the long co-evolution of insects and their microorganisms, the relationship of symbionts may be useful in assessing the phylogeny of leafhoppers. Previously constructed cladograms of Auchenorrhyncha symbionts appear to be valuable for inferring the phylogeny of host insects [17, 20, 52, 72, 80, 90]. In this work, symbionts of the genus Karelsulcia were used to construct the cladogram (Fig. 4). These bacteria are obligate ancient symbionts of Auchenorrhyncha [17, 24, 28, 72]. The 16S rDNA sequences come from many members of the Cicadomorpha infraorder. The sequences obtained from our samples indicate that the symbionts of I. lanio are most closely related to symbionts associated with other Iassinae (Hyalojassini and Gyponini tribe), then to Coelidiinae and Deltocephalinae Karelsulcia bacteria. This result is generally consistent with the phylogeny presented in recent works [12, 52], showing that the subfamilies Coelidiinae, Neocoelidiinae, Neoballinae, Deltocephalinae, and Ledrinae (but only two species) are the most closely related groups to Iassinae. Unfortunately, there is a lack of data on the symbionts of the subfamilies Neocoelidiinae (only one species, Neocoelidia tumidifrons) and Neoballinae (no data) to determine their symbiont phylogeny. The above conclusions rely solely on the cladogram generated from short sequences of a single symbiont. To perform a more accurate phylogenetic analysis of the species, it is necessary to perform high-throughput long-read sequencing to obtain symbiont genomes and host mitogenomes from many individuals. However, acquiring many individuals in times of climate change and declining numbers may prove to be a major limitation.

Conclusions

The microbial system of I. lanio consists mainly of bacteria: Karelsulcia, Sodalis, Wolbachia and Cardinium. The Karelsulcia bacteria reside in separate bacteriomes, whereas the Sodalis and Cardinium settle in the insect fat body. Illumina and ONT sequencing provided similar results, with some differences in bacterial abundance. In addition to bacteria, yeast-like symbiotic fungi of the genus Ophiocordyceps occur in the fat body. Both the Karelsulcia bacteria and Ophiocordyceps fungi are transovarially transmitted to the offspring. The similarities of the 16S rRNA gene sequence of the Karelsulcia bacteria indicate a close relationship with the Karelsulcia symbionts of Iassinae, Coelidiinae and Deltocephalinae leafhoppers.
