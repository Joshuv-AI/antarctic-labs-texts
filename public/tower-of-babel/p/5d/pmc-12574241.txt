Pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward the management of couple infertility
Authors: Simon Azonbakin, Yossounon Chabi, Etienne Goulai BI, Judith Tokpo, Alice Tuo-Iwaka, Godwin Soglo, Habib Ganfon, Anatole Laleye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCouple infertility affects approximately 15% of the population in sub-Saharan Africa. This is a condition with important sociocultural implications since, in Benin, one of the objectives of marriage is procreation. The purpose of this work was to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and practices of community pharmacists in the management of infertility in Benin.Framework and methodThis is a cross-sectional study with descriptive and analytical aims that took place from July 4 to September 30, 2024; the study involved exhaustive sampling and covered community pharmacists working in pharmacies in 4 departments located in southern Benin involved in the dispensing of medicines. The data were collected via a questionnaire established on a survey form and analyzed via EPIINFO software version 7.2.1.0 and SPSS version 25. Associated factors were sought via logistic regression.ResultsIn total, 142 pharmacies were included in the study, and 167 pharmacists responded to the survey questionnaire. The average age was 34.41 years, with a standard deviation of 1.74. Males were the most represented, with a sex ratio of 1.06. Pharmacists with less than 10 years of experience were the most represented (69.46%). The good knowledge score was 79.64%, the satisfactory attitude score was 68.86%, and the satisfactory practice score was 97%. Low KAP was significantly associated with age, professional qualifications, internships or training on infertility and having received cases of infertility from the couple at the counter.ConclusionThe overall KAP level of the pharmacists was satisfactory. This level is influenced by certain characteristics that must be taken into account in decisions that can improve the quality of pharmaceutical services in this area in Benin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08058-x.Keywords: Couple infertility, Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices, Pharmacies

Introduction

Having children is a deeply held desire for many couples. However, fulfilling this desire is not always easy. Infertility is defined as the inability to achieve pregnancy after at least 12 months of regular, frequent, and unprotected sexual intercourse [1]. It is a complex medical condition that affects numerous couples worldwide. In April 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that one in six people globally experienced infertility at some point in their lives. In Africa, the prevalence of infertility ranges from 12.6% to 17.5% [2]. In Benin, the available data suggest a rising prevalence of infertility in the population. A retrospective study of patient records at a hospital center revealed that more than 72% of male partners sought consultation for infertility in 2018 [3]. These are alarming figures, which may still be underestimated, as comprehensive data collection remains a challenge.

Reproductive health is an issue that draws the attention of many healthcare professionals, including pharmacists. In addition to their core responsibilities—such as dispensing medications, ensuring proper medication use, prevention, screening, and collaboration with other health professionals—pharmacists play a vital role in Benin’s healthcare system. They are especially important in providing information and guidance to patients regarding their reproductive health [4].

In pharmacies, self-testing devices for male fertility, ovulation, and fertilization are already available for purchase. Additionally, pharmacies are often the first point of contact for patients experiencing reproductive tract disorders, particularly infections. The diagnosis and treatment of infertility can cause significant emotional distress and, in some cases, even lead to depression in affected individuals or couples. In this context, the pharmacist’s ability to listen, show empathy, and offer emotional support—although seemingly simple—are crucial and impactful gestures [4].

It is therefore essential to understand the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of dispensing pharmacists regarding couples’ infertility. This study aims to assess these aspects to identify gaps and offer recommendations for improving the quality of pharmaceutical services related to infertility in Benin.

Methodology

This was a cross-sectional observational study with both descriptive and analytical objectives. It was conducted from July 4 to September 30, 2024, and covers four departments south of Benin: Atlantic, Littoral, Ouémé, and Plateau. The south of the country consists of a low-lying coastal plain dotted with marshes, lakes, and lagoons. It is bordered at east by Nigeria, to the west by Togo, and the Atlantic Ocean, with a 120-kilometers coastline.

Pharmacists on duty in pharmacies located within these departments during the study period were eligible to participate. Recruitment was exhaustive, targeting all pharmacists practicing in the selected areas who voluntarily agreed to take part in the study. Flowchart of study participants regarding the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of community pharmacists in the management of infertility

Pharmacists were contacted via the Order of Pharmacists and a telephone appointment was arranged with them before the pharmacy visit. The questionnaire was pre-tested and validated on a small sample of around ten pharmacists.

The participants completed self-administered survey forms, and only fully completed forms were included in the final analysis. Pharmacists who did not provide consent or who submitted incomplete forms were excluded from the study.

The survey questionnaire covered five major categories: sociodemographic characteristics, general information, knowledge (K), attitudes (A), and practices (P).

Knowledge was assessed via 15 questions addressing definitions, causes, diagnostic procedures, prevention, and treatment of infertility in couples. Attitudes were evaluated through five questions exploring pharmacists’ perceptions of couples’ infertility. Practices were assessed via nine questions focused on practical recommendations, including patient guidance and counseling.

The questionnaire included closed-ended questions (yes/no, true/false, I do not know) and multiple-choice questions offering several qualitative answer options. Three composite scores were created: a “knowledge” score (questions Q13–Q27), an “attitudes” score (Q28–Q32), and a “practices” score (Q33–Q41). Each correct answer was awarded 1 point, whereas incorrect or “don’t know” responses received 0 points.

For each category, the total score was calculated as the sum of the points obtained for its respective questions. The results were interpreted on the basis of the model proposed by Essi et al. and the knowledge, attitude, practice (KAP) framework commonly used in medical research [5]. The following thresholds were applied: fewer than 50% correct answers indicated a poor level, 50–65% an insufficient level, 66–85% an average level, and above 85% a good level.

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices were considered dependent variables, each categorized into four levels: insufficient, poor, average, and good. Data were collected via a structured questionnaire and recorded via KoboCollect software before being exported to an Excel 2017 database. Data analysis was performed via Epi Info version 7.2.1.0 and SPSS version 25.

Quantitative variables are presented as the means with standard deviations when normally distributed or as medians with interquartile ranges when distributions are skewed, as determined by the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies.

Tables and figures were created via Excel 2017, and text processing was performed via Microsoft Word. Chi-square (χ²) tests were used to identify factors associated with pharmacists’ levels of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding the management of couples’ infertility. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from ABMED under N°ABMED/2024/1762/DHE/SE/SA, and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. consent was obtained from all the patients. The questionnaire was completed anonymously, and data confidentiality was strictly maintained.

Results

Survey participation and demographic characteristics

Among the 265 pharmaceutical offices recruited across the four departments, 143 agreed to participate in the survey, representing a participation rate of 53.96%. A total of 167 pharmacists completed the questionnaire.

A total of 55 pharmacists (32.93%) reported having received training or completed an internship related to fertility during their academic studies. The average duration of this training was 3.35 ± 0.90 months, with durations ranging from 1 to 12 months.

Additionally, 19 pharmacists indicated that they had participated in a postgraduate course specifically focused on couple fertility.

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding infertility

With respect to knowledge of couples’ infertility, 79.64% of the participants demonstrated a good level (fair or good) (Fig. 2). Additionally, 68.86% of the pharmacists surveyed exhibited satisfactory attitudes toward cases of couple infertility (Fig. 3).

With regard to their practices, 97% of the pharmacists reported engaging in satisfactory behaviors when managing cases of couple infertility. Specifically, a significant proportion—150 pharmacists (89.82%)—stated that they had provided explanations to couples experiencing infertility. Most also indicated that they referred these patients for appropriate medical consultation.

Discussions

This study involved 143 of the 265 pharmacies, yielding a participation rate of 53.96%. Although this rate may be considered moderate, it falls within the range reported in other surveys involving dispensing pharmacists [6]. Factors such as time constraints and the inherent workload in pharmacy practice often pose barriers to participation.

The mean age of the participating pharmacists was 34.41 ± 1.74 years, indicating a relatively young workforce. This finding is consistent with most studies conducted across Africa [7]. However, it contrasts with similar research carried out in Canada in 2013, which reported a mean participant age of 41 years [6]. Nonetheless, the age distribution observed in our study aligns well with national demographic data [7]. Additionally, the sex ratio of 1.06 reflects a nearly equal representation of men and women in the profession, mirroring the current trend toward the feminization of pharmacies.

The average professional experience of the participants was 9.33 ± 1.5 years, ranging from 1 to 42 years. However, experience alone does not guarantee specific expertise in infertility management. The literature underscores the importance of ongoing, targeted training to maintain and enhance pharmacists’ competencies, especially in dynamic and evolving fields such as reproductive health [8]. Therefore, while professional experience may facilitate effective patient communication and treatment management, it must be complemented by specialized training to ensure optimal care for infertility cases.

Furthermore, only 32.93% of the surveyed pharmacists reported having fertility-related training during their initial education. This finding highlights a significant gap in their preparation, particularly since reproductive health is a complex and rapidly evolving field requiring comprehensive and up-to-date knowledge. A recent study supports this conclusion, revealing that 77% of respondents believe that there is a substantial disconnect between the content of pharmacy education and the realities of pharmacy practice [9].

This inadequacy underscores the urgent need to reevaluate and adapt training programs to better equip future pharmacists with the demands of daily practice, especially in terms of fertility counseling and patient support. Moreover, the average duration of fertility-related training reported in our study—3.35 ± 0.90 months, ranging from 1 to 12 months—may be insufficient to fully master the complexities of infertility management. National and international guidelines emphasize the importance of structured, in-depth continuing education programs that empower pharmacists to play an active role in caring for infertile couples [10].

Additionally, only 19 pharmacists reported having received postgraduate training in couple fertility, reflecting a lack of specialized educational opportunities in this area. Postgraduate training is essential for updating knowledge and acquiring new skills in reproductive health, as documented in the literature [6]. Therefore, it is critical to develop accessible and relevant continuing education programs to strengthen pharmacists’ expertise in infertility management.

With respect to knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), the results revealed that the majority of the pharmacists (79.64%) reported a good level of knowledge (average or good) about couples’ infertility. However, it is important to highlight that approximately 20% of pharmacists exhibited gaps in this area.

This finding underscores the need for ongoing, specialized training to ensure that pharmacists can provide optimal care for infertile couples, as emphasized by experts [11].

Additionally, 68.86% of the pharmacists displayed a satisfactory attitude toward infertility cases, which is encouraging. Nonetheless, there remains room for improvement among the remaining 31.14%. Given the sensitive nature of infertility and its association with significant psychological distress [12], an empathetic and respectful attitude from pharmacists is crucial.

With regard to practices, nearly all the pharmacists (97%) reported satisfactory behaviors. This is a positive indicator, as pharmacists not only provide explanations and referrals for medical consultation but also play an important role in monitoring treatment adherence, managing side effects, and offering emotional support to patients [8].

Limit of the study is the fact that the study was conducted only in 2 departments of Benin and on the bias related to the responses, some pharmacists may not have answered sincerely. Also Cognitive bias among the respondents can be important. In this case, these results certainly cannot be generalized to all pharmacists in Benin.

Conclusion

This study underscores the vital role of dispensing pharmacists in the management of couples’ infertility. However, the findings reveal significant gaps in pharmacist training on this topic. Therefore, it is essential to implement targeted measures to enhance both initial and continuing education for pharmacists. Strengthening pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices through specialized training is crucial. Furthermore, fostering closer collaboration between pharmacists and other healthcare professionals involved in infertility care is necessary to provide comprehensive support to affected couples. Enhanced training and multidisciplinary collaboration will empower pharmacists to play a more active and effective role in managing infertility and improving patient outcomes.
